## 15. The Price Named Aloud

**Yara**

There is a particular kind of morning that comes before a death you've agreed to. The light is ordinary. That's the insult of it. Birds argue about nothing in the pines above the Wolf Reaches, the wind carries woodsmoke and wet fur, and somewhere below the sentry ridge a wolf-kin child is being scolded for stealing honeycomb. The world does not know I've volunteered to die in three days' time, and it has the audacity to keep being beautiful about it.

I sat on the flat stone outside the guest-den—*prisoner-lodging*, if we're being honest, though Ravn had the grace not to use that word—and turned the covenant-fragment over in my palm. It was warm. It had been warm since the Judgment, since the pack council pronounced their verdict with all the comfort of an avalanche: *the debt stands. One life, freely given, from the realm that broke its word.*

Freely given. There was the joke, wasn't it. Twenty years ago, human lords had tried to take my life for this same stone without asking, and I'd run, and the covenant broke, and the storms went wild, and Vellmark outlawed magic, and children grew up afraid of their own hands. All because nobody asked me nicely.

So now I was asking myself nicely. Progress.

"You're doing the face," said Ravn, dropping onto the stone beside me with the boneless ease of someone who'd never once worried about furniture. She was wolf-blooded, tall as a door, with grey shot through her black hair and eyes the color of a river deciding whether to freeze. "The face where you pretend you're at peace."

"I am at peace."

"You're rehearsing peace. It's different. Rehearsing has gaps in it." She plucked the fragment from my hand and held it up to the light like a jeweler assessing a bad bargain. "My mother told me about yours. Yara's mother, she'd say, could call lightning out of a clear sky and apologize to the tree it hit. You have her mouth when you lie."

"I'm not lying."

"No," Ravn agreed, handing it back. "That's the worst part. You mean it. You've decided dying is tidier than living, and everyone here is so grateful to be getting anything at all that no one will tell you it's a terrible trade." She stood, brushed off her knees. "One human life for a whole realm's weather. Your arithmetic is very flattering to us, Eriksen. It's also cowardice wearing ceremony."

"Thank you for your candor."

"Anytime." And she loped off toward the sentry ridge, tail—she didn't have one, but the walk suggested one—all but wagging, leaving me alone with the warm stone and the ordinary light and the growing suspicion that I'd been so busy preparing to die that I hadn't checked whether anyone would let me.

I should have been watching Soren closer. But I'll get to that.

* * *

**Soren**

I had spent twenty years learning how kingdoms actually worked, which is to say: how they lied. Proclamations were lies with seals on them. Inquisitions were lies with swords. And the king's great hunt for the gifted—the hunt I had served, the hunt that had burned the night market and taken Yara's friend and put three frightened children in chains—was the largest lie of all, a harvest dressed as justice, funneling stolen magic into a hollow man's endless appetite.

I knew how lies worked. Which is why, on the second night after the Judgment, I went looking for Ravn, and I did it quietly, the way you do a thing you already know is wrong.

I found her at the meat-racks above the river, salting haunches by lantern-light, and she watched me come the way wolves watch things: without blinking, without hurry, with a running tally of your weaknesses already half-finished.

"Inquisitor Vale," she said.

"Not anymore."

"So they say. What do you want?"

I had prepared three openings and abandoned all of them. "The price," I said. "One life, freely given. Does it specify whose?"

Ravn set down her knife. That was either good or very bad; I genuinely couldn't tell. "It specifies a willing heart from the realm that broke faith. The old lords meant to give a child who hadn't been asked. The council judged Yara was a pawn, not a traitor—but the stone doesn't read verdicts. It reads willingness."

"And if another willing heart offered? A human one? Would the stone know the difference between the exile who fled and the servant of the crown who hunted her kind for a decade?"

The lantern hissed. Somewhere downriver, a wolf sang and was answered.

"You'd balance it," Ravn said slowly. "A betrayal paid by a betrayer. The arithmetic would please the old blood. It might even please Fenris—it's the kind of symmetry gods enjoy." Her eyes narrowed. "But the stone takes what steps onto it freely, Vale, and it doesn't take substitutions made by trickery. If you mean to offer yourself, you'll have to say it aloud, to them, before the stone. No sneaking. No lying to the stone. It hates that more than anything."

"I don't intend to sneak."

"You intend to not-tell Yara until it's done. There's a difference, and it's the same difference that got your king hollowed out—one convenient silence at a time." She picked the knife back up. "Say it aloud or don't say it at all, inquisitor. The stone will taste the difference."

I walked back down through the pines with her words stuck in my chest like a splinter working toward the heart. Say it aloud. I had spent my career making reports, tidy documents in which truth was a material to be shaped. I had lied exactly once in twenty years—for Yara, over a child's drawing of wolves—and it had cracked something open that still hadn't closed.

Fine. Aloud, then. But not yet. Not while there was still time to think of a better way, some third door, some clause in the old law—

Who was I lying to? The splinter knew. I just hadn't let it reach the heart yet.

* * *

**Yara**

They gave us two days of grace before the rite, and I spent the first one doing inventory. It sounds morbid. It was worse than morbid; it was domestic. I mended the seam in my coat. I sharpened the little knife I'd used for twenty years to carve charms, and thought about leaving it to the gifted children from the market, if any of them ever learned where I'd gone. I wrote instructions for calming a storm in my worst handwriting, sealed them, and addressed them to *whoever needs this*, which is either a will or a prayer depending on how generous you are.

Soren watched me do all of it. He sat across the small den with his ruined inquisitor's coat folded beside him—he'd stopped wearing it after Ilse defected and brought us word of the king's march north—and he watched with that stillness he had, the stillness of a man cataloguing everything for a report no one would ever read.

"You're writing your own eulogy in charm-notes," he said finally.

"I'm writing *instructions*. Someone has to keep the storms polite after I'm gone."

"Yara."

"Don't." I kept my eyes on the page. My handwriting had gone wobbly, which was humiliating. "If you start saying my name in that voice, I'll lose the thread, and the thread is the only thing keeping this dignified."

"There's nothing dignified about it. You're twenty years old inside a forty-year-old body—young enough to die stupidly and old enough to know better."

"That's rich, coming from the man who spent a decade dragging the gifted into cells."

"Yes," he said, without flinching, because he never flinched, which was one of the four hundred things about him I refused to examine. "And I've been paying for it since the market burned. Let me pay the rest."

I looked up then. He was watching me with an expression I'd seen once before, in a shepherd's hut in the snow, right before he confessed he'd known about my exile all along and said nothing. It was the expression of a man standing at a door he'd already opened in his mind.

"Soren. What did you do?"

"Nothing. Yet." Too smooth. Twenty years of interrogations and he still couldn't lie to me; it was almost sweet.

"What. Did. You. Do."

* * *

**Soren**

Here is what I had failed to account for, in all my careful planning: I had taught Yara to read weather, and Yara had taught me to read people, and neither of us had ever managed to hide anything longer than a day.

She stood up, and the temperature in the den dropped—not her storm-calling, just her, the way fury moved through her like cold front. "You went to Ravn."

"I spoke with Ravn."

"You asked her if the stone would take you instead."

"The stone requires a willing human heart. I am, whatever my many failings, human, and I assure you my willingness is complete. It's the most certain thing I've ever been."

"Don't you dare make it sound like a tax audit." Her voice cracked on the last word, and I hated myself for the relief I felt at hearing it crack—proof she wasn't calm either, proof this cost her too. "You don't get to *volunteer*, Soren. This isn't a posting. It isn't a penance you can sign for. It's mine. I broke it loose when I ran. My feet, my flight, my debt."

"Your feet were nineteen years old and following orders you'd never agreed to. Mine were thirty-five and carrying warrants." I stepped closer. "You want to talk about debts? Fine. Let's talk honestly, for once, without your martyrdom in the room. You fled a sacrifice. I executed the hunt that punished everyone who reminded the crown of you. Every gifted person I processed, every family I tore apart in Vellmark—I was the instrument of the fear you spent twenty years hiding from. If anyone owes this stone a life, it's the man who enforced the breaking."

"That's not—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "That's not how it works. The council named me. They showed me the evidence. I stood in front of the whole pack and they said my name, Soren. *Yara Eriksen breaks the covenant; Yara Eriksen pays.* If I let you step onto that stone instead, I'm doing exactly what the old lords did. Substituting a body. Spending a life like coin."

"They asked you to be a sacrifice without consent. I'm offering consent. Consent is the entire moral difference, and you know it, because you've spent three days lecturing yourself with it."

"Stop being reasonable at me!"

"There it is," I said, and couldn't help the ghost of a smile. "The famous Eriksen temper. I was starting to worry grief had improved you."

Something flew at my head. It was a boot. I caught it, mostly.

* * *

**Yara**

He caught the boot. Of course he caught the boot. Twenty years of dodging hard questions had made him quick.

We stood there breathing at each other, the den smelling of pine resin and lamp oil, the covenant-fragment sitting on the table between us like a chaperone with opinions. Outside, the pack had gone quiet—they always went quiet when we argued, the whole Reaches holding its breath, and I understood suddenly that they could hear every word. Wolf ears. Wonderful. Our entire doomed romance had an audience of sixty.

"You can't have this," I said, quieter now. "I need you to understand why. It's not pride. Well—it's some pride. But mostly: if you die on that stone, I have to live in a world you died to fix, knowing I let you. I did small for twenty years, Soren. I sold trinkets and calmed storms through keyholes and called it survival. I am not going to spend the rest of my life being the person who survived you. I'd rather—"

"Don't say rather."

"I'd rather the stone take me and be done."

"And I'd rather it take me," he said, "because I have spent my whole life serving lies, and this is the first true thing I've ever been offered. Do you understand what you'd be taking from me? Not my life. My one chance to make the ledger balance. You'd leave me alive in a world where I never once paid for what I did, and you'd call that mercy." His voice finally, finally roughened. "I freed six prisoners and burned my own name to do it. Ilse chose her side. Let me choose mine."

"You chose it months ago. On the road. When you stayed. When you carried the children across the ford." I crossed to him, close enough to see the exhaustion under his eyes, the grey coming in early at his temples. "You don't owe the stone a death. You've been paying in installments this whole time."

"That's not how gods keep accounts."

"No," I admitted. "It isn't."

* * *

**Soren**

We reached the stalemate at midnight, the way all our worst arguments did: exhausted, unresolved, and closer than either of us would admit. She slept—or lay down facing the wall, which for Yara was the same thing—and I sat by the banked fire turning the problem over like a lock I couldn't pick.

The stone required willingness spoken aloud. Ravn had been clear. So the answer was simple, and terrible: speak first. Stand on the stone before the rite, before Yara could stop me, and give myself while the giving was still possible. The stone wouldn't refuse a willing heart. Whatever happened afterward—whether it accepted me outright or merely complicated matters—she would be alive to hate me for it, and hating me was a livable condition. I'd grown fond of it.

I was still turning it over when her voice came from the bedroll, dry as winter grass.

"You're planning something. I can hear you thinking. It sounds like paperwork."

"I'm admiring the fire."

"You're going to try to sneak onto that stone, aren't you. Before the rite. Because you heard what Ravn said about speaking aloud, and you thought: *speak first, win.*"

I said nothing, which was answer enough.

She sat up. In the dark her eyes caught the ember-light, and for a moment she looked exactly like what she was—a storm-caller deciding where to put the lightning. "Soren Vale. I have hidden from an empire for twenty years. I have out-waited inquisitors, out-run hunters, and out-stubborned a god. What makes you think you can out-sneak *me*?"

"Hope," I said honestly. "And a general decline in my respect for rules."

Something happened to her face then—anger losing a wrestling match with something else. She crossed the space between us and took my face in both hands, storm-callused palms, steady as bedrock.

"Then hear me aloud, since everyone insists on aloud," she said. "I love you. I have loved you since the hut in the snow, probably since the ruins, possibly since the frost on my windowpanes, and I will not build a future on your grave. So we solve this together or not at all. No sneaking. No substitutions. No convenient silences." Her thumb traced the scar along my jaw. "Swear it."

And because I was, at the end of everything, an honest man in exactly one direction, I swore it.

Which is when the horns began, far to the south—three notes, repeated, the signal Ravn's scouts used for an army on the move—and the den filled with wolf-song rising from every ridge at once, high and urgent, and Ravn burst through the door with frost on her shoulders and terror she wasn't bothering to hide.

"They're here," she said. "The king is here. And he's brought the machine."

The covenant-fragment on the table flared white-hot, and somewhere beneath the earth, something ancient opened one eye and began, very softly, to growl.
