## 16. The King Comes North

I have marched behind a great many banners in my eleven years of service. Banners for coronations, banners for funerals, banners for wars that were over before we finished the march to them. I have learned that a banner tells you nothing about what you're following and everything about what the man in front wants you to believe you're following.

The banner at the head of this column was white silk worked with a grey wolf, jaws open, and beneath it rode King Aldric Vane, the Undying, the Beloved, the Man Who Had Not Aged In Forty Years — though the heralds had stopped using that last title about a decade ago, when people started noticing.

He did not look like a king. He looked like a coat hung on a hook, if the coat had been worn by a hundred different men and none of them had bothered to shake out the shoulders. His face was smooth the way river stones are smooth, and his eyes were the pale grey of weather that has given up on being anything but weather. When he smiled — and he smiled often, because smiling cost him nothing, being mostly a memory of smiling — it arrived a half-second after the rest of his face decided it should.

I rode three lengths back and to the left, which is where you put a captain you trust with your soldiers but not with your thoughts.

Behind us came the machine.

They called it the Tithe Engine, which was a joke made by someone who had never heard it run, or by someone who had heard it run and thought that was funny. It traveled on twelve iron wheels, each taller than a hay wagon, drawn by forty oxen whose eyes had gone dull as pewter since the day they were harnessed to it. Its body was a cathedral's worth of black iron shaped like — and I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left — like a funnel laid on its side. Like something built to pour. Copper veins ran along its spine, and from those veins hung chains, and from the chains hung cages, and the cages were not empty.

I had been told they were empty. That was the official line: *the cages are for transport of the condemned to the northern works.* I had signed requisition forms confirming the cages were empty. I had counted them empty twice a week for the length of the march south, and then again on the march back north, and every time I counted, my arithmetic came back wrong, because there were sounds coming from inside them that empty cages do not make. Small sounds. The kind of sounds a person makes when they have stopped believing anyone is listening.

Captain Ilse Brandt, protégée of the disgraced Soren Vale, keeper of accurate counts, had begun keeping two sets of books.

* * *

The column made camp on the third night below the moorlands, in a fold of hills where the grass grew in patches and the patches grew further apart the further north you looked. I walked the picket lines because walking the picket lines is what I do instead of sleeping, and because soldiers tell picket-walking captains things they would never tell a captain sitting by a fire.

Corporal Denna Hark was on the east line, watching the Tithe Engine's dark bulk against the stars. She'd been with me since she was nineteen. She was twenty-six now and had stopped humming while she stood watch, which is the sort of thing you notice only after it's gone.

"Captain," she said, without turning. "The oxen."

"What about them?"

"The ones pulling the engine. They don't eat." She said it flatly, the way you report a supply discrepancy. "We feed them. They eat. But they don't get thinner and they don't get fatter and their eyes—" She stopped. Started again. "My father kept oxen. I know oxen."

"I know," I said.

"And the cages, Captain."

"The cages are for transport—"

"Don't." Just the one word, quiet, and then she turned and looked at me, and her face in the torchlight was the face of someone asking permission to stop pretending. "Please don't say it to me. I've been saying it to myself for six weeks and I can't make it hold anymore. There's a boy in cage nine. I hear him at night. He hums. Same four notes, over and over, like he's trying to remember a song his mother sang." Her jaw worked. "Empty cages don't hum, Captain."

"No," I said. "They don't."

She waited. I understood then that she wasn't asking me to fix it. She was asking me to say it out loud so that it existed outside her own head, so that she wasn't the only one carrying it, because a thing carried alone will rot you from the inside, and I knew that better than anyone on this hillside.

"There are sixty-one people in those cages," I said. "Fifty-eight adults, three children. They are not being transported to the northern works. The northern works *is* the machine. And the reason the king has not aged in forty years is chained to the back of our own column, drinking through copper veins, and every league we march north we are delivering him more."

Denna closed her eyes. Opened them. Nodded once, the way soldiers nod, which is to say completely and without any comfort in it.

"What do we do?" she asked.

That was the question, wasn't it. Eleven years of service, and it came down to a corporal on the east picket asking me what we do, and meaning: *you're the one who decides when we stop being this.*

"We wait," I said, because it was true, and because the truth buys time, and time was the only weapon I had that night. "And we count."

* * *

The king summoned me to his pavilion on the fifth morning, which surprised me until it didn't. Hollow men collect hollow officers; we are easy to carry.

His pavilion was warm in a way no tent should be warm, as though heat itself had been poured into it from somewhere else. Maps covered the table — the moorlands, the river country, and beyond them the Wolf Reaches, marked in fresh ink with a circle and the words *PRIMARY VEIN* in a clerk's careful hand.

"Captain Brandt." He didn't look up. "Your master taught you to read ground, I'm told. Tell me what you see."

I looked at the map. I saw what he wanted me to see: the Reaches, the covenant-stone at their heart, the old roads converging like veins toward a wound. I saw what I actually saw, which was the same thing, described honestly.

"A reservoir," I said. "All the wild power of the north pooled behind one stone. Break the stone or tap it, and everything flows downhill to whoever holds the spigot."

Something moved behind his eyes — pleasure, or the memory of it. "Vale trained you well. Pity about Vale." He traced the circle with one finger, and where his finger touched the map, the ink seemed to dim, the way candlelight dims near cold glass. "The stone must be broken, Captain. Not tapped. Tapping leaves structure, and structure can be rebuilt. We break it, drain it dry, and the wolf-kin go the way of their god — into story. My engineers assure me the engine can be positioned within three days of arrival. Your task is the three days. Hold the approaches. Nothing reaches the stone. Nothing reaches *me*."

"And the prisoners?" I asked, before I could stop myself. "Are they for the engine as well?"

Now he looked up. For a moment — just a moment — the smoothness slipped, and underneath it was something old and starving and terribly patient, the way a well is patient. "Everything feeds the work, Captain. Everything. That is what an empire is: a machine for making sure nothing is wasted." He smiled, and the smile arrived late again, a guest who knows he isn't welcome. "You've done well. You'll be remembered."

*I'll be remembered.* From a man who could not remember being young. I saluted, because my arm knew how even when the rest of me had resigned, and I walked out into the cold morning air feeling like a woman who had just been shown the inside of a clock and could never again unhear the ticking.

* * *

That night I wrote two letters.

The first was to the garrison at Vellmark, resigning my commission, citing health. It was a lie, and it was also the most honest document I had produced in eleven years, because my health was precisely the problem: I had been healthy enough, all this time, to keep counting.

The second letter I burned. It had been addressed to Soren Vale, care of no address at all, and it said, in full: *You were right. I'm sorry it took me this long. I am coming north, and I am bringing what I can carry.*

What I could carry turned out to be thirty-one soldiers, though I didn't know that yet when I sat down to write it.

* * *

Desertion is a logistics problem, which is why so few attempt it properly. Loyalty makes men stupid; I intended to be disloyal with precision.

I spent two days building the architecture of my own treason. I rotated the watch so that the east picket — my picket, Denna's picket, the picket that had stopped humming — was staffed entirely by soldiers I had chosen over six years of choosing. I arranged for the remount herd to graze, quite reasonably, on the north side of camp. I drafted orders authorizing a reconnaissance patrol toward the Reaches, signed by me, countersigned by a staff officer too tired and too ambitious to read past the first line. Thirty-one names went onto that patrol roster. Every one of them was a name I trusted, and eleven of them were names that had come to me quietly in the dark over the previous two nights, soldiers who had found reasons to stand near my fire and ask questions about cages.

On the third night, the moon failed us, which was courteous of it, and the patrol rode north with survey instruments we did not intend to use.

We rode hard for a day and a half. On the second evening, Denna drew rein beside me on a ridge above the moorlands and pointed south with her chin. Far behind, small with distance, the Tithe Engine's escort fires pricked the dark like a constellation that had fallen and couldn't get up.

"They'll notice by tomorrow noon," she said. "Ilse — Captain — they'll send Brandt's own people after Brandt. Someone will enjoy that."

"Let them enjoy it," I said. "We have until noon tomorrow to find two fugitives, a pack of wolf-kin, and a covenant-stone, and warn them that an army and a machine are coming to eat the world." I nudged my horse forward down the ridge. "I suggest we skip the wolf-kin and start with the fugitives. Soren always did have a gift for finding trouble. It stands to reason trouble remembers where he lives."

* * *

We found the shepherd's hut by smoke — a thin thread of it, domestic and careful, the smoke of people who had learned that smoke gets you killed. I left the patrol in the trees with Denna and rode in alone with my hands visible and my sword slung behind my saddle rather than at my hip, which is the oldest peace-sign there is, and the least reliable, and the only one I had.

Soren Vale stepped out of the hut before I reached the door. He looked worse than disgrace — disgrace is clean compared to what the road does — but his eyes were the same eyes that had trained me, and they went from my face to the treeline to my empty scabbard and back with the old unhurried arithmetic.

"Captain Brandt," he said. "Come to finish my education?"

"Something like that." I stopped ten feet out. Behind him, in the doorway, stood Yara Eriksen — smaller than the storm I'd seen tear apart a ford, wrapped in a blanket, watching me with the particular stillness of prey deciding whether the predator has really committed to this. "The king is coming north. Full column, four thousand foot, the engine, and him. Three days behind me, maybe four. The engine isn't a siege device. It's a tap. He means to break the covenant-stone and drain the Reaches dry, and the cages on the back of it are already full of the people your storms helped escape, plus fifty-eight more nobody escaped."

Yara's stillness changed quality — from caution to something colder and older. "How many cages?"

"Sixty-one prisoners. I counted." I held their gaze. "I've been counting since Vellmark. It's the only thing I'm good at that matters anymore."

Soren exhaled slowly. "Why?" he asked, and it wasn't hostile, which somehow made it harder. "Why defect? Why now? Why ride three days through open country to hand the crown's plan to the people the crown wants dead?"

Because of a corporal on the east picket. Because of four notes hummed in the dark. Because I had signed requisition forms for empty cages until the word *empty* curdled in my mouth. Because somewhere between the ford and the moorlands I had realized that I had spent eleven years learning to serve, and not one hour learning to choose, and that the difference between those two things was the entire difference between a soldier and a weapon.

But I said none of that, because Ilse Brandt does not perform her feelings for audiences, even audiences who deserve them.

"Because I'm good at my job," I said, "and my job is now yours. Four thousand infantry, a drained-and-draining machine, a king who cannot be killed by ordinary means because there is almost nothing ordinary left in him to kill, and three days' warning. That's what I bring. Take it or leave it, but take it quickly, because noon tomorrow they'll notice I'm gone, and they'll send someone competent."

From inside the hut, a low sound — not a growl exactly, more like a door opening in a house of growls. A shape resolved at the edge of the firelight: grey fur, amber eyes, a wolf-blooded woman with a scarred jaw who looked at me the way you look at weather coming in off the sea.

"She says the pack will want to decide whether to trust you," Soren translated, though I hadn't needed translation. "Or eat you. Possibly both, in that order."

"Efficient," I said. "I respect that in an organization."

Yara pulled the blanket tighter. "Three days," they said quietly. "One stone. One storm left in me worth the name." They looked at me, and then past me, south, toward the constellation of falling fires, and I watched them do the same arithmetic I had been doing since the east picket — counting what was coming, counting what we had, and finding the sums obscene.

Then they straightened, and the air around them tasted faintly of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"Well," Yara Eriksen said, "we'd better wake the wolves."
