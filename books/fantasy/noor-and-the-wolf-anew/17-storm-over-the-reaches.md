## 17. Storm Over the Reaches

I have called storms in secret for twenty years. Little ones. Polite ones. Storms that apologized on their way out the door, that bent themselves small so no one would look up and wonder.

The storm I call now is not polite.

It begins, as all my storms begin, in my chest — a pressure like a held breath, like grief with nowhere to go. I stand on the ridge above the covenant-stone with Soren's hand at my back and the whole grey morning waiting, and I let the breath out.

The sky answers.

* * *

You should understand what came before the answering, because it was not quiet either.

We had one night in the Wolf Reaches before the horns. One night of pine-smoke and Ravn's people watching us from the treeline with yellow eyes and old grievances. The pack council had given us until the next full moon to prepare the rite — a mercy, Ravn said, though her face said *a sentence deferred*. The stone itself stood in a clearing two miles north of the sentry lines: a broken ring of granite, half-swallowed by moss, humming faintly underfoot like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. When I touched it, Fenris's voice moved through me like cold water finding old channels. *Come,* he said. *Bring what you owe.*

Then, before dawn, Ilse Brandt rode into camp alone, mud to the thighs, her captain's braid torn out, and said four words that ended the last of our sleep:

"He's coming. Now."

Not next week. Not at the full moon. The hollow king had learned where we were — someone had sold us, or scried us, or simply followed the weather, because my storm over the ford had been visible from thirty miles, a green-grey pillar of rain standing on the moorland like a warning written too large. And he was not coming with a column. He was coming with the machine.

Ilse described it in a flat soldier's voice while Ravn's scouts confirmed it: a great wheeled engine of iron and glass, drawn by forty oxen and a hundred conscripts, its heart a lattice of covenant-shards — stolen fragments of stones like ours, ripped from shrines across the realm. It drank magic the way a leech drinks blood, and everything it drained fed the king's endless hunger, his ageless, hollowing thirst. He meant to park it beside our covenant-stone and drink the border dry. Every wolf-kin within a hundred miles would feel their wildness gutter out like lamps. Every gifted human would wake empty.

"They'll reach the clearing by midmorning," Ilse finished. "Maybe sooner if the ground holds."

Ravn looked at me across the fire. "Then we fight there, not here."

"Or," I said, and my voice did something strange, "we don't fight at all. We do the rite this morning. Pay the price. Reforge it before they arrive."

Soren went very still beside me. He had spent three days since the shepherd's hut not-quite-saying things, and I had spent three days letting him, both of us carrying the same arithmetic in our pockets: the covenant wanted one life, freely given, and I had already decided whose it would be, and he had clearly decided otherwise, and neither of us had found a way to say it aloud without it becoming an argument that would end with one of us tied to a tree for safekeeping.

But the morning took the choice out of our hands. Because the rite needed preparation — songs, fasts, the pack's assent — and the king's vanguard crested the southern hills while the pack was still arguing about whether humans could be trusted to stand in their battle line.

So: war first. Covenant after. If there was an after.

* * *

Now the sky is answering, and I am learning what I actually am.

Here is what nobody tells you about power buried for twenty years: it does not come up gentle. It comes up like a well poisoned at the bottom, like all those small swallowed storms finally having their say. I stand on the ridge with my arms open and I pull, and the horizon bends toward me.

Clouds come off the mountains in a wall. Not drifting — *marching*, shouldering each other aside, going black as wet slate. The temperature drops so fast my breath turns to smoke. Somewhere below, the first ranks of the king's army stop marching, because soldiers know weather the way farmers do, and even hollow men flinch when the light goes wrong.

"This is the part," Soren says at my shoulder, "where I admit I'm frightened."

"Don't be frightened yet," I say. "Save it."

"For when?"

"For when I tell you to run."

He doesn't laugh. He takes my hand instead, briefly, hard, and then goes down the slope to where the line is forming — wolf-kin on the left flank, a loose crescent of them, spears and teeth; Ilse's defectors on the right, maybe sixty soldiers in mismatched armor who chose treason at a river crossing and have been surprising themselves ever since; and between them, holding the center like a seam, a handful of the freed gifted from Vellmark, children no longer, staffs in their hands, faces set.

I stay on the ridge. That was the plan. I am the artillery; I am the weather. Ravn said it plainly: "You are not a spear. You are the storm. Stay high, stay alive, and when the machine shows its throat, tear it out."

The machine shows its throat at midmorning.

It comes over the hill like a cathedral on wheels, and I feel it before I see it — a *pulling*, a hunger with a compass-needle, dragging at the wildness in the air the way my storm drags at clouds. The wolf-kin nearest the road stagger. One of the young ones drops to her knees and howls, and the sound comes out thin, frayed, a wolf-song with the wolf taken out of it.

And behind it, on a raised platform of black wood, stands the king.

I have seen him once before, from a distance, at a coronation I wasn't invited to. He looks worse than portraits. Portraits show a man; what rides the platform is a man-shaped absence, skin stretched over decades of drinking, eyes like wells with nothing at the bottom. Even at this distance I can feel him *reaching* — not with the machine, but personally, greedily, the way a starving man looks at bread.

The army deploys. Drums. Banners. The machine's crew cranking its lattice open like a flower made of knives.

Below me, Ilse's voice carries along the right flank, steady as a drill-yard: "Hold. Hold. Let them come to the stone." The wolf-kin don't need telling. They flow forward low and silent, and the left edge of the king's line meets them and dissolves into screaming.

Then everything happens at once, which is war's only honest schedule.

I call the lightning down.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because I will only do this once in my life and I want to remember it accurately, even if accuracy costs me.

It is not bolts. Bolts are what storms do when they're being reasonable. What I do is open a door between the cloud and the ground and leave it open, and the sky pours through. Three strikes, five, ten, walking down the king's line like fingers drumming a table, each one turning a banner to ash and a pike to slag. The thunder isn't a sound anymore; it's a condition, something you live inside. Rain comes sideways. Hail the size of eggs hammers the machine's glass lattice, and the crew scrambles to shield it, and I grin — actually grin — because for the first time in twenty years I am not hiding, not shrinking, not apologizing. I am *loud*. I am the loudest thing in a hundred miles and every gifted child who ever flinched at their own frost is somewhere inside that sound, vindicated.

But armies are not weathered away, only weathered. The king's lines close ranks under the barrage, shields locked, and push. And the machine keeps pulling, and my storm — my beautiful, furious storm — starts to fray at the edges, because the machine is drinking the air itself, drinking the charge out of my clouds, sipping at me through the distance like a thief dipping into my purse.

I feel it find me.

A tendril of that hunger, threading up the ridge. Cold. Patient. It wraps around my gift and *tugs*, and my knees buckle, and suddenly I'm on one knee in the mud with hail bouncing off my shoulders, watching my own lightning gutter mid-strike, watching the wolf-kin line bend, watching Ilse's soldiers give ground yard by yard toward the stone —

— and then the pressure shatters, because Soren has done something insane.

He has walked out of the line. Alone. Into the no-man's-land between armies, in his old inquisitor's coat, arms wide, and shouted — I hear it even through the thunder, because the storm carries his voice the way it carries mine now, as if it has decided we are a matched set:

"YOUR KING IS A THIEF! I served him! I read his ledgers! Everything he promises you, he is drinking — your years, your children's gifts, your land's own soul! Stand down and I will show you the receipts!"

And some part of the enemy line — not much, a handful, a dozen — wavers. Shields dip. Because Soren Vale was famous, once, for never lying, and even disgraced, even traitor, that reputation has weight.

The machine's crew, spooked, crank the lattice wider to drink faster, and that is their mistake, because a lattice opened that wide has a throat, and throats can be choked.

I get up off my knee.

I stop fighting the pull and *push into it* — pour myself down that hungry tendril like floodwater down a drain, more than it can swallow, a storm's worth of storm forced through a straw. The machine shrieks — actual metal shrieking, glass crazing — and lightning crawls all over it like white ants, and two of its oxen drop dead in their traces, and the crew flees.

That's when Fenris arrives.

* * *

He does not come as a dream this time. He comes as weather.

The storm stops — my storm, all of it, everywhere, mid-fury, rain hanging in the air like beads on a string — and out of that suspended silence walks a wolf the size of a chapel, grey as the underside of clouds, eyes like the moment before lightning. His paws make no prints. Where he passes, the fighting stutters and dies, because wolf-kin and men alike fall still, and even the hollow king leans forward on his platform with an expression I can only call *appetite*.

Fenris crosses the battlefield as if it were empty and stops before the covenant-stone, and turns his head, and looks at me.

His voice, when it comes, is inside my bones rather than my ears.

**YOU HAVE MADE MUCH NOISE, LITTLE STORM-CALLER.**

"I've been saving it up," I manage.

**THE COVENANT IS BROKEN AND BLEEDING. THE STONE DRINKS NOTHING. YOUR ENEMY COMES TO DRINK WHAT REMAINS.** His gaze sweeps the field — the dead, the wavering lines, Soren standing exposed in the mud, Ravn bleeding but upright, the young wolf-kin whose song came out threadbare. **SO PAY THE PRICE NOW. HERE. MID-BATTLE, IF YOU MUST. ONE LIFE, FREELY GIVEN, FROM EACH REALM. THE STONE DOES NOT CARE FOR YOUR CONVENIENCE.**

My mouth goes dry. "Now?"

**NOW.**

And here is the terrible thing, the thing I will carry: for one heartbeat, I am *relieved*. Because I have been carrying the arithmetic for weeks — one life, freely given, and I had chosen mine — and the choosing had been eating me alive, and now the bill has come due and at least the waiting is over. I start walking down the ridge toward the stone, toward the god, toward the price, and my legs are steady, and that steadiness frightens me more than the battle does.

Behind me, boots in mud, running. Soren's voice, ragged: "Yara — Yara, wait —"

"No," I say, without turning. "You heard him. One life. Mine. I've known for weeks. Don't you dare take this from me."

"You don't get to decide that alone—" He catches my arm, spins me, and his face is wrecked, rain and fury and terror. "You absolute stubborn— I negotiated with Ravn three days ago. While you slept. My life balances yours. A human betrayal, a human payment. It's *cleaner*—"

"It's *worse*—"

"It's *mine to give*—"

And we are shouting at each other on a battlefield, in front of a god, while two armies hold their breath, and Fenris watches us with those lightning-gap eyes and says, with what I swear is amusement:

**AH. I SEE THE PROBLEM.**

"What problem?" I snap.

**YOU HAVE EACH DECIDED TO DIE FOR THE OTHER, AND NEITHER OF YOU WILL ALLOW IT.** The great head tilts. **THE OLD TERMS WOULD CALL THIS AN IMPASSE.**

Somewhere behind us, the machine's crew, regrouping, begins cranking the lattice again. The king rises from his platform. There is no time. There was never any time.

Fenris says:

**BUT I AM NOT BOUND BY THE OLD TERMS. I WROTE THEM. AND I HAVE WATCHED YOU BOTH SINCE THE FORD, AND I HAVE A COUNTEROFFER —**

The king's voice rolls across the field, hollow and vast, cutting the god off mid-word:

"**GIVE ME THE STORM-CALLER, AND I WILL SPARE THE REST.**"

Every head turns. The hollow king stands atop his machine, arms spread, and the lattice behind him flares, drinking deep, and his empty well-eyes fix on me with something almost like love, the way a leech might love a vein.

Fenris's growl shakes the ground.

**CHOOSE QUICKLY, THEN,** he says to me, to us, to everyone. **HIS OFFER OR MINE. THE STONE IS OPEN. THE PRICE IS NAMED. AND THE NEXT WORD SPOKEN WILL BE THE ONE THAT REWRITES THE WORLD.**

Soren's hand finds mine in the mud and rain.

I open my mouth.
