## 20. Aftermath of Weather

I have counted the dead after raids, after border skirmishes, after the winter the fever came through the dens and took the old ones in their sleep. Counting is a wolf's work. You do it because someone must, and because numbers are honest when grief is not. Grief lies to you. It tells you the dead are only sleeping, that they'll wake hungry and complaining as always. Numbers don't lie. Numbers say: forty-one. Numbers say: nine of them cubs or near-cubs. Numbers say the field where the king's machine stood is scorched in a circle so wide you could fit three longhouses inside it, and nothing will grow there for a generation, maybe two.

So I counted. All morning, while the snow fell soft as an apology, I walked the lines of the fallen with a strip of charcoal and a slate, and I wrote down names, and kinfolk came to me one by one to confirm what I'd written, and some of them wept on my shoulder, which is a thing wolves do not do lightly, and I let them, because my shoulders were the broadest available and grief does not care about dignity.

By midday my slate held forty-one names and my shoulders held most of the pack's sorrow, and I was tired in the way that lives in the bones rather than the muscles.

Then Halvard found me and said, "The storm-callers are waking," and I put down the slate.

* * *

They had been laid side by side in the healing den, under furs, near the fire, because no one knew what else to do with them. The covenant-stone had taken them the way a river takes two stones dropped from a bridge — swallowed them whole, rolled them over, and given them back changed. Yara Eriksen and Soren Vale, the exile and the inquisitor, the storm and the man who used to hunt storms. They had walked into that light together holding hands like cubs crossing a frozen lake, and the light had taken them, and then the battle ended — not won, exactly, but *ended*, the way a fever breaks — and the two of them had collapsed among the wolf-kin and the defected soldiers like puppets with cut strings.

For two days they did not wake.

I sat with them, off and on, between the counting and the burying. Partly it was duty; Ravn of the Reaches, who remembered their mother, who vouched for them before the council, who argued for them when the council wanted to turn them away — Ravn should be there when they opened their eyes, if they opened their eyes. But partly — and I am old enough to admit this to myself if to no one else — partly it was something less dignified. I wanted to look at them. I wanted to understand what the stone had made of them, because whatever it was, we would all have to live with it.

Here is what I saw, in those two days of watching:

Their breathing had synchronized. I noticed it the first night and thought it coincidence. By the second dawn it had not broken once. Two chests rising and falling in perfect time, like a single creature wearing two skins.

Frost formed on Yara's lashes even though the den was warm, and melted, and formed again. And Soren — the inquisitor, the crown's hound, the man whose hands I had expected to be cold forever — Soren's fingers twitched in his sleep in small patterns, and where they twitched against the fur beneath him, the fur *moved*, gently, the way grass moves when wind passes over it though the air in the den was still.

Old Maren, our healer, who has forgotten more about magic than the rest of us ever learned, stood over them on the second evening and said a word I hadn't heard since I was a cub: "*Tvibundne.*" Twice-bound. She said it quietly, the way you say the name of a sickness or a blessing, and she wouldn't say more, and that frightened me more than anything the king's army had done.

* * *

Yara woke first, on the third morning, and the first thing they did was apologize.

I want that recorded somewhere, in case the songs get it wrong. Not a battle cry. Not a prophecy. An apology — hoarse, half-swallowed, aimed at me: "Ravn. The wounded — how many? How many didn't—"

"Forty-one," I said, because they deserved the number straight, no cushioning. "Nine were young."

Yara closed their eyes. A tear froze on their cheek before it could fall. I watched it happen — watched the salt water crystallize, glitter, and then, slowly, deliberately, melt again, as if Yara's own grief had asked permission of their magic and been granted it. Twenty years of burying that gift, and now it answered to their sorrow without being called. I saw them realize it too. I saw the flinch.

"I can't make it stop," they whispered. "It's — everything's louder now. The weather. The ground. There's a river under the Reaches I never knew about, I can hear it, it's been down there for centuries and nobody told it what to do and now it's telling *me*—" They pressed the heel of a hand against their sternum, hard, as if trying to quiet something knocking from inside. "And there's more. It isn't just the storm-side anymore. I can feel the pack. Not thoughts. Just — presence. Warmth. Like lying near a fire in another room."

"That's the bond," I said. "Maren calls it twice-bound. You and the inquisitor split the price between you, and the covenant — well. Covenants are greedy things. It took the bargain you offered and then it kept the change."

Yara laughed. It came out cracked and wet and real. "Kept the change. Fenris would be proud."

"Don't joke about the god in front of the god's mountain," I said, and then, because I am not made of stone myself, I sat down beside them and took their frozen hand in mine. Wolf-kin don't do that either, usually. But their mother had been my friend, and their hand was very cold, and some debts you pay in warmth.

"You're half-heard now," I told them. "Do you understand what that means? The human villages will smell the wild on you. The deep dens will smell the hearth-smoke. You'll walk through Vellmark and every gifted child will turn to look at you without knowing why, and every wolf on the border will lift its head when you pass. You belong to both realms now and neither. It's the hardest road there is. I've watched people try to walk it." I thought of others, long dead, and did not name them. "Most don't finish."

Yara looked at their own hand in mine — the frost-marks that had risen along their knuckles during the binding, pale silver traceries like lightning burned into skin — and said, quietly, "Good."

I blinked. "Good?"

"I spent twenty years belonging nowhere on purpose," they said. "Hiding in a city that hated what I was, selling charms to people who'd have burned me. Belonging to neither realm by *choice*. At least now it's honest. At least now it's a bridge instead of a grave." Their jaw set in a way I recognized — their mother's jaw, gods rot it, making the same stubborn shape it made the day she walked out of the Reaches carrying a child and a secret. "I didn't survive all this to go back to being small, Ravn."

No. I didn't suppose they had.

* * *

Soren woke at dusk, and waking, he screamed.

Not from pain — Maren checked, twice, and found his body mended, only the ordinary exhaustion of a man who had offered himself to a god-stone and lived. He screamed because of what he heard. I held his shoulders while he shook, and he gripped my arms hard enough to bruise, and between gasps he tried to explain: "There are voices — hundreds — the soldiers, I can hear the soldiers at the ford, they're arguing about whether to march home, and there's a woman crying in Vellmark, she's miles away, how is she *miles away* — and the wolves, the wolves are *singing*, I can hear the singing, why can I hear the singing—"

"Because you're ours now," I told him, and I confess I enjoyed saying it more than was strictly kind. "A little. Half-heard, half-seen, half-kin. The covenant doesn't do things by halves except when it does. You gave half your life to bind the realms together, inquisitor. This is what binding feels like from the inside."

He stared at me. Grey eyes, the eyes of a man who had spent his career making other people afraid, and now the fear had come home to roost behind them. "I hunted people like Yara," he said. "For eleven years. I dragged them out of their homes in front of their children. And now I'm—" His voice broke. "Now I can hear a woman crying in Vellmark and I know it's because her son was taken in the purge, I *know* that, the way I know my own name, and I can't unhear it. Is this the punishment? Is this what the stone thinks justice looks like?"

"No," I said. "The stone doesn't punish. That was the king's trade, not the covenant's. What the stone gives is *witness*. You asked to stand between the realms. Well. Now you stand there. Forever. And from that doorway, you will hear everything both sides suffer, and you will spend the rest of your life deciding what to do about it." I leaned close. "That's not punishment, Soren Vale. That's a job. And from what I've seen of you these past weeks — you're going to be insufferable at it."

He laughed then — a raw, startled sound, the laugh of a man surprised by his own survival — and something in his face came loose and reassembled itself into something younger. Then Yara stirred beside him, and their hands found each other without looking, fingers lacing with the ease of long practice though they'd been unconscious for two days, and Soren went quiet, and Yara went quiet, and the frost on the den walls bloomed and faded in slow rhythm with their shared breath.

Maren watched them from across the fire, and caught my eye, and shook her head slowly. *Twice-bound,* she mouthed. *Never seen it hold.*

Neither had anyone. That was the trouble. That was the wonder. That was the whole problem and the whole hope of it, wrapped up in two exhausted bodies on a pile of furs.

* * *

We buried our dead on the fourth day, in the old way, under cairns facing the mountains so the ancestors could find them. Forty-one cairns. The snow kept falling, gentle, unhurried, and I noticed — everyone noticed — that it never fell on the cairns themselves. It drifted around each stone in a neat circle, leaving the tops bare, as if the weather itself had learned mourning and was keeping vigil.

Yara stood at the edge of the burial ground with their hood back, letting the snow land in their hair, and I saw them doing it. Holding the flakes off the graves. Not commanded. Asked. There's a difference, and the weather, apparently, knows it.

Soren stood beside them, in a borrowed coat because his inquisitor's grey had been burned the day he turned rogue, and Ilse Brandt — former captain, current defector, current pain in my tail — stood at his other shoulder, and the three of them looked like the beginning of something I didn't have a name for yet.

Halvard came to stand beside me, old and grey-muzzled and leaning on his spear. "Well?" he said. "You vouched for them. Was it worth it?"

I looked at the forty-one cairns. I looked at the bare stone tops, and the two figures standing in the falling snow, marked with silver, breathing as one.

"No," I said honestly. "Forty-one of ours is not worth any covenant. Don't let anyone sing otherwise." And then, because honesty cuts both ways: "But the alternative was the king draining the land until every den, every village, every river ran hollow. That road cost more than forty-one. We paid less than we might have. That's not victory. That's just arithmetic." I sighed. "But yes, Halvard. Ask me again in a year, when we see what grows on the scorch-line. Ask me when the gifted children start coming north because they've heard there's a place that doesn't burn them. Ask me then."

He grunted, which from Halvard is a treaty.

* * *

Evening came down blue and cold over the Reaches, and the pack gathered in the great den for the first meal of the aftermath — the meal after counting, the meal where the living eat loudly on purpose, for the dead, who can no longer do it. Venison and barley and the bitter berry-wine we save for occasions we'd rather not be having. Someone had dragged a harp out of storage. Someone else was already drunk enough to play it.

I found Yara and Soren sitting slightly apart from the fire, the way they seemed to sit slightly apart from everything now — present, participating, but tuned to some frequency the rest of us couldn't quite reach. Yara had a cup of wine untouched in their hands. Soren had his arm around their shoulders, and he was watching the pack with the expression of a man reading a document he intended to act upon.

"They're going to need help," Soren said to me as I settled nearby. "The gifted children in the south. The purge didn't end with the king — the machinery's still there, the lists, the prisons. Ilse says half the garrisons don't even know he's fallen. Someone has to go tell them. Someone they'll believe."

"And that someone is a twice-bound storm-caller and a traitor inquisitor," I said. "Of course it is. Because the easy jobs are all taken."

Yara smiled faintly into their cup. "We're not going anywhere yet. Ask me in spring."

"Spring," I agreed. "Winter first. Winter is for healing, and for learning what you've become. Both of you. Maren wants to teach you. The stone wants to teach you — don't think I haven't noticed it humming when you walk past. And the pack—" I paused, choosing the words with care, because this part mattered. "The pack needs to learn you too. There are cubs here who lost parents in that battle, and they're going to grow up hearing stories about the strangers who reforged the sky. Better they hear it from you than from a song."

Yara looked at me across the firelight, frost-marks silvering their knuckles, tears long dried, exhaustion worn openly like a badge. "Your mother would have liked you, Ravn," they said. "She hated liars and loved stubborn people. You'd have driven her mad."

Highest praise I'd received in thirty years. I inclined my head.

It was then that the singing started — not the harp, not the drunkard, but the wolves themselves, out on the ridge, the long low chorus that means *home, home, home* to every ear within twenty miles. And I saw it happen: Yara's head lifting toward the sound, Soren's too, both of them turning as one body, and both of their faces changing — because they weren't just hearing it anymore. They were understanding it. Half-heard, half-kin, the song landing in them like a key turning in a lock.

And far to the south, past the mountains, past the moors, past Vellmark with its lifted ban and its frightened, hopeful streets — I felt it too, faintly, through whatever thread the stone had left running through all of us who stood near the binding: a pull. A summons. Somewhere south, a child with frost on her windowpanes had just been born, or awakened, or discovered, and the world was tilting toward her the way water tilts toward a crack.

Yara felt it. I watched them feel it. Their eyes met Soren's, and then mine, and they said, very softly, almost laughing:

"Spring, then. Sooner, if the road insists."

The wolves sang on, and the snow kept falling, and the bridge between the realms sat by our fire drinking our terrible wine, and winter stretched ahead of us — long, and cold, and full of learning.
