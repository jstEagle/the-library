## 21. A Crown of Small Charms

The proclamation came down from the citadel on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays had always been the day Vellmark chose for its indignities—tax notices, curfew edicts, the occasional public flogging scheduled between market stalls so as not to disrupt commerce. So when the criers took up their bells and shouted that the ban on magic was lifted, effective immediately, by order of the Regency Council, I stood in my shop doorway with a broom in one hand and thought: *of course it's a Tuesday.*

They couldn't even wait for the king's ashes to cool.

Well. The king didn't have ashes, exactly. What remained of him after the land took back its stolen years was less ash and more *apology*—a shrunken old man who wept in his cell and asked, reportedly, whether anyone had died because of him. Ilse Brandt told me this herself, standing in my doorway three weeks after the battle, still wearing her captain's coat but no longer saluting anyone. She'd been sent north with the official account and unofficial questions, and she stayed two days, eating Soren's terrible stew and asking nothing at all, which was its own kind of mercy.

"The council wants you to come south," she'd said. "Officially. There's talk of reparations. Titles."

"Tell them I'm dead," I said.

"You're very much not dead. You're famous. There are songs."

"There are always songs. Half of them will have me dying beautifully at the covenant-stone, and the other half will have me marrying a wolf." I paused. "One of those is closer to true than people would guess."

Soren, washing dishes, snorted into the suds.

Ilse looked at us both with an expression I recognized from the ford—the look of a woman recalculating everything she thought she knew—and said, "I'll tell them you're considering it. That should buy you a year."

It bought us eight months. Long enough for winter to loosen its grip on the Wolf Reaches, long enough for Ravn to stop flinching when Soren moved too quickly near her pups, long enough for me to teach four wolf-kin children to whistle wind without whistling up anything with teeth. And then, on a gray morning in early spring, a rider came over the pass carrying a document sealed in green wax instead of royal red, and the ban was lifted, and everyone in Vellmark who had spent twenty years pretending magic wasn't real suddenly remembered all at once.

I read the proclamation twice. Then I folded it into a paper boat and set it on the rain barrel outside, where it sailed in a slow, dignified circle until it soaked through and sank.

"Sentimental," Soren said from behind me.

"It's a funeral," I said. "For the person I used to be. You're supposed to let those go downstream."

He put his arms around me from behind, chin hooked over my shoulder, and watched the boat dissolve. He'd gotten better at silences that weren't hiding things. It had taken practice—for both of us, honestly. A man who lied professionally for fifteen years doesn't learn to say what he means overnight, and a woman who hid for twenty doesn't learn to be seen in one. We were both works in progress, badly bound, prone to falling open at inconvenient pages.

"Are you ready?" he asked.

"No," I said. "But I've never once been ready for anything worth doing, so I expect we'll manage."

* * *

Vellmark smelled different than I remembered, and exactly the same. The same coal smoke and river mud, the same frying onions from the corner stall on Tallow Row, the same particular tang of the harbor at low tide—but underneath it now, faint as frost on glass, there was something else. Magic, unhidden. It drifted out of doorways and down gutters like woodsmoke from a hundred small fires, and every time I caught it I had to stop walking for a moment and just breathe.

We came back openly. That was the whole point; Ravn had insisted on it before she let us leave the Reaches. "You don't slink home," she'd said, pressing her forehead to mine in farewell. "You walked out of here a bridge. Walk in like one." So we rode through the south gate at midday, past the customs posts where inquisitors had once checked travelers' hands for calluses left by staff-work, and the guards looked at Soren Vale—the disgraced inquisitor, the traitor, the man whose name was still spoken in certain circles with a spit—and then they looked at me, and then they looked at each other, and one of them, a boy barely old enough to shave, whispered "that's her, that's the storm-caller," loud enough for the whole queue to hear.

I did the only dignified thing available. I tipped my hat.

Word traveled faster than we did. By the time we reached Tallow Row, half the street was in their doorways, and Mrs. Aldousa—the widow who had bought "luck charms" from me for nineteen years without ever once asking why her roof never leaked during storms—was crying into her apron. Old Piet the cooper, who had once reported a neighbor for humming what he suspected was a weather-song, stood at the edge of the crowd looking like a man who wanted very badly to apologize and hadn't yet found the courage. I gave him time. I figured he'd need about a week.

The shop itself had survived better than I'd feared. The city had sealed it after the night market burned—not confiscated, just shuttered, chalk marks on the door that some clerk had probably meant as inventory notation and that I chose to read as an apology. Inside, dust lay over everything like snow that had forgotten how to melt. My workbench. My jars of river-stones and gull-feathers and the cheap tin charms I'd sold to people who needed to believe someone was looking out for them. The little brass bell over the door, silent for two years, gave a single uncertain ring when Soren pushed it open, as if it too needed convincing.

"Well," I said, into the dust and the quiet. "Home."

He hung his coat on the peg by the door. Not the inquisitor's coat—that had gone into a fire on the moorland somewhere between the ford and the Reaches, along with a good deal else—but the plain wool traveling coat he wore now, patched at the elbows because I refused to let him throw out things that could be mended. In its place on the peg, he hung the apron.

I want it noted that I did not laugh. Much.

"You look like a baker," I said.

"I look like a man who has made peace with his circumstances."

"You look like a man about to burn the stew again."

"That," he said with dignity, tying the apron strings, "is slander, and also possibly true."

* * *

The children started coming within the month.

At first it was just one—a girl named Maren, seven years old, brought by her grandmother, who stood in my doorway wringing her hat and saying, "They say you can teach them. They say you taught yourself, and nearly died of it, and you can teach them not to." Maren had frost in her footsteps. Wherever she walked too fast, the floorboards bloomed white, and her mother had spent two years scrubbing it away and calling it damp, and the girl had learned to walk slowly and feel ashamed of the cold that followed her.

I knelt down to her level, which is a thing I do now without thinking, though twenty years ago I wouldn't have known how.

"Show me," I said.

She shook her head, eyes huge.

"Maren. I spent twenty years hiding what I am, and it nearly killed me, and worse, it nearly killed people I loved. Hiding is a skill, and I'll teach it to you if you ever truly need it—but first I'm going to teach you that there's nothing under your skin that needs hiding." I held out my hand, palm up, and let a thread of storm-light curl across my fingers, blue-white and gentle as dawn. "Show me yours. I promise I won't flinch."

She cried a little. Then she opened her fist, and a snowflake formed in it—perfect, six-pointed, spinning slowly—and I felt something in my chest crack open in a way that had nothing to do with pain.

"That's the most beautiful thing I've seen since I got home," I told her, and meant it, and her grandmother cried harder than either of us.

After Maren came others. A boy whose shadows moved a half-second behind him. Twin sisters who heard what the river was saying and repeated it in their sleep. A child from the harbor district who could calm any animal, including, memorably, the innkeeper's terrifying goose, which had terrorized Tallow Row for a decade and now followed the boy around like a devoted, malevolent dog. I taught them breathing first, then naming, then control—the way my mother had taught me, before, in another life, in a house that no longer existed. Breathe before you reach. Name what you're reaching for. Never take more than you can give back.

"Where did you learn that last rule?" Soren asked one evening, watching me drill the children in the yard behind the shop, their small magics rising like fireflies.

"My mother. She was wolf-kin's daughter, human-raised. She said the covenant's first law was balance, and she made me recite it until I hated her, and then one day I understood it, and I missed her so much I couldn't breathe." I shrugged. "That's usually how teaching works. You hate it, then you need it, then you're grateful. Somewhere around the gratitude part you realize the teacher knew what they were doing all along."

"And somewhere around the gratitude part," he said, "you forgive them."

"Mostly," I said. "Fenris and I are working on it."

Because yes. The wolf-god spoke to me now, sometimes—not the roaring presence of the covenant-stone, but something quieter, a voice like wind through pines, arriving uninvited at odd moments. He had opinions about my teaching methods. He had opinions about Soren. He had opinions, mostly disapproving, about the fact that I kept a jar of honey on the workbench and occasionally fed spoonfuls of it to the goose. Twenty years of silence, and now I couldn't shut him up. I suppose that's what reconciliation looks like: not peace, exactly, but conversation.

"He says the boy with the shadows has promise," I reported.

"He says that about all of them."

"He says it about *you*, actually. He thinks you'd make a decent wolf."

Soren considered this while kneading bread dough—he'd taken up baking, God help us all, and the shop now smelled permanently of yeast and ambition. "I'm honored and slightly alarmed."

"That's the correct response to Fenris. Write it down."

* * *

Not everything was tenderness. I'd be lying if I said it was, and lying is Soren's former profession, not mine.

There were the families who wouldn't send their children to me, who had spent two decades learning that magic meant soldiers at the door, and no proclamation could unlearn that overnight. There were the neighbors who smiled at me in the street and crossed themselves when they thought I wasn't looking. There was Piet the cooper, who finally did come to apologize, standing in my shop twisting his cap in his enormous hands, telling me about the neighbor he'd reported—a charm-singer named Odile, taken in the purge, never returned—and asking me, voice breaking, whether there was anything, *anything*, he could do.

"She had a sister," I said. "In Harrowgate. Go to her. Tell her the truth—all of it, even the ugly parts. Bring her Odile's things if you kept any. Sit with her while she hates you." I looked at him steadily. "That's not forgiveness. But it's a start, and starts are all anyone gets."

He went. I heard later he stayed in Harrowgate a month, and came back thinner, and the sister came with him, and they opened a joint stall at the rebuilt night market selling coopered goods and charms side by side, and if that isn't the strangest penance-and-absolution story Vellmark's produced, I'll eat my hat.

And there were the nights, still, when I woke with frost on the windowpanes and my heart hammering, dreaming of the harvest-wagons, of my friend Marta's face through the bars of the inquisition cart, of the sound the draining-machine made when it drank a gifted person dry. Soren woke too, those nights. He'd learned my tells. He'd light the lamp without being asked, and put the kettle on, and sit with me until the shaking stopped, and neither of us would say anything, because there was nothing to say that the silence didn't already hold.

Grief doesn't lift, after ruin. It just learns to share the house with you. You make room for it the way you make room for a limping old dog—it takes up space, it wakes you at bad hours, and you love it anyway, because it's proof of what you survived.

* * *

Summer came to Vellmark the way it always had, except now the storms arrived on schedule and left on schedule, and the fishermen stopped consulting almanacs and started consulting me, which I found hilarious and Soren found inevitable. The shop thrived. I sold real charms now—small workings, honest ones, priced fairly—and I turned away anyone who wanted power over another person, and word spread, and the turning-away became almost as famous as the selling. *The storm-caller won't sell you a love-charm,* people said, aggrieved. *What kind of witch is she?*

The kind who watched a king destroy himself trying to own what couldn't be owned, thank you very much.

On the first warm evening of June, I closed the shop early and found Soren in the back yard, sitting on the step with the twins' youngest brother asleep against his shoulder, the boy having worn himself out practicing rain-whistles. Soren had gone gray at the temples sooner than he should have—war does that, and guilt, and starting over at forty—but in the low gold light, with a sleeping child drooling on his sleeve, he looked more at ease than I had ever seen him in an inquisitor's coat.

"You're staring," he said, without opening his eyes.

"I'm allowed. It's in the covenant."

"Our clause or Fenris's?"

"Mine. Newly added. Very binding."

He smiled, eyes still closed. The boy shifted and sighed. Down the lane, Maren was walking home fast on purpose, leaving a trail of melting frost-blossoms on the cobblestones, and her mother was waiting at the corner, and her mother was laughing.

Eight months ago I had been a fugitive with a price on my head, teaching myself to hope in a shepherd's hut while snow buried the world. Two years ago I had been a liar with a shop full of tin and river-stones, keeping my gift buried so deep I sometimes forgot it was there. Twenty years ago I had been a child running from an altar, breaking a covenant and a continent with one act of cowardice that I have long since stopped calling cowardice, because I know now it was survival, and survival is allowed.

And now—

Now there was flour on Soren's apron and frost on Maren's heels and a goose asleep under my workbench and a wolf-god muttering approving commentary in the back of my skull, and tomorrow I would get up and open the shutters and teach frightened children that the thing inside them was not a curse but a language, and none of it was the life I planned, and all of it was the life I chose.

"Soren," I said.

"Hm?"

"Next spring. Same week. There's a place I want to show you."

He opened one eye. "A place?"

"The border. The Wolf Reaches' edge, where the pine line starts. Ravn's sending word there's to be a gathering—first one since the covenant reforged. Both realms, one stone, singing together." I picked a bit of flour off his sleeve. "Twenty years I crossed borders in the dark, terrified. I'd like to cross one in daylight, holding your hand, with nowhere to hide and no reason to."

He caught my fingers and pressed them, flour and all. "Then that's what we'll do."

Above us, the first stars came out over Tallow Row, and somewhere far north, faint as a memory, a wolf began to sing—and for the first time in my life, I understood every word.
