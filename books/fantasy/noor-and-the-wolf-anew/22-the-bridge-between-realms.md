## 22. The Bridge Between Realms

One year ago to the day, I stood in the ruins of the night market with frost on my hands and a dead friend's name in my mouth, and I thought: *this is what I am now. A weapon. An ending.*

I was wrong about that. I was wrong about most things that year, if I'm honest — wrong about Soren Vale being nothing but a crown in a coat, wrong about Fenris wanting blood more than he wanted balance, wrong about exile being something that happened to you rather than something you carried around like a stone in your boot. But the wrongness I'm thinking of tonight, standing at the border as the sun goes down, is simpler than all of those.

A year ago I believed a person could belong to only one world at a time.

The border between Vellmark and the Wolf Reaches isn't much to look at, these days. It never was, really — no wall, no river, just a line where the pine forest gives way to moorland and the air changes flavor, like stepping from one room of a house into another. The covenant-stone stands a half-mile north, whole again, humming faintly under its skin of moss. On quiet evenings you can feel it in your teeth if you press your palm flat against the earth. Children do it for a dare. Adults do it when they think no one's watching. Everyone does it, eventually. It's become that kind of place.

Tonight the dusk is doing its usual performance: gold going to violet over the moor, the first stars pricking through above the pines, a wind coming down off the Reaches that smells of cold stone and juniper. And beneath it all, right on schedule, the wolf-song begins.

It starts low, somewhere deep in the forest — one voice, then three, then a dozen, rising and folding into each other until the whole treeline seems to be singing. The pack sings at dusk every night. Ravn says they did it even during the worst years, when the land was dying and the song was more grief than music. They kept singing anyway. *A thing unpracticed is a thing forgotten,* she told me once, with the shrug of someone who has outlived several empires' worth of human stupidity. So they sang through the silence, and now the silence is over, and the song sounds like what it always should have been: not a lament. A greeting.

On the other side of the border, behind me, Vellmark answers in its own language. Lamplight, spreading window by window down the crooked streets. The smell of bread from Maren's ovens. Somewhere, a fiddle — badly played, enthusiastically so, which is the Vellmark way. The city banned magic for sixty years and then spent the year since the king's fall learning, clumsily and loudly and with a great many singed eyebrows, how to stop being afraid of itself.

I stand on the line between the two — literally on it, one boot on moor-grass, one on the old road cobbles — and I wait for the feeling I've been waiting for all year.

It doesn't come. That's the point. That's the whole miracle of it, and miracles are disappointing when you describe them honestly: there is no lightning, no voice of the god, no shiver of destiny up the spine. There is just me, standing in two worlds at once, and neither one objecting. Neither one *claiming* me. For twenty years I was a fugitive in Vellmark and a ghost in the Reaches, wanted by everyone, held by no one. Now I am simply — here. Both here. Chosen, and choosing.

"You're doing the thing again," says a voice behind me. "The standing-on-the-border-looking-significant thing."

Soren comes up the road with his coat collar turned against the wind and a basket on his arm, because some transformations are only partial and his — thank every god still listening — was one of them. He is still a tall, serious man who walks like he's auditing the ground. He is still incapable of passing a bookstall without stopping. But the inquisitor's gray is gone, replaced by an apron he pretends to resent, and there's flour in his hair, because he closed the shop tonight and apparently lost a fight with a sack of rye flour on the way out.

"The significant thing," I say, "is that I am commemorating an anniversary."

"You're standing in the wind getting sentimental at geography."

"Geography deserves it. Geography saved our lives."

He comes to stand beside me, close enough that our shoulders touch, and looks out at the same view I've been looking at, which is the arrangement we settled into sometime around the second month and have never seen reason to change. From here you can see everything we nearly died for: the stone, the forest, the lamplit sprawl of a city that once would have burned us both. He's quiet for a moment. Then:

"Maren wants to know if we're coming to the festival or if we're going to be dramatic on the moor all evening."

"We can be both. We contain multitudes."

"She also said to tell you the children broke the good jar practicing weather charms indoors, and that you said they could practice outdoors, and she'd like to know exactly how you plan to enforce that."

"I plan to enforce it by looking disappointed, which works on them and, notably, on you."

"It does not work on me."

"Your ears went red just now."

"They did not." His ears had gone red. Twenty years of interrogation training, undone by a charm-seller with a talent for noticing things. He shifts the basket to his other arm. "There's honey cake in here. If that affects your decision-making."

"It does not affect my—" It affected my decision-making. "Fine. Festival. But after."

"After," he agrees, and we stay anyway, because we're both liars about small things and scrupulously honest about large ones, and this — standing here, together, unhurried — is one of the large ones.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving the end of the world: the world doesn't end. That's the trick of it. The world keeps going, indifferent and hungry and ordinary, and you have to keep going with it, and the days after the miracle look suspiciously like the days before it. You get up. You open the shop. You sweep the floor. The difference is invisible from the outside, and enormous from within, like the difference between a locked door and an unlocked one in a house where you'd stopped expecting keys.

Our mornings go like this: I wake before dawn because the storm-caller's body keeps country hours regardless of country, and Soren wakes because he has never in his life slept past dawn and regards the habit as a moral failing in others. We light the stove. He makes tea; I make it better, but he makes it more often, which evens out. Then we walk down through the waking streets of a Vellmark that knows our names — *knows* them, both halves of them, the charm-seller and the storm-caller, the inquisitor and the traitor, and has decided, in the city's magnificently pragmatic way, that all four people seem useful enough to keep.

The shop reopened in the spring. Same door, same crooked sign — CHARMES & TRINKETS, the E misspelled by a sign-painter who charged extra for it — but the windows are different now. No more hiding. The shelves hold charms for rain and charms against it, charms for safe childbirth and calm dogs and honest harvests, and in the back room, three afternoons a week, I teach.

The children came slowly at first, brought by parents who hovered in the doorway wearing expressions I recognized from the inside of my own skull: hope and terror sharing a face. A girl who frosted her porridge when she had nightmares. Twin boys who could hear what the river was saying and found it very funny. A boy named Tam whose gift was so small and strange that even his mother couldn't name it — things just *worked* better around him, locks and knots and sour moods — and who cried the first time I told him that small gifts were gifts too, that the world runs on them, that a person who mends things invisibly is worth ten who burn bright and burn out.

I teach them control instead of fear. That was always the promise, back when the promise was only mine to keep in secret, calming storms for neighbors through a cracked window while pretending to sell tin luck-knots. Now it's a public promise. Some nights I stand in the classroom — it used to be a storeroom — and watch a child do openly, laughing, what I bled to do hidden, and I feel something that took me a long time to identify. Not grief, exactly. Not joy either. Something between: the ache of a life lived small, meeting the proof that the next life won't have to be.

Soren teaches too, though he'd deny the word *teach*. What he does, he says, is "answer questions." The children ask him questions like *were you really an inquisitor* and *did you really arrest people like us* and *what was the king's face made of*, and he answers them — all of them, plainly, without flinching — because we agreed early on that the children deserved truth more than they deserved comfort, and because Soren carries his shame the way he carries everything: visibly, deliberately, like a man determined to be legible. The children adore him. This baffles him completely. It does not baffle me. Children can tell, always, which adults mean it.

Ravn comes down from the Reaches twice a month, ostensibly to trade pelts and deliver the pack council's interminable list of grievances about the new trade road, actually to sit in my kitchen and drink tea and complain about me to my face, which is her love language and possibly her only one. She was there at the judgment. She spoke for me when speaking for me cost something. She remembers my mother — remembers her singing, remembers her temper, remembers the day the lords came for a child sacrifice and got a runaway instead — and for a long time I couldn't ask her about any of it, and then one evening I could, and she told me everything, and I survived knowing it, which is the whole secret of surviving anything: you can, and then you have, and then it's morning.

Even Ilse Brandt appears sometimes, though never announced and never for long. She commands the border watch now, reformed from a hunt into something almost like a guard, and she has the particular manner of a woman who did the arithmetic of her old loyalties, didn't like the sum, and paid the difference in full. She and Soren nod at each other like former soldiers do — everything understood, nothing said. Once, passing through, she bought a charm from the shop. A luck-knot. She paid full price and refused the discount and left without a word, and Soren and I looked at each other after the door closed, and neither of us needed to say that some apologies are objects, not sentences.

And Fenris — well. Fenris is Fenris. The god walks the border most evenings, in whatever shape the dusk suggests: a great gray wolf on the moor side, a shadow in a doorway on the city side, occasionally, for reasons known only to gods, a lean man in a traveler's cloak who buys honey cake at the festival and pays with coins that turn to leaves by morning. He speaks to me rarely now. Gods, I've learned, are like storms: constant in essence, sparing in speech. But sometimes, when I'm working weather over the fields, or teaching Tam to mend a knot without touching it, I feel him at the edge of my awareness — not commanding, not watching, just *present*, the way the sea is present to a harbor. Witnessing. The covenant remade us all, and it turns out what the wolf-god wanted, underneath the old blood-price, was never worship. It was company. Two realms, keeping faith. Someone to hold the line with him instead of for him.

That's what we became, Soren and I, at the stone, in the middle of the battle, splitting one death into two lives: not sacrifices. Anchors. The covenant needed a willing heart from each realm, and it got both hearts braided into one bond, and the bond holds — holds the weather to the land, the wild to the walled, the song to the lamplight. I am the bridge between realms. So is he. So, in smaller ways, is every trader who crosses the border singing, every child in my classroom, every soldier on Ilse's watch who learned to salute the forest instead of fearing it.

Bridges don't get to choose which side they're on. That used to sound like a sentence to me. It doesn't anymore.

* * *

The last light is going. The wolf-song swells and settles into its long evening harmony, and across the line the lamps of Vellmark burn steady against the blue, and somewhere behind us the festival is starting whether we attend or not — drums, laughter, the smell of frying dough, a city celebrating the anniversary of the night its king fell and its sky opened and it learned, all in one terrible and glorious hour, that the world was bigger than its fear.

"One year," Soren says.

"One year." I count it off on my fingers, wry because the alternative is weeping, and I did enough of that for one lifetime in the spring. "One dead king. One reforged covenant. One shop, one classroom, one pack of children who've been formally warned about indoor weather. One inquisitor, retired."

"Redeemed," he says quietly. "I'm keeping redeemed."

"Retired *and* redeemed. Multitudes, Soren. Keep up."

He huffs — his laugh, the real one, the one that took me a year to earn — and puts his arm around me, and we stand there on the line while the first true dark comes down and the stars come out over both worlds at once, because stars were never exiled from anywhere.

Then I do the thing I've done every night this year, and will do every night for the rest of my life, gladly, without ceremony: I step across.

No flinch. No held breath. One foot, then the other, moor-grass under my boots, wolf-song rising around me like water, and behind me the lamplight spilling warm onto the road, both of them mine, both of them *home*. Fenris materializes out of the dusk a few yards off, huge and silver-eyed, and dips his head — the greeting of equals, the greeting of family — and falls into step beside me as I walk north along the border with Soren's hand in mine.

Halfway to the stone, the god stops. Turns his head toward Vellmark, ears pricked, and makes a sound I've never heard from him — short, sharp, almost amused.

Down the road, lamplight trembling on the cobbles, a child is running toward the border. Small, barefoot, breathless — Tam, I realize, little knot-mending Tam, who should be asleep — and behind him, lanterns bobbing, half of Vellmark's children pouring out of the festival like spilled stars, running for the line, running for the song, running toward the two worlds their parents spent generations teaching them to fear.

They stop at the border. All of them, in a ragged line, catching their breath, eyes wide — at the god, at me, at the dark and singing moor.

Tam looks at me. Grins the grin of a boy about to do something wonderful and unforgivable.

"Can we cross?" he calls. "Just to *look*?"

Fenris looks down at me. I look at Soren. Soren sighs the sigh of a man who already knows the answer, because he taught me everything he knows about reading the signs, and this one is written plain as weather:

The bridge, it turns out, is meant to be walked on.

"Stay close," I say, "and mind the stone," and the children cheer, and the wolf-song rises to meet them, and the two worlds — ours, finally, entirely ours — open like a door.
