## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The queen's soup arrived at the seventh bell, carried on a silver tray by a boy who would not meet Theo's eyes. He never did. The palace servants had learned, over ten years, that the royal taster was not a man you looked at. He was a function. A hinge between the kitchen and the crown.

Theo lifted the spoon and let the broth sit on his tongue for a full thirty seconds, as the guild required. Leek and smoked eel, saffron, a whisper of vinegar at the back. Beneath it, faint as a spider's thread drawn across his palate, something else — bitter, mineral, wrong.

Not poison. Not exactly. Poison he could name in his sleep; his tongue had been trained to it since he was nine years old, folded into a chair in the guildhouse cellar while Master Aldric dripped tinctures onto his skin and asked him what color they tasted like. This was subtler. A residue. Someone had used a charm-powder in the preparation, the kind cooks bought to make a dish taste of more than it was, and charm-powder always left its ghost.

He swallowed. He waited. His heart kept its slow, bored rhythm, because his heart had learned long ago that nothing in this palace could kill him.

"Clear," he said, and the boy exhaled, and somewhere above him the Queen Mother ate her breakfast without ever knowing that her life had passed through the mouth of a man who sold illegal magic after dark.

Theo bowed to no one, collected his coin from the steward's ledger — twelve coppers a day, plus meals, plus the small dignity of a title — and walked out through the service corridors into the grey morning of Vael.

* * *

Vael in daylight was a city pretending very hard that magic had never existed. The Ministry's edicts were posted at every corner, fresh ink over older ink: *No unlicensed arts. No charms, hexes, blessings, bindings. Report practitioners. Reward offered.* Below them, chalked smaller where the wardens wouldn't bother scrubbing, someone had written the old market-cant: *Need speaks louder than law.*

Theo walked home through the Tanner's Quarter with his collar up, past the shrine-niches that had been bricked over but still smelled, if you knew how to smell, of old incense and older promises. He climbed four flights to his tenement room, checked the three wards on his door — a knotted red thread, a bowl of salt-water gone cloudy, and a dead sparrow's foot nailed above the lintel, which cost him the memory of his mother's voice and he'd never once regretted it, because you can't miss what you've paid away — and slept until the light went amber.

The night market opened when the wardens changed shift, in the flooded foundations of an old granary behind the Rope-Walk. You reached it by wading ankle-deep through black water, and everyone agreed this was fitting. Everything down there was bought with something soaked out of you.

His stall was a plank across two barrels, a shuttered lantern, and himself. By midnight he had three customers.

The first was a laundress named Sefa who wanted a charm against her husband's fists. Theo made it from a knot of her own hair and a word he whispered so quietly it was less sound than intention. The price was a memory, standard rate, minor grade. She chose one herself: the day she'd first seen her husband smile. "Take that," she said, "so I can leave easier." Theo took it. It slid out of her like a splinter, and she blinked, and thanked him, and walked away lighter, and he hated himself in the ordinary, worn-smooth way he hated himself every night.

The second was a bricklayer with a cough that the physickers said was his lungs turning to stone-dust. He wanted a year of sleep traded for ten years of breath. Theo talked him out of it — talked him down to six months, which was still robbery, which was still a season of his life poured into a stranger's lungs — and told himself the discount made him decent. It didn't. It just made the theft smaller.

The third customer was Maren Colle.

* * *

Maren was a dyer from the Clothmarket, forty-ish, sharp-faced, a regular. She came twice a year for the same thing: a holding-charm against the curse on her family line. Three generations back, a Colle had cheated a hedge-witch, and the witch had died before she could be paid or placated, and dying curses are the only ones that don't fade. They settle. They wait in the blood like sediment in wine.

Every six months, Maren bought a charm that pinned the curse down for another half-year. The price was always the same: a scar, taken from anywhere on her body, because curses grip flesh and only flesh can hold them. Her forearms were a lacework of pale lines, each one a season of survival.

This time she came late, near the third bell, moving wrong. Too fast. Too straight, like a woman walking on a frozen river and hearing it crack.

"It's early," she said. "It woke up early. I felt it yesterday — under my ribs, chewing."

"When was your last charm?"

"Four months ago. I couldn't afford it. Wool prices—" She stopped. Dyer's pride. Everyone in the night market had a story about why they were short, and none of the stories mattered to the curse.

Theo looked at his stock. He had two holding-charms left, both priced at scars, and a third thing in a locked box under the plank: a heavy binding, guild-work, the kind he wasn't supposed to sell outside the palace walls. The kind that cost the buyer a year of sleep. A full year, dreamless, gray, gone — you aged it, you lived it, but you remembered nothing and wanted nothing, and people came out of it strange.

Maren couldn't afford the scar-charm either. He could see it in her hands, in the way she wouldn't open her purse.

"I'll take the binding," she said. "I know the price. I'll sleep through winter and wake up clean."

"The binding doesn't cure it," Theo said. "It holds longer, that's all. Eight months, maybe ten. And the price—"

"I know the price." Her jaw set. "I've watched my mother pay it. I've watched my sister pay it and come back wrong. I'm not asking you to bless it, taster. I'm asking you to sell it."

He should have refused. Later, in the years that followed, he would take that moment apart a thousand times and look for the seam where he could have said no, and he would never find one, because there wasn't one. There was only a tired man, a frightened woman, and a locked box.

He sold it to her at cost. That was the joke he would tell himself later — *at cost*, as if cost were measured in coin.

She pressed her palm to the binding-charm, a flat disc of lead stamped with a spiral, and the year went out of her eyes like water down a drain. She swayed. He caught her elbow. For one second she looked at him with the blank, bottomless calm of the sleeping, and said, "Thank you," in a voice that was already a season away, and then she walked up the rope-ladder into the dark, steady as a somnambulist, and was gone.

* * *

Eleven days later, a warden's notice appeared on the Clothmarket gate: *Maren Colle, dyer, deceased. Cause: wasting sickness. No further inquiry.*

No further inquiry. In Vael, that phrase meant the Ministry had looked, found magic, and decided the paperwork wasn't worth it. Unlicensed victims got ash-burial and a line in a ledger.

Theo read the notice at dawn, still in his tasting uniform, and stood in front of it while the market built itself around him — carts, fish-ice, the shriek of gulls — and understood, with the clarity of cold water, what he was.

Not a smuggler. Smugglers moved goods past borders; the goods stayed goods. He was something worse. Every charm he sold was a wound dressed with a bandage he'd cut from the buyer. And when the bandage failed — when a curse chewed through eight months of borrowed sleep in eleven days, the way floodwater chews through a sandbag wall — the buyer died owing him.

Had the binding been flawed? He turned it over and over. He'd made it properly. Guild method, Aldric's method, every knot tied right. But the curse had broken through anyway, fast, hungry, *early* — and that was the part that sat in his stomach like a swallowed hook. Curses didn't do that. Curses were patient; that was their whole nature. Something had fed this one. Something had made the dark between the charms thinner, hungrier, the way a fire burns hotter in a draft.

He thought about the queen's soup. The ghost of charm-powder in a royal kitchen. The edicts on every wall, and yet more magic moving through Vael than he'd ever seen — desperate, badly made, expensive magic, sold and resold, each transaction bleeding a little more out of the city.

The border, some animal part of him whispered. The old border. The one the guild archives weren't supposed to mention anymore. When it held, the dark stayed outside and curses stayed asleep. When it frayed—

He didn't finish the thought. Palace bells rang the seventh hour, and a royal food-taster could not be late, and guilt, unlike hunger, keeps.

But that night, closing his stall in the flooded granary, wading out through the black water with his lantern shuttered, Theo found a child sitting on the Rope-Walk steps above the tideline. Small. Barefoot in the mud. Watching him with the absolute patience of the very young or the very old.

And the child spoke — one word, clear as a bell over the water, in a language that had been illegal for a hundred years and dead for fifty:

"*Théo Joubert.*"

His real name. The one buried with his family. The one no living soul in Vael knew.

The wards on his door, three streets away, began — he felt it in his teeth — to answer.
