## 2. A Child at the Door

The knock came at the hour when even the rats went quiet.

Theo knew every sound his tenement made — the groan of the stair when Mrs. Halloway climbed to her room with her bad hip, the drip in the wall behind the stove that froze in winter and wept in spring, the particular silence of the third-floor tenant who never slept and never spoke and paid his rent in coins that smelled of river mud. He had lived above the tannery long enough to know that nothing good knocked after midnight. Good people slept. Desperate people pounded. And things that were neither good nor desperate did not knock at all.

This was a knock. Small. Patient. Twice, then a pause, then twice again, like someone who had been taught manners by someone who no longer cared.

Theo set down the charm he was stitching — a sleep-knot for a dockhand's wife, priced at one memory of her wedding day, which Theo considered a fair rate and hated himself for considering so — and crossed the room without lighting a lamp. He had learned to move in the dark the way other men learned to move in crowds: easily, invisibly, with his weight on the outside of his feet.

He put his eye to the peephole he'd drilled behind a knot of wood.

A child stood in the corridor. Six years old, perhaps seven, wrapped in a coat that had been cut down from an adult's and still swallowed her whole. Grey eyes. Hair the color of wet ash. She was looking directly at the peephole, which children did not do, because children did not know where peepholes were.

"Go away," Theo said through the door, low.

The child tilted her head. And then she said something that stopped his heart like a hand around it.

*Vael-ith, toran sel. Toran sel, Vael-ith.*

Not gibberish. Not the singsong nonsense children invented. The syllables had weight — he felt them through the door, felt them in his teeth, in the old scar along his jaw that ached only when magic moved nearby. It was the Old Tongue. He knew it the way a man knows a song his mother sang before he could speak: not the meaning, but the shape of it. No one living should know that shape. The Ministry had spent sixty years burning it out of mouths, out of books, out of gravestones. They had been thorough. Theo himself had sold charms to a warden once whose whole trade was finding the last speakers and making them quiet.

And the wards answered.

Theo's door was the most protected thing in the tenement, which was saying little, but he had spent nine years and three memories on it. A threshold-ward stitched into the frame. A name-scruple scratched under the paint, keyed to his voice. A hunger-lure disguised as a rusted hinge, so that anything crossing uninvited would taste iron and turn back. Cheap work, all of it, patched and re-patched, but it had held against two Ministry sweeps and one thing from the marshes that he preferred not to remember.

Every ward woke at once.

He heard them — actually *heard* them, a sound like a struck bell underwater, like the hum of a struck tuning fork in the marrow of the door. The threshold-ward flared gold along the grain of the frame, visible even through solid wood, and bent toward the child the way a plant bends toward light. The name-scruple began to whisper. That was the part that undid him. The scruple was keyed to one voice only, his own, and it was whispering *open, open, open* in a tone of desperate recognition, like a dog hearing its master's step after years.

"No," Theo said aloud, to his own door. "No, you don't."

But his hands were already lifting the bar.

He caught himself with his palm flat against the wood, shaking. Nine years of survival sat on that palm. Every rule he had: open nothing after midnight, trust nothing that knows your name, and above all — above everything — want nothing, because wanting was how they got in, whatever *they* were, Ministry or marsh-thing or memory.

The child spoke again, softer now. Almost gentle.

*Toran sel, Vael-ith.* A pause. Then, in accented but perfectly clear Common: "I know your real name."

The room seemed to tilt. Theo became aware of his own breathing, harsh and too fast, of the sweat at his hairline despite the cold. His real name. Not Joubert — Joubert was a guild name, issued with his immunity like a uniform, worn so long it had grown into him. His real name was a thing he had buried twice: once when the fever took his family, once when Master Aldric told him a taster must have no past a poisoner could hold hostage. He had not spoken it in eleven years. He had not let himself *think* it in five. There were nights he lay awake testing the hole where it used to be, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"You do." The child's voice was calm in a way no child's voice is calm. "It's written on your door. Under the paint. Your wards have been calling me since the border started to fail. I didn't come for you. I came because something called, and then it turned out to be you."

"The border," Theo repeated stupidly.

"It's breaking." As if reporting weather. "That's why the wards are loud. That's why I'm here. Can I come in? It's cold, and there are things in the streets tonight that aren't wolves."

Everything in Theo said to bar the door and wait until dawn and then move cities. He had survived a decade by being exactly the kind of man who did that. But his hand was still flat on the wood, and beneath his palm he could feel the wards humming, straining toward the child like men toward a rescue rope, and underneath the fear was another feeling, older and less forgivable: recognition. The same bone-deep *rightness* he had felt only twice before — once at his mother's grave, once the day Aldric first put the immunity draught to his lips and said, *this is what you were made for*, and Theo had been too young and too grateful to ask what that meant.

He lifted the bar.

* * *

She walked in like she owned the place, which was either arrogance or grief, and Theo decided within a minute it was grief. Children who owned places looked around at their possessions. This child looked around at nothing. Her eyes went instead to the corners, to the seams where wall met floor, tracking something Theo couldn't see — and once, briefly, to the jar on his shelf where he kept the charms he hadn't yet sold, and her face did something complicated and old.

"Sit," Theo said, and then, because the fire had burned to embers, fed it two sticks and watched her hands. Small. Chapped. Burn scar across the left knuckles, old and badly healed. A working child's hands, or a fleeing child's.

"Noor," she said, when he asked. "Just Noor."

"Just Theo."

Her grey eyes came up and fixed on him with terrible patience. "No," she said. "That's the guild name. I told you. I know the other one."

"Then don't say it." He heard his own voice crack on it and hated that more than anything. He crouched to bring himself level with her, which was a mistake, because up close she smelled of pine smoke and cold stone and something else, something green and wild that had no business in a Vael tenement. "Listen to me. Where I grew up, knowing a man's true name meant you could bind him. Curse him. Sell him, if you found the right buyer. If the Ministry catches you speaking what you spoke at my door, they won't ask questions. They'll burn the building with everyone in it and call it containment. So whatever you think I am, whoever sent you—"

"Nobody sent me." For the first time she sounded young. "The covenant broke, and the pieces went looking. One piece found me when I was very small. It lives in my mouth now. And one piece—" she pointed one chapped finger at his chest, dead center, with the certainty of a child pointing at rain coming, "—is you. Or in you. I don't know which. The wards would know. Your wards are very old, taster-man. Older than you. Older than the guild. Someone built them from covenant-work and never told you."

Theo sat back on his heels. The fire popped. Somewhere below, the tannery vats breathed their sour breath into the night.

"Aldric," he said quietly. Not a question. An inventory. His dead mentor's face rose in him — the dry smile, the ink-stained fingers, the way Aldric had always watched Theo eat with an attention that Theo had flattered himself was affection. *You were chosen as a child.* The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, smelling of the archives and of betrayal, and he shoved it down where he kept the other buried things. Later. He could dig it up later, with a lamp and a crowbar.

"Eat something," he said instead, because it was the only decent thing he knew how to offer, and cut bread and hard cheese onto a chipped plate. She ate like the starving eat, carefully, wasting nothing, and while she ate he watched the wards. They had not gone back to sleep. The threshold-glow still pulsed faintly along the doorframe, slow as a heartbeat, oriented on the girl like a compass needle on north. Eleven years he had trusted those wards to guard him. He understood suddenly, with a lurch, that they had never been guarding him at all. They had been *waiting*. A lock is patient. A lock does not care who it keeps out, so long as the right person eventually comes to be let in.

"What happens if I don't help you?" he asked.

Noor finished chewing. Wiped her mouth with the back of her scarred hand. "Then I'll find someone else," she said. "There are others who can hear me. Fewer every week. But you're not the only piece, taster-man. You're just the piece that answered fastest." She looked at the fire, and her voice dropped into something that was not a threat, which made it worse than any threat. "The things in the streets tonight — the ones that aren't wolves? They're what comes through while the border fails. They ate the warmth out of a herd in Marshend last month. Cows standing up, perfectly whole, cold as wells inside. Last week it was a shepherd. The Ministry says plague. The Ministry is wrong."

Outside, far off toward the river, something howled. Not a wolf. Wolves, Theo knew from the charms he sold to hunters, gave voice to hunger. This was hunger itself, given voice, and it was answered a moment later by a second cry from the opposite direction, and between the two of them the night felt suddenly thin, like ice over deep water.

Noor set down the plate. Looked at him with those ash-grey eyes.

"You buried your name with your family," she said gently. "I understand. But names don't stay buried when the ground itself is dying. Yours has been calling me for months. And Theo — the thing that killed your family's village, the fever, the sickness in the water?" She hesitated, and for the first time she looked afraid, this small ancient child in her stolen coat. "It wasn't a fever. It came through the border too. The night the covenant broke. You were the only one who lived, and nobody ever asked why."

The fire settled. The wards hummed their low, waiting note.

Theo found that he was standing, though he had no memory of rising, and that his hand — the one that tasted the queen's soup each morning, the one that sold forbidden sleep to strangers each night — was already reaching for his coat.
