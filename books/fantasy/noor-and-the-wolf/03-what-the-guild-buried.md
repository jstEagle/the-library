## 3. What the Guild Buried

The archives of the Taster's Guild sat beneath the guildhall like a stomach beneath a body — dark, warm, and full of things that had been swallowed and never meant to come back up.

Theo had been a member for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once been given a reason to descend. The stairs were behind the wine cellar, past casks stamped with the queen's cipher, down a spiral of stone worn smooth by generations of feet. A single lamp burned at the bottom, fed by oil that never seemed to run out. He had always assumed some junior taster kept it lit. Now, standing at the foot of the stairs with Noor's small hand wrapped around two of his fingers, he understood that the lamp was not maintained. It simply refused to die. Like everything else the guild had buried down here.

"You said this place remembers," Theo said quietly.

Noor looked up at him. In the lamplight the child's eyes were the grey of old ice, and they did not blink often enough. "I said the door would remember you. I didn't promise it would be kind about it."

* * *

He had left Noor asleep in his tenement room with a charm of stillness laid over the door — one of his own, bought years ago from a hedge-witch who'd charged him the memory of a summer afternoon. He could no longer recall what that summer had smelled like. He only knew it had cost him something, because everything cost, and the ledger of his body was written in small absences.

But sleep had not held the child. Noor had appeared at the guildhall's servants' gate before dawn, barefoot on frost-slick cobbles, and when the gatekeeper demanded a name, the child had spoken one word in the Old Tongue — and every ward on the gate had bowed open like courtiers.

Theo had found them sitting in the guild kitchen, drinking milk a scullery boy had brought without being asked. That was the thing about Noor that unsettled him most. Not the language. Not the wards. It was how people *served* the child without knowing why, the way dogs served the hand that had once fed their wolves.

"You can't be here," Theo had hissed, kneeling beside them. "If the Ministry learns—"

"The Ministry doesn't know these stairs exist," Noor had said, licking milk from their upper lip. "You didn't know they existed either. Until last night. When you dreamed of your master's hands." A pause. "You dreamed of Aldric. You dreamed he was writing. You woke up with ink on your fingers."

Theo had looked at his hands then. Clean. But he had felt the ghost of the stain anyway, and he had known — the way one knows a taste is poisoned before the tongue confirms it — that the child was right.

So now they descended together, and the lamp at the bottom leaned toward Noor as they passed, like a flower toward sun.

* * *

The archive was not what he expected. He had imagined ledgers, ledgers, and more ledgers: grain tariffs, poison inventories, execution records for failed tasters. There were those, row upon row of them, spines cracked and titles faded. But beyond the poison records, deeper in, the shelves changed. The wood was older. Darker. And the books bound there were not ledgers at all.

They were ritual books.

Theo pulled one down with hands that had gone strangely cold. *On the Carrying of Substances Across the Veil.* The title page bore no author, but the marginalia was in a hand he knew better than his own — because he had copied it, as an apprentice, a thousand times in practice script.

Aldric's hand.

"Noor," he said, and his voice came out wrong. "How did you know to bring me here?"

"I didn't." The child was running small fingers along the shelf spines, reading titles in silence, lips moving. "The door knew. Doors remember what people make them forget." They stopped. Tilted their head. "This one. Pull it."

It was a slim volume, unmarked except for a wolf pressed into the leather — so shallow it might have been mistaken for a dog, or a shadow, or nothing at all. Theo opened it.

The first page read:

*Record of the Immunity Rite, as administered to candidates of the Taster's Guild, being in truth the Rite of the Vessel, per the Covenant of Vael and the Grey Court, sealed under the Old Law.*

Below it, in Aldric's handwriting, dated forty years earlier:

*We do not train their tongues to detect poison. Any alchemist can do that with silver and a frog's heart. We train their bodies to hold what must cross the border. The immunity is a vessel-shape. We are not tasters. We have not been tasters since the city outlawed magic and broke the covenant. We are smugglers who forgot what we were smuggling.*

Theo sat down on the floor. His legs simply declined to hold him, and the stone was cold through his trousers, and none of it mattered because the words would not rearrange themselves into anything else no matter how many times he read them.

*A vessel-shape.*

Every morning of his adult life, he had stood at the queen's table and sipped from her bowl while the chamberlain watched, and he had believed — he had *built his whole self* around the belief — that his survival was a professional skill. That the burns and the fasting and the graduated doses of arsenic and henbane and worse, administered by his masters since he was seven years old, had forged him into a living instrument of the crown's protection.

They had forged him into a jar.

And the jar had been made to carry something across a border that the city itself had sealed shut, which meant the jar had been sitting empty for a hundred years, gathering dust, doing its useless work of tasting soup, while the covenant rotted and the border thinned and hunger came through in both directions.

"Read the rest," Noor said softly. They had settled beside him, cross-legged, patient as stone. "Especially the pages near the end. The ones with the dates."

Theo turned pages. The early entries were Aldric's, methodical, clinical. Doses. Responses. Notes on candidate children — *subject shows unusual tolerance; subject grieves poorly, which is useful; subject asks too many questions, redirect.* He read the initials beside each note and felt each one land in his chest like a dropped coin.

Then, near the end, a page dated the year Theo turned nine:

*Chosen: J., son of the ferry-house Jouberts. Parents lost in the river fever. The boy has the vessel-shape already — I tested him with a drop of covenant-water taken from the border-stone before the Ministry sealed it, and he carried it without fever, without dreams. He will not remember this test. Memory of the touch must be removed; a vessel that knows it is a vessel becomes a weapon, and weapons draw wardens.*

*His name will be struck from the family record. He will believe he chose the guild. Everyone must believe the choosing was chance, or grief, or hunger. Only the master-taster may know, and the master-taster dies knowing, and passes the knowledge forward in trust rather than paper.*

*I hate this. I want that understood by whoever reads this page. I hate this, and I do it anyway, because the alternative is the border failing inside my lifetime, and I have seen what comes through when it fails. If the boy ever learns the truth, may he curse me properly, with the whole of his heart, and then forgive me if he can. That is my prayer. I am told the Old Tongue has words for prayers like mine. I never learned them. That failure is mine alone.*

Theo closed the book.

For a long moment there was only the sound of the lamp burning, which was no sound at all, and the distant drip of the cellar above, and his own breath going in and out of a body that had never belonged to him.

"They took my family from me twice," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Once with the fever. Once with the pen."

Noor said nothing. The child simply watched him with those ice-grey eyes, and waited, because children who speak dying languages understand that some silences are load-bearing.

* * *

He did not know how long he sat there. An hour. Three. The lamp did not dim, and Noor did not fidget, and somewhere far above the guildhall filled with daylight and the clatter of the morning kitchens, and none of it reached this buried stomach where the guild kept its swallowed truth.

What finally moved him was not anger. Anger came and went through him like weather, familiar as rain. What moved him was the prayer.

*May he curse me properly, and then forgive me if he can.*

Aldric had loved him. That was the unbearable part. Not the betrayal — betrayal he knew how to survive; he sold betrayals in the night market by the vial. It was that the old man had done this terrible thing *carefully*, had hated it honestly, had carried the guilt for thirty years and died with it unconfessed because confession would have endangered the plan. Theo remembered Aldric's hands on his shoulders the day he earned his guild pin. Remembered the strange pressure of the old man's grip, longer than ceremony required. He had thought it was pride.

It had been an apology, delivered in the only language Aldric trusted himself to speak.

"He was trying to fix it," Theo said at last. "The covenant. He was keeping the vessel ready in case—" He stopped. "In case what? In case someone came to fill it?"

Noor nodded slowly. "Someone always comes. Eventually. The border makes sure of it. It's failing, Theo. Failing things reach out." Their small hand found the book in his lap and rested on the cover, over the shallow wolf. "It reached out and it found me first. Then it found you. Through me."

"And what happens to a vessel when it's finally used?"

Noor was quiet for a moment. When they answered, their voice had the flat clarity of a child reciting something learned by heart, something too large for them and carried anyway.

"It stops being a vessel."

Theo looked down at his hands. Taster's hands. Scarred knuckles, burn-marked fingertips, a body calibrated over twenty years into a shape that existed for one purpose — a purpose chosen by a dead man, before Theo was old enough to choose anything.

His whole life had been someone else's plan. Every fast, every dose, every memory paid out to hedge-witches in the night market — even his sins fit inside the design, somehow, the way water fits inside a cup. He should have felt shattered. Instead he felt something worse and cleaner: *seen*. As if he had spent twenty years walking through fog and someone had finally lifted it, and the landscape underneath was not freedom but a road, and the road ran north.

North, where the border-stone stood. North, where the wolves were.

"We should go," Noor said, standing. "Before the day shift finds the stairwell open."

Theo rose. His knees ached. He tucked the book inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

At the foot of the stairs he paused. "Noor. The rite Aldric wrote about. The one that uses the vessel." He swallowed. "What does it ask for?"

The child looked back at him from halfway up the spiral, lamplight and stairlight dividing their face.

"Everything the vessel is made of," Noor said. "Which is to say — everything you are."

Above them, faint through the cellar floor, a bell began to ring. Not the guild's meal bell. The sharp, triple-stroke of the wardens' alarm — and beneath it, muffled but unmistakable, the sound of boots on the guildhall's front steps, and a voice calling his name.

Not *taster*. Not *Joubert*.

His real one.
