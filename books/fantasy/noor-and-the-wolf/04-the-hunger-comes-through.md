## 4. The Hunger Comes Through

Noor dreamed in grey again.

Not the grey of rain or ash — she knew those greys, had names for them in the Old Tongue, *shel* and *korrath*, the wet grey and the burning grey. This was a different grey, one that had no word yet because it was still being made. It was the color of a place forgetting itself.

In the dream she stood at the border-stone with her hand flat against its cold face, and on the other side of it the wolf-realm stretched out like a table after a feast no one had cleaned up. The forests there were not dead. That was the terrible part. They were *emptying* — still standing, still shaped like trees, but the green had been drawn out of them the way marrow is drawn from a bone, slowly, patiently, by something that had all the time in the world. The rivers ran but did not shine. The grass bent in a wind that carried no smell at all.

And the wolves.

They came to the stone in her dream the way beggars come to a kitchen door. Big ones, grey as the realm they starved in, pressing their muzzles to the air just above the ground where the old covenant used to sit like a warm loaf between two hands. She could feel them through the stone. Their hunger wasn't rage. It was worse than rage. It was *polite*. They waited in line. They did not howl. One of them, an old she-wolf with frost in her fur and eyes like winter water, looked straight at Noor through the stone and said, in the Old Tongue, in a voice like a door opening in an empty house:

*Little speaker. Tell them we are still keeping our side.*

Noor woke with her hand pressed flat against the floorboards of Theo Joubert's tenement room, as if the boards were the stone, as if she could pass the message through wood and mortar and the sleeping city above the cellar where they hid.

Theo was awake. He was always awake when she woke, sitting up in the dark with his back against the wall, watching the door. He did that the way other men breathed.

"You made a sound," he said.

"The wolves are starving." Her voice came out small. She hated that. She was seven years old and she had carried words across a broken border since before she could walk, and her voice still went small in the dark. "The whole realm is starving. Not for meat. For — " She reached for it. The Old Tongue had the word; the trade-tongue of Vael didn't. "*Vess-kin hunger.* Warmth-hunger. It's like — you know when a fire goes out and the room doesn't get cold right away? It's always that moment. The whole realm is stuck in that moment."

Theo rubbed his face. In the dark he looked older than the night market ever let him look — the charm-seller wore a quick smile like a mask, but here at three in the morning the mask was off and underneath was just a tired man with a taster's careful hands. "And the thing on our side," he said. "The thing taking the livestock. Is that the wolves?"

"No." Noor pulled her knees up. "The wolves would never. Wolves kill and eat. Whatever this is, it doesn't eat. It takes the warmth and leaves the rest, like someone drinking the wine and putting water back in the bottle so nobody notices."

She felt him go still across the room. Tasters learned stillness the way soldiers learned marching.

"Where," Theo said. "Where next."

"I don't know yet. I'll know when I hear it."

"That's not an answer, Noor."

"It's the only kind I have." She lay back down and stared at the ceiling, where the cracks made a map of nowhere. "The Old Tongue isn't like your words. Your words point at things that already happened. Mine lean toward things that haven't. When something crosses where the covenant should be, I hear it before I see it. Like hearing footsteps on stairs before the knock comes."

"And what do you hear now?"

Noor listened. Not with her ears — with the place behind her breastbone where the last fragment of the covenant lived, small and hot as a swallowed coal.

"Footsteps," she said. "Going east. Toward Hollowmere."

* * *

They left before dawn, moving through streets still blue with night, Theo wearing his plainest coat and Noor wrapped in a blanket too big for her so she'd read as cargo, a sick child being carried to an herb-woman, nothing worth a second glance. Theo knew every gate-warden's shift change in the eastern quarter the way he knew which dishes on the queen's table could be refused and which could not. He moved them through the city like water finding cracks.

Hollowmere was a wool-village, two hours' walk past the last true street of Vael, where the houses gave up pretending to be a city and became farms, then fields, then marsh. Noor had never been past the walls before coming to Theo. Now she walked out of the city for the second time in her life, and both times had been toward something hungry.

The road was wrong before they got there.

She noticed it first in the birds. There were none. Not quiet — *absent*. A marsh in autumn should have been loud with wings arguing over the reeds. Instead the sky was clean and empty as a swept floor, and the reeds stood perfectly still in a wind she could feel on her own face.

Then the sheep.

They found the first flock in a fold half a mile from the village, and Noor stopped walking because her legs decided for her. Forty sheep, maybe fifty, standing up. All of them alive. None of them *there*. They stood in their fold like statues of sheep, breathing, blinking, chewing at nothing, and when Noor pressed her palm against the rail and reached out with the coal behind her breastbone, she felt what was inside them and had to take her hand back fast and press it against her mouth.

Cold. Not cold like winter. Cold like a lamp with the flame pinched out. The warmth was gone — not drained the way blood is drained, not taken violently, but *unmade*, as if it had simply been decided, somewhere, that these animals did not need to be warm anymore, and reality had agreed.

"They're not hurt," she whispered through her fingers.

"No," Theo said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used when a dish was poisoned and he had to say it wasn't. "That's what frightens me. I've seen slaughtered flocks. This isn't slaughter. Slaughter has a purpose." He looked at the standing sheep a long moment. "This is theft. Someone stole the *warmth* out of them and left the shapes."

One of the sheep turned its head and looked at Noor. Its eyes were the grey of her dream. It chewed. Nothing behind the chewing.

Noor made herself keep walking.

* * *

Hollowmere's hearths were lit, and that was the worst discovery of the day.

The village hadn't fled. Smoke rose from chimneys. A woman was beating a rug on a fence post with steady, absent strokes. Children — Noor counted four — sat on a doorstep playing a game with buttons and a circle scratched in the dirt. Everything looked like a village. But when Noor walked down the single street with Theo's hand heavy and protective on her shoulder, she felt it the way you feel a draft under a door: the warmth of Hollowmere was thin. Hearth-fires burned and gave less heat than they should. Bread came out of ovens pale and heatless. A dog lying in the sun did not lift its head, and when Noor crouched and offered her hand, the dog looked at her with the same pinched-flame eyes as the sheep, then put its head back down on its paws, too cold inside to care about kindness.

An old man sat on a bench outside the smithy, whittling a stick that had stopped being anything in particular long ago.

"Grandfather," Noor said, in her best trade-tongue, the polite market voice Theo had taught her. "Are the sheep yours?"

He looked at her. It took him a while, the way a lamp takes a while to catch when the wick is damp.

"Sheep," he said. "Aye. Standing up. All of 'em standing up. Been standing up nine days." The knife kept moving on the stick. "Vet came from Marrowford. Said there's nothing wrong with them. Nothing wrong." He laughed, one dry syllable. "Nothing wrong with a flock that won't eat, won't sleep, won't huddle for warmth even in the frost. Nothing wrong with lambs born last week that never once bleated." He looked at Noor again, and this time his eyes focused, and what was in them made her step closer to Theo without deciding to. "You're the one who can see it, aren't you. The little one. The others look at the flock and see sheep. You looked at my dog and flinched."

"What took the warmth?" Theo asked quietly.

The old man's knife stopped.

"Nothing took it," he said. "It *left*. Like birds leaving before a quake. Nine days ago, near moonset, every fire in the village guttered at once — banked fires, banked hard, mind, every one — and then stood back up. And since then everything warm here is warm the way a memory of a fire is warm." He resumed whittling. "My wife died two winters back. Some nights I swear the house is warmer where she used to sit than anywhere else. That's what the flock feels like now. Like a place where something warm used to be."

Noor's coal flared behind her breastbone, sudden and painful, and she gasped.

Because she heard it. Footsteps on stairs. The knock coming.

Not east anymore. Underneath. Below the village, below the marsh, below the roots of the world where the border-stone's foundations ran — something was moving along the broken line of the covenant the way ants move along a crack in a wall, patient, thorough, drinking warmth wherever the seal had failed, and it was not wandering. It was *following the line*. Following the fragment. Following the last piece of the old promise still burning anywhere in the world.

Following her.

"Noor." Theo had crouched to her level, his taster's hands steady on her shoulders, and his face was white but his voice was the voice from the night market, the one that talked people off ledges. "What is it. Talk to me."

"It knows about me," she said. "It's not eating villages. It's *searching*. The warmth is just — just crumbs along the trail. Theo, the covenant broke into pieces when Vael outlawed magic, and everyone thinks the pieces scattered. But they didn't scatter. They *hid*. And I'm the biggest piece left, and it's coming up the line, and it will be here—"

She didn't know how soon. The Old Tongue leaned toward things that hadn't happened, but it wouldn't give her the hour. Only the direction, and the certainty, and the image that rose behind her eyes whether she wanted it or not: the old she-wolf from her dream, frost in her fur, muzzle pressed to the far side of a stone, saying *tell them we are still keeping our side* — and behind the she-wolf, in the grey distance of the dying realm, dozens of gaunt shapes turning their heads at once, all together, toward the border. Toward Vael.

Toward the same scent.

"The wolves are coming too," Noor whispered. "Starving ones. Looking for the same thing it's looking for." She gripped Theo's sleeve. "We have days. Maybe less. And Theo — if the hunger reaches me first, and the wolves reach the border after, there won't be anyone left to tell them we're sorry."

Theo stood slowly. Down the street, the woman beat her rug. The children played their button game. The hearth-smoke rose thin and heatless into an empty sky, and somewhere beneath the marsh, unhurried, something climbed the broken line of a broken promise toward the last warm thing it could find.

"We go back tonight," Theo said. "And Noor — tomorrow, we find out what the guild buried. Because whatever's climbing toward us, I don't think charms are going to stop it."

Noor looked back once as they left Hollowmere, at the flock of sheep standing upright in their fold like mourners at a funeral for warmth itself.

Behind them, faint and getting fainter, one lamb finally made a sound. Not a bleat.

Something closer to a knock.
