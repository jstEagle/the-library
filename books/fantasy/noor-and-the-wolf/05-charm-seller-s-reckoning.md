## 5. Charm-Seller's Reckoning

The night market smelled of tallow and wet wool, of fried onions gone cold in their pans, of the particular sourness that rose off a crowd when the weather turned. Theo moved through it the way he had for ten years — hood up, pace unhurried, hands tucked into his sleeves where no one could see them working. He knew every stall by its shadow. The fishwife who sold luck-knots braided from eel-skin under her counter. The lamplighter who kept a jar of borrowed courage behind his teeth and sold sips of it for a copper. The old man at the corner of Tanner's Row who traded years of sleep for charms against dreaming, and who had forgotten his own daughter's face so gradually he now believed she'd never existed.

Theo had sold to all of them, and bought from all of them, and none of them knew his name.

That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement: the queen's food-taster by day, a ghost by night, a man made of transactions. He carried his stock sewn into the lining of his coat — small things, desperate things. A knot of red thread that would hold a fever back for three days. A pinch of grave-dirt blessed backward, which let a mother hear her drowned son's voice one last time. A sliver of mirror that showed you not your reflection but your reflection's regret. Each one had cost someone something irreplaceable, and each one would cost its buyer something too, because magic in Vael was a ledger and every line balanced in blood or memory or sleep.

He'd told himself for a decade that this was survival. Tonight, with Noor's small hand gripping two fingers of his left hand — the child insisted on contact, always contact, as if afraid the city might swallow him if they parted skin — he could no longer quite believe it.

"They're watching," Noor said.

Noor spoke Vaelish the way a bird speaks — accurately, mechanically, without any sense that words might be soft. But sometimes the child slipped into the Old Tongue mid-sentence, and when that happened, the air changed. Lamps guttered toward him. Dogs went quiet. Once, on their second night together, a locked door had simply opened, sighing like something relieved of a long duty.

Theo scanned the crowd. "Who?"

"The ones who smell of iron." Noor's grip tightened. "They came at dusk. They are standing very still."

Theo looked. At first he saw only market-crowd: porters, pickpockets, a knife-grinder packing up early. Then he saw what Noor meant. Near the fountain, four men in grey oilcloth coats stood motionless amid the current of bodies, the way herons stand in shallow water. Not talking. Not buying. Watching faces.

Wardens. The Ministry of Unlicensed Arts hadn't worked the night market in six years — not since the Purge of the Lantern Street cellars, since they'd burned a charm-broker alive in the square as an example and then lost interest. Six years of quiet. And now, in the same season a child had walked through Theo's door speaking a language dead three hundred years, the wardens were back.

*They're hunting the source,* Theo thought, and the thought landed in his stomach like a swallowed stone. *Not charms. Charms they tolerate, grudgingly, the way a body tolerates worms. They're hunting the Old Tongue.*

"Keep your mouth closed," he murmured to Noor. "Whatever happens. Whatever you see. Not one word."

Noor nodded, solemn as a judge.

* * *

The trouble started at Maren's stall.

Maren had been Theo's client for eight years. She ran a boarding house near the tanneries and had come to him originally for a charm against her husband's fists — a knot of dog-whisker and river-clay that Theo had woven himself, badly, guiltily, charging her half price because he couldn't bring himself to charge full. The knot had held. Her husband had left. She'd come back since for smaller things: a blessing on her lodgers' dreams, a ward against the coughing sickness that swept the tannery rows each winter.

Tonight she wasn't at her usual corner. Tonight she stood beside the wardens by the fountain, pointing.

Theo saw it happen in pieces, the way disasters arrive — too slowly to stop, too fast to deny. He saw Maren's arm rise. He saw her finger track across the crowd like a compass needle finding north. He saw the wardens turn as one, four grey heads swiveling, and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that she was pointing at him.

*She's buying her pardon.* The thought arrived cold and complete. *Her own little crimes, her little knots and blessings — she'll trade them all for one name. Mine.*

He didn't blame her. That was the worst of it. He knew exactly what the Ministry offered informants: full remission, a clean slate, a stamped paper that said your sins were forgiven by the Crown. Eight years of small kindnesses, and it had taken one frightened woman one evening to sell him. Survival made everyone complicit; he'd known that since chapter one of his own life. He just hadn't expected to be on the reading end of the lesson.

"Noor." His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "We're leaving. Now. Don't run — walk."

They turned down Tanner's Row, unhurried, two figures among dozens. Behind them, he heard the wardens begin to move — not running, but cutting through the crowd with that terrible heron-stillness translated into purpose. A fishwife shouted. A cart overturned. Someone screamed that the Ministry was here, and the word spread through the market like fire through dry grass, and the whole night began to come apart.

Theo did the arithmetic as he walked. His stock was sewn into his coat lining — forty-odd charms, some harmless, some dangerous, all of them evidence. If they took him alive, they'd find the stock, and they'd find Noor, and they would take the child apart looking for the source of the Old Tongue. Wardens were not gentle with children who did impossible things. There were cells beneath the Ministry building that had never been described in any document, because no one had ever come out to describe them.

There was a brazier at the end of Tanner's Row. Lamplighters used it to warm their pitch.

Theo stopped walking. "Noor. Listen to me. Do you remember the drain? Behind the tannery wall, the one you showed me, the one you said the city forgot about?"

Noor's eyes went wide. "You can't fit—"

"I don't need to fit. I need you to be somewhere they can't reach." He crouched, took the child by both shoulders. "I'm going to do something loud. When I do it, you run. Don't look back. Don't wait for me. Go to the drain and wait until dawn, and if I'm not there by dawn—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "If I'm not there by dawn, go north. Alone. You know the way. You've been drawing it in the dust all week."

"You said we'd stay together."

"I said a lot of things." He touched the child's cheek. "This one I mean."

Then he stood, pulled his coat open, and walked to the brazier.

* * *

What he burned, he burned deliberately, charm by charm, naming each one as it went into the coals — not for the wardens, but for himself. A penance, maybe. An inventory.

The fever-knots went first, curling green. Then the dream-charms, which screamed — softly, in voices he recognized, voices of people who had sold him their sleep. The grave-dirt flared white and for one horrible moment the smoke shaped itself into a boy's face, mouth moving, saying something no one living could hear. The mirror-slivers burst like ice. The luck-knots of eel-skin caught and stank of the river.

By the time the wardens reached him, the brazier was a column of greasy black smoke and the night market had emptied around him like water down a plughole. He stood in the open with his arms out, letting them see his empty sleeves, his empty coat.

"Well," he said. "Here I am."

The lead warden was a woman with a face like a folded letter. She circled him once, slow. "Theo Joubert," she read off a slip of paper. "Royal food-taster. Unlicensed practitioner. Informant says you've been dealing in forbidden arts for upward of a decade." She smiled without warmth. "And harboring something the Ministry wants rather more than you."

His blood went cold. "I don't know what you—"

"A child," she said. "Small. Dark-haired. Seen at your tenement. Seen in the market tonight, before you sent it running." She tilted her head. "Where is it, Joubert?"

*It.* As if Noor were a thing. As if the child were cargo.

"The informant," Theo said carefully, "is mistaken."

"Maren of the Tannery Rows," the warden recited, enjoying herself now. "Eight years your client. She gave us a very thorough account." She leaned close. "Including the part where the child talks to doors, and the doors answer."

Behind the warden's shoulder, at the far end of Tanner's Row, Theo saw a small shape slip along the wall toward the tannery drain — and freeze. Noor had stopped. Had turned. Was looking back at him, and even at forty paces Theo could see the child's expression: the terrible literalness of a child who has been told *don't wait for me* and is discovering that promises have weight.

Two wardens peeled off, moving toward the shadow where Noor stood.

Something in Theo's chest tore loose — something he'd kept stitched down for ten years, since the day he'd buried his family's names along with his own. It wasn't courage, exactly. It was closer to the moment a drowning man stops conserving air.

"Hey," Theo shouted, loud enough to crack. "Hey! You want the Old Tongue? It's me. It's been me the whole time. I'm the source." He raised his voice higher, pitched it strange, and let fall from his mouth — clumsily, terribly, in a language he barely knew and had learned only in fragments from a child's whispering — three words of the Old Tongue.

He didn't know what they meant. He only knew how Noor pronounced them, and that when Noor pronounced them, wards answered.

Every lamp on Tanner's Row went out at once.

The dark was total, absolute, a held breath. In it he heard the wardens shouting, heard steel drawn, heard the warden-woman snarl *seize him* — and then he was moving, blind, hands out, crashing into crates and bodies, following the memory of the row's geography while shouts split the blackness behind him. Someone grabbed his collar; he twisted out of his coat entirely, left it in the warden's fist like a shed skin, and ran.

A small hand found his in the dark. Found it unerringly, the way it always did.

"This way," Noor breathed, and the words were half Old Tongue, and though Theo couldn't translate them he understood them perfectly, the way you understand falling.

They went over the tannery wall together — Noor first, light as ash, then Theo, tearing his palms on the boards — and into the drain, a stone throat that smelled of a century of runoff. Behind them, torches bloomed along Tanner's Row. Voices called orders. Somewhere, distantly, the warden-woman's voice promised, calm as arithmetic, that they would find them, that the city had few enough holes left, that the Ministry was patient.

In the dark of the drain, pressed against wet stone, Noor's hand still in his, Theo started laughing — a low, cracked sound that scared him more than the wardens had.

"What is funny?" Noor whispered.

"Nothing." He wiped his face with his free hand and found it wet. "Nothing's funny. I just finally understand the shape of my life." He stared into the black ahead of them, northward, toward the border no one crossed and came back from. "Ten years I told myself I was surviving. Keeping my head down. Buying my quiet with other people's memories." His voice steadied. "There's no quiet left to buy, Noor. They know my name. They know yours. Maren sold us for a piece of stamped paper, and I can't even hate her for it, because I'd have done the same."

Noor was silent a moment. Then, in the Old Tongue, soft as a hand on a wound, the child said something short and certain.

"What does that mean?" Theo asked, though some part of him already knew.

"It means," Noor said, translating reluctantly, "that some debts can't be paid in the same country where they were made." A pause. "It means we go north."

Theo closed his eyes. Behind them, Vael — the queen whose soup he'd tasted every morning for a decade, the guild that had built him for purposes he was only beginning to guess at, the whole crooked edifice of his careful, cowardly, purchased life. Ahead of them, the wolf-realm, and whatever waited there for a man built to carry magic in his blood.

He thought of Master Aldric's pages, hidden in his floorboards back at the tenement he would never safely enter again. *Chosen as a child. Built for the crossing.*

Well. The choosing was done. All that remained was to find out what for.

"We'll need supplies," he said. "And we leave before the watch changes at dawn." He squeezed Noor's hand. "Stay close to me, and whatever you hear behind us — don't look back."

Noor nodded. And then, quietly, in a voice that had no business being so old, the child added one more word in the Old Tongue, and every rat in the drain turned and fled ahead of them, clearing the road north.

Theo decided not to ask.

Some questions, he was learning, you saved for the country where they could be answered.
