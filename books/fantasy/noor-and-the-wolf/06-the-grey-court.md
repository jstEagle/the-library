## 6. The Grey Court

Theo had thought the border would be a wall. Something with stones in it, something a man could point to and say *there, that is where the world ends.*

It was not. It was a smell.

Three days north of the last farmstead — the one where the sheep had stood hollow-eyed in their pasture, still breathing, still warm to the touch, but emptied of some other warmth that no fire could put back — the air changed. Theo tasted it before he saw anything: iron and rain on old stone, the scent of a cellar that has been shut for years and then opened all at once.

Noor stopped walking. The child's small hand found his sleeve without looking, the way you'd reach for a rope in the dark.

"It's here," Noor said. In Vaelish first, then again in the Old Tongue, as if the second saying were the true one and the first only its shadow. "The edge of it. Can't you feel your teeth?"

He could. His teeth ached, all of them at once, a deep ringing ache like biting foil. Ten years of tasting for the queen had taught him to read his own body like a ledger, and every line of it was telling him the same thing: *something in this air disagrees with you, and it isn't poison, or rather it is nothing so simple as poison.*

Beyond the next rise there were no more farms. There was not even no-more-farms in the way he understood it. The land simply *changed its mind* about being Vael. The grass went grey at the roots but stayed green at the tips, as though color itself were rationed here. Trees stood in rows too straight for nature and too crooked for planting. And the light — the light came from everywhere and nowhere, sourceless, the grey of an overcast sky at the hour when the sun has set but the dark hasn't committed.

"Stay behind me," Theo said, which was absurd — he was a food-taster with a satchel of burned charm-ash and a knife better suited to cheese — and Noor, who was six years old and had led him here, looked up at him with an expression of great patience and said nothing at all.

They walked into the wolf-realm the way you walk into cold water. One step, then another, and then it has you.

* * *

*The man smells of ash and fear,* Noor thought, trotting at his side, *and under that, of milk-honey and burnt sugar, which is what magic smells like when it's been swallowed instead of spoken.*

That was the thing Noor had never told him. That every charm he'd ever sold, every forbidden little working he'd carried across the city in his blood and breathed out through his hands — Noor could taste them on him, faint as a memory of a meal. The child had known what Theo was from the doorway of the tenement, from the first word of the Old Tongue that had made his wards sit up like dogs hearing their names.

Now the whole realm knew it too.

They heard the wolves before they saw them — not howling, which would have been frightening enough, but *singing*, low and ragged, a sound like a choir that had forgotten most of its hymns. Then they were simply there, between the crooked trees: five of them, six, grey as the grass, grey as the light, their ribs showing beneath fur gone dull as old pewter. Starving. Not the desperate scrambling starvation of stray dogs — these wolves stood with terrible dignity, heads high, hunger held in check by something older than hunger.

One of them spoke.

Not in barks. In words. The Old Tongue, rolling out of a throat built for it, each syllable round and resonant as a bell struck underwater. Noor answered before Theo's mind had even finished flinching, and the two voices wove together, question and answer, and Theo stood in the middle of a conversation conducted entirely above his head like a man standing in falling snow.

Then the largest wolf turned her head and looked at *him*, and the conversation stopped.

Her eyes were the exact grey of the border-light. They did not threaten. That was the worst part. They assessed, the way a jeweler assesses a stone — turning him over, weighing, finding flaws.

"The taster," she said, in accented but unmistakable Vaelish. "The guild's little spoon. Walk."

* * *

They walked. Behind the wolves, flanked by the wolves, through a country that grew stranger with every mile. Rivers ran uphill in slow silver threads. A field of white flowers turned, all together, to watch them pass. Once, far off, something enormous moved through the treeline with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and the wolves around them did not react, which told Theo everything about how much worse things could get.

Noor's hand found his again and did not let go.

"You're shaking," the child said quietly, in Vaelish.

"I'm cold."

"You're lying. You lie the way you taste — carefully." Noor squeezed his fingers. "Vess won't hurt us. She's sad. Can't you hear it? She sings sad all the time."

"I can't hear anything she sings."

"No," Noor agreed. "But she can hear you. Everything you've ever swallowed. She's listening to it right now." The child's eyes went distant, listening to whatever children who speak dying languages listen to. "She says you're very loud."

Theo had spent ten years being quiet. Quiet footsteps, quiet sales, quiet guilt. The idea that he might be *loud* — that everything he'd buried might be audible to the country around him — made his skin crawl. He thought of the charms. The memories he'd sold off himself, cheap, in the lean years. The scar-price, the sleep-price. All of it sitting in his blood like coins in a purse, jingling with every step into this listening land.

The trees ended. Without transition, they stood at the lip of a vast shallow bowl of land, and in its center, under the sourceless grey light, stood the Grey Court.

It was not a building. It was a gathering. Wolves beyond counting sat ring upon ring upon ring down the curve of the bowl, hundreds of them, thousands, silent, and at the center on a stone worn smooth as glass lay a single figure — a she-wolf larger than any horse Theo had ever seen, her coat the grey of ash and her eyes closed.

The rings parted for them. It happened without command; the wolves simply flowed aside, and the path unrolled toward the center like a tongue.

"Don't stop walking," Noor whispered. "And don't look away from her. Looking away is answering a question with silence."

"What question?"

"You'll know it when you hear it."

* * *

Vess opened her eyes when they were ten paces out, and Theo forgot how to breathe.

There was no menace in her gaze. There was *age*. He had seen the queen's regalia, the guild's oldest charters, the sealed archives under the Ministry — and none of it had prepared him for eyes that had watched a covenant signed and watched it break. Grief lived in those eyes the way water lives in a well: deep, drawn on daily, never empty.

She spoke, and her voice filled the bowl, and every wolf in every ring laid back its ears to listen.

"A human child carrying the last living fragment of the covenant. And a man of the taster's guild, soaked to the bone in stolen magic, walking into my realm as if he owned his own blood." Her muzzle curled — not quite a snarl, not quite a smile. "Which of you will speak?"

"He doesn't speak it," Noor said, stepping forward, small and unbowed before a creature that could have ended the child with one careless motion. "I'll speak for both until he learns."

"And I speak Vaelish," Vess said, "as I spoke it at the signing, when your ancestors' ambassadors came trembling to this stone. So we will waste no interpreters." Her gaze shifted to Theo, pinning him like a specimen. "Taster. Tell me what you are."

"I don't—" He stopped. Swallowed. Carefully, the way he'd learned to answer dangerous questions across a queen's table: "I taste for poison. That's the work. That's all the work has ever been."

"Liar," Vess said, almost gently. "Or rather — a man lied to so long he believes the lie is his own. Child, show him."

Noor reached up and took Theo's hand, and spoke three words of the Old Tongue against his palm.

The world opened.

He felt it all at once — every charm he'd ever carried, every working he'd smuggled in his veins and sold in the night market, lighting up along his nerves like lamps down a dark street. But beneath them, deeper, older, was something else: a channel. A shape carved into him before he could walk, before he could speak, a road built through his body by other people's hands and paved over with the innocuous name of *immunity*. He was not resistant to poison. He was a vessel, fired and glazed, and the poison-work was merely the glaze that kept anyone — including himself — from noticing what the vessel was *for*.

"They chose me," he said. His voice came out very small in the enormous silence. "When I was a child. Aldric. The guild. They *made* me."

"They made a bridge," Vess corrected, "and called it a man. We wondered, these long years, whether the guild had forgotten its half of the bargain or simply hidden it. Now we know. Hidden. Buried under paperwork and poison-tasting, waiting for a border to fail badly enough that someone would come digging." She rose, and rising, she seemed to grow; the whole bowl of wolves stirred like wind moving over wheat. "Hear the terms, heir-taster. Hear them once."

The grey light dimmed. Every wolf in the court went still.

"The border fails within a season. On one side, the hunger comes through — you have seen its work, the warm things emptied. On the other side, my realm starves, for the covenant fed us as much as it fed you, and what remains of it gutters like a lamp out of oil. There is one reforging left in the old words, one rite, and it requires a vessel freely given." Her eyes held his, and for the first time he saw the terror in them, terror worn smooth by centuries into something like courtesy. "Your immunity, Theo Joubert. The glaze that keeps the magic out of you. Surrender it, freely, knowingly, and the covenant-magic will pass through you raw — and it will burn, and it may kill you, and if it does not kill you it will change you past any calling-back. Refuse, and you may walk home. We do not compel. Compulsion broke the covenant the first time."

Silence. The sourceless light. Noor's hand tight around his.

"And if I refuse?" Theo asked. "If I walk home?"

"Then within a season, child-speaker, teach your city the word for *famine*," Vess said. "In both tongues."

* * *

Later — how much later he could not say — Theo sat at the edge of the great bowl while the court dispersed like fog, and Noor climbed the smooth stone beside him and sat with legs dangling.

"You're going to say yes," the child said.

"I haven't decided."

"You decided in the village, when you saw the sheep. You decided at my door." Noor leaned against his arm, feather-light. "You're just deciding it again now, slower, because you're brave in the slow way."

Theo looked out over the grey realm, at the starving dignity of it, at the border behind him that he could no longer see but could still feel in his teeth. Somewhere south of it was Vael, and the night market, and the queen's soup, and the small quiet life he had clawed out of the world one careful taste at a time. It was already ash. The wardens had seen to that; the betrayal had seen to that. There was no door to go back through — only the one in front of him, and it was shaped exactly like himself.

"When?" he asked.

"At the border-stone," Noor said. "Under the split moon. Nine days." The child looked up, and in the grey light those young eyes were ancient and kind. "Nine days to learn the words that will name you. So we'd best begin tonight, taster. Say after me: *I am the cup, and I am willing.*"

Theo opened his mouth to repeat it — and found, to his horror and his wonder, that the Old Tongue rose to meet him, already waiting on his tongue like water finding its level.
