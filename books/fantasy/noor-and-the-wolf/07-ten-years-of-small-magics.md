## 7. Ten Years of Small Magics

The Grey Court kept its accounts in bone.

Theo learned this on his third morning in the wolf-realm, when Vess led him to a hollow beneath the border-stone where the old covenant-keepers had once stored their ledgers — ribs of elk and aurochs, each one notched with marks too fine for any human hand. She set a fresh rib before him in the grey grass and said, in her voice like wind through a door left open: *Write down what you spent. All of it. A man who does not know what he carries cannot give it away.*

So he began.

He built the inventory the way he'd built his charms: methodically, in order of price. The small things first. A charm against rats, sold to a miller in the Tallow Quarter — that had cost him the smell of bread baking, which he had not noticed losing until he tried to remember it and found only a hole shaped like warmth. A charm to sweeten sour milk, bought by a widow with three children; that one had taken his memory of rain on a tin roof. A luck-knot for a fisherman, priced at a year of sleep, though sleep was cheap and he'd paid it gladly. He listed them all, scratching the notches with a knife Vess gave him, a knife of grey steel that never dulled and never shone.

The list grew long. Longer than he wanted it to be. By the second day he stopped counting charms and started counting years.

* * *

It was Noor who made him go deeper.

The child sat across from him in the hollow every evening, cross-legged, patient as stone, watching him work with those dark eyes that seemed to hold more winters than summers. On the fourth night, when he laid down the knife and rubbed the ache from his wrist, Noor reached out and turned the bone toward herself. Her finger traced the notches.

"You've written what you sold," she said. "Now write what they took."

"Same thing."

"No." She shook her head, and her hair — pale as ash, always slightly moving as if in a wind he couldn't feel — stirred around her face. "A price is what you paid. A taking is what's gone. You have to go find it. Some of it can come back."

"How?"

"The tongue remembers." She tapped her own chest, just below the collarbone. "Everything you paid with went somewhere. Nothing is destroyed here. It's only... held. The way water is held in a winter river under ice." She looked up at him. "You're going to break the ice anyway, when you do the rite. Everything you are will pour out of you. Better to know what's in there first."

Theo looked at the bone ledger, at the hundreds of fine notches, each one a transaction, each transaction a piece of himself parceled out to strangers who needed magic more than they needed to be whole. Ten years of small magics. Ten years of selling himself off one memory, one scar, one season of sleep at a time, because survival demanded coin and coin demanded commerce and commerce, in Vael, meant giving away whatever the Ministry couldn't tax.

"And if I don't want to know?" he asked.

Noor smiled, and it was an old smile on a young face. "Then you'll die not knowing what you were. Vess says the rite takes everything. If some of it comes back to you after — if you wake on the other side carrying your own life — wouldn't you want it to be all of it? Even the bad parts?"

*Especially* the bad parts, something in him answered, and he hated how clearly he heard it.

"All right," he said. "Teach me."

* * *

The Old Tongue did not sound the way language sounded.

That was the first thing Theo understood, sitting in the frost-bright grass while Noor taught him the first word. She spoke it — *vaelith*, the word for threshold, for doorway, for the place between — and the air changed. Not moved. *Changed.* The way a room changes when someone opens a window onto a garden. The word didn't describe a threshold. For the length of its speaking, it *was* one.

"That's the danger," Noor said, watching his face. "In your guild tongue, words sit still. In the Old Tongue, words do things while you say them. You can't say *vaelith* without opening something, even if it's only a little crack inside yourself."

"What comes through the crack?"

"Whatever you buried deepest." Her eyes were kind and merciless. "Usually."

She taught him one word a day. That was all either of them could bear.

The second word was *sennet*, which meant grief — not sadness, but the specific grief of something unfinished, a debt unpaid, a door unopened. When Theo spoke it, following Noor's careful shaping of his clumsy human mouth, he felt it settle into his chest like a swallowed stone, and that night he dreamed of his mother's hands for the first time in nine years.

He woke with wet cheeks and did not remember crying, and Noor sat beside his bedroll waiting, because she had known. "That's the ice cracking," she said simply. "The word found what it was shaped like. Grief calls to grief. Now we know where to dig."

* * *

They dug on the sixth day.

Vess stood watch at the edge of the hollow, a grey shape against the greyer sky, and Noor sat before Theo with both their hands open between them, palms up, the way one holds out grain to a bird you want to trust you. She gave him the word — *mireth*, the word for return, for what comes home — and told him to speak it into his own open hands while thinking of nothing else but the ledger. The notches. The prices. The holes.

He spoke it.

The world tilted.

It came back like cold water — there was no other way to describe it, no gentler image. Cold water poured into an empty vessel, shocking, bracing, filling every dry space at once. He gasped. His hands closed around nothing and everything.

The smell of bread baking — his mother's kitchen, the low ceiling, flour in the creases of her knuckles. Rain on a tin roof, the summer he was nine, the whole family sleeping in one room because the storm had driven even the mosquitoes indoors. His father's voice singing badly, gloriously badly, a harvest song with half the words wrong. The taste of plums. The weight of his sister asleep against his shoulder on the cart ride to market, her breath warm on his neck. The name of the village. The name of the—

The name of himself.

Not Theo Joubert. The name before the name, the one he had buried with his family when the fever took them and the Ministry took everything else and a boy of fourteen decided that a boy with no past could survive anything. He felt it rising in him like a drowned thing surfacing, and he clamped down on it, hard, because if he said it now — here, in this hollow, with the Old Tongue loose in the air — he was afraid of what would hear it.

Noor watched him struggle with steady eyes. "You don't have to take it all today," she said. "But you have to stop running from it. The rite won't work otherwise. You can't offer a self you refuse to look at."

"I looked," Theo said hoarsely. "I looked and I—" He pressed his fists against his eyes. "Why does it hurt more coming back than going?"

"Because when it left, you chose to let it go. Coming back, you have to choose to keep it." She was quiet a moment. "My people had a saying. *What is remembered cannot be unmade twice.*"

"And what about what's remembered and then lost again?"

Noor's smile flickered, sad and certain at once. "That's why you're doing the rite, isn't it? So nothing gets lost again. Not yours. Not mine. Not theirs." She nodded northward, toward the deep grey country where wolves starved in a realm gone colorless. "The covenant was a promise that memories could cross the border safely. Yours will carry it. But only if you're willing to carry *all* of it."

* * *

The days took on a rhythm, and the rhythm became a discipline, and the discipline became something almost like peace.

Mornings: the ledger, updated now with two columns — what was sold, what was reclaimed. The reclaimed column grew faster than he'd dared hope. The Old Tongue, it turned out, was less a language than a keyring, and every word Noor taught him unlocked another drawer in the cabinet of his stolen self. *Orenn*, the word for patience, gave him back three years of sleepless nights by making them meaningful instead of merely endured. *Halvet*, the word for scar-tissue, let him trace each mark on his body and remember the specific bargain that bought it — and, strangely, to forgive the man he'd been for making them. *Sennet* he spoke nightly now, voluntarily, a bell rung over deep water, and each ringing brought up more of his mother, his father, his sister, the village whose name he could finally say aloud without his throat closing: *Carrowmere*. Carrowmere, on the fen-road south of Vael. Carrowmere, where he had been born under a different name entirely.

Evenings: Noor taught, and Theo learned, and both of them changed.

He saw it happening to the child and could not stop it and was not sure he should. Every word she taught, she relearned — that was the nature of the tongue, it reshaped its speakers with each speaking — and Noor was growing older in a way that had nothing to do with years. Her sentences came longer, stranger, threaded with images no eight-year-old should carry. Sometimes she paused mid-lesson and stared at her own hands as if they belonged to someone she was becoming, and Theo understood with a lurch that he was watching the first bridge-speaker be forged in real time, hammered out word by word, and that no one — not Vess, not the Grey Court, not the dead covenant-keepers whose bones lined the ledger-hollow — knew whether the forging would make her or break her.

"It's all right," she told him once, catching his worry. "The tongue doesn't take anything. It only shows you what you already are, bigger." A pause. "You're changing too. Have you noticed?"

He had. His dreams had gone vivid and prophetic-seeming. His senses had sharpened until he could smell rain an hour before it fell and hear the heartbeats of hares in the tall grass. And sometimes, speaking the Old Tongue, he felt the words move through his body like water through a channel — a channel that had been carved in him as a child, by men in guild robes, for exactly this purpose. His immunity, his taster's gift, his decade of surviving poisons that should have killed him: all of it was a roadbed, graded and packed and waiting for traffic.

He was not a man learning a language. He was a road remembering it was a road.

* * *

On the last evening, Vess came down into the hollow and stood over the finished ledger, reading the notches by moonlight that fell strange and doubled through the failing border — two moons overhead now, the split moon of the old stories, waxing toward the night of the rite.

"You have recovered much," she said.

"Not everything." Theo turned the bone in his hands. "Some of it's still held. The charms I sold that are still working — the miller's rat-charm, the widow's milk-charm — the pieces of me in them are still out there, doing their small work. I can feel them. Like fingers I left in other people's houses."

"They will return when the covenant reforges," Vess said. "All debts flow home when the river runs again." Her amber eyes fixed on him. "But one thing remains unrecovered. One thing you have not written in either column."

Theo knew. He had known since the night the cold water came back, since the name rose in him like a drowned thing and he clamped his hands over it. He looked at Noor, and Noor looked back, and the child nodded once, gravely, the way one nods at a truth already agreed upon in private.

"My name," he said. "The first one. The real one."

"Speak it," Vess said, "or the rite has nothing to stand on. An offering must be named. You cannot surrender what you will not claim."

Theo opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

And in the silence before the name could come, far to the north, beyond the grey hills, something howled — long, hungry, and wrong, a sound with too many voices in it — and every hair on Theo's arms rose, and Vess's head snapped toward the sound, and Noor whispered:

"It's early. The hunger's crossing early. We have days, not weeks."

The split moons hung above them, closer together than they'd been that morning, closing like a wound — or like jaws.
