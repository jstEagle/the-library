## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to its last honest inch when the knock came—three soft raps, then one, the way smugglers knocked and temple novices never did.

Emrys Brightwater set down his pen, blotted the line he'd just finished—a shipping manifest for the Choir of Saint Vess, forty barrels of lamp oil, perfectly innocent, perfectly dull—and blew out the candle. For a count of ten he sat in darkness, listening. The knock came again. Patient. Unhurried. Whoever it was knew the rules: no light in the window, no answer on the first ask, and never, ever a name spoken aloud until you were both inside and certain of the street.

He opened the door a hand's width. Rain-smell, pipe-smoke, and the broad silhouette of a woman built like a dock crane.

"Evening," she said.

"Evening," said Emrys, and stepped aside to let her in, because that was all either of them was permitted to say until the door was shut and the bar was drawn.

Her name was Maren Coal, though tonight she might be someone else entirely; names were currency down here, and nobody spent more than they had to. She was a runner for the Saltgate crews, and she'd been coming to his room above the tannery off Candlewick Lane for eleven months now, always after midnight, always with something rolled in oilcloth or sewn into her coat lining.

"You're looking well," she said, unwinding her scarf. "For a man who works two jobs."

"I work one job," Emrys said mildly. "I'm a scribe."

"Mm." She produced the oilcloth bundle from inside her coat and laid it on his desk with the reverence other people reserved for relics. "And I'm a wool merchant."

Emrys lit a stub of candle behind the tin screen he'd rigged for exactly this purpose—a scribe's trick, borrowed from the archive masters who guarded their eyes from glare—and unrolled the bundle. Inside was a map. Not a copy of a map. An original, drawn on hide, the ink gone brown as old blood, showing the mountain roads above Thornfell in a hand that predated the current kingdom by a century at least.

His heart did something complicated and quiet, the way it always did.

"Tide roads," Maren said. "Under the old mine-works. My people want three copies. Clean ones. Your best work."

"The tide roads flood," Emrys said, tracing a route with one finger. "Twice a day, if this chart is to be believed, and charts lie less often than merchants."

"They want them anyway. There's talk of something opening up under Thornfell." She shrugged, elaborately casual, which from Maren meant she was worried. "New buyers asking questions. Rich ones. The kind who don't take *no* for an answer and don't leave witnesses."

Emrys looked at the map a long moment. Then he pulled fresh paper toward him, ground his ink, and began.

This was the arrangement, and had been for nearly a year: by day, Emrys Brightwater was the Choir's most reliable scribe, a young man of neat hand and neater habits who owed the temple nine years of service against a debt his dead father had signed and Emrys had inherited like a bad knee. He copied psalters. He indexed donations. He fetched Archcantor Halden's tea and endured Halden's sermons on the virtue of settled accounts with the patient face of a man who had learned that patience was cheaper than trouble.

By night, he was nobody. A room above a tannery, a screened candle, and a pen that moved for whoever could pay in coin small enough not to be missed. Smugglers' maps. Contracts between parties who could not sign their true names. Letters for lovers on opposite sides of a feud. Once, memorably, a divorce written in cipher for a woman who paid him in pickled eels and thanked him so fiercely he'd blushed for an hour.

He called it his ledger of small debts—not what he owed, but what others owed him, kept in a little book hidden beneath a loose floorboard. Names, services, favors. Not one entry in it used a real name. That was the whole art of it. Emrys Brightwater did not exist after dark. Invisibility, he had decided somewhere around his seventeenth year, was not cowardice. It was craft.

"You're frowning," Maren observed, watching him work. "You only frown when the money's wrong or the danger's right."

"The danger's right," Emrys admitted. "Who are these rich buyers?"

"Wouldn't you like to know." She grinned, then lost the grin. "Actually—yeah. You would. Because they're asking about the Choir, too. About old prophecies. Sealed archives, that sort of thing."

Emrys's pen paused for exactly half a heartbeat before continuing, smooth and even, because a scribe's first discipline was that his hand must never tell tales his mouth hadn't approved. "The Choir's archives aren't sealed. They're merely tedious. Nobody bribes their way into tax records."

"These people would. They asked my captain to find them a scribe. Someone discreet. Someone *inside*." She let that sit. "I told them I already knew one."

"Maren."

"I didn't give a name! I gave a description. 'Skinny, polite, looks like an apology.'" She laughed at his expression. "Come on, Brightwater. This is the biggest purse we've ever been offered, and you're the only man I trust to hold a pen straight while the world tilts."

Emrys finished the third copy of the tide roads, sanded it, rolled it, and tied it with the plain gray string that meant *ordinary goods, nothing to see here*. He took her coin—more than the job was worth, less than the risk—and walked her to the door.

"Forget I mentioned the buyers," she said, pausing on the threshold, rain silvering the street behind her. "Or don't. But whatever they're digging for under Thornfell—" she glanced back over her shoulder, and for once there was no performance in her face at all, "—my captain says the mountain's been humming lately. Like a throat clearing."

Then she was gone into the rain, and Emrys barred the door, and sat for a while in the dark doing sums he didn't like.

* * *

It was nearly three by the time he told himself he'd sleep, and precisely three when he instead knelt, pried up the floorboard, and took out the little book.

Not the ledger of debts. The other one—the private one, the dangerous one, the one that would see him flogged out of the Choir and possibly hanged if any living soul read it. His collection. Over four years he had copied, in stolen hours, fragments of everything the temple locked away: heresies, histories, songs the Choir had declared unsingable. He told himself it was research. He told himself knowledge was a debt too, one he intended to repay to somebody someday. Mostly he suspected he simply couldn't help himself. A scribe who copies only what he's permitted is a mirror, and Emrys had never wanted to be a mirror. Mirrors show you what's already in the room.

Tonight's acquisition had come to him that very afternoon, folded inside a delivery of vellum: a single leaf, ancient, brittle, sold to the Choir's own library by a scrap-dealer who hadn't known—or hadn't cared—what it was. The archivists had catalogued it as *fragment, apocryphal, value negligible*, and shelved it where neglected things go. Emrys had carried it home in his sleeve like a coal he couldn't stop touching.

Now, by the screened candle, he flattened it and began to copy.

It was a prophecy. They usually were; the sealed shelves sagged with them, most of them gibberish, all of them officially nonsense per the Doctrine of Settled Accounts, which held that the future, like a ledger, balanced best when nobody peeked ahead. But this one was older than the Doctrine, and it was written in the tight, confident hand of someone who had seen rather than guessed.

*When the Choir's ninth decade wanes,* it read, *a thief shall come to the Hollow Court beneath Thornfell, and steal back a soul that was sold.*

Standard stuff. Souls, courts, theft. Emrys had transcribed dozens of these. His pen moved with the easy rhythm of routine—

—and stopped.

Because the next lines were not standard. The next lines were specific in the way that made the hair on his arms stand up and take attendance.

*Beneath the waning moon of the harvest's end,* wrote the long-dead hand, *in the year the temple counts as nine-and-ninety of the ninth decade.*

Emrys did the arithmetic without wanting to. The Choir counted its decades from the founding. Nine decades and ninety-nine years. This year. And the harvest's end, the waning moon—

Tomorrow night.

His mouth had gone dry. He kept copying, because stopping felt like admitting something, and admitting things was how invisible men became visible.

*A scribe of the Choir, bound by debt, whose hands have touched both holy page and smuggler's road.* The old ink seemed darker here, as if the writer had pressed harder. *He shall carry the thief's name within him, though he does not yet know it. And the Court's regent shall know him on sight, for the regent keeps a ledger too, and the thief's name is already written in it.*

Beside this passage, cramped into the margin in the same hand, was a date. Tomorrow's date. And beside the date, a name.

Not *a name*. *His* name. Written in letters a century old: **EMRYS BRIGHTWATER**.

The candle guttered. Emrys did not move. Somewhere below him, the tannery yard lay silent, and beyond it Candlewick Lane, and beyond that the sleeping city, and beneath all of it—under the roots of Thornfell, where the old maps showed tide roads flooding twice a day—something hummed like a throat clearing.

"No," Emrys said aloud, reasonably, to an empty room. "No, absolutely not. Prophecies are gibberish. The Doctrine says so. *I* have copied the Doctrine. Twice."

The leaf said nothing. Old paper is very good at saying nothing. It simply lay there being correct.

He thought of Maren's rich buyers asking after sealed archives. He thought of the mountain humming. He thought, absurdly, of the woman who'd paid him in pickled eels, because his mind, when frightened, reached for anything warm, and found almost nothing within reach.

Here was the truth he arrived at, sitting alone at three in the morning with a century-old accusation in his own handwriting staring up at him: invisibility had never been safety. It had been a hiding place, and hiding places only worked until someone came looking specifically for you. He had spent years making himself unfindable, and some dead prophet had still written his name down, and tomorrow—tomorrow the moon would begin to wane whether he copied another manifest or not.

Very carefully, Emrys picked up the leaf, carried it to the hearth, and held it over the cold ashes.

He stood there for a long time.

Then he took it back to the desk, finished the transcription down to the last faded letter—including the final line, which he read three times and understood none of:

*And what the thief steals, he must choose to keep.*

He hid the copy beneath the floorboard with the rest of his forbidden little library, put out the candle, and lay down in the dark with his heart going like a courier's boots.

Tomorrow, he told himself, he would rise at dawn, report to the scriptorium, copy forty pages of donation records, fetch Archcantor Halden's tea, and be perfectly, safely, invisibly ordinary.

Tomorrow, the prophecy began.

Somewhere across the city, faint as a memory, he could almost swear he heard the mountain humming.
