## 2. What Wren Sold

The rain came down on Saltmarsh Row like it had a grudge, and Wren Talis came up the alley with her hand pressed to her side and a smile she'd been wearing since the docks, because the first rule of smuggling was that nobody ever saw you bleed.

She counted the temple's windows as she went. Third floor, the one with the crooked shutter. Light behind it, gold and steady. He was working.

Good. She needed him working. Working men didn't ask questions.

The stairwell smelled of tallow and old ink, which was fitting, because Emrys Brightwater was made of both—half candle, half ledger, all of him bent over a desk like a man who'd been folded there at birth. She'd been coming to him for two years now. Maps, mostly. Routes through Thornfell's dead mine-roads drawn in his careful, cramped hand, each one worth more than she made in a season. He never asked what she carried. She never asked why a temple scribe sold contraband to smugglers. It was the most honest arrangement she had.

She knocked the way they'd agreed: twice, pause, once.

"Client hours are over," came the voice through the door, warm even when it was pretending to be stern.

"Client hours are whenever I say," Wren said, and pushed inside before her legs could disagree.

Emrys looked up from his desk, quill still in hand, and whatever he saw in her face made him set it down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"You're dripping on my floor."

"I'm leaking," Wren corrected, "which is different, and also your floor has seen worse. You told me about the bishop."

"The bishop was an accident." He was already moving, dragging a stool over, pulling a cloth from somewhere—the man kept cloths the way other people kept secrets—and pressing it into her hands. "Sit. Press there. Hard."

"It's nothing. A crate fell."

"A crate fell *on* you?"

"A crate fell near me, and then some other crates got involved. It was a whole thing." She sat, and the sitting cost her more than she wanted it to, and she saw him see that too. Emrys had eyes like a copyist—nothing got past them, everything got written down. "Anyway. I need a map."

"At this hour? Bleeding?"

"Especially bleeding. Urgent cargo."

He crouched by the little stove in the corner, feeding it a coal, and for a moment the only sound was the rain and the tick of iron heating. When he turned back, he had that look on his face. The one where he was deciding whether to be kind or clever, and couldn't find the seam between them.

"Wren." He said her name plainly, no title, no client-voice. That was another thing about him. Two years of business and he'd never once pretended not to know her name. "You're shaking."

"I'm cold."

"You're shaking and lying, which is worse. Lying while cold takes talent."

* * *

Here is what Wren would not tell him, not yet, not while her blood was still deciding whether to stay inside her:

Three days ago, in a tavern in Lowmark, a man with a face like a closed book had slid a contract across the table to her brother Tam. Not to her—to Tam, because the Hollow Court did its business through the desperate, and Tam had been desperate since the fever took his lungs last winter. The contract offered one cure, complete and immediate. The price column had been left blank.

Tam hadn't filled it in. Tam couldn't read the old script. So he'd signed where the man pointed, and the man had smiled with someone else's mouth—a mouth Wren recognized, later, in her worst hours, as *her own*—and said, "The price is already paid. Your sister settled it years ago."

Wren had sold something once. Down in the dark under Thornfell, three winters back, when the smuggling run went wrong and her crew was drowning in debt to people who collected debts in fingers. A broker of the Court had found her. Had offered to make the debt vanish. Had asked only for "the part of you that isn't flesh"—and she'd laughed, because she'd thought it was a figure of speech, and she'd signed, because Tam was hungry and the crew was scared and what was a soul anyway, on paper?

She'd felt nothing leave. That was the horror of it. You could give away the largest thing you owned and feel exactly as heavy afterward.

But now she knew what it bought. Now she knew her brother breathed easy because she'd paid in the only coin the Court truly wanted, and now she knew the contract wasn't finished—it was *held*, accruing, waiting—and the Court beneath Thornfell did not let accounts lie dormant forever.

And now she knew, from the same closed-book man, delivered like a courtesy, like a receipt:

*"Your soul is safe with us, Miss Talis. Safe as anything we keep. And we keep everything."*

* * *

"So," Emrys said, when she finally stopped talking, because apparently she had started talking at some point, words spilling out of her like water through a cracked hull. "You want a map into the Hollow Court."

"I want a map *toward* it. Getting in is my problem."

"That's not how problems work."

"That's exactly how problems work. Problems are like doors. Someone else can get you to the door; walking through is your own affair."

Emrys stood at his desk with both hands flat on it, and she watched him do the thing he always did—weigh it. She could practically hear the scales ticking behind his eyes. Risk against coin. Coin against conscience. Conscience against the terrible arithmetic of a man whose entire life was conducted in margins.

"No," he said.

Wren blinked. "No?"

"No. I won't draw you a road into that place. Wren, the Court doesn't have entrances. It has *appetites*. Everything I've ever copied that mentions it—and I've copied things that would get me burned—says the same thing. People go down. Ledgers come up. People don't."

"People are dramatic," Wren said. "I've been underground my whole career. Caves, mines, cellars, one memorable tomb. I'm very good at not dying in holes."

"And yet here you are, leaking on my floor."

"That was crates."

"Wren." He said it again, softer this time, and that was somehow harder to argue with. "You want to walk into the one place in the world that keeps souls as inventory, to take yours back out of a locked drawer, alone, wounded, with a hand-drawn map. Do you hear it? Say it out loud and listen to yourself."

She opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"I hear a woman who knows where she's going," she said, with dignity.

Emrys laughed—one short, helpless breath of it—and then pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes, and when he took them away he looked older than his years, which were already considerable for a man who drank tea like a grandmother.

"I can't help you," he said. "I'm sorry. Truly. If I drew that map and you died, it would be my handwriting on your death. I know what my handwriting looks like. I couldn't stand it."

And that, Wren thought, was the strangest kindness anyone had ever refused her. Most people said no with their chins up, protecting themselves. Emrys said no like it hurt him more than her.

"Fine," she said, standing, and the room tilted politely and righted itself. "Fine. Forget it. Forget I asked."

She got two steps toward the door.

"Wren."

"Don't." Her voice came out rougher than she meant. "Don't do the thing where you say something gentle and I end up crying in a stairwell. I have a reputation. It's bad enough I bled on your floor."

"Then don't turn around," Emrys said. "Just listen. There's a verse in the Vessan liturgy—buried, nobody sings it anymore—that says every debt written in ink can be unwritten in ink. Every contract has a counter-word. Even theirs." A pause. "*Especially* theirs. I don't know the counter-word. But I know where the texts that might name it are kept, and I know that I am the only person alive with the keys, the boredom, and the moral flexibility to read them."

Wren turned around after all. Reputation be hanged.

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying no tonight," Emrys said. "I'm saying ask me again tomorrow, after I've read things I shouldn't. I'm saying—" and here he faltered, actually faltered, the quill-and-candle man, "—I'm saying I don't want your death in my handwriting, but I think I might hate your absence in my ledgers more. There's a blank line where your next order should be. I keep looking at it."

It was, Wren reflected later, the closest thing to a declaration of loyalty she'd heard in ten years of trusting no one. It was also possibly just scribe-speak for *you're good for business.* With Emrys you never could tell, and that was rather the charm.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "Ask me again tomorrow."

"Get stitched first. Madam Corvel, on Tanner's Lane. Tell her the bishop sent you."

"The bishop story again?"

"It's a good story. It holds up."

* * *

He waited until her footsteps faded down the stairwell, until the rain swallowed them entirely, before he let himself move.

Then Emrys Brightwater did the thing he had never done in two years of double-dealing: he opened his private ledger—not the client book, the *other* one, the one bound in grey leather that lived under a loose floorboard—and turned past pages of debts and favors and small corruptions to a clean page.

At the top, in his smallest, most careful hand, he wrote:

*Wren Talis.*

Not a client entry. No price. No service rendered. Just the name, sitting there on the page like a lit candle.

He looked at it for a long time.

Because here was the truth Emrys had discovered about himself tonight, and it frightened him more than any prophecy or contract or bleeding girl on his floor: he had said no, and meant it, and then immediately begun planning yes. His mind had been running the whole conversation on two tracks—the one that spoke and the one that schemed—and the scheming track had already listed what he'd need. Keys. The restricted archive. The third sub-vault, where the Choir kept the texts about the Hollow Court that even Archcantor Halden pretended not to know existed.

He had said no because it was true. He had started saying yes because it was truer.

"There's a blank line," he muttered to himself, disgusted. "I keep looking at it. Honestly. Who *says* that."

But he didn't cross the name out.

Instead, beneath *Wren Talis*, he added a second line, smaller still, almost hidden in the margin where he kept the thoughts he didn't charge anyone for:

*Find the counter-word. Whatever it costs.*

Outside, the rain kept up its grudge. Somewhere across the city, in a mountain full of dead roads, something ancient turned a page of its own ledger and noted, without surprise, that a scribe had begun to write.

And far below Thornfell, in a court where every borrowed name hung like a lantern, one lantern flickered—the shape of a girl who'd sold herself cheap—while in the dark beside it, something patient began, very quietly, to count the days until a certain scribe's name appeared where it had always been expected.

The prophecy, after all, had a date.

It was tomorrow.
