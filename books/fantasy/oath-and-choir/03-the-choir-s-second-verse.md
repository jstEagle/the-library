## 3. The Choir's Second Verse

The summons came at dawn, which was the first wrong thing.

Temple business in Saint Vess's Choir happened on a schedule as fixed as the tides. Matins at the fourth bell, copying at the fifth, the Archcantor's public address at noon. Nobody summoned anybody at dawn. Dawn was when the temple pretended to sleep so that its sins could get an early start.

Emrys Brightwater stood outside the Archcantor's study with ink still under his fingernails from the night's work—three maps of the Thornfell mine-roads, copied for a smuggler who paid in silver and never asked questions—and tried to look like a man whose conscience contained nothing heavier than breakfast.

"You may go in," said Brother Ossian, Halden's doorkeeper, with the particular gentleness reserved for men walking toward their own funerals.

The study smelled of beeswax and old vellum, and it was full of morning light, which Emrys had always considered a design flaw in a room where uncomfortable conversations happened. Archcantor Halden sat behind his desk like a well-fed hawk pretending to be a grandfather: white beard, kind eyes, hands folded atop a single sheet of paper.

"Scribe Brightwater." He smiled. "Sit. Please."

Emrys sat. His chair had been placed closer to the desk than furniture naturally arranged itself, close enough that he could read the paper upside down.

He wished very much that he hadn't been able to read it upside down.

It was the prophecy. Not his transcription—the one he'd copied two nights ago from the cracked folio in the restricted stacks, the one with tomorrow's date sitting beside a name that happened to be his own—but an older copy, yellowed, annotated in at least four different hands across what looked like decades.

*When the thief of small debts finds his name beside the date,* the old lines ran, *he shall go down beneath Thornfell, and either the soul returns to the living—or the mountain is sealed, and the debt is paid in stone.*

Someone had underlined the second option. Twice.

"Archcantor," Emrys said carefully, "I'm not sure why I've been—"

"Your hand is distinctive." Halden tapped the annotations. "That looping descender on the letter *y*. You've been told before you should moderate it. You never do." The smile stayed fixed, pleasant as a painted saint. "I have known your handwriting for eleven years, Emrys. I knew this transcription the moment it crossed my desk. Which was, incidentally, three days after you made it. We are not slow here. We are merely patient."

The floor did not open up and swallow him, though Emrys gave it a fair opportunity.

"You've known about the prophecy," he said.

"For years. Since before you were a novice." Halden rose and went to the window, hands clasped behind his back, silhouetted against the waking city. "Do you know what the Choir of Saint Vess actually sells, Emrys? Not hymns. Not blessings. Certainty. The city looks up at our spire and believes someone is keeping the accounts of heaven balanced. That belief is worth more than gold. It is worth more than armies. And every hundred years or so, something comes along that threatens to unbalance the ledger publicly." He turned. "This prophecy is such a thing."

"It's a scrap of verse in a cracked folio."

"It is a *date*, scribe. Tomorrow's date. In a text older than the temple's founding charter. Do you understand what happens if we ignore it and the mountain opens anyway? Or worse—if some smuggler girl's stolen soul walks back into the daylight and everyone learns the Hollow Court exists? That souls can be *sold*? That the Choir has known for a century and done nothing?" He shook his head sadly, a grandfather disappointed by the state of the world. "Panic. Riots. The end of certainty. Unless."

"Unless," Emrys repeated, because the man clearly wanted him to.

"Unless the second clause is fulfilled first. The sealing. If Thornfell is closed—permanently, ritually, with the soul inside—then the Court is buried beyond reach, the prophecy is fulfilled in the manner most convenient to us, and the Choir stands for another century as the institution that kept the dark dark." Halden returned to his desk and sat, folding his hands over the annotated page as if tucking it into bed. "The ritual requires a seal-song, a blood-right of entry, and forty days of preparation. We have the singers. We have the right—we have always had the right; why do you think the temple owns the mountain's deed? All we lack is discretion."

* * *

Here was the thing about Archcantor Halden that Emrys had learned in eleven years of copying his correspondence: he never threatened. Threats were crude. Threats created martyrs. Halden offered things instead—small, reasonable, beautifully wrapped things—and let you notice, all on your own, what refusing them would cost.

"Your debt," Halden said now, gently. "Fourteen years of service remaining. Am I correct?"

"Thirteen and a half," Emrys said. "I keep count."

"I know you do. You keep count of everything. It's one of your finest qualities." He drew a fresh sheet toward himself, dipped a pen, and began writing in a firm, flowing hand. "So here is what I propose. You will say nothing of what you transcribed. You will continue your duties as normal. And when the sealing is complete—when the mountain is closed and the matter settled—you will find your ledger cleared. Every mark of it. Freedom, Emrys. Full and unconditional. You could walk out those doors and never copy another line for anyone."

The words landed in Emrys's chest like coins dropped into a well. Thirteen and a half years. He had counted them the way other men counted scars. He had lain awake doing arithmetic on his own life: thirty-one minus thirteen was eighteen, and eighteen was young enough to start over, except starting over required money he didn't have and freedom he couldn't afford, and so the arithmetic always ended the same way, with him rolling over and going back to sleep.

And here it was. Offered. For silence.

"That's it?" he heard himself ask. "Silence, and I walk free?"

"That's it." Halden finished writing and turned the page around. It was a formal instrument of manumission, dated forty days hence, awaiting only a signature. "You'll notice I haven't asked you to do anything but be quiet. No perjury. No sabotage. The sealing will happen whether you speak or not—I merely thought you might prefer to be free when it does." He set down the pen and regarded Emrys with genuine, terrible warmth. "There will be a girl involved, I expect. There usually is. Some client of yours, some desperate creature who wants her soul fished out of the deep water. And you're wondering what happens to her." He sighed. "Emrys, I want to be honest with you, because honesty is cheaper now than it will be later. She sold her soul. That was her choice, made freely, for her brother's life—a noble choice, even. But choices have weight, and hers has already been weighed. The Court holds her contract lawfully. What the Court holds, the Court keeps. We are not stealing from her. We are simply declining to knock a hole in the world to fetch out what she signed away."

"And if she dies in there when the mountain closes?"

"The mountain is already closed, in every way that matters." Halden spread his hands. "We are only closing it more thoroughly."

* * *

Emrys walked back through the cloisters in a daze, and the terrible thing—the thing he would be ashamed of for a long time afterward—was how good the offer felt.

Not the freedom itself. He'd expected the freedom to glitter, and it did, but that wasn't the seduction. The seduction was smaller and meaner: it was the relief. The sudden, honey-sweet permission to stop carrying it. To be a scribe again, just a scribe, a man with clean hands and a cleared ledger, while other people did the heavy work of burying a living soul. Halden hadn't asked him to betray anyone. He'd asked him to stand still. And standing still was so easy. Standing still was practically restful.

*You could be free,* whispered the arithmetic in his head. *Eighteen years old again. Your own name, your own hours, no more contraband maps, no more bleeding smugglers on your doorstep.*

*And Wren.* He didn't even know her real name yet—he knew her as a client, a woman with a knife-scarred forearm who paid extra for discretion and flinched at bells. Whatever she'd sold, she'd sold it for love of her brother, and tomorrow's date was coming whether Emrys spoke or not, and if he stayed silent he wouldn't be innocent. He'd just be comfortable.

There was a difference. Halden's whole theology lived in that difference, and Emrys suddenly hated it with a clarity that felt almost physical.

He stopped in the middle of the cloister walk, in a square of morning sun, and took stock of himself the way he took stock of a ledger. On one side: freedom, safety, thirteen years of his life handed back. On the other side: a name in a prophecy he hadn't chosen, a woman he barely knew, and the plain fact that he had already broken half the Choir's rules so thoroughly that freedom granted by the Choir was freedom granted by people who didn't know him at all.

They thought they were buying his silence. They weren't. They were buying the silence of a man who didn't exist. The real one had been awake for three nights, copying maps of the mine-roads, and had already written one name into his private ledger in his own looping, unmoderated hand.

Still. Thirteen years.

Emrys stood in the sunlight a moment longer, letting himself feel the whole weight of what he was about to refuse, because he suspected he would need to remember exactly how heavy it was.

Then he went to find a smuggler.

Behind him, in the Archcantor's study, Halden watched the cloisters through his window, saw the young scribe pause in the light—saw him hesitate, truly hesitate—and smiled at the hesitation the way a moneylender smiles at a debtor counting coins. Hesitation meant the price had been understood. Understood prices could still be met.

He dipped his pen and added a note to the sealed orders already prepared for the hunters:

*If the scribe proves difficult, proceed without him. The prophecy names a thief. It does not require our cooperation—only our absence of objection. And see that the girl is identified before the descent. The sealing goes easier with the soul accounted for.*

Forty days, he wrote beneath it, then crossed it out.

No. Given the scribe's expression just now, he suspected he had less time than that. Perhaps far less.

Halden rang the little bell on his desk, and Brother Ossian appeared.

"Ready the hunters," said the Archcantor. "Tonight."
