## 4. A Company of the Unwanted

* * *

**Emrys**

The trouble with asking favors, Emrys had learned over nine years of careful anonymity, was that it required admitting you needed something. And needing something, in his experience, was the first step on the road to owing something, and owing something was how a temple scribe ended up copying contraband maps in a cellar that smelled of tallow and wet stone.

But the prophecy had not asked his opinion. It had simply written his name beside tomorrow's date, in letters a century old, and left him to do the arithmetic.

So he went looking for Brannoc.

He found him where the disgraced always gathered in Vessport: the Drowned Bell, a tavern built into the hull of a shipwreck, where the floor tilted just enough to make every patron look slightly drunk whether they were or not. Brannoc sat alone at the tilting table nearest the hearth, nursing a cup of small beer and a grievance in equal measure, his lute wrapped in oilcloth across his knees like a sleeping child he no longer trusted himself to hold.

They had met twice before—once when Emrys copied out a petition for him, once when Brannoc had wept on the scribe's shoulder about a woman whose name Emrys had tactfully forgotten. Neither occasion suggested the man would make a good soldier for a descent into a hollow mountain.

But neither occasion had involved the Court's songs.

"Brannoc," Emrys said, sliding onto the bench opposite. "I need to know if you remember them."

The musician's eyes were red-rimmed and quick. "Remember what."

"The songs. The ones you played at the Duke's winter court, before—" He stopped, because Brannoc flinched like a man touched with a hot iron. "Before you were dismissed."

"I didn't play them." Brannoc's voice dropped low, and the tilt of the floor seemed to lean in closer to listen. "That's the whole of my disgrace, scribe. I wouldn't play them. The old songs from under Thornfell—the ones the Duke's grandfather brought back as trophies. Three notes each, they say, and each song opens a different door down there. I told the court musicians' guild they were cursed, and the guild told the Duke I was mad, and the Duke told me to take my lute and my opinions somewhere they'd be appreciated less." He laughed, a dry, cracked sound. "Which is here, apparently. Where nothing is appreciated at all."

"You were right," Emrys said quietly. "They're real. I've read the accounts. And I need someone who knows all three."

Brannoc stared at him for a long moment. Then he looked down at the lute in its oilcloth wrappings, and Emrys watched something move behind the man's face—not hope exactly, but the place where hope had been amputated, aching in cold weather.

"They took everything from me for refusing those songs," Brannoc said. "My position. My rooms. My name in the guild rolls—they struck it out with a line so thick you can still see the groove in the page." His fingers curled around the lute's wrapping. "And now you come to tell me the songs are real and you want me to sing them anyway?"

"I want you to sing them *well*. There's a difference. One of them has to be sung without deceit, and I'm told that's harder than it sounds."

"It's impossible," Brannoc said. "No one can sing without deceit. Every note is a little lie—you hold it longer than true, you bend it sweeter than honest, because that's what music *is*, scribe. Beautiful lying."

"Then you'll have to find three minutes of honesty in your whole life. Can you manage that?"

Brannoc looked up, and for the first time there was something in his face besides grievance—a spark of the man who had once stood before a duke and said *no*.

"For that," he said slowly, "I'd need to know what we're singing toward."

"A door beneath Thornfell. A soul behind it. And a prophecy that says I'm the one who steals it back."

Brannoc was quiet a moment. Then he began unwrapping the lute.

"Well," he said. "At least this time, when they strike my name out, it'll be for something worth striking."

**Sister Ivet**

Sister Ivet had been praying in the same chapel for eleven years, which was remarkable considering she had been excommunicated for nine of them.

The Chapel of the Ninth Candle stood at the edge of the salt marshes, half-swallowed by fog, and no one came to it anymore except heretics, gulls, and the occasional lost fisherman who received, depending on Ivet's mood, either soup or a lecture. She kept the candles lit herself. Nine of them, one for each virtue the Choir had struck from its official doctrine, back when the doctrine was being written by men who found certain virtues inconvenient.

She was relighting the fourth candle—*Doubt, which is love asking questions*—when the door opened and a young man stepped in, shaking rain from his sleeves, with the particular posture of someone raised in a scriptorium: shoulders apologetic, spine guilty.

"Sister Ivet," he said. "I'm Emrys Brightwater. Of the Choir of Saint Vess."

"Then you're either very brave or very stupid, coming here. The Choir burned my books, you know. All forty-one volumes. I watched from the marsh road. It was quite a bonfire." She blew out the match and studied him. He had ink stains on three fingers and fear in his eyes, and she had learned long ago to trust the combination. Honest fear plus working hands meant a man in trouble for a reason. "What did they send you for? Conversion? Assassination? The last one was years ago, and honestly he was very polite about it."

"No one sent me. That's rather the problem." He glanced at the candles. "You wrote a commentary on the Prophecy of the Hollow Gate. Before they burned it."

"I wrote several things before they burned them. It was a productive decade."

"The prophecy has two clauses. Everyone remembers the first—the thief who steals back the stolen soul. But your commentary argued the second clause wasn't a warning. It was a *choice*. That sealing the mountain would be an act the prophecy merely records, not requires—and that whoever fulfills it gets to choose which clause comes true."

Ivet set down the candle-snuffer very carefully, because her hands wanted to shake and she refused to let them.

"That commentary cost me everything," she said. "My vows, my order, my name in the rolls of the faithful. I spent thirty years learning that doubt is a form of devotion, and they excommunicated me for teaching it. And now a Choir scribe walks into my chapel in the rain to tell me I was right?" She laughed, short and sharp. "Why? Why does it matter now?"

"Because tomorrow is the date in the prophecy. Because the soul in question belongs to a smuggler girl who sold it to save her brother. And because Archcantor Halden intends to fulfill the second clause—seal the mountain with her inside—and he offered me my freedom to keep quiet about it."

"And you came here instead of taking it."

"I came here because I couldn't stop thinking about your book. All those years in the archives, and yours was the only voice that ever sounded like it was telling the truth."

Ivet looked at him for a long moment—at the ink-stained fingers, the rain-wet hair, the terrible earnestness of him. Then she reached up and lit the fourth candle again.

"Doubt," she said, "which is love asking questions. Do you know why they struck that virtue from the doctrine, Emrys Brightwater? Because a person who doubts is a person who might choose. And institutions cannot abide choosers." She turned to face him fully. "I'll come with you. Not for you—for the choice itself. Thirty years I've waited to see whether the prophecy would prove me right, and I refuse to die in a marsh chapel without knowing."

"There may be dying involved regardless."

"There usually is," said Sister Ivet, and began gathering her books—or the four the Choir had never managed to find. "Fetch my boots from the alcove. If we're going to commit heresy together, we'll do it with dry feet."

**Wren**

Wren found Emrys at the harbor wall at dawn, which surprised her, because she had assumed he would run.

She'd known him for two years as the Scribe—faceless, hooded, the man who copied her charts and never asked where she got them. Two weeks ago she'd learned his name, and yesterday she'd bled on his floor and begged him to get her into a hollow mountain, and he'd said no in the gentlest voice she'd ever heard, which was somehow worse than shouting.

But here he was. At dawn. With a nun and a musician waiting behind him like the world's saddest parade, and a look on his face that said he'd rehearsed whatever he was about to say and hated every word of it.

"You changed your mind," Wren said. It wasn't gratitude in her chest. It was suspicion, sharp and bright, because gratitude got people killed in her trade.

"I did some thinking," Emrys said. "About what I owe you."

"You don't owe me anything. I paid you fair for every chart."

"That's not what I mean." He rubbed the bridge of his nose, and behind him the nun made a small impatient noise. "Wren. I said no because going down there will likely kill us all, and I thought if I refused kindly enough, you'd stay safely alive and hate me instead. But I read the contract terms last night. Your brother's debt doesn't end when you die—it transfers. The Court holds your soul *and* his life, and if you go down there alone, you lose both."

Something cold moved through Wren's chest. "You could have led with that."

"I could have led with a lot of things." He met her eyes, finally, and she saw the cowardice in him fighting the honesty and—this time—losing. "I lie, Wren. It's what I do. I lie to the Choir, I lied to you, I've been lying to myself for nine years that invisibility was the same thing as safety. I'm done. So I'm telling you the truth now, even though the truth is ugly: I don't know if we can win. I only know that Halden means to seal you inside that mountain, and I would rather die badly with you than live cleanly without."

Behind him, the nun snorted. "That was almost eloquent. Give him ten more years, girl, and he'll be dangerous."

Wren studied them all: the scribe with his guilty spine, the nun with her four forbidden books, the musician clutching his lute like a shield. Strangers. Damaged, disgraced, exiled strangers, every one of them unwanted somewhere, assembled on a harbor wall in the gray light like pieces of a machine nobody had finished designing.

It should have felt hopeless. Instead—she was horrified to discover—it felt almost like the crew she'd never managed to keep. People who stayed not because they were paid, but because they had nowhere better to be and something to prove.

"All right," she said. "But I'm navigating. And if any of you slows us down, I'm leaving you for the temple hunters. Consider it family."

"Family," said Sister Ivet approvingly, "is exactly that arrangement with better meals."

Brannoc tuned a string and said nothing, but his fingers had stopped shaking.

Emrys almost smiled. "There's one more thing," he said. "Last night, after you left, I checked the mine-roads ledger against the old survey charts. There's a way down through the flooded galleries that the hunters won't know—but it adds a day, and it passes close to something the old maps mark only as *the listening dark*."

Wren felt the hook of it sink in, cold and clean. "Close to what?"

"No one knows," Emrys said. "The surveyor who mapped it wrote one word in the margin and then never mapped anything again."

"What word?"

Emrys looked at her, and for the first time since she'd known him, the scribe smiled without any apology in it at all.

"*Awake,*" he said. "* * *
