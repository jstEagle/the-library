## 5. Down the Smuggler's Road

The mouth of the old mine-road looked, in the grey hour before dawn, like a wound that the mountain had never bothered to close.

Emrys stood at its edge with a lantern he had no right to own and a map in his head that he had no right to know, and told himself—firmly, in the tone he used on junior scribes—that this was simply another night's work. Longer than most. Deeper than most. But work.

Behind him, his company of the unwanted arranged themselves with all the grace of a dropped bookshelf. Brannoc was tuning a lute he'd sworn he wouldn't need, which meant he absolutely would. Sister Ivet had brought a satchel of heretical pamphlets "for morale," which Emrys suspected she intended to leave in strategic locations even underground. And Wren stood closest to the dark, arms crossed, looking at the mountain the way other people looked at old enemies across a market square.

"You've been down here before," she said. Not a question.

"I've *copied* things about down here," Emrys corrected. "There's a difference. One is dangerous. The other is merely a career."

"Uh-huh." She stepped past him into the dark without waiting for permission, which Emrys was learning was simply how Wren did everything.

He lit the lantern and followed, and the mountain swallowed them one by one.

* * *

The smuggler's roads beneath Thornfell were older than the temple above them, older than the town, older, if you believed Ivet's muttering, than most of the gods currently taking credit for the region. They had been cut by miners chasing silver, abandoned when the silver ran out, and reclaimed by men who preferred their commerce unrecorded—which, Emrys reflected, made them the only honest institutions left in Thornfell.

He knew them because he had spent four years copying their maps by candlelight for clients who paid in coin and never asked where a temple scribe learned to draw a tunnel so lovingly. He knew which passages flooded in spring. He knew where the timber shoring had rotted to lace. He knew the junction called the Widow's Elbow, where three roads met and two of them lied.

What he had not accounted for was traveling with people who talked.

"The acoustics," Brannoc said, for the fourth time, striking a soft chord against the stone. "Do you hear it? The mountain's holding the note. It wants to sing along."

"It wants us to be quiet," said Ivet.

"That too."

Emrys led them left at the first fork, then down a stair cut so narrow they had to go single file, Wren first, lantern light jumping along wet stone. The air grew warmer as they descended, which everyone agreed was wrong—the deep should be cold—and which nobody commented on further, because commenting felt like inviting an answer.

At the second hour, they heard the bells.

Faint, far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of rock: the Choir's morning bells, ringing Prime over Thornfell. Everyone stopped. Even Brannoc's hands went still on the strings.

"They'll have found my desk by now," Emrys said quietly. "The false ledger. The maps missing from the archive." He had left both deliberately, a trail of breadcrumbs pointing away from the mine-road, toward the river gate and the coast road. A lie, carefully constructed. He was good at those.

"And when they don't believe it?" Wren asked.

"Then they'll send hunters who know these tunnels better than I do." He smiled, because smiling was what you did when the alternative was admitting your stomach had dropped through the floor. "Fortunately, I'm very fast at copying things, and only moderately fast at walking."

Nobody laughed except Ivet, who laughed precisely because nobody else had, on the principle that fear unchallenged grows teeth.

They walked on.

* * *

It was at the third fork—the one marked on no map Emrys had ever copied, known only to him because a dying smuggler had once paid his debt in secrets instead of coin—that Wren caught him lying.

The passage ahead sloped down toward a sound: water, moving fast, somewhere below. There was a way around it. There was also a way through it, along a ledge over the underground river, faster by half a day—and narrower than a coffin, and slick with spray, and with a drop at its end that the smuggler, gasping out his last, had described with the enthusiasm of a man describing his favorite meal.

Emrys looked at the ledge. Then he looked at Wren, whose brother's soul sat in a ledger somewhere below them, and whose face, in the lantern light, held the particular patience of someone waiting for you to finish underestimating her.

"We'll take the long way," he said. "Around the river. It's safer."

"How much longer?"

"A few hours. Maybe more. The ground's uneven—" He gestured vaguely, and here is where he made his mistake: he gestured *confidently*, the way he gestured at junior scribes, the gesture of a man who knows exactly what lies ahead.

Wren looked at the gesture. Then at him. Then at the ledge.

"No," she said.

"Wren—"

"You just did the thing with your hand. The 'I definitely know this route' hand. You did it before the stairs too, and before the fork with the collapsed beam, and I let it go because we were moving and tired." She stepped closer, and up, because she was shorter and had clearly decided this would not stand. "You've been lying to us since the mouth of the road. Little ones. Comfortable ones. 'It's safer this way.' 'It's not far now.' 'The drop isn't that bad.'"

"I haven't—" Emrys began, and then stopped, because the reflexive denial arrived a full heartbeat after the knowledge that it was untrue, and Wren saw the heartbeat. She saw everything. That was, he was beginning to understand, simply how Wren did everything.

"The ledge," she said. "How bad?"

He could have lied again. It was right there, ready, smooth from use. Instead he heard himself say, honestly, wretchedly: "Bad. Four men died on it the year the shoring went. I know because I copied the coroner's ledger. Twice—one for the magistrate, once for the widow who wanted to know why."

The silence after that had weight. Somewhere below, the river kept its own counsel.

"Why?" Wren asked, and her voice wasn't angry anymore, which was somehow worse. "Why hide it? We're going into the Hollow Court to steal a soul back from a regent who wears dead men's faces. I can survive knowing a bridge is bad, Emrys. What I can't survive is finding out halfway across that the man leading me thought I couldn't take the truth."

"It's not that I think you can't take it." He set the lantern down on a flat stone, because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. "It's that kindness is easier. Telling someone 'it's fine' costs nothing. Telling someone 'four men died here and I'm asking you to cross anyway'—that costs. Every time. And I've spent four years learning that the cheap version keeps everyone calm and nobody asks questions, and questions are how scribes end up in front of Archcantors."

Brannoc, wisely, had become very interested in his lute. Ivet had become very interested in a pamphlet.

"So you're a coward," Wren said.

"Yes," Emrys said.

She blinked. Clearly she had expected a defense. Possibly she had been hoping for one, so she could enjoy dismantling it.

"But," he went on, because apparently honesty, once started, was difficult to stop, like a keg tapped at the wrong end, "I'd rather be a coward who tells you the truth than a brave man who lies to you. If those are the options. I've been the second kind for four years and it turns out the pay is terrible."

Something moved at the corner of Wren's mouth. It might have been a smile deciding whether to commit.

"Four men died on that ledge," she said. "And you're asking me to cross it anyway?"

"I'm asking all of us. I'll go first. If I fall, you'll know exactly how bad it is, which is more than I gave you an hour ago."

"That's the worst apology I've ever heard."

"It's the first one I've ever given. Give me time; I'm told repetition improves penmanship."

That did it. The smile committed. It was brief and sharp and gone again, but Emrys filed it away with the care he reserved for rare manuscripts.

"Fine," Wren said. "Truth inside the kindness from now on. Both together. That's the deal."

"That's the deal." He picked up the lantern. "Also, fair warning: the Hollow Court's outer galleries are said to echo, the regent collects faces, and I am, objectively, terrified."

"Better," said Wren, and stepped onto the ledge.

* * *

They crossed in single file, backs to the dripping wall, the river roaring below like a congregation that had finally been given permission. Brannoc sang—quietly, a working song, some dockside tune about a wife and a barrel and a regrettable goat—and the mountain carried the note down the dark ahead of them, and for a few minutes the crossing felt almost easy.

Almost.

Because at the far end of the ledge, where the passage widened into a gallery floored with old rail-ties, the lantern light caught something that stopped Emrys mid-step.

Wax. Fresh candle wax, still soft at the edges, pooled beside a stub of tallow burned down to nothing. And pressed into the wax, clear as a signature:

The seal of the Choir of Saint Vess.

"They're ahead of us," Ivet breathed. "Not behind. Ahead."

Emrys crouched, touched the wax, and felt the truth of it settle over him cold and complete. Halden hadn't taken the bait. Halden had never needed to chase. The Archcantor had known these roads for years—had probably helped cut half of them—and had sent his hunters down the *other* entrance, the one Emrys had never been told about, the one reserved for the Choir's own secrets.

Which meant the sealing party wasn't following them into the Court.

It was waiting for them there.

"Well," Emrys said, standing, and found—to his mild astonishment—that his voice came out steady. "I have some news. And this time I'm telling you all of it."
