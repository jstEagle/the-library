## 6. The Singing Gate

The gate was smaller than Brannoc had imagined, which was the first insult. He had spent three days of descent building it up in his mind—a cathedral mouth, a wall of black iron taller than the mine-shafts themselves, something worthy of the terror he was nursing in his chest like a hot coal he couldn't put down.

Instead it was a door. A plain, arched door of grey stone set into the mountain's root, no taller than two men and no wider than a cart. It had a lintel carved with running vines and, above them, a line of old notation—pitches scratched into the rock by someone who had loved music enough to leave it where only the desperate would find it.

"That's it?" Wren said. She said it with the particular flatness she reserved for things that had disappointed her personally.

"That's it," Emrys confirmed. He had his ledger out—he always had his ledger out—and was cross-referencing the notation against a page of cramped notes. "The Vessan rite calls it the Threshold of Honest Sound. Everything past this point is supposed to be heard truly."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning," Sister Ivet said mildly, from where she sat on a fallen beam rewrapping her blistered feet, "that the door doesn't open for flattery."

Brannoc looked at the notation again. He had been looking at it, on and off, since they'd stopped, because he already knew what it was. He'd known from the moment Emrys described the gate, three days ago, when there had still been time to invent an excuse and turn back. The carvings were a *confession-song*. An old form—the oldest form, from before the Choir standardized its music into something clean and safe. A confession-song wasn't a hymn. It was a wound set to melody. You sang the worst thing you had ever done, in full, without ornament, and the song either carried you or it didn't.

There was no way around it. There never had been. That was why Emrys needed him, and why Brannoc had come, and why he now stood in the cold breath of the mountain feeling like a man asked to undress in a market square.

"You all knew," he said. Not accusingly. Wearyingly. "You knew what the gate required, and you let me walk three days toward it."

"We didn't let you do anything," Wren said. "You're a grown man with excellent boots."

"Ivet knew," Brannoc pressed on. "Emrys knew. Did *you* know, Wren?"

Wren considered him with those sharp smuggler's eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "I figured it out around the second day, when you stopped humming. You've hummed every hour we've walked since Thornfell's edge. Musicians hum when they're comfortable. You went quiet." She shrugged. "I assumed you'd tell us when you were ready. Or not."

That was somehow worse than being ambushed. Brannoc turned back to the door because it was easier than turning back to them.

* * *

They made camp in the antechamber, because none of them wanted to sleep in the open tunnels with temple hunters somewhere behind—Ivet had heard bells at dusk, faint and wrong-pitched, and had said nothing about it until directly asked, which was very like her. Emrys built a small fire against all sense, arguing that if the hunters were close enough to see it, they were close enough to catch them regardless, and a man deserved warmth before the end.

Brannoc sat apart, tuning a lute that did not need tuning.

It was a beautiful instrument. That was part of the punishment, really. Court instruments were made by masters, and his had been made by Master Ovelin himself: rosewood and spruce, a neck worn smooth at exactly the places Brannoc's hands had worried it for eleven years. Eleven years of playing for the Duke of Carrowmere's court. Eleven years of being *the* voice of the great hall, the man whose songs dukes requested and whose approval young players begged for.

And one evening—one stupid, flattering, cowardly evening—that had ended.

He had told himself a hundred versions of the story over the years. In some of them, the Duchess had been scheming. In others, the Duke had known all along and used Brannoc as a test. In the version he told strangers, he'd been framed entirely, poor innocent artist destroyed by politics. But the true version was simpler and uglier: the Duchess had asked him to write her a song, and he had written her a love song, and he had sung it in the great hall knowing full well what it was, because he wanted the Duke to hear it. He wanted the whole court to hear it. He had been tired of singing other people's feelings and he had spent his own, carelessly, publicly, like a drunkard spilling coin, and he had not thought once about what it would cost the woman who inspired it.

She had been banished within the month. Brannoc had kept his post for another year before the shame of staying became worse than the shame of leaving. The Duke hadn't even punished him. That was the detail that ate at him in the dark hours. The Duke had simply stopped requesting songs. As if Brannoc's music had become, overnight, a thing that smelled of rot.

"Your strings are going to snap if you keep strangling them."

He looked up. Ivet had settled beside him with the soundless competence of a woman who had spent thirty years moving through rooms unnoticed. She held out a strip of dried apple. He took it mostly to have something to do with his hands.

"You've done confession before," he said. "In your order."

"Every week of my life, until they burned my order's books and called us heretics." She chewed thoughtfully. "Though our confessions were duller than yours will be. Sins of bread theft. Impure thoughts about the miller's son. Yours has a duchess in it."

"You know the story."

"Everyone in the capital knows the story. They just don't know it *true*, which is the difference between gossip and confession." She nudged him with her elbow, gently. "Here is what I learned, Brannoc, in thirty years of telling the truth to ceilings: the ceiling does not care. The relief isn't in being forgiven. It's in finally saying the thing out loud and discovering you are still standing afterward."

"And if I'm not?"

"Then we'll catch you. That's rather the point of company."

* * *

He performed at dawn, because Emrys said the gate's notation was keyed to first light—something about honest sound requiring honest shadow—and because if he waited any longer he would find a reason to wait longer still.

The others stood back near the fire. Even Wren, mercifully, said nothing. Emrys held the little lamp high so the light caught the carved notation above the lintel, and Brannoc stepped up to the door alone, lute in hand, and discovered that his hands were shaking badly enough that he had to press them flat against the stone to stop it.

The stone was warm. That surprised him. Everything else down here had been cold as a creditor's heart, but the door held a heat like a living body, faint and steady, as though something behind it slept and breathed.

"Don't think of it as performing," Emrys called softly. "Think of it as paying a toll."

"It's the same thing," Brannoc muttered. "Everything is the same thing. That's what I learned at court."

He tuned. He checked the notation one last time—it matched the opening phrase of the confession-form exactly, the old descending third that every child in the capital once learned alongside their prayers—and then he did the thing he had not done in eleven years of professional performance.

He decided not to be good.

Not careless. Not lazy. But he set down the whole apparatus of craft—the breath control, the polished phrasing, the instinct to make even grief beautiful—and left himself only the raw instrument. Because that was the trap of the confession-song, the reason so few could sing it: a skilled liar can make anything sound true. The gate would hear through beauty straight to the bone. If he decorated, he lied. If he made the Duchess's banishment tragic and lyrical, he was doing it again—spending someone else's pain for effect.

So he played the opening third plainly, almost ugly, and began.

"I wrote her a song," he sang, and his voice cracked on the second word, which no audience had ever heard him allow. "I wrote her a song and I meant it, and I sang it where he could hear."

The notes came out thin. His fingers found the chords the way a penitent finds his knees. He told it in order, because the form demanded order: the request, the writing, the long nights choosing each word to be unmistakable, the morning of the performance when he had dressed carefully and told himself it was art, art, only art.

"I knew what it was," he sang. "I knew, and I called it music, because music was the one place no one could accuse me. No one asks a song what it intends."

The warm stone under his palm seemed to pulse, very slightly, like a listening throat.

"I watched her face while I sang it. That's the verse I never tell. Everyone knows the ballad ends with the banishment, but I watched her face while I sang, and she was *afraid*, and I kept singing, because stopping would have confessed, and I wasn't ready to confess, so I made her afraid for four more minutes instead. Four minutes. I have counted them for eleven years."

His voice failed there. Actually failed—not the pretty theatrical crack of a performer working his material, but a real collapse, air gone, throat shut. He stood at the door with his hand on the warm stone and his lute hanging silent and thought, distantly, *this is where I die, at a small grey door, unable to finish.*

"Keep going," Wren said quietly, from the fire. Not kind. Just certain. "Finish it. Whatever it is, finish it."

So he finished it. He sang the last verses barely above a whisper, the confession proper: that he had loved the attention more than the woman; that he had never once asked her permission to make her his subject; that when she was banished he had felt, for one shameful flickering moment, *important*, because his song had moved a duke's household, had rearranged lives, had mattered—and then he had hated himself for that flicker every day since, and had told himself the hating was penance when it was only more attention, more of the same appetite wearing a hair shirt.

"I wanted to matter," he finished, "more than I wanted to be good. That is the whole of it. That is everything I am."

Silence.

Then the stone beneath his palm shivered, and the carved notation above the lintel began, very faintly, to glow—a pale gold rising along the vines like sap climbing a branch—and the door swung inward without a sound.

Warm air rolled out, smelling absurdly of beeswax and old paper, like a library that had been loved. And from somewhere deep inside, from far down the dark corridor beyond the threshold, came a sound that raised every hair on Brannoc's arms.

A slow, deliberate inhale. The kind of breath a large thing takes when it has been sleeping a long time and has just been sung to.

Somewhere in the Hollow Court, something opened its eyes.

"Oh," said Sister Ivet, into the silence. "It liked you."

"That," Brannoc managed, "is not comforting."

"No," Emrys agreed, stepping forward with his ledger clutched to his chest like a shield, his face lit gold by the doorway. "But it means we're expected."

Wren was already moving, knife drawn, walking toward the dark like it owed her money. Behind them, faint and far down the tunnels, a bell rang—wrong-pitched, closer than yesterday.

The hunters had found the antechamber.
