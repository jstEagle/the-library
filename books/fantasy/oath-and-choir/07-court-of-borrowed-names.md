## 7. Court of Borrowed Names

The gate closed behind them like a book snapping shut, and Wren thought—absurdly, hysterically—that it even sounded like a book. That soft *whump* of pages settling. Somewhere behind her, Brannoc was still wiping his eyes from the confession-song, and Sister Ivet had her hand pressed flat against the stone as if checking whether it breathed.

It did breathe. Wren could feel it now that they were inside: a slow, tidal pull in the air, in and out, in and out, like the whole mountain was asleep on its back.

"Everyone," Emrys said quietly, "stay close to me."

"You've been here before?" she asked.

"No." He adjusted the strap of his satchel—the one full of copied maps and stolen texts and, apparently, a destiny he'd neglected to mention for three days of walking. "But I've read about places like this. Ledgers everywhere. Don't sign anything. Don't say your name aloud. And whatever you do, don't accept a gift."

"What if someone offers me tea?"

"Especially tea."

Wren decided she liked him better when he was terrified. It made his face honest.

They walked down into the Court proper, and Wren's breath went out of her all at once.

She had imagined a dungeon. She had imagined chains, maybe, or fire, or some grand cathedral of evil with bones stacked into pillars. What she found instead was an office. A vast, endless, beautiful office—shelves rising out of sight into the dark, each one crammed with books and ledgers and scrolls, ladders on brass rails sliding between them, lamps burning with a light that cast no shadows. The air smelled of ink and candlewax and, faintly, of her mother's kitchen, which was so specifically cruel that Wren's eyes stung before she understood why.

"It's a library," Ivet whispered. "Saints preserve us, it's a *library*."

"It's a counting-house," Emrys said. His voice had gone thin. "Look closer."

So Wren looked closer. At the nearest shelf, she pulled a ledger free—a slim green volume, well-kept—and opened it to a marker of black ribbon.

Inside, in a hand so neat it looked printed, was a name. *Tomas Underhill, cooper.* Beneath the name, columns. Dates. Interest accrued. And beneath that, in a column labeled simply **Held**, a single word: *soul — condition good.*

Condition good. Like a barrel. Like a wheel of cheese.

Her hands were shaking when she put it back. "These are people."

"These are accounts," said a voice behind them, warm as fresh bread. "Though I prefer to think of them as guests. Long-term guests."

* * *

The regent wore her brother's face.

That was the first thing. Not a monster, not a shadow with teeth—just Dann, her Dann, twenty years old and grinning, except the grin sat wrong, like a coat buttoned by someone else. He stood at the head of a long table set with tea things (Emrys made a small strangled noise), and around the walls of the chamber hung faces—not masks exactly, not paintings—faces that watched with half-lidded patience. Faces Wren recognized. The ferryman who'd carried her south once. A temple cantor who'd died two winters past. An old woman from Thornfell village whose name she'd never known but whose face she'd seen at every market day of her childhood.

"He wore my brother's face when I signed," Wren heard herself say. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "You dressed yourself up as the thing I loved most, and you let me trade."

"I find it speeds negotiations," the regent said, with Dann's shrug, which was worse than any monster's roar. "Grief makes buyers generous. Please, sit. The tea is only tea—I promise you nothing is in it but tannin and bad decisions."

Brannoc laughed, high and unhappy. Nobody sat.

"Where is mine?" Wren asked. "My soul. My account."

The regent studied her with Dann's brown eyes, and behind those eyes something older turned over, slow as the mountain breathing. Then he smiled—Dann's smile, the gap-toothed one, the one she'd grown up following through orchard rows—and gestured to a side table where a single ledger lay open, weighted with a stone.

"There. As it happens, we were just discussing it."

She crossed the room because her legs decided before her fear could veto them. The ledger was fine paper, cream-colored, and there at the top, in her own handwriting—her actual handwriting, the slanted scrawl she used for cargo manifests—was her signature.

**Wren Ashvale. One (1) soul, entire, undivided. Received: the life of Dann Ashvale, restored and sustained. Term: perpetual.**

Below the signature, in the regent's neat printed hand, a valuation:

**Appraised value: three coppers' worth of sentiment, one debt of love, unpriced.**

Three coppers. She stared at it until the letters swam. She had crossed the sea twice, outrun a customs cutter, taken a knife in the shoulder over cargo worth less than this. She had haggled smugglers down from robbery to mere theft. And she had given away the entire, undivided, irreplaceable everything of herself for the price of a bad supper.

"I didn't know what I was signing," she said.

"You knew exactly what you were signing." The regent poured tea no one would drink. "That's what makes it binding. Coerced contracts can be voided; courts are fussy about coercion. But a girl who walks in with open eyes and signs anyway—" he clicked Dann's tongue, "—that's artistry. Yours, I mean. I merely provided the pen."

"Wren." Emrys had come up beside her, close enough that she could feel him trembling too, and there was strange comfort in that. "Don't listen to him. He inflates guilt the way merchants inflate prices. It's how he keeps everyone too ashamed to argue."

"Your scribe is learning," the regent said pleasantly. "Yes, Brightwater, do defend her. You're rather central to today's business, though perhaps not in the way she thinks."

Wren looked up sharply. "What does that mean?"

"It means," the regent said, setting down the teapot, "that your soul is the least interesting thing in this room. Oh, I'll keep it—it's collateral, and collateral is comfort—but I never wanted it. A smuggler's soul, even a brave one? Common stock. Half my shelves are smugglers. No offense."

"Some taken," Wren said.

"But a name freely given—" The regent's voice changed then, and for one moment Dann's face couldn't hold it, stretched around something vast and hungry, and every hanging face on the walls opened its eyes at once. "A name surrendered without force, without trickery, without price demanded. Do you know how rare that is? Everything in this Court was traded. Bought. Won. Every soul on these shelves came to me through want or fear or love twisted sideways. But a *gift*—" he spread Dann's hands, helpless, covetous, "—a gift cannot be owned. And yet everyone wants to try. Especially your temple friends, who have been trying for a hundred years."

"The prophecy," Emrys said. His voice had gone very quiet.

"The prophecy," the regent agreed, delighted. "Thief of souls, breaker of oaths, the one who comes to take back what was sold. Your Choir has read that verse nightly for a century, Brightwater, praying you into existence. They didn't send you here to save this girl. They sent you because a prophecy needs fulfilling, and fulfilled prophecies are worth more than souls. And when you stand before me and offer your name to buy hers—" he leaned forward, Dann's grin gone wide and wrong, "—I will take it. Freely given. The one thing in creation I cannot hold, and I will hold it anyway, because I am patient, and greed is a kind of faith."

The lamps guttered. On the shelves, somewhere far off in the dark, ten thousand ledgers rustled like leaves in wind—or like pages turning toward a marked date.

Wren grabbed Emrys's sleeve. "Whatever he just said—whatever plan is forming behind your stupid careful face—you don't give him anything. You hear me? We came for my soul, not to feed him yours."

"We came for your soul," Emrys agreed gently. "And I intend to leave with it."

"And you?"

He didn't answer. He was looking at the regent with that expression Wren had learned to hate on the road down—the one where kindness and calculation wore the same coat.

"That's what I thought," she said. "You're going to lie to me again. Or worse—you're going to tell me the truth and expect me to stand aside while you do something noble and idiotic."

"I would never expect you to stand aside," Emrys said. "I've seen you with customs men."

Despite everything—despite the hungry dark and her brother's stolen smile and the ledger that priced her heart at three coppers—Wren laughed. It came out cracked, but it was hers.

Above them, deep in the mountain, a bell began to toll. Slow. Heavy. Familiar.

Sister Ivet appeared in the doorway, gray-faced. "Choir horns," she said. "At the gate. They're not waiting for the prophecy anymore—they're coming to finish it."

The regent poured himself a cup of tea at last, wearing her brother's face, and smiled.

"Well," he said. "Right on schedule."
