## 8. Halden's Hand

The Hollow Court did not echo. That was the first thing Emrys had noticed upon entering it, and the thing he still could not stop noticing: every word they spoke fell into the dark and simply stayed there, as though the Court were a ledger itself, recording them.

Now their words were being recorded by something far less abstract.

They had come to the Hall of Entries—a vault of shelves that climbed past the reach of any lamp, each shelf stacked with contracts bound in leather the color of dried blood. Wren stood before hers. She hadn't touched it in an hour. Brannoc sat on the floor with his back against a shelf, picking silent notes on a lute that no longer made sound down here; Sister Ivet was reading a contract two rows over with the expression of a woman grading scripture.

Emrys was copying.

He couldn't help it. The regent's clerks—things that might once have been men—had left inkwells unattended, and the ledgers here contained a script he'd only seen in fragments: covenant-hand, the old binding alphabet, written in a flowing hand that made even damnation look elegant. His fingers itched. A scribe's curse, Wren called it. *You see writing and you have to know what it says, even when knowing is the worst thing you could do.*

"You're doing it again," she said without turning around.

"Doing what?"

"The face. The one where you're committing theft with your eyes."

"I am *cataloguing*," Emrys said with dignity. "Theft would imply I intend to take something."

"You intend to take everything. You've been planning to take everything since the mine-road." She turned then, and her face was pale in the lamplight—not frightened, exactly. Frightened was a fire you could run from. This was something colder. "I keep thinking I should be used to it by now."

"Wren—"

Whatever he might have said was interrupted by the sound every one of them knew too well: boots. Many boots, in disciplined rhythm, coming up out of the dark of the smuggler's road behind them.

Brannoc's hand closed over his silent strings. Ivet shut her contract with a soft, terrible click. And Emrys felt the strange, suspended calm of a man who has been waiting for bad news so long that its arrival feels almost like relief.

"They found the gate," Ivet said. "Of course they found the gate. Halden has known about the gate for thirty years."

"He didn't need to find it," Emrys said quietly. "He needed us to open it. The gate opens to truth, remember? And the Choir has never once told the truth in its history."

The torchlight came first, spilling yellow around the corner of the shelf-row, and then the hunters came—six of them in the grey-and-gold of Saint Vess, crossbows slung, warded blades at their belts—and behind them, unhurried as a man entering his own garden, walked Archcantor Halden.

He looked older than he had a week ago. Or perhaps Emrys had simply never looked closely before, and now saw what proximity revealed: the fine cracks around the eyes, the way the Archcantor's gaze moved across the Hall of Entries not with fear or wonder but with *appraisal*, like a merchant pricing a warehouse.

"Master Brightwater," Halden said warmly. "And company. You've saved me a great deal of trouble."

"Halden." Emrys stepped forward, putting himself between the Archcantor and Wren, an old instinct wearing new clothes. "How did you get through the gate?"

Halden smiled. It was a good smile. It had opened a hundred doors for the Choir and closed a thousand more. "The gate opens to truth, boy. I told it the truth. *I want to come in, and I will pay any price that costs me nothing.*" He spread his hands. "It seems the gate has a sense of humor."

Behind Emrys, Brannoc made a small wounded noise—the sound of a musician hearing a beautiful chord played wrong on purpose.

"You're not here to bring us back," Emrys said.

"No." Halden walked the aisle slowly, trailing one finger along the contract-spines the way a bishop might trail a finger along a pew. "I'm here to finish the work. The prophecy has two clauses, Brightwater. You were so busy reading your own name in the first that I don't think you ever truly read the second. *And the mountain shall be sealed, and the debt within it kept forever.* A century of influence bought at the price of one soul. The Choir's elders made this bargain before either of us was born. I'm merely the honest man who intends to honor it."

"You call that honesty?"

"I call it bookkeeping." Halden turned to the darkness beyond the shelves and raised his voice, courteous as a dinner guest. "Regent! We should talk terms!"

* * *

The regent came the way weather comes.

There was no door. There was simply a place where the shadows between the shelves deepened and then gathered and then stood up, and where a face should have been there were faces—dozens of them, overlapping like leaves, shifting slowly. Emrys recognized some of them. He recognized all of them, actually, because he had copied their names: the debtors of Thornfell, the smugglers and miners and failed merchants who had sold pieces of themselves across four hundred years. The regent wore its customers the way other men wore rings.

"*The Choir speaks,*" the regent said. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way the Court swallowed echoes and gave back only this. "*At last. Four centuries I have waited for the temple to descend and treat as equals.*"

"Equals." Ivet spat the word like a seed. "You wear dead faces and call it diplomacy."

"I wear *paid-for* faces," the regent said, and one of those faces—it belonged, Emrys realized, to a girl no older than Wren—smiled briefly, horribly, with the regent's amusement. "*Payment makes equals, little sister. Ask your Church. They invented the arithmetic.*"

Halden bowed, shallow but genuine. "Then let's speak plainly. My predecessors promised the Choir another century of influence if the mountain stays sealed with the debts inside. I'm prepared to deliver more than my predecessors did. The Choir will seal the gate from above—with consecrated stone, with wards my order has spent generations perfecting. Nothing leaves. Nothing enters. Your collection becomes untouchable. In exchange—" he gestured, almost apologetically, toward Wren, "—that one remains. Her contract stands. The sealing requires a soul held at the moment of closure. Tradition demands it. I'm sure you understand tradition."

"I understand *inventory*," the regent said. "*Yes. Leave the girl. The bargain pleases me.*"

"There we are." Halden turned to Emrys with the air of a man concluding a tedious errand. "You see? Everyone reasonable gets what they want. You'll come home, Brightwater. Your debts forgiven—all of them, the copying, the contraband, the smuggling, the lot. A fresh page. Freedom. Isn't freedom what all this has been about?"

And there it was. The offer he had wanted for eleven years, laid out on a silver tray in a hall of damned souls, and Emrys found—to his genuine surprise—that it weighed nothing at all. He examined it the way he examined a forged signature: turned it over, checked the hand, and found the flaw immediately.

"It's a lie," he said. "Not yours. Mine. I'd be lying if I took it, and the gate would know, and I'd deserve what it did to me." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "Do you know, I spent years thinking invisibility was safety. Copying in the dark, keeping my name off every page, being nobody so nobody could touch me. And then a date showed up beside my name in a century-old prophecy, and I thought that was the worst thing that could happen." He looked at Wren. At Brannoc, watching him with his ruined musician's face. At Ivet, who nodded once, slow and fierce. "But the worst thing was thinking that staying invisible was the same as being free."

"Pretty," said Halden. "Irrelevant. Seize him."

The hunters moved. And Emrys did the only thing a scribe could do against six armed men in a hall full of books.

He reached into his coat and pulled out his ledger.

Eleven years of it. Every contraband map, every forbidden text, every name copied in secret for coin—his whole double life, bound in cracked calfskin, heavy as a brick and twice as damning. He'd carried it down the mountain because he hadn't been able to imagine leaving it behind. Now he understood: he had carried it because he'd still believed, somewhere under everything, that the ledger was him. That the debts *were* him.

"Master Brightwater," Halden warned, "that book is Choir property."

"No," Emrys said. "It never was. That was always the problem."

He had no flint. He didn't need one. Sister Ivet pressed something into his hand—a stub of blessed candle, lit, heretic-fire, the kind that burned whatever it was pointed at and asked no permission—and Emrys touched flame to the corner of his ledger and held it up so the whole hall could watch.

The fire caught greedily. Eleven years went up in light.

"Emrys Brightwater owes nothing," he said clearly, into the silence that swallows echoes, so that every soul in the Court heard it—including, he suspected, the ones wearing the walls. "No debt to the Choir. No debt to the dark. Whatever I do next, I do it freely, and freely given things can't be collected." He dropped the burning book at Halden's feet, where it lay guttering like a fallen star between them. "That includes my name. Write that down. I know you'll want to. You won't be able to."

The regent's borrowed faces all turned toward him at once—every mouth, every eye, four hundred years of the bought and the sold—and something in that vast attention shifted, the way a ledger shifts when an entry refuses to balance.

"*Freely given,*" the regent murmured, in a dozen stolen voices. "*No power can hold a gift. Who taught you that, scribe?*"

"A friend," Emrys said. "She's standing right there, and she's leaving with me."

Halden stared at the ashes of the ledger, and Emrys watched something happen in the Archcantor's face that frightened him more than the regent had: not anger. Calculation. The look of a man whose plan has just acquired a flaw worth more than the original design.

"So that's how it works," Halden said softly. "*Freely given.* Then I don't need the girl at all, Brightwater. I need you to give."

And behind him, the six hunters raised their warded blades, and the regent began, very gently, to laugh with everyone's mouth at once.
