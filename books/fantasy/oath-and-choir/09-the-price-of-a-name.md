## 9. The Price of a Name

The ledgers of the Hollow Court did not sit on shelves. They hung in the air like lanterns, hundreds upon hundreds of them, each one glowing faintly with the light of the soul it recorded. Emrys had spent his whole life among books, and he had never once been afraid of them. Now he understood that he had simply never met a book that was hungry.

Wren stood beside him, her hand pressed flat against the contract that bore her signature — a scrawl she didn't remember making, made anyway, in ink the color of a winter bruise. The regent drifted toward them across the vaulted dark, wearing the face of Wren's brother Tam. It smiled with Tam's crooked grin and none of Tam's warmth.

"You've come all this way," it said, "to argue bookkeeping."

"I've come to correct an entry," Emrys said.

Behind him, he heard Brannoc's breath catch, and Sister Ivet murmur something that was either a prayer or a curse — with Ivet it was usually both. Halden stood at the far end of the chamber with the temple hunters, their white cloaks ghost-pale in the ledger-light, and his voice came smooth as oiled wood: "Emrys. Step away from the girl. The Choir has already negotiated terms."

*Terms.* The word landed in Emrys's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He thought of the Archcantor's study, the offer of freedom, the gentle suggestion that some souls were simply the price of a century's peace. He had almost said yes. That was the part that shamed him most — not that Halden had offered, but how easily Emrys had *understood* the offer, the way a lock understands a key.

"No," Emrys said. It came out small. He cleared his throat and said it again, louder, the way you'd raise your voice over a congregation. "No. You don't get to negotiate with her soul. Nobody does. Not you, not the regent, not me."

"Then what," the regent said, tilting Tam's borrowed head, "do you imagine you have to trade? A scribe's wages? Your little ledger of smugglers' debts?" It gestured, and the air filled with pages — Emrys's private ledger, torn from his satchel, its entries fluttering like startled birds. Every name he'd ever copied for coin. Every secret he'd kept to stay invisible. "I know everything you've sold, scribe. You've been selling pieces of yourself for years. You simply called it survival."

"That's true," Emrys admitted, and his voice shook, but he let it shake. Wren had taught him that much on the mine-road: hiding truth inside kindness was still hiding. "I have sold things. Cheaply. Badly. I'm rather an expert on bad bargains, actually, which is why I can spot yours."

The regent's smile flickered.

"A gift," Emrys said, "cannot be owned."

He had found the line three nights ago, copying the prophecy by candlelight, in the margin where some long-dead annotator had written in a cramped, desperate hand: *What is given freely cannot be held. What is held was never given.* He had copied it without understanding, the way he copied everything. Now he understood it the way you understand a rope when you're hanging from one.

"My name is Emrys Brightwater." He said it plainly, no flourishes, the way his mother had said it over a cradle in a house that no longer existed. "Son of Maren Brightwater. Scribe of the Choir of Saint Vess. Debtor. Coward, on at least four occasions I could name, and probably more if I sat down with a quill and honest ink. And I give it to you — freely. No coercion. No debt. No price asked or paid. Take my name, and void her contract."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the hanging ledgers seemed to lean in.

"Do you understand what you're offering?" the regent said, and for the first time its voice wasn't amused. It sounded almost — hungry wasn't right. Hungry it always was. *Eager.*

"Better than you do," Emrys said. "You collect names that were *sold*. Sold names are heavy. They drag. They fight the ledger every day of eternity, because nobody sells their name unless they're tricked or starving, and some part of them knows it. But a gift—" he took a breath— "a gift comes clean. There's nothing to hold onto. You can't own what was never traded. You can only carry it. And carrying it will be like carrying water in your hands."

"He's bluffing," Halden said sharply, from across the chamber. "Regent, ignore him. The Choir offers you the mountain sealed, the girl inside, a century of tithes—"

"Yes, do take Halden's deal," Brannoc called out, and there was a wobble in his voice that meant he was terrified and doing the thing anyway, which was, Emrys was learning, simply what this company did. "Everyone loves a man who sells other people's souls while standing safely behind them."

"Hush, Brannoc," Ivet murmured. "Let him work."

But Emrys wasn't working anymore. He had done all the work there was to do, walking down the mine-roads, lying about dangers, being called a coward by a smuggler girl who saw through him like glass — and then telling the truth instead, once, twice, until truth started to feel less like falling and more like flying badly. All that remained was the giving.

"Wren," he said, turning to her. Her eyes were wet and furious, which he had learned was her way of saying nearly anything important. "This isn't a sacrifice. I want to be clear about that, because you'll try to argue with me otherwise, and you're very good at arguing. This is arithmetic. One name, freely given, against one soul wrongly taken. It's the best trade I'll ever make."

"You absolute *scribe*," Wren whispered.

"I know."

"It won't hold," the regent snarled, and now the borrowed face of Tam was sliding, melting like wax, unable to keep its shape under the strain of wanting. "Names are currency. Currency is power. Power is *mine*—"

It reached for him. Emrys felt it take hold — felt the name lift out of him like a splinter drawn from a wound, and strange, strange, it didn't hurt at all. It felt like setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was carrying. For thirty years he had been Emrys Brightwater, invisible, indebted, careful. The name rose out of him and hung in the regent's grasp, glowing soft as a candle flame.

And the ledgers began to unravel.

It started small. A page near the ceiling curled and went dark, its soul-light guttering out — freed, somewhere far away, into whatever waits for souls that are finally let go. Then another. Then ten. The regent clutched Emrys's name in both hands and *pulled*, trying to make it a possession, a purchase, an entry — and the name would not become any of those things. It slipped through the regent's fingers like water, like song, like morning. Every attempt to grip it tore a hole in the ledger that held it, and every hole tore holes in the ledgers around it, because a court built on ownership has only one foundation, and gifts dissolve foundations.

"What are you *doing*," the regent shrieked, its form rippling through a hundred stolen faces — Tam, a fisherman, an old woman, a child — none of them holding. "Stop it. STOP IT—"

"You can't hold it," Emrys said, and heard his own voice go strange and light, lighter than it had been since childhood. "That's the whole point. I told you it would be like carrying water. I *did* warn you. I'm a scribe; we always note the fine print."

Across the chamber, Halden had gone pale beneath his white cloak. "Seal it!" he shouted to the hunters. "Seal the vault NOW, before—"

But the hunters were looking at the unraveling ledgers, and at the souls streaming free through the holes like sparks up a chimney, and at their Archcantor, who had offered a girl's soul as a bargaining chip not five minutes past. One of them — a young hunter with a freckled face Emrys vaguely remembered from the scriptorium — quietly lowered her sealing-stone to the ground and stepped back from it.

Halden lunged for the stone himself.

And the Court, dying, did what courts do with greedy men. The floor opened — not dramatically, not with fire, just a seam of darkness opening beneath the Archcantor's feet like a ledger accepting one final entry. Halden grabbed at a hanging contract to stop his fall, and the contract wrapped around his wrists, and another around his ankles, and another, patient pages binding the man who had tried to buy a century with a girl's soul.

"No," Halden said. "No — I am the Archcantor of Saint Vess — I am owed—"

"*Owed*," said the regent, in a voice grown vast and thin as it came apart, "is our favorite word."

The last thing Emrys saw of him was Halden's furious, frightened face sinking into the dark, wrapped in contracts of his own negotiating, sealed not by prophecy or by stone but by the simple weight of everything he'd tried to keep.

* * *

Afterward there was silence, and falling dust, and Wren's fist connecting solidly with Emrys's shoulder.

"Ow," he said.

"You gave away your NAME."

"I did mention the arithmetic."

"You mentioned *nothing*, you insufferable, noble, *illiterate*—" She ran out of insults, which Emrys suspected was a first, and settled for grabbing the front of his robe with both hands, glaring up at him from a foot away. Around them the great ledgers were dissolving into drifting motes of light, and through the widening gaps in the dark overhead Emrys could see stars — real ones, Thornfell's night sky, coming apart like a curtain. "Do you even remember who you are?"

Emrys considered the question honestly, which was, after all, the year's great project.

"No," he said. "Isn't it wonderful?"

And then, because the moment seemed to ask it of them both, Wren laughed — a real laugh, cracked and wet and furious — and pulled him into a hug that knocked what remained of his dignity entirely loose. Behind them, Brannoc was weeping openly and pretending it was dust, and Ivet was gathering the fallen sealing-stone with reverent hands, muttering, "Well. *That's* a relic."

The last ledger in the chamber — the old one, the prophecy, its final line still blank — drifted down between them and lay open on the floor. Emrys looked at the empty space where the ending should have been.

Wren followed his gaze. "So what happens now? If the line's blank?"

Emrys smiled, and reached for the quill at his belt out of thirty years of habit, and found — nothing there. Nothing at all. No name, no debt, no ink that belonged to him.

"Now," he said, "we write it."

Above them, the mountain groaned once, deeply, like a sleeper turning over — and began, gently, to open.
