## 10. Choir of the Free

*Wren*

The light at the end of the mine-road was not gold. Wren had expected gold — the way stories promised it, a door of shining air and trumpets. Instead it was gray and ordinary and smelled of rain on stone, and she loved it more than anything she had ever seen.

She climbed out of Thornfell into morning.

Not dawn. Morning. The sun already up and doing its honest work over the valley, gulls arguing over something by the river, smoke rising from chimneys in the town below like the world had gone on without her and hadn't even had the decency to apologize. Her hands shook. She let them. Shaking was what living hands did.

Behind her, the mountain rumbled once — a long settling groan, like an old man turning over in bed — and then went quiet in a way mountains rarely bothered with. The sealing Halden had begun collapsed inward on itself, stone folding around the Archcantor's ambition and closing like a fist. No one spoke of it. There would be time for grief later, or perhaps there wouldn't, and either seemed survivable now.

"You're leaking," Brannoc said beside her.

She touched her cheek. Tears. She'd forgotten they worked that way without the soul's ledger to tax them.

"Leaking," she agreed, and laughed, and it came out cracked and ugly and completely hers.

Her brother was waiting at the treeline.

Tam looked older than his nineteen years ought to allow — thinner, watchful, with the particular stillness of someone who has spent two years being alive *because of* something terrible and knows it. He'd been told nothing except *come to the mountain, bring no one, trust the scribe*. He was gripping a walking stick like a weapon and scanning the treeline for threats.

Then he saw her.

He didn't run. That was the thing Wren noticed first, the thing she would carry: he stood absolutely still, as if moving might break whatever was happening, as if his sister walking out of a haunted mountain in daylight was a debt he couldn't afford to wake from.

"Say something," Tam said finally. His voice broke on the second word. "Because I have imagined this about four hundred times and in none of them can I remember what I'd say."

"You look awful," Wren managed.

"So do you."

"I've been underground for three days fighting a regent made of other people's faces. What's your excuse?"

And then he did run, and she did too, and the collision knocked them both into the wet grass, and Tam was saying her name over and over like he was paying off a loan — *Wren, Wren, Wren* — and she was holding him hard enough to bruise because she could. Because her arms were hers, her tears were hers, and somewhere under Thornfell a ledger had burned to ash with her soul written out of it.

After a long while Tam pulled back and frowned at her with the exact expression their mother used to wear.

"You sold your *soul*," he said. "To save *me*. You absolute idiot."

"The paperwork was very clear," Wren said. "There was no clause about idiocy."

"There should have been." He hugged her again, fierce. "I'm going to be angry at you for approximately the rest of my life."

"Get in line."

Emrys stood a little apart, watching them, wearing the expression of a man who has just remembered families exist and is trying to decide whether he's allowed one. Wren caught his eye and jerked her head: *get over here.* He hesitated — of course he hesitated, he hesitated the way other people breathed — and then came, awkward as a heron, and Tam stuck out his hand, and Emrys shook it with the solemnity of a man signing a treaty.

"She talks about you," Tam said. "In letters. Well — one letter. Well — half a letter, the rest was directions to a fence who buys stolen temple wine—"

"*Tam.*"

"—but the half-letter was very complimentary."

Emrys went pink to the ears. Wren decided to let him live.

*Ivet*

Sister Ivet had been expelled from the Order of Saint Vess eleven years ago for the crime of asking questions during homily. She had kept a tally, in all that time, of how many questions the Church answered honestly: zero. She had assumed the number would hold until her death.

It took the fall of the Hollow Court four days to change it.

They found the prophecy's original scroll in Emrys's satchel, water-stained and singed but legible, and Ivet — because old habits die screaming — read it aloud as they made camp above the valley. Nine lines. Nine fulfilled. And the tenth:

*And the last line is unwritten, and waits.*

"It's blank," Brannoc said, peering over her shoulder. "That's not a prophecy, that's a *draft*."

"That," said Ivet, "is the most honest sentence any holy text has ever contained."

She thought about it through supper. She thought about it through the night watch, which she shared with Wren, who slept sitting up against a tree with her brother's cloak over her shoulders and woke every hour to check he was still breathing. She thought about it while the others argued in the morning over whether Emrys's namelessness meant he needed a new name immediately (Brannoc lobbied hard for "Ox," on grounds Ivet chose not to examine).

By the third day she knew what she was going to do, which was unprecedented, and told them over breakfast.

"I'm founding an order," she announced.

Brannoc choked on his tea. "You're a *heretic*."

"I'm a nun with a blank line." Ivet spread her hands. "Eleven years ago they threw me out for questions. Fine. I'll start a house where questions are the liturgy. No relics, no tithes, no sealed mountains. We write our own last lines. Every member gets one, and no one else fills it in for them."

"And you'll call this...?" Wren asked, amused.

"The Order of the Unwritten Verse." Ivet had workshopped it. She was not ashamed. "We take no vows except honesty. Our first rule is that we tell each other hard truths, especially when kindness would be easier."

"That sounds miserable," Brannoc said.

"It is," said Emrys quietly, from across the fire. "It's also the only thing worth belonging to."

Ivet looked at him — the nameless scribe, the oath-breaker, the man who had traded everything and come out owning more than the Choir ever gave him — and felt the old question rise, the good kind, the kind that built things.

"Well then," she said. "Founding members. Who's in?"

To her lasting surprise, everyone raised a hand, including the smuggler girl, including the brother, including — after a long pause and a mutter about *never having wanted to sing in a cathedral anyway* — Brannoc.

* * *

*Brannoc*

The first song Brannoc sang after Thornfell was in a tavern in Lowmarsh, on a night he'd swear afterward he hadn't planned.

He had not sung in six years. Not properly. He'd hummed, he'd played accompaniment for better voices, he'd taught children scales in exchange for soup. But singing — singing required a voice, and a voice required a self, and Brannoc had spent six years convinced his self was forfeit, spent on one cowardly night at the Court of Saint Vess when he'd sung a lie so beautiful the whole hall wept and a true thing died unspoken behind it.

But the gate beneath Thornfell had opened to a confession-song. He had stood in the dark with four people who had every reason to despise him and sung the worst night of his life, note by note, and the mountain had listened, and none of them had left.

So when the tavern keeper called for a song and the room went quiet, Brannoc stood up before the fear could catch him.

He sang the confession again. Not the gate-version, stripped for function. The full version, with the melody he'd been forbidden to give it, the one that made the shame into music instead of just a wound. Halfway through, his voice cracked, and he kept going, because that was the whole lesson of the Hollow Court: the crack is where the truth gets in.

When he finished, the tavern was silent for three full seconds — an eternity, in tavern terms — and then a woman near the fire began to cry openly, and an old soldier bought him a drink, and a boy of maybe twelve pushed through the crowd and said, breathlessly, "Where do you learn to sing like *that*?"

"Underground," said Brannoc. "From a disgraced nun and a nameless scribe and a smuggler with a brand-new soul. It's a small school. Terrible food. Best education available."

"Is it taking students?"

Brannoc looked at the boy — at the hungry, hopeful, unruined face of him — and felt something in his chest unlock that six years of silence had rusted shut.

"Ask Sister Ivet," he said. "She handles admissions. Fair warning: the entrance exam is telling the truth about yourself, and nobody's ever passed on the first try."

*Emrys*

Emrys did not sleep well for a month. This surprised no one.

He woke at odd hours reaching for a name that wasn't there, the way a man reaches for a tooth that's been pulled. *Emrys Brightwater* was gone — given away, freely, to a regent made of borrowed faces, and the giving had unraveled the Court's ledgers like a pulled thread, and the regent had tried to hold what was never sold and failed, and the failure had been the end of it. A gift cannot be owned. He had learned it in the oldest classroom in the world, under a mountain, at the price of everything the Choir had ever promised him.

He did not regret it. That was the strange part. Some mornings he woke grieving the name and smiling anyway, both at once, like weather.

What he had instead was this: a rented room over a chandler's shop in Lowmarsh, a table, a lamp, a pot of ink, and a new ledger — plain paper, no temple seal, no debt-clauses hidden in the margins. He had bought it with the last coins of the money the smugglers had paid him for maps back when his life had been two lives stitched together badly.

He sat down on the first evening and opened it to the first page and held the pen over the ruling for a long time.

A ledger needs entries. That was the whole nature of the thing. Debts owed, debts paid, names bound to obligations. He had copied ten thousand of them in the Choir's scriptorium, each one a small elegant cage.

His pen touched the paper.

*Debts forgiven,* he wrote, in a careful hand, and ruled a line beneath it, and left every page after that blank.

It was the shortest ledger in the history of accounting. He suspected it would also be the hardest to keep.

A knock at the door — the specific knock, two quick and one slow, that Wren had invented on the road down and refused to explain. He opened it to find all four of them crammed onto the landing: Wren grinning, Tam clutching a loaf of bread like contraband, Ivet carrying a crate of candles ("from the chandler downstairs; he gives me a discount if I stop asking theological questions"), and Brannoc holding a fiddle he'd sworn three days ago he'd never play again and had apparently already broken a string on.

"We're starting lessons Thursday," Wren announced, pushing past him into the room. "Reading. Writing. Ivet's teaching doctrine, Brannoc's teaching music, and you—" she dropped into his chair, put her boots on his table, and tapped the blank ledger, "—you're teaching ledgers. Proper ones. The kind where nobody owes anybody anything they didn't choose."

"That's not how ledgers work," Emrys said.

"It is now," said Wren. "You rewrote the rules. We're all just following your handwriting."

He looked at them — the smuggler and her brother, the heretic, the musician, filling his narrow room like it was a great hall, arguing already about candle placement and tuition fees and whether Tam was allowed to learn songs with swearing in them — and felt the absence where his name used to be, and found it wasn't empty at all. It was full of these people. Known without being owned. That was the trade he'd made, and it was, he thought, the best bargain anyone had ever struck under a mountain.

"Thursday," he agreed, and picked up his pen, and added a second line to the ledger, beneath the first:

*Lessons: free. Attendance: mandatory. Family: chosen.*

Outside, the evening bells rang from some ordinary parish church — wrong notes, slightly, the way human things always are — and Emrys Brightwater, who was no longer called anything, closed the book gently on its single entry and went to make room at the table for everyone who belonged there.

All of them did.

**THE END**
