## 2. What the Guild Buys

The Ledgerhouse of Saint Veyra kept its ugliest accounts in the prettiest rooms.

Kestrel had learned this in her first year, when she was still young enough to be assigned the cellar ledgers and naive enough to think the assignment a punishment. The counting floors above were all marble and morning light, where traders in silk-trimmed robes negotiated the sale of a widow's last happy year or a soldier's courage, extracted neat as a tooth. Down below, in the vault corridors where the actual goods were stored, the walls sweated. The lamps burned low not for atmosphere but because some of the inventory—bottled lullabies, jars of first snowfall—had opinions about brightness.

She went down on the third morning after the prayer-scroll, with her mother's handwriting folded against her ribs like a knife she hadn't decided how to use.

The requisition ledger for Vault Nine was kept by a half-blind archivist named Doss who owed Kestrel three favors: two for balancing his columns during audit season, one for never mentioning what he kept in his lunch tin. She did not spend the favors. She simply stood at his desk while he worked, close enough that he grew uncomfortable, until he sighed and turned the ledger toward her without being asked.

"Looking for something specific?"

"Miracles," Kestrel said. "Bottled ones. Recent acquisitions."

Doss's eyebrows climbed. "That's palace-channel work. Sealed class."

"I know what class it is. I'm asking what it *is*."

He looked at her a long moment—the look of a man deciding whether the person across from him was brave or already dead—and then, because the Ledgerhouse made cynics of everyone eventually, he turned pages. "Three consignments this quarter. All routed through the same broker's mark." His finger stopped on an entry, and Kestrel felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath her feet.

The broker's mark was a small crowned circle, drawn in the old style, before anyone alive had been born to draw it.

"That sigil," she said carefully. "Whose is it?"

"Nobody's. That's the point of it. It predates registration." Doss tapped the page. "The guild doesn't sell things without a name attached. Names are our whole business. But these came in pre-marked, pre-sealed, and the palace pays whatever's asked. You want my advice—"

"I don't."

"—you'll stop asking." He said it kindly, which was worse than if he'd said it cruelly. Kindness meant he thought she was in danger.

* * *

Vault Nine held the sealed consignments behind a door of black iron that required three keys and a countersignature Kestrel did not have. But the Ledgerhouse was built like a body, and bodies have more ways in than the mouth. There was a ventilation channel cut into the stone above the doorframe, dating from some earlier architect's paranoia about damp, and through it—if a person of Kestrel's slight build were willing to be very uncomfortable for eleven minutes—came the smell of the vault's contents.

She went at night, when the corridor lamps were banked. She went because the alternative was going home to her narrow room above the bindery and lying awake listening to her own heartbeat, wondering whose name it beat under.

Inside, the air tasted wrong. Not foul—wrong the way a chord is wrong, one note bent just past true. Bottled miracles, she decided. Enough of them in one place would season the air itself.

By lamplight shielded with her sleeve, she found the crates. Palace-bound, stenciled with the crowned circle, each crate packed with straw and glass. She lifted one bottle free and turned it to the light.

It should have glowed. That was what bottled miracles did in the traders' pitch-rooms—soft light, warm hum, something a buyer could watch and feel their skepticism die. This bottle did not glow. It was dark as a stopped eye, and inside the darkness something moved with the slow patience of sediment settling.

Kestrel uncorked it one finger-width, no more.

A voice came out. Thin as thread, but a voice—a woman's voice, cracked with age and hope, saying *please* over and over in the cadence of prayer. Except it wasn't only the woman. Beneath her plea, woven through it like a second thread, was an answer. A reply in a register no human throat could hold, vast and unhurried, promising what the woman had begged for.

Two voices in one bottle. A prayer and its answer, pressed together like flowers in a book.

Kestrel corked it with shaking hands. She knew what the guild sold. Grief, years, names, luck, the memory of a face you couldn't bear to keep. She had recorded every category a hundred times over. But prayers belonged to the gods, or to nothing. Prayers were the one thing even the Ledgerhouse had never figured out how to own.

Someone had solved that problem. And the answer being sent up to the palace sounded exactly like the answer written on the scroll in her mother's hand.

* * *

Master Corvane found her the next morning, which told her he already knew everything.

He was not a frightening man, which was precisely why he frightened her. Small, gray, precise, with ink-stained fingers and a habit of speaking as though every sentence had been costed in advance. In nine years he had never once raised his voice at her, and never once praised her either; he simply corrected her errors and left her otherwise alone, and Kestrel had come to understand that this was the Ledgerhouse's version of affection.

He set two cups of tea on her desk. He had never done that before.

"Vault Nine," he said. "Eleven minutes in a ventilation shaft. You'll want a bath; the dust up there is old plaster and older sins."

"Master Corvane—"

"You opened a bottle." He sat down across from her, uninvited, and wrapped both hands around his cup as though the warmth mattered. "Sealed consignment. Do you understand what I could do with that? What the guild does with scribes who touch inventory? Your contract has clauses you've never read. I've read them. They are not gentle."

Kestrel kept her hands flat on the desk so he wouldn't see them tremble. "Then report me."

"No." He drank his tea. "I'm going to tell you a story instead, and you're going to listen, and then you're going to go back to your columns and live a long invisible life, which is the only kind the guild permits."

He told her about a broker, decades ago—young, ambitious, the best closer the house had ever produced—who took a commission from the palace to source something unprecedented. A king's petition, unanswered by any god, needed answering. The broker found a way. The broker was paid magnificently. And the broker discovered, too late, that the thing he'd sourced had not been acquired but *invited*, and that invitations, once accepted, cannot be resold, returned, or written off.

"The broker's name," Kestrel said quietly, "is struck from the rolls. I've seen the gap in the archive. Page after page with a burn-line where an entry should be."

"Yes." Corvane's eyes, for the first time in nine years, looked tired rather than merely careful. "You've seen the gap. Which means you've gone further than the shaft, further than the bottles, and I am now having a conversation I swore I'd never have." He leaned forward. "Listen to me, girl. Whatever you think you're chasing—it is not a fraud to expose. It is not a crime to report. It is a *trade*, and the trade is still open, and the counterparty has never once lost. Every person who has tried to audit it is either bound to it or erased from it. There is no third column."

"What is it?" she asked. "What did the broker find?"

"A crown." Corvane said it flatly, the way one says *a debt*. "Older than the kingdom. It answers prayers because it has eaten enough kings to know what kings promise. And it is not finished eating."

The word landed in her chest like a dropped coin. She thought of the scroll in her mother's hand—her dead-ledgered mother's living hand—and of the answer beneath it, patient and vast.

"My mother," Kestrel said. "Her entry was burned. Her name is in a gap in the archive. Is she—" He watched her assemble it and didn't stop her. "Is she part of the trade?"

Corvane rose, collected his cup, and stood there a moment with his back to her, gray and small against the tall window.

"I warned you gently," he said, "because someone once warned me gently, and I didn't listen, and here I am." He turned. "Go home, Kestrel. Balance your columns. Be invisible. It's a mercy, being unseen. Some of us paid a great deal for it."

He left. She sat with the tea going cold, and the dread sat with her, heavy as wet wool.

And underneath the dread—small, warm, entirely new—something else stirred. For nine years she had been a line in someone else's ledger, a hand that recorded, never a name that acted. Now she had touched the inventory. Now she had seen the gap in the archive. Now Master Corvane, who feared nothing visible, had begged her to stop.

Which meant she mattered to it. Which meant she could matter against it.

Kestrel took out the prayer-scroll, unfolded her mother's handwriting, and read it again slowly, the way one reads terms before signing.

Then she picked up her pen, opened a fresh page in her private notebook—the one no auditor ever saw—and began, in a hand she kept deliberately unlike her own, a ledger of her own invention.

Debits: one bottle, unlawfully opened. One warning, unheeded.

Credits pending: one mother. One truth. One crown.

She wrote the heading at the top of the page, and her pen did not hesitate:

**What the Guild Buys — and What It Cannot.**

Tomorrow, she would follow the crowned circle backward through the burned records, to the oldest gap of all.

Somewhere in the ash of the archive, she suspected, she would find her own name waiting.
