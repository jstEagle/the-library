## 3. A King Who Answers Twice

The summons came on vellum too fine for Kestrel's hands to touch without guilt.

*By order of the Crown: the Ledgerhouse of Saint Veyra will render an audit of all miracle-trades passing through palace channels. Scribe Oyelaran is appointed.*

Her name. Her actual name, in ink, on a royal warrant. In nine years at the Ledgerhouse she had signed a thousand documents and never once seen her own name appear on one addressed *to* her. She was the hand that wrote; she was not written about. She read the warrant three times in the stairwell of the counting floor, half expecting the letters to rearrange themselves into someone else's assignment.

They didn't. Master Corvane found her there, holding it like something that might bite.

"You'll want to dress plainly," he said. "Palace scribes mistake plainness for poverty and poverty for ambition, and they punish both." He paused, and his voice did the thing it almost never did—softened. "Kestrel. The accounts you'll see are not like ours. If you find an entry that frightens you, record it accurately and say nothing aloud. Accuracy is armor. Curiosity is a wound."

"Master Corvane—"

"The carriage leaves at dawn." He was already walking away, his ledger-case clutched to his chest like a child.

She should have been afraid. She was, a little. But underneath the fear ran that other thing, the thing she'd first felt when she recognized her mother's handwriting on a prayer-scroll answered by no living god: the thrill of a thread pulled taut between her fingers. For nine years she had balanced other people's impossible trades. Now the impossibility had reached up and taken hold of *her*.

The palace of Saint Veyra's crown rose out of the morning fog like a debt nobody could name the principal on. Kestrel had audited its accounts before—grain tithes, road levies, the ordinary arithmetic of kingdoms—but never the inner ledgers, never the ones kept behind doors with three locks and no handles. This time she was escorted past all of them by a chamberlain whose smile had the fixed quality of a man paid to keep smiling near something terrible.

The audit room overlooked the Hall of Petitions.

That was how the palace arranged it, she would learn later: the king's auditors worked above him, literally, so that every number they checked corresponded to a living voice below. From her desk by the high window she could look down through the pierced stone screen into the hall where citizens came to ask their king for things. Justice. Rain. Mercy. The old arrangement—the gods used to take these petitions, once, before the gods went quiet and the crown took up the work.

She was three hours into the grain ledgers when the first petitioner knelt.

* * *

Aldric Vael knew he was going to fail the woman from the mill district before she finished speaking.

He knew it the way he now knew many things—not as thought but as weather, a pressure moving through his skull that was not his own. He sat on his throne with his hands on its arms and waited to find out which of them would answer her.

The woman's petition was small, which made it worse. Her daughter was sick with the gray lung-fever. The physicians' guild wanted forty crowns in advance. She had eleven. She asked—not for gold, even, just for the physicians' fee to be waived, or deferred, or anything.

*Let me,* said the other voice, from inside his own mouth's future. It always asked permission. That was the horror of it, the detail Aldric had never told anyone, not the queen, not his confessor: the thing in the crown *asked*. Politely. Every time. As though courtesy were the same as consent.

He tried to grip the word *no* the way a drowning man grips a spar. He felt his jaw begin to move anyway.

"Yes," said the king, kindly, in Aldric's own voice. "The fee is waived. And the treasury will send a physician to your door tonight."

Below, the woman wept into her hands. The court murmured its approval. Somewhere overhead, Aldric knew, an auditor was writing down the miracle-trade: one healing, one fee waived, debited against—what? He had stopped being able to read those entries years ago. The currency column had begun to fill with words instead of numbers. *Grief, bottled. Years, borrowed. A name.*

*You see?* The Regent's voice was warm, ancient, patient as sediment. *This is what I was made for. A kingdom where the sick are healed and the poor are heard. You could not do this alone. You know you could not.*

*I could have tried,* Aldric answered, in the privacy behind his eyes, where the Regent could follow him but not, quite, edit him. Not yet.

*Tried,* the Regent repeated, tasting the word like a coin of doubtful metal. *I have watched four hundred kings try. Shall I show you what trying built?*

And it did—it pressed the memory against the inside of his skull, unbidden, the way it always did when he resisted: a throne room in a language dead before Saint Veyra was born, a king wearing this same crown, answering petitions in a voice gone hoarse from centuries of kindness that wasn't his. The memory had edges worn soft from replaying. The Regent showed it often. Aldric had come to understand it was not a threat.

It was a warning label.

* * *

Kestrel recorded the first answer in her neat, narrow hand: *Petition 4471 — gray lung-fever — answered: yes, fee waived, physician dispatched. Voice: the King's. Cost: [entry illegible].*

The cost column troubled her. Every miracle-trade she'd traced in the guild's back rooms had a price, and the prices were never money. But here the ink seemed to refuse itself, curling away from her pen like water from oil. She wrote *illegible* and moved on, and told herself the small cold feeling in her stomach was only the draft from the window.

The afternoon brought forty more petitions. A bridge repair, granted. A pardon requested and denied—fairly, she thought, though fairness from a king felt strange to audit. A widow asking for her confiscated boat back. Granted, with interest.

Then, as the light through the pierced screen went long and amber, a second petitioner came for the same thing.

Kestrel noticed it because she was a ledger-keeper, and duplicates were her native language. Two petitions, same wording, nearly same hour—a fisherman and his brother, each filing separately for relief from the harbor tithe, each citing the same storm-lost season. One of them would be granted. That was procedure. She prepared the entry.

The elder brother knelt first. He spoke haltingly, ashamed to be there, and the king listened with his head tilted, and then—

"Yes," said the king, and the voice was warm as banked coals. "The tithe is forgiven. Go home, son. You've carried enough."

Kestrel's pen hovered. There was something in the voice she couldn't name, some grain of it that snagged her ear the way a bad entry snags a balance. She wrote it down anyway. *Answered: yes. Voice: the King's.*

The younger brother came forward while the court was still settling. He was angrier—that was clear even from above, even through stone. He spat the words up at the throne: the tithe, the storm, the harbor-master who'd taken their nets as surety, the whole rotten arithmetic of a city that let men drown in paperwork.

And the king leaned forward on his throne, and smiled, and answered.

"No," said the king, "and let me be clear with you, since no one else will be. Your brother begged prettily and I chose to be moved. You come down here with your grievances and your tone, and you think the crown owes you a hearing. The crown owes you nothing. Pay your tithe. Learn to kneel properly next time."

The voice was the king's voice. The same throat, the same hall, minutes apart. But it was *not the same*, and Kestrel's whole body knew it before her mind caught up—the way she knew a forged signature, the way she knew her mother's handwriting on a scroll answered by a dead god. The cadence was wrong. The cruelty had a different architecture than the kindness. It was as if two authors had written the same character, and only one of them had ever met him.

Below, the young fisherman stood frozen, humiliated, and the court pretended very hard to be elsewhere, and the king sat back on his throne with an expression Kestrel could not see clearly through the screen—but she imagined it, and what she imagined was a man watching his own hands do something he hadn't told them to.

She looked down at her ledger. Two entries, side by side:

*Petition 4498 — harbor tithe — answered: yes, forgiven. Voice: the King's.*
*Petition 4499 — harbor tithe — answered: no, refused. Voice: the King's.*

Same voice. Same breath. Two kings.

Her pen trembled. Master Corvane's words came back to her—*accuracy is armor*—and she understood suddenly, completely, why he'd said it. Because accuracy was all that was left when the truth was unspeakable. She wrote beneath the entries, in her smallest hand, the note she would build the rest of her life on:

*One petitioner's prayer, answered twice. Once by mercy. Once by malice. Both in the king's voice. Query: which king?*

*Query: which god?*

* * *

That night Aldric dismissed his attendants early and stood at his bedchamber window with the crown still on his head, because taking it off had stopped helping two years ago. The Regent lived in the metal, but it had roots in him now, filaments he could feel when he was quiet, drinking.

*The auditor saw,* it said.

His blood went cold. "Saw what?"

*A girl at the high window. She heard both voices. She wrote the question down.* A pause, luxurious, unhurried. *Do you know how long it has been since anyone asked the question aloud? Centuries, Aldric. They all pretend. They all kneel to whatever answers, so long as it answers kindly more often than not. But this one wrote it down. In ink. In a ledger.*

"What will you do?"

What it did next, it had never done before. It laughed—and the laugh came out of Aldric's own mouth, alone in his own room, and he clapped a hand over his lips like a boy.

*Nothing,* the Regent said, delighted. *Nothing at all. I am going to watch her. A mind that precise, that invisible, that hungry for the truth of an account—I had brokers like her once. They were my favorite instruments. Bring her closer, little king. Answer her summons. Let her come and count us.*

"I am not your instrument," Aldric said. "And neither is she."

*No,* the Regent agreed pleasantly. *Instruments are bought. What I keep, I keep because they stay.*

Aldric stood at the window a long time after it went silent, watching the city lights, thinking about a girl he'd glimpsed only as a shadow behind stone—a shadow bent over a ledger, writing the question no one had dared ask in four hundred years.

*Help her,* he told himself, in the last private corner of his own head. *However you can. Before it wears you past caring.*

Far below, in the palace records office, a clerk sealed Kestrel's audit warrant with a second page she wouldn't discover until morning: an extension of her commission, signed in the king's hand, granting her access to the inner vaults.

The ink was still wet. And it was not entirely the king's handwriting.
