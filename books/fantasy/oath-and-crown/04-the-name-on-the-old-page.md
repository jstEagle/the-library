## 4. The Name on the Old Page

The Ledgerhouse kept its dead records the way a miser kept his coins: buried deep, guarded jealously, and never counted in daylight.

Kestrel had worked eleven years for Saint Veyra's guild and had never once been sent below the third sublevel. That was not an oversight. The clerks who descended into the Burnery came back changed—quieter, or not at all—and Master Corvane had made it policy that scribes of her grade did not handle ash-ledgers. *Ash remembers what paper forgets,* he'd told her once, early, when she was still new enough to ask questions. *And what ash remembers, it does not forgive.*

She went anyway. Of course she went anyway.

Three nights after the prayer-scroll with her mother's handwriting, three nights of tracing ledger-threads through the guild's official channels until they all bent downward like roots toward water, Kestrel stood before the iron door at the bottom of the fourth sublevel and let her lamp show her what waited: a door of black metal, unmarked by hinge or handle, with a slot at eye level exactly the width of a guild seal.

Her seal was brass, second-grade, stamped with the scales of Saint Veyra and her own number—*Scribe 4471, Kestrel Oyelaran*—and when she pressed it to the slot, the door considered her for a long, cold moment.

Then it opened. She hadn't expected that. Doors in the Ledgerhouse opened for authority, and she had none. But the door opened anyway, slow as a mouth deciding whether to swallow, and the breath that came out of the dark beyond it smelled of char and old tallow and something sweeter underneath—something like the inside of a bottle where grief has been kept too long.

*It knows me,* she thought, and could not decide if the thought comforted her.

* * *

The Burnery was not burned. That was the first lie the name told. It was a cathedral of shelves rising into darkness, row upon row of ledgers bound in grey vellum that had once been white, each spine scorched along its edge as though the books themselves had passed through fire and been pulled out at the last possible moment. Burned records, the guild called them. What Kestrel saw, walking the central aisle with her lamp held high, was something closer to rescued. Or ransomed.

There were no other lamps. There were no other people. But the shelves breathed. She felt it against her skin, a faint exhalation of warm air every time she passed a spine, as if ten thousand books were sleeping with their mouths slightly open.

"Ledger-thread," she whispered to herself, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be followed. "Follow the thread."

The prayer-scroll was in her satchel, wrapped in oilcloth. She had copied its contents three times and hidden two copies, but the original she carried, because the original was evidence and evidence was the only currency a scribe possessed. A petition from a fisherman's widow in the Saltward districts, asking the old god Veyra-of-the-Weir for rain enough to fill her cisterns. And beneath the widow's cramped, hopeful hand, an answer in a script Kestrel knew better than her own heartbeat—the slanted capitals, the little hook on the terminal *r* like a bird's foot.

Her mother's hand. Answering prayers in a dead god's voice. From a woman who had been dead-ledgered nine years ago, her account closed, her existence struck from the rolls with the formal black stroke that meant *no further trades may be conducted with this soul.*

Dead-ledgered people did not write. Dead-ledgered people did not, as far as Kestrel had ever been able to determine, do anything at all. And yet the thread said otherwise. Every miracle-bottle that had moved through the guild's back rooms in the past season carried a provenance line, and every provenance line, traced far enough down through transfers and consolidations and the quiet shuffling of assets between accounts, terminated in the same place: a closed account. Account number 0001-VEY. *Oyelaran, Mirei. Status: settled.*

Settled. As if a person were a debt that could be paid off and forgotten.

Kestrel found the shelf by feel more than sight. The thread in her mind—that sense she'd developed over eleven years of reading the guild's impossible accounts, the sense that every entry pulled gently on every other entry like threads in woven cloth—went taut here, humming, and the grey spines around it seemed to lean toward her hand.

The ledger was thin. That frightened her more than anything else so far. Thin ledgers meant short lives, or short memories, or both. She set her lamp on the shelf ledge and opened the cover, and the smell that rose up was her childhood: tallow smoke and river mud and the particular sweetness of burnt honey cakes, which her mother had made on festival days in a kitchen too small for two people and somehow always large enough.

*Account 0001-VEY. Oyelaran, Mirei. Debtor class: indigent.*

Page one was ordinary enough—a weaver's debts, a loom repossessed, a winter of small borrowings written in the neat columns the guild had used forty years ago. Kestrel turned pages with hands that would not quite stay steady. Her mother had been a weaver before she was anything else. Before the debts. Before—

Then page nine, and the world stopped.

*Collateral received:* the entry read, in a clerk's hand older than Kestrel herself. *One (1) name, personal, whole and unencumbered. Name: KESTREL. Surname waived per debtor request. Appraised value: negligible-to-moderate, pending development. Term: perpetual, or until redeemed at assessed worth plus interest. Note: debtor requests that the asset be held inactive. No further entries to be made against this name without guild sanction.*

Below it, in the margin, someone had added a smaller note in faded brown ink:

*Asset exhibits unusual properties. Subject does not attract attention. Recommend retention. Recommend no sale. Recommend observation.*

Kestrel read it four times. The words did not change. The words were very simple, and very complete, and they took apart everything she had ever believed about herself with the gentle efficiency of a clerk striking a line through a balance.

Her mother had sold her name. Not her labor, not her years, not her memory—her *name*, the thing the guild considered the root of a person, the thing every contract hung from. Sold it to the Ledgerhouse itself, as collateral, and asked—*requested*, the word sat there like a stone in Kestrel's chest—that it be held inactive. Held quiet. Held invisible.

And the guild had obliged. For twenty-six years, Kestrel Oyelaran had walked through the world unnoticed—not because she was plain, or dull, or careful, though she had told herself all of those things—but because her name had been appraised, shelved, and deliberately dimmed. *Does not attract attention.* She had built her whole self on that foundation. The quiet corners. The overlooked desks. The way conversations slid past her like water around a stone. She had called it a temperament. She had called it a skill, even, a scribe's gift for being furniture.

It was purchased. It was collateral. She was a line item.

*No further entries to be made against this name without guild sanction.* Which meant that somewhere in this building, someone held the sanction. Someone could have woken her name at any time, could have made her visible, audible, *real* in the way the world rewarded—and had chosen, year after year, to leave her shelved. Useful. Unnoticed. Owned.

Kestrel pressed her palm flat against the page, and the old paper was warm under her hand, warmer than lamplight could explain, warm like skin.

"You knew," she said aloud, to her mother, to the dead-ledgered woman whose handwriting answered prayers three floors above. "You didn't just sell me. you *asked* them to hide me."

Betrayal arrived first as heat, then as cold. She thought of the festival honey cakes. She thought of the night her mother had left—she had been seven, maybe eight; there had been a knock, men in guild grey, papers signed at the table while Kestrel watched from the stairs, and her mother had knelt and held her face and said, *Be small, little kestrel. Be small and be safe.* She had carried those words for twenty years like a blessing.

They were instructions. Delivery terms for merchandise.

And yet. And yet. Kestrel stood in the breathing dark of the Burnery with her mother's debt spread open before her, and the betrayal curdled slowly, terribly, into something else—because she was a scribe, and scribes read twice. *Debtor requests that the asset be held inactive.* Not *the guild requires*. *Requests.* And the note in the margin: *Recommend retention. Recommend no sale.* Someone at the guild had looked at a child's name and decided it was dangerous enough to keep locked away from every buyer—including, perhaps, buyers worse than the guild. Including, perhaps, whatever wore crowns and answered prayers twice.

Her mother had hidden her the way you hide a candle flame behind your cupped hands. Not because the flame is shameful. Because the wind is hungry.

"That doesn't mean I forgive you," Kestrel whispered. "That doesn't mean any of this is *fine*."

But her voice shook, and not entirely with anger, and she hated that.

* * *

She copied the page. All of it, every word, including the marginal note, in her own hand, in the quick cipher-scribe shorthand the guild taught for exactly this purpose—for theft, essentially, dressed as diligence. Then she closed the ledger, and as she did, the warmth of the paper seemed to press up against her palm one last time, deliberate as a squeeze.

The shelves had gone quiet around her. The breathing had stopped. In the silence, Kestrel became aware of a sound from the far end of the aisle: footsteps. Slow. Unhurried. The particular cadence of someone who owned the dark they walked through.

She pinched out her lamp and pressed herself between two shelves, heart hammering against the grey spines, and watched a light approach—not a lamp's light but a paler glow, sourceless, the color of moonlight through bottle glass.

Master Corvane stepped into the aisle. He was not in his day-coat. He wore no coat at all, only his shirtsleeves, and he looked older than he had that morning, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with hours. He stopped at the exact shelf where the ledger sat, and he did not look for her. He looked at the book.

"I told you once," he said to the empty air, in the gentle voice he used perhaps twice a year, "that ash remembers what paper forgets." He laid one hand flat on the ledger's cover, the way a man quiets a dog. "I should have told you the rest. Ash also remembers *who*."

He turned his head then, precisely, and looked directly at the shadow between the shelves where Kestrel stood holding her breath.

"Scribe 4471," Corvane said quietly. "Or should I say—*Kestrel*. It's been a long time since anyone in this house used your name aloud. Come out. We need to talk about your mother."

He knew. He knew about the prayer-scroll, or the account, or the note in the margin—or all of it, everything, and the look on his face as he waited in the pale light was not the look of a supervisor catching a thief.

It was the look of a man who had been waiting eleven years for her to find this shelf.

Kestrel stepped out of the dark.
