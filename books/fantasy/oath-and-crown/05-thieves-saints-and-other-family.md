## 5. Thieves, Saints, and Other Family

*Sefa*

The debt-thief found Kestrel before Kestrel found her, which was how Sefa liked it. She had spent thirty years making sure she was never the one being sought.

She spotted the girl from a rooftop in the Tanners' Reach—no, not a girl anymore. A woman with ink-stained fingers and a walk that apologized to no one, moving through the fish-market crowd like a page moving through a ledger: present, accounted for, invisible. Sefa's chest did something complicated at the sight of her. She had last seen that face at nine years old, crying into a bowl of barley soup while men in gray took down her name on a slate.

*Little Wren.* That was what they'd called her then. Sefa had been the one who taught her to pick pockets, back when picking pockets was just survival and not yet a philosophy. Then the Ledgerhouse came for the mother's debts, and Sefa had run, because running was the only thing she'd ever been good at, and she'd told herself for twenty years that there had been nothing else to do.

The woman below stopped at a stall, bought nothing, and left a folded paper under a fish head where only a thief would look.

Sefa went and got it.

*The name on my old page is mine,* the note said. *I know you were there when it was written. I'm not collecting a debt. I'm asking.*

Sefa read it three times. Her hands, which could lift a signet ring off a sleeping magistrate without waking him, shook like an apprentice's.

"Damn it, Wren," she said to the gulls, and went to find her boots.

* * *

*Kestrel*

Brother Ilem lived above a chandler's shop in Candlewick Row, in a room the size of a confession booth, and he answered the door before Kestrel knocked.

"I've been expecting you," he said. He was younger than his voice—a soft man gone gray early, with the calloused knees of someone who prayed more than he stood. "Since the third prayer, actually. The one answered in the dead god's hand. I wrote it."

Kestrel stared at him. "You wrote a petition to Saint Veyra. It got answered. By something that isn't Veyra."

"Yes." He stepped aside to let her in. The room held a cot, a stool, and a wall of candle stubs, each one burned down to a different height. "I was defrocked eleven years ago for insisting the saints still listened. The guild said the age of answering was over—that prayer was now a commodity, bought and sold through proper channels, and any priest who claimed otherwise was defrauding the faithful." His smile was thin as vellum. "I kept praying anyway. Out of spite, mostly. And then one morning there was an answer on my scroll, in a hand I didn't write, promising things no saint would promise."

"What things?"

"Restoration. Return. Everything taken, given back." Ilem sat on the stool, and the cot creaked as if relieved to share the weight. "That's how I knew it wasn't holy. Saints don't lead with refunds."

Kestrel laid her copied ledger-page on the floor between them—the burned-archive fragment, her own childhood name rendered in faded trade-script. Ilem looked at it a long time.

"You're the collateral," he said quietly. "From the Oyelaran default."

"My mother's debt. My name." She kept her voice flat, professional, the voice of the Ledgerhouse. It cracked anyway. "I came because your name is on the witnesses' line. You were there, Brother. The day they sold me."

He nodded slowly. "I was the parish scribe. I recorded the transaction because someone had to, and the guild doesn't leave blank lines." He met her eyes. "I have prayed for your family every night since. Not that it seemed to do anything. Until recently."

"Then pray now," Kestrel said. "Because whatever is answering prayers has a crown, a king, and a broker inside the Ledgerhouse, and I intend to unmake all three. But I can't read the old trade-scripts alone, and I can't get into the vaults alone, and—" She stopped. Breathed. "And I apparently can't do anything alone anymore. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? That's what they took from me first. Everyone."

Ilem rose and began pulling candle stubs off his wall, wrapping them in cloth with the tenderness of a man packing relics. "Saints teach that debts are heavy so we'll learn to set them down together," he said. "The guild teaches that debts are heavy so we'll learn to carry them alone. I've been waiting eleven years for someone to ask me which lesson was true." He blew out the last stub. "Where do we start?"

* * *

*Sefa*

They started with the mother, and Sefa hated every stair of the way down.

The Ledgerhouse's lowest floor was called the Undercount, and it existed below even the archive of burned records—a place where the guild put people who owed too much to ever work free but too little to be worth selling. Debtors scrubbed the ledgers' stone floors with brushes made of their own confiscated belongings; Sefa recognized a hair-comb bristle in one woman's hand and looked away quickly.

Mara Oyelaran was on her knees in the third gallery, wringing out a rag, and she looked up at the sound of footsteps with the reflexive flinch of the long-condemned—and then she saw Kestrel, and the rag fell, and twenty years happened to her face all at once.

"Wren," she said. Just that. Just the name, the old name, the one that wasn't on any ledger anymore because it had been sold, and saying it anyway, spending it freely, like a woman throwing coins into a river.

"Mama." Kestrel's voice came out very small. Sefa turned to study the wall, because some things were not for watching, except that she watched anyway, because she'd waited twenty years to watch this.

Mara crossed the wet stone and stopped an arm's length away, hands hovering, afraid to touch—as if contact might be billed. "They told me you were dead-ledgered," she whispered. "Written off. I signed the papers, Wren, I signed them, I thought—I thought if they took the name instead of you, if the debt closed clean, they'd let you go somewhere better, somewhere I couldn't follow because I'd have dragged you down—"

"You sold me to the house that owned you."

"I sold you *out* of it." Mara's chin came up, and Sefa saw where Kestrel got that particular stubborn angle. "It was the only trade they'd take that didn't end with you in the Undercount beside me. I checked. I made Corvane show me every option twice." Her voice broke on the broker's name. "I have regretted it every day since, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I don't expect you to forgive either of those things."

Kestrel stood very still. Somewhere above them, a bell marked the hour, and the sound came down the stairwell like water finding its level.

"I'm not here to forgive," Kestrel said finally. "I'm here because the thing that owns this house owns a king, and it eats names, and yours is next on its list unless we move first. I need you to tell me everything about the day you signed. Every word Corvane said. Every line you didn't read."

Mara wiped her face with the debtor's rag, leaving a smear of gray across her cheek like a badge. "Then you'd better sit down, little wren. It's a long page."

* * *

*Ilem*

By lamplight, in the chandler's room that smelled of tallow and old smoke, they assembled.

Sefa spread her stolen maps of the Ledgerhouse vaults across the floor. Mara talked with her hands, reenacting the signing, and Kestrel wrote it all down in a notebook that was becoming, Ilem noticed, less a record than a weapon. They argued about approach routes. They argued about whether Sefa's lockwork or Kestrel's credentials would open the collateral vault. They argued about tea.

It was, Ilem thought, watching them from his corner, the most sacred thing he had witnessed in eleven years of praying into silence. Four people who had each been carried alone, choosing to carry together. No contract bound them. No debt held them in the room. Sefa could walk. Mara could refuse. Kestrel could stop writing. That none of them moved for the door was the whole miracle, and no dead god required.

"We'll need a blind spot," Sefa was saying, tapping the vault schematics. "The Regent sees through the guild's sight-lines—I felt it today, a prickle between my shoulders every time I passed a counting-house window. We need eyes it can't use."

Ilem reached into his coat and brought out the last thing he owned that mattered: his ordination medallion, silver, worn smooth by thirty years of a thumb worrying it in prayer. Consecrated under the old rites, before the guild bought the church's silence. It was, technically, the only object in the city the Regent's brokers had never managed to price.

"This," he said, "burns very brightly."

Kestrel looked up from her notes. Around the lamplight, four faces turned toward him—thief, debtor, debtor's daughter, defrocked priest, the whole improbable congregation—and Brother Ilem smiled for the first time in years.

"In the old rite," he said, "we called it witness. Not payment. Not trade. Witness—freely given, freely received, owing nothing after." He set the medallion in the center of the maps, where the lamplight caught it and threw a small fierce star across the vault plans. "Perhaps it's time someone witnessed for us."

For a moment the room held still, the way a held breath holds.

Then Sefa leaned over the medallion and frowned. "Someone's scratched a mark on the back. Recent—see the bright metal in the grooves?"

Kestrel picked it up, tilted it to the light, and went white as paper.

It was a ledger-line. A single, elegant stroke—the mark the Ledgerhouse used to open an account.

And beneath it, in handwriting Kestrel knew better than her own, because it was her own: *Received with thanks. Terms to follow.*
