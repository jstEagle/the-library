## 6. The Crown Remembers

Aldric Vane had been king for eleven years, and he had begun to forget the first three.

Not the way a man forgets a bad winter—dimly, with effort, the shape of it retained even as the details sloughed away. This was different. This was subtraction. He would reach for a memory the way one reaches for a stair in the dark and find nothing there: no coronation morning, no taste of the oath-wine, no face of the priest who had set the crown upon his head. In their places stood other mornings. Older ones. A dawn over a city of white stone that had never existed in his kingdom's histories. A throne room where the banners were stitched with eyes. A voice—his own voice, but deeper, worn smooth like river stone—saying words in a language he did not know and yet understood perfectly.

The crown remembered them for him.

That was the courtesy of it. That was the horror.

He woke before the bells on the third morning of the month, in the dark of his chamber, with snow-scent in his nostrils though it was high summer. For a moment he lay still and let the memory play itself out, because fighting it only made the headache worse. A courtyard. A hundred kneeling figures in grey wool. Himself—younger, or older, or neither—walking between them while they pressed their foreheads to frozen mud. *The Regent walks among us,* someone chanted. *The Regent is patient. The Regent provides.*

*I am Aldric,* he said to the dark, the way he said it every morning now, a prayer with no priest attached. *I am Aldric Vane, son of Maren, who held the ford at Callowmere. I am Aldric.*

The word *Regent* sat behind his teeth like a coin he could not spit out.

*You are both,* said the voice from the crown, gentle as a hand smoothing a bedsheet. It did not speak aloud; it never spoke aloud. It spoke in the space where his own thoughts began, so that he sometimes caught himself answering questions no one had asked. *Why do you grieve the loss? You have two reigns now instead of one. Two kingdoms. Mine was greater, but yours has better wine—I will grant you that.*

"I want my memories back," Aldric said to the empty room. His voice sounded small. Kings were not meant to sound small in their own chambers. "The ones you took."

*Took.* The Regent made a sound that might have been laughter if laughter were something done to a ledger rather than a joke. *Nothing was taken, Aldric. Things were traded. I am very old, and I have learned that everything goes easier when it is called a trade. Your people understand trades. They built an entire guild for them. You gave me room at the top of your head, and I gave you three hundred years of rain-harvest law, the true names of nine rivers, and the knowledge of how to make peace last longer than a generation. Show me which part of that bargain you regret.*

"The parts where I stop being me."

Silence, then—the particular silence of something vast considering him. When the Regent spoke again, its tone had shifted, and Aldric felt the shift like weather against skin.

*There is a scribe,* it said. *Small. Quiet. She balances your accounts on the days she comes to audit. She has been pulling threads.*

Aldric sat up. The candle he had not lit lit itself, flame standing straight as a soldier. "Kestrel Oyelaran. The Ledgerhouse sent her." He had noticed her, actually—noticed her the way one notices a mouse in a granary, quick and gray and gone. But there had been a moment, second day, when she had watched him answer a petition—a widow asking relief from a toll—and he had answered it twice. Once kindly, in his own voice. Once cruelly, in a voice like cold water, before the court's eyes slid off the moment and forgot it, because the Regent had smoothed it away from every mind but his. And hers. He had seen her go pale. He had seen her *write it down.*

"Yes," he said. "She saw."

*She sees everything,* the Regent said, and here, for the first time in all their long cohabitation, Aldric heard something in the old voice that he recognized from mirrors: hunger. *She was born under a sold name, this one. Her mother traded her to the Ledgerhouse before she could walk, and the guild has kept her invisible ever since—unremarked, unrecorded in any heart, a debt walking upright. Do you know what that makes her, Aldric?*

"Tell me," he said, though he dreaded the answer.

*Perfect collateral,* said the Regent. *And perfect thief. No one guards what no one sees. She has found the prayer-trade. She is following it up the channels toward us, and she does not yet know that I have known since the day she first touched the scrolls. I could crush her inquiry with a thought. I could have her ledgered dead by Friday's bell.*

"Don't," Aldric said. It came out faster than he intended, and he hated how much it revealed—that he had stood in front of her and felt, for one strange hour, less alone than he had in years. Someone else had seen the thing wearing him. Someone else had refused to forget it.

*Aldric,* the Regent said, warm now, reasonable, terrible. *I am not going to hurt her. I am going to give her a gift.*

* * *

Kestrel Oyelaran worked late. Aldric knew this because the Regent knew it, and the Regent knew it because the crown's sight ran through the palace like root through soil—through the ledgers she touched, through the ink she used, through the little lamp she carried back down the servants' stair each night. Aldric could not see what the Regent saw. But he could feel it seeing, the way a man feels frost forming on a window.

On the fourth night, the Regent took him along.

It did not ask permission. It simply opened him, the way one opens a book to a marked page, and suddenly Aldric was looking out through the crown's attention at a narrow room above the counting-floor where a small woman sat cross-legged on a cot with stolen documents spread around her like a card hand. Kestrel. Up close she was thinner than the audit-room had made her look, with ink stains to the elbow and shadows under her eyes deep enough to hold water. She was reading a page of the prayer-scrolls, and her lips moved as she read, and her expression was the expression of a woman reading letters from home.

*Watch,* the Regent said, and did something Aldric had no name for.

It reached into *him*—into the place where his own childhood lived, the part not yet overwritten—and pulled loose a single thread. Not his. Hers. A memory the guild had taken from her years ago, held in vault-storage like a bolt of cloth, and the Regent had simply... collected it. Lifted it from the Ledgerhouse stores the way a king lifts an apple from a bowl. Aldric felt the memory pass through him on its way out, and it burned like ice: a kitchen. A woman's hands kneading dough. A song sung low, half in another language. A girl of six sitting on the table kicking her heels, laughing at flour-dust in the lamplight. The mother's face turned toward—

Toward Kestrel. It was Kestrel's memory. Kestrel's mother. Alive in the memory, whole, singing.

And then the Regent folded it small, sealed it in light the color of candleflame, and set it on the sill of Kestrel's window like a gift left by a saint.

Below, in the narrow room, the small woman looked up. Aldric watched her see it. Watched her go absolutely still, the way prey goes still, and then—this was the part that frightened him—watched her rise slowly and open the window and take the light into her hands. Watched the memory pour into her. Watched her face break open with a joy so naked and so total that Aldric had to turn away inside his own skull, except there was nowhere to turn; he was made to watch all of it.

She wept. She laughed. She said her mother's name aloud—"Mama"—in a voice six years old, and then she sat down hard on the floor and held her own arms and shook.

*See how she thanks me,* the Regent said. *No one has ever given that woman anything. No one has ever even looked at her properly. And now I have returned to her the single most precious thing she owned, freely, without price, wrapped in light. Do you understand what I have purchased with that gift, Aldric?*

"You've purchased nothing," Aldric said hoarsely. "You gave it free."

*Freely,* the Regent repeated, tasting the word. *Yes. That is the beauty of it. A gift creates a debt that no ledger can hold and no debtor can refuse. She will come to me now. She will climb every channel she has been crawling up, and she will arrive at my door with her hat in her hands, and she will ask me—not for the rest of her memories, at first, she is too proud for that, but for her mother. And I will say yes. I will say yes to everything, Aldric, and each yes will be another stitch, and by the time she understands what garment she is wearing, it will fit her perfectly.*

"That's not giving," Aldric said. "That's hunting."

*It is both,* said the Regent, serene. *Everything good is both. Now sleep, Aldric. Tomorrow you hold audience, and tomorrow you will need to remember how to be merciful. I will lend you mine.*

But Aldric lay awake long after, staring at the canopy, doing the arithmetic the Regent could not see him doing—could it not see him doing it?—the sums of a desperate man.

Because he had watched Kestrel hold that light, and he had seen what was underneath the joy, in the set of her shoulders even as she wept. Gratitude, yes. But also calculation. Also fury banked and burning low, the way coal burns, the way things burn that are built to burn all night.

She had read the prayer-scrolls. She had seen him answer twice. She knew, as no one else alive knew, exactly what sat on his head and what it wanted.

And the Regent, ancient and vast and clever beyond the counting of reigns, had just made the oldest mistake in the ledger.

It had shown a debt-bound woman a kindness—and mistaken her gratitude for surrender.

In the dark of his chamber, King Aldric Vane did something he had not done in eleven years. He prayed—to no god he could name, to whatever might be listening in the cracks of his own besieged mind—that the small gray scribe would climb the channels quickly, and that whatever she carried in her hands when she arrived would be sharper than a gift.

Three floors below the counting-floor, a lamp went out.

The hunt had begun, and for once, the crown could not tell who was running down which corridor.
