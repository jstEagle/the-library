## 7. Terms of Surrender

The summons came the way all the Ledgerhouse's worst news came: on cream paper, sealed in green wax, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet her eyes.

*Kestrel Oyelaran. The Regent's Office. Ninth bell.*

Not *the King's Office*. She turned the card over twice, as if the correction might be hiding on the back. It wasn't. Somewhere between the prayer-scrolls and the burned archive and the night Sefa had pressed a stolen ledger-page into her hands with shaking fingers, the palace had stopped pretending there was a king answering petitions at all.

She dressed carefully. Not in her good coat—the one with the ink-stained cuffs she'd stopped being ashamed of years ago—but plainly, in the grey of a working scribe. If she was walking into a trap, she wanted to walk into it as exactly what she was.

Master Corvane was waiting for her at the foot of the stairs.

He looked terrible. That was the first thing she noticed, before the words he'd clearly rehearsed all morning could leave his mouth. Corvane had been the fixed point of her entire adult life—dry, precise, immovable as the archive shelves—and now he stood gripping the banister like a man on a deck in weather, and his eyes had the wet shine of someone who had been crying or was about to start.

"You shouldn't go," he said.

"I'm going."

"Kestrel." He said her name strangely, tasting it. "Do you know how long I've known your name?"

"Since you hired me. Eleven years."

"Longer than that." He let go of the banister and straightened, and some of the old Corvane came back into his spine, the broker's posture, the man who balanced accounts no one else could read. "Come upstairs with me first. There's something you need to see before you sign anything. And you *will* be offered something to sign. It always offers. It can't help itself."

*It.* He used the word the way she'd begun to use it in her own head, capital and alive. Her stomach dropped through nine floors of Ledgerhouse.

"Corvane," she said quietly, "what are you?"

He almost smiled. "Come and see."

* * *

His office sat at the top of the guild tower, a room she'd never been invited into, and it smelled of dust and old tallow and, beneath both, something sweeter and wronger—like the air inside a bottle of grief left open too long. He unlocked a cabinet with a key from around his neck, and from it he took a ledger. Not one of theirs. The binding was leather gone soft as cloth, the pages furred at the edges, and when he laid it open on the desk she saw handwriting she knew better than her own.

Her mother's hand. The same slanted, patient script that had answered dead gods' prayers.

"This is my contract," Corvane said. "Signed forty-one years ago. Before you were born." He turned pages with a curator's gentleness. "I was a young broker with ambitions and debts of my own. The crown came to me—or rather, I was brought to it, by my own master, who had been brought to it by his. We are a chain, Kestrel. A ledger-line running backward further than the guild's founding. Every Master of Accounts has served it. That is what the Ledgerhouse *is*. You thought you were recording trades in impossible things. You were recording its digestion."

The room tilted. Kestrel put her hand flat on the desk to steady herself and found her fingers resting beside an entry in her mother's writing:

*Received: one name, Oyelaran, Kestrel, age six. Collateral against debt of Maren Oyelaran. Held in perpetuity. Broker: E. Corvane.*

"You," she whispered. "You held my name. All this time."

"I held the page. The crown held the name." His voice cracked on it. "I told myself for thirty years that keeping you here, invisible, small, was protecting you. Out of its sight, out of its reach. And then you started pulling threads, and I discovered that protection was just another word for the cage I'd built, and that it had never once kept you safe—it had only kept you *mine to lose*."

"Why tell me now? Why not let me walk up those stairs ignorant?"

"Because I know what happens in that room." He finally looked at her fully, and she saw that behind the tears was something harder: forty-one years of arithmetic. "I have watched three scribes sit where you're about to sit. Two signed. I can show you their entries. They are still in the building, Kestrel. They sweep the third floor. They smile at nothing. They are *content*, and it is the most obscene thing I have ever witnessed, because I wrote the terms they smiled about."

* * *

The Regent's Office had been the King's Audience Chamber once; she could see the old bones of it under the renovations. The throne dais was screened off behind silk. The desks were new, low, arranged in a half-circle like a merchant's parlor, and the light came greenish through windows hung with dyed gauze. Everything was designed to make a visitor feel they'd wandered into a negotiation rather than a judgment.

The thing behind the middle desk wore Aldric's face the way a glove fits a hand—well enough at a distance, wrong up close. The eyes moved a half-beat too smoothly, tracking her like a lens. When it rose and inclined its head, courteous as any broker, its shadow fell wrong, pooling toward the dais instead of away from the window.

"Scribe Oyelaran." Aldric's voice, but pitched lower, richer, resonant as a vault door. "At last. I've so enjoyed watching you work."

"I haven't agreed to work for you."

"No one ever does. They agree to terms." It gestured to the chair across the desk. She didn't take it. It sat anyway, folding Aldric's long body down with a grace the king had never possessed. "You've been busy. The burned archive. The thief girl. The priest. Your mother—I confess that thread surprised even me. I thought that account closed decades ago." It steepled its fingers. "You've been assembling something. A little family of debts. It's touching. It's also, unfortunately, a threat vector."

"To what?"

"To the order of things. To every contract, every collateral, every held name. You've noticed, I think, that names behave strangely near you lately. That pages warm in your hands. That is not an accident of your birth, scribe. It is an accident of your *sale*. A name held long enough in trust begins to accrue interest. Yours has compounded for twenty-three years." Its smile was Aldric's smile worn inside-out. "You may be the most valuable asset this house has ever carried. And assets should not be permitted to wander."

It slid a document across the desk.

The paper was cream. The seal was green wax. But the script was her mother's—that same slanting, patient hand—and reading it felt like being touched by a ghost.

*Terms of Surrender,* the header read, and below it, in flawless ledger-form, the offer unspooled:

*The Crown receives: all remaining memories of Kestrel Oyelaran, held in perpetuity, transfer effective upon signature.*

*The Signatory receives: full discharge of Maren Oyelaran, freed, named, restored to personhood. Dissolution of the Ledgerhouse of Saint Veyra, all contracts voided, all collateral returned. Discharge of E. Corvane, bound forty-one years. Discharge of Sefa Ilves and Brother Ilem, accounts closed without penalty.*

Every name she loved. Every chain she'd spent a month learning to hate. Cut, all of them, in exchange for everything she was.

"The guild dissolved," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "All of it. You'd give up your feeding ground."

"The guild was a tool, and tools blunt. I have other instruments. What I do not have—" and here the thing leaned forward, and for one vertiginous moment the mask slipped and she saw what looked out through Aldric's eyes, vast and patient and *hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food*— "is a mind like yours, freely rendered. Memories given in trade are coin. Coin spends. But a whole life, complete, surrendered at once—that is a vintage. I would drink it for a century."

"And me? After?"

"After?" It tilted the king's head. "There is no after, scribe. That is what surrender means. You will be content. You will sweep a floor, perhaps, or tend a garden. You will smile at nothing. And everyone you love will walk free under an open sky, remembering you fondly, briefly, and then not at all—because they will not be able to hold the memory of someone whose memory no longer exists. Memory is mutual, Kestrel. Erase yours, and theirs of you goes soft as wet paper."

The despair arrived first, cold and total, flooding her like the vault floods had flooded Sefa's legs. She saw it all: her mother free and not knowing why she wept at certain songs. Sefa laughing in some sunlit market with a hole in her past shaped exactly like a small dark girl. Ilem praying to a god who might, at last, answer—and no one named Kestrel anywhere in the world to hear it.

Then, underneath the despair, quiet as a ledger-line, clarity.

She picked up the pen. The Regent's whole attention narrowed onto her hand, avid, certain, and she let it watch while she dipped the nib, while she drew the document toward her, while she turned to the signature line—

And wrote, instead, on the blank space beneath the terms, in her careful scribe's hand:

*No sale. No trade. No price.*

"What are you doing?" The voice was still Aldric's, but something under it had begun to grind, stone on stone.

"Checking your arithmetic." She set down the pen. "You price everything. That's what you are—a ledger wearing a crown. Grief by the dram. Years by the season. Names by auction. And you think a life is just the sum of its entries, so you've drawn up terms for mine." She stood. "But you've made one error, and it's the only error that matters, and I learned it from the best broker in this house."

Corvane's face rose in her mind—not the broken man on the stairs, but the teacher, the one who had taught her that the deadliest entries in any ledger were the ones that couldn't be reconciled.

"A gift," she said. "A gift has no counter-entry. No debit to balance, no consideration to record, no terms to enforce. Your whole existence runs on exchange. And I am going to give you something that cannot be exchanged—for free, without condition, without signature—and you will have to *take* it, because refusing kindness isn't in your nature any more than pricing it isn't in mine. And it will starve you from the inside, one unpayable mercy at a time."

The Regent rose. The green light guttered. Behind the silk screen on the dais, something vast shifted its weight, and Aldric's borrowed mouth opened, and what came out was not one voice but many, layered, ancient, afraid:

"You cannot mean that. No one gives. No one has *ever*—"

"Now you understand why it will work."

She walked out past the desks, past the gauze-hung windows, down the long green corridor, and her heart was hammering and her hands were ice and she had never in her life been more terrified or more certain. Behind her, faint through stone, she heard the sound of a contract tearing itself apart, page by page, looking for a clause that did not exist.

There was only one thing left to do, and it would cost everything, and it required a family to pull off.

She went to find hers.
