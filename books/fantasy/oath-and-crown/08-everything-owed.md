## 8. Everything Owed

The Ledgerhouse had never once, in all its centuries of operation, been robbed.

This was not because it could not be robbed. It was because the Ledgerhouse had made theft impossible by definition: everything inside its walls already belonged to someone else. To steal from the vaults was to take what was owed, and what was owed could not be stolen—it could only be *paid*, or paid for. The guild's lawyers had spent generations building a fortress out of grammar.

Sefa had spent thirty years proving that grammar had doors.

"They'll have warded the name-vault against force," she said, crouched in the shadow of the coal-chute that ran beneath the counting floors, her breath fogging in the dark. "Force they can price. Force is just another commodity. But nobody in that building has ever given anything away for free, so nobody there knows how to defend against it."

She looked at Kestrel. In the chute's darkness, with soot on her face and her mother's old coat around her shoulders, Kestrel looked less like a scribe than like a page torn out of something and folded small.

"You're sure about your part," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. It was a last chance to be told no.

"I'm sure."

"Then we go up through the ash-records. Ilem first. He's the loudest thing we own."

* * *

Brother Ilem had not worn his vestments since the day the guild defrocked him—stripped him of office, of orders, of the right to speak any blessing aloud. They had left him one thing, deliberately, the way a butcher leaves a knife in reach of the animal: his reliquary. A sliver of candle-stub from Saint Veyra's own vigil-fire, sealed in glass, worth nothing to anyone who did not believe.

He had carried it for nineteen years without lighting it. Lighting it would use it up. That was the whole of faith, he'd come to understand: everything holy was spent, never kept.

Now he stood at the mouth of the ash-records with the reliquary in his fist and three people behind him—a debt-thief with a crowbar, a scribe with a ledger-line burning cold in her pocket, and a woman whose face Ilem recognized even after twenty years, because some faces you pray for so long they wear grooves into you.

"Marisol," he said quietly.

"Don't," said Kestrel's mother. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "Don't give me absolution yet. There's a door I have to open first."

Ilem nodded, and turned to the task, and did the thing he had sworn he would never do again: he prayed aloud in a house of accounts.

"Saint Veyra, keeper of the vigil—" His voice cracked on the old words, rusted from disuse. "You watched over debts too long. Watch over this instead." He struck the glass against the stone floor.

The reliquary broke, and the candle-sliver caught—not flame exactly, but something older, the light of a fire that had burned in a saint's hands while she refused, year after year, to sell what she was given. It filled the ash-records like water filling a cup.

And every sight-line in the Ledgerhouse went blind.

Kestrel felt it happen—the Regent's attention, which had rested on her for days like a hand on the back of her neck, suddenly *sliding off*, grasping at surfaces, finding nothing to read. Somewhere far above them, in a throne room or a counting room or whatever room the crown occupied when it wore its king, something ancient screamed without lungs.

"It can't see us," Kestrel breathed. "It can't see *anyone*. Every record in this building just went dark to it."

"Not dark," Ilem said. His face was lit gold and he was crying without seeming to notice. "Lit. There's a difference. It can look at anything that's owned. It can't look at anything that's *given*." He gestured down the corridor, toward the vaults, his hand shaking. "Go. While the whole house is full of the only thing it can't price."

They ran.

* * *

The name-vault was a long room of lead drawers, each drawer holding a single page, each page holding a single name, and every name in the building hummed faintly as they passed—thousands of small voices, collateralized, whispering their own syllables in the dark like prisoners who had forgotten any other word.

Sefa went to work on the master lock with her crowbar and, more importantly, with her knowledge. "Every lock in this house is a contract," she muttered, working the mechanism. "And every contract has a clause the drafter hoped you wouldn't read. Here—" Something clicked, deep and expensive-sounding. "*Here.* 'Payment rendered in good faith voids all liens.' Good faith. They wrote good faith into their own locks, because they never imagined anyone would come down here meaning it."

The vault opened.

What came out was not treasure. It was sound. Names spilled into the air like birds from a broken cage—Kestrel heard a fishwife's name wheel overhead, heard a child's name, heard names in languages dead a thousand years, all of them crying out their own letters, all of them *remembering themselves*. The drawers stood open and empty and the pages inside had gone blank, because a name returned to its owner leaves its record behind like a shed skin.

Marisol Oyelaran walked the length of the vault reading labels until she found the drawer marked with her own debt, and inside it, the page with her daughter's name written in a younger version of her own handwriting.

She took it out. She held it. She did not weep, though her whole body asked permission to.

"I sold this," she said to Kestrel. "I tell you that every day now, and I'll say it every day after, and neither of us gets to be finished with it. But I'm holding it, Kestrel. I'm the one holding it."

"Give it back to me, then," Kestrel said. "Not sell it. Not trade it. Give it."

Her mother pressed the page into her hands, and Kestrel felt her own name settle back into her like a bone returning to its socket—and then, because a gift cannot sit still, she turned and began pressing pages into every hand within reach, calling out the owners' names, sending Sefa running for the stairs to scatter names through the lower city like seed.

That was when the alarm changed pitch.

* * *

The Regent could not see them. But it could hear the ledger-lines snapping, thousands of them, and it did the only thing an ancient hunger can do when starved: it called in its remaining debts.

Master Corvane came down the vault stairs alone.

He looked smaller than Kestrel had ever seen him—his broker's seals stripped from his coat, his hands bare, his eyes carrying the flat shine of a man being ridden hard by something with spurs. When he spoke, two voices came out, braided: his own, tired and old, and beneath it the Regent's, smooth as poured wax.

"*Little scribe.* You are robbing me."

"We're not robbing you," Kestrel said. She stepped forward, putting herself between Corvane and the others. "We're *returning* your inventory. That's different. Look it up."

The Regent laughed through Corvane's mouth, and the laugh shook dust from the ceiling. "*I hold your mother's lien still. I hold the broker's bond. I hold a hundred names you haven't reached. Stop, and I will forgive—*"

"No," said Sefa.

She had moved while it talked—of course she had; she had spent her life learning that powerful things love the sound of their own terms. She planted herself in the doorway, crowbar across her body, between the vault and the stairs, between everyone she'd come to care about and the thing wearing a man like a coat.

"You want a debtor?" she said. "Come collect from me. I've owed this city my whole life. Take it out of me."

Corvane lunged—or the Regent lunged through him, fast and wrong, moving the borrowed body like a puppet remembering flesh. Sefa met it with the crowbar and held the doorway, and it was not a fight so much as a *price being paid in real time*: she gave ground inch by inch, gave blood freely, gave her grip, her breath, her footing, everything, and gave it all without invoicing a soul.

"Sefa!" Kestrel screamed.

"Vault first!" Sefa snarled, teeth red. "Names before me! That's the *order of operations*, girl, I taught you better—" The crowbar rang. She staggered. She did not fall. "Ilem! How much longer does your saint burn?"

Ilem was on his knees at the top of the stairs, hands cupped around the last blue ember of the reliquary-light, feeding it everything he had left. "As long as someone needs it to," he said—which was not an answer, and was the truest thing spoken in the Ledgerhouse that night.

Kestrel grabbed the last armful of pages—her mother grabbing more—and they ran for the stairs past Sefa, who was bleeding from somewhere terrible and grinning like a woman watching her life's arithmetic finally balance.

"Get them out," Sefa said. "Every one. Then get Aldric. Then—" her knees buckled; she caught the doorframe and held it, literally held it, arms locked around the wood as the Regent tore at her through Corvane's body— "then somebody write down that I died *owing nothing*."

"No," Kestrel said. "You're coming with us. That's not a negotiation, that's a *fact*—"

"Everything in this house is a negotiation except this," Sefa said, and smiled at her, and let go of everything except the door.

* * *

They came up into the night air with the last of the names, and behind them the Ledgerhouse was screaming in a voice that was not Corvane's and not the Regent's but both, a sound like a contract tearing down the middle. Ilem's light guttered out at last, spent to its final grain, and the saint's vigil-fire went wherever spent holiness goes—nowhere, everywhere, into the hands of whoever needed it next.

Sefa did not come up the stairs.

They got her as far as the courtyard, the four of them carrying her between them, her blood writing its own bright ledger across the stones. She was conscious. That was the worst of it. She looked up at Kestrel with clear eyes and said, "The crown. Throne room. Before it finds new collateral. Go *now*, girl. I've held worse doors than death."

And above the city, in the high windows of the palace, lights were kindling one by one—and in every window, as the flames steadied, Kestrel saw the same silhouette: a crowned figure standing perfectly still, watching, waiting, patient as interest.

The Regent had stopped screaming.

It had started, instead, to *count*.
