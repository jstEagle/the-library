## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley woke the way it always did: slowly, and pretending it had never been asleep.

Paloma Sterling stood at the edge of the escort yard with her breath steaming in the grey light, watching frost burn off the fence rails, and thought — as she thought every morning — that there was no better place in the world to be bored. Boredom was a wage. Boredom meant the roads were quiet, the toll-gates were manned, and nothing had come down from the passes to test the valley's edges. She had buried enough of her company to know that excitement was just another word for the moment before grief.

"Contract's simple," said Maren Oakes, the toll-warden, pressing the job-slate into her hands. The slate was cold enough to sting. "Two wool merchants to Greyford Crossing and back. Standard rate, standard terms."

"Standard terms," Paloma repeated, because the words were a kind of prayer.

She read them anyway. She always read them. *Escort to be rendered faithfully. Route to be kept as written. No deviation. No delay beyond one night at any licensed waystation.* And beneath that, in letters so old they'd been carved into the slate's original casting rather than painted on: *No one leaves.*

It wasn't a rule about the merchants. It was never about the merchants.

"No one leaves," she said, and felt the old familiar weight settle behind her sternum, the weight of an oath sworn by eleven people around a fire twenty years ago, ten of whom were dead. "I'll have them back by dusk tomorrow."

Maren grunted and turned away, already shouting at a carter whose ox had decided the toll-line was negotiable. Paloma tucked the slate into her pack and went to find her charges.

* * *

The wool merchants were brothers, which meant the trip would be half conversation and half arbitration. Eddic was the talker; Sorrel was the one who counted. They met her at the south gate with two carts, six sheep-smelling bales, and the particular nervousness of men who had heard stories about the road and didn't believe them but wanted to, a little.

"You're the Sterling?" Eddic asked, looking her over the way men looked over mercs — counting scars, judging the state of her boots. Her boots were bad. She'd stopped caring what that told them years ago.

"I'm the Sterling," she said. "There's only ever been one."

"That's what I heard." He hesitated. "They say your company swore itself to the Quiet God. That he used to answer."

"He did," Paloma said. "Once."

"And now?"

"Now we keep the oaths anyway." She swung up onto her horse. "That's the whole of the religion, if you want it. Keep the oath whether or not anyone's listening. Especially then."

Sorrel, who had been silent, spoke for the first time. "My grandmother said the god of this valley went quiet because he was ashamed of something."

"Grandmothers say a lot of things," Paloma said, and nudged her horse through the gate.

But the words followed her out onto the road like a dog that wouldn't be called off. Ashamed of something. She'd heard a hundred versions of that story growing up — the god who turned his face away, the god who lost a war, the god who was never there at all and the silence was simply the truth arriving late. Children in the valley learned the silence the way they learned weather. You didn't ask why the rain fell. You carried oilskins.

Her own theory, held privately, worn smooth by handling: the god hadn't left. He was listening. He simply had nothing left to say that wouldn't break them.

She checked that thought into the same locked room where she kept her company's faces, and rode.

* * *

The valley earned its name by noon.

Magic-dead wasn't a metaphor here; it was a fact you could feel in your teeth. Ten miles past the gate, the air went flat as pond water. Birds still flew, rivers still ran, but everything moved a little too honestly, without the shimmer other lands supposedly had. Paloma had never seen the shimmer. She'd been born inside the flatness, and the elders said that was mercy — magic was a fire, and the valley was a hearth-stone, and nothing burned on a hearth-stone.

The iron rule sat under that like a foundation stone: no one leaves. Not because the valley was a prison, the elders insisted, but because it was a promise. Somewhere out there, something had been traded for this safety, generations back, and the trade only held while everyone stayed inside the walls of the bargain. Leave, and whatever protection had been bought came due.

Paloma had tested the rule exactly once, at nineteen, walking to the boundary stones with her heart hammering and her hand on her sword. She'd gotten within thirty paces of the last marker before the silence behind her sternum — the god-shaped silence, the oath-shaped silence — had become so loud she'd doubled over vomiting. She'd walked back. She'd never spoken of it. And every year since, the boundary had felt less like a wall and more like the edge of a debt she couldn't see the ledger for.

"Strange country," Eddic said around midday, squinting at the hills. "Flat, somehow. Like a room with the furniture taken out."

"It's honest country," Paloma said. "Everything here is exactly what it is."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's a survival trait."

They made Greyford Crossing by late afternoon, sold wool, drank terrible cider, and turned back with the light going long and gold across the flats. At the licensed waystation — the only one on the route, its license older than the building — Paloma paid for one night's shelter per the contract's terms and stood first watch, because she always took first watch. Someone had to be awake when the dark came down, and she had never trusted anyone else to do it since the night her company died.

That was the thing about oaths. They didn't end when the people who swore them ended. They just got heavier, and there was only one pair of shoulders left to carry them.

She was thinking about that — about Hallow, who'd sworn loudest, and Brannic, who'd sworn last, and Ilsa, who had—

She shut the door on Ilsa. Twenty years of practice. The door barely even groaned anymore.

The stranger arrived an hour past midnight.

* * *

Paloma heard the horse before she saw it — wrong rhythm, favoring a foreleg, ridden hard and then not ridden at all. She was on her feet with a lantern before the gate creaked, and what came through it was less a rider than a bundle of exhaustion draped over a dying animal: a thin woman in travel-stained scribe's grey, ink-stained to the elbow, with a courier's case strapped across her chest like a breastplate.

The woman slid off the horse, missed the ground entirely, and sat down hard in the dirt.

Paloma crossed to her. "Waystation's full. There's floor space by the hearth if you can walk to it."

"I can walk." The woman looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and ferociously intelligent, the eyes of someone who had spent her life reading things other people shouldn't write down. "I need— I'm looking for someone. A mercenary. Sterling."

Paloma's hand drifted to her sword hilt, easy, conversational. "Why?"

"Because—" The scribe laughed, breathless and awful. "Because the book says so."

"What book?"

The woman's arms came up around the courier's case the way a mother shields a child. "You're her. You're Paloma Sterling. I've been riding for nine days and the book said I'd find you at a waystation on the Greyford road and I told myself that was impossible, the valley doesn't let strangers in with purpose, but here you are holding a lantern over me at—" she stopped. Looked at the sky. Looked at Paloma. "You're holding a lantern over me at the exact hour the book describes."

"The valley doesn't let strangers in with purpose," Paloma said slowly. "That's true. So either you stumbled in by accident, which nobody does, or—"

"Or the valley let me." The scribe got to her feet, unsteady, ink-black fingers fumbling at the case buckles. "Which means the thing I'm carrying matters more than the rule. Which means—" She exhaled. "My name is Wren Halloway. I was a court scribe. Emphasis on *was*. And I need you to look at a page."

"No."

"You haven't asked what page."

"There's only one kind of page a stranger rides nine days to show a mercenary at midnight, and I don't want any of them." Paloma raised the lantern higher. Behind her, the waystation windows stayed dark; the brothers slept on, blessedly ignorant. "The valley has rules, scribe. Nothing comes in carrying purpose. Nothing goes out carrying anything at all. And books that predict things get burned — that's not my rule, that's the elders', and it's older than me. Whatever you're carrying, you should have fed it to a river three kingdoms ago."

"If I feed it to a river, it dries itself off." Wren's laugh cracked in the middle. "I've tried. I've tried burning it. It comes back with the scorch marks spelling out how disappointed it is. Please. I didn't ride nine days to be turned away at the last door. I rode nine days because I read my own name in it too."

That stopped Paloma the way cold water stops a lung.

Wren saw it land. She opened the case with hands that shook, and lifted out a book.

Paloma had seen old books. The elders kept ledgers going back four hundred years, vellum gone brown and soft as moths' wings. This was not old the way those were old. This was old the way mountains are old. The cover was some hide she had no name for, dark as a closed eye, and it was warm. Not sun-warm. *Warm*, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and when Wren set it on the waystation's stone step and opened it, the pages moved against each other with a sound like slow breathing.

"This is impossible," Paloma said. "This is outside the valley. Nothing from outside gets in."

"Nothing with purpose," Wren whispered. "And I think — I think the purpose isn't mine. I think I'm just the courier it picked. Read the marginalia. The hand changes every century. Someone's been writing in this book for a thousand years, waiting for tonight's date to matter."

"Why tonight?"

"Read it."

Paloma looked at the page. She meant to glance — a mercenary's glance, cataloguing threat, finding none, closing the covers. But the script caught her the way a hook catches cloth, and she read.

It was a prophecy. She'd expected riddles and got plainness, which was worse. *A thief shall go down beneath the mountain. A soul shall be stolen back from the court below. The guttering flame shall be rekindled or go out forever.* Plain words, plain shapes, and threaded through them, names and dates in a dozen different hands, centuries of annotators adding their small desperate notes — and there, in ink so fresh it shone, in a hand that made her stomach drop because it was almost, almost her own handwriting:

*Paloma Sterling takes the first road. Tonight.*

Not a date of death. Worse. A date of departure.

"Where did this come from?" Her voice came out level. She was proud of that later.

"The last dragon," Wren said. "He dictated it. He's dying, and his memory is the longest thing left alive, and he's been dictating for a thousand years to whoever would carry the book. Scribes. Pilgrims. Fools like me." She wrapped her arms around herself. "The page isn't finished. See? There's more, but the ink hasn't dried. It writes as it happens. I watched it write my name three weeks ago and I ran, and running just — moved me along the path faster. The book doesn't predict, Sterling. It *keeps pace*."

Paloma stared at the wet line of ink where her own name sat drying in the lantern light, and felt the valley's flatness press in around her like a hand.

Tonight. The word had teeth. Tonight was the watch she was standing, the brothers asleep upstairs, the contract half-kept, the route home planned to the hour. Tonight was ordinary. Tonight was supposed to be boredom, and boredom was a wage, and she had built twenty years of survival on the theology of boredom.

"Burn it," she said. "Elders' rule. We burn prophecy-books. It's the only sane law we have."

"I told you. It comes back."

"Then we bury it."

"It digs itself up. I've read the accounts. Nine carriers before me. Every one tried to destroy it. Every one delivered it." Wren's voice dropped. "Every one found their mercenary."

The wind moved through the pass above the waystation, and in the flat honest air of the valley it made exactly the sound wind makes and nothing else, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet — that the world could keep being exactly what it was while a book breathed on the step between them.

Paloma crouched. She didn't touch the pages. She looked at her name instead, at the wet ink, at the small next line already beginning to surface beneath it like a shape rising through shallow water — letters not yet legible, forming, patient.

Behind her sternum, in the place where her god's silence lived, something shifted.

Not a voice. Not an answer. Just a pressure, faint and enormous, like a door leaning against its frame from the other side.

Twenty years of silence, and the silence had never once leaned back.

Paloma Sterling stood up slowly, and discovered that her hands were steady, and understood that this was dread — real dread, the kind she'd disguised as routine for two decades — finally taking off its mask.

"Get your horse seen to," she said to Wren Halloway. "First light, you'll want to be at the elders' hall before they hear a stranger's in the valley. Because they will hear. And when they demand the book be burned—"

"They'll demand it," Wren agreed. "What will you do?"

Paloma looked at the page one last time. The new line had resolved, just barely, in the breathing lamplight:

*A thief must choose to be chosen.*

"I don't know yet," she said. "But I'm going to read my own name aloud first. Before they can stop me. I think — I think it has to be me who says it."

Above them, far up the black shoulder of the mountain that the valley had been bought to forget, a single point of light flickered — guttering, the way a candle gutters when it is nearly done — and neither woman saw it.

But the book, warm on the stone between them, wrote it down.
