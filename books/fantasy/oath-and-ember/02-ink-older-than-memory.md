## 2. Ink Older Than Memory

The elders came for the book at dusk, as Paloma had known they would.

She heard them before she saw them — the shuffling gait of Elder Maren's bad hip, the creak of the council bench being dragged across the meetinghouse floor, the low murmur of voices that had spent a century perfecting the art of sounding reasonable while doing terrible things. The valley was good at that. The valley had to be.

Paloma stood in the doorway of her rented room above the tannery and watched Wren Halloway clutch the book to her chest like a child.

"They'll burn it," the scribe said. Her voice was thin but steady, the voice of someone who had already decided how she would lose. "You understand that. They'll burn it and scatter the ash in the Greywater and by morning no one will remember it existed, because that's what this place *does*. It forgets on purpose."

"I know what this place does." Paloma pulled her coat off its hook. The leather was stiff with old rain and older decisions. "I've lived here my whole life."

"Then you know I can't let them."

"You're one woman with a book."

"I'm a scribe of the Vharn Court Archive," Wren said, and something in her spine straightened, some ghost of a title she no longer had any right to wear. "I have copied ten thousand documents and forged four hundred more, and I will tell you what I know about books: they are patient. They wait. This one waited a hundred years to be opened, and it opened for *you*, mercenary. That is not nothing. That is not ever nothing."

Below them, the elders' voices rose and knotted together. Maren's cut through clearest: *"It is not our business. Nothing outside is our business. That is the whole of our survival."*

Paloma looked at the book.

It was small — smaller than a psalter, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, its corners worn round as river stones. No title on the cover. No clasp. Just a faint warmth she could feel from three feet away, like standing near an oven door, like pressing her palm to the flank of a living thing. She had felt it last night in the tavern when Wren first showed it to her, and she had felt it again this morning when she woke, and both times the feeling had been the same: recognition. The horrible, animal recognition of hearing your own name called in a crowd.

"The page," Paloma said. "Show me the page again."

Wren hesitated. Then she set the book on the table between them — carefully, the way one sets down a sleeping animal — and opened it to the marked leaf.

The paper was vellum once, now yellowed to the color of old teeth. The ink had gone brown at the edges but the letters themselves were sharp, unblurred, as if written yesterday. Not the looping hand of a court scribe. Something older. Something that pressed hard enough into the page to leave grooves Paloma could feel with her fingertip, like scars.

*In the year the flame gutters, a thief shall come out of the dead valley.*

*Her name is Paloma Sterling.*

*On the night of the frost-moon's setting, she dies to the valley and lives to the mountain.*

*She steals back what was sold: a soul of fire, heir of the last dragon, kept beneath the stone in payment of an oath her people swore and forgot.*

*The thief breaks the contract or the world goes dark.*

Paloma read it through once in silence. Then again. The words did not change. That was the worst part. She kept waiting for them to shift, to resolve into something else — a different name, a different night, a joke, a forgery, anything — and they did not move. They sat on the page with the terrible patience Wren had described, and they were about her, and tonight was the night of the frost-moon's setting, and outside the window the moon hung low and white over the eastern ridge like a coin someone had dropped and couldn't pick up.

"My grandmother's name is in here too," Wren said quietly. "Not on this page. Deeper. There are names going back six generations — valley names, all of them. Every fifty years or so, one of us walks out and doesn't come back, and the elders say they died on the road, and we light a candle, and we forget." She turned a few leaves with a scholar's delicacy. "We didn't die on the road, Paloma. We were *called*. And whatever called us wrote it down first."

"And you brought this here." Paloma's voice came out flat. "Into the valley. Where forgetting is the law."

"I didn't bring it here. It brought *me*." Wren laughed, short and unhappy. "Three months ago I was copying tithe ledgers in Vharn. Then I found this in a sealed annex that isn't supposed to exist, in a drawer that wasn't locked, and every time I tried to put it down I found it in my hands again. I walked north for six weeks following a feeling. I've been exiled for less rational reasons."

Downstairs, the council had stopped pretending to deliberate. Boots on stairs. Two of the elders' sons, broad-shouldered boys Paloma had drunk with at midwinter, climbing toward the room with the grim faces of men doing something they'd been told was necessary.

Paloma made her decision the way she made most of them: fast, and then forever.

"Wren. Can you run?"

"I'm a scribe. I sit for a living."

"Window, then."

* * *

They went out over the tannery roof while the elders' sons hammered on the door below, dropping into the yard behind the lime pits where the smell covered everything and no respectable person followed. Wren landed badly, gasped, laughed — a wild, cracked sound — and clutched the book tighter.

"This is insane," she said. "You understand this is insane. You're fleeing your own elders over a page of ink."

"I'm fleeing my own elders over a page of ink that knows my name." Paloma took the lead, cutting west along the drainage ditch toward the old mill road, where the valley's edge lay half a mile out past the fallow fields. "There's a difference."

The frost-moon was setting. She could see it happening as they ran — the great pale disc sliding down behind the ridge, thinning, going the color of bone underwater. Somewhere in her chest, under the ribs, under the breath, something began to pull.

She had sworn her oath eleven years ago, kneeling in the dark with her company around her, five voices in the cold saying the words of the Silent God: *Hold what is held. Keep what is kept. Speak only in deed.* And the god had never answered — not once, not in eleven years of keeping faith — and Paloma had stayed faithful anyway, because an oath sworn in silence is still an oath, maybe the purest kind. But she had never felt anything from it. Never warmth, never weight, never anything except her own stubbornness reflected back at her.

What she felt now was not her own stubbornness.

It was a hook. A hook of warm iron set behind her sternum, and someone — something — far away, past the ridge, past the edge of everything she knew, taking up the slack.

"The mill road," she said, and her voice sounded strange to her, stretched thin. "How far to the boundary stones?"

"A mile. Maybe less if we don't stop." Wren was running beside her, book against her chest, breath smoking in the cold. "Paloma — the elders will send someone after us. The rule is no one leaves. You told me. The rule is *no one leaves*."

"I know the rule. I've enforced the rule." She had. Twice. She had dragged two would-be runaways back over those fields, one crying, one silent, and handed them to the council, and never asked what happened to them after, because asking was the first step out and the valley could not afford first steps. "That's why we're running instead of walking."

Behind them, horns. Three notes, repeated — the valley's oldest signal, older than the council, older than the church of the Silent God whose chapel stood empty at the valley's heart. The alarm for breach. For leaving. For *her*.

* * *

They reached the boundary stones as the moon touched the ridge.

Paloma had seen them ten thousand times: seven grey monoliths in a broken ring, lichened, unremarkable, the kind of thing you stop seeing by age six. Farmers leaned their scythes against them. Children dared each other to spit on them. In all her life she had never once thought of them as a wall, because a wall implies an outside, and the valley did not teach its children to imagine outsides.

Now, thirty feet away, she could see the outside.

It shimmered. That was the only word. The air beyond the stones moved like heat-haze in deep winter, like the surface of water seen from below, and through it — dimly, distorted — she glimpsed things that made her breath catch: trees that were *green*, impossibly green, green with a color the valley's grey pines had never managed; a sky with actual clouds in it, moving; and threaded through all of it, visible now that she looked, a faint golden filigree running through earth and air and light alike, like veins in marble, like —

"Magic," Wren breathed. "Oh, gods. Oh, you poor people. You've been living inside a wound this whole time."

"Don't romanticize it yet. First we get through." Paloma slowed. The hook behind her ribs had become a rope, and the rope had become a chain, and every step toward the stones made it sing. Her skin prickled. Her teeth ached. The book in Wren's arms was glowing — faintly, along its spine, a thread of ember-light leaking between the pages like heat through a shut stove door.

Horns again, closer. Riders on the mill road — torchlight bobbing among the fallow fields, six or eight of them. Maren's voice carrying across the cold air, cracked and furious and, underneath it, terrified:

*"PALOMA STERLING! YOU SWEAR THE OATH! YOU HOLD WHAT IS HELD!"*

And there it was. The whole trap of her life, laid out in one sentence. She did swear the oath. She had sworn it alone in the dark when everyone else who'd sworn it with her was dead or faithless, and it was the only thing she had left, the only shape her life had, and the oath said *hold what is held* and the valley said *no one leaves* and every part of her that was good had always meant exactly that.

But the oath also said *speak only in deed*. And the god had never spoken. Eleven years of silence. And now a book older than memory said her name, and a chain of warm iron hauled at her chest toward the shimmering edge, and somewhere beyond it a soul of fire sat sold and caged in payment of a debt her people had sworn and forgotten — *sworn and forgotten*, the words rang in her head like a struck bell — and Paloma Sterling understood, with the cold clean clarity that came before battles and after betrayals, that the valley's protection and the god's silence and the empty chapel and the rule of no-leaving were all the same thing wearing different clothes.

A debt. All of it a debt. And debts, her mercenary mother had taught her, are meant to be paid.

"Elder Maren taught you that oath herself," Wren said, watching her face. "Didn't she? When you were young?"

"She administered it. My company swore it together." Five voices. Kneeling in the dark. *Hold what is held.* "Four of them broke it within two years. One died keeping it. I'm the last one holding, and I am very tired, scribe, and I would like to know what my god actually wants before I die holding something wrong."

The riders were close enough now that she could hear the horses blowing. Close enough that she recognized the lead rider's silhouette — Tam Broadhurst, Maren's nephew, a boy she'd taught to hold a spear. He was shouting something. His torch threw his shadow huge and wavering across the field, a giant with a burning hand.

Paloma turned to face the boundary stones.

The pull behind her ribs was agony now, sweet agony, the ache of a held note finally allowed to resolve. The nearest monolith stood an arm's length away, and up close she could see that the lichen on it wasn't lichen at all but script — thousands of tiny carved characters worn nearly smooth, and among them, near the base, at the height of a kneeling person's hand, a groove filled with something dark and old and long-dried.

Blood. Oath-blood. Generations of it.

"We swore here," she whispered. "Every fifty years. We came out here and we knelt here and we swore here, and then we walked back in and forgot." She put her palm flat against the stone. It was warm. Everything about this place was warm, warm as the book, warm as the hook in her chest, warm as a thing alive that had been waiting a very long time. "Wren. If I read my name aloud — what happens?"

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. Prophecy isn't instruction, it's — it's weather. You don't ask the storm for permission." Wren's hands were shaking around the book, but her eyes were dry and fierce. "But I'll tell you what I believe. I believe that book has been waiting a hundred years for exactly one reader, and I believe you're standing at the door of your whole life, and I believe the elders are forty seconds behind us. So. Read it or don't. But choose it, Paloma. Whatever you do — choose it. That's the only currency prophecy respects."

Tam Broadhurst shouted her name again. Behind him, the frost-moon slipped fully behind the ridge, and the moment the moonset completed itself, the pull became unbearable — a fist closing gently but absolutely around her heart, the way you'd close your hand around something precious that was trying to fly.

Paloma chose.

"*In the year the flame gutters,*" she read aloud, her voice cracking on the second word and steadying on the third, "*a thief shall come out of the dead valley. Her name is Paloma Sterling.*"

The world went white-gold.

It was not pain. It was the absence of a pain she hadn't known she was carrying — eleven years of silence lifting out of her chest all at once, a splinter drawn, a debt acknowledged. The boundary stones blazed with carved light. The blood-grooves in the rock seemed to pulse. And through the shimmering veil ahead of her, the golden filigree of the living world flared bright as a answered prayer, and from everywhere and nowhere, from the stones and the sky and the book and the blood in her own body, came a voice.

Not words. Not yet. Something older than words — a pressure, a presence, a vast attention turning toward her like a lighthouse beam, like an eye opening after a century of sleep. It said, in the language beneath language:

*At last. At last. Come and finish what your people started.*

Then the veil parted, and Paloma Sterling stepped out of the valley that had raised her, and the world with teeth closed over her like a mouth.

The last thing she heard from behind was Tam Broadhurst's voice, breaking: "Paloma — *come back* —"

And the last thing she felt was Wren Halloway's hand seizing her wrist, and the book between them burning steady as a heartbeat, pulling them both forward into the green.
