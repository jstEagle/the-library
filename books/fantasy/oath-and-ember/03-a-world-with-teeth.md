## 3. A World With Teeth

Paloma woke to the sound of grass growing.

That was the first wrong thing. Grass did not grow loudly anywhere in the world she knew, and certainly not in the valley, where everything lived at a hush — where rain fell without drumming and rivers ran without singing and even thunder arrived apologetic, like a guest who had forgotten himself. In the valley, the world was muffled. Here, whatever here was, the world was *loud*.

She lay still with her eyes closed and took inventory the way her captain had taught her, back when captains were people you trusted. Limbs: present, aching. Weapons: gone. The knife at her hip, the short sword across her back, the three throwing knives sewn into her left sleeve — all of it stripped away, which meant either that someone had robbed her or that someone had carried her somewhere and been thorough about it. Her pack was gone too. Wren Halloway's book was gone.

Her god was silent.

He was always silent, but here the silence had a texture to it, a weight, like pressing your tongue against a tooth that ached. Ten years of silence and she had learned to live inside it the way a deaf woman lives inside quiet — but this was different. This silence wasn't empty. It was the silence of a room where someone has just stopped speaking.

Paloma opened her eyes.

The sky was the color of a bruise healing, deep violet shading to gold along the horizon, and it was full of things that should not have been there. Lights drifted between the stars — not clouds, because they glowed from within, pale green and slow as lanterns on a flood. One of them passed low overhead and she heard it humming. Not mechanically. *Musically.* A chord held so long it stopped being sound and became weather.

She sat up too fast and the world tilted, and when it righted itself she saw what she was lying in.

Grass. But not valley grass, which grew gray-green and stubborn and never above an ankle. This grass came to her ribs, silver-bladed and faintly luminous at the edges, and it moved against the wind instead of with it, parting around her body in slow, deliberate waves, like water deciding whether to let a stone sink. When her hand brushed a blade, it chimed — a small, clear note, barely audible, but real. She snatched her hand back. The note hung in the air for three heartbeats before fading.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them ordinary. "Okay."

She got to her feet. The ground beneath the grass was warm. Not sun-warm — the sky above suggested dusk, or dawn, or some third thing the valley had never bothered inventing — but warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and it pulsed very faintly under her boots, once every few seconds, patient as a heartbeat. Paloma stood on the pulse of something enormous and tried not to think about what.

*Magic.* The word arrived without permission. Every story she'd ever been told as a child, every warning the elders carved over the valley gates — *the world beyond is teeth* — all of it had used that word the way you'd use the name of a disease. Magic was what the valley's deadness protected them from. Magic was why no one left. Magic was the reason her company had sworn their oath to a god who asked nothing and gave nothing, because in a dead place, faith was the only power anyone could afford.

And now she stood in a field of it, and it hummed in the grass and pulsed in the ground and drifted glowing through the sky, and it didn't feel like teeth at all.

It felt like being spoken to by everything at once.

Paloma pressed her palm flat against the nearest boulder — a black stone furred with moss that glittered like crushed glass — and felt it answer. A vibration ran up her arm, not unpleasant, almost affectionate, and for one vertiginous moment she had the insane certainty that the rock knew her name. Knew it the way Wren Halloway's book had known it. Written down somewhere, catalogued, expected.

She pulled her hand away. The feeling of being known followed her like smoke.

*Find high ground,* said the mercenary voice in her head, the practical one, the one that had kept her alive through eleven years of contracts. *Find water. Find out who took your gear.* Good advice. Clean advice. She started uphill through the chiming grass, and the grass leaned after her, whispering its little notes, and the silence where her god should have been grew heavier with every step, until it was less like absence and more like a hand pressed over her mouth.

* * *

She found the road first.

It cut across the hillside below her, a ribbon of pale stone that seemed to have been grown rather than laid — the surface seamless, veined with lines of soft blue light that pulsed in time with the ground's slow heartbeat. It stretched in both directions until distance dissolved it. Along its edge stood markers every hundred paces: waist-high posts of white wood, each one carved with a spiral, each spiral turning slowly. Not spinning. *Turning*, the way a person turns to watch you pass.

Paloma crouched in the grass above the road and watched it for a long time. Nothing came. The lights drifted. The markers turned. Somewhere far off, something howled — a long, rising cry that was almost a wolf's howl except that it ended in words. She couldn't make out the words. She decided she didn't want to.

She was working up the nerve to approach a marker when the air beside her folded.

There was no better way to describe it. The space two arm-lengths to her left creased like cloth pinched between invisible fingers, and out of the crease stepped a beast.

Later, she would try to describe it to Wren and fail, and Wren would consult three books and find four contradictory illustrations and declare that ward-beasts were less creatures than *enforcements* — the shape that a boundary takes when it decides to have claws. What Paloma saw, in the moment, was this: something the size of an ox, built like a hound, its hide a moving mosaic of the same blue light that veined the road, its faceless head crowned with antlers made of script. Words. Actual words, tiny and dense, grown into bone. She could read none of them, but she understood their tone perfectly, the way you understand a shout without hearing the language.

The ward-beast looked at her. It had no eyes, and it looked at her anyway, and every hair on her body rose.

It made a sound like a ledger slamming shut.

Then it charged.

Paloma ran. No thought in it, no tactics — just the oldest instinct, older than soldiering, older than oaths. She crashed downslope through the chiming grass, blades whipping her face, notes shrieking around her in discordant alarm, and behind her the ward-beast came on in total silence, which was somehow worse than any sound. She could hear it *not*-hearing itself, a hole in the world's noise shaped like pursuit.

She was fast. Eleven years of escort work and border skirmishes had made her fast, and for perhaps forty strides she actually believed she might outrun it. Then the ground pulsed — harder than before, a deliberate pulse, the world taking sides — and the grass ahead of her knotted itself into a wall, and Paloma Sterling learned that in this place, the terrain was not neutral.

She skidded, turned, drew breath to curse the grass, and the ward-beast filled her entire vision.

Up close it smelled like ink and ozone. Its script-antlers had begun to rearrange themselves, the tiny words crawling over one another, assembling something new — a sentence, she realized dimly, a sentence about her, a verdict being drafted in real time. Its forelimb came up. The paw was not a paw; it was a seal, a stamp, a round disc of light that would come down on her chest and do whatever such things did to whatever the Court — *the Court? where had that word come from?* — considered trespass.

Paloma had no weapon. So she did the only thing left in her arsenal: she planted her feet, raised her hands like a woman catching a door, and swore.

"I swear by the Silent God," she snarled, "that I am not your enemy—"

The beast paused. The script on its antlers shivered.

For half a heartbeat, half a heartbeat in which hope flared bright and stupid in her chest, Paloma thought the oath had worked. That her god, silent ten years, silent in the valley, would reach even here, would put his hand on this creature's shoulder and say *mine, she is mine.*

The beast's antlers finished their sentence. The verdict, apparently, was *insufficient evidence.*

It lunged—

—and was struck from the side by a woman on a horse.

Not struck. *Intercepted.* The horse was huge and gray and armored in overlapping plates of pale leather, and it hit the ward-beast like a battering ram hitting a fence post, and the beast went down in a tangle of limbs and light. The woman flowed off its back before it stopped rolling — a tall figure in a long coat the color of wet slate, a sword already bare in her hand, and she moved the way nobody in Paloma's valley moved: fluidly, extravagantly, each motion trailing a faint afterimage, as if magic were leaking out of her muscles.

The ward-beast regained its feet. The woman did not attack it. Instead she reached into her coat, produced a strip of parchment stamped with a sigil Paloma didn't recognize, and slapped it against the beast's brow.

The script on its antlers unraveled. The whole creature unraveled after it, folding inward like a page crumpling, and then there was nothing there at all except drifting motes of blue light and a scrap of parchment fluttering to the grass.

Silence. The good kind, the ordinary kind.

The woman turned around.

Paloma had spent six years building a version of Captain Ilsa Vane that she could live with — a traitor in the abstract, a shape without a face, a betrayal without a voice. She had done it deliberately, carefully, the way you build a wall, stone by chosen stone. And now the wall fell down all at once, because the woman standing in the chimeric grass with a bloody sword and a tired mouth was not the abstract at all. She was older. There was a scar through one eyebrow that hadn't been there before, and gray coming in at her temples, and her eyes — those were unchanged, gray-green and level and unbearably familiar, the eyes that had looked at Paloma across a campfire for four years while teaching her everything she knew.

"You're dead," Paloma said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "I watched the valley close behind you. You're dead, or you never existed, because no one leaves."

"Ilsa Vane doesn't get to be dead," the woman said. Her voice was rougher than memory. "Too many debts." She glanced at the dissolving motes, then at Paloma, and something flickered across her face — recognition, yes, but underneath it something worse. Something like dread. "Sterling. Of course it's you. The book said—"

"What book?"

Ilsa Vane closed her mouth. Looked at the sky. Looked at the place where the ward-beast had been. And Paloma watched six years of practiced hatred collide, mid-swing, with the fact that this woman had just saved her life, and neither of them won.

"You're going to tell me," Paloma said slowly, stepping forward, and the grass chimed around her boots like small bells ringing an alarm, "how you're here. You're going to tell me what book. And you're going to tell me—" her voice cracked despite everything, despite six years of armor, and she hated it, hated her, hated the humming world that had arranged this reunion like a stagehand, "—why you're the second person in two days to know my name before I gave it."

Ilsa didn't step back. She never had; it had been one of the things Paloma had loved about her, once, in the years before the word *once* applied.

"The dragon sent for you," Ilsa said quietly. "Ashfall. He's dying, Sterling, and he's been reading an old book, and your name is in it. Your name and tonight's date." She hesitated, and for the first time since the wall came down, Paloma saw her look genuinely afraid. "And mine. All of ours. It says we're supposed to go under the mountain together."

Far off across the luminous hills, the howl came again — longer this time, and answered, from two directions at once, by voices that were definitely, unmistakably, saying words.

Paloma stood in a world with teeth, unarmed, unmoored, her god more silent than he had ever been, and discovered that the worst part of leaving the valley was not the magic at all.

It was who had been waiting outside.
