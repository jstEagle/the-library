## 4. The Last Dragon's Bargain

They climbed for three days.

Paloma had never climbed anything in her life that wasn't a wall or a ladder or the back of a supply wagon, and her body let her know it with every switchback of the Serpent's Spine — a trail so old no one remembered cutting it, worn into the mountain's flank like a scar that had decided to become a road. The air grew thinner and stranger as they rose. In the valley, air was just air: dead, neutral, tasting of dust and cold iron. Here it tasted of things. Pine resin. Snowmelt. On the second day, a wind came up from the south carrying something she had no name for — warm and mineral and faintly sweet, like the smell of a struck flint left out in honey.

"Ember," Ilsa said, when Paloma stopped to breathe it in. "That's what dragon-fire smells like when it's dying. Like a hearth going out room by room."

"You've smelled it before?"

"No." Ilsa adjusted the strap of her pack and didn't look at her. "But my grandmother did. Everyone past the mountains has a grandmother who remembers the last time a dragon burned bright. It's the kind of thing you inherit whether you want it or not."

*Inherit,* Paloma thought, and said nothing, because everything she'd inherited had come wrapped in silence.

They spoke little otherwise. That was its own strange mercy. Three days ago Paloma had wanted to put a knife between Ilsa Vane's ribs, and the wanting hadn't gone away so much as been shelved — set on a high ledge where she could see it but not reach it, because Wren Halloway's parting words still rang in her skull like a bell struck too hard: *The prophecy requires all three. The thief, the traitor, and the scribe. Break the pattern and the words break you.*

So they walked. Ilsa set a pace like a metronome, tireless and impersonal, and Paloma matched it out of spite, and by the end of the first day spite had settled into something almost companionable, the way two soldiers can share a fire without sharing a history. Almost.

On the third morning they crossed the tree line, and the world opened.

Paloma had seen the valley's rim from below all her life — a gray ceiling of stone, featureless, final. She had never imagined what sat on top of it. Range upon range of peaks rolled away to every horizon, white-capped and blue-shadowed, and far to the east a plain of forests unrolled like green velvet thrown carelessly from a table. Rivers caught the sun and threw it back in bright broken pieces. Somewhere down there, magic hummed in everything — she could feel it now, faintly, the way you feel a neighbor's music through a wall. A pressure behind her teeth. A warmth in old scars.

And above it all, at the center of the highest peak ahead, a plume of smoke rose thin and gray into the hard blue sky.

"That's him," Ilsa said quietly. "Ashfall."

"How do you know?"

"Because there's nothing else up here big enough to smoke." She paused. "And because my grandmother told me he'd be the last. Dragons don't die in battle anymore, Sterling. They die of arithmetic. There aren't enough of them left to keep the fire going. Fire needs — " she made an odd gesture, cupping one hand over the other, " — company."

* * *

The cave mouth was tall enough to ride a warhorse through standing, and it breathed.

Paloma felt it before she saw anything: a slow exhalation of warm air rolling out of the dark, smelling of ash and hot stone and that same dying-hearth sweetness, ten times stronger now. Her skin prickled. Every instinct she owned — instincts honed in ambush alleys and burning garrisons — screamed at her that something vast was watching from the darkness, and that running would accomplish nothing except confirming she was prey.

She walked in anyway. Behind her, after a moment's hesitation long enough to count, Ilsa followed.

The passage descended in wide spirals, the walls worked smooth not by chisels but by centuries of scale and claw, until the stone gleamed like polished obsidian. Old carvings surfaced out of the blackness as they went — dragons in flight, dragons coiled around mountains, dragons curled around small figures of men and women who stood with arms raised, not in fear but in — Paloma tilted her head — in greeting. In partnership. The carvings were older than any script Wren could have read, older than the valley, maybe, and every single one showed the same thing: fire shared, not hoarded.

Then the passage opened into a cavern the size of a cathedral, lit by a light that had no source she could find, and Paloma stopped breathing.

He was dying. She understood that in the first instant, the way you understand a wound is mortal before you understand anything else about it. Ashfall lay coiled in the center of the cavern like a hill that had learned to breathe, and his scales — she had expected gold, or crimson, some heraldic splendor — were the color of cooling charcoal, gray shot through with veins of dull orange that pulsed slowly, slowly, like embers under ash. His wings were folded along his back, and the membranes were translucent as old parchment, veined with light that guttered when he exhaled. One eye opened as they approached. It was the size of a cartwheel and the color of molten glass, and it held a weariness so profound it seemed less like tiredness than like geology.

"Well," the dragon said. His voice filled the cavern the way thunder fills a valley — not loud, exactly, but total, vibrating in Paloma's sternum and the fluid of her eyes. "The thief arrives. And she brings the traitor. How very faithful the old words are. Faithfulness is the cruelest habit prophecies have."

Paloma found her voice somewhere near her boots. "You know why we've come."

"I have known why you would come since before your grandmother drew breath." The great eye tracked her, slow as a season changing. "Come closer, thief. I am old, and my sight is failing, and I would like to look at the shape of the debt before I explain it."

"A debt," Paloma said. "Wren told me you'd say that. She said everything in this business comes down to debts."

"Wren Halloway. Yes. The scribe reads well; that is rare. Most people read only what flatters them." The eye closed, then opened again, nearer now — Ashfall had moved without seeming to move, his head drifting across the cavern floor like a ship coming to dock, close enough that Paloma could feel the furnace-heat of his breath washing over her, could see herself reflected tiny and warped in the dark mirror of his pupil. "You are smaller than I expected. Prophecies usually choose larger vessels. But then, the best blades are rarely the biggest."

"I'm not a blade. I'm a mercenary. There's a difference. Blades get used; mercenaries get paid."

Something moved at the corner of the vast mouth. It took Paloma a moment to recognize it as a smile, because it was so tired. "Good. Cynicism will serve you better than courage down there. Courage is what the Undercourt feeds on." He settled his chin onto the stone, and the floor shivered. "Sit, thief. This will take as long as it takes, and I do not have much longer than that."

* * *

They sat — Paloma cross-legged on the warm stone, Ilsa standing, because standing was how Ilsa apologized for existing, apparently — and Ashfall told them the story of the world's longest con.

"The valley you were born in," he began, "was not always dead. Do you know what it was?"

"A refuge," Paloma said. "Everyone says so. When the wars came, our ancestors hid there, and the gods sealed it against magic so nothing could follow. That's the whole catechism. We learn it before we learn to tie our boots."

"It is a beautiful catechism," Ashfall said, "and it is a lie with excellent posture. Your valley was not sealed *against* magic, child. It was sealed *out of* it. There is a difference, and the difference has a price, and the price has been paid every day for four generations by someone who never agreed to pay it."

His breath guttered. For a moment the orange veins in his scales flared, and shadows leapt enormous across the vaulted ceiling — wings, wings, always wings — and then dimmed again.

"In the last century of the Dragon Wars, the courts of men grew afraid. Not of us — of each other. Magic had become a weapon that answered to whoever paid most, and the paying never stopped. So certain lords sought a way to end the arms race entirely. They could not destroy magic; magic is not a thing, it is a — a circulation, a tide, the blood in the world's body. But they could dam it. And to build their dam, they needed a soul of sufficient fire. Not a dragon's whole soul — merely the flame of it. The living heat. The part that wakes the rest of the magic sleeping in stone and river and root."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "Ember."

"Ember," Ashfall said, and the name came out of him like a coal dropped in snow. "My heir. My granddaughter, if dragons may be permitted such soft arithmetic. She was young. Barely fledged. Her fire was the brightest born in three hundred years — bright enough, the magistrates calculated, to fuel their dam for a century or more." The eye fixed on Paloma, and she felt pinned beneath it like a moth under glass. "And so a bargain was struck. The founders of your valley — your order, thief, the Silent Company, sworn to the god whose voice you have never heard — went to the Undercourt as brokers. They sold Ember's flame. And with the payment, they bought your valley its deadness. Its safety. Its forgetting."

The cavern was very quiet. Somewhere far above, water dripped once, twice, counting seconds.

"My grandmother signed it," Paloma heard herself say. "Wren found the contract. Her name was on it. Maren Sterling."

"Maren Sterling brokered it personally. She was eloquent. She believed, I think, in what she was doing — they always believe. She traded one child's fire for ten thousand children's safety, and she called it mercy, and the Court called it collateral, and Ember called it nothing at all, because no one asked her." Ashfall's voice roughened, and the sound of it was like rockslide. "I have spent a hundred years trying to reach her. Do you know what a contract-cage is, thief? It is not bars. It is *terms*. She exists inside clauses. She can see out. She can hear. Once a decade, when the Court's ledgers are audited, she is permitted one hour of speech, and she spends it screaming, and the screams are filed under *incidental noise* and archived."

Paloma's hands had curled into fists without her permission. Her nails bit her palms. "Why me? Why does your book say my name? Why tonight?"

"Because contracts expire, and cages weaken, and the flame they stole is guttering at last — you smelled it on the wind, both of you, do not pretend you did not. When Ember's fire dies, the dam fails, and the magic your valley hoarded floods back into a world that has forgotten how to carry it. Some will call that salvation. Most will call it apocalypse. The Court does not care which; chaos is simply new collateral to them." The dragon exhaled, slow and vast. "But a contract can be broken before it burns through. Broken from outside, by hands it does not hold. And the old words — the ones your scribe carries — name the only hands that qualify. The thief must steal back what her own line sold. The debt runs in your blood, Paloma Sterling. You were not chosen despite your inheritance. You were chosen *because* of it."

"Inheritance," Paloma said. The word tasted like copper. "I thought I inherited nothing. A dead valley, a silent god, a company of graves. Turns out I inherited a bill."

"Now you begin to understand," Ashfall rumbled, and there was something almost gentle in it, which somehow made it worse. "Your whole life was funded by a stolen soul. Every safe night you ever slept was purchased with her screaming. That is not a gift, child. That is a debt wearing a gift's clothing. And the Court — " his eye flicked, briefly, to Ilsa, and back — "the Court will offer to forgive it. That is what Courts do. They will dress the debt in rescue and ask only that you keep collecting for them. Remember, when that moment comes, that forgiveness purchased with someone else's chains is just a longer lease."

Beside her, Ilsa had gone very still. Paloma noticed, and filed it away, and hated that she noticed.

"And if I refuse?" Paloma asked. "If I walk back down the mountain and go back to escorting wool carts through a dead valley?"

"Then in twenty years, perhaps thirty, the flame gutters out on its own, and the world breaks anyway, and Ember dies having never been asked a single question in a hundred years." Ashfall's head lowered until his brow nearly touched the stone before her, an act that looked — impossibly, terribly — like supplication. The oldest creature she had ever seen, bowing toward a mercenary with dust on her boots. "I am dying, thief. I will not live to see it either way. All I can give you is the truth, and one thing more."

"One thing more," Paloma repeated.

"When I am gone, whatever fire remains in me goes where I send it. I had meant to send it nowhere. Let it gutter with dignity." The molten eye held hers. "But the words in your scribe's book say the thief will need a key, and keys are made of fire, and I am running low on dignity these days."

"You're offering to give me your fire."

"I am offering to give you my *debt*, dressed as a gift. You should know by now there is little else on offer in this world." The great head turned away, toward the shaft of gray daylight falling from the cave mouth, and his voice dropped to something almost private. "She was hatched in spring. She used to sleep in the crook of my wing and set my scales steaming with dreams. Bring her home, thief. Or fail. But do not come back and tell me you chose comfort. I have heard that answer before, from better people than either of us, and it sounds the same every time."

* * *

They camped that night in the cave mouth, where the dragon's warmth reached them like a memory of every fire they'd ever lost.

Paloma couldn't sleep. She sat with her knees drawn up, watching the smoke-plume rise off Ashfall's distant coils into the star-scattered dark, and turned the word over and over in her mind like a coin she suspected was clipped.

*Debt.*

Her whole life, the valley had taught her one lesson above all others: the silence was holy. The god didn't speak because speaking was pride. The walls didn't open because leaving was greed. Deadness was safety, safety was virtue, and virtue was the whole of the law. She had built a mercenary career on that law, taken oaths on it, buried friends under it.

And underneath it, funding all of it, a girl in a cage of clauses, screaming once a decade into an archive.

"She's right, you know."

Ilsa's voice, from the dark beside her. Paloma didn't startle; years of mercenary work had burned the startle out of her. She just turned her head.

"Who's right."

"The dragon. About the Court dressing debts as gifts." Ilsa was sitting with her back against the stone, sword across her knees, staring at the same smoke. "I've seen it done. Small scale. A captain offers to cover a soldier's gambling debts, and suddenly the soldier's wages, his letters home, his choice of posting — all of it belongs to the captain. Nobody chains anybody. They just... sign." A pause. "I signed."

"I know what you signed," Paloma said flatly.

"Do you?" Ilsa's jaw worked. "Do you actually know, Sterling, or do you just know the version that lets you hate me cleanly? Because the clean version is easier, I'll grant you. I sold the company's route to the Vharn enforcers, six of ours died in the ambush, you alone kept the oath, I'm the traitor, roll credits." Her laugh had no humor in it at all. "The version I live with has a signature on it too. Mine. Under a debt I couldn't pay. Which doesn't make it better. It just makes it the same story. Everyone in this prophecy is somebody's collateral."

Paloma looked at her for a long moment — at the woman she had sworn to kill, sitting in a dying dragon's doorway, offering up her shame like a toll.

"Don't," Paloma said finally. "Don't make us the same story. Not yet. Maybe when Ember's free. Maybe never." She pulled her blanket over her shoulders and lay back against the warm stone. "But tell me one true thing, Vane. Just one. When we get down there — under the mountain — when the Court makes its offer, because the dragon says it will — what will you do?"

Ilsa was quiet for so long Paloma thought she wouldn't answer.

"I'll stand next to you," she said at last. "Wherever you decide. Even if you decide wrong. *Especially* if you decide wrong." A ghost of the old captain's dry humor crossed her face and was gone. "Someone has to witness it. That's the one thing I'm still good for."

Above them, the smoke rose thin and gray, and far below in the dark of the cavern, Ashfall's ember-veins pulsed once — twice — like a heart counting down.

And in the deepest hour of the night, half-asleep against the stone, Paloma dreamed of a voice she had never heard, saying words she had never spoken, in a language made entirely of signatures — and woke with her own name burning on her tongue, and the certainty, cold and absolute as the mountain overhead, that somewhere beneath them a girl made of candlelight had just felt her dream, and turned toward the surface, and begun, for the first time in a hundred years, to hope.
