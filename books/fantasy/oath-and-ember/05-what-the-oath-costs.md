## 5. What the Oath Costs

The fire had burned down to a red eye in the dark, and Paloma could not sleep.

She sat apart from the others — a habit older than her scars — with her back against a fallen pillar and her knees drawn up, turning her oath-knife over in her hands. It was not a weapon, whatever the shape of it suggested. The blade had never been sharpened. It existed for one purpose only: to be pressed against the tongue when the words were spoken, so that the god would taste the truth of them.

Iron and salt and blood. That was the recipe of every vow she'd ever sworn.

Behind her, Wren Halloway slept sitting up, her scribe's case hugged to her chest like a child. Beyond her, Ilsa Vane kept watch at the edge of the firelight, because of course she did — Ilsa had always volunteered for the worst watches, as if punishment were a currency she could spend down to nothing.

Paloma looked at her and felt the old wound open its mouth.

* * *

Ten years ago, there had been nine of them.

The Company of the Quiet Vow — that was what they'd called themselves, half in jest, back when jesting was still something they did. Nine mercenaries out of the valley, which was to say nine people who had grown up breathing air where magic went to die, who had never seen a spell work or a ward sing or a dragon cross the sky. They took contracts no one else wanted: escorting pilgrims through the passes, guarding caravans of salt and tin, standing watch over graves that needed watching for reasons no one explained and paid well enough not to ask about.

And on the night they made themselves into more than a collection of hired blades, they swore.

It had been Ilsa's idea. That was the thing Paloma could never stop remembering — that it had been *Ilsa's idea*. She had stood them all in a ring on the mountain above the valley, the eight of them plus herself, snow coming down soft as ash, and she had said: *We are going to swear ourselves to the silent god. Not the way the priests do it, with their tithes and their temples. Our way. We swear to keep faith with each other and with anyone who hires us honestly, and we swear it so hard that even a god with no voice will have to hear it.*

They had laughed. Some of them. Tam had laughed loudest, Tam who was dead now, whose laugh had been mostly teeth. But they had all knelt in the end, all nine, and pressed the unsharpened knife to their tongues, and spoken the words together:

*I keep what is given me. I guard what is entrusted. I do not sell what is not mine to sell.*

Nine voices in the snow. And then — nothing. No thunder, no warmth, no sign. The silent god had been silent for years by then; the whole valley said the god had died or left or never been anything but a story to frighten children into honesty. The elders said oaths sworn to silence were just words.

But Paloma had felt something. A stillness behind her ribs, like held breath. Like something enormous leaning close to listen.

She had told no one. For ten years she had carried that stillness like a coal cupped in both hands, feeding it with every contract kept, every promise honored, every small fidelity no one ever saw. When the company took a job, she was the one who checked the terms twice. When a client tried to short them, she was the one who stood in the doorway until the coin was counted out full. She thought — she had *believed* — that the stillness was the god, listening through her, keeping itself alive on the interest of her good faith.

Then came the Marrowford contract. Then came the night Ilsa opened the gate.

* * *

"Paloma."

She hadn't heard Ilsa approach. Ten years ago she would have; ten years ago she'd have heard the woman's heartbeat across a courtyard. Now Ilsa stood three paces off with her hands visible and empty, the way you approach a dog you've kicked before.

"You should sleep," Ilsa said.

"I don't take orders from traitors."

Ilsa absorbed it the way stone absorbs rain. "No," she agreed. "You don't." She didn't leave, though. She lowered herself onto the far side of the fire, deliberately, slowly, and sat with her forearms on her knees. "Wren says the prophecy needs three. She's been saying it since noon. You've been pretending not to hear since noon."

"Wren says a lot of things. She also said the book was a forgery right up until it pulled me through the world like a fish on a line." Paloma turned the knife in her fingers. The firelight ran along the dull blade. "Prophecies need three, does it? Convenient. Prophecies always seem to need exactly the people standing nearby."

"It needs a thief, a keeper of records, and a soldier of the vow." Ilsa recited it flatly, without inflection, the way you recite something you've read forty times hoping it will change. "That's the wording. Wren showed me the page."

"A soldier of the vow." Paloma laughed, and hated the sound of it, thin and rusted. "There's exactly one of those left, Captain, and she's holding the knife."

"That's what I'm telling you."

"No." She was on her feet before she decided to stand, the knife closed in her fist, the fire between them. "No, here's what happened last time someone decided what the vow required. Last time, the vow required opening a gate at Marrowford. Last time, the vow required letting slavers' men through a door while six of my — while six people who trusted you slept on the other side of it. So you'll forgive me if the phrase *the vow requires* makes me want to put this knife somewhere other than my tongue."

Ilsa didn't flinch. That was the worst part. Ten years ago Ilsa Vane had flinched at everything — at loud noises, at bad news, at her own reflection some mornings — a big woman with a big heart worn on the outside of her armor where any fool could cut it. This Ilsa had flinch nowhere left in her. Whatever had happened to her after Marrowford, it had built walls where the softness used to be.

"I've read the book," Ilsa said quietly. "All of it. There are pages in there about us, Paloma. About the company. About what we swore and what it cost and—" She stopped. Started again. "About what I did. It's written down. In a book older than either of us, it's written down, like the world has been carrying the receipt this whole time waiting for someone to come collect."

"Good," Paloma said. "Let it collect from you."

From the bedroll, a voice: "It can't."

Wren Halloway sat up, hair escaping its braid, eyes already sharp behind the sleep. Scribes woke like cats; Paloma had learned that much about her in two days. "It can't collect from her alone. That's not how the contract works." She fumbled for her case, drew out the book — the book, ink older than memory, pages that had shown Paloma her own name and tonight's date and dragged her out of the only home she'd ever known — and set it on her knees without opening it. "May I? Since neither of you is sleeping anyway, and one of you is threatening the other with a knife, which, for the record, is not how negotiations are conducted in any archive I've worked in."

"Nobody's negotiating," Paloma said.

"Everyone is always negotiating," Wren said. "Mostly with themselves. Sit down."

Paloma sat. She would deny later that it was the tone.

* * *

The snow had gone on falling all that night, ten years ago, after the swearing. Nine sets of prints filling in soft, nine mercenaries walking down the mountain lighter than they'd climbed it, though they'd sworn nothing and received nothing. Tam had sung. Brannic had carried little Sefa on his shoulders. Ilsa had walked beside Paloma the whole way down, shoulder almost touching hers, and said, "Whatever happens now, we did one true thing. Nobody can take that out of the world."

Six of them had died at Marrowford within the year. Tam first, then Brannic, then Sefa, then the twins, then Old Hesk, then quiet Maren who had never said ten words a day and whose last word had been Paloma's name.

Not killed. *Sold.* That was the word the survivors' letters used, the word the few witnesses managed before they stopped speaking altogether. Sold — handed over, delivered, traded to parties unknown through a gate that Ilsa Vane had opened from the inside, at night, with the company's own countersign.

Paloma had hunted her for two years afterward. Not to kill her — worse than that, she'd told herself at the time. To make her say why. To drag the reason out into the light where it could be looked at, weighed, understood, because an act that size without a reason was a hole in the world and Paloma couldn't walk past holes in the world.

She'd found her once, in a river town, across a crowded market. Ilsa had seen her and run. Had run *from her*, Ilsa who had once stood between Paloma and a charging boar with nothing but a tent pole and sheer offended stubbornness. And Paloma had stood in the market amid the fish-stalls and the shouting and understood, finally, that some doors don't open twice, and had gone back to the valley, and had taken the escort jobs, and had kept the vow alone, alone, alone, pressing the dull knife to her tongue in the dark and feeling the stillness behind her ribs grow fainter every year, like a fire fed nothing but ash.

Until a stranger came up the pass with a book that shouldn't exist, and the book knew her name.

* * *

"The prophecy doesn't require three people," Wren said, when she'd finally coaxed the book open to the page. Her finger traced lines of script that seemed to shift when looked at directly, settling only under a reader's honest attention. "It requires three *offices*. Read the marginalia — this isn't poetry, it's contract language. Whoever wrote this was thinking in terms of obligations. A thief, to take back what was stolen. A keeper of records, to find the terms and break them lawfully, because a soul freed unlawfully just changes owners. And—"

"And a soldier of the vow," Ilsa finished.

"Because," Wren said, gently, in the voice she probably used on frightened junior clerks, "a soul that was sold was sold *against an oath*. Someone swore protection over Ember and then broke it, or let it be broken, or profited by its breaking. And a broken oath can only be answered by an oath. That's the symmetry. That's the whole architecture of the thing." She looked up at Paloma, and her eyes were kind, and Paloma hated being looked at kindly. "It doesn't have to be Ilsa. But it has to be someone who was there when the vow was made, someone who carries the weight of what was lost. And by my count, that's two people in this world, and they're both sitting at this fire refusing to look at each other."

"The company didn't sell any soul," Paloma said. "We guarded caravans. We escorted pilgrims. What does a mercenary company out of a magic-dead valley know about dragons?"

"Read the page," Wren said. "The one about the founding. Please."

Paloma didn't want to. Every instinct she had — and her instincts had kept her alive through things that would have killed a braver, stupider woman — said don't, said the book takes things from you when you read it, said you already gave it your name and your date of death and look where that got you.

She read the page.

The script swam, settled, resolved. It described a bargain struck generations ago, before her grandmother's grandmother: a valley bought its safety from something beneath the mountain, and the price had been written in the oldest currency there was. It described a dragon's heir, young and bright, given over to the Undercourt in perpetuity. It described the flame that kept magic alive in the wide world guttering, year over year, century over century, as the soul that fueled it was spent down like a candle in a drafty room.

And it described the valley's protectors — the order sworn to guard the border of the bargain, to ensure no one ever left and nothing ever followed, generation after generation, wearing different names, taking different shapes. Mercenaries. Wardens. A company of nine who knelt in the snow and swore to keep what was entrusted, never knowing what it was they'd been entrusted with, because the knowledge had been carved out of the founding the way a tumor is carved out, leaving only the habit of obedience.

*I keep what is given me,* the words said, in the language of the vow, in the cadence of the vow, and Paloma's mouth went dry around them. *I guard what is entrusted.*

"We weren't a mercenary company," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "We were the lock. All those pointless contracts, all those caravans and pilgrims and graves — it was all just — motion. Busywork. Keeping the lock oiled. Keeping us *here*, inside the wall, doing our duty, generation after generation, so no one ever asked what the wall was for."

"Or," Ilsa said, very carefully, "that's one reading."

"What's another?"

"That the vow was real even if the job was a lie." Ilsa's jaw worked. "That whatever the founders intended, what *we* swore that night was ours. We meant it. You meant it. I watched you mean it harder than anyone I've ever known." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the scar along her cheekbone that hadn't been there ten years ago. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to consider that the prophecy named a soldier of the vow, and there's one left, and she's the only person alive who knows what that vow weighs, and if she walks away from this, the weight goes with her."

Silence. The fire cracked and settled. Somewhere out in the dark, the strange loud world made its strange loud noises — wind through trees that might have been listening, water over stones that might have been singing. Magic hummed in everything out here. Paloma had lived four days inside that hum and it still felt like standing too close to a hive.

"Why?" she asked. It came out rougher than she intended. "Why did you open the gate, Ilsa? Two years I hunted you for the answer. Ten years I've hated you without it. If you're going to stand there and ask me to fight beside you, you'll give me that much. Why?"

Ilsa looked into the fire for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was level in the way of things balanced on an edge.

"Because they had Sefa."

The name landed like a dropped stone. Little Sefa, seventeen years old, who'd joined for the food and stayed for the family, who used to fall asleep against Brannic's shoulder on long marches.

"They took her. Before Marrowford. I never knew who *they* were — men with papers, men with authority, men who knew the company's countersigns better than I did and told me they'd been sent by the elders of the valley, and maybe they had, maybe the rot goes all the way up, I don't know, I've never known. They had Sefa in a room, and they had documents, and they told me the gate would be opened or the girl would be sold south, and I believed them, because I was a fool who believed that people who write things down mean what they write." Her hands had curled into fists on her knees. "So I opened the gate. And they sold her anyway. Or they never had her at all. I went looking, after. I looked for three years. There was no record of Sefa anywhere, Paloma. No ransom demand, no body, no ledger entry. It was as if she'd been erased. And I have spent ten years wondering whether I betrayed six people to save one, or whether I was simply the tool they'd chosen, and I cannot tell you which, because I don't know, and not knowing is the sentence I serve every single day."

The fire burned. Paloma found that her eyes were wet and did not permit them to do anything about it.

"Sefa's alive," Wren said suddenly.

Both women turned. The scribe had gone pale, staring at the open book, her finger pressed flat against a line near the bottom of the page as if holding it in place.

"This entry — it's recent, the ink is recent, the book updates, I've seen it do it —" She looked up, and her voice shook. "There's a name in the prisoner manifest of the Undercourt. A soul held in collateral, acquired ninety-eight years ago, transferred, re-transferred — the records go back further than that, the Court keeps everything — but the name. The name is Sefa."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, the humming world went very quiet, as if it too were listening.

Paloma looked at Ilsa Vane — at the woman who had broken her, who had been broken worse, who had just confessed to a crime that perhaps had never been hers alone — and saw the exact moment the sentence she'd served for ten years collapsed into something else. Something worse. Something with a door in it.

"Get your kit," Paloma said, standing, sheathing the oath-knife, hearing her own voice come out steady and cold and absolutely certain for the first time since the book had spoken her name. "Both of you. We leave before dawn."

Wren blinked. "To the mountain? Already? We haven't—"

"The Court has Sefa." Paloma pulled her pack tight. "Which means the Court has something of mine. And I keep what is given me. I guard what is entrusted." She looked at the dark horizon, where the mountain waited, patient as a creditor. "It seems I've been guarding it my whole life without knowing. Time someone paid the debt."
