## 6. The City of Ledgers

Wren Halloway had spent eleven years learning the grammar of power, and Vharn was its mother tongue.

She felt it before she saw it — a pressure in the ears, like standing too near an organ mid-note. The city announced itself not by walls or spires but by sound: the tick and rustle of ten thousand quills moving at once, carried on the wind off the lowlands like the whispering of a field of dry wheat. Ink had a smell here. It hung over everything, sharp and mineral, cut through with lamp-oil and hot wax and the faint sweet rot of paper left too long in damp rooms.

"Close your mouth," she murmured, without turning around. "You look like a tourist."

"I *am* a tourist," Paloma said behind her. "I've never seen a city."

"You've seen cities." Ilsa's voice, flat as slate.

"I've seen garrisons with delusions of grandeur."

Wren allowed herself a small smile and crested the last rise of the pilgrim road, and there it was.

Vharn did not glitter. That was the first lie the travel books told — she'd read them all, in her first year at court, hungry for anywhere that wasn't the archive's windowless third floor. Vharn *gleamed*, which was worse. Every surface was polished: the white limestone of the Archive Spire that rose from the city's heart like a filed tooth, the copper roofs gone green with verdigris, the ledger-stone streets worn smooth by centuries of clerical boots. Light came off the city in sheets. And threaded through every gleaming surface ran lines of dark script — carved into lintels, inlaid in cobbles, stitched into the awnings over the tea-houses — so that from this distance the whole city looked like a page someone had written on until there was no blank space left.

"What are all the words?" Paloma asked. Her voice had gone quiet in the way Wren was beginning to recognize: the mercenary went quiet when something frightened her, and covered the silence with dryness afterward.

"Names," Wren said. "Mostly names. Every soul born inside those walls is entered into the Grand Register within three days of birth. The entry is read aloud, witnessed, sealed, and then inscribed somewhere permanent. A doorframe. A gutter. A gravestone, pre-emptively, because the Court likes to be thorough." She adjusted the strap of her satchel — her whole former life compressed into ink pots, blotting sand, a bone folder, and forty sheets of good rag paper she'd stolen from her own desk on the way out. "Your name isn't yours here. It's collateral. You spend it your whole life and the Court collects the balance at death."

"And if you don't pay?"

"There's no *not paying*. That's the genius of it." She started down the road, into the smell of ink. "The debt is the self. The only question is who holds the note."

* * *

They came through the Salt Gate at midmorning, and Wren performed the role she'd rehearsed for two hundred miles: a junior archivist of the Surface Court, traveling under writ, accompanied by two hired guards of unremarkable competence.

The guards were the problem. She'd specified *unremarkable*. She had received instead a woman built like a siege engine whose sword had its own weather system, and a second woman who kept touching her amulet the way other people touched wounds.

"Name?" said the gate-clerk, without looking up. He was very young and very bored, which was the best possible combination. Bored clerks wanted the transaction finished; suspicious ones wanted it prolonged.

"Wren Halloway, Archivist Third Class, Bureau of Precedents." She slid the writ across the counter — genuine, hers, real seal, real signature, one of the few honest pieces of paper she owned. "Traveling under Section Nine authority. Two retainers, contracted escort, listed herein."

The clerk glanced at the writ. Glanced at Ilsa. Glanced at the writ again, as though checking whether paper could lie about what stood in front of it.

"Retainers are usually smaller," he said.

"Bandits on the north road are usually larger," Wren said pleasantly. "Section Nine compensates for local conditions."

He stamped it. Of course he stamped it. That was the thing no one outside ever understood about Vharn, the thing Wren had tried and failed to explain to her mother before her mother sold the family's name-portion to cover a debt and vanished into the Registry of the Redeemed: the Court did not run on fear. Fear was crude, expensive, and required enforcers, and enforcers were people, and people were unreliable. The Court ran on *procedure*. On the beautiful, self-sealing logic of forms filled out correctly. A properly executed document moved through Vharn like blood through a vein, and no muscle in the body politic could refuse it, because refusing procedure meant admitting the whole organism was voluntary — and the moment anyone believed that, the organism died.

Power here was written, not fought. Wren intended to teach Paloma Sterling that lesson if it killed them both.

They passed under the gate arch, where the keystone bore a single inscription in letters a hand tall: *ALL ACCOUNTS SETTLED.* Beyond it, the city opened up around them like a throat.

* * *

Wren had forgotten how loud Vharn was up close. Not the quills now — those faded once you were inside the machine — but the *transactions*. Every street corner hosted some small theater of obligation: a pawnbroker reading terms aloud to a weeping woman so witnesses could confirm consent; children playing a skipping game whose rhyme was, Wren noted with distant nausea, a simplified repossession chant; a marriage contract being signed on a tavern table while the couple's families watched with the grim attention of shareholders. Above it all, from a hundred towers, the Notaries' bells rang the quarter-hour, and at each ringing, passersby would touch their collars — where every citizen wore the thin lead seal of their Register entry, dull gray against the skin, stamped with a number.

Paloma touched her own bare throat once, noticed herself doing it, and stopped.

"It's a collar," she said quietly, watching a fruit-seller count change with the seal glinting at his neck. "The whole city. It's just a prettier kennel than mine."

"The valley has walls," Wren said. "This has paperwork. I honestly can't tell you which is crueler, but I can tell you which one I know how to pick." She turned down Archive Row, past the stationers and the seal-cutters and the shop that sold nothing but lamps calibrated to burn at the exact color temperature mandated for public record-keeping. "We need lodging that doesn't ask questions, and papers that do answer them. In that order."

"Lodging first," Ilsa said. "I don't forge well tired."

"You don't forge at all. You're going to stand by the door and look intimidating."

"That I can do tired."

* * *

The lodging was a rooming house above a bindery on Quill Street, chosen because the landlady, a widow named Sereth, had once been caught falsifying her late husband's death-entry to keep his pension flowing, and therefore asked exactly zero questions of anyone, ever. She took Wren's coin, handed over a key on a ribbon, and said only, "No open flame after the second bell. The bindery below remembers what happened in '41."

The room was narrow, smelled of glue and leather shavings, and had one window looking out on a light-well where the neighbors' laundry made a soft, shifting map of other people's lives. Wren cleared the table, laid out her tools in their ritual order — knife, sand, pens, the little brass scale for weighing seals — and looked up to find both mercenaries watching her like she was about to perform surgery.

"You're really going to make us papers," Paloma said. "Out of nothing."

"Out of *precedent*," Wren corrected. "Nothing is the one material I never work in. Watch."

She pulled a sheet of rag paper and began, first, with age. Tea, applied cold in uneven washes, dried before the window's draft. Then the fold-lines — documents were folded in thirds along official creases, and a forgery without creases screamed from across the room — pressed in with the bone folder and rubbed with graphite dust so they'd take grime naturally. Then the hard part, the part that separated forgers from hanged forgers: the hand.

"Every clerk writes the way they breathe," she said, working as she talked, because teaching kept her hands steady. "Speed, slant, the little hesitations. This writ needs to come from the Office of Transit Permits, Second Desk. Master Odo Fenwick holds that desk. He's sixty, half-blind in his right eye, drinks vinegar-water because he read somewhere it preserves the humors, and he presses hardest at the start of each stroke and trails off at the end, like every word is leaving him reluctantly." She demonstrated: a firm entry, a fading exit. "I copied four hundred pages of his hand before I was promoted out of his office. He cried at my farewell. He'd have cried harder knowing what I use it for."

Paloma leaned over the table, close enough that Wren could smell road dust and steel oil on her. "And the seal?"

"The seal is wax and geometry. Anyone can copy a seal." Wren held up the stamp she'd carved on the road, nights by the fire, from a piece of boxwood — the transit office's crossed-keys-and-quill, rendered slightly wrong in the way that *all* impressions were slightly wrong, because seals wore, because the wax pooled, because reality was imprecise and the Court's power lived entirely in pretending otherwise. "What you can't copy is the *registration*. Every seal impression is logged. Impression number, date, document class. So—" she tapped the forged writ, where a registration line waited in faint pencil, "—we don't invent a number. We borrow one. There's a genuine transit permit issued three weeks ago to a wool merchant named Aldous Cray, permit 44,117. He completed his journey. His permit is expired, archived, and — this is the beautiful part — scheduled for pulping next month, because expired permits get pulped. For the next thirty days, permit 44,117 exists in the registry, valid, attached to a journey already finished. No one will cross-reference it, because no one ever does. The system is airtight, and that's precisely why it leaks everywhere. Airtight things have no inspectors."

Ilsa, by the door, made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "You've done crimes with this brain."

"I've done *bureaucracy* with this brain. Same thing, better handwriting."

* * *

It took most of the afternoon. Three documents: the transit permit, extending their lawful stay; a requisition chit authorizing "archival retrieval, sub-level access, Bureau of Deep Records" — which would get them into the surface archive where the Undercourt's tithe-schedules were mirrored; and, hardest of all, a letter of introduction naming them as agents of a minor noble house, House Verelain, which Wren had invented from whole cloth but grounded in real heraldry, real geography, and a genuine extinct family whose arms she remembered from a funeral banner she'd catalogued nine years ago. Dead houses made excellent patrons. They never wrote to check up on you.

Paloma watched the whole time. At first she watched the way soldiers watch anything unfamiliar — for threats, for exits, for the moment it turned on them. But somewhere in the second hour the watching changed. She started asking questions. Good ones. Why fold before writing rather than after. Why the ink had to be iron-gall and not the lampblack Wren preferred. What happened if the clerk who'd issued the borrowed permit died before the forgery surfaced.

"Then the permit outlives him," Wren said. "That's the entire point of writing, Paloma. It's the only magic that works after the magician's dead. Words outlive intent. Outlive empires. Outlive—" she caught herself, pen hovering, and finished more carefully, "—outlive most things worth outliving."

"My god doesn't," Paloma said. "Answer, I mean. Ten years of oaths and nothing. Whatever speaks back in this world, it isn't words."

Wren set down the pen. Outside the window, the light-well had gone amber; the laundry shadows stretched long across the opposite wall. Somewhere below, the bindery's press thumped in slow rhythm, like a patient heart.

"Can I tell you something heretical?" she said. "I worked in the deepest archive in the Surface Court for eleven years. I have read contracts older than kingdoms. And I'll tell you what I learned about oaths." She turned the fresh-forged permit face-up on the table, ink still shining. "An oath is just a contract where both parties mean it. That's all. Strip away the incense and the Latin and the kneeling, and it's two wills agreeing to be bound. Which means your god isn't a voice that answers, maybe. Maybe your god is the binding itself. Every promise kept by everyone who ever meant it — that's not silence. That's a very large thing being very patient."

Paloma stared at her. Something moved behind her face — the same expression she'd worn at the gate, looking up at the inscription, *ALL ACCOUNTS SETTLED*, like a woman reading her own sentence.

"That's either the wisest thing anyone's ever told me," she said finally, "or the cruelest joke I've ever heard."

"In my experience those arrive in the same envelope."

* * *

Sereth brought supper up at dusk — bread, hard cheese, a jar of pickled things — and with it, unexpectedly, conversation. She lingered in the doorway, drying her hands on her apron, and asked Wren, in the tone of one professional recognizing another, whether the Bureau of Precedents still used the old double-entry ledgers or had gone over to the Vharnese column method.

"Double-entry," Wren said. "The Bureau considers column method flashy."

"Good. Flashy hides errors." Sereth nodded, satisfied, and then, halfway out the door, paused. "Your tall friend. The one with the sword. She fixed my stair rail while you were working. Didn't ask. Just saw it was loose and fixed it." The widow's eyes were shrewd and not unkind. "People who fix things without asking are either very good or very sorry. Watch which."

When she'd gone, Ilsa examined the stair-rail story with the expression of a woman who'd been complimented and resented it. "It was loose," she said defensively, to no one.

"Yes," Wren agreed solemnly. "Terribly suspicious behavior."

"Shut up, scribe."

But something had shifted in the room, Wren thought. Small, and structural, like a joint settling. They ate together — actually together, around the one table, instead of in the armed truce of separate corners. Ilsa broke the bread and dealt the cheese like cards. Paloma told a story about a contract job gone wrong in the valley, a bandit chief who'd surrendered to the wrong mercenary company and spent a week politely trying to surrender to each of them individually, and for the first time since Wren had known her, she laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years locked, but a laugh. Ilsa almost smiled. Almost, in Ilsa's currency, was a fortune.

Later, by lamplight, Wren finished the last document — the House Verelain letter — and blotted it, and sat back, and realized both women had drifted toward the table again, drawn like moths to the small domestic miracle of work shared.

"Tomorrow," she said, tapping the requisition chit, "we go into the Bureau of Deep Records. Somewhere in the mirrored schedules down there is the tithe-route to the Undercourt — the accounting thread that connects the surface to whatever's under the mountain. Ember's sale will be recorded. Everything is recorded. That's the Court's strength and it's going to be its wound."

"And if the records aren't there?" Ilsa asked.

"They'll be there."

"And if we're caught?"

"Then we're caught holding forged instruments of the Surface Court, which carries a penalty I'd rather not describe before supper settles." Wren capped her ink. "So we won't be caught. I've been caught exactly once, and I was nineteen and sentimental. I've cured both conditions."

Paloma was quiet a moment. Then she reached across the table and turned the House Verelain letter toward herself, studying the invented arms, the dead family resurrected in ink for one more errand.

"You said words outlive the magician," she said. "Whose words are these? Yours? Or theirs — the dead house, old Odo Fenwick, everyone you copied?"

"Mine," Wren said. "That's the terrifying part. Every document is a little act of godhood. You write a thing clearly enough, witness it, seal it — and reality signs underneath." She smiled, tired, and blew out the lamp so that only the city's glow remained, seeping amber through the shutters, gilding the edges of the forged papers like old gold. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we go hunting through the guts of the machine."

She was nearly asleep herself when the knock came.

Three soft raps. Past midnight, past the second bell, from a stairwell where no one had any honest business. Ilsa was up with her blade drawn before Wren had fully surfaced; Paloma was at the door with her hand flat against the wood, listening.

Another knock. Smaller. And then a voice — a child's voice, thin through the door, pitched carefully under the sound of the bindery's night-press below:

"Please. I heard you talking about the mountain." A pause; the press thumped twice, patient as a pulse. "I hear things. Things people say when they're alone. And last night I heard the mountain say something back."

Wren looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at Ilsa. The lamplight lay dead between them, and the child knocked again, softly, like a very small oath asking to be let in.
