## 7. The Child Who Hears Gods

The rain found them an hour past Vharn's outer wards, and by the time the light died it had gone from rain to something with intent behind it — a cold, needling downpour that turned the road to porridge and made even Wren stop complaining about her boots. Which was how I knew we were in trouble. Wren complained about everything. Silence from Wren was a weather warning all its own.

"There," she said, lifting her chin toward a smudge of orange in the dark. "Light. Someone's home."

"Someone's home means someone's questions," Ilsa said. She had taken point since the city, moving like a woman who expected the world to bite her, and I hated that I kept matching my steps to hers without meaning to.

"It also means walls and a roof," Wren said, "and I have ink in places ink should never be, and if I don't get dry soon I'm going to dissolve into a puddle of very expensive failure."

We went to the light.

It came from a squat stone house at the crook of the river, one of those buildings that looked like it had been arguing with the floodplain for two hundred years and losing slowly but with dignity. Smoke curled from a chimney that leaned like a drunk telling a secret. A garden lay drowned and dark around it, rows of something green surrendering to the mud.

Ilsa knocked. I stood where I could see the door and the window both, hand loose near my knife, because that was the shape my body made when it didn't know what else to do.

The door opened on a chain, then not on a chain, and a child looked out at us.

She couldn't have been more than ten. Small for it, even — thin wrists, a face all elbows and angles, hair chopped short with what looked like shears and no particular plan. She wore a man's shirt belted twice around the waist and boots that had belonged to someone with bigger feet and better luck. Her eyes were the problem. Most children look at armed strangers the way sparrows look at hawks: still, calculating, ready to bolt. This child looked at us like she'd been expecting us. Like we were late.

"You're wet," she said.

"We're aware," Ilsa said.

"You're four." The girl counted us with a finger, unhurried. "One sword-woman who sleeps facing doors. One ink-lady who talks when she's scared. One big quiet one—" she nodded at me, and something in my chest went strange, "—and one ghost."

"We're not a ghost," Wren started.

"I wasn't talking to you." The girl stepped back and pulled the door wide. "Come in before the river changes its mind about you. It does that."

* * *

Her name was Pip. She gave it the way children give names — flatly, as a fact, no ceremony — and then put us to work, which was somehow more disarming than any welcome would have been. There was wood to stack under the eave, a leak over the hearth to catch in a dented kettle, soup already on that smelled of onion and river-fish and something green I couldn't name. She directed Ilsa to the wood without hesitation, which told me either the child had no fear or excellent judgment, and Ilsa went, which told me the captain was more rattled than she let on.

The house inside was one room and a loft, and it was full of paper.

Not books, exactly — though there were books, water-swollen and dog-eared, stacked into furniture. Paper everywhere else: nailed to beams, layered on tables, hanging from strings strung across the ceiling like laundry. Drawings. Lists. Chalk-rubbings of gravestones. And writing — columns and columns of small, careful script in a hand too old for the child who owned it.

Wren drifted toward the papers the way I drift toward exits. "Whose is all this?"

"Mine," Pip said.

"You wrote this?" Wren lifted a sheet. Her voice did the thing it did when she was interested and trying not to be — went precise, clipped, a scholar pretending at boredom. "This is liturgical notation. Old liturgical notation. Nobody's used this form since—"

"Since the god stopped answering," Pip said. "I know. He doesn't answer. But he still says things. Somebody has to write them down."

The kettle caught another drip. The fire popped. Ilsa came back in with an armload of wood and stopped dead just inside the door, looking at the paper forest, and I watched her face do something complicated and quickly hide it.

"Eat first," Pip ordered, ladling soup into mismatched bowls. "Sad people talk better with soup. That's just true."

"We're not sad," Ilsa said.

Pip looked at her the way you'd look at a fish claiming it could climb. Then she handed her a bowl anyway.

* * *

I ate because eating is a discipline and disciplines are what hold me together, and because the soup was good, and because refusing food from a starving child in a leaking house felt like a sin even my silent god might notice. Around me the others talked — Wren interrogating Pip gently about her sources, Ilsa asking careful, soldierly questions about the road, the wardens, whether anyone came here looking for runaways. Pip answered everything and nothing. She had the trick of it, the same trick I'd spent twenty years learning: give them true words that aren't the truth they want.

I stayed quiet. Quiet was my country. I had been its only citizen for six years.

But the papers bothered me. Not their existence — orphaned children collect strange things; grief makes magpies of us all. What bothered me was the notation. I knew it. I had no right to know it, and knowing it sat in my stomach like swallowed river-stone, but I knew it. My company's chaplain — Brother Cassian, who drank and swore and wept at solstices — had taught it to us around fires exactly like this one, saying *if he ever speaks again, you'll want to know how to listen.* We had learned it the way soldiers learn anything: badly, jokingly, and completely.

Nobody had used that notation since the god went silent. Since our god went silent. Since the Oathbound stopped hearing anything but their own hearts.

So why did a river-orphan in the mudlands outside Vharn write in it fluently?

"Pip," I said. It was the first word I'd spoken since the door. She looked up at me across the fire, and again that terrible expectation in her face, that patience, like a ferryman waiting for a passenger who keeps circling the dock. "Where did you learn this?"

"Nowhere," she said. "He gives it to me."

"He being—"

"The quiet one." She said it simply, without drama, the way you'd say *the north wind*. "The one who stopped talking. He talks through me sometimes. Mostly at night. Mostly when someone nearby has something they need to say and can't."

"That's not how gods work," Ilsa said flatly.

"How would you know?" Pip asked, genuinely curious, not cruel. "Have you met one?"

Silence went around the table like a passed dish. Wren set down her spoon. Ilsa's jaw worked. And I sat very still, because the honest answer was *yes, once, and then never again*, and I had built six years of walls over that answer and did not intend to let a child with a ladle pull them down.

"The Oathbound," Wren said carefully — she'd gotten the story out of me somewhere on the road, in pieces, the way you give up contraband — "swore themselves to a god whose name isn't written anywhere. A nameless one. The god of oaths kept in good faith. Ten years ago he went silent. All his sworn servants stopped feeling him."

"I know," Pip said.

"You know," Wren repeated.

"He mentions it." She shrugged, a small sharp motion. "A lot, actually. When it comes up. Which is more often than you'd think, because—" and here she turned and looked directly at me, and the firelight did something unkind to her young face, making it older, making it certain, "—he mostly talks about you."

The room got very small.

I am not a woman who frightens easily. I have stood in shield-lines with arrows coming down like black rain. I have held a pass for three days against odds that would make a bard lie. Fear and I are old colleagues; we nod in the street. But that — a mud-splattered child, ten years old, saying *he mostly talks about you* in a voice like a bell struck once — that went through every wall I owned as if the walls were paper, and I understood suddenly why she'd hung so much paper in this house. Maybe she needed the world padded. Maybe when you carry a god's voice, everything else needs to be soft.

"What did he say," I said. Not a question. A demand. My hands had gone flat on the table without my permission.

"Tonight? Nothing yet." Pip cocked her head, listening to some interior weather. "He's quiet tonight. He gets quiet when he's sad. But last month—" she slid off her bench, crossed to a string of hanging pages, and took one down with the care of a priest handling relics. "Last month he said this. Over and over. Three nights running. I wrote it down in his marks because he likes his own marks best."

She set the page in front of me.

I read it.

And the world tilted.

* * *

The notation was Cassian's — the old Oathbound cipher, vowels as knots, consonants as strokes, meant to be read aloud rather than seen. I hadn't spoken it in six years. My mouth remembered anyway. Words assembled themselves behind my eyes before I consciously translated, the way a scar aches before a storm:

*I swore it alone in the dark. No witness but the rain. If no one else keeps faith, let it be me. Let it be me until the end of the fire.*

I knew those words.

I had written those words.

Six years ago. In the smoking ruin of our last camp, after Vane's betrayal, after the ambush that should have killed us all and did kill everyone but me. I had knelt in the mud with my company's blood soaking through my knees and sworn — aloud, alone, to a god who had already gone silent — that I would keep the oath for all of them. Every dead mouth. Every broken vow. I would carry it alone, unwitnessed, unrelieved, until the end of the fire.

No one heard me. No one could have. I had checked — God help me, I had crawled the perimeter of that ruin afterward, half-mad with grief, checking for survivors, for witnesses, for anyone — and there was no one. Rain and corpses and me.

And this child had written it down a month ago, in a cipher she claimed no one taught her, three nights' walk from where I'd spoken it, years after I'd spoken it, word for word.

"No," I said.

"Yes," Pip said gently.

"No. You found it somewhere. Someone wrote it down, some survivor, some—"

"Paloma." Wren's voice, soft. "Look at the margin."

There was a mark in the margin. A small one, almost nothing — a knot-vowel repeated three times, the notation for *witnessed*. Under the old forms, a vow marked *witnessed* was binding beyond retraction. It meant the god himself had stood present at the swearing.

I had sworn unwitnessed. I had been certain of it. Certain the way you're certain of the weight of your own bones.

"He was there," Pip said. "That night. In the rain. He always comes when someone swears alone in the dark — that's his favorite place, he told me, that's where he lives, in the space between a person and the promise they mean. He was there. He heard you. He just couldn't answer yet." She hesitated, and for the first time the child-armor slipped and underneath was just a kid, tired, holding something heavy. "He wanted to. He says sorry a lot. Did you know gods can be sorry?"

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs simply reported for duty and moved me, away from the table, away from the page, toward the wall, because walls were what I understood.

"Paloma," Ilsa said, and there was something in her voice I refused to name.

"Don't." 

"Six years I thought—"

"*Don't.*"

Because if I let her speak, if I let anyone speak, I was going to come apart, and I had carried this thing so long and so carefully that I no longer knew who I was without its weight. Six years I had believed my god had abandoned me — abandoned *us*, all of us, at the moment we needed him most, leaving his sworn soldiers to die unheard. I had built my whole self on that abandonment. It was load-bearing. Take it out and everything above it came down.

And now a child with a ladle was telling me he'd been there all along. Kneeling in the rain beside me. Unable to speak — *why unable, why silent, what could silence a god* — but present. Witnessing. Keeping the only promise left to him: to hear.

My hands were shaking. I watched them shake with a kind of distant interest, a mercenary's detachment, cataloguing tremor and dampness in the eyes the way you'd catalogue enemy movements, and then the detachment failed, all at once, like a rope parting strand by strand and then all at once.

I wept.

Not gracefully. Not the single beautiful tear of storybooks. I put my forehead against the cold stone of Pip's wall and I sobbed like a gut-shot animal, six years of it, the whole buried country of it opening at once — Cassian teaching ciphers by firelight, Marta laughing with her mouth full, the twins' terrible harmonies, the morning the camp burned, the mud, the blood coming warm through my kneepads, the vow torn out of me raw, and then the long gray silence after, the silence I had mistaken for absence, the silence that had been presence all along, listening, helpless, *sorry* —

The house held me while it happened. That's the only way I can say it. Nobody spoke. Wren quietly moved the soup off the table so I wouldn't knock it. Ilsa stood between me and the door, facing outward, on guard, giving me the one gift she knew how to give: her back, her watchfulness, her refusal to leave. And Pip sat cross-legged by the fire with her head tipped, listening to something, nodding occasionally, small and patient as a stone in a stream.

When it was over — minutes or hours, I couldn't say — I turned around. My face felt scoured. New skin.

"He says," Pip reported, in the tone of a child relaying a message she'd been given to deliver, "that you kept it. All six years. Alone. And that keeping it was the answering. That oaths kept in the dark are the only prayers he can hear anymore, and yours was the loudest thing in the world."

"Why can't he answer?" My voice was wrecked. I didn't care. "If he hears, if he's there — why the silence?"

Pip's face changed. The certainty flickered, and underneath it was fear — real fear, a child's fear, sudden and total.

"Because somebody took his voice," she said. "He told me that much. Something under a mountain holds a debt so old it binds even him. He's been trying to get free of it for a hundred years." She looked up at me, and the firelight caught her eyes, and I saw that she had known — had known since the moment she opened the door and counted us, one sword-woman, one ink-lady, one big quiet one, one ghost — exactly why we had come. "He says you're going to fix it. He says it like he's afraid to say it. Like he's been wrong before."

Outside, the rain eased. Somewhere upriver, thunder rolled — long and low, less sound than pressure, the sky clearing its throat.

And far to the east, past the drowned garden and the dark hills, the mountain that held Vharn's foundations showed for one heartbeat between clouds — a black shape against a lesser black, vast and patient, wearing its crown of lights like a debtor wears chains.

Pip looked at it. So did I.

"He's stopped talking again," she whispered. "He only ever goes this quiet when They're listening."
