## 9. Descent Beneath the Mountain

The tithe-shaft opened in the mountain's flank like a wound that had learned to smile.

That was Wren's phrase, not mine. She'd said it while tracing the seam of the entrance with two fingers, her scribe's eye reading the stone the way I read tracks — and she'd smiled when she said it, a thin, unhappy smile, because that was the thing about Vharn and everything beneath it: even the ugly parts had been made beautiful, on purpose, so no one would ever think to grieve them.

The shaft mouth was an arch of white granite, carved with figures dancing hand in hand. Generations of rain had softened their faces into smoothness. You could stand there, as Pip stood now, head tipped back, and see only a pretty door. You had to look closer — as I looked closer — to see that every dancing figure wore a collar, and every collar was linked to the next by a chain of tiny, meticulous chisel-work, running around the arch and down into the dark.

"They called them tithes," Wren said. "Souls owed to the Court, paid yearly. This was the delivery door."

"How many?" Ilsa asked. Her voice was flat the way a blade is flat before it goes in.

Wren's fingers moved over the arch, counting without touching. "The carvings repeat in panels of nine," she said. "There are... a great many panels."

Pip let go of my hand for the first time since we'd left the smuggler's road. She walked up to the nearest figure — a dancer no bigger than my palm, its face worn to nothing — and pressed her own small palm against it.

"She was cold," Pip reported, matter-of-fact, terrible. "But she liked the dancing part. Somebody gave her that much."

Nobody answered her. There wasn't anything to say. I put my hand on her shoulder and drew her back, and we went into the mountain together, four of us and one dying dragon's borrowed spark, down a hole made pretty so the paying would be easier to bear.

* * *

The first hundred fathoms were ordinary rock, and I hated how grateful I was for it. Ordinary rock is honest. It doesn't look at you.

We descended by way of a spiral stair cut into the shaft wall, steps worn shallow in their centers by centuries of feet — feet that came down carrying debts and did not come back up. Rungs of black iron were bolted alongside for cargo. Crates, presumably. Cages, possibly. I didn't let myself finish the thought. Ilsa went first with a lantern, then Pip between us where the drop couldn't reach her, then Wren, and I took the rear out of habit — rear guard, the position you give whoever you trust least to run, or whoever you trust most to stay. With me it had always meant both.

The air changed at the second turning. Not colder — deeper places are always colder, everyone knows that — but *thicker*. The lantern flame leaned toward the center of the shaft like a plant toward sun, and the dark below us had a color to it, a faint gold-brown haze, the exact shade of light through cider, through honey, through amber.

"Gods below," Wren whispered, and then, catching herself, "forgive the figure of speech."

"What?" I asked.

She didn't answer. She just raised the lantern higher, and the light caught the shaft wall beside her, and I saw what she'd already seen.

They were in the rock.

Faces. Figures. Whole people, suspended in the stone the way insects hang in amber — not pressed against it, not carved into it, but *within* it, sunk to varying depths, some barely dimpling the surface and others far down in the gold-brown heart of the mountain, small with distance, drifting. A farmer with his hoe still in his fist. A woman mid-turn as if someone had called her name. A child curled like a question mark. They weren't moving. They weren't dead, either; I knew dead, and this wasn't it. Dead lets go of the face. These faces were all still *holding on*.

"Forfeited souls," Wren said quietly. "Defaulted debts. When the Court collects, it doesn't destroy what it takes. Destruction wastes value." She swallowed. "It stores it."

I had seen battlefields. I had seen what men do to each other when they've stopped seeing each other. I thought I had finished being able to be shocked. But there is a difference between cruelty done quickly, in blood and noise, and cruelty done slowly, administratively, over a hundred years, in a place so beautiful no one thinks to scream — and something in my chest went over a cliff I hadn't known was there.

"The strata," Wren went on, because she was a scribe and knowledge was the only prayer she had. "See how they're layered? Each band is an era of collection. That dense layer—" she pointed to a vein where the figures crowded thick as river gravel, "—that was the Famine Tithe, I'd guess. And that sparse band above it, the years the Court was feeling merciful."

"Merciful," Ilsa repeated. The word cracked in half coming out of her.

"Merciful," Wren confirmed. "Fewer collections. Same right to collect."

Pip had gone very still. She does that — goes still the way water goes still before it freezes. Then she said, in a voice too old for her body, "They can hear us. Not the words. The *wanting*. They all still want things." A pause. "Mostly they want to be remembered by name."

So we did the only decent thing available to us on that stair. We walked down slowly, and Wren read the names off the oldest, deepest figures from the ledger-memory she carried in her skull — names of debtors from contracts three centuries dead — and spoke each one aloud into the dark. And whether it helped them or only helped us, I will never know. But the gold-brown dark seemed, after a while, less like honey and more like soil. Things grow in soil.

* * *

It began at the third turning, or maybe the fourth. Time goes strange in a spiral; you measure it in turnings or you don't measure it at all.

At first I thought it was the lantern. Heat on my forearm, a line of warmth along the inside of my left arm where the oath-mark lived — the brand every sworn member of my company carried, the silent god's sigil burned into us at our swearing, cool and quiet for ten years like a scar should be. I pushed back my sleeve.

The sigil was glowing.

Not bright. Not yet. A dull ember-color under the skin, the way coals show through ash, and warm — warm the way a fever is warm, warmth with an opinion about it.

"Paloma?" Pip was looking at my arm. Of course she was. Children notice everything and understand most of it.

"It's nothing," I said. "Old wound. Deep places disagree with it."

Which was a lie, and the mark knew it, and flared as if in rebuke — one hard pulse of heat that made me bite down on my tongue. I pulled the sleeve down and kept walking.

By the sixth turning it wasn't deniable. The glow bled through the wool of my sleeve, a faint orange tracery, and the heat had spread — up past my elbow now, following veins I could suddenly feel individually, each one a thin wire of fire. And with the heat came something else, something worse than pain: *pulling*. The same hook-behind-the-ribs feeling from the night Wren read my name aloud, except inverted, downward-pointing, as if the oath itself were a fish and something far below was reeling.

"Stop." Ilsa's hand on my shoulder. She'd been watching me sidelong for two turnings; betrayal teaches a person to watch. "You're marked. Show me."

"I'm fine."

"You're *glowing*, Sterling." She set the lantern down and gripped my wrist before I could stop her, and turned my arm to the light, and pushed the sleeve to my elbow — and went white.

Because it wasn't just the sigil anymore. From the brand, fine lines of ember-light ran outward across my skin like cracks in fired clay, branching, precise, deliberate — and I saw then, with the particular horror of understanding arriving all at once, that they weren't random. They were *letters*. Script in a language older than any Wren kept, winding down my arm, and more of it appearing as we descended, sentence by sentence, the oath writing itself visible the deeper I sank toward its creditor.

"It's the oath," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "The vow we swore. It's — being read. Down here. Where everything gets read."

Wren had come back up two steps to look, and her face did the thing faces do when a scholar meets a fact that rearranges the furniture of the world. "Your contract," she breathed. "Paloma — your oath isn't a vow, it's a *document*. It was always a document. Sworn in good faith, witnessed by your god — and filed somewhere." Her eyes lifted, traveled down the golden dark of the shaft, down and down toward the bottom of the world. "*Filed here.*"

The pulling in my chest yanked, hard, once, like a hooked line testing its catch.

"And everything filed here," Ilsa said slowly, "belongs to the Court."

Silence, then. Just the drip of water somewhere below, and Pip humming — she hums when she's frightened, a little tuneless thread of sound — and the soft, awful glow of my own arm lighting the stair better than the lantern did.

"Then why hasn't it collected me?" I asked. "Ten years of silence, and the moment I come down here it starts — why wait?"

"Because," Wren said, and I watched her arrive at it the way you watch someone walk into a trap they built themselves, "a collection requires a *default*. An unpaid term. Your oath has no terms, Paloma. It was sworn open-ended, freely, in good faith — which means it's the one instrument in this entire mountain the Court can't foreclose on." She looked at me, and behind the fear in her eyes there was something else, something almost like awe. "It can't take it. It can only *read* it. And it wants it very badly."

* * *

We camped at the ninth turning, on a landing where the stair widened into what must once have been a toll-chamber — there was a desk built into the wall, stone, ancient, its surface worn glossy by centuries of elbows, and a ledger-niche empty as a socket. Ilsa barred the single doorway with her knife driven into the frame-crack, old soldier's trick, and we ate cold ration bread and tried not to look at the wall.

It was impossible not to look at the wall. On this level the souls sat close to the surface, near enough that the lantern showed their faces clearly: a young man with an accountant's ink-stains still on his fingers; an old woman whose lips were parted mid-word; a soldier in armor a century out of fashion, his hand still raised in a salute no one had returned. The gold-brown stone held them the way winter holds a field — totally, patiently, without malice, which is the worst way to hold anything.

Pip sat closest to them. She always does. She'd tucked herself against the wall between the old woman and the soldier, knees up, arms around them, listening.

"The soldier's name is Aldric Vey," she said presently. "He saluted because he thought the person collecting him was an officer. He waited a long time for someone to salute back."

I looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at the floor. Then, stiffly, like a joint that hadn't bent in years, she raised two fingers to her brow and held them there — a mercenary's salute, company-style, the kind you give a fallen sister — and held it, and held it, until her hand shook, before letting it fall.

"Aldric Vey," she said aloud. "Captain of the Ninth Tithe-Watch. Honored." And the soldier's face, in the stone, did not change — but the lantern flame stood perfectly straight for the space of three breaths, as if something had drawn itself up to attention, and then flickered, and was only a flame again.

I had to get up and check the door-bar twice after that, and neither time was because I feared pursuit.

My arm had not cooled. If anything the script had spread past my elbow in the hours since, curling toward my shoulder, and lying down was a lesson in discomfort — every pulse of the pull landed in my sternum now, steady as a second heartbeat, patient as interest accruing. I lay on my bedroll watching the ember-lines write themselves another clause along my collarbone and understood, finally, viscerally, what Wren had been teaching me since Vharn: that in this country I was not a person with a sword. I was a page. And pages do not get to choose what is written on them — unless, some stubborn voice in me added, they are willing to tear.

Sleep took me sideways, unwilling, and gave me back to the descent before I felt rested.

* * *

On the eleventh day — or the eleventh turning; the distinction had rotted away — the shaft ended.

Or rather, the stair ended, and the shaft continued, but differently: below us the walls fell away entirely, and the tithe-shaft opened into a vast descending hollow, tier upon tier vanishing downward into a glow that rose from beneath like the memory of a sunset. Bridges arched across the hollow at intervals, slender and white, and on the lowest tiers we could see movement — figures, processions, the glint of official things. Somewhere down there a bell tolled, slow and silvery, marking an hour nobody up top had ever needed to count.

"The Undercourt," Wren said. She said it the way you name a diagnosis.

And my arm chose that moment to speak.

The heat crested — not pain exactly, pain would have been simpler — a full-body resonance, the sigil blazing through cloth and skin until I could have read by it, the script racing up my throat, across my jaw, and I dropped to one knee on the final stair with a sound I'm not proud of, and Pip was there instantly, small hands on my burning arm, and her eyes went wide.

"It's not hurting you," she said. "Paloma. Listen. It's *knocking*."

"Knocking," Ilsa repeated, flat.

"At a door." Pip pressed her palm flat against the brightest line of script, and her face went distant, the god-distance, the listening-place. "A big door. Way down. It's saying—" her brow furrowed, translating, and whatever she heard next made her look up at me with an expression I will carry to my grave, half pity and half wonder. "It's saying *finally*. Like it's been waiting at the door for ten years with its hand raised, and nobody home."

The bell below tolled again. And out across the great hollow, from the lowest tier, a light answered my arm — a cold, orderly, administrative gleam, row upon row of lamps being lit in perfect synchrony, one after another after another, spreading outward like ink through water.

Something down there had noticed the page walking in on its own feet.

"They know we're here," Ilsa said, drawing her sword, though there was nothing yet to fight.

"No," Wren said softly, staring down at the spreading lamplight, and then at my arm, and then at me, with the expression of a woman rereading a contract she'd signed too fast. "Worse. They know *what* we're carrying."

The bell tolled a third time. And far below, at the foot of the last bridge, a delegation began to assemble — small at this distance, formal, unhurried — to receive us.

To receive *me*.

I got up off my knee. I rolled my sleeve down over the burning script, though we all knew it was pointless now; the light bled through the wool like dawn through a shutter. I checked my blade, and Pip's hand found mine, and Ilsa took point, and Wren squared her shoulders with the ledger of names still whispering behind her teeth.

Whatever waited at the bottom of that hollow had been waiting ten years for me to knock.

It had just never imagined I'd bring witnesses.
