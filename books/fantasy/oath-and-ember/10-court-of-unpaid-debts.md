## 10. Court of Unpaid Debts

The tithe-shaft opened like a throat.

Wren Halloway had spent eleven years in the archives of Vharn's surface court, and she had believed herself prepared for anything the Undercourt could show her. She had read the ledgers. She had copied them, in her neat scribe's hand, thousands of pages of obligations and forfeitures and terms of surrender. She had thought paper was paper, that ink was ink, that a contract was only words arranged in an order that made cruelty legal.

She had not understood that down here, the words were *load-bearing*.

They stepped out of the shaft onto a gallery of black stone, and Wren's breath left her in a sound she would be ashamed of later — a small, involuntary *oh*, the sound of a girl seeing the sea for the first time.

The Undercourt did not look like a court. It looked like a cathedral that had been designed by an accountant with a poet's soul and no conscience whatsoever.

It rose in tiers, vast beyond reason, carved from the mountain's living rock and then *improved* upon, because nothing here was ever allowed to simply be what it was. Everything had been leveraged. The pillars were not pillars but columns of compressed debt — Wren could see it now, with the sight the descent had given her, the way one sees the grain in wood once it has been pointed out: every block of stone was inscribed, microscopically, edge to edge, with names. Thousands of names per pillar. Millions overall. Souls who had owed something to someone, once, and whose owing had been rendered into masonry.

The light came from chandeliers that were not crystal. They were promises. Each facet caught the glow of the deep-flame vents below and refracted it into colors that hurt to name — a gold that tasted like relief, a blue that felt like the moment a debt is forgiven, a red like the instant before you sign something you shouldn't. Wren recognized the craft. She had catalogued its surface-court imitations for years, little glamour-lamps sold to merchants who wanted their counting-houses to feel trustworthy. These were the originals. These were made from the real thing.

"Stop staring," Ilsa Vane murmured at her shoulder. The captain's voice was flat, professional, and entirely unconvincing; her hand had gone to a sword hilt she'd been forced to leave wrapped and peace-bonded at the shaft's mouth, because the Undercourt did not permit armed guests, only indebted ones. "Staring marks you as new. New is *expensive*."

"Everything here is expensive," Wren said. "That's rather the point."

Paloma said nothing. She had said very little since the strata began — since they had walked down through the layers of ambered souls embedded in the rock like insects in honey, each one faintly luminous, each one faintly *aware*. Wren had watched Paloma press her palm against one without thinking, some farmer's soul from three centuries back, and watched the farmer's light lean toward her touch like a plant toward sun. Since then Paloma walked with her arms crossed tight, as if holding herself shut.

Pip held Wren's hand. The child had gone quiet in the way that frightened Wren more than screaming would have — Pip, who chattered at gods and ghosts and ward-beasts alike, had looked out over the tiers of the Undercourt and simply stopped talking. Her small fingers were cold.

"It's beautiful," Pip said finally, in a whisper. "That's the worst part, isn't it? It's *beautiful*."

"Yes," Wren said. "Yes, it is."

* * *

They descended the great stair in the company of their escort — two magistrates' clerks in grey silk, faces smooth as vellum, who had met them at the shaft's foot with documents already prepared. Of course they had. Wren had forged their passage papers herself, in the archive-city above, and she had done it well, but she understood now that the forgery had never really mattered. The Court had known they were coming the way a ledger knows when a page turns. You did not sneak into the Undercourt. You were *entered* into it.

The clerks walked ahead without speaking. Their footsteps made no sound on the stone, which meant, Wren realized with a lurch, that they had no weight here — or rather, that their weight was recorded elsewhere. Everything about them was recorded elsewhere. She wondered what column of what book held the clerk walking on her left, and whether he knew, and whether knowing would change anything, and suspected the answer to both questions was no.

"You're doing the thing," Ilsa muttered beside her.

"What thing?"

"The reading thing. Your eyes go all—" Ilsa waved vaguely. "Archival."

"I'm a scribe, Captain. Reading is the whole of my personality." But Wren slowed, because they had reached the first landing, and the landing held something she needed to see properly.

It was a fountain. That was the polite word for it. A basin of black glass, and rising from it not water but a slow, silver upwelling of — Wren leaned close, ignoring the clerk's disapproving pause — *obligation*, rendered liquid. Promises, dissolved. She could pick phrases out of the shimmer the way one picks stars out of the night sky: *I will return by harvest. I will care for her always. I will pay it back, I swear it, I swear.* Each promise glowed briefly as it surfaced, and then sank again, and was drunk down by the basin, and became part of the Court's beauty.

"They drink oaths," Paloma said behind her. Her voice was strange. Hollowed. "That's what this is. They take the things people swear to each other and they—"

"Bottle them. Refine them. Spend them." Wren straightened. Her hands had gone cold; she tucked them into her sleeves where no one could see them shake. "I told you power here is written, not fought. I undersold it. Power here is *sworn*. Every honest promise anyone has ever made is raw material, if you can get your hooks into it before it's kept." She gestured at the fountain. "A kept oath belongs to the people who made it. An *unkept* one floats around loose, and the Court collects the loose ones. That's the entire business model."

"That's obscene," Ilsa said.

"That's *accounting*," said the left-hand clerk, without turning around. His voice was pleasant. It was the most horrifying thing Wren had heard yet, and she had heard a god-touched child repeat words sworn alone in the dark. "We prefer the term *stewardship*. Unkept obligations are a waste. We ensure nothing is wasted."

Wren bit down on eleven years of trained politeness and the newer, sharper thing underneath it, and said nothing, and wrote everything down — mentally, in the shorthand she used for things too dangerous to commit to paper.

* * *

The Hall of Standing Terms received them the way a spider receives weather: with total indifference.

It was the central chamber of the Undercourt, though *chamber* was inadequate; it was a cavern dressed as a courtroom dressed as a temple. The ceiling was so far above them that the chandeliers of promises hung like constellations. The floor was a single slab of polished obsidian, and across it ran lines of gold — Wren counted the converging paths, lost count past a hundred — every line running from a door somewhere in the dark perimeter toward a central dais, where the magistrates sat.

There were nine of them. Nine chairs, nine figures, and Wren's archival eye did what it always did under stress, which was categorize.

Three were old in the way mountains are old, robed in ledgers — actual bound volumes, sewn into the fabric of their garments, spines cracked with use. Two were young and beautiful in the specific way of people whose faces have been purchased rather than grown. One was mostly shadow and a voice. One appeared to be a woman from the waist up and a stack of contracts from the waist down, which should have been absurd and instead was merely terrible. One wore no face at all, only a page where a face should be, blank and waiting.

And the ninth —

The ninth magistrate stood when they entered, which none of the others did, and Wren understood immediately that this was the chief, and that the standing was theater, and that the theater was the point. He looked like a grandfather. That was the trap of him. Silver hair, kind creases around his eyes, a cardigan of deep green wool that belonged in a country house by a fire, and hands folded gently over a single slim volume.

"Wren Halloway," he said warmly. "Court scribe, second class, discharged for irregularities in the matter of the Aldery census. We've been expecting your hand for some time. It's a fine hand. Precise. Honest-looking." He smiled. "Honest-looking hands are worth ten honest ones, down here."

"I'm flattered," Wren said, "that you read my personnel file."

"We read everything. It's what we're *for*." He gestured, and chairs rose from the obsidian to meet them — smooth, silent, upholstered in something soft as skin. Wren sat because refusing to sit would have been a statement, and statements were priced. Beside her, Paloma remained standing until the chief magistrate's smile widened by exactly one degree, and then she sat too, jaw tight, dragging Pip down beside her.

"Now," the magistrate said, folding his kind hands. "You've come about the Ember contract. Everyone comes about the Ember contract, eventually. Dragons are sentimental creatures, and sentiment generates litigation." He opened the slim volume on his knee. "But before we discuss terms — and we will discuss terms, everyone discusses terms — I wonder if I might show you something first. A courtesy. Call it a tour of our holdings."

He snapped his fingers, or perhaps he didn't; perhaps the sound was only the turning of a page somewhere enormous.

The floor lit up.

* * *

Gold lines blazed along the obsidian, and where they blazed, images rose — not illusions, Wren realized, but *records*, projected up from the vaults beneath their feet the way heat-shimmer rises off summer stone. Contracts. Founding contracts. The originals.

"This," the magistrate said, strolling along the glowing lines with his hands clasped behind his back like a man showing off his garden, "is the Charter of the Ninth Tithe, year one of the Court. Note the clause structure — elegant, for its era. This one established the principle of collateralized birth. Every child born within our jurisdiction enters the world already owing the cost of its own making. We find it simplifies matters. No one can claim they never agreed; they simply inherit the agreement, the way they inherit their eyes."

The image turned. Another document.

"The Concordat of the Grey Harvest. Famine year. Three provinces mortgaged their dead to us in exchange for grain. The grain was real. The dead were real. The interest compounded for sixty years, and now those provinces send us a tithe of souls annually, and their harvests have never failed since, and everyone is quite satisfied, and if you ask any farmer there whether the arrangement is just, he will say yes, because his grandfather signed, and gratitude is heritable too."

Another. Another. Wren watched the history of the world scroll past her in instruments of surrender, and her scribe's soul — the part of her that loved documents, that had loved them since childhood, that had gone into the archives the way other girls went into convents — sickened and grieved, because the documents were *beautiful*. That was the unforgivable part. Whoever had drafted these had been magnificent. The clauses interlocked like joinery. The language sang. There was not one wasted word, not one loophole left open, not one mercy that had not been converted into leverage.

Corruption, Wren had always believed, was sloppy. Greedy men kept bad books. She had built her whole quiet rebellion on that faith — that somewhere in the ledgers there must be errors, gaps, arithmetic mistakes she could exploit.

There were no mistakes. There had never been any mistakes. The evil was *peer-reviewed*.

Then the next image rose, and Wren forgot to breathe.

It was smaller than the others. Older. A single sheet, and even projected vast across the obsidian floor, Wren could see that the original was written on dragon-vellum — pale, faintly iridescent, taken from a creature that had given its skin willingly, because in the old days dragons signed their greatest pacts on themselves. The script was archaic High Court hand, and the seal at the bottom was pressed in wax the color of banked coals.

Beside her, Paloma went rigid.

"The Sterling Concession," the magistrate said softly, and his warm grandfather's voice wrapped around the words like a blanket around a child. "Year thirty-one of the Court. Signed by one Maren Sterling, of the valley settlement, acting head of household and oath-keeper of her line." He glanced sideways at Paloma, kindly, terribly kindly. "You have her jaw, did you know?"

"Don't," Paloma said.

"The terms are simple. Elegant, even. In exchange for perpetual protection — the valley's magic-deadness, which you have lived inside all your life and called safety — the Sterling line conveys to this Court one asset." He turned the page. The asset was described in a single line, and Wren read it upside down from where she sat, because she could not help it, because reading was the whole of her personality:

*One (1) juvenile dragon of the Ashfall line, being the heir apparent, together with all fire, flame, and vitality appertaining thereto, in perpetuity.*

"Soul, fire, and future," the magistrate recited, almost tenderly. "Sold by her own grandmother. Witnessed by two elders of the valley. Notarized by our founding clerk, whose hand, I'll note, was nearly as fine as yours." He closed the slim volume on his knee. "Your valley has eaten well for a hundred years, Miss Sterling. Every meal funded. Every winter survived. Every child raised safe behind walls paid for by a candle burning in our cellar. You have been living on credit your entire life."

The silence afterward had texture. Wren felt it press against her eardrums.

Pip moved first. The child slid her small cold hand into Paloma's clenched fist and held on, and said nothing, which from Pip was louder than prophecy.

Paloma stared at the fading image of her grandmother's signature — and Wren watched her read it, really read it, the way Wren read everything: the pressure of the pen, the hesitation in the descender of the final letter, the tiny tremor that said *the hand that wrote this was crying, or shaking, or both*. And Wren saw the exact moment the interpretation landed, the moment Paloma understood that her grandmother had not been a monster, had probably been a desperate woman in a starving valley signing away something she barely comprehended to save the people in front of her — and that this changed nothing, and that it changing nothing was the cruelest thing of all.

"There it is," Paloma said at last, very quietly. "The paper trail."

"There always is," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "That's what I've been trying to tell all of you since the valley. Empires don't run on cruelty. Cruelty is just the interest payment. They run on *signatures*." She stood, because her legs demanded it, because sitting felt like complicity. "And signatures, my lord magistrate, can be examined. Challenged. *Voided*. That's also what they're for."

The chief magistrate regarded her for a long moment. Around the dais, the eight other magistrates had not moved at all throughout — not one breath, not one blink, and Wren understood suddenly that they might not need to breathe, that they might be, themselves, nothing but terms wearing robes.

"A scribe after my own heart," he said warmly. "Examination. Challenge. Yes. We honor all of those processes — we invented them, in fact. Our dispute procedures are legendary. Come." He spread his hands, magnanimous, and the great doors at the chamber's far side swung open onto a corridor lined, wall to ceiling, with shelves. "Let me show you where the Ember contract sleeps. And do bring your fine honest-looking hand, Miss Halloway. You may find, when you see the original up close, that there is nothing to challenge at all."

He turned and led the way, confident as a host, certain as a ledger.

And Wren followed, because following was what guests did, and guests were priced — but as she passed the last glowing line on the obsidian floor, she let her eyes drop, casual as breathing, to the corner of the Sterling Concession still lingering in the gold.

To the witness signature. The second name, small, beneath her grandmother's.

She had seen that handwriting before. Recently. In a margin note, in a certain century-old book of prophecies, in a dying dragon's keeping.

She said nothing. She filed it away in the deepest shelf of her mind, next to the dangerous things, and she smiled her honest-looking smile at the magistrate's broad back, and she followed him into the shelves.

Some paper trails, it turned out, ran both directions.
