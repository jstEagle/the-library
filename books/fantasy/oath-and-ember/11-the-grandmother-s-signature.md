## 11. The Grandmother's Signature

The contract was beautiful.

That was the first obscenity of it, the thing that made Paloma's stomach turn before her mind caught up to what she was reading. It lay on a lectern of black glass in the Court's reading gallery, and it was beautiful the way a spiderweb is beautiful when morning light catches the dew — intricate, silvered, and made for killing.

Wren had told them power here was written, not fought. Paloma had believed her the way you believe a story about weather in a country you've never seen. Now she stood beneath arches carved from what looked like frozen debt — every stone etched with names, thousands upon thousands of them, so dense the walls seemed to ripple like water — and understood that Wren had been describing this room the way a drowning woman describes the sea.

* * *

They had come to the archive-gallery under Wren's forged writs, four figures in the livery of minor clerks, moving through corridors where the air tasted of iron filings and old promises. Pip walked between them with her hand fisted in Ilsa's coat hem, eyes wide, mouth shut — a miracle of restraint that had lasted three hours now and was, Paloma suspected, nearing its natural end.

The gallery itself was circular, open to a shaft above through which no light fell; the illumination came from the contracts themselves, which glowed faintly, each one a small warm coal of someone else's obligation. Clerks moved among the shelves like pale fish. No one questioned them. Here, apparently, the deepest crime was to be unrecorded, and their forged papers made them more real than their own faces did.

"There." Wren's voice came out thin. Her finger trembled as she pointed to the lectern at the gallery's heart, raised on a dais of seven steps — seven, Paloma noted, because everything in this place counted, measured, priced. "Founding Contracts. First tier. They keep them out where petitioners can see them. It's a warning."

"A warning of what?" Ilsa asked.

"That everything," Wren said, "was always for sale."

Paloma climbed the steps. She didn't remember deciding to. Her boots sounded very loud on the glass.

* * *

The contract bore a title in script so fine it might have been stitched rather than inked: *Instrument of Perpetual Custody, Being the Soul-Fire of One Ember, Heir of Ashfall, Conveyed unto the Undercourt in Exchange for Certain Considerations Herein Named.*

Below it, two signatures.

The first she recognized from Wren's breathless briefing: the seal of the Undercourt's first magistrate, a coiled signature like a snake swallowing its own beginning.

The second —

The second stopped her heart in her chest the way a fist stops a clock.

*Sterling.*

Not her name. Not quite. But close enough to be its mother, its grandmother, its root. *Maren Sterling*, written in a strong, careful hand, the letters of a woman who had learned her letters late and valued them accordingly. Paloma knew that hand. She had seen it pressed into the leather cover of the company's oldest ledger, the one that sat behind the altar of their silent god in the valley chapel. She had traced it with her finger as a child, sitting on cold flagstones while the elders prayed to a god who never answered, and she had thought: *someday my name will mean something like hers does.*

She read the considerations named.

Her lips moved without sound at first. Then aloud, because the horror needed air to live:

"...in exchange for the conveyance herein described, the undersigned receives, for herself and all descendants of her line in perpetuity: immunity from the workings of magic; sanctity of borders unbreachable by court, craft, or curse; and the perpetual silence of divine claim upon said line..."

"No," Paloma said.

The word came out flat and stupid. A clerk nearby glanced over with mild, professional interest, the look of a man watching someone discover a fee they'd already paid.

"No," she said again, louder, and Wren was suddenly beside her, hand on her arm, whispering *carefully, carefully, not here, not like this* — but Paloma couldn't stop, because the contract kept going, kept itemizing, kept doing what this place did best, which was making ruin legible.

*Immunity from the workings of magic.* The valley's deadness. The thing every child was taught to call a blessing, a shield, the god's favor made manifest in soil and stone. Magic couldn't touch them because magic had been *sold away from them*, traded like a pelt, like teeth. Their valley wasn't protected. It was *emptied*. A pocket picked so thoroughly the victim called the absence a gift.

*Sanctity of borders.* No one leaves. The iron rule. Of course no one left. You don't walk off with collateral. She had spent thirty years inside a fence built around a debt, guarding an asset, and calling herself free.

And *the perpetual silence of divine claim* —

There it was. There was her god's silence, written down in a century-old hand, priced and purchased and filed. Her order hadn't been forsaken. They'd been *bought out*. Every prayer she'd ever spoken into that aching quiet had landed on deaf ears by contract, by clause, by her own grandmother's signature.

"They sold us," Paloma whispered. "Before we were born. Before our mothers were born."

"Paloma—" Wren started.

"And they sold *her*." Paloma's hand rose, almost without her willing it, and hovered over the second consideration, the conveyance itself, the soul-fire of one Ember, heir of Ashfall. A dragon-child. A candle in a cage a mile beneath their feet. Bought so that Maren Sterling's grandchildren could live in a hole in the world and call the hole holy.

Every meal she'd ever eaten. Every winter survived. Every night of sleep in the safe dark of the valley. All of it, funded. All of it bought with a stolen soul, and the bill had come due tonight in a book of prophecies with her name in it, because debts in this place, she understood suddenly and completely, were the only things that truly lived forever.

"I'm the interest," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "A hundred years of interest, walking around wearing my face."

* * *

What broke after that did not break loudly.

She had imagined, in the long descent, that if she found the founding contract she would feel rage, clean and hot, the kind you can swing a sword with. Instead what filled her was colder and worse: a terrible *arithmetic*. She stood there doing sums she could not stop doing. Thirty years of her life divided by one dragon-child's century of captivity. The safety of everyone she'd ever loved weighed against a single guttering flame in a contractual cage. And the sums kept coming out the same. They would always come out the same. That was what a contract was — arithmetic that had won.

Her knees found the seventh step. She didn't remember kneeling either.

Somewhere above, the arches rippled with ten thousand names, and every one of them, she thought, had knelt somewhere too, had done these same sums, had lost.

"Get it away from me," she heard herself say.

"Paloma." Wren crouched beside her, scribe-quick, already scanning the contract's margins with hungry professional eyes even as she spoke gently. "Look at me. This is what it *does*. This is the weapon. It doesn't fight you — it shows you the ledger and lets you defeat yourself—"

"Then let me defeat myself!" The words tore out of her, and heads turned across the gallery, pale clerks pausing mid-stride. She didn't care. "Don't you see? If I'd known — if anyone had *told* me — I'd have torn the valley open with my own hands a decade ago. But I didn't know, and now I do, and every good thing I ever did there was bought goods. My oath. My *oath*, Wren. I swore it on ground that was payment. What does that make me?"

"It makes you someone they lied to," Ilsa said.

Paloma hadn't heard the captain come up the steps. Ilsa Vane stood at the edge of the dais with her arms folded, wearing the expression she wore before battles she expected to lose.

"They lied to all of us," Ilsa went on. "That's the whole architecture of this place. You're standing in it. The question is whether you let it finish the job."

"The job," Paloma repeated, and laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound she'd ever made. "You want to talk about finishing jobs? Fine. Here's mine, per the prophecy. Steal back the soul. Break the contract. And do you know how contracts break here, Captain? Do you know what the clerks told Wren?" She jabbed a finger at the black-glass lectern. "*Conveyance may be dissolved only by counter-conveyance of equal or greater value, freely rendered.* Equal or greater. Freely given. So the fix is simple. Somebody signs. Somebody pays. That's how it works — that's the *only* way it works — and we both know who's standing here with a name worth something."

The gallery had gone quiet around them. Even the clerks had stopped pretending to work.

Isla's jaw tightened. "You're not signing anything."

"Somebody has to."

"Not you."

"Then *you*?" Paloma surged to her feet, and old fury — the fury she'd carried since the pass at Corren's Ford, since the day Ilsa's betrayal had left her the last keeper of a dead company's vow — came up like floodwater. "You'd love that, wouldn't you? Redemption by ledger. One clean signature and you're square with the world. Well, no. I watched you sell us once. I'm not going to watch you buy your way back."

Ilsa took it without flinching, which was somehow more infuriating. "Better me than you."

"Why?" Paloma demanded. "Because you think your soul's worth less? Because that's the arithmetic again, isn't it — somebody's got to pay, so pick the cheapest—"

"Yes," Ilsa said simply. "Yes, exactly that. Because that's what I've been worth since the ford, and paying is the only thing left I'm good for—"

"*Stop.*"

The voice was small and enormous at once, the way a child's voice is when it has never once been ignored, and every head in the gallery turned toward the bottom step.

Pip stood there with her fists clenched at her sides, and her eyes — her strange, god-touched eyes, the color of deep water — were fixed on Paloma with an expression far too old for her face.

"You're doing it wrong," Pip said.

"Pip—" Wren began.

"You're all doing the sums," Pip said, marching up the steps with the ferocious dignity of the very small, "but you're doing *their* sums. With their numbers. On their paper." She reached the lectern and planted both palms on its edge, glaring up at the beautiful, obscene document as though she could burn it by scowl alone. "It wants you angry. Did nobody notice? It's *glowing brighter* since you started shouting. It eats this. Anger is just another thing you owe, here. Get angry enough, promise enough, swear enough — and some clerk comes along with a pen and says, oh, how lovely, the lady wishes to convey her wrath, shall we value that at a soul or merely a name?"

Paloma stared at her. At the little girl who heard gods. At the small hands on the contract that had ruined her life before she was born.

Because Pip was right. She could feel it now that it was named — a faint warmth in the parchment, a hunger in the glowing ink, the sense of a mouth opening. The Court didn't need her to sign. It only needed her to *offer*. Rage freely rendered was still freely rendered. Despair, sworn aloud in a court of record, was still a vow. She had climbed these seven steps ready to shatter, and shattering, here, was currency.

The lowest point of her life, and it had a price list.

Paloma closed her eyes. Breathed. Beneath the rage, beneath the grief, she found the one thing in herself the contract had no column for: the memory of kneeling in the valley chapel at fourteen, alone in the dark, swearing an oath no one asked of her, to a god no one had heard from in years. *If any soul of ours is ever held against its will, I will come for it. I don't care whose. I don't care the cost.* Sworn freely. Kept silently. Worth nothing on any ledger in this place — and therefore worth everything.

That was hers. That had always been hers. They could sell the ground under her, the sky over her, the magic out of her blood — but that oath had been minted nowhere, signed by no magistrate, and it was the only inheritance her grandmother had left her that was clean.

She opened her eyes.

"We don't sign," she said quietly. "Any of us. We don't offer, we don't vow, we don't swear, we don't get angry *on the record*. From here on, we are the worst customers this court has ever had." She looked at Ilsa, and something passed between them that was not forgiveness — not yet, maybe not ever — but was at least a ceasefire. "Nobody buys their way square. Nobody pays. We find the other way."

"There is no other way," Wren murmured — but she said it the way she said things when she was already halfway to proving herself wrong, fingers twitching against her thigh as though counting invisible pages. "Equal or greater value, freely rendered... unless the rendering party isn't party to the instrument at all. Unless value could arrive from *outside* the contract entirely..." Her eyes had gone distant, flicking through some mental archive. "There are older laws. Pre-founding. Things the magistrates pretend not to remember..."

Above them, through the great shaft, a sound rolled down from the mountain's roots — vast, muffled, and final. Like a bell struck somewhere below the world.

The clerks froze. Every contract in the gallery flickered at once, a thousand small flames guttering.

Pip's head tipped back. Her eyes went white-rimmed, her small body rigid, and when she spoke, her voice was layered, doubled, coming from somewhere deeper than a child's chest:

*"He is spending it,"* she said. *"The old one. He is spending his last fire to open the way — and the vault will not stay shut past dawn."*

Paloma looked up at the shaft, at the darkness the sound had fallen out of, and felt the ember Ashfall had pressed into her keeping — the inheritance she'd carried against her sternum since his death — pulse once, hot as a heartbeat, answering.

The dragon was dead. His fire was not. And somewhere below them, a vault of names had just begun, slowly, irrevocably, to open.
