## 12. Ember in a Cage of Terms

The corridor of amber souls ended at a door that was not a door.

It was a clause.

Paloma saw it before she understood it — a rectangle of darkness set into the living rock, and around its frame, words. Not carved, not painted. *Written*, in the way the Court wrote everything: as though the stone itself had been made to sign something. The letters ran vertically down both jambs and across the lintel, silver-grey and small as stitching, and when she tried to read them they slid out from under her eyes like fish in shallow water. She caught only fragments — *in perpetuity*, *conditions precedent*, *the party of the first flame* — and each fragment left a faint cold taste on her tongue, like licking a coin.

"Careful," Wren said behind her. Her voice had changed since they'd come below; it was quieter here, flatter, the voice of someone reading terms aloud in a room where every word might be entered into evidence. "That's a threshold covenant. You don't open it. You *agree* to it."

"What does it say?"

"Roughly? That anything inside belongs to the Court, that anything we carry in becomes discoverable, and that by entering we accept service of process." Wren's fingers hovered over the frame without touching it. "I can draft us an exception. It'll take an hour."

"We don't have an hour." Ilsa stood at the rear with her sword drawn and pointed at the floor, which was the only way to hold a sword down here without insulting anyone. She'd been quiet since the tithe-shaft, since the strata of forfeited souls had begun to glow around them like veins of trapped honey. Quiet, but watching Paloma constantly, the way one watches a fuse. "The oath-marks are spreading. Look at her arms."

Paloma looked. She didn't need to. She could feel them — the vow she'd sworn at nineteen, kneeling in the ash of her company's chapel, rising up her skin in lines of dull gold, branching like frost across her forearms, creeping past her elbows. Every step deeper into the mountain, the old oath burned a little hotter, as if the god's silence were a held breath and this place was pressing on its chest.

"The marks mean we're close," Pip said.

The child sat cross-legged on the rock floor, which was obscene and comfortable in equal measure, because everything in the Undercourt was smooth where it should have been jagged and warm where it should have been cold. Pip's eyes were half-closed. She'd been like this for the last mile, head tilted, listening to something the rest of them couldn't hear — or wouldn't. "It's not the god," she'd said earlier, when Paloma asked what she heard. "It's older than the god. It's like... a bell that got put away in a box, but the box still hums."

Now the box hummed through the door.

"I'll take the hour," Paloma said, and hated how her voice shook. "Draft your exception. But do it fast, Wren. Because I think whatever's in there has been waiting a hundred years, and I don't intend to make it wait on paperwork."

Wren gave her a look — the scribe's look, the one that said *everything is paperwork, you absolute mercenary* — and knelt, unrolling a leather case of pens and ink and small sharp knives for scraping errors. Errors, down here, apparently being things one could cut out of reality with the right tool and the right phrasing.

Ilsa came and stood at Paloma's shoulder. Not too close. Never too close, not since the betrayal, not even after the ambush, not even after Paloma had spared her and hated herself a little for how much lighter that mercy had made her feel.

"You're going to want to promise it something," Ilsa murmured. "When you see it. Everyone wants to promise the hurt ones something. Don't. Promises are currency here. If you say it in front of the cage, it counts."

Paloma said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't already true: she had spent her whole life keeping promises in a valley where promises were the only magic, and now she stood in a country where promises were money, and she did not know yet which kind of poor she was about to become.

Wren worked. The lamp-light guttered. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of ledgered stone, the Undercourt went about its evening business — the soft chime of obligations transferring, the dry rustle of contracts renewing themselves, a sound like a forest of paper breathing.

Then Wren said, "Done," and the door was only a door.

* * *

Inside was a room the size of a chapel, and it was full of light that had no right to exist.

It came from the center. From a cage.

Not iron — Paloma had expected iron, had braced herself for iron, for chains and manacles and the crude architecture of captivity. This was worse. The cage was built of language. Bars of it, dense and interlocking, whole paragraphs twisted into rods and woven into a lattice, and between the bars hung clauses like cobwebs, sticky with implication. It was beautiful, the way a spiderweb is beautiful when the morning light catches it and you forget for one merciful second what the web is *for*. The words caught the lamplight and threw it back in colors that didn't belong to fire — pale gold, ash-rose, the blue-white of a flame starved down to its mathematical minimum.

And in the center, no bigger than a candle-flame, burning steady and low and utterly awake:

Ember.

Paloma had imagined a dragon. Of course she had. Everyone who grew up on the valley's stories imagined dragons — vast, winged, furnace-hearted, the kind of creature whose passing scorched the clouds into shapes. Ashfall was old and shrunken and he was still the size of a barn, still filled a cave with the heat of his breathing.

This was a flame the size of her two cupped hands, hovering a foot above a plinth of black glass, and it was looking at her.

She knew it was looking because flames don't have attention, and this did. The little fire bent toward her — not flickering, *bending*, deliberate as a person turning their head — and when it spoke, the voice came from everywhere in the room at once, from the walls and the bars and the air itself, a young voice worn thin by a century of use.

"A thief."

Paloma stopped walking. Beside her, she felt Wren go very still, and behind her, Ilsa's sword-hand tighten.

"That's what the book says," Ember said. "A thief comes. A thief steals back what was sold. I've had a hundred years to read my own contract, thief. It's very thorough. It accounts for thieves. It accounts for rescuers, for ransomers, for armies, for grief, for love, for cleverness, for force. It does not account for—" the flame guttered, a tiny hitch, almost human, "—for someone asking me first."

Paloma's mouth was dry. "So I'm asking."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against her skin like deep water.

"No," Ember said, "you're not. You're announcing. There's a difference. Announcing is what people do when they've already decided the ending and just want the victim to play their part in it." The flame rose slightly off the plinth, and Paloma saw — with a lurch, with a horror that settled into her bones and would never fully leave — that beneath the fire there was a shape. A body. Small, coiled, translucent as smoke, the ghost-outline of wings folded tight against a ghost-outline of back. A dragon the size of a cat, made mostly of absence, wearing its own soul like a coat three sizes too large because everything else had been taken. "You walked in here with your gold-marked arms and your scribe and your soldier and your god-touched child, and you have a speech prepared. I can smell it. Everyone who comes down here has a speech. The magistrates had speeches. My grandfather's envoy had a speech. Say yours, thief, and then be quiet, and let me tell you what a hundred years of listening to speeches sounds like."

Paloma opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Because Ilsa had been right, an hour ago, in the dark. The promise was right there, fully formed, sitting on her tongue like a coin: *I swear I'll free you. I swear it on my god, on my company's ashes, on my name.* And she understood, suddenly and completely, that the moment she said it, it would stop being hers. It would become a term. It would hang in this room with all the other terms, and Ember would own it, or the Court would own it, or worse — it would simply become another bar in the cage, one more thing binding the dragon to this place, one more obligation wrapped around a creature who had been drowning in other people's obligations for a hundred years.

She swallowed the promise. It went down like a stone.

"I don't have a speech," she said.

The flame flickered. Suspicion, maybe. Or laughter, the bitter kind.

"Everyone has a speech."

"Then I'm the first failure you've had in a century." Paloma took another step forward, slow, hands visible and empty at her sides. Up close, the cage-bars hummed against her teeth; she could taste the contract in the air, ink and iron and old, old arithmetic. "I have a book that says my name and tonight's date. I have a valley that raised me on lies it paid for with you. I have a grandmother's signature I haven't seen yet but I already know is ugly. And I have—" her voice caught, and she let it catch, because hiding it felt like one more term, "—a god who hasn't spoken to me in ten years, and I kept the oath anyway, because it was the only thing I had left that was mine."

"And now you've found out it wasn't yours either." Soft. Terrible. Accurate. "The vault. You found the vault."

"How—"

"I'm a soul in a contractual cage, thief. Everything the Court owns passes through the ledgers, and the ledgers pass through *me*. I felt your name get pulled this morning. I felt it the way you'd feel someone pick up a key that fits your lock." The flame dimmed, and for a moment it was so small, so tired, that Paloma's chest physically hurt. "Your oath was sworn with stolen goods. Your whole life was bought with me. So let's not pretend you came here for my sake. You came here because you found out your house was built on a stolen hearth, and you can't stand the cold."

Pip made a small sound behind her. Paloma didn't turn around. Turning around would have been a mercy, and she suspected she hadn't earned one.

"You're right," she said.

The flame stilled.

"I didn't come for you. Not first. I came because a book told me I'd die tonight if I stayed in the valley, and I'm a coward, and I chose the door instead of the grave. Then I came because Ashfall told me what my family did, and I wanted it to not be true, and it was true, and I couldn't unknow it. Then I came because the magistrate offered me everything I ever mourned, and I wanted it, and I said no, and I still don't know entirely why." She took a breath. The gold marks on her arms pulsed with her heartbeat, slow and patient as a tide. "Every single reason I walked down here is selfish. I'm not going to stand in front of you and launder them into virtue. That's what the magistrates do. They dress theft up as rescue. I've read enough of their paperwork on the way down to know the style."

Silence. The long kind. The kind that weighs.

Then Ember said, "Wren Halloway."

Wren started. "Yes."

"You forged our passage papers. In Vharn. Three sets, and one of them used a dead clerk's seal, and you slept four hours in nine days, and you gave the child the last of the water twice." The flame turned — there was no other word for it — toward the scribe. "Why?"

Wren's chin came up, the reflex of a woman who'd spent her life being accused in beautiful rooms. "Because the archive lied to me for twenty years and I helped it do it, and I am done being neat handwriting on other people's crimes."

"And you, Captain Vane. You sold her. You sold all of them. Why are you here?"

Ilsa's jaw worked. Paloma watched her decide, watched the old armor fail to close. "Because coerced is a word and guilty is a weight, and I've been carrying the wrong one for six years."

"And the child hears gods. Child — why are you here?"

Pip shrugged, enormous and small at once. "'Cause nobody else was gonna come get you."

The flame burned very quietly for a moment.

"Huh," Ember said.

* * *

They talked until the lamps burned low. Not a negotiation — Paloma refused to let it become one, and Ember, to her surprise, seemed grateful for the refusal. They talked the way people talk in the small hours, sideways, in pieces. Ember told them about the first decade, when the rage was hot enough to keep her warm. About the magistrates' clerks who came to read her the annual renewal of her own imprisonment, and how she'd learned to recite it faster than they did, out of spite, until they stopped coming. About Ashfall — *my grandfather flew over the mountain once, eleven years ago, high enough that the wards couldn't touch him, and he screamed for a day and a night, and I screamed back, and neither of us could do anything but scream, and that is what my family's love looks like now.*

Paloma told her about the valley. All of it, unflattering parts included — the escort jobs, the iron rule, the elders who'd wanted to burn the prophecy book, the way she'd read her own name aloud partly out of defiance and partly, if she was honest, out of a hunger for anything that would finally make the silence make sense.

"You kept an oath to a silent god for ten years," Ember said at one point. "In a valley that forgot the world existed. Why?"

"Because breaking it would have meant the silence won."

"And now?"

Paloma looked at the small flame, the caged heir, the candle that had once been a sun. "Now I think maybe the silence was never a victory. Maybe it was a wound."

Something shifted in the room. Not the flame — the *cage*. One of the hanging clauses trembled, a cobweb shivering in wind that wasn't there, and Wren's head snapped up, scribe-fast, tracking it.

"It heard that," Wren whispered. "Contracts down here aren't inert, Paloma. They listen. They especially listen near the collateral." Her eyes moved across the trembling clause, reading, and Paloma watched the color leave her face. "Oh. Oh, that's a *contingency trigger*. That's—"

"Say it plainly," Ilsa said.

Wren swallowed. "The cage isn't just holding Ember. It's holding the *terms of her release*. And one of those terms just activated. It says—" she read it twice, lips moving, the way she read anything that frightened her, "—it says the contract may be voided by the original debtor's line, upon presentation of a claim freely given, freely broken, or freely *transferred*."

The room went cold.

"Freely transferred," Paloma repeated.

"A soul-claim," Wren said quietly. "Someone would have to give theirs. Into the contract. To displace hers."

And Paloma felt it happen — felt the thought arrive in Ilsa before Ilsa spoke, saw it in the set of her shoulders, the same terrible readiness that had made her offer her soul in the vault-council above, and Paloma opened her mouth to kill the idea before it finished being born—

But it was Ember who spoke first.

"Don't," the dragon said, and for the first time in a hundred years, the voice cracked clean through, young and furious and afraid. "Don't any of you dare. I have watched this cage eat a century. I know exactly what it does with a soul, and I will burn the memory of every one of you out of my own mind before I watch it happen again. You want to free me? Then find the loophole that costs nothing, or find none, and sit down in this room with me until the mountain wears away. But you will not pay my debt with yourselves. That is how the Court wins. That is the *only* way the Court wins."

The flame blazed. For one heartbeat — one impossible, glorious heartbeat — it was the size of a bonfire, and the shadow it threw on the walls had wings, vast wings, and the whole Undercourt's paper-forest rustled in a wind that smelled of cinders.

Then it guttered back down to candle-size, and Ember said, exhausted, almost amused:

"Well, thief. Now you have a speech. I hope you're proud of yourself."

Paloma wasn't. She was terrified, and lit up, and more certain than she had ever been of anything: somewhere in that lattice of words was a flaw, and Wren Halloway was going to find it, and it was going to cost something unbearable, and they had exactly one night to decide which unbearable thing they could live with.

Above them, faint through a hundred fathoms of stone, a bell began to ring.

Wren went white. "That's the summons chime. The magistrate's chime." She looked up at Paloma. "He knows we're here. He knows we've seen the contingency. Paloma — he's giving us his answer before we give him ours."

The bell rang again. Patient. Certain. Like a pen being dipped in ink.
