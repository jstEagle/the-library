## 13. Loophole Hunters

The contract was written on skin.

Not parchment, not vellum — skin, cured and stretched and inked in a hand so fine it might have been etched by a needle dipped in starlight. Wren Halloway had spent eleven years in the archives of Vharn's surface court. She had handled deeds signed in blood, marriage contracts sealed with grief, a warrant of execution written on the back of a condemned man's confession. She had thought herself past flinching at anything the law could do to paper.

She flinched now.

The soul-contract of Ember, last heir of Ashfall, lay unrolled across three supply crates in the smuggler's tunnel where they'd made camp, and it was beautiful, and that was the worst part. The Court had made it beautiful on purpose. Beauty was a kind of anesthesia. No one wanted to look too closely at something lovely; looking closely felt like ingratitude.

"Clause one," Wren read aloud, because reading aloud was how she thought, and because silence in this tunnel had begun to feel like the inside of a throat. "The party of the first part — that's the Court Beneath, though they've styled themselves 'the Custodians of Perpetual Flame,' which is a lie with good posture — receives into perpetual custody the animus, herein called *the Ember*, of the party of the second part."

"And the second part?" Paloma asked from the mouth of the tunnel, where she stood watch with her back to them, which was her way of participating without having to look at anything.

"The second part is a hatchling." Wren's voice came out steadier than she expected. "A hundred years old, which for a dragon is barely weaned. There's a claw-print here instead of a signature." She touched it, gently, the way you'd touch a child's handprint pressed into clay. "They let her mark it. They probably told her it was a game."

Pip, curled against Ilsa's side with a heel of black bread she wasn't eating, said nothing. The child hadn't spoken since they'd come up out of the tithe-shaft, but her eyes tracked everything — the contract, the torchlight, the way Paloma's shoulders sat like a drawn bow. Pip heard things. Wren had stopped doubting that around the time the girl had recited an oath sworn alone in the dark by a woman she'd met two days prior.

"Clause four," Wren continued, forcing herself down the page. "Terms of release." Here the ink changed color — a deep arterial red-brown, older than the rest. Amendments. Someone had come back to this clause over the decades and nailed its doors shut, one by one. "The custody may be dissolved upon: first, the payment of a life freely given, of equal or greater weight to that held."

"Equal weight," Ilsa repeated flatly. She sat with her sword across her knees, not because she expected trouble but because her hands needed occupation. "Meaning another dragon. Or meaning—"

"Or meaning someone whose soul has accrued enough debt-weight to balance a century of stolen fire." Wren tapped the margin, where tiny annotations crawled like ants. "The Court weighs souls here the way goldsmiths weigh coin. A mercenary who's kept every oath for twenty years? That's heavy metal. You'd balance."

Paloma didn't turn around. But Wren saw her spine change.

"Second condition," Wren went on, quieter. "The return of the name under which the contract was executed. Not a copy. The original. Spoken aloud, in this place, by its rightful owner."

"Whose name?" Pip asked. Her first words in hours, small and clear as a bell in an empty church.

Wren ran her finger down the execution block. Seals hung from it like dead moths — wax stamped with sigils she recognized from her archive years, the marks of magistrates long ash. And beneath them, in the space reserved for the guarantor of the transaction, the party who had put up collateral to make the sale binding:

*Sterling.*

"Your grandmother," she said to Paloma's back. "Maren Sterling. She guaranteed the contract with her own line. Clause nine: 'Should the custodial terms be challenged, the guarantor's descendants stand surety, their names held in pledge until the debt is discharged or extinguished.'"

Now Paloma turned. In the torchlight her face did something complicated — not surprise. Confirmation. The look of a woman finding the load-bearing wall of a house she'd always suspected was hollow.

"So when I read my name out of Wren's book," she said slowly, "and the valley elders burned their censers and muttered about omen — that wasn't superstition. My name was already in their ledgers. It's been in their ledgers since before I was born."

"It's why the prophecy found you," Wren said. "You weren't chosen. You were *collateral*. The book just knew where the debt lived."

The tunnel was quiet except for the torch eating its pitch. Somewhere far above, through forty feet of mountain, the Undercourt hummed its ledger-hum, the sound Wren had decided was the city dreaming of arithmetic.

"There'll be a third condition," Ilsa said. "There always is. Courts don't build two doors when three will confuse the debtor."

Wren read on. Found it. Felt it land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"The third condition," she said, and had to stop, and start again. "The custody may be dissolved if the guarantor's line produces an heir who breaks, freely and with full understanding, an oath sworn in perfect faith. Not a false oath — a true one. The Court cannot price a broken truth. It's the one thing in their whole architecture with no column in the ledger."

"That's—" Paloma began.

"That's you," Wren finished. "That's your oath to your silent god. Twenty years. Kept alone, kept faithful, kept when there was no reason left to keep it. It's the heaviest single object any of us owns." She looked down at the red-brown amendments, at the neat little nails driven in one by one across a century. "And they wrote this condition in knowing it. They wrote it in *because* of it. This isn't a loophole, Paloma. It's bait."

* * *

They argued for six hours.

Wren kept notes, because she was who she was — a scribe to the marrow, someone who believed that if you could write a thing down honestly enough, it would eventually tell you the truth. By torchlight her margins filled with the shape of the debate:

*Ilsa offers her soul. Twice. Then a third time, louder, when Paloma pretends not to have heard the first two.*

*Paloma refuses. Not with argument — with a wall. With the tone she uses on strangers who ask too many questions about the scar on her jaw.*

*Pip says, once, very quietly: "The god doesn't want anybody's soul. I'd know." Nobody asks her what she means. Everybody wants to.*

*Ember's cage-terms sit in Wren's memory like a splinter: "You come to feel better, not to free me."*

It was Wren who finally broke the circle, because Wren understood something the soldiers didn't, something she'd learned over eleven years of watching powerful people sign terrible things: **the document always wins unless you stop negotiating on its terms.**

"Stop," she said, and rapped her knuckles on the stretched skin until everyone looked at her. "We're doing exactly what the Court designed us to do. Look at us. We're pricing ourselves. We're standing in their shop comparing our own weights on their scale. That's the trap — not clause four, not clause nine. The trap is that this contract makes love look like currency."

Ilsa's laugh had no humor in it. "Welcome to Vharn, scribe. Everything's currency here. Your archive taught you that much, surely."

"My archive taught me something better." Wren stood, and her knees cracked, and she didn't care. "It taught me that every contract, no matter how beautiful, no matter how old, no matter how many nails they've hammered into it — every contract has a *drafting history*. And drafting histories leave scars." She turned back to the skin-page, to the arterial amendments, and made herself look at them properly for the first time. Not as doors slammed shut. As *edits*.

Edits meant hesitation. Edits meant someone, somewhere in a hundred years, had worried.

"Look here." She traced the second amendment, the one sealing the name-condition. "Different ink. Different hand. This was added maybe sixty years ago — after the original signing. And here—" the third amendment, the oath-breaker clause, "—this is newer still. Forty years? Thirty?" She looked up, and her voice dropped the way it did in the archive stacks, where loudness was sacrilege. "Someone has been coming back to this contract for a century, terrified of each possible escape, nailing them shut one at a time. Which means the Court isn't certain. The Court is *scared*. You don't amend a contract you believe is airtight."

"Meaning what?" Paloma asked.

"Meaning the conditions aren't loopholes. They're *confessions*. Every unbearable price listed here is a price the Court is afraid someone might actually pay — and afraid of what happens *after*." Wren's finger hovered over the third amendment. "A life freely given doesn't enrich them, Paloma. Read the accounting language. There's no credit column. A soul given freely *destroys* the ledger entry — it can't be resold, can't be fractionalized, can't be collateralized. It burns a hole in the page. Same with the name returned. Same, most of all, with the broken oath. These aren't exits they've priced. These are exits they've *priced wrong*, because they can't imagine anyone paying."

Silence. The torch crackled. Pip had gone very still against Ilsa's side, the stillness of a child listening to something no one else could hear.

"So we pay one," Ilsa said. "My soul balances. It's clean — I kept it out of their hands all these years precisely so it would be worth something someday. Let it be worth this." She was already reaching for her knife, the ritual gesture of a soldier making an offering formal, and Wren saw with awful clarity that Ilsa Vane had been carrying this moment folded in her breast pocket for years, waiting for a cause that would justify spending herself.

"No." Paloma crossed the tunnel in three strides and put her hand flat on the blade, bare-palmed, daring Ilsa to move. "No. You don't get to do this. You don't get to buy your way clean. I carried your betrayal for eight years, Ilsa — I built half my personality out of it — and if you die down here buying forgiveness, I never get to stop carrying it. It becomes a monument. Do you understand? Your sacrifice would be the cruelest thing you've ever done to me."

"That's not fair."

"Nothing about this is fair. That's the entire point of the building we're standing under." Paloma's voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, Wren could see her hating it. "History does not repeat. I won't let it. I watched a company swear itself away piece by piece because each piece seemed bearable, and I'm the only one left keeping the vow, and I will not stand here and watch the same arithmetic eat the last two people I—"

She stopped. The sentence had been heading somewhere she hadn't authorized.

Ilsa lowered the knife. Slowly. Like setting down something heavier than steel.

* * *

Later, when the others slept — Ilsa on watch, Paloma pretending to sleep with her hand near her sword, Pip actually asleep with her mouth open and one fist full of Wren's spare quill — Wren sat alone with the contract and did the thing she was best at in all the world.

She read it again. Slower.

And this time she stopped trying to find the flaw in the terms, and started reading the *margins*.

Because margins were where scribes lived. Margins were where the honest work happened — the doubts, the corrections, the little human scratches that official text pretended didn't exist. Eleven years in the archive had taught her that no power structure, however vast, could stop its own clerks from muttering in the edges of things.

The margins of the soul-contract were dense. A century of clerks had worked this document, and clerks gossip even in ink.

*Balanced against the estate of A. Sterling, deceased,* one note read. *Weight insufficient. See supplemental.* Another: *Hatchling's fire measured at 4 parts; valley protection drawn at 3; remainder retained by Court per Schedule K.* Bureaucratic cannibalism, bite by bite.

Then, near the bottom of the final leaf, so faint it might have been breath on glass:

*If anyone reads this: the weighing is false. Fire cannot be weighed in parts. We were made to write it so. Forgive us. — T.V., underduress, yr. 63*

Wren sat very still for a long time.

T.V. Some clerk, sixty-three years into the contract's life, some conscripted hand in the Court's machine, had used the only weapon available to the powerless — a whisper in a margin — and had told the truth: the numbers were cooked. The debt was manufactured. Ember's imprisonment wasn't the collection of a legitimate obligation; it was fraud wearing liturgy.

Which meant the contract wasn't merely breakable.

It was *voidable* — if the fraud could be proven before the right authority. And there was exactly one authority in all the world above the Court's own law, one jurisdiction even magistrates feared, one court older than theirs:

An oath, freely broken, sworn in perfect faith. The clause they'd nailed shut because they couldn't imagine anyone paying it.

Wren looked across the dying torchlight at Paloma Sterling — sleeping badly, guarding even her dreams, twenty years faithful to a silent god — and felt the full dreadful weight of what she'd found settle onto her like a wet cloak.

Every road out of this contract ran through the same person. And every one of those roads required Paloma to burn down the last thing she had left.

The torch guttered. In the dark, softly, Pip spoke without waking — or perhaps not without waking, or perhaps not entirely as Pip:

*"Tell her the god kept count. Tell her the silence was the sound of Him holding His breath."*

Wren's pen froze above the page.

She reached for fresh ink, hands not quite steady, and began to draft the hardest document of her life: the proof of a fraud a century deep, addressed to the only judge who outranked the Undercourt.

Outside the tunnel, far above, the mountain hummed its arithmetic — and for the first time, Wren thought she heard a wrong note in it. Like a ledger somewhere, adding and adding, about to arrive at a number it had never budgeted for.
