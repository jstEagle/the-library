## 14. The Magistrate's Offer

They came for her at the hour the Undercourt called morning, though no sun had ever reached this deep.

Paloma knew it was morning only because the lamps along the corridor changed — from the bruised violet of perpetual dusk to a pale gold that mimicked dawn so faithfully it made her homesick for a valley she'd left four days ago and would never see the same way again. The light fell through slats of black glass overhead, warm on her face, and she hated how much she wanted to believe in it.

Two attendants escorted her, silent as furniture, their livery stitched with thread that moved. She'd noticed it yesterday: the Court's servants wore their contracts on their sleeves, literally, lines of fine script crawling along the cuffs like slow insects. When she'd asked one what the words said, he had looked at her with eyes gone smooth as river stones and answered, *Everything I am is written down. Isn't that a comfort?*

It was not a comfort. It was the opposite of comfort. It was a cage described in beautiful calligraphy.

The magistrate received her in a room that should not have existed this far beneath a mountain. It was a garden — or the memory of one, or the receipt of one. Grass underfoot that smelled of rain it had never touched. A tree heavy with white blossoms, each petal perfect, each petal, when Paloma looked too long, faintly translucent, like paper held up to flame. Somewhere water moved over stone.

Chief Magistrate Corvane sat beneath the tree at a table of dark wood, pouring tea.

He was not what she had expected. She had expected something carved, something wrong — a face assembled from other people's debts, beauty built the way Wren said everything here was built, out of what others owed. Instead he was an old man, silver-bearded, with kind creases around his eyes and ink stains on his fingers like any scribe's. He could have been a grandfather shelling peas on a porch. He could have been anyone's lost father.

That was the first trap, and Paloma walked into it knowing it was a trap, because the second trap was that knowing didn't help.

"Mercenary Sterling," he said, and his voice was warm gravel, a hearth-voice, a voice for telling children stories. "Sit. The tea is real. That's more than most things down here can claim."

She did not sit. "Where are my companions?"

"Safe. Fed. Asleep, if they've any sense." He gestured to the chair across from him with the teapot. "Wren Halloway is in our archives even now, reading things she'll dream about for years. The child Pip is eating honey cake with a kitchen staff who adore her. Captain Vane is doing push-ups in her cell, which she insists is not a cell, because the door is open." A small smile. "You may verify all of this yourself afterward. I have nothing to gain by lying to you, Paloma Sterling. Lies are for people who lack collateral."

"And you have mine."

"I have everyone's," Corvane said mildly. "Yours simply happens to be interesting."

She sat. Not because she trusted him — because her legs chose that moment to remind her she'd been walking through strata of screaming amber rock for two days, and because refusing tea felt like a move in a game she didn't yet understand the rules of. Her grandmother had played games with this court. Paloma intended to learn the board before she tipped it over.

The tea was smoky and bitter and perfect. It tasted like the campfires her company used to build in the passes above the valley, before the passes, before the betrayal, before there were no more company campfires anywhere in the world.

"You know what I'm here for," she said.

"Ember." The name left his mouth gently, the way one says the name of a beloved horse. "Ashfall's last heir. The flame that keeps magic breathing beyond your dead little valley. Yes. I know why you came. I read the same prophecy you did — we keep excellent records." He refilled her cup unasked. "I know about the guttering god, too. The one who stopped answering you ten years ago. The one you still pray to every night, alone, in a voice you'd be ashamed for anyone to hear."

Her cup stopped halfway to her lips.

"How—"

"I told you. Records." He folded his hands. His ink-stained fingers were steady. "Every oath sworn in good faith echoes somewhere, mercenary. Most of them echo here. Yours has been echoing for ten years, getting quieter, and do you know what I hear in it? I hear a woman praying to a door that never opens. And I thought — that poor creature. Someone ought to offer her a key."

* * *

He laid it out the way a jeweler lays out stones. One at a time. Letting each catch the light.

"First," he said, "the dragon."

The garden changed. The grass rippled, and where it rippled images rose like heat-shimmer: Ember, the candle-flame in her cage of terms, but *alight*, suddenly alight, wings unfurling in a blaze that turned the contractual cage to drifting ash. Free. Flying. The image was so vivid Paloma could feel the heat of it on her cheeks, smell the ozone-sweetness of dragonfire, and she realized her hands had gripped the table's edge hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

"The contract binding her is old, but old is not the same as unbreakable," Corvane said. "Contracts break all the time. They break when someone pays. They break when someone trades. They break when someone signs a new one worth more than the old." He watched her across the steam. "I can break it tomorrow. One signature. Ember walks — flies — out of my court by sundown, and the world gets its magic back, and the last dragon dies content instead of desperate. All of that, yours to give her. Yours."

Paloma made herself breathe. "And the price?"

"Patience. I'm not finished laying the table."

"Second," he continued, and the garden shimmered again, "your company."

This time the image hurt worse. It was not grand. That was the cruelty of it — it was *small*. It was the six of them around a fire in the foothills, the night before the pass, before Ilsa's knife found her back in every way a knife can find someone. Dree arguing about card rules nobody had agreed to. Maren asleep sitting up with her spoon still in her hand. Old Tam teaching Pip-before-Pip — no, not Pip, some other orphan, some other child they'd fed for a season — to whittle. And Brenna laughing, head thrown back, at nothing, at everything, the way she always laughed, the laugh Paloma had spent ten years failing to forget.

"They died owing the world nothing," Corvane said softly. "But souls don't scatter the way priests tell you. They settle. Like sediment. And sediment can be stirred." He let the image burn a moment longer — let Brenna laugh one more time — then smoothed it away like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. "Six souls. Recoverable. Restorable. I could put them at a table in a tavern in the spring, alive, confused, complaining about the ale. You could buy them breakfast. You could tell them what happened after. You could—" his voice gentled further, crueler for its gentleness, "—apologize to them. For surviving. I know you want to. It's in the echo."

Paloma discovered she was crying only when a tear hit the back of her hand, hot as a coal. She wiped it away with the heel of her palm, furious, and Corvane had the grace — the practiced, weaponized grace — to look away and refill the teapot as if nothing had happened.

"Third," he said.

And this one he did not show her. This one he spoke plainly, watching her face the whole time, because he knew — he *knew* — that the third gift needed no imagery at all.

"Your god," he said. "The Silent One. The Oath-Keeper. Whatever your company called it in the dark." He leaned forward. "It isn't silent because it abandoned you, Paloma. It's silent because it's *bound*. Has been for a century. Bound by the oldest debt in my ledgers — the debt your valley's founders took on when they bought their safety with a dragon's soul. Every prayer you've sent into that silence has been landing against the inside of a locked door. But doors open." He spread his ink-stained hands. "Break the founding contract — which I can do — and the god comes back. Not just to you. To everyone. Every honest oath in the world starts echoing again. And yours — yours it will answer *first*, because you're the last one still keeping faith with it. Ten years of prayers in the dark, mercenary. Imagine hearing a voice come back through all of them."

The garden was very quiet. Water moved over stone somewhere. A blossom detached from the tree and drifted down between them, and Paloma saw that up close its translucence was worse than she'd thought: pressed inside the petal, flat as a flower in a book, was a tiny human silhouette. A soul, dried and pressed and used as decoration.

She set down her cup very carefully, because if she didn't she was going to throw it at him.

"And me," she said. "What do you want from me. Nobody breaks three seals out of charity. Not here. Charity doesn't survive the descent past the third stratum — I heard them screaming in the rock."

Corvane smiled, and for just a moment the kindly grandfather flickered, and underneath him she saw it: something vast and patient and *hungry*, something that had been counting for a thousand years, something whose kindness was simply the shape its hunger wore when it wanted to be held.

"There it is," he said, almost fondly. "I wondered when you'd ask the real question. Yes. There is a price. There is always a price; the difference between me and the world above is that I write mine down honestly." He drew a sheet of paper from his coat — fine paper, cream-colored, and the pen that followed it seemed to rise out of his sleeve like a serpent out of grass. "I want an enforcer."

"A what?"

"My court runs on obligation. Debts, defaults, forfeitures. Above ground, the world is drowning in bad bargains — kings who sold their rivers, guilds who sold their dead, farmers who sold next year's harvest and the year after that. Someone must collect. Someone must *hold the line of the law* when the law is all that stands between people and chaos." He began to write as he spoke, the pen whispering. "I want you to be that someone. My Hand. You would walk the surface world with the authority of this court behind you. You would collect what is owed — and here is the part your righteous friends will hate — you would decide, case by case, what is truly owed. Mercy or seizure. Forgiveness or forfeiture. Your judgment, Paloma. Not mine. I'm tired. I'm so very old, and I am *tired*, and I need someone with your particular talent for keeping oaths."

He slid the page toward her. She made herself look at it.

The contract was short. That frightened her more than a long one would have. Three clauses. Ember freed. The company restored. The god unbound. And in exchange, one line, written in ink that shifted like the servants' cuffs:

*Paloma Sterling, called the Last Faithful, binds herself to the Undercourt as Enforcer of Debts, for a term of one hundred years, renewable.*

"One hundred years," she said.

"You'd live them. The position confers longevity — you'd need it, to do the work properly. You'd see the world your valley walled you out of. Every city, every coast, every mountain road. You'd have power no mercenary captain has ever dreamed of. And you'd be *good* at it." He tapped the page once. "Don't mistake this for corruption, child. Look at what I'm offering. I'm offering you the chance to spend a century deciding who deserves mercy. Do you know how many magistrates in history have offered that? Usually we take the choice away. I'm handing it to you with both hands."

And the worst thing — the thing Paloma would carry out of that garden and into the long night that followed — was that he wasn't lying. She could feel it, the way she'd learned to feel lies on the road: the flatness in the air, the wrong note under the music. There was no wrong note. He meant it. He would free Ember. He would raise her dead. He would unlock her god's door. And she would spend a hundred years collecting the souls of farmers and kings, and she would be *kind* about it, and she would tell herself every single day that kindness made it different, until the day she looked in a mirror and couldn't remember what her face had looked like before the ink got into it.

Power dressed as rescue. Wren would have a phrase for it. Wren probably had a *citation* for it.

"Why me?" Paloma asked quietly. "Truly. You have enforcers. I met them on the stairs. They wear your livery and their sleeves crawl."

"Because they were taken," Corvane said. "Taken young, broken to the collar, hollowed out and filled with procedure. They collect perfectly and judge never. But you—" he studied her with those kind, terrible eyes, "—you kept an oath for ten years to a god who never answered. Out of nothing but stubborn love. Do you understand how rare that is? How *valuable*? I don't want another hollow instrument. I want the Last Faithful. I want the woman who prays to a locked door. Because if *she* takes the collar, then every idealist in the world looks at her and thinks: if it's good enough for her, it's good enough for me." He smiled again, and this time he let her see all the way down. "Your faith is the finest advertisement my court could ever buy."

The garden hummed. The pressed-soul blossom lay on the table between them like a small pale corpse.

Paloma stood. Her legs held, barely.

"No," she said.

Corvane raised his eyebrows, genuinely amused. "No? Not *no, never*? Not *I'd rather die*? Just... no?"

"Not tonight. Tonight you've shown me a dragon burning free and my dead laughing at a fire and a god saying my name, and I'm not stupid enough to answer anything while those images are still warm." She looked down at the contract, at the shifting ink, at the hundred years coiled inside it like a snake in tall grass. "One night. I'll give you one night. That's not a negotiation. That's the only honest thing I have left to trade."

For a long moment the chief magistrate said nothing. Then he laughed — a real laugh, low and delighted, and it was somehow worse than the hunger had been.

"Oh, *good*," he said. "Oh, that's marvelous. Do you know how many people say yes on the spot? Hundreds. Thousands. They see the dragon and the dead and the god and they sign before the tears dry. But you want to *sit with it*. You want to let it curdle." He rose, and bowed — actually bowed, deep and courteous. "One night, then. Take the whole of it. Walk the corridors. Talk to your scribe, your child, your traitor-captain. Sleep on silk, if silk helps. And in the morning, Paloma Sterling, come tell me what the silence says."

He snapped his fingers, and the garden folded itself away like a letter being closed, and the corridor lamps returned to their bruised violet dusk, and the two silent attendants were waiting to walk her back.

At the door she stopped. She didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "Brenna wouldn't have come back for you. None of them would. They hated exactly what you are."

Behind her, warm as a hearth, the old man's voice: "Then it's fortunate I'm not asking them, isn't it?"

* * *

They gave her a room, and it was a trap too, of course. Everything here was a trap; that was the local geology, traps all the way down. The bed was soft as forgiveness. The window — a window, this far underground, which meant it showed somewhere else, or nowhere, or whatever the Court wanted her to ache for — looked out on a hillside at golden evening, and on the hillside stood a farmhouse with smoke rising from its chimney, and standing in the farmyard, waving, were six people she had buried in her heart one at a time over ten years.

She pulled the curtain shut and did not look again.

Instead she sat on the floor with her back to the door, the way she'd slept on watch for fifteen years, and she took out the only thing she owned that mattered: a scrap of oathsilk, worn soft, bearing the words her whole company had spoken together the night they bound themselves to the Silent One. *We hold. We keep. We do not sell what is not ours to sell.* Her thumb found the frayed edge and worried it, the way it had ten thousand nights.

*We do not sell what is not ours to sell.*

And hadn't her grandmother sold? Hadn't the valley? Wasn't Paloma herself, by the vault's cold arithmetic, already sold — her true name filed away as collateral before she ever drew breath?

Somewhere down the corridor, faint through stone, she heard singing. Small voice. Off-key. Pip, who heard gods, singing some nonsense song to a kitchen full of contract-slaves, and the slaves humming along, and for a moment — just a moment — the whole vast weight of the Undercourt seemed to lean toward that sound like grass toward sun.

Paloma closed her eyes.

She had one night. In the morning she would say yes or no, and either answer would cost a world.

She only wished she believed she'd be the one choosing.
