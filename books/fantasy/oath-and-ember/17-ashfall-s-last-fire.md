## 17. Ashfall's Last Fire

Ilsa Vane had counted her sins the way other soldiers counted coin — often, and never finding enough to make change. But she had never once, in all her years of ledgers and betrayals and small necessary cruelties, imagined she would end up standing in a dragon's deathbed, holding a candle.

Not a real candle. A shard of ember-glass, no bigger than her thumb, that Ashfall had pressed into her hands an hour ago with instructions written in a language older than any court: *carry it to the vault door. Do not let it touch stone. Do not let it touch water. Do not let it touch your fear.*

The last instruction was the only one Ilsa had ever been given that she could not follow.

* * *

The mountain's under-halls breathed around them like something sleeping badly. The air was warm in the wrong places — warm where there should have been cold, cold where their torches said warmth should pool — and every surface glistened faintly with condensation that was not water. Wren had called it *condensed obligation*, which was either scribe humor or genuine horror; with Wren it was impossible to tell.

Ahead of them, Ashfall moved through the dark like a shipwreck given legs.

Ilsa had seen him whole for exactly one moment, back at the first meeting — one vast turn of his head, one eye like a banked furnace, old enough that his gaze felt less like being looked at and more like being *filed*. Even then, dying, he had been enormous. Now he was smaller. She could see it happening hour by hour, the way you can watch ice thin on a spring river if you stare long enough: the great ribs rising and falling shallower, the scales along his throat gone from bronze to ash-grey, the firelight behind his eyes guttering down to a coal's stubborn red.

He was spending himself. That was the truth none of them said aloud. Every ward he burned through, every sealed gate he melted open, every thread of the Court's defensive lattice he unpicked with claws older than the Court itself — each one cost him flame he did not have. He was not leading them to the vault so much as *paying* for their passage, toll by toll, out of the last account he possessed.

"Left," he rumbled, and his voice came out like a landslide deciding to be gentle. "The corridor ahead is warded with debt-script. It will read your names and demand payment. Do not answer it."

"What happens if we answer?" Pip asked. The child rode on Paloma's shoulders, as she had for most of the descent, small hands laced under Paloma's chin. Pip's voice had a strange doubled quality down here — the god spoke through her more often now, in fragments, like a man remembering a song.

"You pay," said Ashfall. "The Court always accepts. That is its genius and its obscenity. It has never once refused a payment, because everything can be priced, and everything priced can be owned." His tail dragged behind him, scoring a line in the rock. "Do not give it anything to own tonight."

They turned left. The debt-script flared on the walls — lines of silver characters that swiveled like insects toward the warmth of living bodies — and then, as Ashfall exhaled a thread of smoke across them, they dimmed and died. The smoke smelled of libraries burning.

Ilsa walked at the rear, because that was where she'd put herself since the ambush, since Paloma had spared her life with a mercy Ilsa still hadn't earned and couldn't return except by walking point into every danger first. Rear guard was a coward's honor, but it let her watch all of them: Wren with her satchel of forged papers clutched like a rosary; Pip humming fragments of oaths she'd never heard sworn; and Paloma—

Paloma walked like a woman carrying something breakable inside her chest. The oath-marks had spread since the Undercourt proper began, climbing her forearms in branching lines of gold-black light, the silent god's covenant made visible at last, burning her skin in slow script. She didn't complain. She didn't speak much at all. And every time Ashfall stumbled, Ilsa saw her flinch as though the stumble were hers.

*You love her,* Ilsa thought, with the flat clarity of a woman who had stopped lying to herself somewhere around the tithe-shaft. *That was always the problem.*

* * *

The third gate nearly killed them.

It stood across a gallery of black stone, twice the height of a cathedral door, wrought not of metal but of compressed contract — ten thousand pages fused into a single slab, every page a soul-claim, the words visible in strata like fossil rings. The Court's oldest wards sang above it in a frequency Ilsa felt in her teeth, in the fillings of her teeth, in the marrow behind them.

Ashfall stopped before it and did not move for a long time.

"Ashfall," Paloma said quietly. "How many more?"

"One," he said. "This one. Then the vault antechamber, and the vault itself, and the magistrate's last tricks, which are yours to face, not mine." He lowered his head until his eye was level with them, and Ilsa saw that the furnace-light in it had shrunk to a single coal. "I have been rationing myself since the valley, mercenary. Did you think a dragon of my age simply *walks* through wards? Every gate I opened, I paid for. This one costs the rest."

"No," Paloma said. It came out fast and ugly. "No. There's another way. Wren—"

"There is no other way," Wren said, and her voice cracked on it, the archivist in her confirming what the friend in her refused. "These are founding-era wards. They predate every forgery technique I know. They can't be picked, bribed, or argued with. Only unmade. And only dragon-fire unmakes founding script."

"I can carry the ember-glass through myself," Ilsa said. "Blast the door from—"

"The door would drink you," Ashfall said mildly. "Contract-stone eats souls, Captain. You'd be a very articulate stain on the wall." He turned back to the gate. "Stand well back. All of you. And Paloma Sterling—" the great head swung to her, and the coal of his eye brightened, briefly, deliberately, "—come here."

She went. Of course she went. Ilsa watched her cross the black stone with the oath-marks crawling up her neck now, gold-black branches reaching for her jaw, and thought: *she looks like a tree in winter, drawn in ink by someone who loved trees.*

Ashfall touched his muzzle to her forehead. Gently. The way you'd touch something you were afraid of breaking, or afraid of keeping.

"When my fire goes out," he said, in a voice pitched for her alone but audible to all of them anyway, because dragons do not know how to whisper, "something remains. Not a soul — souls are the Court's currency, and mine is spent. A spark. An inheritance. Dragon-fire does not die; it *changes hands*. It has been waiting a century for hands worth the trouble." His breath washed over her, hot and smelling of cinders and, absurdly, of rain. "I am told yours are oath-scarred. Good. Fire trusts scars."

"I don't want it," Paloma whispered.

"No," Ashfall agreed. "The best inheritances are never wanted. Wanting makes them purchases. This is a gift, and gifts must land on unwilling ground or they take root wrong." He drew back. "Now stand away. All of you. This will be loud."

* * *

Ilsa had stood before charging cavalry. She had stood before a god-touched storm on the Salt Marches. She had once held a bridge against forty men for six hours with a broken arm and a borrowed sword.

None of it prepared her for watching a dragon spend his last fire.

Ashfall reared, and the gallery filled with light. Not flame — not at first — but *heat made visible*, a pressure like the world inhaling, and then the fire came, white at its heart, gold at its edges, threaded through with colors Ilsa had no names for: the deep violet of very old magic, the green of growing things, the blue of oaths kept past all reason. It struck the contract-gate and the gate *screamed*. Ten thousand pages tried to burn individually, each soul-claim shrieking its own terms, and the fire answered each one, unwriting clause after clause in a conflagration that was somehow also a reading aloud — every stolen name named, every forfeited soul spoken back into the dark.

Pip was crying without sound, tears running down her grinning face. Wren had both hands pressed over her mouth. And Paloma stood rooted, arms half-raised against the heat that should have killed her and didn't, the oath-marks on her skin blazing in sympathy, gold answering gold, and Ilsa understood with terrible certainty that the fire recognized her. Had perhaps always been traveling toward her. A hundred years of guttering flame, looking for the hands worth the trouble.

The gate came down in a rain of ash and freed paper. Pages fluttered everywhere, blank now, their claims voided, drifting like snow that had finally been forgiven.

And Ashfall folded.

There was no other word for it. The vast shape collapsed inward, wings settling like sails, head lowering to the stone with a tenderness that broke Ilsa's heart precisely because it was so careful — even dying, even empty, he did not want to crack the floor. The fire was gone from him. What remained was grey and still and breathing in intervals so long Ilsa caught herself counting between them, the way you count for a fevered child.

They gathered around him. There was nothing else to do. There is never anything else to do.

"Wren," Ashfall said. His voice was almost nothing now, a draft through a ruined house. "The archive you dreamed of building. Build it. Build it *outside* the courts. Let people read their own contracts." Wren made a sound and knelt and pressed her forehead to the great grey brow. "Pip. Child who hears. Tell them — tell *him* — that I kept faith longer than faith deserved. He will understand. He keeps everything." Pip laid both small hands on his snout and nodded solemnly, and for a moment her eyes went silver-white, and whatever passed between the dragon and the god, Ilsa was not given to see.

Then Ashfall looked at Ilsa.

Of everyone, he looked at *her*. The betrayer. The sold soul. The woman who had spent twenty years paying interest on one bad night.

"Captain Vane," he said. "You carried the glass without letting it touch your fear. Mostly." One eyelid drooped, and Ilsa would have sworn on any god living or silent that it was a wink. "Fear carried carefully is just courage with better posture. Go and hold the door."

And then he turned to Paloma, and the last thing left in him — the spark, the coal, the inheritance — rose out of his chest like a star leaving orbit.

It crossed the space between them slowly. Ilsa saw Paloma try to step back. Saw her jaw set, and her fists close, and every instinct of a woman who had survived by taking nothing from anyone war against the thing descending on her. Saw her lose.

The spark settled into the center of her chest, into the place where the oath-marks converged, and *lit*.

Paloma made a sound Ilsa had never heard from her — not pain, not quite, though it contained pain the way a bell contains the hammer. Light ran through the gold-black branches of her covenant-scars, filling them, and for three heartbeats Paloma Sterling stood outlined in fire, a silhouette of script and flame, a woman rewritten as a lantern.

When the light faded she was on her knees, smoking gently, alive, changed. She looked at her own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. When she spoke, her voice carried a harmonic it hadn't before, a second tone underneath, ancient and patient.

"He's not gone," she said. "He's — *distributed*. Oh. Oh, that's what fire *is*—" She stopped herself. Swallowed. Looked up at the rest of them with wet eyes and a spine like a drawn blade. "We keep moving. He bought us this far. We don't waste it."

* * *

They left him there in the gallery of blank pages, because there was no way to carry a dragon and no time to dig, and because Wren said — quietly, reasonably, in the voice of a woman holding herself together with cataloguing — that the vault's collapse might yet be averted, and if it was, they could come back and do properly by him. A pyre. A monument. A valley, maybe. Dragons should not end in basements.

Ilsa took the rear again, and at the threshold of the antechamber she stopped and looked back once.

The great grey shape lay still among the drifting blank pages, and the pages had begun, very softly, to settle over him — covering him the way snow covers a fallen tree, gently, without malice. As she watched, one page touched his nostril and did not burn. Nothing about him burned anymore. He was finished spending.

*You kept faith longer than faith deserved,* Pip had told the silent god, on his behalf. Ilsa, who had kept faith with nothing but her own survival for twenty years, found that her eyes were burning too, and let them, because the dead deserve witnesses and she was the only one looking.

Then she turned, drew her sword, and followed the others into the antechamber — where the walls were lined with doors, and every door bore a nameplate, and every nameplate bore a name she knew.

The first was STERLING.

The second was VANE.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a lock turning — and beneath it, low and delighted, the voice of the chief magistrate, speaking to someone unseen:

"She's inherited the fire. Wonderful. Amend the offer — double everything. Fire commands such a *premium*."
