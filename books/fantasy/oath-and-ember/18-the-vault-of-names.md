## 18. The Vault of Names

The door to the vault was not a door.

It was a mouth.

Paloma stood before it in the dark of Ashfall's breach — a wound torn through the Undercourt's wards, still smoking with the last fire a dying dragon had spent for them — and understood that she was looking at something that ate. The stone around it had been carved into lips, or had grown them; the distinction no longer mattered down here, where everything beautiful was also a ledger and every surface remembered what it was owed. Beyond the lips lay a throat of black glass, and beyond that, if Wren's stolen schematics were true, the Vault of Names: every soul the Court had ever collateralized, filed and shelved like seed grain against a famine that would never come.

"Say your name to it," Wren whispered. Her scribe's hands were shaking so badly the schematic crackled. "That's the toll. It only opens for what it already owns."

"That's not ominous at all," Ilsa said. Her voice was flat, her sword drawn out of habit rather than use. Down here steel meant nothing. She knew it. She drew it anyway, because her hands needed something honest to hold.

Pip pressed close against Paloma's side. The child had been quiet since Ashfall fell — quiet in the way a held breath is quiet — and when Paloma glanced down she saw that Pip's eyes were fixed on the vault-mouth with an expression far too old for her face.

"It's sad," Pip said. "The door. It's full of names nobody says anymore."

* * *

They went in single file, because the throat allowed nothing wider, and the black glass swallowed their footfalls so completely that Paloma felt deaf as well as blind. There was no light source. There didn't need to be. The walls themselves gave off a faint grey luminescence, and it took her three steps to understand why, and then she wished she hadn't.

The glow came from names.

They were written into the glass in script finer than spider silk, tens of thousands of them, hundreds of thousands, each one faintly luminous, each one pulsing with a rhythm almost but not quite like breathing. Some burned bright. Some had dimmed to embers. A few — and these made her stomach turn over — had gone dark entirely, leaving only the ghost of an indentation where a name used to be, the way a scar is the ghost of a wound.

"The bright ones are current," Wren murmured, running her fingertips an inch above the wall without touching. Scribes could read by proximity; Paloma had watched her do it in Vharn's archive, coaxing meaning out of sealed documents like heat out of coals. "The dim ones are... matured. Fully collected." She stopped. Swallowed. "The dark ones are spent."

"Spent how?" Ilsa asked, though her tone said she already knew.

"Spent the way Ember was nearly spent," Wren said. "Fuel."

Paloma walked on. She did not let herself stop at any particular name. If she stopped, she would start reading, and if she started reading she would find children here, and grandmothers, and people who had once sworn oaths in good faith under open sky, and she could not afford that yet. Not yet. First the thing she had come for. Grief could have its turn after.

The passage descended in a slow spiral, and the air changed as they went — grew warmer, then cooler, then warm again, cycling through seasons in miniature, and Paloma realized with a lurch that the vault was *breathing*. The whole mountain breathed through this place. Every soul shelved here was a lungful.

"How big?" she asked quietly.

Wren consulted the schematic even though she couldn't see it in the dark. "The founding ledgers list eleven thousand original entries. But compounding interest—" she laughed, a small broken sound, "—compounding interest on souls, Paloma. Debts inherited by children. Names pledged against debts their great-grandparents never finished paying. The last audit I saw listed forty thousand."

Forty thousand names. Forty thousand lungs.

And somewhere among them, filed under Sterling, or whatever older name the Sterlings had carried before the valley renamed them, her own.

* * *

The central chamber opened without warning — one moment the spiral throat, the next a vast round hall like the inside of a bell. And it rang, too, in its way: a hum so low Paloma felt it in her back teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the marrow the Court technically owned.

Shelves rose from floor to unseen ceiling in concentric rings, and on the shelves sat books. Not ledgers — Paloma had expected ledgers, had braced herself for columns of figures. These were books the size of prayer volumes, bound in leather of every shade, some cracked with age, some new enough that the binding still smelled of tallow. Each bore a single title on its spine.

A name.

"True names," Wren breathed. She had gone white as paper, which for Wren, who lived among documents the way fish live in water, was saying something profound. "Not the names people answer to. The ones underneath. The ones you're called by when you're dreamed about." She turned slowly in the center of the room, and the light of ten thousand names moved across her face. "This isn't a vault. This is a *choir*. All these names, humming together — that's the sound. That's what we've been hearing since the tithe-shaft. The mountain sings with what it holds."

Ilsa stood very still near the entrance, sword finally lowered, her eyes moving across the shelves with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "My company," she said. "When the Court took my soul as leverage, they'd have filed it here." Her voice came out rough. "Somewhere on those shelves is a book with my name on it, and I've been walking past it for six years."

"We can find yours later," Paloma said.

"No." Ilsa shook her head once, hard. "Later there might not be a later. Keep moving. Sterling. Find Sterling."

But Paloma had already found it.

She hadn't been looking. That was the terrible part. She had walked into the third ring of shelves with no plan at all, letting her feet choose, and her feet had stopped at a shelf at chest height where a gap waited between two fat old volumes — and in the gap, leaning slightly, as if it had been waiting longer than she had, stood a slim book bound in grey leather the exact color of the valley's winter sky.

No title on the spine.

Just a small stamped sigil she knew better than her own heartbeat: the mark of the Silent Order, the oath-sigil her company had all worn branded into their left wrists, the sign of the god who had gone quiet ten years ago and never answered again.

Her hands were steady. She noted that distantly, the way you note weather. Her hands were steady and her heart was a drum and she reached out and took the book off the shelf, and the moment her fingers closed on the cover the hum of the whole chamber changed pitch — dropped, deepened, went almost subsonic, so that Pip clapped her hands over her ears and Wren staggered.

Every name in the vault was looking at her. She felt it the way you feel sun through a window.

"Paloma," Pip said, and her voice had that doubled quality it got sometimes, the quality Paloma had wept over in a smuggler's loft while a child repeated words she'd sworn alone in the dark. "Open it. It wants to be opened. It's been holding its breath for a hundred years."

* * *

She opened it.

The first page was not hers.

The handwriting was old-fashioned, formal, the ink browned with age: **Sterling, Maren. True name: Maren-who-kept-the-gate. Collateral accepted against debt of protection, valley charter, year one.** Below it, in different ink, newer: a string of transactions, renewals, transfers. Protection renewed. Protection renewed. Interest accrued. Principal unpaid. And at the bottom of the page, in ink so fresh it gleamed:

**Debt inherited in full by descendant: Sterling, Paloma.**

Her grandmother. Paloma had never known her grandmother's name — the valley forgot its dead on purpose, that was the iron rule, that was the mercy — and now here it was, kept perfectly, lovingly preserved by the only institution in the world that never forgot anything, because forgetting was bad for business. *Maren-who-kept-the-gate.* Who had walked out of the mountain a century ago carrying a contract instead of a child's soul, and had told no one, or told everyone, and either way the valley had folded the truth away like a letter never sent.

Paloma turned the page.

The second page was hers.

Not her name as she answered to it. Something underneath it, something that made her breath catch, because seeing it written was like hearing a song you'd been humming your whole life and never known the words to. It fit. It was hers the way a scar is hers. And beside it, the entry:

**Collateral status: active. Pledged value: one oath, freely sworn, to the god of the Silent Order.**

She read it twice. Three times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

"I don't understand," Wren said softly. She had come to stand at Paloma's shoulder, reading upside down the way scribes do, and her voice had gone thin. "Your oath. Your oath to the silent god — it's listed as *collateral*? As something the Court holds?"

"Not holds," Paloma heard herself say. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Owns."

And then it came together, all of it, the way a fall comes together — not all at once but in one long accelerating horror.

The Court owned her true name. Had owned it since before her birth, inherited down a chain of signatures like a farm or a feud. Which meant that everything she had ever done under that name, everything she had built with it, everything she had *sworn* with it — none of it had ever fully been hers to give.

Including the oath.

Ten years ago, bleeding and grieving in the ruins of her company, she had knelt in the ash and sworn herself to the silent god with her whole heart, her whole name, her whole self. The most honest act of her life. The one thing she had left. And the god —

The god had gone silent that same night.

She had thought it was abandonment. She had thought it was punishment, or death, or indifference, the cold arithmetic of gods who outlive the people who need them. Ten years she had carried that silence like a stone behind her ribs, and she had hated the god for it, and hated herself more for still keeping faith with something that wouldn't speak.

But the god hadn't abandoned her.

The god had received her oath and found it *tainted*. Found that the name swearing it belonged, by ancient lien, to the Court beneath the mountain. Found that his most faithful servant was offering him stolen goods — had been, all along, was *made of* stolen goods, a woman whose very self was mortgaged. What kind of god accepts a gift he knows was taken? What kind of god binds himself to a soul that another power already holds paper on?

"He wasn't silent because he left," Paloma said. The words came out of her like splinters. "He was silent because he was *ashamed*. Because answering me would have meant accepting a pledge the Court could call due. He stayed quiet to keep me free of him. Ten years. He took ten years of my hatred to keep the Court from using him as leverage against me." She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and cracked. "I cursed him for abandoning me. He was protecting me the only way a god of oaths can — by refusing to be party to a false contract."

Pip was crying silently, tears sliding down her face, and her mouth moved with words that weren't hers. Paloma caught fragments — *she knows, she knows, tell her* — and then, clear as a bell rung in an empty church, the child spoke in a voice that was not a child's:

*"You were never forsaken. You were never mine to keep safe. I am sorry, little oath-keeper. I am sorry. I am sorry."*

Then Pip swayed, and Ilsa caught her, and the voice was gone, and the chamber hummed on, and Paloma Sterling stood in the vault of everything the world had ever lost and held her own name in her hands like a bird with a broken wing.

* * *

For a long time nobody spoke. The vault breathed around them. Somewhere far above, alarms would be rising — Ashfall's breach could not stay open forever, and the magistrates would be coming with their guards and their pens and their beautiful instruments of obligation — but that was a problem belonging to later, and later could wait its turn.

It was Wren who finally broke the silence, and she broke it the way she broke most things: precisely, and with ink.

"This changes the terms," she said. Her finger hovered over the collateral line without touching it. "Do you understand what this means, legally? The magistrate offered you a bargain yesterday — your service for Ember's freedom. He could make that offer because he believed he held assets against you. Your name. Your oath. Your *compliance*, purchased through leverage." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce behind the exhaustion. "But an oath sworn with stolen goods isn't valid collateral. It never was. It's voidable. It has *always* been voidable — the Court just made sure no one ever learned enough law to know it."

"Voidable how?" Ilsa asked.

Wren's gaze slid to Paloma. And Paloma already knew the answer, had known it since the loophole-hunting nights around the smuggled fire, when every path out of the contract had required something unbearable. A life. A name. An oath freely broken.

"You break it," Paloma said quietly. "You break the oath. Freely. Deliberately. The one act the Court can't price, because it's freely given ruin — it destroys the asset they're holding. You can't collect interest on a burnt field."

"But your god—" Ilsa started.

"My god will understand." She looked down at the book in her hands, at the sigil stamped on its cover, at the true name glowing faintly on the page like a coal refusing to go out. "He's spent ten years being silent to protect me. Breaking my oath to him isn't betrayal. It's the first thing I'll ever have given him that was actually mine to give."

The hum of the vault shifted again. Paloma would swear, afterward, that in that moment the ten thousand names on the shelves leaned toward her — that the whole buried choir of the stolen and the sold bent its attention upon a mercenary from a dead valley holding a grey book — and that somewhere in that vast collective sound was a note of hope.

Because if her name could be broken free, so could theirs. Every one of them. Every lungful the mountain breathed.

Pip tugged her sleeve. "It's time," the child said simply. "He's waiting. He's been waiting so long, and he's so tired, and he wants to hear your voice again — even angry. *Especially* angry."

Paloma closed the book. Held it against her chest, over her heart, over the brand of the Silent Order on her wrist.

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the first alarm bells began to ring.

"They're coming," Ilsa said, drawing her sword at last for a reason that mattered. "Whatever you're going to do, oath-keeper — do it fast."

Paloma opened her mouth, and began to speak the words that would unmake her.
