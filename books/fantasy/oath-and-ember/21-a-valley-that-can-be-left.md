## 21. A Valley That Can Be Left

The valley had a smell.

Paloma had never known that until she left it. For twenty-nine years the air of her home had been simply air — flavorless, weightless, the way water is to a fish — and only now, walking back down the pass she'd been dragged out of a lifetime ago, did she understand what everyone else in the world had been breathing all along. The valley smelled like nothing. Like a room swept clean and then shut. Like a held breath.

But as she crested the last ridge at dawn, with Wren's mule complaining behind her and Pip asleep against Ilsa's back in a sling of borrowed wool, Paloma felt it before she saw it: the breath releasing.

Magic moved through the pass like weather. She could feel it on her skin, a faint static warmth, a hum in her teeth. Grass along the trailside leaned toward them as they passed, not wind-blown but *attending*. A hawk wheeled overhead trailing sparks of gold from its wingtips, and when it cried out, the sound rang like a struck bell and hung in the air three heartbeats longer than it should have.

"Gods," Wren breathed, reining the mule to a stop beside her. The scribe's ink-stained fingers were pressed to her own sternum, as if checking that her heart still sat where she'd left it. "It's *in* the stones. Do you feel it? It's in everything here."

"I feel it," Paloma said.

She had spent her whole life in a place where magic went to die, and she had been told — had believed, had built her entire self upon the belief — that this was simply what the world was. Flat. Silent. A god who didn't answer because there was nothing left in the world for him to speak through. Now the deadness was gone, unmade in the collapse of the Undercourt along with every debt it held, and the valley was filling up like a dry riverbed under spring melt. And Paloma stood at its edge feeling less like a woman coming home than like a stranger reading someone else's letters.

"It's going to be different," Pip said sleepily, not opening her eyes. The child's voice carried that odd doubled quality it sometimes had, as though two speakers shared one throat. "Different isn't bad. But it's going to be different."

"Thank you, oracle," Ilsa muttered. There was no bite in it anymore. There hadn't been since the Court came down, since Ilsa had stood in the doors of the Undercourt with her sword drawn and her soul finally, irrevocably her own, and held the line while the world unmade itself around her. Some betrayals, once confessed and once forgiven, burned away entirely. What remained underneath was older and quieter: two women who had sworn the same oath to the same silent god, and who had both, in their ways, kept it.

They descended into the valley together.

* * *

The village of Sterrenfall looked smaller than Paloma remembered, which was impossible, because she had measured it a thousand times in her childhood — the distance from the well to the chapel, the chapel to the elder's hall, the hall to the palisade gate. She knew its dimensions the way she knew her own hands. But memory had been measuring a cage, and cages always seem larger from inside.

The magic-dead valley had been a fortress. That was the truth Ashfall had given her on his mountain, in his dying voice like gravel sliding: *Your elders did not build a sanctuary, thief. They built a vault. And vaults are for keeping things from getting out.* The valley's deadness had hidden Ember's stolen soul from the world — no, more than hidden it. The deadness had been the lock itself, a hole in the weave of magic so deep and wide that no dragon-fire, no god, no prophecy could reach across it. Her whole life had been lived inside a keyhole.

Now the lock was broken, and the villagers stood in their doorways staring at the grass glowing faintly green-gold under their feet, at the well water that shimmered when you drew it up, at old Marta the weaver whose arthritic hands had stopped aching overnight and who kept flexing her fingers and laughing and then crying and then laughing again.

They stared at Paloma too.

She had expected anger. She had braced for it the whole way down the pass — the woman who broke the valley, who let the magic in, who ended a hundred years of safety with one broken oath. She had rehearsed defenses in the dark hours of the road. But the faces that turned toward her in the lane were not angry. They were afraid, yes, and grieving, and lost — but underneath all of it, unmistakably, they were *awake*. They looked at her the way drowning men look at the shore.

Elder Corvane met them at the chapel steps. He was older than she remembered, or she was older than he remembered; either way, the iron authority had gone out of him, leaving a tired man in good boots. His eyes went to the mark on Paloma's forearm — the oath-scar, which had burned black during the descent and now shone faintly silver, like moonlight caught under skin.

"You're alive," he said. It did not sound like relief. It sounded like a man discovering the bill has come due.

"The valley's protection is gone," Paloma said. No preamble. She'd learned that from dragons. "I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and I'm not going to apologize. You should hear what it cost before you decide how to grieve it."

So she told them. In the chapel, with the whole village packed in among the pews, she told them about the founding contract — the one signed by her grandmother, Maren Sterling, a hundred years ago, selling the soul of a young dragon named Ember to the court beneath the mountain in exchange for the valley's deadness, its safety, its blessed forgetting. She told them how every harvest, every safe birth, every quiet night they'd ever slept had been paid for out of another creature's fire. She told them about the strata of forfeited souls embedded in the rock beneath the mountain like flies in amber, and about the magistrates who had built their beauty entirely out of what other people owed.

Pip stood beside her the whole time, holding the hem of her coat, and when Paloma's voice failed — twice, at the parts about the children sold into collateral, at the part about her grandmother's steady hand on the signature line — the child squeezed her finger, and the words came back.

When she finished, the chapel was silent except for old Marta, weeping openly in the third row.

"We didn't know," Corvane said at last. It was not an excuse. Paloma gave him credit for not offering it as one. "We didn't know. We thought we were the faithful. We thought the silence was a test."

"The silence was a shame," Paloma said. "Ours, not his." She touched the silver scar. "He was never gone. He was in every honest oath anyone ever swore here. He just couldn't look at what our safety was standing on."

An old woman near the front — Paloma recognized her, barely, as the mother of a boy she'd trained with in the militia years ago — rose from her pew, walked to the front, and knelt. Not to Corvane. To Paloma. Or perhaps past her, to something behind her, in the space between the chapel walls where the air had begun, very faintly, to shine.

"What do we do now?" the woman asked. "Tell us what to do."

And Paloma, who had spent ten years taking orders from no one and giving them to no one, understood suddenly that this was the question the whole world would be asking in the months to come — the world waking up out of its ledgers, out of its debts, out of its comfortable forgetting. She thought of Wren's archive, half-burned, full of contracts waiting to be voided. She thought of Ilsa's veterans, scattered and soul-shaken, looking for a banner worth following. She thought of a young dragon learning to fly on new fire.

"Don't kneel," she said gently. "Stand up. That's the first thing. Stand up, and when someone hands you a contract, read it all the way through before you sign. That's the whole religion. That's all he ever wanted."

* * *

They buried the company on the hill above the mill, where the grass grew thickest and the view ran all the way down the valley to the southern pass.

There had never been graves before. That was the rule of the valley and the mercy of it: mercenaries who died abroad stayed abroad, their names entered in the chapel book and their bodies left to whatever ground had taken them. Nine names in the book. Nine names Paloma had read aloud alone, every year on the anniversary, in the dark, to a god who didn't answer. Kestrel. Brannoc. Little Sefa, who was nineteen. Dain, who owed her four coppers and a rematch at cards. The twins, Halle and Maren — no relation, though Halle used to joke they must be, because Maren Sterling spelled wrong looked exactly like Halle Marren. And four others whose voices she could still summon if she was very quiet and very brave.

Wren had copied the names onto fresh vellum in her beautiful court hand, each letter a small act of restoration. Ilsa had carved the markers herself, refusing help, working through the afternoon with her sleeves rolled and her jaw set, until nine rough stones stood in a row on the hill. Pip had gathered wildflowers from the meadow below — flowers that, this week, had begun to glow faintly at their centers, as if the valley's soil remembered sunlight differently now.

They buried the markers empty. The bodies were dust and distance. But Paloma brought things instead: Kestrel's whetstone, which she'd carried eleven years. Sefa's carved wooden bird. Dain's four coppers, counted out one by one into the earth, debt paid at last. Each object went into the ground with a name spoken over it, and with each name, Paloma felt something shift in her chest — not lighter, exactly. Set down.

When it was done, the sun was low and gold, and the hilltop smelled like cut grass and woodsmoke and, for the first time in the history of the valley, like magic — green and alive and humming.

Paloma stood before the nine stones a long time after the others had drifted back down toward the village lights.

"You'd hate this," she said conversationally. "All the ceremony. Kestrel would make that face. You know the face." She could see it perfectly: Kestrel's crooked grimace, one eyebrow up, the face he made at funerals and formal dinners and anything involving the word *duty*. "And Sefa would cry and pretend she wasn't crying, and Dain would say something filthy, and Halle would laugh, and Maren would elbow him, and Brannoc—" Her voice caught. She let it. She was done pretending her voice didn't catch. "Brannoc would just put his hand on my shoulder. That was his whole speech, every time. Hand on the shoulder. That was enough."

The wind moved through the grass. Somewhere below, a dog barked, and the bark rang faintly bell-like, and faded.

"He speaks now," she said. "You know that. When an oath gets kept, anywhere in the world, he says the name of whoever kept it. Wren heard him say hers. Said it sounded like her father's voice, which nearly killed her." She smiled, wet-eyed, at the middle stone. "He hasn't said yours yet. Any of yours. I keep waiting. I think maybe he's saving it. I think maybe oaths don't finish being kept until the people who swore them are properly laid down, and now you are, so." She spread her hands. "Any time. No rush. But any time."

The wind came again, warm, from the south, carrying the smell of far places.

And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, so quietly she almost missed it, a voice that was not wind said nine names — all nine, together, like a roll call at evening — and the grass on the hill bowed once, all in the same direction, toward the southern pass, toward the world.

Paloma laughed and cried at the same time, alone on the hill, and found that both fit fine in the same mouth.

* * *

Ember came at dusk.

Paloma saw the light first — a glow rising over the western ridge like a second sunset — and then the shape inside it, wings wide, scales running molten gold and copper, every beat of those enormous wings scattering embers that burned out slowly in the darkening air like falling stars. The valley's children screamed with delight from the rooftops. The adults screamed more quietly, from behind their doors, which Paloma considered fair.

The dragon landed in the fallow field below the mill with a sound like a landslide being polite about it, folded her wings, and lowered her head until one ancient eye — bright now, blazing now, nothing like the guttering candle-flame Paloma had first seen caged in terms beneath the mountain — regarded her from a distance of ten feet.

"You walk strangely," Ember said. Her voice was smoke and bells. "For someone returning home."

"I'm not sure it's home," Paloma said. "I'm not sure it ever was. I think it was a lock, and I lived in it."

"Mm." Smoke curled from the dragon's nostrils, fragrant, like cedar burning. "I spent a century in a cage made of sentences. I have some thoughts about the difference between a home and a container." The great eye blinked, slow. "You buried your dead today. I watched from the ridge. You did it properly. Names spoken. Debts paid. Even the coppers." Something that might have been a smile moved the vast scaled face. "That was well done. My grandsire would have approved. He had opinions about funerals. Mostly that there weren't enough fires."

Paloma huffed a laugh. "Is that a compliment? From you?"

"It is an observation," Ember said gravely, "that functions as a compliment. Learn the difference. It will serve you in negotiation." The dragon shifted, settling her bulk, and the ground hummed. "I did not come to trade compliments, thief. I came to make you an offer."

"I don't make bargains anymore."

"This is not a bargain. Bargains have terms. This has none. Listen." Ember's voice dropped, and the firelight in her throat dimmed to a coal-glow, intimate. "I am the last heir of Ashfall. The fire is returned to me — mine, and his, and the fire of every soul the Court swallowed and gave back when it fell. I am young, as my kind measures youth, and I am alone, as my kind should never be. Dragons do not fly well alone. We burn too hot and too narrow. We need—" the word seemed to cost her— "*company*. Not servants. Not worshippers. Company. Companions of the road and the sky."

She extended one foreleg. On the broad flat of it, between talons long as swords, the firelight gathered into a shape: a small flame, cupped and steady, offered.

"Fly with me, Paloma Sterling. Not as my thief. Not as my debtor or my rescuer. As my friend. There is room at a dragon's side for a whole life, and I am told you are very hard to kill, which is a quality I have come to appreciate."

Paloma looked at the offered flame for a long moment.

Behind her, up the slope, she could hear the others coming — Wren's quick footsteps, Ilsa's heavier ones, Pip's bare feet slapping the path, the mule protesting yet again. Her found family, arriving precisely when she was making a decision, because that was what families did.

A year ago — a month ago — she would have said no. She would have said no the way she'd said everything for ten years: flatly, safely, from inside the walls. But the walls were gone, and the valley glowed, and her god spoke in oaths, and nine names had been set down properly in the earth, and the southern pass stood open under the first stars.

"Yes," Paloma said.

She climbed onto the dragon's foreleg, up onto the warm scaled shoulder, and settled there between two ridges of spine that fit her back almost exactly, as if the seat had been waiting a century for her.

Ember's head swung around, one blazing eye level with hers. "Hold on," the dragon said, with unmistakable relish. "The first flight is always rough."

Below, Wren arrived at a run, took one look, and started shouting something about liability. Ilsa arrived a step later, took one look, and started laughing — actually laughing, head back, the sound ringing off the hills. Pip simply raised both arms and yelled, "Take me too!"

Ember's wings opened across the whole sky.

And Paloma Sterling, mercenary, oath-breaker, soul-free, gripped the dragon's spine, looked once back at the nine small stones on the hill and the lamplit village below and the open pass beyond — *home*, all of it, all of it home now, and none of it a wall — and said the last thing she said in the valley that raised her:

"Fly."

* * *

They flew north at moonrise, four riders and a dragon, chasing the curve of the world.

It was Pip who spotted it first — a smudge of red on the horizon, too steady for a fire, too low for a star. Ember banked toward it without being asked, drawn the way rivers are drawn to the sea. As they closed, the smudge resolved into a beacon burning atop a border-stone at the crossroads below, and beside it, small in the vast dark, a figure waving both arms, and beside the figure, a cart, and on the cart, a banner Wren recognized from her archive days and went very still at the sight of:

The sigil of the Free Companies — the old brotherhood of oath-mercenaries, thought extinct for a generation, whose founding charter had been voided by the Court a hundred years ago and locked in the vault Paloma had broken open.

The figure below cupped her hands and shouted up into the night, and the wind kindly carried it to dragon-height:

"Message for Paloma Sterling! From the free towns! They're reforming the Companies — and they want *her* to write the first oath!"

Paloma looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at Wren. Wren looked at Pip. Pip grinned like sunrise.

"Well," Paloma said to the dragon, into the rushing dark, already reaching for the words. "Let's go swear something."
