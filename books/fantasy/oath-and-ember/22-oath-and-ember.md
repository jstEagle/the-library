## 22. Oath and Ember

The road out of the valley was the same road she had walked a hundred times in dreams, except that now her boots left prints in real dust, and the dust was ordinary dust, and nothing about it tried to forget her.

Paloma Sterling had spent a year learning what that felt like.

She rode at the head of a column of five — four on horseback, one above — down through the hill country where the world had gone soft with spring. Behind her, Ilsa Vane argued with Wren Halloway about the wording of a contract; ahead of her, Pip hummed an off-key song about a fisherman who married the moon. Overhead, a shape like a knife drawn across the sky wheeled once, twice, and settled into a long glide, casting a shadow wide enough to shade all of them at once.

Ember had grown. A year ago she had been a candle-flame in a cage of terms, small enough to cup in two hands, bitter enough to cut. Now her wingspan darkened whole fields as she passed, and when she exhaled — casually, the way a person sighs — the grass along the roadside steamed and greened in the same breath. Dragon-fire did that now. It didn't burn so much as *wake*. Magic had come back to the world like blood returning to a sleeping limb, all pins and needles and sudden unbearable feeling, and everything it touched remembered what it had been made to be.

Paloma still wasn't used to it. She suspected she never would be. In the valley where she'd been born, magic had been dead the way a language is dead — not absent, exactly, but unspoken, its bones buried under the ordinary work of living. She had built her whole self in that silence. She had learned to fight without blessing, to heal without charm, to keep an oath to a god who gave back nothing but the weight of keeping it. And then she had learned, in a vault beneath a mountain, that even her silence had been purchased. Even her emptiness had a paper trail.

She flexed her right hand inside her glove. The oath-marks had faded from her skin — the burning script that had crawled up her arm through nine strata of forfeited souls — but sometimes, in cold weather or deep thought, she could feel where they had been, like scars from a fever. The god had written on her once. It had not written since.

But it spoke. That was the difference. That was everything.

A year ago, in the collapsing dark of the Undercourt, when Ember's fire had come back and every held soul in that place had torn itself free of its ledger-line, the silent god had finally opened its mouth — through Pip, through a thousand honest oaths sworn in good faith across a hundred years, through Paloma herself, though she hadn't known until later that the words leaving her own lips hadn't entirely been hers. The god had never gone anywhere. It lived in every promise kept, every vow honored past the point of sense, every debt paid that no one would ever know about. The Court beneath the mountain had starved it by owning the debts; Paloma had fed it by breaking hers — freely, ruinously, in the one currency the Court could never price.

Now it spoke rarely. But always when an oath was kept. Always, without fail, like a bell rung somewhere just behind the world.

Pip said you could hear it best in small things. A farmer shaking hands on a fair price. A soldier standing a watch he could have abandoned. A child returning a borrowed knife. Each time, Pip's eyes would go silver for half a heartbeat, and she'd smile at nothing, and say, *There it is again.*

Paloma heard it differently. She felt it in her chest — a warmth like a coal set down gently in a cold room. Not words. Just acknowledgment. *Seen,* the warmth said. *Kept.*

It was more than the valley's god had ever given her in thirty years of faithful silence. Or maybe it was the same thing, and she simply hadn't known how to listen. She suspected that too.

* * *

They took contracts the way other companies took loot: carefully, and only the ones worth having.

The company had no name yet, which drove Wren quietly mad. She had drafted eleven proposals, each more ornate than the last, ranging from *The Oathbreakers' Guild* (rejected by unanimous vote, mostly because Paloma refused to be celebrated for the worst day of her life) to *Sterling, Vane & Associates* (rejected because Ilsa threatened to resign over it) to something involving a phoenix that Pip vetoed on the grounds that *phoenixes are show-offs.* For now they operated without letterhead, which suited Paloma fine. Word traveled faster than ink anyway. Word traveled the way fire did now — waking everything it touched.

Their business was simple to describe and complicated to do: they freed people from bad bargains.

There were more of those than anyone wanted to count. The Undercourt's collapse had scattered its ledgers to the winds — literal winds, in some cases, since Wren swore the archive-vault had vented three centuries of contracts into the sky during the fall, and farmers as far away as the coast reported finding binding clauses caught in their hedgerows like strange gray moths. Most of the Court's holdings had burned clean when Ember ignited. But a contract is a stubborn thing. Some survived in copies. Some survived in memory. Some survived in the habits of the magistrates who had escaped the fall and were even now, in scattered cities, rebuilding their trade in smaller rooms with quieter doors.

Those were the contracts Paloma's company hunted. Not the magistrates themselves — though Ilsa had opinions about that, and expressed them weekly — but the paper. The terms. The fine print wrapped around some widow's house or some orphan's name or some village's water rights, signed by an ancestor who hadn't understood, or hadn't been given the choice.

Wren was the blade for that work. Disgraced court scribe, keeper of the book that had started everything, she could read a soul-contract the way Paloma read terrain — fast, flat-eyed, looking for the weakness. She had voided forty-one contracts this year alone. She kept a list. She was very proud of the list.

Ilsa was the shield. Former captain, former betrayer, current penitent, she handled the negotiations that couldn't be won with ink — which is to say, the ones where the other party owned swords. She had a way of standing in doorways that made violence seem like a poor investment. She and Paloma had not forgiven each other, exactly. But they had arrived somewhere past forgiveness, into a country with no name for itself, where trust was rebuilt daily instead of declared once, and where each of them kept choosing, every morning, to ride beside the other. Paloma had decided this was better than forgiveness. Forgiveness was a door you walked through once. This was a road you walked together.

And Pip — Pip was the compass. Eleven years old, god-touched, orphaned twice over (once by fire, once by paperwork), she heard oaths being kept the way other children heard weather coming. When a client sat across from them and swore their grievance honestly, Pip would nod along, satisfied. When a magistrate's agent lied through his teeth about the terms of a release, Pip would tug Paloma's sleeve and whisper, *His mouth says yes but his oath says no,* and that was usually the end of the negotiation and occasionally the end of the agent's career.

As for Paloma herself — she was still deciding what she was. Captain, the others called her, though she hadn't accepted the title formally. Thief, the prophecy had called her, and there was truth in that too; she had stolen a soul back from hell, and she supposed she'd steal others if the terms required it. But mostly, these days, she was a woman who read contracts very slowly before signing anything, and who slept well at night, and who was learning — slowly, clumsily, like a language picked up in adulthood — how to be warm.

* * *

The contract that brought them down the spring road concerned a miller in the town of Harrowgate, whose grandfather had signed away the mill's *flour-soul* — Wren's term, and a useful one — to a traveling magistrate in exchange for surviving a famine winter. For sixty years the mill had ground flour that fed people and emptied them, a little, each loaf: a spoonful of vigor here, a season of good dreams there, siphoned up the old tithe-lines toward a court that no longer existed but whose pipes, apparently, still drew.

The miller's daughter had found one of the fallen contract-leaves caught in her garden hedge, recognized her family's seal, and walked eleven days to find the company everyone was starting to whisper about. *The ones who break the paper,* people called them. Paloma hated the nickname. Wren loved it. They had agreed to disagree.

Harrowgate smelled of bread and river-water when they rode in, and the mill turned lazily on a wind that seemed, to Paloma's newly educated senses, slightly wrong — a wind with a lean to it, a pull, like a current running toward a drain that someone had forgotten to cap.

"Still drawing," Wren confirmed that evening, kneeling by the millrace with a lens and a lamp, reading the faint script threaded through the water like oil. "The lines are dead upstream, but the local loop persists. Someone's been maintaining it." Her jaw tightened. "Someone's been *feeding* it."

"A magistrate?" Ilsa asked, from the doorway, arms crossed.

"Or a remnant. Or a true believer." Wren looked up, lamplight catching the ink stains on her fingers — permanent now, she'd said, part of her forever, and she'd smiled when she said it, which told Paloma everything about what the archives had meant to her. "The Court is dead. Courts don't stay dead politely. They leave priests."

They worked the problem for six days. Wren unpicked the contract's grammar while Ilsa ran down rumors of a hooded figure seen at the millrace after dark. Pip played with the miller's daughter and listened to the town's oaths — most of them honest, a few strained, one or two that made her frown and say nothing, which was worse than anything she could have said. Ember circled high and far, conspicuous enough to warn off trouble, present enough to reassure. The townspeople had been afraid of dragons their whole lives. By the third day, children were waving at the shadow overhead, and the miller's daughter had taken to calling her *the big warm bird*, and Ember pretended to be offended and was visibly delighted.

On the sixth night, they caught the priest.

He was young. That was the thing Paloma hadn't prepared for. She had expected some ancient survivor, some relic of the old horror, and instead the hood came back to reveal a face barely older than Ilsa's, soft with conviction, holding a ledger-book against his chest like a child holds a blanket.

"The Court kept order," he said, backed against the millrace, water-script glowing faintly around his ankles where he'd waded in to feed the lines. "You think the world works without debt? You think people keep their word out of *goodness*? Goodness is a luxury. Debt is a structure. We held the structure."

"We held people," Paloma said. "In amber. In rock. For centuries." She thought of the strata they had descended, the faces in the stone, the souls embedded like flies in honey. She thought of Ember, lucid and bitter in her cage of terms, asking, *You come to feel better, not to free me.* "I've seen your structure from the inside. It doesn't hold people up. It holds them down."

"They consented," he whispered. "Their ancestors consented—"

"Their ancestors were starving," Wren said quietly, "or frightened, or deceived, or all three. I've read the founding papers. There isn't a single free signature in the entire archive. I checked." She held out her hand. "Give me the book."

For a moment he seemed about to bolt — Ilsa shifted her weight in a way that closed the doorway of the night behind him — and then, strangely, he wept, and gave it over, and stood empty-handed in the glow of the dying lines while Wren, by lamplight, pronounced the last clause void.

The millrace went quiet. Not silent — quiet, the way a room goes quiet when a clock that has ticked all your life finally stops and you realize how loud it had been. The wind lost its lean. The flour, the miller swore afterward, tasted like flour again.

That night, the priest — name of Aldous, twenty-three years old, formerly of the Office of Perpetual Obligations — sat at their fire because Ilsa refused to let him wander off to find another ledger, and ate their food, and asked, in a small voice, what he was supposed to believe in now, if not debt.

Pip answered him. She was roasting a apple on a stick, and she didn't even look up.

"Oaths," she said. "Not the ones you're forced into. The ones you mean."

Somewhere behind the world, a bell rang. Paloma felt the coal settle warm in her chest, and saw Pip's eyes flash silver, and watched the boy's face change — not convinced, not yet, but *cracked*, light getting in.

* * *

They left Harrowgate on a morning so clear the mountains looked cut from glass.

Ember flew low for the first mile — low for her, which still meant the horses flattened their ears — and fell in beside Paloma's window of the sky, one great eye level with her, iris like a struck match.

*You're doing it again,* Ember said. Her mind-voice had matured with her body: less bitter, more dry, the tone of someone much older wearing a young dragon's patience. *Counting.*

"Counting what?"

*What it cost. What it bought. Whether they balance.* The eye blinked, slow, reptilian, amused. *You've done it every morning for a year. I can hear it from the air. It sounds like scales tipping.*

"I'm a mercenary," Paloma said. "We're born with scales in our heads."

*Mm.* Ember banked, wheeled, came around again. Below, the others had stopped to let Pip race her pony against Ilsa's warhorse, a contest rigged so thoroughly in the pony's favor that even the warhorse seemed embarrassed. *My grandsire used to say that accounting is grief that hasn't found a grave. He was a fool about many things — he sold my mother's soul, after all — but he was right about that.* A tendril of smoke curled from Ember's nostrils, fragrant, like cedar. *Bury it, Sterling. The valley. The company. The grandmother. All of it. You've paid. Let it rest in the ground.*

"I buried the company," Paloma said. "Last autumn. Proper stones, proper names. Wren carved them."

*I know. I flew over. It looked right.* A pause, wings beating once, twice. *Did you bury yourself?*

Paloma looked at her hands on the reins. The scar-places where the oath-script had burned. The calluses of thirty years of sword-work. The ring she wore now — plain iron, forged from a nail pulled out of the Undercourt's vault door, which Wren had insisted on keeping and which Paloma had claimed before Wren could turn it into a bookmark.

"Not yet," she said. "I'm still deciding who I am without the debt. Give me another year."

*Take ten,* Ember said generously. *I intend to live a very long time, and I refuse to spend it watching you audit your own soul.* Then, softer, in the voice she used perhaps twice a year: *You gave your claim to free me. Everything I am now is interest on that gift. I have decided to pay it forward at a generous rate.*

"You don't owe me—"

*I know,* Ember said. *That's what makes it an oath and not a debt. Do keep up.*

She rose then, spiraling up into the blue until she was a spark, then a suggestion, then only a warmth in the sky's memory, and Paloma rode on smiling despite herself, and did not notice until evening that she had stopped counting.

* * *

Summer found them in the west, where the mountains came down to meet the sea and the old tithe-shafts had become, of all things, tourist attractions — gawkers paying copper to peer into the dark holes where souls had once been lowered in chains. Wren found this obscene. Pip found it fascinating. Ilsa found it profitable, having negotiated a fee from the local council for inspecting the shafts and certifying them clean, which was the first time in recorded history a mercenary company had been paid to prevent trouble rather than cause it.

It was there, on a cliff above the sea, that the god spoke to Paloma directly for the second time in her life.

The first time had been in the Undercourt, in the moment of breaking, and she had been too shattered to parse it. This time was quiet. Ordinary. She was standing watch at dawn — old habit, though nothing needed watching — when a fishing family came down the path below her, arguing cheerfully about whether the youngest son was old enough to take the boat out alone. The father stopped at the cliff-edge, looked his boy in the eye, and said, "Bring her home safe, and I'll teach you the deep-water nets myself. My word on it."

The boy swore it back. Grinning. Careless. Meant.

The coal in Paloma's chest flared — and then, for the first time, it *spoke*. Not in words. In certainty. Like sunlight landing on closed eyelids; like knowing, without knowing how, that somewhere far away, something vast had turned its attention, briefly, fondly, toward two people on a cliff making a promise about fish.

*Kept,* the warmth said. Or would say, someday, when the boy came home with salt in his hair and his father taught him the deep-water nets, and the god rang its bell behind the world, and Pip, three villages away, looked up from breakfast with silver eyes and a smile.

Paloma stood her watch and wept a little, the way she had wept in Pip's shelter a year ago — easily now, without shame, grief and gratitude sharing the same water — and when the sun cleared the sea she went down to make breakfast, and nobody asked why her eyes were red, because everybody already knew.

* * *

Autumn brought them back east, back toward the valley, because Wren had received a letter — an actual letter, sealed, formal — from the elders of Paloma's homeland, requesting, with the stiff courtesy of people unused to asking, that the company come consult on a matter of contracts.

The valley was changing. That was the marvel of it. With magic returned to the world, the valley's protective deadness had become obsolete — a wall around a house whose war was over — and the elders, in their cautious way, were trying to decide what their home should be now. Open? Connected? Part of the waking world?

Some of them were terrified. Paloma understood. She had been terrified too, the first time she'd walked out and the boundary had simply... let her pass, like a hand opening.

But others were curious. Children mostly. Children who had heard rumors of a world where rivers remembered songs and dragons crossed the sky like weather, and who looked at the valley's borders the way Paloma had once looked at them — except that now, when they looked, they saw a door instead of a wall.

The consultation took a week. Wren reviewed the valley's founding charter — the original one, predating the soul-sale, and it turned out the valley had been founded as a sanctuary, a deliberate quiet place, not a prison; the prison-ness had accreted later, like rust. There was nothing in the charter requiring isolation. Only tradition. Only fear.

"We can amend it," Wren said, on the last night, quill hovering. "Open the borders. Formalize passage. Or we can leave it as it is, and let people simply... walk. No law at all. Doors don't need charters."

Paloma thought about it for a long time, sitting outside the circle of firelight, looking at the hills she had never expected to see again and the stars she had never expected to share them with.

"No charter," she said at last. "Let it be a door. Charters get enforced. Doors just... open."

Wren wrote it down anyway, because she was Wren. But she wrote it lightly, in pencil, and never inked it, and somehow that mattered.

* * *

Winter they spent south, in a stone house Ember had more or less annexed — a ruined watchtower on a warm coast, which the dragon had re-roofed with flame-hardened slate and declared *suitable*, in the tone of a duchess reviewing lodgings. Pip hung her treasures from the rafters: river-glass, a falcon's feather, the stick she'd roasted apples on in Harrowgate ("it's lucky," she said, defying all argument). Ilsa drilled in the courtyard every morning, teaching Pip staff-work and Wren how to throw a punch that wouldn't break her own wrist. Wren built shelves. So many shelves. The tower began to fill with rescued contracts — the voided ones, kept as records, as warnings, as history — organized in a system only she could navigate, labeled in three colors of ink.

And Paloma — Paloma cooked. Nobody had planned this. It had simply happened, the way it happens in families: she'd made dinner once, and then again, and then it was hers, and one evening she'd looked down at a pot of stew she was seasoning by instinct and realized she was humming, and that the humming had no tune, and that she was, improbably, unfathomably, content.

On midwinter's night, they all climbed the tower roof, because Ember insisted, because the stars were out, because Pip had heard from the god that something was going to happen and refused to say what, which was her most annoying habit and her most endearing one.

At midnight, the sky answered her.

It began far north — a ripple of color, green shot through with gold, spreading south like a banner unfurling. The old-timers would have called it a sign of ill fortune. But the gold in it was new; everyone felt that. The gold moved the way dragon-fire moved, the way magic moved now, waking everything it touched. As it spread, lights kindled along the ground to answer it — distant, scattered, everywhere: hearth-fires and lanterns and ward-lamps in villages across the whole curve of the world, each one flaring briefly brighter, as if the land itself were blinking awake.

And in every one of those places, Paloma knew, someone was keeping a promise. Standing a watch. Paying a debt no one would ever record. Saying a small true thing and meaning it. And behind the world, the god — her silent god, silent for thirty years, silent for a century, silent only because it had been gagged by paper and shame — was ringing its bells, one for each, a carillon no single ear could hear but which the whole night somehow carried.

Pip was crying and laughing. Wren had both hands over her mouth. Ilsa stood at rigid attention, then slowly, deliberately, relaxed — the way a soldier stands down — and Paloma saw her mouth move and caught the words: *at ease. all of you. at ease.*

Overhead, Ember rose without wings, borne on the updraft of her own joy, and breathed fire at the aurora — a thin bright thread joining earth to sky — and the aurora bent around it, acknowledging, and for one impossible moment the whole night was a single held chord.

Then it faded. Stars again. Cold again. The tower roof, the family, the fire in the brazier.

"Well," said Wren, after a while, voice unsteady. "I have absolutely nothing in my archive about that."

"Good," said Paloma.

* * *

Spring came around again, as springs do, and with it the road.

They went out the way they always did: Ilsa checking the harnesses, Wren triple-locking the tower (unnecessarily; Ember guarded it), Pip racing ahead on her pony, Ember spiraling up into a sky rinsed clean by rain. Paloma locked the door last, then stood a moment with her hand on the iron — the same iron as her ring, the same iron as the vault door, iron that had held a thousand stolen names and now held nothing but a latch.

A year ago she had been a woman with one foot in a grave of her own digging: bound to a silent god, marked by a prophecy, owned without knowing it by a court of debts. Tonight's date had been written in a book a century old, and she had read it aloud anyway, chosen the hook behind the ribs, walked out of the only world she knew into one with teeth.

She had lost nearly everything. The valley's protection. Her certainty. Her belief that the silence meant abandonment. Her clean, simple anger at Ilsa. Her identity as the woman the debt had built.

She had gained the rest. A god that spoke. A family that stayed. A dragon overhead. Work worth doing, in a world worth doing it in.

The road ran south and then west, toward a river-town where a ferryman had sworn an unfair contract over his own daughter's name and regretted it every day since; Wren had the letter in her saddlebag, and the terms were ugly, and the loophole, Wren suspected, was beautiful. Four days' ride. Maybe five, if Pip made them stop for every interesting rock.

Paloma swung into the saddle. The others fell in around her, easy, practiced, a formation that had never been drilled and couldn't be broken. Above, the shadow passed over them, wide enough to shade them all.

"Contract's simple," she said, loud enough for all of them, the way captains speak and mothers do. "We go, we read the paper, we break what needs breaking. Anybody swear to anything on the way, mean it."

Pip laughed. Ilsa snorted. Wren said, "That's not a formal oath structure," and Ember, from the sky, said, *Sign nothing, Wren. Some things hold better unwritten.*

Paloma raised one hand to the wind — to the road, to the dragon, to the god behind the world, to all of it — and swore, lightly, the way you'd toss a coin to a fountain:

*"Home by harvest. All of us. My word on it."*

Behind the world, a bell rang — warm, certain, glad.

And she knew, riding out under the wide spring sky with her family around her and her god awake in every kept promise on the road ahead, that it would hold.
