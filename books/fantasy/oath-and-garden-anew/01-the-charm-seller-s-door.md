## 1. The Charm-Seller's Door

The frost came early to Vellamark that year, and Lior Zhou was glad of it. Cold kept people moving, and moving people bought charms—little knots of braided hemp and river-reed meant to hurry them along their errands, to keep the wind from their collars, to make a shopkeeper's scales tip generous. Nobody lingered in the cold long enough to ask questions, and questions were the one thing Lior's stall could not afford.

They set up in the gray market before dawn, as they had every morning for ten years, laying out the luck-knots on a square of undyed wool with hands that had learned stillness the way other hands learned instruments. Each knot took eleven turns. Eleven, never twelve; twelve was a number the old stories gave to binding, and binding was a word that made informants sit up straighter over their bread. So: eleven turns, a drop of beeswax, a whispered word that was not a word at all but only breath shaped like one, empty of everything except the habit of power. A charm that did nothing, sold to people who needed it to do something. It was honest work, in its way. It was also a mask worn so long Lior sometimes forgot there was a face beneath it.

"Two coppers for the small knots," they said to a fishwife whose knuckles were split from the brine. "Three if you want the blue thread."

"The blue thread keeps my man off the drink?"

"The blue thread is pretty," Lior said. "The rest is between him and the drink."

She laughed and bought two anyway. That was the trick of it, the whole architecture of Lior's careful life: sell people the shape of hope and let them pour their own meaning into it. Hope was legal. Hope was even encouraged, so long as it came in knots and not in words, so long as no beast ever cocked its head at you and *listened*.

A pigeon landed on the awning pole above the stall. Lior did not look at it. Ten years of practice went into not looking—the pigeon, the rats threading between the market stalls, the mangy yellow dog that slept behind the chandler's and dreamed loud enough some mornings that Lior could taste its dreams like copper on the tongue. The city was full of beasts, and beasts were full of memory, and memory was the thing the crown had outlawed. Not magic, exactly. Magic was too diffuse to burn out of a whole population. What the Wardens hunted was the old speech, the First Tongue, the language that had once set fangs and wings against thrones—and the people who carried any echo of it in their blood.

Lior had spent a decade being nobody's echo. They sold knots. They paid their market-tithe promptly. They nodded to the Warden who walked the gray market's north row, a young man with chapped lips and bored eyes, and they let their mind go smooth and shallow as a frozen pond whenever he passed. *I am a charm-seller. I have always been a charm-seller.* The lie had grown roots. Some days it felt more true than anything else about them.

By midmorning the frost had turned to a thin, needling rain, and the crowd thickened despite it. Lior sold a knot to a boy who wanted luck at cards, another to an old man who wanted his dead wife to visit his dreams kindly. The old man's hands shook. Lior tied the knot slowly for him, letting the ritual of it be the comfort, and charged him one copper he didn't have, and told him the debt was forgiven before he'd finished apologizing. This too was part of the mask: kindness was unremarkable. Kindness drew no eyes.

It was nearly dusk when the light changed.

Not the sky—the sky had been going gray to slate all afternoon—but something in the quality of the noise around the stall, a hush spreading outward like oil on water. Lior looked up from a half-finished knot and saw the market turning to look at something behind them, at the narrow door of the shuttered dyehouse where Lior rented a room above and stored wool below. And in the doorway, swaying like a cut sapling, stood a child.

Barefoot. Barefoot in the freezing rain, feet black with street filth, toes blue. A girl, or a boy—it was hard to tell under the matted hair and the oversized coat that might once have belonged to a grown man. Maybe eight years old, maybe ten. Small for either. The child took three steps into the market, and the hush followed them, and Lior understood with a cold sliding feeling in their gut that the silence wasn't curiosity.

It was the animals.

Every rat along the north row had come up out of the drains and sat in the open, in the rain, in plain view of a hundred boots, facing the child. Pigeons crowded the eaves until the gutters sagged. Behind the chandler's, the yellow dog stood with its ears forward, trembling, and did not bark. The whole gray market's hidden population had surfaced at once, the way fish rise before a storm, and every eye of them—wet, black, patient—was fixed on the barefoot child staggering between the stalls.

Lior's hands went still over the luck-knots. Eleven turns undone in their mind, one by one, back through ten years.

The child reached the stall. Up close they smelled of smoke—not hearth-smoke, not torch-smoke, but the deep, wrong smell of a place burned all the way down—and of blood gone old on cloth. Their eyes were enormous and unfocused, tracking nothing, seeing perhaps everything. Their lips moved. No sound came. Then, as their knees buckled, sound did come—a string of syllables in a cadence Lior's body knew before their mind could refuse it, a rising-and-falling pattern like wind through tall grass, like a mother's voice heard from inside the womb.

The First Tongue. Spoken by a child of eight, maybe ten, in the middle of Vellamark's gray market, in front of forty witnesses and one Warden.

The child collapsed. Lior caught them without deciding to—hands moving on old instinct, gathering the small burning-cold weight against their chest—and in the same instant the beasts moved. The pigeons lifted off the eaves in a single clattering sheet and wheeled once over the market, all of them together, one creature wearing a thousand feathers. The rats poured back into the dark. The dog lay down flat, chin on paws, watching Lior with an expression Lior refused, *refused*, to name.

And down the north row, unhurried, the Warden with the chapped lips stopped walking. Turned. Looked at the wheeling birds. Looked at the charm-seller holding a unconscious, barefoot, smoke-smelling child.

Lior smiled at him. It cost more than any knot they'd ever tied.

"Just a beggar child," they called, light and bored. "Fainted. I'll see them to the charity house."

The Warden's eyes stayed on the sky a moment longer, where the last pigeons were settling, all facing the same direction. Then he shrugged and walked on, and Lior stood in the rain with a heartbeat like a war drum and a decade of silence cracking down its length like river ice in spring.

They carried the child inside, bolted the door, and lit no lamp. In the dark, the small body breathed fast and shallow, and muttered—always muttering now, fragments of that grass-wind cadence, words Lior had sworn at nineteen never to understand again and understood perfectly, word for word, syllable for syllable.

*Run,* said the child, in the language older than walls. *Run, the teeth are coming, run—*

Outside, in the rain, something four-legged sat down in the alley beneath Lior's window and began, very softly, to wait.

* * *

Lior did not sleep. They sat on the floor beside the pallet where the child lay, knees drawn up, back against the wall, listening to the rain and the muttering and the small animal sounds of a body running from something even in sleep. Once, near midnight, the child's hand shot out and gripped Lior's wrist with shocking strength, and the muttering resolved into a single clear phrase, repeated twice, urgent:

*Hide-the-small-one. Hide-the-small-one. The-ash-man-takes-the-speakers.*

Lior pried the fingers loose, gently, and the child sank back into nightmare. *The ash-man.* Lior turned the phrase over the way they'd turn a coin of doubtful minting. It had the ring of a name earned rather than given. Somewhere out there was a man—or a woman—whose name the beasts had already learned, which meant somewhere out there was a village that no longer existed, because the crown did not send Wardens to take speakers alive unless it wanted what speakers knew.

And it wanted this child. Or wanted whatever the child carried.

Toward dawn the muttering stopped, and the breathing evened, and Lior crept to the window and eased back the shutter a finger's width. The alley was empty. Whatever had waited there had waited long enough and gone. But on the wet cobbles below, pressed into the thin skin of ice, was a track the size of Lior's spread hand—four toes, a pad, claws sunk deep—and leading away from it, dozens more, a line of them marching off toward the river as neat as a procession.

Big dog, Lior told themselves. Big dog, escaped from some drover's wagon.

Big dogs did not leave tracks in processions. Big dogs did not wait beneath windows all night, silent, sentry-still. Big dogs did not answer to children who spoke in their sleep.

Lior closed the shutter and leaned their forehead against the cold wood and did the arithmetic they had been avoiding since dusk. Forty witnesses in the market. One Warden who had seen the birds turn. A child who could not stop speaking the outlawed tongue even unconscious. And Lior's own name, Lior Zhou, charm-seller, tithe-payer, nobody—about to be spoken in rooms where names became sentences.

There was a version of this where Lior carried the child to the charity house at first light and washed their hands and went back to selling knots and lived another ten quiet years, probably. There was a version where Lior smothered the child in their sleep, quick and kind, the way you put down a wounded animal, and buried the guilt under the floorboards with everything else.

And there was a third version, standing in the corner of Lior's mind like an old friend they'd sworn never to speak to again, wearing the face of everything they'd buried: the grass-wind cadence, the wheeling birds, the procession of prints walking away toward the river like soldiers going home.

On the pallet, the child stirred and opened their eyes—dark, clear, ancient in a way no eight-year-old's eyes should be—and looked directly at Lior, and spoke again, awake this time, deliberate, in the First Tongue:

*You-remember. You-remember-the-speaking. Help.*

Not a plea. A recognition.

Lior closed their eyes. Ten years fell over them like snowfall, soft and total and burying.

"No," they said aloud, in plain Vellamark speech, voice rough with disuse and fear. "No, I don't. I'm a charm-seller. I sell knots."

The child watched them, unblinking, while outside the window the rain turned to sleet, and somewhere across the sleeping city a bell began to toll—not the hour, not the alarm, but the slow nine strokes that meant the Wardens had found something burned, and would be asking the morning market who had seen anything, and paying for answers in bread.

Lior listened to the bell finish, and looked at the child, and understood that the decade was already over.
