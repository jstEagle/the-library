## 2. A Language Older Than Walls

The cellar smelled of turnips and old stone, which was, Lior thought as they barred the door behind them, exactly the smell of safety. Ten years of it. Ten years of harmless charms—luck-knots and fever-wards and little knotted blessings that did nothing but make people feel watched over—and ten years of a life so deliberately gray that even the rats in Vellamark's walls had forgotten to look at them twice.

The child was curled against the far wall where Lior had left them an hour ago, beneath the shelf of pickled things, wrapped in a blanket that had once been a tablecloth. Their eyes were open. They had not slept.

"You're still here," Lior said, and immediately felt foolish for it. Where would the child have gone? Barefoot, half-starved, into a city where informants were paid in bread and a strange accent was worth a week of meals.

Sef looked up. The firelight from the little lamp caught their face—the hollowed cheeks, the split lip, the eyes that were too old for the rest of them. "The small ones came back," the child said.

Lior went cold. "What small ones?"

"The ones with the quick hearts." Sef lifted one hand from the blanket, and something moved in the dark behind the pickle shelf—a shape, low and sleek, that resolved itself into a rat. Not fleeing. *Waiting.* It sat on its haunches at the edge of the lamplight like a dog at a hearth, whiskers trembling, and its black eyes were fixed on the child with an attention no rat had ever wasted on Lior in ten years of living above them.

"Get out," Lior hissed at it. The rat did not move.

"They brought me bread," Sef said. "From the floor of the market. It wasn't dirty."

"That's—" Lior stopped. Swallowed. Started again, more carefully, the way one walks across ice. "That's not how rats work."

"It is how they work when you ask them properly." The child said it simply, without pride, the way another child might say *the sun comes up in the morning*. "You just have to use the right words. The words before the walls."

*The words before the walls.*

Lior set the lamp down on the step because their hands had begun to shake, and if the light shook too, the child might see it. There was a word for what Sef was speaking. Of course there was a word; there was always a word, buried under other words, under the ash of a war that had ended before Lior was born. Every charm-seller knew the story the way every charm-seller knew not to believe it: that once there had been speakers who talked to beasts in the First Tongue, and the beasts had come to war at their calling, and the war had been terrible, and then it had ended—not won, not lost, but *ended*, the way a fever ends, leaving everyone alive and no one able to say why.

And then magic had been outlawed, and the Wardens had been founded, and the stories had been filed away with the luck-knots and the fever-wards, in the place where harmless things lived.

Except the rat was still sitting there. Except the child's voice, when it spoke again, made a sound in the air that Lior's bones recognized the way a bell recognizes its own note struck across a city.

"*Kesh-vara,*" Sef said softly, to the rat. "*Kesh-vara, im-mal.*"

Go home, little one. Be safe.

The rat turned and flowed back into the dark between the stones, obedient as breath.

Lior sat down on the stairs. It was not a decision; their legs simply ended their employment. Above them, muffled by floors, Vellamark went on with its morning—carts, gulls, the fishwife shouting about herring—and none of it knew that the oldest language in the world had just been spoken in a root cellar under a charm-shop that sold nothing but knots.

"Where did you learn that?" Lior asked. Their voice came out strange. Flat. Careful.

"I didn't learn it." Sef frowned, genuinely puzzled. "It's just how you talk. Don't you talk it?"

"No," Lior said. "No one does. No one has for—" They stopped themselves. *A hundred years,* they almost said, and heard, underneath the sentence, the whisper of a different number. A smaller one. A number with their own name attached to it.

There was something at the edge of that thought, the way there is something at the edge of a dark room. Lior did not go toward it. They had gotten very good, in ten years, at not going toward things.

"Eat," they said instead, pressing a heel of yesterday's bread into the child's hands. "Sleep if you can. I have to open the shop."

Sef took the bread but didn't eat it. "You're frightened," the child said. Not accusing. Observing, the way they observed everything, with those too-old eyes. "Of me?"

"Of what you are," Lior said, before they could stop themselves, and saw the words land, and hated it. "Eat your bread."

They climbed the stairs two at a time, because if they climbed them slowly they might turn around, and if they turned around they might do something unforgivable, like stay.

* * *

The morning passed the way mornings were supposed to pass. Gray light through gray windows. A widow bought a knot for her son's fishing boat. A clerk bought a fever-ward he didn't believe in, because believing in nothing was worse. Lior tied the knots and spoke the little harmless words over them and took the coins and smiled the smile they had practiced until it fit their face like a scar.

But twice, they caught themselves listening.

Once, when the pigeons on the roof ridge all turned their heads at the same moment, a hundred small faces swinging toward the shop like compass needles finding north. Once, when a cart-horse in the street below shied violently away from the doorway, whites showing, while its driver cursed it—and Lior stood very still in the middle of their own shop, hands full of colored cord, and understood that the horse hadn't spooked at anything visible at all.

The beasts could feel the child. Or the child was calling them. Or both. And every beast in Vellamark that turned its head toward this street was a sign, and signs were what the Wardens followed.

Lior had just decided—they would close early, they would take the child and leave tonight, they would think of somewhere—when the knock came.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. Patient in the way that only one kind of visitor is patient.

Lior's hands went still over the knot they were tying. Through the window, in the street, they saw the gray cloak first, then the badge: the crowned tower of the Warden's office, stamped in white metal. The man wearing it was young, broad, with the careful pleasant face of someone who had learned that courtesy got doors opened faster than force.

He knocked again.

Ten years. Ten years of knots that did nothing, of words that meant nothing, of being a gray woman in a gray market selling gray comforts. Ten years since Lior Zhou had last worked a charm on a living mind—and they remembered, with horrible clarity, why they had sworn never to do it again, though the reason itself sat in that dark room at the edge of their memory, refusing to come forward.

The knock came a third time.

Lior crossed the shop and opened the door before the fourth knock, smiling, and the smile was already working, already weaving, because some part of them had started the charm before any conscious part gave permission.

"Warden," they said warmly. "Cold morning to be walking the rounds."

"Just routine, mistress." He touched his badge, half salute, half habit. "We're asking after strangers. A child, possibly. Seen anyone pass through?"

"A child." Lior let her brow crease with mild, honest puzzlement. Underneath the crease, beneath the warmth of her own voice, she could feel the charm taking hold of him like current taking hold of a swimmer—gentle, total, intimate. She could feel the shape of his attention and bend it, the way a hand bends a willow switch. It would be so easy. It had always been so easy. That was the horror of it. "Children pass through here all day, Warden. Buying sweets, chasing gulls. Is one missing?"

"Not missing." His eyes moved past her shoulder, into the shop, and Lior felt him *look*—felt the charm catch his gaze and slide it gently off the trapdoor in the corner, off the scuffed cellar door, off everything that mattered, redirecting it toward the display of luck-knots as if they were the most interesting thing in Vellamark. "Runaway. Dangerous. There's a reward for information." He said it lightly, but his hand had drifted to rest near the long knife at his belt, and Lior understood that the reward was not the point. The point was the looking. The Wardens were not searching for a runaway. They were searching for a *sign*.

"Well," Lior said, and heard her own voice stay soft and sorry and utterly trustworthy, "I've seen nothing like that. Only the usual faces. But I'll keep my eyes open—Vellamark looks after its own, doesn't it?"

"Doesn't it," the Warden agreed, and his eyes were warm now, agreeable, agreeing with whatever she suggested, and some deep animal part of Lior recoiled from how easily it worked, how *good* it felt, the old power rising in her chest like blood into a numb limb, pins and needles, painful and sweet. Ten years of silence, and the gift had not rusted at all. It had only been waiting.

"Good day to you, mistress."

"Good day, Warden. Mind the ice on the corner steps."

She closed the door. She stood with her forehead against it and listened to his boots recede down the street, and only when they had faded entirely did she realize she was shaking—not with fear, or not only with fear.

Because for one moment, standing in her own doorway with a Warden's mind open beneath her hands like a book, Lior Zhou had felt *powerful*. And underneath the terror, underneath the guilt, some old buried thing had purred.

* * *

She found Sef awake at the bottom of the stairs, bread untouched, watching the door.

"Was it the grey men?" the child asked.

"Yes."

"They're following the beasts' voices." Sef said it with the flat certainty of a statement about weather. "The beasts are all talking about me. I can't make them stop. I tried."

Lior crouched down to the child's level, and the question came out of her before she could weigh it. "Why did they burn your village, Sef? Think carefully. What were they looking for?"

Sef's chin trembled, just once, and stilled. "Old Marta said they'd come for whoever the beasts remembered." The child's voice dropped, went small. "She said the beasts remember everybody who ever spoke to them. All the way back. She said—" Sef swallowed. "She said I should run to the city and find the charm-seller, because the charm-seller would know what to do."

The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere overhead, pigeons shifted on the roof ridge, dozens of them, settling in for a long wait.

"Why would Old Marta send you to me?" Lior asked slowly. "Have we ever met?"

Sef looked up, and in the lamplight their too-old eyes held something that was not puzzlement this time. Something closer to pity.

"You don't remember," the child said. It wasn't a question either. "She said you wouldn't. She said that's how you'd know the price was paid."

Outside, above the shop, every pigeon on the roof ridge rose at once, a thunder of wings against the gray morning sky—and Lior, staring up through the floorboards, felt the dark room at the edge of their memory open one inch wider, and heard, from very far away, the sound of wings.
