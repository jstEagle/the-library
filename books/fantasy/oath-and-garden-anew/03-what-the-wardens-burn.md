## 3. What the Wardens Burn

Fire first.

Before the dogs, before the shouting, before the boots — fire. Sef knew it was coming the way the birds knew rain. The sparrows had gone quiet on the thatch three days ago, all of them facing south, all of them saying one word over and over in their small gray voices: *come, come, come.* Sef had told Ama. Ama had gone still the way she went still when she was listening to something far away, and then she had said, *then we go north tonight,* and they had not gone north, because Papa said the roads were watched and waiting was safer, and grown-ups always thought waiting was safer.

The fire came at the hour when the bread was proofing and the whole village smelled like warm wheat.

Sef was under the bed with the cat, because the cat had dragged them there by the sleeve of their nightshirt, claws gentle through the cloth, eyes huge and yellow. The cat's name was Sorrel and Sorrel was saying *hide hide hide* so fast it blurred into a single long sound, like a bee trapped under a bowl.

Through the gap between the floorboards Sef could see feet. Boots. So many boots. The boots came into the house without knocking, which no one did, which even strangers did not do, and the door hung off its hinge behind them like a broken wing.

*Where is the child,* said a voice above the boots. It was a woman's voice. It was not loud. That was the worst part. Everyone else was screaming or roaring or burning, and this voice was quiet as a hand laid flat on water.

*I don't know what you mean,* said Papa, and his voice shook, and Sef had never heard Papa's voice shake, not when the ox kicked him, not when the well froze, never.

Something heavy fell. The house jumped. Dust sifted down through the floorboards into Sef's open mouth, and it tasted like ash already, though the fire hadn't reached them yet. Sorrel pressed close, heart going like a bird's, and whispered: *still, still, still.*

* * *

Here is what Sef understood about the world, in the way children understand things — sideways, all at once, without the words to stack the pieces neatly:

Beasts talked. Not the way people pretended they didn't hear it. Really talked. The hens gossiped about foxes. The ox complained about his shoulders. The crows kept a list of every debt owed in the village and recited it at dusk. And Sef could answer them back, in the old language, the one that lived under words the way roots live under grass. Ama had taught them the first word when they were very small — *stay* — pressing Sef's hand flat against a horse's hot neck. *You were born holding it,* Ama said. *It's only borrowed through you. Be careful who hears you use it.*

And now someone had heard. Someone had heard from very far away, and sent boots.

The woman with the quiet voice walked through the village like she was reading it. She stopped at the well. She stopped at the goat pen. She knelt once, at the edge of the trampled garden, and put two fingers in the dirt, and Sef felt her doing it — felt her touch land on the earth the way a hand lands on your shoulder when you've been caught doing something wrong.

She was listening for them. Through the ground. Through the beasts. Everything alive in the village was a door, and she was opening each one and looking inside.

Sef made themselves small and silent as a stone. But stones are not silent to the things that crawl beneath them, and the worms came wriggling up away from the frost, and the worms said, in their blind soft way, *warm thing here, warm thing here,* and the woman's head turned toward the house like a compass needle finding north.

* * *

Ama found them before the woman did.

There was blood on Ama's face, a lot of it, more than Sef had ever seen on a person, and her left arm hung wrong, and she moved fast anyway, fast and low, the way the hare moves when the hawk's shadow crosses the field. She pulled Sef out from under the bed and held their face in both hands and her hands were shaking and her voice was not.

*Listen to me.* In the First Tongue, so no boot could follow it. *Listen. You run for the river. You get in the water and you stay in the water until you can't feel your toes, and then you keep going. Water hides you. Water has no memory. Do you hear me?*

*What about you,* Sef said. Their voice came out very small. Smaller than the worms'.

*Ama will come after. Ama promises.* And she crossed her bloody thumb over Sef's chest, the old way, the way you seal a bargain with something that can't lie. *But listen — the most important thing. If they catch you, if they corner you, you do not speak to save yourself. Whatever they do, whatever they show you — you swallow the Tongue. Swallow it whole. Because it isn't yours alone, little root. It belongs to everything that ever spoke it, and if they take it out of you, they take it out of the world.*

Outside, the goats were screaming. Goats scream like people. Sef would hear that sound for the rest of their life and only later understand why.

*Now go,* Ama said, and pushed them toward the loose board behind the flour chest, the one Sef used for hiding from chores, and the last thing Sef saw of her was her turning toward the door with her good arm raised, and the last thing Sef heard her say was not to Sef at all but to the house itself, to the mice in the walls and the moths in the rafters: *blind them. Blind them all.*

* * *

The river saved them. The river was cold enough to stop a heart, and Sef stayed in it anyway, wedged under the roots of an alder where the bank folded inward, teeth chattering the old words for *hidden hidden hidden* at the fish until the fish closed ranks around them like a wall of silver scales.

From the water, the village looked like a festival seen from far away. Orange light. Sparks climbing. The kind of thing you'd run toward if you didn't know better.

And the beasts came out of the burning.

That was the part Sef could not make sense of, then or ever. The dogs did not bark. The horses stood in the paddock with their heads down and did not run, though the fence was down and the gate was open and fire was eating the stable roof. The crows sat on the ridgepole of the smithy in a row, perfectly still, watching the soldiers with their heads tilted, and they were not afraid. They were *counting.*

And the woman in the gray coat walked among all of it untouched, and the fire bent away from her like grass in wind, and when she reached the well again she stopped, and looked straight at the alder roots across the water, straight at the place where Sef was, and Sef knew — knew with the whole skin of them — that she could not see them, but she could smell them, the way you smell rain coming. The way the beasts remembered.

*Come out,* she called across the river. Her voice carried impossibly, quietly, the way the First Tongue carried. *I'm not going to hurt you. I only want to talk.*

Liar, said the fish. Liar liar liar, said the crows, all together, all at once, and the sound of forty crows saying *liar* in unison was so terrible and so true that the woman flinched — actually flinched, one small step backward, her boot heel grinding in the mud — and Sef learned something in that moment that no grown-up had ever told them:

The beasts did not obey the Wardens.

The beasts *remembered* them.

* * *

They caught Ama at dawn.

Sef saw it from the ridge, half a mile off, wet to the bone and shivering so hard their jaw clicked, held back by Sorrel, who had found them somehow, who had crossed the burning village and the black fields to press against their side and say *no no no* — because Sef had been about to run down the hill, straight into the ring of gray coats, straight into the fire that wasn't burning anymore because there was nothing left to burn.

They made Ama kneel by the well. Her well. The well she'd drawn water from every morning of Sef's whole life, singing to it so it wouldn't silt up.

The woman in the gray coat stood over her. Up close, in the gray dawn light, Sef could see her face for the first time, and it was not a cruel face. That was the horror of it. It was a tired face, a scarred face, with a burn scar crawling up one side of her throat like ivy made of wax, and she looked down at Ama the way you look at a wound of your own.

*You should have given them up,* the woman said. *I'd have trained them gently. I was trained gently. Look how well it worked.*

*You have nothing left to train with,* Ama said, and her voice was strong, and Sef's heart broke along the exact seam of it, because Ama was talking to the woman the way you talk to someone you pity. *They burned it out of you and left the hunger. I can smell it from here.*

The woman's hand twitched — just the fingers, just once, like a dead limb remembering — and then she nodded to the soldiers, and they took Ama by the arms, and Ama did not struggle, because struggling was for people who expected to win.

Instead she lifted her head and looked directly at the ridge. Directly at Sef. Half a mile, gray light, smoke haze — impossible. But Ama had always been able to find Sef in any crowd, any dark, any dream, and she found them now.

She did not call out. She did not weep. She spoke in the First Tongue, one word, pitched to carry the way only speakers could pitch it, and the wind brought it up the hill and set it down inside Sef's chest like a coal:

*Remember.*

Then the wagons rolled, and the crows followed the wagons, all of them, the whole murder, flying low and slow, and Sef understood that too, finally, lying in the frost with Sorrel's heart hammering against their ribs: the crows were not following to mourn.

They were following to remember. Someone had to. That was the covenant nobody had written down — the beasts kept the ledger, and the ledger was never allowed to burn, no matter what the Wardens fed to the fire.

* * *

Sef walked north for nine days.

They ate what the land offered and the land offered generously, because Sef asked politely in the old language and the old language was older than ownership. Roots dug themselves up and lay waiting. A dead elk, fallen in a ravine, seemed almost relieved to be useful. On the fourth night a badger shared its sett without being asked, grumbling the whole time, complaining about Sef's cold feet, and Sef slept warmer than they had any right to.

On the ninth day the land ended in stone — a wall so tall the birds nesting in it looked like dust motes, a city wearing its walls like armor, and beyond the walls, chimneys breathing gray breath into a white sky.

Vellamark. Even the sparrows on the road spoke of it with their feathers flattened: *bad place, bad place, chains and bread and bad place.*

Sef stood at the treeline with their toes bleeding through split boots and their stomach a hollow drum and Ama's single word still glowing in their chest like a swallowed ember.

*Remember.*

But remember what? They were seven years old. They remembered fire, and boots, and goats screaming, and a woman with a scarred throat whose fingers twitched like something trying to grow back. They remembered a promise sealed with a bloody thumb: *Ama will come after.*

So that was the plan. Simple as a seed. Get inside the walls. Find Ama. Bring her home.

The city gates opened at dawn to let in the carts, and Sef slipped in among them, small and dirty and invisible, while the crows on the gatehouse watched them pass and said nothing — but turned, all together, all at once, to watch them go.

Inside Vellamark, somewhere in the tangle of streets and smoke and gray-faced people, there was a door with luck-knots hanging beside it. The sparrows knew. The sparrows had been gossiping about it for days: *the charm-seller, the charm-seller, the one who smells like the old times and doesn't know it.*

The sparrows led Sef there by morning.

By evening, the door would open.
