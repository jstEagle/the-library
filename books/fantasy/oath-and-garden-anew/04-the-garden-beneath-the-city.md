## 4. The Garden Beneath the City

The fence Maren kept shop in the drowned quarter, where the canals had silted to brown veins and the houses leaned together like conspirators sharing a secret. Lior left Sef asleep in the cellar with a bowl of barley porridge and a charm of quiet dreams knotted into the child's collar—harmless work, the kind of charm that had kept them alive for ten years—and went out into a morning the color of dishwater.

Vellamark did not wake so much as it seethed. Fishwives argued over eel prices. A dray horse coughed its way up the Tanners' Ramp. Everything ordinary, everything gray, and Lior moved through it the way they'd taught themselves to move through all things: unremarkable, unhurried, a charm-seller with ink-stained fingers and nothing worth a Warden's glance.

But the city was wrong this morning. Lior felt it before they could name it.

The pigeons on the cornice above the fish market were not pecking. They stood in a row, still as carved saints, every head turned toward the same point downriver. The gulls that usually screamed over the fish stalls were silent. And when Lior passed the tannery yard where three old mastiffs dozed behind a fence, all three lifted their heads at once—not barking, not growling, just *watching*, their eyes tracking Lior the way water finds a crack.

*They know,* something whispered in the oldest room of Lior's mind, the room they had locked and bricked over a decade ago. *They remember you.*

Lior pulled their hood lower and walked faster.

Maren's door was behind a chandler's shop, down an alley that smelled of tallow and old rain, marked by nothing but a brass knocker shaped like a coiled adder. Once—ten years and more ago—that door had opened onto a trade in things the crown called contraband: speaker-talismans, beast-bone flutes, pages torn from the First Tongue's grammar written in ink mixed with blood so the words would hold. Lior had sold Maren exactly one thing, once, and then buried the past and never come back.

They knocked anyway.

A long silence. Then a voice like gravel settling: "Who knocks?"

"A customer for luck-knots."

"Wrong door."

"Maren." Lior kept their voice low and level. "It's Zhou. From the market. I need—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "I need the kind of luck that isn't sold in knots."

Another silence, longer. Then bolts slid back, one, two, three, and the door cracked open on a face like a dried apple gone soft in cider—wrinkled, yellowed, but the eyes still sharp as awls. Maren looked Lior up and down, and something in that gaze made Lior feel ten years younger and infinitely more foolish.

"Well," Maren said. "The dead knock after all. Get inside before the walls learn your name."

* * *

The room beyond the door was smaller than Lior remembered, and fuller. Shelves climbed every wall, sagging under jars, bundles, boxes sealed with wax and prayer. A single lamp burned low. Maren poured two cups of something dark and bitter from a dented pot and pushed one across the table without asking.

"You look terrible," Maren said.

"I've been careful for ten years. It ages a person."

"Careful." Maren snorted. "I heard about the market. Don't make that face—the whole gray quarter heard about the market. A child falls down at the charm-seller's stall speaking gibberish, and every rat within three streets comes running like it's been summoned to court." Maren's eyes narrowed. "Gibberish that wasn't gibberish. I'd know. I used to sell the real thing."

Lior wrapped both hands around the cup and didn't drink. "How bad?"

"How bad?" Maren laughed, and there was no humor in it, only rust. "Bad enough that the Wardens posted a notice at the Salt Gate yesterday. Not the usual 'report unlawful charms, earn bread.' This one said: report *beast-sign*. Anyone seen talking to animals. Anyone animals gather to. Anyone the beasts *follow*." Maren leaned forward, lamplight pooling in the creases of their face. "You know what beast-sign is, Zhou? Of course you do. You're wearing it like a second skin and pretending it's a coat."

"The child doesn't control it," Lior said quietly. "Sef doesn't even understand what's happening. The gift just... pours out."

"Then the gift is a lantern in a blackout, and the Wardens are moths with knives." Maren sat back. "There's more. You'll want to sit for it, though you're already sitting, so just listen. My cousin works the stables at the Warden barracks—don't ask how, the answer is bribery—and she says they've got a new method. They're not searching house to house anymore. They're walking the streets with caged birds. Starlings, mostly. Cheap, common, everywhere." Maren's voice dropped. "When the starlings go quiet near someone, they mark the door. Three doors marked on Tanner's Row last week. Two on Coilmaker's Lane. The families are gone. Not arrested—*gone*, no wagon, no notice, no trial. Just empty rooms and neighbors who've suddenly forgotten how to speak."

Lior's stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking on water. "Because the birds go quiet near speakers."

"Near anyone the birds *remember*." Maren fixed them with those awl-sharp eyes. "That's the part nobody understands who hasn't handled the old trade. The Wardens think they're hunting magic. They're not. Magic they can burn; magic burns pretty and makes good theater for the mob. What they're hunting is memory. The beasts carry it—all of it, every speaker who ever lived, every word ever spoken in the First Tongue, stored away in crow-mind and wolf-dream going back further than the throne itself. A speaker isn't a person with a talent, Zhou. A speaker is a key that fits a lock the crown has spent two hundred years pretending doesn't exist."

"And if the lock opens?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. Outside, faintly, a dog began to howl—then stopped, abruptly, as if corrected.

"Then the debt gets called," Maren said. "And no one alive knows the full size of it. Including me. *Especially* me—I only ever sold the edges of it." They drained their cup and set it down with a click. "Now. Why are you really here? Charm-sellers don't cross the canal for conversation."

Lior hesitated. Ten years of discipline said *lie, deflect, leave*. But the starlings were already going quiet on the rooftops above their own street; he'd watched them do it that morning, a slow cold tide of silence following him to the bridge. The hiding was already failing. The only question left was what shape the ending took.

"There's a place," Lior said. "Outside the walls. The old garden-ruins, past the leper fields, where the conservatory used to be. My grandmother told me stories—said the speakers' order kept a sanctuary there, before the purges. That some of it survived underground. Greenhouses grown over, cellars, paths only the roots remember." Lior met Maren's eyes. "I need a way in. And I need it to be a way the Wardens don't know."

Maren stared at them for a long time. Then the old fence laughed again, softer this time, almost fond.

"You always were the one who came back for the dangerous errands." They rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and shuffled to the back of the shop, hauling aside a shelf of wax-sealed jars to reveal a stone in the wall that wasn't quite a stone. From the cavity they drew a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the table: a map, hand-inked on hide, so old the vellum had gone the color of tea.

"Not my work. Older. Came to me with a dead man's debts thirty years back, and I've been too afraid to sell it ever since." Maren's finger traced a line from the city's western wall, out past the marked fields, into a sprawl of drawn trees and broken geometry. "The conservatory. Or what's left of it. See here—the gate is gone, but the *walls* aren't. Thorn hedge, grown wild since before your grandmother's grandmother. There's a way through on the north side, where the greenhouse collapsed into the hill. And listen to me now, because this is the part I've said to no one in thirty years."

Maren looked up, and for a moment the fence fell away and Lior saw the person underneath: young once, in a Vellamark where such a shop could exist openly, holding pages that smelled of blood.

"The beasts remember everyone who ever spoke. Everyone. Not just the great ones, not just the war-leaders. If your grandmother told you stories—if she ever hummed to the garden birds in the old words without knowing it—if a single drop of the Tongue passed through your family's blood, then the beasts out there already know your name, child. Walking into that garden isn't walking into hiding. It's walking into a room full of creditors who've been waiting two hundred years for you to show your face."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere above them, muffled by floors and plaster, a bird went silent.

"That's not a warning against going," Lior said slowly. "You wouldn't have given me the map."

"No," Maren agreed. "It's a warning about the price of arriving. There's a difference, and clever people die confusing the two." They folded the map, pressed it into Lior's hands, and closed Lior's fingers over it with both of theirs—dry, strong, trembling slightly. "Take the child. Take the north path. And whatever greets you in that garden—whatever *remembers* you—do not lie to it. Beasts can't be charmed, Zhou. I sold charms my whole life, and I never once found one that worked on the things that keep the world's accounts."

Lior stood. The map was warm from Maren's hands, or perhaps from something older. At the door, they paused.

"Maren. Why help? After all these years?"

The old fence smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle thing.

"Because someone has to pay the debts eventually, and I'm too old to run." They turned back to their shelves. "Go. And don't come back here afterward. Whatever happens next, this door stays shut."

* * *

Lior took the long way home, along the canal, and the wrongness followed them the whole way.

The starlings had come out. They lined the telegraph-worn wires above the water in their hundreds, and as Lior walked beneath them, section after section fell silent—a moving pocket of hush, a shadow made of absence, tracking them down the bank. Passersby noticed. Lior saw a fish-porter frown up at the wires, see the strange quiet, and then look down—at Lior, at the hooded figure walking too fast—and Lior read the beginning of arithmetic in the man's face.

Three streets from home, a Warden patrol stood at the corner of Coilmaker's Lane, and beside them, on a perch like a small gallows, sat a caged starling. Silent. Its head turned as Lior approached, smooth as oil, and its black eye fixed on them with an attention no bird should own.

Lior stopped. Turned casually toward a shuttered window as if checking their reflection, heart slamming, and let the patrol see only a charm-seller pausing to fix a hood. One of the Wardens—a young one, bored, hand resting on his baton—glanced over. Held. Looked at the starling.

The starling did not scream. It did not flutter. It simply kept watching, and in its silence Lior heard Maren's voice: *the beasts remember everyone who ever spoke.*

*Not me,* Lior thought, with a desperation that felt like prayer. *Not me. It's the child. It was always the child.*

But the bird's eye did not waver, and somewhere beneath the brick and mortar of Lior's buried life, behind the wall they'd built a decade ago, something old stirred in its sleep and turned over, listening.

The Warden lost interest. The patrol moved on. Lior stood in the gray light until their hands stopped shaking, then walked home the last three streets as fast as dignity allowed, the map burning against their ribs like a coal.

Inside, down in the cellar, Sef was awake. The child sat cross-legged on the dirt floor in a ring of rats—not trapped, not frightened, but *conversing*, head tilted, making small sounds in that language older than walls while the rats sat entranced as though hearing scripture.

Sef looked up, smiling, gap-toothed and radiant.

"They knew you were coming," the child said. "They said the garden is waiting. They said it's been waiting a very long time—for *both* of us."

Lior stood at the top of the stairs with the map in their hands, and understood that the choice had already been made for them. There was no staying in Vellamark. There was only how fast they ran.

"We leave tonight," Lior said.
