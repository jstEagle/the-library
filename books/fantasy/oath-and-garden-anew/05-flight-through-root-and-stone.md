## 5. Flight Through Root and Stone

The underways began behind Maren's cellar, down a stair that smelled of wet stone and old tallow, and Lior went first with a lantern held low so its light would not spill up the steps like a shout.

"Stay close," they whispered. "Touch nothing. Speak nothing."

Sef nodded, solemn as a small monk, and pressed one bare foot into Lior's bootprint and then the next, the way children do when they have decided to trust you completely. It was the completeness of it that frightened Lior most. Trust was a debt. Lior had spent ten years learning to owe no one anything.

Maren's map was inked on a scrap of oilcloth, soft as cloth from handling. *Follow the water sound. Third fork, take the dry throat. Do not wake what sleeps in the flooded gallery.* The last line had been underlined twice, and beneath it, in a different, shakier hand: *or feed it.*

The tunnels of Vellamark were older than Vellamark. That was the thing the city never said aloud—that its gray respectable streets were laid over the bones of something else, aqueducts and cisterns and processional ways from an age when people had still built for beauty instead of fear. Lior's lantern found carvings in the walls as they walked: vines, wings, long-necked beasts drinking from stone basins. Someone had chiseled the wings off every one of them. The marks were old and even, done by patient hands.

"They cut them," Sef said softly, at Lior's shoulder. Their voice in the dark was very loud. "Why did they cut the wings?"

"Because they were afraid of them."

"Were the wings bad?"

"No." Lior kept walking. "That's not why people are afraid."

* * *

Sef counted their footsteps because the woman with the kind hands and the hard voice had told them to stay close, and counting was how you stayed close when you couldn't hold someone's coat. Four hundred and eleven. Four hundred and twelve. The tunnel smelled like the inside of the earth, like rain that had never fallen yet.

They passed a door of rusted iron and Sef felt it before they saw it—a warmth, a breathing, a great slow attention on the other side of the metal. They stopped. The woman's boots went on three more steps and then stopped too.

"Sef."

"There's something in there," Sef whispered. Something enormous and patient, curled around its own loneliness the way Sef curled around theirs. "It's sad. It's been sad for a really long time."

"Don't look at it. Don't listen to it. Keep walking."

But you couldn't just *not* listen. That was like being told not to hear your own name. The sadness behind the door had a shape, and the shape had a sound, and the sound was almost words—old words, deep words, the words that lived in Sef's chest before Sef knew any other language, the words the burning lady had breathed into their ear with her last breath: *run until you find the speaker, tell them the garden remembers.*

"It wants—" Sef began.

"*Walk.*"

Sef walked. But the sadness followed them through the iron, a thread of attention thin as spider silk, and Sef carried it like a splinter, all the way down the dry throat of the third fork, all the way into the wide dark place where the ceiling opened up like the inside of a swallowed cathedral.

* * *

Here the lantern light died too soon. The flooded gallery, Maren's map had called it, and Lior could hear the water—black and still somewhere below the path, a causeway of collapsed masonry running along the wall. The air was cold enough to see their breath. On the far side of the black water, the darkness had texture. Weight. Lior told themselves it was only shadow, only stone, only the way caves make animals of everyone's imagination.

Then the lantern guttered—not wind, there was no wind—and in the space of that flicker Lior heard, distinctly, a breath that was not theirs and not Sef's. Long. Wet. Drawn through nostrils the size of cupped hands.

Lior did the arithmetic of terror quickly: distance to the far arch, maybe two hundred paces; weight of the child; whether they could run it carrying Sef or whether they would both go into the water. Behind them, back toward the rusted door, was longer. In front was shorter. Everything in Lior's life had come down to choosing between lengths of dark.

They were fifty paces across the causeway when Sef stopped.

"Sef." Lior's whisper cracked. "What did I say about stopping?"

"He's crying," Sef said. Not whispering. The child's voice rang off the vaulted dark like a dropped bell. "Can't you hear him? He's been crying since before the city. Since before the cutting. He's so *lonely*, and nobody ever comes down here except the ones with knives—"

"*Quiet.*" Lior grabbed the child's shoulder, and Sef flinched, and in the flinch the words came out anyway—the way blood comes out of a wound you've pressed your hand over. First Tongue, rising out of the child like water finding a crack. Low, resonant, wrong for a human throat, older than the walls above them and the walls around them and every wall ever built to keep the wild world out.

The black water answered.

It did not part so much as *rise*—a hill of it lifting against the far wall, shedding darkness like a cloak, and the lantern showed Lior pieces of what stood up out of the flooded gallery: a shoulder scaled like riverstone, an eye pale as moonlit ice, vast and mild and ancient, turning toward the small bright voice with the unmistakable hunger of a creature that has not been spoken to kindly in five hundred years.

"Oh," Sef said, delighted, radiant, reaching out both arms. "*Oh, hello.*"

* * *

Later—much later, when Lior had time to be ashamed—they would understand that the beast had never meant harm. That it had bent its head down to the child the way a dog bends to a friend, that the rumble in its chest was greeting and not threat. But in the moment, in the lantern light, with a leviathan's face descending toward them out of the dark, Lior did what ten years of hiding had trained into their bones.

They threw the charm.

It was a deception-knot, one of the harmless ones, woven to turn a glance aside, to make a Warden's eye slide off a stall of forbidden goods. Lior had never used one on a person. They had certainly never thrown one at a creature of the old pact—but panic does not read fine print. The charm left their fingers trailing green sparks and burst against the beast's brow like a soap bubble, and the great eye blinked—

—and the beast *screamed.*

Not in pain. Lior understood that even then, even mid-fall backward with Sef clutched to their chest. It screamed the way a person screams when a stranger strikes them in the middle of an embrace. The sound filled the gallery, filled the tunnels, rolled away down every passage of the underways at once, and Lior knew—with the sick certainty of a charm-seller who knows exactly which knot has slipped—that every listening thing in the dark now knew precisely where they were.

And things in Vellamark's underways listened. Things with many legs. Things with Wardens' boots.

"Run," Lior gasped, hauling Sef up. "Run *now*—"

"But he was only saying hello—"

"The whole city heard him say hello! Move!"

They ran. The causeway crumbled behind them; the beast wailed once more, lower, wounded, and sank back into its black water, and Sef looked back over Lior's arm with tears streaming down their face, and Lior could not tell if the child was crying for themselves or for the monster, and suspected—horribly—that it was the monster.

* * *

They came up through a grate into a cistern chamber where the air moved, and Lior slammed the grate down and put their back against the wall and shook. Sef sat in the middle of the damp floor, knees drawn up, watching Lior with an expression no child should have to wear: the careful watchfulness of someone deciding whether a person is safe.

"You're angry," Sef said.

"Yes."

"At me."

Lior wanted to say no. The lie assembled itself readily—Lior was good at lies, that was the whole rotten architecture of their life—but the child's eyes were pale and steady and waiting, and something in Lior, some muscle long unused, refused to lift the weight.

"Yes," Lior said. "And afraid. And angry at myself, and angry at the Wardens, and angry at everyone who ever made you think you had to hide what you are. All of it at once. That's what anger is, sometimes. A crowd."

Sef considered this with terrible seriousness. "He wasn't bad," the child said finally. "The water one. He remembered songs. Old ones. He sang one to me, inside my head, and it was about a garden with no fences, and everyone in it was full of teeth but nobody bit anybody." Sef's chin trembled. "You hurt his feelings. You threw your spark-thing at him when he was just saying hello. Why do grown-ups always hurt the feelings of things that say hello?"

"Because the last time things said hello to people like us," Lior said, and their voice came out strange, scraped, "the people who answered were burned. All of them. Every speaker in the old order, and the beasts went to war over it, and the war ended with a bargain I don't fully remember and you shouldn't know about, and the crown has spent three hundred years making sure no one can call that debt again." They heard themselves, heard the decade cracking open, words pouring out that they had sworn never to say. "Because a child speaking the First Tongue in a tunnel is not a hello, Sef. It is a bell tolling. And Ashk's Wardens are already on the stairs."

"How do you know that word?" Sef whispered. "Ashk. I never told you her name."

Lior froze.

"I never told you," Sef repeated, standing slowly, backing toward the far wall of the cistern, water dripping somewhere in the dark behind them. "I never told anyone her name. She took the speaker-lady away and I promised the lady I'd forget her name so they couldn't pull it out of me." The child's eyes had gone wide and glassy, reflecting lantern light that was beginning, faintly, to flicker gold. "So how is your mouth full of her name?"

Around them, in the dark of the cistern, something with feathers began to sing. Then something with fur. Then something with neither, answering from the direction of the garden-ruins, from beyond the walls, from everywhere at once—the way a choir answers when one voice finds the key.

Sef hadn't called them. Lior could feel that much, could feel the child's gift straining and terrified and *leaking*, a breached dam, a language spilling out of a body too small to hold it.

The beasts were simply coming. To the bell. To the child. To the last living vessel of everything the throne had drowned.

Lior snuffed the lantern, took Sef's hand, and ran for the ruin-gate Maren had marked on the oilcloth, while the dark above Vellamark filled, wing by wing by wing, with everything that remembered.
