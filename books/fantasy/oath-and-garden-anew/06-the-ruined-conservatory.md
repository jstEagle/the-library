## 6. The Ruined Conservatory

The garden-ruins did not look like ruins at first. They looked like a country that had decided, long ago, to stop pretending it was anything but a garden.

Lior had expected broken walls, blackened beams, the usual grammar of the crown's destruction. Instead they found a valley folded into the hills beyond Vellamark's last watchtower, where glass roofs lay shattered into fields of green light and the old conservatory walls were so furred with ivy that stone and plant had become indistinguishable. Roses grew out of gutters. A fig tree had split a marble bench in half and then, with what seemed like apology, grown a second trunk to cradle the pieces. The air was thick enough to drink—wet loam, overripe fruit, the green smell of things growing faster than they should.

Sef walked ahead with their arms slightly out from their body, palms open, the way they always moved through green places. The birds did not startle when the child passed. That was the thing Lior could not stop noticing: nothing here startled. A heron stood in a dry fountain and watched them come with one yellow eye, and did not fly, and Lior felt its attention settle on them like a hand on the back of the neck.

*It knows me,* Lior thought, and hated how much of them wanted that to be true.

They had walked two days since the underways. Two days since the tunnel-beast had risen out of the dark at Sef's frightened call—a thing like an eel the length of a cart, blind and gentle, which had carried them past the Warden checkpoint in its slow current of displaced water before dissolving back into the deep channels. Two days since Lior had screamed at Sef until the child cried, and then sat all night in the dark listening to the small wet sound of that crying and understanding, finally, that there would be no more screaming. You could not scold a bell for ringing. You could only decide what you were willing to have heard.

"Flowers," Sef said now, crouching over a bed of white blooms Lior didn't recognize. The child said it in trade-speech, but their mouth kept trying for something older underneath, a second word humming behind the first like a bee in a wall. "Lior. Flowers that remember."

"Don't say things like that."

"They said it." Sef looked up, unbothered, dirt on their chin. "Not me."

Beyond the roses, the path climbed between hedges gone feral and enormous, and then opened—and Lior stopped walking.

A conservatory stood at the head of the valley, or the skeleton of one: a cathedral of iron ribs and surviving panes, half-swallowed by wisteria, its great doors hanging open like a mouth mid-word. And before it, filling the whole forecourt, were graves.

Hundreds of them. Small ones, mostly. Marked not with stones but with living trees—each grave crowned by a sapling, some ancient and vast, some no taller than Sef's knee. Row upon row of them descending the slope in careful lines, an orchard of the dead, every tree a different species, every tree tended. The grass around each was cut. The roots were mulched. Someone had been doing this work for a very long time.

An old man knelt among the smallest graves, his back to them, turning soil with a hand trowel worn down to a sliver. He was singing—not trade-speech, not quite the First Tongue either, but something between, a gardener's pidgin of both, and as he sang, the weeds around the little grave leaned away from his hands like animals making room.

Sef made a sound Lior had never heard from them before. Small. Wounded. *Recognizing.*

The old man turned. His face was brown and seamed as bark, his eyes the pale washed-out blue of faded cloth, and when he saw Sef he dropped the trowel.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, *oh.* It came anyway. After everything, it came." He tried to stand and failed, tried again, and came toward them down the row of graves with his arms out, weeping without any shame at all. "I told them the debt would find a vessel. I told them. They said the Tongue was dead, they said—" He stopped in front of Sef, swaying, and lowered himself slowly to his knees so that his eyes were level with the child's. "Hello, little vessel. Hello. What do they call you?"

"Sef," Sef whispered.

"Sef." The old man closed his eyes as if tasting it. "Ovid," he said. "I was the last speaker of the Southern Order. Now I am the man who buries speakers. There have only been four of us to bury, these forty years, so I have had time to get good at other things." He gestured at the orchard of trees without looking at it, the way you gesture at a wound that has become part of you. "Come inside. Both of you. The night comes early in this valley, and there are things you must hear while you can still choose to disbelieve them."

* * *

Inside, the conservatory was a world. Rain-light fell through the surviving panes in long silver columns, and beneath them grew everything the old order had saved: fruit trees in tubs, herbs gone leggy and strange, a fig with a trunk like a braided rope, ferns unfurling in the dark corners with the slow confidence of things that had never once been interrupted. Ovid moved through it all touching leaves as he went, the way another man might touch the shoulders of children.

He fed them—bread hard as roof slate, honey, stewed apples from a tree that Lior suspected had ripened months out of season—and while Sef ate, Ovid studied the child with an expression Lior recognized because he had spent ten years practicing its opposite: grief and wonder occupying the same face without fighting.

"You hid it," Ovid said to Lior. Not accusing. Measuring. "The gift. How long?"

"Ten years."

"And how?"

"Charms. Luck-knots, finding-spells, weather-blessings. Small magics. Harmless magic." Lior wrapped their hands around the cup of apple tea they hadn't asked for. "Magic nobody burns you for."

"Mm." Ovid settled onto a stool amid his ferns, joints cracking like green wood in fire. "And did it work? Did the silence hold?"

"It held."

"Did it?" The old eyes lifted, pale and terrible in their gentleness. "Then why did the beasts of this valley know your name before your feet crossed my gate? Why did the heron watch you walk in like a kinsman? Child, the gift doesn't die when you starve it. It goes quiet and it *waits*, and the beasts wait with it, because they remember everyone who ever spoke to them. Every voice is written somewhere in that long dark memory. Yours is in there. Ten years of silence hasn't scratched it out. It has only made the beasts curious about why."

Lior set down the cup because their hands had begun to shake. "What do you want from us?"

"What I wanted from no one," Ovid said, "and now must want from you. The truth. Sit. Listen. This is the inheritance, and I would rather give it to you sitting than have it fall on you standing."

* * *

(Sef, later, alone among the ferns while the adults spoke low, would remember this hour as a warmth rather than words—the old man's voice going on and on like rain, and the plants leaning in to listen too, and Sef understanding none of it and all of it, the way you understand a lullaby.)

* * *

"The war," Ovid began, "was not won. Everyone in Vellamark learns it was won, because crowns write the lessons. But wars between men and beasts are not won; they are ended, the way fevers end, or griefs. In the last years of it, the beasts had learned our language better than we had learned their silence. They could unmake a legion. They had already unmade three. And then, at the field called Harrowmere, they stopped."

He rose, slowly, and led them deeper into the conservatory, to a wall where the plaster had fallen away from old brick, and on the brick someone had carved a frieze: ranks of beasts on one side, ranks of speakers on the other, and between them a table—or an altar—or perhaps simply ground where both knelt.

"The First Speakers offered terms," Ovid said. "Not victory. Terms. The beasts would end the war, withdraw beyond the walls' reach, forget their grievances—but the grievance lived in language, you see, and language lives in people. So the price was this: the speakers would surrender their memories of the war. All of them. Every speaker who had ever commanded a beast would give up the years of blood and burning, would carry the peace forward *without knowing what it cost*, so that no one could ever rebuild the grievance, because no one could remember it."

Lior stared at the carved figures kneeling. "That's not a covenant. That's an amputation."

"It was mercy," Ovid said sharply, and then, softer: "It was both. Most true things are both. The beasts took the memories as payment—they hold them still, all of them, in that collective dark. Which means the war is not forgotten, Lior Zhou. It is *archived*. Kept safe in ten thousand minds that never sleep. And the throne knows it." He tapped the frieze, on the small kneeling human figures. "Why do you think the Wardens burn speakers? Not fear of power. A speaker can be killed. But a vessel—" his finger moved to Sef, who had gone very still, apple spoon suspended—"a vessel of the First Tongue is a key to the archive. A vessel can call the debt. Can ask the beasts to return what they were paid. And if the memories come back—if the grievance comes back—"

"The war comes back," Lior said.

"The war comes back." Ovid lowered himself onto a bench beneath the fig tree, suddenly looking every one of his years. "The old order believed the debt should sleep forever. We buried our own Tongue, generation by generation, and I buried the last of us—" he gestured vaguely toward the orchard outside, toward the small graves, and his voice caught and steadied—"and we thought we had succeeded. And then this child walks out of the fire carrying the whole language in their mouth like a coal that wouldn't cool."

Sef put the spoon down. "Is the coal bad?" the child asked. Very quietly. "Am I bad?"

Ovid's face did something complicated and then simply broke open, and he reached out and gathered the child against his threadbare coat. "No, little vessel. No. You are a door. Doors are not bad. But doors must be guarded, and the ones who should have guarded yours are ash in a village you will not remember kindly, and so it falls to strangers." Over Sef's head, his pale eyes found Lior's. "It falls to you."

"I don't want it," Lior said. "I spent ten years making sure I didn't want it."

"Yes," Ovid said. "And the beasts spent those same ten years wondering why. You cannot refuse an inheritance, child. You can only choose whether to receive it on your feet or have it land on you." He released Sef and stood, and his voice changed—flattened, hardened, the voice of a man reciting a ledger. "So hear the terms as they were given to me, and as I will give them to no one else. If the vessel's gift wakes fully—if the debt is called—there is a price. There is always a price. The beasts do not take promises; they take *payment*. Memory. Years. Blood. Whatever the caller values most, the beasts will weigh, and take their portion of, and leave the caller to count what remains."

Lior thought of the cellar in Vellamark. Of the luck-knots. Of ten gray years, folded small and hidden, that had somehow been the happiest shape their life could make.

"And if no one calls the debt?" they asked. "If we just—hide again. Deeper this time. Beyond the walls, in the wild places. If the vessel never speaks—"

Ovid was shaking his head before Lior finished. "The gift doesn't sleep, I told you. It waits. And the Wardens are already following beast-sign through the wilds—you said so yourself. They will find beast-sign here, too, soon enough. This valley is the loudest quiet place in the world." He looked out through the fogged panes toward the orchard of graves, and the light was failing behind the hills, and for a moment the old man looked less like a gardener than like a sentry at a post long abandoned, still holding it. "You have time. Days, perhaps. Use them to decide what you are willing to pay."

* * *

That night Lior slept in the fern-house on a pallet that smelled of mint and rot, and woke twice: once to see Sef asleep in the crook of Ovid's arm, the old man awake above them, keeping watch over the door as though he had been keeping that exact watch for forty years and had only just remembered why.

And the second time, near dawn, to a sound outside—wings, many wings, settling softly on the conservatory roof, one after another after another, patient as snowfall.

Through the fogged glass, against the graying sky, Lior counted the silhouettes and stopped counting at thirty.

They were not attacking. They were waiting.

And in the morning, when Lior told Ovid, the old man only nodded, unsurprised, and said the thing that turned Lior's blood to winter water:

"They've smelled the vessel. Word travels fast in the dark places. And word is coming from the city too, little charm-seller—faster than wings. The Wardens have a beast on a chain, and it remembers *you*."
