## 7. Ashk at the Gate

The beast they had given her was dying.

Ashk could feel it in the way the rope went slack between her gauntlet and the creature's throat—a marsh-hound, gray as river fog, its ribs showing through wet fur like the staves of a broken barrel. It had been magnificent once. The Wardens had taken it from a fisher-village on the Sedge, muzzled it, chained it to a cart, and starved it into obedience, and now obedience was all that remained of it, and even that was thinning.

"Water," she said, and Corporal Vess brought the skin without being asked twice. Ashk crouched and held it to the hound's muzzle herself. Around her, the company pretended not to watch. They knew better than to look too long at their captain kneeling in the mud with her hands on an animal's face. There were stories about that. There were always stories about captains.

"Drink," she murmured. "We're nearly there."

The hound drank. Its eyes—pale, rheumy, old—found hers and held them, and for one unguarded moment Ashk felt the thing she had not felt in twenty years: a pressure against the inside of her skull, soft as a moth against glass. *Thank you,* said something that was not a word. *I know what you are.*

Then nothing. The moth beat itself against the glass and slid away, and Ashk stood, wiping her hands on her cloak as if they were dirty, because in a way they were.

*You know what I am,* she thought bitterly. *A door bricked over. A hearth swept cold.*

She gave the order to move, and the company moved, forty men and women in Warden gray winding up the old drover's road toward the hills above Vellamark, where the maps showed nothing but ruin and scrub and, if you believed the oldest surveys, a conservatory that had been dead longer than the dynasty.

The hound did not believe the maps were wrong. It pulled toward those hills like a fish on a line, nose down, breath sawing, following a scent only it could parse—the trail of a gift used and used again, sweet to beasts the way blood is sweet to their noses. Someone out there was speaking. Someone out there had been speaking for days, carelessly, joyfully, the way children do things, without knowing that every word in the First Tongue rang like a bell through every ear that still remembered how to listen.

Ashk remembered how to listen. That was the cruelty of it. They had burned the gift out of her when she was nineteen—burned it properly, with iron and salt and the litany of severance, in a cellar beneath the Wardenhall while her mother signed the consent papers because the alternative was the whole family going on the speaker-lists—and the burning had worked. She could not command so much as a sparrow now. But the place where the gift had been did not close over. It stayed open, the way a pulled tooth leaves a socket, and cold air moved through it constantly, and some nights she woke with her jaw aching and her ears ringing with a silence that had texture, that had weight, that leaned on her like snow on a roof.

They told recruits the speakers were dangerous. That was true. They told recruits magic was outlawed because power must belong to the crown alone. That was also true, and also a lie, the way official truths are lies wearing uniforms.

The real reason hung in the Wardenhall's deepest archive, three floors down behind a door Ashk had opened exactly once, on the night of her promotion, when Captain-General Ilvane had put a lamp in her hand and said, "Read it. Then decide whether you want the rank."

She had read it.

It was a treaty. Treaties are dull things; this one was not. It was written in two columns—one in dynastic script, one in the First Tongue—and it recorded, in language of terrible plainness, what the beasts had been promised in exchange for ending the war that had nearly eaten the world: that no speaker would ever be raised against them again; that the pact would sleep; and that the crown would keep, forever, the one secret that made the peace possible.

The founding crime. The thing the first king had done to make the beasts stop fighting—not defeated them, not bargained with them honestly, but *tricked* them, using a speaker of his own blood as bait, and the beasts had learned it too late, and their grief had been so vast that they chose silence over vengeance, and the silence had held for three hundred years because everyone who might have told them the truth had been found and burned.

Every speaker burned was not justice. It was housekeeping. It was making sure the letter never reached its addressees.

And now a child was shouting it from the rooftops of the wild gardens, or would be soon enough, and the bells of the old pact were ringing in every ear from here to the coast, and Ashk had been given forty soldiers and a starving hound and orders written in Ilvane's own hand: *No witnesses. No prisoners above the age of ten. Burn the ground after.*

She had read the orders twice and then folded them into the inner pocket of her coat, over her heart, where she kept everything she intended someday to use as evidence.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the shell of a drover's inn, its roof long fallen, its hearth still standing like a monument to someone's ordinary life. Ashk walked the perimeter out of habit, checking pickets, checking sightlines, and ended—as she always ended—at the cart where the hound lay chained.

Vess was there already, sitting on his heels just outside the chain's reach, sharing his ration salted meat with the animal in small forbidden pieces. He was twenty-two and had joined the Wardens because the pay fed his sisters, and he had never yet been required to burn anything living, and Ashk knew the day was coming when he would be, and she watched him feed the hound and felt something in her chest turn over like a stone in a streambed.

"She likes you," Ashk said.

Vess scrambled up, guilty. "Captain. I didn't—"

"At ease." She crouched beside him, keeping her voice low, pitched under the wind. "Tell me what she does when you feed her."

He frowned, wanting to answer well. "Her tail moves. Slowly. And she... watches me. Not my hands. Me."

"Not your hands," Ashk repeated. "Remember that. Beasts don't watch hands unless they've been taught to expect them. Hands are what hold irons." She looked at the hound, and the hound looked back, and the cold socket where her gift had been ached the way it always ached near animals, near the sea, near the archive door. "When we find who we're looking for, there may be a moment when things go quickly. When they do, you'll be near me. That's an order."

"Yes, Captain." He didn't understand. He wouldn't, until he did, and by then either it would matter or it wouldn't.

She left him there and climbed to the inn's broken upper floor, where she could see the hills black against the last gray light, and took out Ilvane's orders and read them a third time by the moon, though she knew them by heart.

*No witnesses.* The child was, by every report, perhaps eight years old.

*Burn the ground after.* Because ash held no memory. Because fire was the dynasty's oldest editor.

Twenty years ago, lying fevered in the severance cellar, Ashk had dreamed of the war. Everyone who underwent the burning did; the litany scraped the channel clean but left the walls thin, and things leaked through. In her dream she had stood on a field of bones that went to the horizon, and the bones were not men's—they were too large, curved like ship-ribs—and the sky had been full of wings, and somewhere a voice had spoken in the First Tongue, and every bone on that field had turned toward the sound like flowers toward sun.

She had woken screaming the words. She hadn't known what they meant. The interrogators had noted it in her file: *subject retains passive comprehension; recommend monitoring.* Monitored for twenty years, then. Promoted for twenty years. Given the dirtiest work for twenty years, because a broken speaker makes the best hunter of speakers—you cannot track what you cannot half-hear.

And now the half-hearing had led her up a drover's road to a dead garden, and tomorrow she would breach it, and either she would do what the crown required or she would do what the treaty required, and the treaty, she reminded herself, was the older document. The older debt. Debts to beasts were not like debts to men. Men forgave. Beasts *remembered*, and their remembering had a body count measured in kingdoms.

From the dark of the hills came a sound—faint, threaded through wind, almost deniable. Not a bird. Not a wolf. Something between a horn and a grief, rising and falling, and then answered from another quarter, and then another, a slow conversation passing over the ridgelines the way beacon-fires had passed in the old wars.

At the cart below, the hound lifted its head and howled back before Vess could clamp a hand over its muzzle.

Ashk stood very still on the broken floor, listening to the hills talk to each other about her, about all of them, and understood with perfect clarity that the conversation had started days ago—that they had known she was coming since the moment the child first spoke aloud—and that whatever waited in the ruined conservatory was not hiding.

It was waiting to be found.

She folded the orders away, over her heart, and went down to give the watch-word, and to tell Vess to double the pickets, and to lie awake under the talking sky counting the hours until dawn, when she would walk through the gate of thorns with her blade drawn and her mother's signature folded somewhere behind her ribs, and decide, finally, which side of the founding crime she stood on.
