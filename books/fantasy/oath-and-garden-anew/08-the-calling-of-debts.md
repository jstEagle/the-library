## 8. The Calling of Debts

The thorns woke before the soldiers did.

Lior felt it as a tremor in the floorboards of the conservatory's ruined gallery, a shiver running up through the roots that had spent a century prying apart the flagstones. Ovid stood at the great broken gate with his hands buried to the wrist in a hedge of black briars, and his head was tilted the way an old man tilts it to hear rain coming.

"They're here," he said. "Earlier than I hoped. Later than I feared."

"How long can you hold them?" Lior asked, though they already knew the answer would be shaped like a grave.

Ovid smiled. His face in the moonlight was all creases and patience, a man who had spent sixty years learning the grammar of growing things. "As long as the garden needs me to. No longer. That's the whole art, isn't it? Knowing when you're finished." He flexed his fingers inside the thorns, and the hedge groaned, thickened, wove itself into a lattice dense enough to stop a crossbow bolt. "Take the child down into the root-cellar. The old speakers built it for nights like this. There are carvings on the walls—show her the ones with the open hands."

Sef did not want to go. She stood at the top of the cellar stairs in her too-large coat, bare feet silent on the stone, and her eyes were enormous and wet in the dark. "What about him?" she whispered.

"He's the gardener," Lior said. "Gardens are what he knows."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind there is tonight."

She went, finally, but slowly, looking back at every step, and Lior heard her settle among the roots below and begin, very quietly, to talk—to the rats in the walls, to the moths at the broken glass, to anything with ears. Comforting them. That was the terrible joke of it: even now, even with fire coming over the wall, the child's first instinct was to tell the small frightened things that everything would be all right.

Lior wished someone could say the same to them.

* * *

The first horn sounded just past midnight.

It was not a Warden's horn—those were short, disciplined things, two notes and silence. This was longer, lower, and it came from beyond the garden walls, from the dark hills where nothing should have been standing watch. Lior froze halfway down the gallery stairs and listened, and heard the horn answered by another, and another, each one farther away and stranger—the coughing bellow of something heavy, the high glass-shatter scream of something winged.

"No," Lior breathed. "No, no—not yet—"

They ran back up. Through the conservatory's shattered roof the sky was clear and cold and full of stars, and against those stars shapes were moving. First a few, then dozens, then more than Lior could count without the counting turning into prayer. Wings. Great slow wings, circling the ruins like ash caught in an updraft.

Below, in the cellar, Sef's voice had changed. It was no longer comfort. It was calling.

* * *

The gate broke at the second hour.

It did not break so much as it was unmade—thorns that had taken Ovid a decade to train torn apart by hooked poles and burning oil, the smell of scorched sap rolling through the galleries like a funeral. Lior watched from the shadow of the fern-house as the Wardens poured through, gray cloaks, gray lanterns, Ashk at their front with a sword she hadn't drawn and a face like a closed door.

And behind her, chained in a collar of iron and runes, came the bloodhound-beast—a gaunt gray thing, half wolf and half memory, its nose to the ground, dragging its handlers toward the root-cellar stairs.

Toward Sef.

Lior moved without deciding to. Ten years of careful, charm-sized living burned away in three steps across the courtyard, and then they were standing between the beast and the cellar door with their arms spread wide and their heart hammering out a rhythm they hadn't heard since they were nineteen years old and stupid with power.

"Stop," Lior said—in plain Vellamark speech, useless, human—and the handlers laughed, or would have, except the gray beast stopped. Its ears came up. Its nostrils flared. And it looked at Lior the way you look at a door you thought had been bricked over for years and find standing open.

Ashk saw it. Of course she saw it. Her scarred throat worked once. "You," she said. "The charm-seller. All this time it was *you* the beasts remembered."

"Not me," Lior said, and their voice cracked on it, because it was only half a lie. "Her. The child. She never learned to be quiet, because no one taught her there was anything to be quiet *from*."

"Then give her to us," Ashk said, "and live."

Behind Lior, from the mouth of the cellar, Sef screamed.

It was not a child's scream—or it was, but it was also something older wearing a child's throat, and every beast within twenty miles heard it and understood. The night tore open. Wings came down through the broken roof in a river of feathers and membrane; the walls shook as things with hooves and things with claws hit the outer hedges; somewhere a bull-thing bellowed the deep war-note that Lior had heard exactly once before, in a story told by a woman who died telling it. The Wardens' formation collapsed inward, lanterns swinging, bolts loosing upward at shadows. Ashk shouted orders in a voice gone raw.

And above it all, riding the chaos like a wave, came the horde's answering cry—a sound with words in it, if words could be made of rage. *The debt. The debt is called. Who speaks? Who answers?*

Lior knew that voice. They had known it in dreams for ten years, the way you know a language you swore never to speak again.

They turned to the cellar door. Sef stood on the bottom step, hair wild, eyes streaming, both hands raised toward the ruined roof as though she could hold the sky shut. Around her the rats had come out of the walls by the dozen and lain flat, trembling, awaiting orders she didn't know how to give. Every beast in the region was converging on this one terrified child, and every one of them was asking the same question, and none of them would leave until it was answered, and the answer was war.

"Sef." Lior dropped to their knees at the top of the stairs, took her face in their hands. "Look at me. Look at *me*, not the sky."

"I didn't mean—" she sobbed. "I was scared and they asked and I *answered*, I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

"I know. I know, little bird. Listen. They're asking who will stand for the pact. If no one answers, they'll take the answer themselves, and it'll be fire again, all of it, everything we ran from." Lior's own hands were shaking. They pressed them still against the child's cheeks. "Someone has to speak for you. Someone has to pay."

"Pay with what?"

Lior looked at her—at the feral, furious, grieving, magnificent child they had known for one impossible season—and felt the shape of the price settle over them like a coat cut to fit.

"With what I have," Lior said. "Wait here. Whatever you hear—wait *here*."

* * *

They walked out into the center of the courtyard, into the falling feathers and the swinging lantern-light, and they opened their mouth and spoke the First Tongue aloud for the first time in ten years.

It came back like blood into a sleeping limb—all the pain of returning, all the heat. The syllables were older than the city, older than the walls, older than the crime the throne was built on, and when Lior spoke them the whole night bent to listen. The wings stilled. The bellowing sank to a low, waiting rumble. Even the Wardens stopped, some of them crossing themselves with charms that had never once worked.

*I am here,* Lior said in the old language. *I am speaker. The vessel is a child, and the child is under my protection. Name your price.*

The horde answered as one voice, ten thousand throats, and the word it spoke was the oldest word in the pact:

*Memory.*

*You ask for memories,* Lior said. *Whose?*

*The speaker's. Freely given. As the covenant was sealed, so shall it be kept.* A pause, vast and patient as a mountain deciding to move. *Ten years. The years you buried us deepest. Give them, and the child walks free, and the war stays sleeping. Refuse, and we remember for ourselves why we ended it—and this time we do not stop at the walls.*

Ten years. Vellamark's gray market. The luck-knots. The careful, harmless, hidden life built plank by plank over a decade of deliberate forgetting. Lior had thought, walking out here, that they might have to bargain. That they might offer five years, seven, and haggle like a fence over silk.

But the horde had named the exact weight of their silence, and there was no bargaining with a debt that precise.

Somewhere behind them, Ashk was shouting—something about holding position, something about the child—but her voice seemed to come from very far away, from the other side of the life Lior was about to lose. Somewhere below, Sef was crying. Somewhere overhead, ten thousand beasts waited with the patience of mountains.

Lior thought of the woman who had taught them the Tongue, dead in a burning village before Sef was born. Thought of Ovid, somewhere in the dark, holding a gate of thorns with his bare hands. Thought of the seed Ovid had pressed into their palm that first evening, saying only: *Plant it when it's over. You'll know why.*

They didn't know why. They wouldn't know why. That was the point. That was the price.

"All right," Lior said aloud, in plain speech, to no one—to everyone. "All right. I remember how to do this."

They knelt in the courtyard, in the trampled frost and the drifting feathers, and they began to speak.

Not names. Not bargains. Memories—whole and warm and specific, poured out like water from a jug. The morning they'd first sold a luck-knot and felt safe in their smallness. The winter they'd nearly frozen in the underways and been pulled out by strangers whose faces they'd never learn. The decade of gray mornings, of counted coins, of doors locked twice. Each memory spoken aloud in the First Tongue became a bright thread leaving them, and the horde drank the threads down, and the night grew quieter with every one, the way a fever breaks.

The last thing Lior gave was the memory of choosing silence—the day, ten years ago, when they had sworn never to speak again, and the exact taste of the fear that oath was made of. They felt it lift out of them like a splinter drawn from a wound, and with it went everything around it: the reasons, the years, the person they had been when they swore.

The horde sighed. All of it, all together, a sound like wind through a forest the size of the world.

*Paid,* it said. *The vessel is free. The war sleeps. Speaker—you may keep the child in your remembering, for she was never part of the price. But the rest is ours now, and yours no longer.*

The wings rose. The great shapes wheeled once over the ruined conservatory, once over the kneeling charm-seller, and then they were gone, north and east and up, dissolving into the stars as though the sky had swallowed its own army.

Silence fell on the garden like snow.

Lior stayed kneeling a long time. They could hear, faintly, a child sobbing in a cellar. They could hear, closer, a woman in a gray cloak breathing hard, her sword still half-drawn, her scarred throat working around words she couldn't seem to find.

Lior looked up at Ashk, and knew her face, and did not know why it mattered.

"Why am I here?" they asked her, honestly, bewildered. "Who are you?"

Ashk stared down at them for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—something old and burned-out stirring in its ashes at the sound of the Tongue, reaching for a gift that had been ripped out of her years ago. Her hand shook on the sword-hilt.

"You don't remember," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Remember what?"

Ashk looked at the empty sky, at the broken gate, at the cellar door where a child's sobs were going quiet. Then she sheathed her sword.

"Get the child," she said to her soldiers, in a voice like a door closing. "We're going home."

* * *

From the shadow of the fern-house, where he had crawled when the last of his strength left him, Ovid watched the gray cloaks withdraw through his ruined gate. He was dying—he knew the feel of it the way he knew the feel of frost coming—and he did not mind. The garden would grow over him by spring, and that was a better tomb than most speakers got.

He watched Lior rise slowly, unsteadily, and go to the cellar door, and kneel, and gather the child up as she came flying out, and hold her while she cried into their shoulder—hold her like someone they had loved for a long time, though their face, when they lifted it, wore the blankness of a man reading a letter in a language he used to speak.

Ovid smiled, and let his head rest back against the thorns, which curled gently around him like hands.

He had one seed left in his pocket. He'd meant to give it to Lior tomorrow, with instructions.

Well. Instructions were overrated. Gardens always found a way to explain themselves to whoever was willing to kneel down and listen.

The last thing Ovid saw was the child leading the charm-seller away through the broken glass, barefoot on the frost, both of them talking softly to the rats in the walls.

The last thing he heard was the garden, already beginning, very quietly, to grow.
