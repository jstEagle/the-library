## 9. The Price Spoken Aloud

The conservatory was screaming.

Not the people—the glass. Every pane that had survived a century of neglect now shivered in its lead frame, and the ones that hadn't survived came down in glittering sheets as the horde descended. Wings blackened the moon. There were crows from the city rookeries and herons up from the delta marshes, foxes pouring through the broken wall like spilled water, a bear that should not have been within fifty miles of Vellamark standing on the ruin of the orchard terrace with its breath steaming in the cold. And above them all, wheeling in slow, terrible spirals, the great horned owls that had not been seen since the war—since the pact—since the last time anyone had spoken the First Tongue aloud.

Sef had called them. The child stood in the center of the ruined nave with their arms flung wide and their small body shaking, tears cutting tracks through the soot on their face, and every beast for twenty miles had answered.

Lior understood, with the clarity that comes only at the edge of ruin, that this was how the old world had ended. Not with armies. With a frightened child and an open door.

"Sef." They kept their voice low, though low meant nothing now; the whole night was a drum. "Sef, look at me."

The child turned. Their eyes were wrong—not empty, but *full*, crowded, a hundred minds looking out through one small face. The beasts were in them, or they were in the beasts, and the border between had worn away to nothing.

"They're loud," Sef whispered, and the whisper carried across the terrace like a shout. "They're all so loud, Lior, I can't—I didn't mean to—"

"I know." Lior crossed the broken flagstones slowly, hands open, the way you'd approach something wild and wounded. Behind them, at the shattered gate where Ovid's thorn-wall had finally given way, boots crunched on glass. Ashk's company was coming through in ranks, torches held high, crossbows leveled—and the crossbows might as well have been twigs. You could not shoot a sky.

Ashk herself stood at the gate's mouth. She had not drawn her blade yet. She was staring upward at the owls with an expression Lior would spend the rest of a life trying to name: hunger and horror wearing the same face.

There was no time. Lior knelt in front of Sef and took the child's face in both hands.

"Listen to me. Only me. The beasts are angry because they think we've come to start the war again. We haven't. But they won't believe words spoken in the market tongue. They need the old words. The real ones."

"I don't know them," Sef said miserably. "I never learned them. They just *come*—"

"I know they do." Lior's throat closed. Ten years of silence, ten years of luck-knots and harmless charms and careful gray nothing, and it had all been building to this: that the only person left who could pay the debt was someone who had spent a decade pretending the debt did not exist. "But I do. I remember enough. And I know what they'll ask for."

"What?"

"Memory. It's always memory. That was the bargain, the first time. The beasts ended the war, and the speakers paid with everything they were." Lior smiled, and found, strangely, that it wasn't hard to smile. "Ovid told us. The covenant runs on remembering—and on forgetting."

"You'd forget—" Sef's voice cracked. "Forget *what*? Forget me?"

"No." Lior pressed their forehead to the child's. "Never you. Whatever else goes, I'll make sure of that. I'm choosing what to give them, Sef. That's the difference between payment and plunder. Do you understand? I get to choose."

It was only half true, and they both knew it. But the child nodded, and the horde above them stilled—not quieted, but *waiting*, a thousand pairs of eyes fixed on the two small figures in the ruins like a jury leaning forward.

Lior stood. Turned to face the beasts. Faced, too, the soldiers at the gate, and Ashk, whose torchlight caught the wet shine on her cheeks that she would deny to her grave.

Then Lior opened their mouth, and spoke the First Tongue.

* * *

It did not feel like speaking. It felt like opening a vein.

The language moved through Lior's chest like cold water finding old channels, and every word cost something—a sound, a scent, a face. The first phrase gave up the smell of rain on the Vellamark rooftops. The second took the taste of the honey-cakes sold three stalls down from theirs in the gray market. Lior felt each memory lift free and fly upward into the dark, and the beasts *caught* them, swallowed them, passed them wing to wing and tooth to tooth until the whole horde hummed with a stranger's life.

*I offer,* said the Tongue through Lior's mouth, in words older than walls. *I offer the years. Ten of them. Take the hiding, take the silence, take the fear that taught me to be small. Take the day I buried my gift beneath a charm of finding-lost-keys. Take the night I first heard a child speak and chose to pretend I hadn't.*

The owls wheeled lower. The bear shifted its weight, and the terrace groaned.

*Is it enough?*

The answer came not in words but in pressure, in presence, in the vast collective attention of every living thing that remembered the war. They weighed Lior the way a merchant weighs coin. They found the offering light.

Of course they did. Ten years of hiding was a thin purse. The beasts wanted more—they wanted the *why*. Why had Lior sworn to hide? What had happened, all those years ago, that had driven a speaker into silence?

And here was the terrible mercy of it: Lior could not remember clearly anymore which memories those were. The offering was already flowing. So Lior did the only thing left, the thing Ovid must have done, the thing every speaker before them had done at the end.

They stopped choosing.

*Take what the debt requires,* Lior said. *All of it. I hold back only one thing—one face, one name, one child. Everything else is yours.*

The horde went utterly still.

Then the taking began in earnest, and it was not gentle. It was a tide going out. Lior felt whole years drain away like water through cupped hands—the cellar where they'd hidden Sef, gone. Maren's smoke-dark shop and the map drawn on butcher's paper, gone. The flight through the underways, the tunnel-beast, the fury and the fraying trust—all of it lifting away, dissolving into the night, feeding ten thousand hungry memories that had waited a century for the taste of a human life.

Lior gripped the one thing they refused to release: a barefoot child collapsing at a charm-seller's door, saying words the rats obeyed. *This one stays. This one stays.* They wrapped the memory around themselves like a knot, tied it with every trick of their old craft, and held on.

The world went white at the edges. Somewhere very far away, Sef was crying their name.

And then—stillness. The pressure lifted. The horde drew back, wings folding, bodies lowering, the great gathering unraveling into ordinary animals again: crows becoming crows, the bear turning ambling toward the tree line, the owls rising once, twice, and scattering into the dark like ash on wind.

In the ringing quiet, Lior stood in a ruined conservatory and could not remember why they had ever been afraid.

* * *

Ashk walked forward alone.

She had ordered her company to hold at the gate—had heard her own voice give the command without quite recognizing it, because her hands were shaking and her eyes were full and there was a sound in her chest she had not felt in twenty years. It had started when the speaker began to speak. The First Tongue, alive, in the open air, after a lifetime of ash.

Her gift had been burned out of her at fifteen. She remembered the procedure better than she remembered her mother's face: the Warden-priests, the iron collar, the smell of her own scorching blood, and then the silence afterward—permanent, surgical, a room in her mind bricked shut. They had told her the silence was a mercy. They had told her the beasts' memory was a disease.

But when Lior Zhou had opened their mouth tonight, something behind that brick wall had *turned over*. Like a hearth catching. Like a wound remembering it used to be a mouth.

She had heard the Tongue, and for three heartbeats she had understood the owls.

That was the throne's crime, then. Not that magic existed. That the beasts *remembered*—remembered the founding, remembered what the first kings had done to bind the land to their line, remembered the oath sworn in blood and the oath broken in blood. Every speaker was a witness the crown could not burn fast enough. Ashk had spent her career hunting witnesses and calling it law.

She stopped before Lior Zhou. The charm-seller looked hollowed out, swaying, a decade lighter in the worst possible way. The child clung to their coat like a burr.

"Captain," Lior said. Their voice was rough, unfamiliar even to themselves. "Do you know me?"

"No," Ashk said honestly. "I know *of* you. I know your file. Ten years of harmless charms." She looked at the child. "And this one is under my writ. The vessel of the First Tongue. My orders say I bring them to the capital alive."

Above them, on the broken ridge of the conservatory roof, a single horned owl had returned. It sat motionless against the stars, and its eyes were fixed on Ashk with a patience older than her office.

Ashk looked at it for a long time. Then she looked at her own hands, and thought about the hearth that had caught, and the brick wall, and what else might be behind it if she ever went looking—and what the beasts would remember about her if she ever spoke again.

"My orders say alive," she repeated quietly. "They don't say tonight. They don't say from these ruins. As far as my report will read, the vessel died in the calling—burned out by their own gift, as speakers die. The crowd dispersed. Nothing left to hunt." She sheathed her blade with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a door closing. "Go north. Don't sell charms in any city I garrison."

Lior stared at her. "Why?"

Ashk almost smiled. It was a terrible thing, that almost-smile, grief and relief fighting over the same ground. "Because you paid a price I couldn't afford, charm-seller. And because somewhere in what you gave away, there might have been a version of me that got to keep her gift—and I find I'd rather not be the reason another one doesn't." She turned. Raised her voice to the company at the gate. "Form up! We're done here. Back to Vellamark."

"But Captain—" a lieutenant began, gesturing at the child, at the speaker.

"*Done here.*"

The company withdrew by torchlight, boots crunching over glass and thorns, and Ashk walked last, and did not look back—though the owl watched her go the whole way down the hill, and she felt its gaze between her shoulder blades like a hand, like a promise, like a debt of her own now, carried in silence toward the city and the throne that had made her.

At the top of the ridge she paused once, just once, and let herself listen—to wind, to wings, to the faint residual music of a language that had nearly cost her everything to hear.

Then Warden-Captain Ashk walked down into the dark, carrying the crime of the founding home in her chest like a coal she could neither quench nor set down.

Behind her, in the ruins, a child took a hollowed-out stranger's hand, and the garden began, very softly, to grow.
