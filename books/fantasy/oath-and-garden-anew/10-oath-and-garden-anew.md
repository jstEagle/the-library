## 10. Oath and Garden Anew

The frost came early that year, silvering the awnings of the gray market before the bells had finished ringing in the hour after dawn. Lior Zhou stood behind the little folding table as always, luck-knots strung in rows of red and river-gray cord, and waited for the city to remember it needed them.

Vellamark was the same. That was the strangest part—the same gray stone, the same bread queues, the same informants hunched over their morning loaves with their eyes on everyone's hands. The Wardens still walked their rounds in pairs, ash-colored cloaks, faces like closed doors. The market still smelled of wet wool and charcoal smoke and the sweet rot of apples nobody could afford.

Only Lior was different, though no one looking would have known it. Ten years gone from inside them like a room walled off in a house they still lived in. They knew the city, knew the knots, knew how to haggle and flatter and keep their voice flat when a Warden paused at the table's edge. What they did not know—what sat in them like an ache without a wound beneath it—was why they had ever learned to be so careful.

*You hid something,* said the ache. *You hid it for a long time.*

And then, softer, in a voice that was not a memory but felt like one: *You kept a promise.*

Lior had stopped trying to argue with the feeling. It came most often at dusk, or when pigeons gathered too near, or when a rat crossed the alley behind the stall without hurrying, without fear, pausing to look up at them with small black eyes full of something almost like courtesy.

"Two knots," said a woman now, breath steaming. "One for my door, one for my daughter's wrist."

"For luck or for love?"

"Luck. Love can look after itself."

Lior laughed and tied the knots while she watched, fingers moving through patterns older than the city, older than any wall. The woman paid, tucked her purchases away, and went. And above her, along the gutter-line of the chandler's roof, a crow shifted its weight and did not leave.

They were always there now. Lior had noticed it within the first week of returning—a crow on the chandler's roof, a gull on the fishmonger's sign, cats that sat in doorways facing the stall instead of passing it. The beasts of Vellamark watched Lior Zhou the way the faithful watch a shrine: patiently, without demanding anything, as if waiting for something they were certain would eventually be remembered.

Once, early on, Lior had thrown a crust to the crow. It had not eaten it. It had carried the crust away carefully, the way one carries an offering, and Lior had stood in the emptying market with cold hands and a hot, wordless grief in the chest, and had not known what for.

* * *

Sef came with the thaw.

Lior woke past midnight to a soft scraping at the shutter—not a knock, never a knock—and opened the window onto a thin moon and a child-shaped shadow crouched on the sill, barefoot even in the last of the winter cold, hair full of twigs, smelling of leaf-rot and rain and living earth.

"Sef."

"You kept the window loose." The voice was deeper than Lior remembered, a season older, though how many seasons had passed since the conservatory Lior could not have counted. "I checked every night for six nights. You kept it loose."

"I don't know why I keep it loose." Lior stepped back to let the child climb through. "Habit, maybe. From someone I used to be."

Sef landed on the floorboards without a sound and looked around the single room with quick, cataloguing eyes—the bed, the kettle, the strings and beads and bones of the charm trade, the wall where Lior had begun, without quite deciding to, pressing seeds into the cracks between stones. Then the child crossed the room and hugged Lior hard around the middle, face pressed into their shirt, and held on the way small things hold on to large things in wind.

"I went to the garden," Sef said against Lior's ribs. "Ovid's garden. I fixed his graves. The foxes help me now. And the boar, when he isn't sulking. And—" the child pulled back, eyes bright, words tumbling, "—there are wolves in the far hills who speak old-old Tongue, older than mine even, and they say the war is still sleeping, they say the debt is paid, they say—"

"They say what?" Lior asked gently.

Sef went quiet. The child studied Lior's face with that unnerving directness, the look of someone reading a language written under the skin.

"They say a speaker bought your life," Sef said slowly. "Ten years of a speaker's remembering. They say it like a story. Like a song." A pause. "I think you're in the song, Lior. I think you're the ending of it."

Lior's hands had gone cold around the kettle handle. "I don't remember any song."

"No," Sef said softly. "That's what buying means."

They drank tea by the banked stove while Sef talked—of the wild gardens spreading beyond the walls, root and vine reclaiming the ruins; of the beasts' slow parliament, the foxes and crows and the great patient tunnel-things; of a world Lior had helped save and could not recall saving. Twice Sef started to explain the covenant and twice stopped, watching Lior's face, and finally said only: "When you're ready. The beasts say memories come back sometimes. Through dreams. Through dirt under your fingernails. They say the ground keeps what people give it, and gives it back slow."

Lior looked at their own hands, ink-stained, cord-burned, and thought of the ache that had no name.

"Tell me one thing," they said. "Just one. Did I do right? Whatever I did—did I do right?"

Sef smiled, and in the firelight the child looked suddenly much older than any season could account for, old as hills, old as pacts.

"You gave everything," Sef said. "And we all lived. So yes."

* * *

Spring came up the walls of Vellamark the way it always did—through cracks, over rooftops, indifferent to law. Lior sold charms through the lengthening days, and the beasts watched from their gutters and signs, and once a whole flock of pigeons rose from the cornice at dusk and wheeled three times above the stall before scattering, and the market folk muttered about omens, and Lior stood very still with their heart going strange and full, and did not know why.

In summer Sef came again, taller, sun-browned, bringing honeycomb wrapped in leaves and stories wrapped in silence. In autumn the child brought a sack of late apples and stayed three days, sleeping curled by the stove like something born to dens, and left before the first frost with a kiss pressed to Lior's knuckles and a promise: *each season, I'll come. The beasts will carry word ahead.*

And each time, when Sef climbed out the window and dropped into the dark, Lior stood listening until the smallest sounds of the child faded, and felt the shape of an enormous absence fit itself perfectly around the shape of an enormous love, and understood nothing, and wept anyway, quietly, into the dishwater.

* * *

It was on the first mild day of the second spring that Lior found the seed.

It had been in the bottom of the charm box all along—through the move back to the city, through the rehanging of the shutters, through whatever ten years of forgetting had done to the person who once hid it there. A seed the size of a thumbnail, dark and ridged, warm to the touch in a way seeds should not be. Lior turned it over and over in their palm on the doorstep and felt the ache rise in them like water finding its level.

*I know this,* said the ache. *I carried this out of a burning gate. Someone died so I could carry this. Someone with soil under his nails and graves for a garden, someone laughing, someone holding a door of thorns—*

Gone. The images broke apart like reflections in stirred water. But the warmth of the seed remained, and the certainty, sudden and total as sunrise:

*This goes in the ground. This has always been going in the ground.*

Lior planted it that evening in the strip of dirt behind the lodging house, where the wall met the shadows and no Warden looked twice. They dug with a kitchen spoon because they owned no trowel, knelt in the cold earth, and set the seed down the way one sets down something sleeping—as carefully as possible, with an apology for waking it later.

Then they covered it, pressed the soil flat with both palms, and stayed kneeling a while in the blue dusk.

Above them, along the rooftop line, the crows had gathered. Not two, not five—a dozen, silent, heads tilted, watching the little grave-shaped patch of turned earth. Down the alley, a cat sat washing one paw and watching too. Somewhere under the street, faint as a heartbeat through floorboards, something vast turned over in its sleep and settled again.

Lior looked up at the birds.

"I don't know what I promised you," they said aloud, voice rough. "I don't know who I was when I promised it. But I'm still here. And the child is free. And whatever else was true about me—it seems I keep my word."

The lead crow dipped its head once, formal as a bow between equals, and the flock lifted as one and poured away eastward toward the wild gardens, toward the green dark beyond the walls where a child ran barefoot among things that loved her.

Lior stayed until full dark, one hand resting flat on the warm soil.

By midsummer, if anyone had asked, there was a sapling growing behind the lodging house that no one remembered planting—broad-leaved, fast-growing, wrong for the climate and thriving anyway—and the neighborhood cats slept in its shade, and the pigeons roosted in it at dusk, and the Wardens walked past it every day and never once saw it at all.

And Lior Zhou, charm-seller, keeper of a loose window and a buried promise, went on selling luck-knots in the gray market, watched over by everything that had wings or teeth or roots, rich in a debt they could not remember owing, and happier—for reasons that arrived each season at the window, barefoot and grinning, smelling of leaf-rot and home—than they had ever been able to explain.
