## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

The mountain spoke first in the small hours, the way it always did.

Orsen Hartwell felt it through her boots before she heard anything—a low tremor in the stone, like a sleeper turning over. She stopped at the head of the column and pressed her palm flat against the cliff face, letting the cold granite bleed into her skin. Beneath the cold there was something else: a faint hum, a wrongness, the way a lute string sounds just before it snaps.

*Restless,* she thought. *The pass is restless tonight.*

"Trouble?" Doss Kellin came up beside her, his lantern hooded to a coin of amber light. He was the caravan master, a broad man gone soft at the middle but hard everywhere else, with hands like knotted rope and eyes that had counted too many betrayals to trust easily. He trusted Orsen, though—or as close to trusting as a smuggler ever came. Three years of guiding his wagons and mules through Thornfell's hidden roads had bought her that much.

"Just ice melt," she said. "The scree's loose above the switchbacks. We'll want to move quiet past the Widow's Teeth."

Doss grunted. Behind them, the column waited—eleven souls, six mules, three wagons stacked with salt-dried eels, uncut garnets, and a crate of something Doss never let anyone inventory. Lanterns shuttered. Breath steaming. The kind of silence that only people breaking the law know how to keep.

Orsen lifted her hand from the rock and flexed her fingers, warming them. She had felt more than loose stone in that touch, if she was honest—and she tried very hard never to be honest with herself about such things. There had been a pulse beneath the granite. Slow. Deep. Patient in a way that geology should not be patient.

She put it out of her mind and led them on.

The hidden roads of Thornfell were not roads at all, not really. They were habits of the mountain—goat tracks widened by generations of feet that preferred not to be seen, deer paths that bent around warding stones older than any kingdom, dry streambeds that ran only when the snows gave permission. The Crown's maps showed three passes through the range. Orsen knew nineteen ways through, and four of them would take a loaded wagon if you knew which ledges to trust and which songs to sing to the mules.

She had learned them from Mother Veyra, who had learned them from the mountain itself, though no one living knew that now except Orsen, and she intended to keep it that way until she died of old age in some warm lowland town where nobody burned witches anymore.

That was the whole of her ambition, these days. Warmth. Anonymity. A garden, maybe, someday, if gardens weren't too much like the one behind the hedge-witch's cottage, where Orsen had spent her girlhood learning things that got women drowned in ponds.

"Hartwell." Tam Wrenfell fell into step beside her, young and wiry and forever chewing a stalk of some bitter alpine herb. He was the closest thing she had to a friend in the caravan, which meant he knew her name was Hartwell and nothing else. "You've been talking to the rocks again."

"Someone has to," she said. "You certainly don't talk to anything but your own supper."

Tam laughed, and the sound was swallowed by the dark pines crowding the track. "Superstition, is what Doss pays you for. But I'll grant you this—the last two guides wouldn't walk Thornfell after dark, and here we are, midnight, hauling garnets under the noses of every toll-warden between here and Marrowgate."

"The mountain doesn't care what we carry," Orsen said. "It cares whether we're respectful."

"And are you? Respectful?"

She smiled and didn't answer, because the true answer was a prayer-shaped thing she'd been swallowing for three years.

They made the Widow's Teeth an hour before dawn—a spine of black rock jutting over the switchback descent, wind-carved into jagged points that did look, in certain lights, like the mouth of something enormous. Below, the valley floor lay wrapped in river fog. Above, the stars wheeled slow and indifferent.

And there, tucked into a crease of the cliff where the path narrowed, stood the shrine.

It was a humble thing, as roadside shrines went: a hollowed niche in the stone, sheltering a bowl for offerings, a stub of tallow candle, and a carving so weathered by centuries of wind that it might have been a face or a knot or simply the mountain's own doing. Moss furred its edges. Someone long dead had left a ribbon tied to a thornbush beside it, faded to the grey of old scars.

The old gods' shrines dotted all of Thornfell. Three hundred years since the Silence, since the gods had stopped answering and the Crown had stopped pretending they existed, and still the shrines remained, because mountain folk were practical about such things. You left a crust of bread or a copper bit not because anyone was listening, but because your grandmother had, and her grandmother before her, and it cost nothing to be polite to the dark.

Orsen always paused at them. It was habit, and penance, and grief, all braided together.

Tonight she stopped longer than usual. The others moved ahead, leading the mules down the first switchback, their lantern-lights dwindling below her like fallen stars. Doss called up something about watching her footing. She raised a hand in answer and turned back to the shrine.

The candle stub was unlit. On impulse, she struck flint to it, cupping the flame against the wind until it caught and steadied. Light moved across the worn carving, and for a heartbeat—just a heartbeat—Orsen could have sworn the grooves deepened, sharpened, became a face looking back at her. Old. Weathered. *Waiting.*

Her heart stumbled. She told herself it was the flame's flicker.

"I'm not her student," she whispered, the words barely breath. "I'm nobody. I'm just passing through." And then, because the lie sat poorly even in her own mouth, softer still, almost too quiet for the wind to steal: "Forgive me. Whoever's listening. Forgive me for hiding."

The flame bowed.

Not flickered—*bowed*, bending toward the carved face as if in deference, though there was no draft in the niche, no wind in the crease of the cliff. The air went still around her, stiller than any mountain night had a right to be, and Orsen felt the hum she'd touched in the granite earlier rise up through her boot-soles, her knees, her ribs, gathering like a held breath.

Then the mountain answered.

It began as sound—a single deep note rolling up from somewhere far beneath the stone, the way thunder rolls when the storm is still beyond the horizon. The candleflame stretched tall and thin. Frost bloomed across the shrine's rim in delicate fern-patterns, spreading outward along the rock, silvering the thornbush, creeping toward her boots.

"*Hartwell.*"

Her name. Spoken in no voice and every voice—in the grinding of stone on stone, in the creak of ancient pine, in the fog rising from the valley floor. Her name, pronounced slowly, deliberately, the way one tastes a word one has not spoken in three hundred years.

Orsen stumbled back from the shrine. The candle snuffed itself—not blown out; *pinched*, precisely, politely, as if by invisible fingers.

Below her, the caravan had stopped. She heard Doss shouting, confused, and then Tam's voice cracking upward: "Hartwell! What in the nine hells did you *do*?"

"I didn't—" Her voice came out a stranger's. "I didn't do anything. I only prayed. I only—"

The pass began to move.

There was no other way her mind could hold it. The switchbacks below her writhed like snakes shedding skin—the path she'd walked a hundred times folded in on itself, ledge sliding against ledge, and where the road down had run clear and open there was now sheer wall, and where wall had been, a new gap yawned, breathing out cold air that smelled of deep places, of roots and iron and the inside of the world. The stars overhead shuddered. Somewhere a peak groaned like an old man rising from a chair.

The mountain was rearranging itself. Around her name. Around her prayer.

"*No,*" Orsen breathed. "*No, please, I take it back—I take it back—*"

But the old gods had been silent for three centuries, and whatever had woken in the deep of Thornfell was not inclined to be refused.

The first crack split the night like a bone breaking. Then the Widow's Teeth themselves leaned, ponderous, terrible, and sheared away from the cliff above the switchbacks.

What happened next, Orsen's memory kept in fragments for the rest of her life. The roar of falling stone. Doss's voice, bellowing orders that the mountain did not care to hear. Mules screaming. A lantern tumbling end over end into the dark, its light spinning like a dying star. Tam looking up at her from the third switchback, mouth open around a word she never heard, as forty tons of black granite came down where he stood.

The impact threw her off her feet. Stone dust engulfed everything, choking, blinding, tasting of iron and lightning. The tremor went on and on, the mountain settling its new shape, and Orsen lay flattened against the path with her arms over her head, weeping without sound, whispering apologies to gods she had sworn she no longer believed in.

When the dust began to thin, greying the first light of dawn, she crawled to the edge of the switchbacks and looked down.

There was no road. There was no caravan. There was a fall of rubble the size of a chapel, fresh-scraped and raw, sealing the pass like a wound closed by fist. Here and there a wagon wheel jutted from the stone. A mule's hind leg, terribly still. One lantern, cracked, its candle guttering in the morning wind.

Eleven souls. Six mules. Three wagons. Gone in less time than it takes to say a prayer.

Orsen knelt at the edge until her knees ached, until the sun cleared the eastern peaks and laid gold across the ruin, and no voice rose from beneath the stone, and no hand stirred, and she understood with a certainty colder than the frost on the shrine that she was the only thing the mountain had left alive.

Because the mountain had heard her name.

She rose on legs that shook, took one last look at the buried road—at Tam's herb-stalk, somehow untouched, lying on a slab of stone like an offering no one would collect—and turned north, away from Marrowgate, away from everything, walking because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant screaming.

Behind her, faint beneath the wind, the deep note sounded once more. Not threatening. Almost gentle.

Almost like a promise.

Something ancient had heard her name, and it was not finished with her yet.
