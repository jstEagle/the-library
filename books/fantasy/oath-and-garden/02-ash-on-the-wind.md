## 2. Ash on the Wind

The mountain let her go grudgingly, the way a sleeping dog lets a stranger pass its bed.

Orsen felt it in her knees first—the old roads softening from stone to clay, the air thickening with river-smell and woodsmoke instead of pine and cold iron. Three days she had walked down out of Thornfell alone, past the buried pass where the rockfall still smoked faintly, as if the mountain itself had been scalded. She did not look at it long. Looking made her remember the sound: not thunder, but something closer to a held breath released. The caravan's mules had screamed once, together, in one voice, and then there had been only stone.

She had dug for two hours. Her nails were still black underneath.

Now the road widened, and Marrowgate rose out of the valley haze like a bruise on the green—low rooftops, a crooked market spire, the brown ribbon of the Marrow sliding past its eastern wall. A town that lived by ferrying smuggled wool and untaxed brandy between the mountains and the king's roads. A town that had never cared who you were, so long as you paid the ferryman.

Orsen pulled her hood lower and joined the trickle of traffic at the south gate. She had done this a hundred times in three years of guiding Hartwell's people through the passes: shoulders rounded, eyes down, a tired drover's slouch that invited no questions. The false papers sewn into her coat lining said *Maren Kettle, drover, licensed for lowland transport*. The name was a stranger's; the stitching was her own work, and good.

The gate guard barely glanced at her. But he was not looking at the road behind her. He was looking up—at the shrine niche set into the gatehouse arch, where travelers had left copper coins and ribbon offerings to the Lady of Crossings for as long as Marrowgate had stood.

The niche was empty. Not robbed—*defaced*. Someone had chiseled the Lady's face away, leaving a smooth blank oval in the stone, and painted across it in tar-black strokes a single vertical line, like a wound or a sealed door. Below it, fresh paste held a printed bill:

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN — WITCH-FINDERS GENERAL**
*The old worship is forbidden. All shrines to be reported. All practitioners to be surrendered.*
*The gods are waking. The gods are lying.*

Orsen read it twice. Around her, townsfolk hurried past the defaced shrine without looking up, the way people avoid looking at a hanged man. An old woman crossed herself with the new sign—fist over heart, not hand over brow—and muttered something that might have been prayer or might have been apology.

*The gods are waking.* Orsen's hand drifted to the charm-bag at her throat before she caught herself and turned the motion into adjusting her collar. Three centuries of silence, the priests said. Three hundred years since the old gods last answered anyone, since hedge-witches were burned not for power but for *nostalgia*. She had spent her whole apprenticeship learning rites her mistress swore were useless—manners offered to empty chairs, letters written to the dead.

And then a roadside shrine in Thornfell had heard her name, and a mountain had moved.

She walked into Marrowgate.

* * *

The market square smelled wrong.

Underneath the usual layers—fish, tallow, wet wool, the cider press working overtime—there was a sweetness gone sour, like fruit rotting in a locked room. Orsen traced it to the grain stalls. Every sack stood open for inspection, and every sack held grain the color of ash. Not moldy, not burned. *Blighted*, in a pattern she knew intimately: black veins radiating outward from a central point, fine as frost-fern, precise as script.

Her stomach dropped through the cobbles.

She knew that pattern. She had drawn it herself, aged fourteen, in charcoal, while her mistress stood over her with a stick of chalk and said, *Learn this shape, girl, so you'll know it if you ever see it growing. Godscourge. It's what happens when a god answers too hard. When the vessel can't hold what pours through. The land blights where the divine touches it, like a scar around a burn.*

*But the gods don't answer anymore,* her younger self had protested.

*No,* Maren had agreed, and her face had been strange and distant. *That's what frightens me.*

Now the pattern spread across half the sacks in the market, and Orsen counted the center-points with a professional's horror. Seven blight-hearts in this square alone. Seven answered prayers, each one leaving its scar on the world like a brand. Whatever was listening in Marrowgate had been busy.

A knot of refugees camped under the eaves of the tithe-house, their carts heaped with everything they'd carried. Orsen slowed near them, head down, ears open. Refugees talked to anyone who looked harmless, and a tired drover girl looked harmless indeed.

"—spoke right out of the well," a lean farmer was saying, his voice scraped raw. "I went down to draw water and the water spoke back. Said my name. Said it *knew* my name. And I thought—three hundred years, everyone says they're dead, so I thought I'd dreamed it. Then the barley went black overnight."

"Mine came in the smoke," said a woman mending a net with shaking hands. "Hearth-smoke curled into a face and told me my boy would come home from the coast. He did. Two days later our whole field looked like someone had poured ink in the furrows." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "They're answering. That's the hell of it. Everything you pray for, you *get*—and everything you get costs the ground it grew from."

"Then stop praying," someone muttered.

"We tried." The farmer laughed, a terrible sound. "You try not praying when your child is fevered. You try it."

Orsen moved on, her pulse loud in her ears. Answered prayers, each leaving godscourge behind. Scattered, small-scale, desperate—a town full of frightened people being granted wishes by something that fed on the asking. In her mistress's letters, in the margins of the old rite-books, there had been warnings about exactly this: *a god fed piecemeal grows hungry in proportion. Beware the age of small miracles; they are the appetite whetting.*

Someone was teaching Marrowgate to pray. Or something was teaching itself to listen.

* * *

She found the third defaced shrine by the ferry stairs, and beside it, the fourth warrant-board, and beside *that*, the reason her day went very badly.

The woman posting the bills wore the slate-grey coat of the Witch-Finders General, cut close and practical, with the crowned eye embroidered at the shoulder. She was tall, dark-haired, maybe thirty, with the kind of stillness Orsen associated with herons standing in shallow water—patience that was also a weapon. A short sword hung at her hip, unadorned. Her voice, as she directed two subordinates nailing up another board, was level and unhurried.

"Center of the square, and one at each gate. Captain Vane wants them seen, not just posted."

Orsen should have walked on. Instead—stupidly, fatally—she stopped to read the newest bill, because the printing was different, finer, and the name on it stopped her heart.

**WANTED FOR QUESTIONING**
*MAREN KETTLE, DROVER*
*Last seen Thornfell passes. Connected to the incident at [illegible]. Approach with caution. Subject may be armed with unlicensed craft.*

Her own false name. Her own false trade. On a witch-finder's warrant.

Which meant the caravan's manifest had been found in the rockfall. Which meant someone had survived, or someone had talked before the mountain closed, and either way the Crown's dogs were already running down a trail of paper toward a girl who didn't exist.

"You find something interesting, drover?"

Orsen hadn't heard her cross the cobbles. The grey-coated woman stood an arm's length away, and up close her eyes were pale hazel and missed nothing. They went, deliberately, to Orsen's hands.

Drover's hands were cracked and rope-scarred. Orsen's were ink-stained and chalk-marked, the fingers of a scribe, a stitcher, a *witch*, and she saw the exact moment the woman noticed.

"Paperwork," Orsen said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Checking my license against the toll changes."

"Show me."

Every instinct screamed. Orsen reached slowly inside her coat—took her time, bought three heartbeats with clumsiness—and her fingers found the stitched packet of false papers. And beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, the other thing: a twist of ash-cloth and bone-char, her emergency kit, the hedge-witch's last resort.

The witch-finder's eyes never left hers. "Slowly."

Somewhere across the square, a bell began to ring—not alarm, just the hour, but it broke the moment. The woman glanced toward the sound, half a second, no more.

Half a second was a career.

Orsen stepped back off the ferry stairs, let gravity take her, and hit the Marrow with a splash that swallowed the world. The river in October was a fist of cold; it drove the breath from her body and dragged her downstream under the pilings before she'd finished deciding to swim. Above her, muffled by water, boots hammered the planks.

She surfaced thirty yards on, behind a moored barge, gasping silently. On the stairs above, the grey coat leaned over the rail, scanning the brown water, patient as a heron. Orsen sank to her chin and let the current carry her along the hull, away, away, until the stairs were out of sight.

* * *

Dusk found her in a reed-cutters' hut on the far bank, naked to the waist and shivering, feeding her soaked false papers page by page into a small fire. *Maren Kettle* curled and blackened and went up in sparks that smelled of cheap ink. The warrant would have her description now, her trade, her route. The name was dead; better to kill it cleanly herself than let them do it.

Through the hut's gap-toothed wall she watched Marrowgate's lights come on across the water, and beyond them, far off, the watch-fires of a witch-finder camp—too many fires for a town this size. They weren't here for one runaway apprentice. They were mobilizing. Something had frightened the Crown, and the Crown was answering with fire and paper and chisels.

And underneath it all, under the smoke and the fear, she could feel it now that she let herself feel it: a faint pull, northward, like a fisherman's line gone taut. The same pressure she'd felt at the shrine before the mountain moved. Something ancient was awake in the world, and it was paying attention, and every answered prayer was a scar, and the scars were spreading.

Her mistress was north. Three days' walk, in the hills above the Marrow's source, in the sanctuary Maren had sworn her to never reveal. If anyone alive understood what was waking—if anyone could tell her why a god had heard her name in a pass full of dying men—it was her.

Orsen dressed, wrung out her hair, and banked the fire. On the charred scrap of paper she'd saved from the burning, she wrote in chalk-stub, quickly, the only message worth sending ahead:

*It's happening. The prayers are being answered. Come home.*

She weighted it under a stone at the hut's door for the reed-cutters to find, though she had no idea who might carry it, or how, or whether any messenger could outrun what was coming.

Then she took the north road, alone, with nothing but her secrets—and behind her, across the dark river, a single lantern detached itself from the witch-finder camp and began, unhurried, to follow.
