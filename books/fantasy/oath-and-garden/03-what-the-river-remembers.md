## 3. What the River Remembers

The letters had said to follow the water.

*Where the river forgets its name, remember it for her,* Maren's spidery hand had scrawled, years ago, in the last letter Orsen had ever received from her. She'd kept them all—eleven letters bundled in oilcloth against her heart, read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. Now she walked upstream along a river that did not seem to know itself, its current sluggish and gray as old dishwater, its banks furred with reeds that had gone black at the tips.

Orsen had been walking for six days. Six days since Marrowgate, since Serah Vane's gray eyes had swept across her like a lantern beam and she had fed her false papers to a brazier's flames and run. The warrant was still pinned inside her mind: *ORSEN HARTWELL, WANTED FOR CRIMES AGAINST THE FAITH.* A name that wasn't even hers. Hartwell was the name on papers bought from a smuggler in Thornfell; her true name she hadn't spoken aloud in four years, not since she'd left Maren's cottage with nothing but a satchel and a promise never to touch the craft again.

She touched it anyway. She couldn't help it. Reading the mountain's moods, reading the river's—it was like breathing. You could swear off breathing about as long as you could hold your nose.

And the river needed reading. Something was wrong with it.

She knelt at the bank on the sixth evening and pressed two fingers to the water, letting the hedge-sense flow through her skin the way Maren had taught her—not magic, exactly, or at least not the kind that burned. Just attention. Just listening with more of yourself than your ears.

The river was *afraid*.

That made no sense. Rivers didn't fear. They ran, they flooded, they froze, they forgot. But this one ran shallow and gray and it flinched beneath her fingers like a whipped dog, and downstream, where the current bent around a fallen willow, she saw why: a slick of something dark that wasn't mud, spreading from the waterline up into the reeds, killing them in a fan-shaped scar.

Godscourge.

Her stomach dropped through the earth. She knew that pattern. She'd learned it at fifteen, hunched over Maren's battered grimoire by candlelight, tracing the fan-shaped blight with one finger while Maren described what the world had been like before the Silence, three centuries ago, when the old gods still answered and the land still bore their fingerprints. *When a god is wounded,* Maren had said, *the wound shows in the ground. Godscourge. It hasn't been seen since the Silence, child. Pray you never see it.*

Someone was wounding gods. And this river ran near—

Orsen stood so fast her knees cracked. The sanctuary. Maren's letters had led here, to the river that forgot its name, and the sanctuary was only a day further upstream. If godscourge stained the water here, then whatever poisoned it came from above.

She ran until dark caught her, then walked, then ran again.

* * *

She smelled the sanctuary before she saw it: woodsmoke, but wrong, sweetish, like meat left too long over green branches. Then she crested the last hill and saw the cottage—or what had been a cottage once, low and stone-walled and tucked into the riverbend like a secret, its herb garden spilling down the slope in ordered rows. Maren had built it before Orsen was born, before the Silence had even finished settling into the world's bones. A hidden place. A safe place.

The door hung open. Half of it, anyway; the other half had been torn from its hinges and lay splintered in the garden, crushing a row of chamomile.

"Maren!" Her voice cracked out of her raw and loud, and she didn't care who heard, didn't care if witch-finders came running, because the smoke-smell was stronger now and there were bootprints in the ash by the hearth—three sets, heavy, military—and blood on the floorboards in a drag-mark leading to the back room.

She found her mentor on the bed beneath the window, and for one merciful heartbeat Orsen thought she was already dead, because Maren lay so still, hands folded, eyes closed, as peaceful as a saint on a tomb lid.

Then the old woman's chest hitched, and her eyes opened, and they found Orsen immediately, as if she'd known, as if she'd been waiting, holding on by will alone.

"There she is," Maren whispered. "There's my girl."

"Maren—" Orsen crossed the room in three strides and fell to her knees beside the bed, and her hands hovered over the old woman because she didn't know where to touch, didn't know where it wouldn't hurt. "What happened? Who—"

"Four men. Witch-finders' boots, but no badges." Maren coughed, and the sound was wet and terrible. "They weren't hunting me, child. They were *feeding* me."

"I don't understand."

"They made me drink it." Maren's hand crawled across the blanket and found Orsen's wrist, grip shockingly strong. "A tincture. Blood and something else, something that sang when I swallowed it. They held my jaw shut till I took it all. Said the gods liked it best from a believer's mouth."

Orsen's skin went cold. "They poisoned you. As a sacrifice."

"As an *offering*," Maren corrected, with the pedantic precision she'd used in lessons, and almost smiled. "There's a difference, girl, and it matters. Poison kills. An offering *feeds*. Someone has been feeding the old gods for years—blood, pain, prayer. That's why they're waking. That's why the mountain moved under you—I heard about Thornfell, I hear everything, don't look surprised—and that's why the crops are blackening. Every answered prayer leaves a scar, and someone is buying answers wholesale."

"The shrine," Orsen whispered. "In the pass. I prayed at a roadside shrine and the mountain—"

"Then it heard *you*, and you should be on your knees thanking every dead god that you're standing here." Maren pulled her closer, and her breath was foul and sweet, the poison working through her. "Listen to me. I haven't got long, and I've wasted too many words on pride already. There's a stone in the loose brick behind the hearth. Third row up, left of center. Get it."

Orsen didn't want to let go of her. She made herself do it. The brick came away easily, greased for removal by decades of practice, and wrapped in the cavity's darkness was a stone the size of a plum, smooth and gray-veined, warm as a living thing.

"Oath-stone," Maren said when Orsen returned. "Older than me. Older than the Silence. Hold it when you pray, and you'll hear which god is listening—which one, and how hungry. Most prayers go nowhere, child. They fall like rain on stone. But someone's dug a channel, deep and wide, and the gods are drinking from it, and a god that drinks grows *wants*, and a god that wants—"

She broke off coughing, and this time there was blood on her lips, black in the firelight.

"—a god that wants will take," she finished. "You have to find the channel. Find who dug it. Stop them before the feeding's done, because when it's done, the gods won't be waking anymore. They'll be *arriving*."

"And I'm supposed to stop that?" Orsen heard her own voice go high and thin. "Maren, I ran from this. Four years ago I swore I'd never—" Her voice failed. "I just wanted an ordinary life. I wanted a house and a garden and to be nobody."

Maren laughed, and it cost her; her whole body shuddered with it. "Oh, my darling fool. Nobody gets an ordinary life. We only get the life we're given, and the question is what we do inside it." Her hand tightened on Orsen's wrist. "I hid too. Forty years in this hole, keeping the craft alive like an ember in cupped hands, telling myself hiding was enough. It wasn't. Hiding was just dying slowly instead of quickly. Don't make my mistake twice."

"Maren, please—"

"You'll want to bury me under the rowan. Do that. And then you'll want to run north, or west, or anywhere, because running is what I taught you best, God forgive me." Her eyes were dimming, but her grip stayed iron. "Don't. Follow the channel. Start with the church—the great church, Caelmere, the cathedral. Whoever feeds gods wears vestments, child. No smuggler, no hedge-witch, no king. Only the faith has the reach, and only the faith would call it devotion."

The firelight guttered. Outside, the river murmured its gray, frightened murmur, and somewhere far off a nightbird called and got no answer.

"Maren," Orsen said, and her voice was very small. "I love you."

"My fierce girl." The old woman smiled, and for a moment she looked forty years younger, the woman from the letters, laughing over her grimoire. "Love you too. Always did. Even when you were hell on my chamomile." Her thumb stroked once across Orsen's wrist, feather-light. "Now take the stone. Take the road. And Orsen—"

She paused, gathering herself, spending her last strength like a miser's final coin.

"When you hear them listening—when the stone goes warm and you feel one of them turn its face toward you—don't flinch. They can smell fear older than language. Stand in your own name. Say it aloud. You spent four years burying it, and it's time you dug it up."

Her hand went slack.

"Maren?"

Nothing. The room was very quiet, except for the fire, and the river, and Orsen's own heartbeat, which sounded suddenly enormous, like something buried trying to dig its way out.

* * *

She buried her under the rowan, as instructed, digging the grave herself with the cottage's worn spade, refusing to use the craft even now, wanting the ache in her arms and the blisters on her palms because pain was honest and grief was not. She laid her in the earth facing the river, packed the soil, set three river-stones at the head because Maren had always said three stones kept the dead oriented, and then sat against the rowan's trunk until dawn came up thin and cold over the hills.

Somewhere in those hours, the grief cooled and hardened into something else. Not peace. Nothing so gentle. It was more like ore smelted down to metal—dense, heavy, edged. She turned it over in her chest and found it had a shape: resolve.

She had run from Thornfell because she'd answered a prayer and a mountain had killed everyone around her. She had run from Marrowgate because a witch-finder looked at her sideways. She had run for four years on the theory that if she was small enough, quiet enough, ordinary enough, the world would leave her alone.

The world had not left Maren alone. The world had held her jaw shut and poured a god's meal down her throat.

Orsen stood at first light, stiff and filthy, and took the oath-stone from her pocket. In the dawn light it looked ordinary, river-gray, humble. But when she closed her hand around it, it warmed against her palm like a sleeping bird, and at the very edge of hearing—beneath the river, beneath the wind, beneath her own pulse—she felt something vast shift its attention, slow as a whale turning in deep water.

Listening.

She thought of the cathedral in Caelmere, weeks of hard road east. She thought of Serah Vane's gray eyes. She thought of a name she hadn't spoken in four years, her true name, buried like the brick-hidden stone.

Orsen Hartwell was a lie she'd wear one more day. Maybe two.

Then she shouldered her satchel, turned her back on the rowan and the river and the grave, and started walking—not away this time, but toward—toward the holy city, toward the channel, toward whatever god had turned its face to the world and begun to drink.

Behind her, faint as memory, the oath-stone pulsed warm against her hip, keeping time with her steps.

Like a second heartbeat. Like something walking with her.

Like something that had heard her name.
