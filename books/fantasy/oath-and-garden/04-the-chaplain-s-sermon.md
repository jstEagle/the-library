## 4. The Chaplain's Sermon

Caelmere smelled of incense and money.

Orsen had never seen so many bells in her life. They hung from every spire of the holy city, great bronze throats that rang the hours, the quarters, the summons to prayer, until she thought her skull would crack like an eggshell. The sound was meant to be comforting, she supposed—a lullaby of order, of a kingdom at peace with its gods. But three centuries ago those same bells had been cast from melted shrine-metal, and Orsen could not look up at them without seeing the old gods' faces hammered flat into ornament.

She kept her hood up and her eyes down and joined the river of pilgrims flowing toward the Great Cathedral of the Concord.

The disguise had cost her nearly everything she carried: a pilgrim's undyed wool robe, a lead badge stamped with the road-shrine sigil, a forged blessing-scroll written in her own careful imitation of clerical script. Her hair she had cropped short with a fish knife in a stable loft, and she still mourned it a little, feeling the wind on her bare neck like an accusation. *Marenne forgive me vanity*, she thought, and then caught herself—*praying again, are you? Careless.* But the words sat warm in her chest anyway, and nothing answered them, and she did not know whether to be relieved or heartbroken.

Inside the cathedral, ten thousand candles burned, and the air itself seemed golden. Pilgrims knelt in ranks upon the pale stone, and above them, on a pulpit carved into the shape of a mountain, stood Royal Chaplain Aldous Renwick.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined, from Serah Vane's wanted-posters and the whispers in Marrowgate, some gaunt fanatic with burning eyes. Instead Renwick was broad and silver-haired, with a grandfather's soft face and a voice like honey poured over iron. He spoke of unity. Of the one true faith that had ended the age of squabbling gods. Of the blight spreading through the lowlands as a test—a trial sent to strengthen the kingdom's devotion.

"The old powers sleep," he said, and his voice rolled down the nave like a tide. "And we do not *wake* what sleeps. We keep the vigil. We keep the bells. We keep each other." His gaze swept the crowd, gentle as a hand on a child's head. "Whoever among you has suffered—whoever has lost fields or family to this darkness—bring your grief here. Lay it on the altar. Grief is the purest offering."

Orsen felt the words slide past her like cold water, almost soothing, almost true—and beneath them something else, something her mentor would have called the *second voice*, the thing a speaker says when his mouth is busy saying something else. She had learned to hear it reading the mountain's moods, reading the shift of wind before rockfall. And Renwick's second voice said: *come closer. Bring me what I need.*

When the sermon ended and the crowd rose to shuffle toward communion, Orsen did not join them. She drifted sideways instead, hood shadowing her face, following the pull of something she couldn't name—the oath-stone, wrapped in cloth against her ribs, humming faintly like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. It had hummed since she entered the cathedral. Since before that, if she were honest. Since Caelmere's walls first rose over the horizon.

It led her down a side corridor behind the reliquary, where the candles thinned and the gold gave way to plain stone, where the pilgrims' murmur faded and only her own footsteps answered her. The stone here was old. Older than the cathedral. Older than the Concord. She knew that age the way she knew weather coming—by taste, by pressure behind the eyes. Someone had built this holy place on top of a place that had been holy first.

At the end of the corridor stood a door of black oak, banded in bronze, and through its seams seeped a smell she knew from her worst memories: blood and burnt feather-smoke and something sweeter underneath, like rotting honey.

The oath-stone flared hot against her skin.

Orsen pressed herself into an alcove as footsteps approached from the far end—two sets, unhurried. She made herself small and silent as the hedge-witch had taught her, breath shallow, heart slowed by a trick of stillness-magic she'd sworn never to use again.

Through the door's seam came Renwick's voice, and he was no longer preaching.

"Three more arrived last night," he said softly. "From Thornfell, if you please. The very mountains. It seems our sleeping friend grows impatient even there."

A pause. Then a second voice—or not a voice at all, but a sound like wind through a bone flute, like a thousand distant wings, like her name being pronounced by something that had no mouth. Orsen's knees went weak. The oath-stone burned through its wrappings and she clamped both hands over it, biting her lip until she tasted copper.

"You will have your solstice," Renwick murmured, patient as a farmer. "But the witch-girl worries me. The Crown's hounds have lost her scent twice now. Twice." A dry chuckle. "No matter. Let her run to me. Every road in Caelmere ends at my altar eventually."

The wing-wind sound swelled, and for one terrible instant Orsen felt it *notice* her—not see, not hear, but taste the edge of her magic through the stone, the way a snake tastes air. She pressed deeper into stillness, deeper than she had ever gone, until she was barely a woman anymore, just a shape of dust and candle-shadow and fear.

The sensation passed. Footsteps receded. And Orsen Hartwell stood alone in a dark corridor of the holiest building in the kingdom, holding proof that the Royal Chaplain spoke nightly with something ancient and hungry—and knowing that if she breathed a word of it to anyone, she would burn as a witch before sundown.

* * *

Serah Vane had stopped believing her reports two weeks ago, and the lie of it was starting to chafe.

She stood in the evidence vault beneath the witch-finders' chapterhouse, surrounded by the collected relics of her own investigation: jars of blackened wheat from Marrowgate, a shard of defaced shrine-stone, sketches of blight-patterns radiating outward in perfect rings. On the table before her lay the warrant for the fugitive Orsen Hartwell, and beside it, the original dispatch ordering her capture.

The ink didn't match.

She had checked seven times. The wax seal was authentic—she'd broken it down to its matrix herself—but the hand that wrote the orders was not the hand of Magistrate Corren, whose signature it bore. The loops were wrong. The spacing was wrong. And the phrasing—"apprentice of the traitor Elowen Marsh, suspected architect of the awakening"—used a word no magistrate used. *Architect.* As though the blight were a building someone had designed.

Witch-finders did not forge documents. That was the whole point of witch-finders: they were the Crown's certainty made flesh. If their paper could lie, then—

Then what? Then the blight had another author. Then the awakening had another cause. Then somewhere above her head—in the chapterhouse, in the palace, in the cathedral whose bells she could hear even through the vault's stone ceiling—someone was steering the hunt away from the truth and toward a runaway girl who, by every honest measure Serah could make, had been in Marrowgate *after* the first shrines were defaced, not before.

"Captain Vane." A novice at the door, nervous. "You're summoned. The Chaplain himself requests your report. Tonight, after compline."

Her stomach dropped. "His Grace the Royal Chaplain takes interest in a borderland blight?"

"He takes interest in *you*," the novice said, and fled.

Serah looked down at the warrant. At the forged, beautiful, lying warrant. Three hundred years of doctrine said the old gods were dead, that magic was a disease, that certainty was armor. But doctrine had never explained why the blight-rings centered not on any witch's hearth, but on the consecrated ground of churches—on places where, if you mapped them carefully enough, the oldest foundations lay beneath the newest altars.

Evidence points upward, her old instructor used to say, you follow it upward. Even if it costs you your rank. Even if it costs you everything.

She folded the warrant into her coat, next to her badge, and went to prepare her report—which would be entirely truthful, entirely incomplete, and delivered while she watched Aldous Renwick's kind grandfather face for the moment it stopped being kind.

* * *

They found each other at dusk, though neither intended it.

Orsen had slipped out of the cathedral district as the bells rang compline, meaning to vanish into the pilgrims' hostels by the river. Instead she rounded a corner onto a processional avenue and walked directly into a cordon of witch-finders sweeping the crowds—six grey cloaks, lanterns raised, checking papers.

And at their center, unmistakable even in the failing light, Captain Serah Vane, holding a sketch of Orsen's own face.

There was nowhere to go. The avenue was walled on both sides; the cordon covered its width. So Orsen did the only thing left: she joined the queue of pilgrims shuffling past the lanterns, head bowed, thumb hooked in her rosary of road-stones, praying with genuine desperation to a goddess who had promised nothing and delivered less.

The line moved. Three pilgrims passed. Two.

Vane looked tired, Orsen noticed absurdly. There were shadows under her eyes and a fresh scratch along her jaw, and she checked papers with the mechanical weariness of a woman doing a job she no longer understood. When Orsen reached her, she held out her forged blessing-scroll without letting her hand shake.

Vane took it. Looked at the sigil. Looked up.

Their eyes met for exactly one heartbeat. Orsen watched recognition arrive like dawn—watched the captain's fingers tighten on the scroll, watched her mouth begin to form the name—and then Vane glanced past her shoulder, at the other witch-finders, at the lanterns, at the crowded avenue full of witnesses.

"*Halt!*" she shouted—but she was already turning, pointing wildly back down the line. "There! Grey robe, running—*stop him!*"

Half the cordon surged away after a hapless dyer's apprentice who had made the fatal error of looking guilty. In the chaos, Vane stepped close to Orsen, and her voice dropped so low only the two of them heard it.

"The warrant against you is forged," she said. "I don't know by whom. I know it isn't you. And tonight I've been summoned to report to the Royal Chaplain, who should have no reason to care about either of us." Her eyes were steady and furious and afraid. "North gate. Tomorrow. An hour past dawn. If you're wise, you'll be far away instead."

She thrust the scroll back into Orsen's hands and walked away shouting orders at the emptying street.

Orsen stood in the middle of the avenue, heart hammering, holding her false papers, watching a witch-finder's grey cloak disappear into the dark.

Every instinct she owned said *run north, run now, alone.* But the oath-stone, warm against her ribs, pulsed once—slow, deliberate, like a nod.

Tomorrow, then. The north gate. An hour past dawn.

Above her, the bells of Caelmere began to ring the night watch, and somewhere behind the cathedral's golden walls, something with a thousand wings turned over in its sleep, listening for her name.
