## 5. Blood in the Reliquary

The reliquary of Caelmere Cathedral smelled of old silver and older lies.

Orsen had expected incense, the cloying sweetness that clung to every corridor above. What she found instead was a dryness, a hush like the inside of a sealed jar—air that had not moved in years and did not wish to. Her candle guttered in it, flame bending toward the door as though even fire wanted out.

She had come down dressed as a pilgrim, gray wool and a traveler's knot at her shoulder, her face smudged with road dust that was real enough. Three days she had walked the cathedral's public halls among the penitents and relic-touchers, counting guards, learning bells, memorizing which doors breathed open and which only pretended to. The letters from her mentor had led her here as surely as they had led her to Marrowgate's blight: *the chaplain collects what should have been buried*, Marenne's voice had said through the oath-stone, cold against Orsen's breastbone even now. *Ask him where he keeps his hunger.*

The reliquary was below the crypts, below even the ossuary, reached by a stair so narrow her shoulders brushed both walls. Someone had wanted this place forgotten. Someone had failed.

Shelves lined the chamber floor to ceiling, and on them sat the gods' leavings—or what the Church called leavings. A stone hand, green with age, its fingers curled around nothing. A bell with no clapper that Orsen's skin remembered ringing. Fragments of shrine-stone wrapped in silk, each one humming faintly against her senses like a struck tuning fork left to die slowly. She had grown up touching stones like these. She knew their voices.

Every piece was awake.

Not fully—not the way the mountain pass had woken under her whispered prayer, shifting rock like a man turning over in bed—but stirring, restless, *listening*. Relics were supposed to sleep for centuries. These had been gathered, arranged, and something about the arrangement kept them half-conscious, the way a shepherd keeps a flock bunched and uneasy.

And beneath the shelves, set into the floor in a pattern Orsen knew from her worst apprenticeship lessons, ran channels. Grooves cut into the living rock, stained dark at their bottoms, converging on a shallow basin at the room's center. She knelt beside it without meaning to. Her fingers found the stain before her courage did.

It flaked under her touch. Rust-brown. Old blood, layered over old blood, layered over older still—years of it, seasons of it, a sediment of sacrifice.

"Oh," she said softly, to no one. "Oh, you stupid brave dead men."

The prisoners. It had to be the prisoners—the ones who vanished from Caelmere's cells, the ones the ledgers marked as *transferred* and never transferred anywhere anyone could follow. Renwick hadn't been hiding them. He'd been pouring them out here, drop by drop, into grooves cut for a god three centuries starved.

The oath-stone burned against her sternum. Not Marenne's voice this time—something rougher, hotter, a pressure like a hand closing around her heart from outside. Vharkun. He could feel her reading his table.

She stood too fast. Her candle swung, shadows lurching across the shelves—and in the lurch she saw what she had missed: fresh scratches on the newest shelf, wood pale where a heavy object had been dragged away recently. And on the wall behind it, chalked in a clerk's careful hand:

*VHARKUN — 47 received. 44 given. 3 held.*

Given. As if the god were owed a debt and Renwick a faithful creditor, paying installments.

Forty-four people. Orsen pressed her palm flat against the basin's rim and let herself feel it, because someone had to. Forty-four small terrified lives, spent one at a time in this dry silent room, and the land itself had begun to scar with every payment—blackened crops, defaced shrines, a mountain pass that folded shut like a wound closing. Every answered prayer left a mark. She had learned that in Thornfell with rock dust still in her teeth.

A sound above. Boot-heel on stone, deliberate, unhurried.

Orsen killed the candle and flattened herself into the gap between two shelves, heart hammering against the oath-stone as if the two were arguing for possession of her chest. The stair brightened. Lamplight spilled down the steps ahead of the boots, and with it came a voice she knew from Marrowgate's warrant-posts, from the market square where she had burned her false papers and run:

"Come out, Hartwell."

Serah Vane. Witch-finder captain, gray coat, gray eyes, a voice like a drawn blade being shown rather than used. Orsen stayed still. Above her, the lamp descended step by step.

"I know you're here," Serah continued. "You've been in my city three days. You bought bread from the baker on Tanner's Row who can't keep his mouth shut. You prayed at the Fountain Shrine—" a pause, and Orsen heard something almost like distaste in it, "—which everyone in this cathedral does, except you actually meant it. I've been following you since the gate."

*Then why haven't you taken me?* Orsen thought, and then, because the stone was warm against her skin and honesty had become a habit she couldn't afford: *Because you already know why I'm here.*

She stepped out of the shadow with her hands visible and her chin up, playing the pilgrim one last time. "Captain Vane. Come to arrest me?"

"That depends." Serah raised the lamp. Its light caught the gray in her close-cropped hair and the exhaustion beneath her eyes—a woman who had not slept well in weeks, Orsen realized, and recognized the look, because she wore it too. "On your answer to one question. Where did you get the name Aldous Renwick?"

Orsen blinked. "I—"

"Don't lie to me. I've hanged better liars." But there was no satisfaction in the words, none of the witch-finder's relish. Just fatigue ground fine as flour. "Three weeks ago I opened an inquiry into missing prisoners. Routine. My superiors closed it in four days and reassigned me to border warrants. Two weeks ago, shrines started weeping black. Ten days ago, the first blight reports. Five days ago, a warrant crossed my desk with your face on it—*Orsen Hartwell, hedge-witch, suspected cause of the awakening.*" Serah set the lamp down on the basin's rim, deliberately, so its light fell across the dark-stained channels. "That warrant is dated nine days before the first blight report. Signed by an archivist who has been dead eleven months."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, the cathedral bells rang the hour, muffled through fathoms of stone.

"You forged it," Orsen said slowly. "Renwick forged it."

"He forged it," Serah agreed, "and I signed the arrest order underneath it, because I didn't look closely enough, and because looking closely at things in this city has recently become hazardous to one's health." She drew something from her coat and held it out: a folded paper, wax seal broken. "So I looked anyway. Prisoner transfers, seven years' worth. Forty-four names. Every one delivered to this cathedral on a holy day, every one received by the Chaplain Royal personally." Her jaw tightened. "I was raised on Renwick's sermons. I gave ten years to a doctrine he wrote half of. Do you understand what you're asking me to believe?"

"I'm not asking you to believe anything." Orsen's voice came out steadier than she felt. "Look at the floor, Captain. Look at the basin. Count the layers if you doubt me—that's blood older than the awakening. Years of it. Whatever woke the gods, it wasn't a hedge-witch with a stolen prayer. It was a man feeding them dinner one course at a time until they remembered they had teeth."

Serah looked at the floor. Orsen watched her do it—the captain kneeling in her gray coat, gloved finger tracing a channel, professional and precise even now, cataloguing evidence that unraveled her whole life. When she rose, her face had gone very still, the stillness of ice before it cracks.

"The war-god," she said. "Vharkun. That's whose name is in the oldest ledger entries. Before the others." She met Orsen's eyes. "You know things, Hartwell. Things no smuggler knows. Things no *pilgrim* knows. What are you?"

What was she? A runaway. A coward, by her own long accounting. The last student of a woman dying in a sanctuary full of poisoned prayers, holding a stone that whispered with a goddess's fear.

"A witch," Orsen said. It cost more than she expected. "A hedge-witch, trained proper, and I've been running from it for six years. And right now I'm the only person in Caelmere who can hear which god is listening—which means I'm the only person who can tell you that Vharkun isn't just awake anymore. He's *hungry*, and your chaplain has been keeping him hungry on purpose. Hunger is control. Feed him just enough and he stays leashed. But the ledger says forty-seven received, forty-four given." She tapped the chalked numbers. "Three held back. Renwick is saving something. And whatever it is, it happens soon—I can feel it in the stone. The gods are waking faster now. Like something's coming to a boil."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, a second bell began to ring—off-pattern, urgent, the alarm peal Orsen had catalogued on her third day and hoped never to hear.

Serah's head snapped toward the stair. "That's the chapter-house call. They ring it when—" She stopped. Her expression did something complicated and terrible. "When the Chaplain Royal wants the guard mobilized."

"He knows I'm here." Orsen was already moving, snuffing the lamp, pulling her pilgrim's shawl up over her hair. "He's known since I touched the basin. The stone told me. Gods gossip, Captain—it's one of the few kind things about them."

"There's a postern stair behind the ossuary racks. Guarded by two men who owe me their careers and dislike Renwick privately." Serah took the lamp, hesitated, then thrust it into Orsen's hands—an act of trust so plain it startled them both. "Don't make me regret this, witch."

"Don't make me regret trusting a witch-finder," Orsen returned, and was surprised to find a ghost of a smile passing between them, brief and wary as two strange dogs circling toward the same fire.

They climbed. Behind them the reliquary settled back into its dry silence, forty-four names written in rust on the floor, three debts outstanding.

At the top of the postern stair, cold night air and the smell of the canal—then, rolling across the holy city from the cathedral's high balcony, a voice amplified by choir-loft acoustics and something less natural, a resonance that made Orsen's teeth ache and the oath-stone sear like a coal against her skin:

*"People of Caelmere! A witch walks among you—one who woke the old horrors, one who desecrates the shrines! Orsen Hartwell is an enemy of the faith, and the faith will not sleep while she breathes!"*

The crowd below roared. Torches bloomed in the streets like fever.

Serah pulled her hood low. "Postern's out. They'll watch the water gates too."

"Then we go the way nobody watches," Orsen said, gripping the stone that burned and sang against her heart. "Down. Through the tombs. There are roads under this city older than the cathedral—and I know how to ask them to open."

The first torchlight appeared at the end of the alley.

* * *

They ran.
