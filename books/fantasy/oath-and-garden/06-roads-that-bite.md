## 6. Roads That Bite

The mountains had teeth. Serah Vane had always known that in the abstract way a city soldier knows things—facts learned from maps and muster rolls, from the grim warnings of veterans who'd served the northern garrisons. But knowing a thing on paper was different from feeling it under her boots, three days out of Caelmere with her lungs full of pine resin and her badge burned to ash behind her.

She had been a witch-finder for eleven years. She had been a traitor for four days.

The thought arrived each morning like a stone dropped into still water, rippling outward through everything she did. *Traitor.* The word should have tasted bitter. Instead it tasted like cold spring water and freedom, and that frightened her more than the zealots at their backs.

"Keep your weight on your heels," Orsen said ahead of her, not turning around. "The scree lies about where it ends."

Serah obeyed. She was learning to do that—to obey the smuggler girl instead of her own training—and every time she did, some small doctrinal stone inside her cracked a little further. Witch-finders were taught that hedge-witches were deceivers by nature, that their words were snares and their knowledge was rot dressed up as wisdom. But Orsen read the mountain the way Serah read a suspect's face: patiently, respectfully, expecting it to lie and knowing how to catch it in the lie.

"How much farther to water?" Serah asked.

"Past the split rock. There's a spring the Crown's maps don't show." Orsen glanced back, and in the slanting afternoon light Serah saw again how young she was—younger than Serah herself, though she carried years like a woman twice her age. "Your maps are good for burning and little else."

"They were never my maps," Serah said quietly. "I just enforced them."

Orsen said nothing to that. She had a way of letting silence do the work of sermons, and Serah was beginning to hate how effective it was.

They came down through a stand of black pines into a fold of the mountain where a stream ran silver over red stone. While they refilled their skins, Serah studied their backtrail out of habit—the ridgeline they'd crossed at dawn, the grey scar of scree, the dark seam of forest. Empty. It had been empty for two days, which meant nothing. Renwick's zealots didn't travel like soldiers. They traveled like weather. You didn't see them coming; you felt the pressure drop and prayed.

*Prayed.* Even her internal vocabulary was betraying her now.

"We should move before dark," she said. "If they've picked up our trail at the river ford—"

"They haven't." Orsen crouched, pressed her palm flat against the streambank mud, and held it there for a long moment. Her eyes went distant, unfocused. When she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, rougher, like something speaking through her rather than from her. "The birds went quiet an hour ago. Something's walking the ridge. Two-legged, many of them, moving wrong. Not hunting us. Herding us."

Serah's hand went to her sword. "Herding us where?"

"That's what worries me." Orsen stood, wiping her hand on her cloak. For the first time since Serah had known her, the smuggler looked uncertain. "There's only one place this road goes if you push someone hard enough. The Hollow Stair. A dead-end canyon. Smugglers call it the Throat because once you're in it, there's no way out but forward or over the top—and the top is a cliff face."

"Then we don't go in."

"There's no other road. They've seen to that." Orsen met her eyes. "Someone told them exactly where we'd be. Someone who knows these passes."

*Renwick,* Serah thought, and then, colder: *or someone feeding him information from inside the witch-finders. Someone who knew I'd run.* She pushed the thought down. There would be time for betrayal later. First, survival.

* * *

They entered the Throat at dusk.

It was less a canyon than a wound in the mountain—a slot of grey stone barely wide enough for two riders abreast, walled so high the sky became a ragged ribbon of dying orange. Their boots echoed strangely, the sound bouncing off stone until it seemed a whole company marched behind them. Water seeped from the walls in dark weeping streaks, and the air smelled of wet iron and old, old cold.

Serah walked with her sword drawn and hated how loud her breathing sounded. Ahead, Orsen moved like smoke, silent even on loose shale.

"They'll come at the narrows," Orsen murmured. "Where the walls pinch tightest. Arrows first, then blades. We won't be able to turn around."

"Then we go through them."

"You've fought zealots?"

"I've arrested them. Broken up their cells. Watched them burn people." Serah's jaw tightened. "I know how they die, if that counts."

"It counts," Orsen said, and there was something almost like respect in it.

The ambush came exactly where Orsen said it would.

A whistle of cloth and rope first—weighted nets dropping from ledges above, meant to tangle and blind. Serah threw herself sideways; one net caught her shoulder and spun her into the wall, and then the arrows came, hissing out of the gloom, and one of them found the gap between her pauldron and her collar.

The pain was white and total. She went down hard on the shale, heard her sword skitter away, heard shouting above—zealot voices crying *unclean, unclean, the witch and her whore-guard*—and understood with terrible clarity that she was going to die in a crack in the earth, far from anything she'd ever believed in, killed by men wearing the same faith she'd worn like armor for eleven years.

Then the world turned green.

Not light—*greenness*, a living color pouring down the canyon walls like liquid, and where it touched, things grew. Moss exploded across bare stone in velvet sheets. Roots burst from cracks thick as a man's arm, lashing out, snatching torches from zealot hands, hurling men against walls with sounds Serah would hear in her dreams for years. Through the chaos she saw Orsen standing in the center of it, arms raised, hair whipping in a wind that existed nowhere else, and her eyes—

Her eyes were the color of deep water, and they were glowing.

*Hedge-magic.* Not the petty charms of the warrants, not the love-potions and luck-knots the Church dismissed as superstition. This was the old thing itself, the thing the doctrine called abomination, and it was saving Serah Vane's life while she lay bleeding on the stones.

The last zealot fled, screaming, back the way they'd come. The green light guttered, dimmed, died. Orsen swayed, caught herself against the wall, and looked down at Serah with an expression of naked terror—not fear of the zealots, but fear of herself, of what she'd just done, of what couldn't now be undone.

"Can you walk?" was all she said.

"Yes," Serah lied, and then, because lying to this woman suddenly felt obscene: "No. Help me."

Orsen helped her. Together, leaning on each other in the dark, they limped deeper into the Throat, away from the ambush, away from everything.

* * *

The cave shrine was hidden behind a curtain of hanging roots, and Serah would have sworn later that Orsen hadn't found it so much as it had let them in.

Inside, it was dry and warm in defiance of the season. The walls were covered in carvings worn nearly smooth by centuries of touching hands—spirals, leaves, faces with closed eyes. In a niche at the back sat a basin of black stone, half-full of rainwater that never seemed to evaporate. Offerings lay scattered around it: dried flowers gone to dust, a child's wooden bird, coins from kingdoms that no longer existed.

"A godshollow," Orsen said, easing Serah down against the wall. "One of the old shrines. Before the Cathedrals, before the Crown, people used to come to places like this to make oaths." She knelt beside Serah, tore open the arrow wound's bandage, and hissed through her teeth. "This needs stitching. Hold still."

"You could have left me," Serah said, as the needle bit. "Back there. Saved your magic for running."

"I could have." Orsen didn't look up from her work. "I spent three years running. It turns out I'm bad at it."

Serah laughed, and the laugh turned into a groan as the needle found a tender depth. "You know what the doctrine says about what you just did. Open working. Unlicensed. That's a pyre offense in nine provinces."

"I know what the doctrine says." Now Orsen looked up, and her ordinary brown eyes held Serah's steadily. "Do you? Because you burned your badge, Serah. Whatever the doctrine says belongs to someone else now."

The words landed like the arrow had. Serah opened her mouth to answer—and stopped, because the basin of black stone had begun to glow.

Faintly at first, like moonlight underwater. Then brighter, the water stirring without wind, rising in slow spirals though nothing touched it. The carved faces on the walls seemed to shift in the glow, their closed eyes becoming somehow more present, more aware.

Orsen went rigid. Slowly, she drew the oath-stone from beneath her shirt—the dead woman's gift, the thing she'd shown Serah once on the road and never explained. It was blazing. White fire trapped in grey stone, pulsing like a heartbeat, faster and faster.

"What is it?" Serah whispered. "What's happening?"

"She's listening." Orsen's voice shook. "Marenne. The goddess of—of rivers, of mercy, of kept promises. The stone hasn't done this since—" She swallowed. "Since my teacher died. Maybe longer."

The glow gathered, concentrated, and for one impossible moment Serah saw it: a shape in the water, vast and sorrowful, a woman made of current and reflection, her features always on the edge of resolving. And though no sound filled the cave, Serah *felt* words the way you feel thunder in your chest—a pressure, a meaning, arriving whole:

***She is watching. She has been watching. And she is afraid.***

The light died. The water stilled. The shrine was only a shrine again, old stone and dust and silence.

Serah realized she was gripping Orsen's arm hard enough to bruise. Neither of them let go.

"Afraid," Serah repeated. Her voice sounded strange in the dark. "Gods aren't afraid. That's the entire point of them. That's what the doctrine—"

"Forget the doctrine," Orsen said quietly. She stared at the oath-stone, still warm in her palm, its light fading like a dying coal. "Something scared a goddess, Serah. Something in Caelmere scared her enough to break three hundred years of silence just to tell us."

Outside the shrine, far off across the mountains, a bell began to ring—or perhaps it was only the wind through the Throat, moaning through stone like a throat itself. But both of them heard it the same way, and neither of them said it aloud:

Three days to the solstice. And whatever was coming, it was coming fast.
