## 7. The God Who Answers

The drowned grove lay three days north of the cave shrine, in a valley that no map admitted to and no road dared approach. Orsen knew it only from her mentor's letters—a place where the river had changed its mind a hundred years ago and drowned the old alder wood rather than abandon it, so that the trees stood knee-deep in black water, dead and alive at once, their silvered branches holding up a sky the color of pewter.

She had not wanted to come. The oath-stone had left her no choice.

Since the shrine, it had not stopped burning. She carried it wrapped in three layers of wool inside her pack, and still she felt it against her spine like a coal, pulsing in a rhythm too slow for a heartbeat and too patient for a drum. *Come*, it said, without words, the way frost says *come* to water. Serah had watched her double over on the second morning, hands pressed to the stone through canvas and wool, and had asked no questions—which was its own kind of answer. The witch-finder was learning that some questions were doors better left shut until you had to walk through them.

Now they stood at the edge of the flooded wood, and the stone had gone quiet at last, the way a held breath goes quiet.

"It's waiting," Orsen said.

"I gathered." Serah's hand rested on her sword hilt out of habit rather than intent; there was nothing in the grove to fight except the light. "Do we knock?"

"You don't knock on a god's door." Orsen pulled off her boots, tied the laces together, and slung them around her neck. "You wade."

* * *

The water was colder than winter had any right to make it, cold enough to ache in her teeth, and it smelled of iron and rot and—beneath those, faint as a memory of a memory—of roses. That was the wrong smell. Nothing rotted into roses. But every few steps the scent rose around her, sweet and funereal, and Orsen understood that she was walking through something's grief, and that the grief was older than the drowning.

Serah followed, sword drawn now, eyes moving constantly. "Three centuries," she said quietly. "That's what the doctrine says. The gods went silent after the Oathbreaking. No voice, no sign, no miracle. The Church built its whole foundation on that silence." Her laugh had no humor in it. "And now I'm standing in a swamp because a rock told a smuggler to."

"A hedge-witch."

"Is there a difference?"

Orsen didn't answer. Ahead of them, the alders thinned into a ring, and within the ring the water opened into a pool so still it looked poured rather than fallen. In its center stood a single tree—not drowned like the others but rooted in an island of black mud no wider than a grave—and from its branches hung offerings: ribbons gone gray with age, a child's shoe, a rusted bell, a crown of woven reeds that might have been fresh that morning.

The oath-stone flared against her chest, and the pool answered.

Light rose from beneath the water—not reflected, *generated*—a green-gold glow like sunlight remembered by the deep. The surface trembled without wind. And then the tree moved, or something wearing the tree moved, and a figure stepped down from the island across the water as though the water were a floor, as though gravity were a suggestion it had politely declined.

Marenne came as a woman made of the grove itself. Her gown was river-silt and lily-root; her hair fell in dark braids threaded with drowned moss; her face was beautiful the way a flood is beautiful, which is to say with total indifference to what it ruins. Where her bare feet touched the water, the surface did not ripple. It *recoiled*.

"Orsen Hartwell." The goddess's voice was the sound the mountain makes when it settles in the night—the sound Orsen had spent her whole apprenticeship learning to read. "Daughter of the last hedge. You carry my sister's stone. You carry her death in your mouth. Speak it."

So Orsen spoke it. The sanctuary, the poisoned well, the way Marenne's priestess—Ilsa, who had raised her, who had taught her to read the moods of stone and stream—had died with the oath-stone pressed into her palm and the truth pressed into her ear: *they are feeding them, girl. Prayers answered in blood. Find the drowned grove. Find her.* She told it plainly, because grief had burned the poetry out of her weeks ago, somewhere between Marrowgate's blackened fields and the warrant with her false name on it.

Marenne listened without moving. When Orsen finished, the goddess said, "You fled us. Three years ago you buried your gifts under a smuggler's name and ran south. Why should I tell you anything?"

"Because I came anyway." Orsen's voice shook, and she let it. "Because Ilsa sent me, and I'm the only one she could send. Because you're afraid." She lifted her chin. "I felt it in the shrine. The stone wasn't calling me. It was *begging*."

Something crossed the goddess's face that on a mortal would have been called pain. The water around them dimmed.

"Yes," Marenne said. "I am afraid. Sit, if your legs will hold. What I have to say is long, and it is worse than you think."

They sat—or Orsen sat on a half-drowned root while Serah remained standing, rigid, her doctrine visibly at war with her survival instinct. Marenne did not sit. Gods, Orsen suspected, did not sit in the presence of what they were about to confess.

"You know the story the Church tells," the goddess began. "That we broke our oaths. That we abandoned you. That silence was our punishment and your freedom." Her eyes were the green of deep water over deep stone. "The truth is quieter. We grew tired. Not of you—never of you—but of being *needed*. Every prayer is a hand on your shoulder. Imagine ten thousand hands, a hundred thousand, all pulling. So we slept. We let the world learn to stand alone, and we sank into the roots of things, and for three hundred years we dreamed."

"And now?" Serah asked.

"Now someone has found the door back in." Marenne's voice dropped, and the whole grove seemed to lean closer. "There is a man in Caelmere. Your chaplain. Your holy man with his relics and his sermons on unity. For years he has been sending us prayers—not whispered hopes, not children's wishes. *Sacrifices.* Blood, given freely by his hand, offered in the old forms, the forms from before the Oathbreaking. Do you understand what that means, hedge-daughter? A prayer answered costs the land. A prayer answered in blood costs the *prayed-to*. He has been paying Vharkun in coin we cannot refuse."

"Refuse how?" Orsen asked. "You're gods."

"We are *old*," Marenne said, and for the first time the goddess sounded tired, anciently tired, tired the way mountains are tired. "Vharkun slept deepest and woke angriest. Each sacrifice cracked his sleep a little wider. Each answered prayer gave him a taste of the world again—war here, a fire there, a battle turned. He does not distinguish, child. War is war. A skirmish feeds him as a siege does. And Renwick knows this. He thinks he is steering a weapon." Her lip curled, and the water shuddered. "He is not steering anything. He is *feeding*."

"Feeding him toward what?" Serah's voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman holding herself together with procedure.

"Toward waking. Fully. And when Vharkun wakes sated, he will not be content with skirmishes. He remembers ruling. He remembers altars running red from coast to coast, and kings who were priests, and mortals who knelt because kneeling was law. He will drag us with him—all of us, whether we will it or no. A god roused in blood calls his kin to war the way a struck bell calls its sisters to ring. We will fight to reclaim the mortal world, and the mortal world will burn, and the land will scar with every answered prayer until there is nothing left worth ruling." Marenne spread her hands, silt and lily-root. "We cannot stop him. We are what he is. You cannot kill him. If he dies, we all die, and the land dies with us—the old gods are the roots of this world, hedge-daughter. Cut the root and the garden goes with it."

"There's always a third option," Orsen said. It came out harder than she intended.

"There is not." Marenne crouched then, bringing her terrible face level with Orsen's own, close enough that Orsen could see the drowned leaves turning slowly in the goddess's eyes. "There is one door and one key. An oath-keeper. A mortal bound willingly—not claimed, not taken, *given*—to all of us, the sleeping and the waking both. Such a one stands between god and god, holds our channels in her two hands, and may seal them or open them. With an oath-keeper, Vharkun can be bound back into sleep without killing the root. Without one—" she straightened— "there is only the burning, or the slow dying. Choose."

The word landed on Orsen like a stone dropped from a great height.

*Choose.* As though it were simple. As though she had not spent three years clawing her way out of exactly this—out of shrines and stones and voices in the water, out of being needed, out of hands on her shoulders. She had buried her gifts in a smuggler's name and thought the grave would hold. Now a goddess stood in front of her asking her to climb back into her own skin and stay there forever, to be the hinge of heaven, to hear the gods behind her eyes until she died.

"And if I say no?" Orsen asked.

"Then pray someone else says yes before the solstice." Marenne's gaze flicked, briefly, to Serah—to the badge-shaped absence where a witch-finder's insignia used to sit. "You have perhaps a season. Perhaps less. Renwick grows bolder. The blight spreads. And Vharkun dreams louder every night."

"No."

The word fell into the pool and the pool swallowed it. Marenne went very still.

"Not yet," Orsen said, and stood, water streaming from her skirts, her voice steadier than she felt. "You want a willing oath. Willing means chosen, and chosen means I've seen everything—what it costs, what it binds, what breaks. You've shown me the shape of the cage, lady, but not the bars. I won't swear my life away on an afternoon's sermon, even yours. Give me until I've seen what Renwick's ritual truly requires. Give me until I know there's no other pair of hands." She met the goddess's eyes. "Then I'll choose. I swear it by the stone I carry and the woman who died giving it to me."

For a long moment the grove made no sound at all. Then Marenne smiled, and it was the worst thing Orsen had ever seen—beautiful and furious and ancient, a smile like ice cracking on a spring river.

"Mortals," the goddess said softly. "Always bargaining at the edge of the flood." She turned, wading back toward the island tree without disturbing a single drop. "Very well, hedge-daughter. Take your season. Take your days. But know this: every hour you spend deciding, Vharkun spends feeding. And when you finally kneel to swear—and you will, because there is no third path and you know it in your bones—do not expect me to remember that you made me wait."

The light beneath the water guttered out. The figure became the tree became the island became a dead alder in a drowned wood, and the pool lay still and pewter-dark, holding nothing but sky.

Orsen stood in the freezing water a long time, shaking, the oath-stone warm against her heart like a small accusing sun.

Serah waded to her side. "Well," the witch-finder said at last. "What do we do now?"

Orsen looked north, toward Caelmere, where a holy man was counting prisoners like coins. "Now," she said, "we go tell a queen her chaplain is feeding a war-god—and pray to every silent god listening that we're not already too late."
