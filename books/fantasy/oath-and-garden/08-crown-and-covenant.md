## 8. Crown and Covenant

**Serah**

The road to Caelmere had never seemed so long, nor so short. Serah rode like a woman pursued, because she was—though the pursuit was not yet visible behind them, only ahead, waiting in the white towers of the holy city where her whole life had been spent in service.

Three days of hard riding through the hill country, and Orsen had not once complained. The witch sat a horse badly and slept worse, waking at every hour to press the oath-stone between her palms as though checking whether it still beat. It did. Serah could feel it too now, faintly, like a second pulse under her own—a thing she refused to name, because naming it would mean admitting what she'd seen in that drowned grove, and what she'd seen could not be reconciled with thirty years of doctrine.

"The gates will have our descriptions," Orsen said at dusk on the third day, as Caelmere's spires rose out of the haze like needles through cloth. "Renwick's had time."

"He'll have more than descriptions." Serah pulled her cloak tighter. In her saddlebag lay the forged warrant they'd taken from the reliquary archives—her own seal copied by an unsteady hand, the queen's cipher slightly wrong, wrong in exactly the way a forger would get it wrong if he wanted the forgery found. "He wants us caught. He wants us dragged into the cathedral square in chains so the crowd can see the blight-makers brought to justice. He's been building toward this sermon for years."

"Then we don't go through the gates."

"We go through the gates." Serah met her eyes. In the fading light the witch looked young and exhausted and utterly unafraid, which frightened Serah more than any zealot's blade. "Because the queen has to hear it from someone standing in front of her, not from a smuggler's rumor. Because half the guard still trusts me. And because if we slink in through the smugglers' tunnels you know so well, Renwick wins before we've said a word."

Orsen held her gaze a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and turned her horse toward the road.

* * *

**Orsen**

The city smelled wrong.

Orsen had grown up learning to read places the way other people read faces—the hedge-witch's first lesson, older than any spell. Marrowgate had smelled of fear and ash. Caelmere smelled of incense laid over rot, thick enough to taste: frankincense and myrrh poured over the canals in quantities meant to smother something. Beneath it, faint but unmistakable to anyone who knew what to smell for, the black-sweet reek of godscourge.

And beneath everything, so low she almost missed it, a voice.

Not words. Not yet. A pressure against the inside of her skull, rhythmic as a drum, patient as tide. The oath-stone burned cold against her sternum. *Vharkun*, she thought. *He's here. He's been here all along, under the cathedral, drinking.*

They came through the south gate at full dark, hooded, in the middle of a grain caravan whose owner owed Serah a favor from better days. The gate guards barely glanced at them—but posted beside the customs ledger, larger than any warrant Orsen had ever seen, hung a broadsheet bearing two faces rendered in crude woodcut.

Hers. And Serah's.

*ORSEN HARTWELL, HEDGE-WITCH AND BLIGHT-MAKER,* the print declared. *SERAH VANE, TRAITOR TO THE FINDERS. ARREST ON SIGHT BY ORDER OF THE QUEEN. REWARD OF FIFTY GOLDEN MARKS.*

"Fifty marks," Serah murmured beside her. "I'm insulted. I was worth eighty when I joined."

But her joke had no floor under it. Orsen watched her stare at the broadsheet—at the queen's cipher, genuine this time, unmistakable—and saw something go out of her face. Not surprise. Confirmation. The last door closing.

"She signed it," Serah said quietly. "She actually signed it. Twelve years I wore her badge. I put down the Kettering riots for her father. I—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "He got to her first. That's all. We just have to get past him to her."

"Serah." Orsen touched her arm. Around them the gate traffic flowed, farmers and drovers and pilgrims, none of them looking twice at two women in hooded cloaks—yet. "If we're seen, there's no talking our way out. You know that."

"I know that."

"Then say what you're going to do, because I can see you deciding something, and I've learned the hard way that your decisions tend to involve fire."

For the first time in three days, Serah Vane smiled. It was not a comforting smile.

* * *

**Serah**

They lodged that night in the Undercroft, the tangle of cellars and smugglers' runs beneath the Tanner's Quarter where Orsen clearly knew every stone by its first name. Serah sat on a straw pallet and cleaned her sword and did not sleep, and near midnight she made her decision, and having made it, found she could breathe again.

At dawn she dressed in her full witch-finder's uniform—the grey coat, the silver badge of the Crown's circle-and-flame polished bright—and walked out of the Undercroft alone.

"You're mad," Orsen hissed after her. "Serah—"

"They need to see me," Serah said. "All of me. What's left of me." She settled the badge at her throat one last time. "Keep to the shadows. Watch the gate square at midmorning. And if it goes wrong—" she paused, then forced herself to finish, "—don't die for me. Die for the realm. It's bigger."

The Gate of Pilgrims opened onto the great square where Caelmere's morning market spilled in a river of noise and color. By midmorning the crowd was thick, and the guard detachment at the gatehouse numbered twenty, and among them, she noted with a cold clarity, at least eight faces she knew by name. Men and women she'd trained. Men and women who had eaten at her table.

She walked into the center of the square and stopped, and let them see her.

It took less than a minute. A young guard—Pell, barely nineteen, still soft at the jaw—went rigid, hand flying to his whistle. "*Vane.* Captain Vane—you're—you're under arrest, ma'am, there's a warrant, I—"

"I know," Serah said, and her voice carried the way it had carried across a hundred mustering yards, the voice that did not ask for quiet but took it. "I know the warrant. I wrote half the laws it breaks. So hear me, all of you, before you whistle for the rest."

The square was going silent around them, market-stalls hushing, faces turning. Somewhere above, bells began to mark the hour and thought better of it.

"For twelve years I hunted witches for the Crown. I believed every warrant I served was justice. Three weeks ago I followed a blight trail across half the kingdom, and I found its source—not in a hedge-cottage, not in a smuggler's camp, but in the holiest house in Caelmere. Royal Chaplain Aldous Renwick has been feeding prisoners to the old gods for years. He is feeding one right now. The blight on your fields, the dead shrines, the blackened crops—it comes from *him*. And he has written warrants against the only two people trying to stop him, and the queen has signed them, because he reached her first."

A murmur ran through the crowd like wind through wheat. Pell's hand still hovered at his whistle, trembling.

"You don't have to believe me," Serah went on. "Believe the evidence. Believe the forged warrant in the reliquary archive with my seal copied wrong. Believe the prison rolls—forty-one prisoners transferred to the cathedral crypts over six years, none ever released, none ever buried. Go look. The records are open to any sworn officer. *Go look.*"

She reached up to her collar.

"But I won't ask you to choose between your oaths and my word while I wear this." Her fingers found the silver badge, the circle-and-flame she had kissed the day she took her vows, and something in her chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there. "So I'm returning it."

She ripped the badge free. Cloth tore. And she dropped it to the cobbles, drew her dagger, and set the flame of it alight with the flint from her pouch, and the silver circle-and-flame of the Crown's witch-finders melted and blackened in the morning light while five hundred people watched and no one breathed.

When it was done she straightened, and her voice, when it came, was quieter and somehow louder both.

"I am still sworn to the realm. To its people, its fields, its children. Just no longer to the men who feed it to their god. Any officer who wants the truth—come find me. Any officer who wants to arrest me—" she spread her empty hands, "—come do it. But do it knowing what you're arresting me *for*."

Silence. One heartbeat. Two.

Then Pell lowered his hand from his whistle. And beside him, slowly, deliberately, Sergeant Ammas—one of hers, grey at the temples, twenty years of service—took off his helmet and tucked it under his arm, which in the grammar of the Caelmere guard meant exactly one thing.

Half the detachment followed him. Half did not, and those half backed away, whistles finally shrieking, toward the cathedral.

* * *

**Orsen**

By nightfall the city knew. Broadsheets were torn down and rewritten; new ones went up by torchlight, some calling Serah a traitor, others—more of them than Orsen would have dared hope—calling her *honest*. The Undercroft filled with whispered visitors: guardsmen, a disgraced archivist, a canal-woman whose brother had vanished into the cathedral crypts six years ago and whose grief had gone to iron.

And at dusk, a boy arrived with no message, only a scrap of parchment covered in a hand Orsen recognized from a hundred sermons' worth of printed piety. Renwick's hand. One line:

*The solstice comes early this year. Three nights hence, the war-god drinks his fill. Bring the oath-stone if you wish to watch.*

Orsen read it twice. Then she walked to the canal-side where Serah stood watching the torches move on the far bank, and handed it to her without a word.

Serah read it. Read it again. When she looked up, her face was ash-pale in the torchlight, but her eyes had gone hard and bright as quenched steel.

"Three days," she said. "He's moving the ritual up. The solstice isn't for a month."

"He doesn't need the real solstice anymore," Orsen said, and the oath-stone pulsed against her chest like a second heart, cold and eager. "He's got forty-one sacrifices' worth of momentum. He just needs enough blood to tip the scale—and he means to use the whole city's faith as the bowl to catch it."

Above them, across the black water, the cathedral spires clawed at the sky, and somewhere deep beneath them something ancient rolled over in its sleep and smiled.

"Then we have three days," Serah said, "to break into the most guarded building in the kingdom and stop a god from being born."

Orsen closed her hand around the stone, and felt it pulse again—once, twice—like a knock at a door.

"No," she said quietly. "We have three days to decide which side of that door we're standing on when it opens."
