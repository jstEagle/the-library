## 10. Oath and Garden

The gods did not leave her. That was the first thing Orsen learned about keeping an oath: it was not a door she had opened but a window that could never again be shuttered.

They came to her in the quiet hours, faint as breath on glass. Marenne's presence was the smell of rain on river stones. Vharkun, even sleeping, was a low iron taste at the back of her throat, like a coin held too long in the mouth. The smaller gods drifted through her dreams—Hesset of the hearth-smoke, old Tallow-and-Thorn, the nameless one who watched over crossroads—and each of them left a small weight behind her eyes when they passed, a pressure like the moment before tears that never quite fell.

She woke before dawn in the guest chamber of Caelmere's palace, where the Crown had lodged her after the burning of the spire, and lay listening to the city breathe beneath her window. Somewhere below, bells were being recast. Somewhere else, masons were already arguing about how to rebuild what she had broken.

*What I saved,* she corrected herself, and then, honestly: *what I broke in order to save.* The spire had come down like a felled tree. Renwick had gone with it, his last prayer cut off mid-word, his channel severed so cleanly that the war-god had simply—collapsed. Vharkun slept now, and would sleep, because Orsen Hartwell held his leash and the leashes of every god his hunger might have woken.

She pressed her palms against her eyes until the pressure behind them eased. It never fully went away anymore. She suspected it never would.

* * *

The queen received her on the third day, in a hall still scented with smoke from the solstice fires.

Queen Isolde was younger than her portraits and more tired than her crown allowed anyone to see. She sat not on the throne but in a plain chair beside it, which Orsen took as either humility or calculation, and with Serah Vane standing at her shoulder—which Orsen took as both.

"Orsen Hartwell." The queen's voice carried the particular flatness of someone who had rehearsed this speech and hated every line of it. "Witch-finder records name you a fugitive. My chaplain's sermons named you an enemy of the faith. And my own eyes watched you kneel atop a burning cathedral and pull a god back from the edge of the world." She folded her hands. "I am told these things cannot all be true."

"They can," Orsen said. "That's rather the point of me."

A flicker at the corner of the queen's mouth—there and gone. "The realm owes you a debt it does not know how to pay. So I will offer what I know how to offer. A title. Lands in the northern valleys. A place at court, if you want it—or a place above it, if you'd prefer that no one ever again raise a warrant with your name on it."

Behind the queen, Serah said nothing. But Orsen felt her attention like sunlight through a crack in a door, steady and warm and waiting.

"I'm grateful," Orsen said slowly, "and I'm going to refuse all of it."

The hall went very quiet. A courtier near the pillars made a small strangled sound, as though refusing a queen were a physical injury.

"The Crown is shaken," Orsen went on, before Isolde could speak. "Shaken crowns reach for anything solid, and right now I'm the most solid thing in your kingdom—a woman who can bind gods. In a year, I'd be your instrument. In five, your weapon. In ten, someone else's Renwick, preaching unity while feeding the throne things it shouldn't eat." She held the queen's gaze. "You don't need an oath-keeper at court. You need to remember why oaths exist. Let me do that from far away, where you can't use me and I can't be used."

Isolde studied her for a long moment. Outside, the recasting bells rang a half-formed note.

"And if the gods wake again?" the queen asked. "If something stirs them? What am I to do then—write you a letter?"

"Pray," Orsen said. "For the first time in three hundred years, someone will actually hear you." She bowed—not deep, not shallow, the bow of one keeper to another sovereign—and turned for the doors.

Serah fell into step beside her in the corridor, badge-less, sword at her hip, wearing the expression of a woman who had already packed.

"You're insufferable," Serah said.

"I've been told it's a hedge-witch trait."

"I told the queen I'd see you safely out of the city. Officially. On the record." Serah's mouth curved. "Unofficially, I resigned last night. There's a road north I'm told only a smuggler knows."

Orsen felt something in her chest loosen—the first true ease since the spire. "It bites," she warned.

"Good. I've been bitten by worse lately."

* * *

They went north as the autumn turned, two women and a mule named Turnip, walking roads that remembered Orsen's feet and, she suspected, had begun to remember her name.

Thornfell in fall was a country of copper and smoke. The passes had healed strangely since the day the mountain buried her caravan—scars of raw stone knitting themselves shut with lichen faster than any natural order should allow, as though the land itself were embarrassed by its wounds. Where shrines had stood defaced, travelers had begun leaving offerings again: bread, buttons, a child's carved horse. Small faiths, returning like birds after a hard winter.

And always, faintly, the weight behind her eyes. The gods murmuring in their sleep. Some nights it was almost companionable, like sleeping in a house full of breathing people. Some nights it was a stone laid on her chest, and she lay awake counting the shapes of their dreams until dawn.

On the twentieth day they reached the high valley where Marenne had once kept a drowned grove, and where, in a cottage under a leaning rowan, Orsen had been raised by a woman the world had called a hedge-witch and Orsen had called Mother.

The grave was where she had left it, marked by a cairn of river stones. Orsen knelt and pulled off her gloves and put her bare hands into the cold earth, and let herself grieve properly at last—all the grief she had postponed through Marrowgate and Caelmere and fire, all of it rising now like groundwater.

"I brought the oath-stone back," she said aloud, when she could speak. "I know you told me to run from it. I know you spent your whole life running so I wouldn't have to carry what you carried." She set the stone on the cairn, where it caught the light like a chip of fallen sky. "I'm sorry. Someone had to keep the door."

The wind moved through the rowan. Behind her eyes, faint and unmistakable, Marenne sighed—the sound of rain beginning somewhere far upstream.

"She heard you," Serah said quietly. She had built a small fire without being asked; the smell of woodsmoke curled into the blue evening. "I'm learning to tell."

"It gets easier to hear them. Never easier to carry them." Orsen wiped her face with her wrist and managed something like a smile. "That's the bargain. That's the whole wretched bargain."

"Then we'd better plant something," Serah said. "Bargains go better with gardens."

* * *

They planted in the grey light before the first frost, working the soil over the cairn with their hands and a borrowed spade. Orsen chose seeds the way her mentor had taught her—not by catalog but by conversation, holding each packet up to the fading light and asking, silently, *who wants to grow here?*

The land answered. Not in words—in leanings. A tug toward foxglove along the north border. A warmth for sage. When she opened the packet of marigolds, the pressure behind her eyes brightened like a struck match, and she laughed for the first time in weeks, a rough, surprised sound.

"Hesset," she explained to Serah. "Goddess of hearth-things. She likes orange."

"Of course she does."

They worked until dark, and by lantern-light after, setting bulbs deep where spring would find them, tucking the roots of a young rowan sapling beside its mother tree. Orsen whispered over each handful of earth—not spells, exactly. Promises. *Grow. Be tended. Be witnessed.* The oldest magic there was, older than gods: the magic of showing up for a thing season after season.

When the last seed was in, she stood in the dark garden with dirt to her elbows and Serah's shoulder warm against hers, and listened.

The valley breathed. The rowan creaked. Far below, a night bird called across the black folds of Thornfell, and somewhere in the sleeping world beneath everything, the gods turned over in their long slumber, and murmured, and were, for now, at peace.

"You'll stay, then," Serah said. Not quite a question.

"I'll keep the roads," Orsen said. "The smuggler paths, the shepherd tracks, the shrine-trails. Someone has to know them, now that the mountains have started answering prayers again. And I'll keep the secret—" she touched the oath-stone in her pocket, cool as a riverbed—"and I'll keep the oath. All three. Every day, for as long as I'm able."

"That's a heavy ledger."

"It's mine, though." She looked up. Above the peaks, the stars were coming out in their ancient indifference, and among them—fainter, newer, unmistakable—she could feel the small bright attention of things that knew her name. "Mine to keep."

Serah's hand found hers in the dark, calloused and certain.

They stood together in the garden over the grave, two women and a sleeping god's dream and a rowan holding its leaves against winter, and Orsen Hartwell felt the weight behind her eyes settle into something almost like company.

Then, from the direction of the pass, faint but clear, came the tolling of a bell that no one had hung there—

and behind her eyes, Vharkun stirred in his sleep, and murmured a single word:

*hunger.*

Orsen tightened her grip on Serah's hand and turned toward the mountains, already reaching for her boots.

The oath, it seemed, had heard it too.
