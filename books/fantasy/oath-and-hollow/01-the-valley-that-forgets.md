## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The gate did not look like much.

That was the first lesson Mirabel Kavanagh had ever learned, though she could not remember learning it — it had simply always been there in her, the way the shape of her own hands was there. The gate was a slab of grey stone set into the valley's western cliff, twice the height of a man, wide as a barn door, and utterly unremarkable to anyone who did not know what they were looking at. No hinges. No handle. No seam where a door might open. Only the stone, and the faint ring of older stone around it, fitted so precisely that rain ran off its face without ever darkening the rock behind.

To the village below it was called the Old Wall, or sometimes just *that thing up there*, said in the flat tone people used for weather. To Mirabel it was the Gate, capital letter, spoken only inside her own skull, because she was the last person alive who still spoke to it at all.

She climbed to it as she did every morning, before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, while the mist still pooled in the hollows of the valley like spilled milk. The path was not a path — no one else walked it, so no one else had worn it — but her feet knew the way the way her heart knew its own rhythm: up through the birch scrub, along the shelf of rock where the goats would not go, over the fallen slab her grandmother had called the Sleeping Stone and which Mirabel privately thought looked more like a loaf of bread left out too long. At the top, the cliff shoulder flattened into a shelf, and the Gate stood against the mountain's flank with the patience of something that had been standing there since before patience had a name.

She knelt.

The ground was cold through her skirts even in high summer; it was always cold here, in a circle perhaps ten paces wide, as though the mountain kept a patch of winter folded away beneath the grass for reasons of its own. She took the salt from the crock she'd carried up — coarse grey salt, cut from the pans at Sallowmere three days' walk east, traded for wool and never spoken of beyond a nod between herself and the salt-wife, who either understood perfectly what it was for or understood nothing at all and preferred it that way.

"Morning," Mirabel said.

She said it every morning. She had said it every morning for twenty-six years, first trailing after her mother on legs too short for the climb, then alone. She did not expect an answer. She had stopped expecting answers somewhere around her fourteenth year, when her mother died at this exact spot — kneeling, hands flat on the stone, mid-sentence — and Mirabel had inherited the conversation the way other daughters inherited a loom or a recipe book.

She poured the salt in a line across the base of the Gate. Not a circle — a line, edge to edge, because a circle was a wall and the salt was meant to be a threshold, a courtesy, a *here is your portion* laid out for something that lived on the far side of the stone. The grains hissed very softly as they settled, almost too quiet to hear. Almost.

Then came the silence.

This was the harder half of the ritual, and the part that made her proud, though pride was a strange thing to feel about sitting still. She folded her hands in her lap, straightened her back, and gave the Gate her silence: no prayer, no song, no stray humming, not even the little mental arithmetic she usually let run in the background like a mouse in a wall. Just emptiness, offered up like a dish held out to someone who might or might not be hungry. Ten minutes in summer, longer in the dark months. Her mother had taught her that the silence was the true food — that salt was only the plate it was served on.

*It doesn't want your words,* her mother had said once, in the voice she used for the few explanations she allowed. *Words are doors. It has enough doors.*

Mirabel had asked, age seven or so, what was on the other side.

Her mother had been quiet for a long time. Long enough that seven-year-old Mirabel had decided the answer must be *nothing*, because surely any real thing — a monster, a god, another country — would have gotten an answer faster than that.

*Empty,* her mother had finally said. And then, quieter, in a tone Mirabel hadn't understood until much later: *Thank all the quiet gods, it's empty.*

She finished the silence now, opened her eyes, and brushed the salt from her palms. The ritual was done. The day could begin.

Below her, the valley was waking, and the valley was beautiful, and the valley did not know her.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of her life, load-bearing and invisible, like the Gate itself. Forty-one households in Hollowmere village, two hundred-odd souls who grew barley and raised sheep and quarried slate from the eastern face, and every one of them knew that the Kavanagh woman went up the mountain every morning and came down every afternoon, and not one of them asked why. It was not a conspiracy. Conspiracies required agreement, meetings, the risk of words. This was something subtler and, Mirabel suspected, far older: a valley-shaped hole in everyone's curiosity. Children were told not to play near the Old Wall the way children were told not to lick frozen iron — as a fact of the world, requiring no reason. Farmers turned their sheep west of the cliff shelf without being told to. The salt-wife weighed out her grey salt each season and never once met Mirabel's eye while doing it.

They had forgotten, her mother said. On purpose. Generations ago, deliberately, the way you'd burn a letter you couldn't afford to have found. *Forgetting is a kind of work,* she'd said. *They've been doing it so long they don't even know they're doing it anymore. That's how good they got.* And Mirabel's line — the keepers — were the only ones who remembered, because someone had to, and memory like theirs didn't pass down through words. It passed down through blood, arriving fully formed in each generation like a scar that had always been there.

So Mirabel bought salt and sold wool and mended fences and was, to the valley, a solitary, slightly grim woman who lived at the valley's edge and kept odd hours. They were polite to her. They were polite to her the way you're polite to the executioner or the plague-gravedigger — warmly enough, distantly, with a careful inch of space maintained at all times. She was invited to no weddings. When old Tammas Vey died two winters past, she'd stood at the back of the churchyard and no one had stood beside her.

Some mornings this hurt. Most mornings, if she was honest — and the mountain made her honest, there was no one up here to perform for — she wore the distance like a coat that fit. Because the alternative to being apart from them was being responsible for them in a way that sat in the chest like a swallowed stone, and the distance let her pretend, for a few hours each evening, that she was just a woman, and not a hinge.

Because that was the truth under everything, the truth her mother had given her in fragments over twenty years and which Mirabel had assembled herself, alone, the way you assemble a lamp in the dark by touch: the Gate was sealed, and the seal was blood, and her blood was the last of it, and whatever pressed against the far side of that grey stone pressed against it every hour of every day, patiently, the way water presses at a dam. Patiently. Hungrily.

And it was empty. Her mother had said so. *Empty,* in that strange grateful voice, and Mirabel had built her whole understanding on that word, because the alternative was unthinkable.

She shouldered her empty salt crock and started down.

* * *

Midday she spent in the village, because even hinges needed flour.

Hollowmere in autumn smelled of woodsmoke and cut barley and the sour-sweet rot of apples nobody bothered to pick from the trees along the millrace. Mirabel moved through it with her basket on her arm, nodding to the people who nodded to her, keeping to her careful inch. Marta Hale was beating rugs outside the smithy and gave her the smile-without-warmth reserved for the Kavanagh woman. The baker's boy, Joss, all elbows and eleven years old, stared at her openly the way children did — not afraid, exactly, but drawn, the way you stare at a dog that might bite. He was the only one in the valley whose forgetting hadn't quite taken. His grandmother had come from over the mountains, Mirabel had deduced years ago; forgetting, apparently, was a local craft.

"Marta," Mirabel said, "have you seen anything strange? In the fields, the sky?"

Marta's rug-beating slowed by exactly one beat. "Strange how?"

"Birds."

"We've birds." Marta resumed beating, harder than necessary. Dust rose. "We're rich in birds."

"I mean birds that shouldn't be here. Big ones. Hunting birds."

Something flickered behind Marta's eyes — not fear, Mirabel noted, but the specific discomfort of a woman feeling her forgetting strain against a question. "Can't say I have. Why would I?" And the shutters came down, pleasant and blank, and Mirabel understood the conversation was over before it had begun.

She hadn't expected anything else. But she had needed to ask, because of the hawk.

She had seen it yesterday, and again this morning, and it was wrong in a way she was still struggling to name. Hawks were not impossible in the valley — a kestrel worked the eastern slopes most summers, and once she'd seen a pair of buzzards riding the thermals over the ridge like scraps of paper on a slow wind. But those birds had belonged here, in the way everything in the valley belonged: contained, modest, part of the valley's closed and self-forgetting economy.

This hawk did not belong. It was too large for a start — a broad-winged, pale-chested thing that hung above the western crags in long patient circles, and it circled the way a sentry walks a wall: methodically, in overlapping arcs, covering ground. Twice she'd watched it stop circling altogether and simply hover, wings shivering, head cocked down toward the cliff shelf. Toward the path. Toward, she was nearly certain, the Gate.

And there was something else, something she hadn't yet admitted even to herself in plain words: the bird cast her a feeling, faint as a smell through a closed window. A cold, orderly attention. The valley forgot things; the hawk, whatever it was or whoever's eyes sat behind it, was clearly in the business of remembering.

Birds did not patrol. Birds did not grid-search a cliff face. Birds did not make her blood hum with a low uneasiness she recognized, with a lurch, from her mother's stories of the world beyond the mountains — stories of kings and decrees, of a crown that had burned its own history a century ago and had lately, her mother had heard before the letters stopped coming, taken to burning the people who carried it.

*If a stranger comes asking questions,* her mother had said, in the last autumn of her life, gripping Mirabel's wrist hard enough to leave marks, *you don't answer. You don't explain. There is nothing up that path but an old woman visiting a rock. Swear it.*

*I swear,* Mirabel had sworn, and had spent eleven years not needing the oath, and had begun, in the last two days, to feel it warming in her chest like a banked coal.

She bought her flour. She bought tallow and thread. The miller's wife shortchanged her by a copper and Mirabel let her, because arguing meant talking, and talking meant presence, and presence was the one thing she couldn't afford today. She walked home along the valley floor with the afternoon light going amber and long, and all the while the hawk rode the air above the western cliffs, patient as a signature waiting to be signed.

* * *

Evening brought the wind around from the west, and with it the smell that ended her ability to pretend.

Smoke — but not woodsmoke. Woodsmoke was sweet and domestic and belonged to the valley's evenings. This was different: a thin, resinous reek, pitch and oil, the smell of fires built to destroy rather than warm. It came down off the pass in a gust while Mirabel stood at her doorway with a pail of water for the morning, and it stopped her mid-step, and the pail's handle creaked under her grip.

Fires beyond the pass. Fires on the road over the mountains — the king's road, the one road, the one that wound down from a world she had never seen and did not want.

She stood very still and listened to the valley not noticing. Down in Hollowmere, lamplight was blooming window by window; someone was laughing near the well; the smithy's hammer had gone quiet for the night. Two hundred souls settling into their suppers and their small forgettings, and above their roofs, faint against the last violet of the sky, the hawk still wheeled — closer now, lower, its circles tightening like a rope drawing in.

Mirabel set down the pail. Her heart was doing something she refused to call fear; she filed it under *alertness*, under *duty*, under the old familiar weight of being the only one awake in a sleeping house.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would rise early and climb and feed the Gate its salt and its silence, and the day after that, and the day after that, the same as every day, because that was the oath and the oath did not bend for smoke on the wind.

But that night she did not sleep. She sat by her cold hearth with her mother's iron key — useless, symbolic, the one heirloom that proved nothing and meant everything — turning it over and over in her fingers, and she watched the western sky through the window, where far beyond the ridge a dull orange glow had begun to pulse against the clouds like a heartbeat. Too steady to be lightning. Too organized to be accident.

Somewhere out there, something was moving down the king's road toward her valley, and it was burning as it came.

The hawk screamed once in the dark — a single raw note over the sleeping village — and Mirabel Kavanagh, last keeper of the Gate, finally put a name to the cold in her stomach.

Not a hawk. A herald.
