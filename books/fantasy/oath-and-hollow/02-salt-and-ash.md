## 2. Salt and Ash

The decree came on the wind before the riders did.

Mirabel felt it first as a wrongness in the morning air — a thinness, like the valley had been holding its breath all night and could hold it no longer. She stood at the gate with her palms still dusted in salt, the dawn ritual half-finished, and watched three hawks circle where no bird should fly. Not one hawk. Three, riding a thermal that rose from the southern ridge like smoke from a chimney.

Smoke from a chimney. The thought arrived fully formed and terrible, and she hated it even as she turned to look south.

There was no smoke yet. Only the hawks, and beneath them, far down the valley road, a glitter of metal moving through the birch trees like a river of knives finding its way downhill.

She counted without meaning to. Twelve riders. No — fourteen. Two more lagging behind with something heavy slung between their saddles: a caged cart, or a brazier on wheels. She knew which. Everyone in the borderlands knew which, now. For two years the stories had drifted up out of the low country like ash on the wind — villages searched, wise-women taken, hedgewives burned in their own doorways, all under a seal of black wax and a single word repeated in every market square: *Decree*.

*All who bear the old gifts will surrender themselves to the crown's mercy, or be given to the fire.*

The crown's mercy. Mirabel spat salt into her palm and pressed it against the gate's cold stone, finishing the rite with hands that no longer felt steady. The gate drank the offering as it always did — silently, hungrily, the salt vanishing into granite as if into snow — and she rested her forehead against it for one long moment, feeling the deep slow nothing of it, the hollow behind the stone sleeping its sleepless sleep.

*Not today,* she told it. *Not while I stand here.*

Then she ran down toward the village of Hollowmere, which had spent ninety years perfecting the art of not seeing her.

* * *

They were already gathered in the square when she arrived, breathless, salt still crusted white along her forearms — a mistake she realized too late, the marks of her office plain as scars. Elder Maren stood on the well's low wall, and the whole village was arranged around her the way iron filings arrange around a lodestone: not by love, but by force they couldn't name.

"They're coming up the valley road," Mirabel said. "Fourteen. Maybe more behind. There's time to scatter the flock to the high pastures, to say they've gone—"

"Quiet." Maren didn't look at her. That was the first wound, though Mirabel wouldn't understand it as such until later — not cruelty, but the deliberate blindness of a woman who could not afford to see what she was about to do. "You'll go back to your gate, keeper."

"The decree says gift-bearers. I'm the only—"

"I said *quiet*." And now Maren turned, and her face was the face of a woman standing in a doorway holding back a flood with her own body, and Mirabel saw that the elder had been weeping already, or was very near it. "Ninety years this valley has been left alone because we were nobody. Because there was nothing here worth a king's attention. One old woman feeding salt to a rock, and a village that minded its own fields." Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "You want me to hide you? To make four hundred souls complicit? They'll burn the valley, girl. Not search it. *Burn* it. I've seen what they did to Corrow Ford. There are no houses there anymore. There is a field of ash and a new church, and a priest who writes down names."

"They won't find anything," someone said from the crowd — Tam's father, actually, though the boy himself was nowhere in sight, and Mirabel would remember that absence later with a strange, stubborn gratitude. "We've nothing here but turnips."

"Turnips," Maren said bitterly, "and one woman whose arms are painted with salt."

The crowd's silence changed quality then. Mirabel felt it move through them like cold water through cloth — not hatred, exactly. Something worse. Arithmetic. She had spent her whole life learning the mathematics of duty: how much silence, how much loneliness, how much of herself the gate required. She had never once considered that the valley below performed its own arithmetic, and that in their sums, she was the remainder to be discarded.

"You fed the gate," said Widow Ashe, gently, horribly, from beside the bake-oven. "Every morning. We all knew. We let you do it so we didn't have to."

"So the thing stayed asleep," Mirabel said. Her voice sounded foreign to her. "So it never came down the hill. Ninety years, and you think the quiet was free?"

"We think," Maren said, "that we have until noon to decide what we are willing to lose. And we have decided it isn't everything."

The words landed like stones dropped down a well. Mirabel waited for the splash, the bottom, some finality — but there was none, only the endless falling of understanding what was being asked of her. Not her death. They weren't asking for her death; the crown would provide that regardless. They were asking for her *surrender*, so that when the hunters came, the valley could point up the hill and say *there, that one, we kept no part of her, we are clean, we are only farmers.*

"Give me until dark," Mirabel heard herself say. "I can seal the gate properly. A full sealing takes hours — if I do it right, no one will ever know there's a gate there at all. Stone looks like stone. You can plow to the edge of it and never—"

"No rites," snapped a man near the front — Halloran, whose son she'd once carried three miles through fever-snow to Wren's grandmother when the boy's gift of warming had guttered and nearly gone out. Halloran, whom she had saved, or whose son she had. His eyes would not meet hers. "No more of your workings on our land. Go up your hill and take whatever you keep up there with you, and leave us clean."

*Clean.* The word rang in her skull like a cracked bell.

She looked at their faces — all of them, the faces she had guarded her whole life without their knowledge or thanks. The miller's wife who gave her day-old bread and called it charity rather than gratitude. The children who dared each other to run up the keeper's path and touch the gate's stone and run away screaming, laughing. Old Piet who'd taught her to fish the cold stream and never asked why she ate alone. Every face she had ever known, arranged against her in the grey morning light, wearing the expression of people watching a flood take the first house upstream and hoping the water stops there.

"I kept you safe," she said. It came out smaller than she meant it. Smaller than it deserved.

"And we let you," said Maren. "Go home, keeper."

*Home.* The gate wasn't home. The gate was a post, a prison, an oath. But the word broke something in her anyway, and she turned and walked up the hill with her spine straight because if she let it bend even a little she would never straighten again, and behind her the village began — immediately, quietly, shamelessly — to discuss what should be done with the grain in her storeroom.

* * *

The full sealing took hours, and she had perhaps two.

She worked anyway. Hands moving through the old forms her mother's mother had set down in a book of stitched hide, salt poured in lines, blood — a prick, only a drop, the gate was modest in its demands — pressed to the stone at the seven points of the star. The gate drank each offering with that same patient hunger, and Mirabel spoke the closing words, the ones that would fold the door down into the mountain's bones until it was indistinguishable from any other cliff-face, unopenable, unfound.

Except it wasn't unopenable. That was the truth she'd carried since childhood and never spoken: the gate answered blood. *Her* blood. Which meant the sealing was a lock on a door whose key hung around her own neck, and somewhere below, men were coming who knew how to ask questions.

The sun was barely past its height when she heard the horses.

She was at the sixth point of the star, salt burning in the cut on her palm, when the first rider crested the southern rise — and she understood in one sick, cascading instant that she had miscounted the morning. Fourteen had been the vanguard. Behind them, winding up the valley road like a black vein, came thirty more. And behind *them*, on the flatbed carts, came the things that made her blood forget how to move: braziers already lit, cages, and a tall banner of black wax-seal red, and men in scale-and-leather with the crown's inverted torch sigil stitched at their shoulders.

Witch-hunters. Not a patrol. A *company*.

They did not ride to the village first. That was the detail she would carry forever, the detail that undid her: they rode past Hollowmere's outskirts without slowing, because the village had already sent a rider out to meet them at the ford, and the rider had handed over a paper, and the paper had a name on it.

Her name. Her location. Drawn on a map in a steady hand — she recognized the hand later, would recognize it in dreams for years after, Maren's careful schoolroom letters, the same hand that had written out the village tithe lists, the same hand that had once, when Mirabel was nine and feverish, copied out a prayer for her recovery.

The company wheeled up the keeper's path at a trot.

Mirabel did the arithmetic of a hunted animal. Thirty riders, forty yards, and her at the seventh point of a sealing that needed the full night to hold. She could finish the rite and die at her post — the way her mother had died, the way her grandmother had died, each keeper found at the stone when the end came, faithful to the last drop. The oath said so. The oath had always said so: *die at the door, and the door holds.*

But a sealed gate with its key walking around alive in the world was worse than no gate at all. If they took her, they could open it — or learn enough to try. And the hollow behind the stone was not sleeping the way she'd told the villagers it slept. It was waiting. She had felt its patience all her life the way you feel a toothache: constant, ignorable, and absolutely certain of itself.

Thirty riders. Forty yards. Closing.

She pressed her bleeding palm to the seventh point and whispered the last word of the partial sealing — *hold* — and felt the gate shudder beneath her hand like a great beast settling, incomplete, uneasy, held together by will and salt and not much else. Then she ran. Not away. Through.

The gate had two sides. In ninety years of keeping, no Kavanagh had ever used the inner side. The lore said the hollow was empty. The lore said many things.

She laid both hands flat against the stone's heart and let her blood speak to it, and the granite opened like a mouth.

* * *

Inside was cold the way the bottom of a lake is cold, and dark the way the inside of your own closed eyelids is dark — not empty darkness but *present* darkness, attentive, aware of her the moment her boot crossed the threshold. The lore was wrong. The hollow was not empty. The hollow was *listening*, and it had been listening for a very long time, and it knew her name.

She did not stop to greet it. She crossed the hollow's throat in forty running strides with the sense of something vast turning over in its sleep beside her, something regarding her with the mild interest of a spider noticing a fly walk across the web's outer edge, and then the far wall was under her hands, and her blood opened that stone too, and she came out through the mountain's northern shoulder into pine-shadow and evening light with the taste of grave-cold in her mouth.

Behind her, below her, the valley was catching fire.

She stood on the high shoulder of the mountain and watched the hunters' work with the detachment of the truly shattered. They had not burned Hollowmere — Maren's arithmetic had bought them that much, the paper with her name had bought them that much. Instead they had made a theater of it. They had built their pyre in the square, and the smoke rose straight and black in the still air, and the villagers stood in a ring at the distance custom required, and whatever burned on the pyre was not yet her, because she was alive and watching, which meant it was her *life* they were burning instead — her stores, her mother's book of stitched hide dragged weeping pages into the flames, the salt, the tools, the small accumulated evidence of ninety years of keeping. She saw a hunter hold up her grandmother's staff to the light like a trophy before feeding it to the fire.

And she saw Maren, at the edge of the ring, watching the smoke, and she saw the exact moment the old woman's shoulders finally bent — the cost arriving, as costs do, after the payment was already made.

*I protected them,* Mirabel thought, and then, with the clarity that comes when everything else has been stripped away: *no. We protected each other, badly, and it was never going to be enough, and they knew it before I did.*

The wind shifted. Smoke rolled up the valley and washed over the mountainside, and in the smoke she smelled her whole life burning — bread and beeswax and Piet's pipe tobacco and the particular smell of the gate's granite warmed by summer sun — and she understood that she was now the only person alive who remembered what the valley had been. The kingdom would erase the rest. The kingdom was very good at erasing.

Somewhere below, a horse screamed, or a man; at this distance they sounded alike.

She turned north. The lore said there was another gate — one other, in a country that maps no longer named, across a kingdom that had spent a century scraping its own memory blank. Her mother had whispered of it once, drunk on fever-medicine: *if ever the door fails you, little door, there is a sister-door where the orchards grow wrong.*

It was a fool's destination. A story. A rumor at the bottom of a burned book.

She started walking anyway, north, with salt in her wounds and ash in her hair and a cold spot between her shoulder blades that she told herself was only the mountain wind — a cold spot that moved with her when the wind did not, and kept pace, step for step, all through the failing light, like something that had finally, after ninety years, gotten exactly what it wanted:

a door, walking out into the world.
