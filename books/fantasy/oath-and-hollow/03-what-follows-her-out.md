## 3. What Follows Her Out

The road out of the valley was a wound that would not close.

Mirabel walked it through the small hours of the morning, and behind her the sky still glowed the dull orange of things burning long past any usefulness. The valley's smoke rose in a single column so wide it seemed less like smoke than like the valley itself standing up to leave. She did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people who had somewhere to return to.

Her feet knew the road even when her mind refused it. Down through the shepherd's switchbacks, past the cairn where travelers left stones for luck — she had never once taken a stone, and now the thought of luck made her laugh, a dry cracked sound with nothing in it. Past the ford where the water ran shallow over grey shale. Out. The word repeated in her like a heartbeat she hadn't agreed to: *out, out, out.*

She had opened the gate.

That was the part her hands kept remembering. Not the fire, not Elder Maren's face going smooth and sorry as she gave Mirabel's name to the hunters — though that was there too, folded into her chest like a knife wrapped in cloth. It was her own hands on the gate's cold iron ring, turning it, feeling the lock yield to her blood the way a dog yields to its master's voice. Twenty-six years of salt and silence and *never touch it, never want it,* and the thing had opened for her as easily as a door in her own house.

Because it was a door in her own house. That was what no one had told her, or what everyone had told her without knowing they were telling it. The keepers' blood was the key. The keepers' blood was the lock. And she had walked through it into a world on fire, and she could not stop wondering, in some cold cellar of her mind, whether the fire had come because she'd opened it — whether the crown had burned the valley to flush her out, or whether something older and hungrier had smelled the opening from far away.

She walked until the dawn came up thin and colorless over hills she did not know, and then she stopped at a milestone worn nearly featureless, and sat down on it, and allowed herself exactly one minute of grief. She counted it the way she'd counted everything at the gate — by breath, by the slow drip of discipline her mother had drilled into her before her mother died. Sixty breaths. In those sixty breaths she let herself feel the weight of every person in the valley whose faces she would not see again, and the specific unbearable shape of Maren's betrayal, and the fact that the life she had built — narrow, lonely, holy — was ash.

Then she stood up, and rolled her shoulders, and began again.

*You are the keeper,* she told herself. *There is no more gate. So you are the keeper of nothing. Fine. Keep nothing. Keep walking.*

It was three miles later, near a shrine to a saint whose name had been scraped off the stone by crown chisels, that she realized she was not alone.

Not alone in the way of travelers, or wolves, or soldiers following at a distance. Alone in the way of *presence.* The air beside her left shoulder was wrong. She noticed it first as a patch of chill on her neck, the kind a draft makes, except there was no wind, and the morning was mild, and the chill moved when she moved — stayed precisely one arm's length behind her and to the left, patient as a shadow on an overcast day.

She stopped. The chill stopped.

She turned. Nothing. A hedgerow, a fallow field, the scraped shrine, the empty road running back the way she'd come. But the air there — she could see through it, and yet she could also see that it wasn't quite air. It had a density. A *stillness.* Like looking through a window into a room where nothing had ever happened, where dust had never been disturbed, where no light had ever needed to enter because nothing inside required seeing.

"Oh," said Mirabel aloud, and her voice came out very small. "Oh, you followed me."

The cold did not answer. But it leaned. There was no better word for it. It leaned toward her the way a cat leans toward a hand it intends to rub against, or the way a starving thing leans toward a lit window. And underneath the lean, faint but unmistakable, she felt something that made her stomach drop through the road and into the earth below it:

It was *listening.*

At the gate, all those years, she had fed it salt and silence and believed the hollow inside was empty. Empty like a well is empty. Empty like a room is empty. She had never once considered that emptiness could be a kind of hunger — that a thing shaped like absence might spend centuries learning the shape of the only person who ever came to feed it.

"Go back," she whispered. Her breath fogged, which it should not have; the morning was warm. "I closed— I didn't close it, but you can't—"

The cold pressed closer. One arm's length became half an arm's length, and the temperature fell around her like a stone dropped down a well, and somewhere in her body — in her blood, in the same blood that had turned the iron ring — she felt a horrible flicker of recognition. Not fear exactly. Fear was in there, drowning, but beneath the fear was something worse: the hollow felt *familiar.* It fit against her awareness the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

"No." She said it louder, and started walking, fast, then faster, breaking into a lurching run along the road with her pack thumping against her spine and the cold pacing her effortlessly, never hurrying, never falling behind. "No, no, no—"

That was how she met the boy: at a dead run, straight into him.

He came out of a gap in the hedgerow like something poured, small and quick, and they collided hard enough to send them both sprawling. He was up instantly, and she saw his hand close around her coin pouch's drawstring — old reflex, faster than thought — and she caught his wrist.

They froze, staring at each other across their joined grip. He was perhaps eleven, maybe twelve, all elbows and sharp angles under a coat two sizes too big, with dark curling hair cut badly by someone who'd used a knife instead of scissors. His eyes were quick and brown and currently very wide, because he was looking past her shoulder at the place where the cold stood, and whatever he saw — or heard — was making his freckled face drain toward white.

"What," he said, "is *that.*"

"You can see it?"

"I can't see anything. I can hear it." His voice pitched high and cracked in the middle. "It doesn't make sound, right? But it's loud anyway. Like when a house is quiet but the walls are still talking. It's saying—" He swallowed. His fingers, trapped in her grip, had gone rigid. "It's saying your name. Over and over. Lady. Lady. Lady. Why's it calling you lady?"

Mirabel let him go. He didn't run, which surprised her. Perhaps terror outranked theft. She got her feet under her and looked at the empty air, and the empty air waited, and the boy flinched at whatever passed between them.

"It followed me out of a place I was supposed to guard," she said. It was the truest sentence she'd spoken in days, and it cost her more than the coin purse would have.

The boy looked at her properly then — took in the singed hem of her cloak, the soot ground into the creases of her knuckles, the salt-stained satchel she carried like a reliquary, the mile-eaten exhaustion of a woman who had walked all night away from a burning valley. He was, she would learn, extremely good at reading people. It went with the ears.

"You're from Vessany," he said.

"There's no such country," she answered automatically, reciting the catechism every child learned, and heard how dead it sounded.

"Yeah, well." He shrugged, and something old and bitter moved briefly behind the quickness of his face. "There's no such gift either. Ask the king." He jerked his chin at the cold patch. "That thing going to eat us, or are we just going to stand here being interesting to it?"

"I don't know."

"Great. Love a mystery." He backed up a step, weighing her, weighing the road, weighing — she understood suddenly — the odds. "Name's Tam."

"Mirabel."

"Lady Mirabel?"

"Just Mirabel." She glanced back at the cold, which had not moved, had not grown, had simply *waited,* with the boundless patience of a thing that had already waited four hundred years. "And I don't know what it wants. I don't know why it followed me. I don't know anything anymore except that the crown burned my valley and I led this out the door I was born to keep shut." Her voice did something humiliating on the last words, wobbled and thinned, and she hated it, and Tam watched her hate it with an expression that was, unexpectedly, kind.

"Right," he said. "So you're running from soldiers, you're hauling something that eats sound, and you've got no plan."

"I have a direction."

"A direction isn't a plan, lady—Mirabel." He shifted his weight. She could see the calculation happening, the street arithmetic of a child who had survived the capital's gutters by knowing exactly when to attach himself to trouble and when to flee it. "Here's my problem," he went on, conversationally, as if they were haggling over apples. "Hunters came through the last village asking about people with gifts. Which is me. Small gift. Stupid gift. But the decree doesn't care about size." He held up his hands, wiggling his fingers. "These hear locks. Don't ask how. They just do. Always have. And last week a woman in Ashford got burned in her own kitchen because her bread rose too good. So I'm running too, and running alone is dying slowly instead of quickly."

"You want to travel together."

"I want," Tam said with great dignity, "to form an uneasy alliance of mutual inconvenience, as described in all the best ballads. You're big and scary-looking when you're angry. I'm small and I know which roads have toll men and which ones have worse than toll men. Also—" and here his voice dropped, and he pointed past her shoulder with one grimy finger, "also I think that thing likes you, and anything a monster likes, I'd rather be standing next to than standing away from. Basic rules of dogs and dukes."

Mirabel looked at him for a long moment. Everything sane in her said: *send him away. You are a keeper with nothing left to keep, and the thing that follows you is drawn to your blood, and anyone within reach of your blood is fuel.* Everything sane in her had, however, been standing in a burning valley three days ago, and she had begun to suspect that sanity was just another name for the silence she'd fed the gate — the silence that had kept the whole valley ignorant and safe and doomed.

"You'll slow me down," she said.

"I'll speed you up. You've been walking on roads like a pilgrim. Pilgrims get robbed."

"You'll steal from me."

"Obviously." He grinned, and for one second the terror drained off his face entirely and he was just a boy with a crooked eyetooth and dirt on his chin, and Mirabel felt something crack open in her chest that was not grief for once. "But only what you can spare, and I'll warn you first, because you look like the type who'd chase me over a ha'penny and win."

*The type who'd spent twenty-six years feeding salt to a sealed door because her mother asked her to,* Mirabel thought. Yes. That type.

"Alliance," she said, and put out her hand.

He shook it with a pickpocket's grip, brief and firm, already cataloguing her rings. Then his eyes went past her shoulder again and his grin faded.

"It's closer," he said quietly. "Since you shook my hand. It came closer. Like it's — pleased? Is it pleased? Things shouldn't be pleased like that, it sounds like a cellar breathing."

Mirabel turned. The cold had drifted nearer during the handshake — half a step, no more. Close enough now that if it had a mouth, she could have felt its breath. And she understood, with a certainty that settled into her bones colder than the presence itself, that it had not followed her out of hunger alone. Hunger was simple. This was something else. It had waited four hundred years behind iron and salt, and the only warmth that ever touched it was a keeper's blood, generation after generation, each one coming to the gate with salt and silence and staying until they died —

—and it had learned them. Learned *her line.* Learned the particular flavor of Kavanagh devotion, the way a dog learns a household, the way ivy learns a wall. It wasn't following her because she was food.

It was following her because she was *home.*

"We need to move," she said.

"That's the first sensible thing you've said." Tam swung his threadbare pack onto his shoulder and set off down the road at a lope, angling off the main way onto a sheep track almost immediately. "Roads are for people with papers," he explained over his shoulder. "Also, question. When you say the crown burned your valley — the whole valley? Because I heard smoke on the wind two nights ago, north-ish, and I heard a lot of screaming, and I heard—" he faltered, just slightly, the first hitch in his chatter, "—I heard a church bell melted. Bells scream when they melt. Nobody tells you that."

"The whole valley," Mirabel said.

"And they wanted you special-like? The officer types? Because the ones through Ashford weren't taking names, just burning. But the ones behind me—" he glanced back at her, and his young face was carefully blank in the way of children who have learned that showing fear gets it fed, "—the ones behind me were asking after a woman. Keeper of somewhere. Alive, they said. Alive specifically. Someone upstairs wants you breathing."

The cold at her shoulder pulsed, very gently, like a heart deciding whether to beat.

Someone at court knew what she was. Someone had always known. The decree, the purge, the careful order to take the keeper alive while her valley burned — none of it was accident. None of it had ever been accident. The realization arrived without shock; she was too tired for shock. It simply settled in alongside the cold and the grief and the ash-smell in her hair, one more passenger.

"Then we should be somewhere they aren't," she said.

"See, this is why alliances work." Tam grinned again, and lengthened his stride, and the sheep track bent away eastward through gorse and bracken, toward hills where the crown's chisels hadn't yet reached. "Keep up, Mirabel-just-Mirabel. And do try not to let your pet winter-wind eat me before breakfast. I'm very fond of breakfast."

Behind them, patient as an inheritance, the cold came on.

They walked until noon, and at noon, on the crest of a hill, Tam stopped and shaded his eyes and swore with impressive fluency for a boy his age. Mirabel followed his gaze to the road far below — the main south road, ribbon-grey in the sun — where a column of riders moved at a steady canter, and even at this distance she could see the royal pennants snapping above them, white and black, the colors of the Hunt Decree.

Riding south. Riding the way they'd come. Casting about, already, like hounds that had found the edge of a scent.

"They're not following your trail," Tam murmured. His hearing-hands had come up unconsciously, palms out toward the distant riders, and his face had gone strange. "They're following mine. Or — no. Both. They've got someone who hears like me, lady. Someone who's been listening to the roads." He lowered his hands slowly. "We need to get off the map entirely. And I only know one person who draws maps of places that aren't on them."
