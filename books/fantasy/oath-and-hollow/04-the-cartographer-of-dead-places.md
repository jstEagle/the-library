## 4. The Cartographer of Dead Places

Tam had three rules for surviving the city of Corvane, and he recited them to himself now like a prayer: never run when you can walk, never look at what you've lifted, and never — *never* — bring trouble home.

He was breaking rule three with every step.

The woman behind him moved wrong for a city. That was the first thing he'd noticed about her, back in the market square where she'd stood frozen among the fish-sellers while everyone else flowed around her like water around a stone. She walked like someone who had spent her whole life in open places and didn't trust walls to stay where they were put. Her eyes kept going up — to rooftops, to ledges, to the gaps between chimneys — as if she expected something to come down out of the sky at her. Which, Tam supposed, given the hawk, maybe it had.

She also had a cold spot following her. He could hear it. Not hear it exactly — more feel it in his teeth, the way you felt a lock before you touched it, that faint singing tension in the mechanism. The cold patch trailed her through the crowd like a dog on an invisible lead, and people stepped away from it without knowing why, shivering and rubbing their arms and blaming the wind off the river.

Tam had decided, somewhere between the market and the tanners' quarter, that he was not going to think too hard about the cold spot. Thinking about it would mean admitting what it probably was, and admitting what it probably was would mean running, and running meant the hunters found you faster. Rule one. Walk.

"Almost there," he said over his shoulder.

Mirabel Kavanagh — though he didn't know her name yet, only that she'd called herself Mira and lied badly about where she was from — said nothing. She hadn't said much since the alley behind the chandler's shop, where she'd watched him talk a padlock into opening itself and then grabbed his wrist hard enough to leave marks and asked him, in a voice like a door slamming, *what are you.*

He'd told her the truth, because lying to her felt like lying to a lit fuse. "I hear locks," he'd said. "All locks. Doors, chests, chains, gates. They sing if you know how to listen. It's a small gift. Small enough they haven't bothered hunting me yet." And then, because she was still gripping his wrist and the cold thing was pressing against his back like deep water: "You're carrying something. Something that came out of wherever you came from."

"I don't want to talk about it."

"Fine. But I know who does."

That was how a pickpocket boy of eleven winters came to be leading a fugitive keeper through the worst streets in Corvane toward the only person in the world who might understand what she carried — a person Tam himself had sworn, on pain of a broken nose, never to bring trouble to.

* * *

Odile Vasse lived above a shuttered dye-house in Sable Row, where the air smelled permanently of vinegar and old iron and the walls wept rust-colored stains. Tam climbed the outside stair first, out of habit, testing each step with his weight before committing it. Behind him Mirabel came slow, one hand trailing the wall, her head turning constantly — cataloguing exits, he realized later. She counted exits the way he counted purses.

At the top of the stair was a door with no lock at all, which was Odile's idea of a joke, or a warning. Tam knocked the pattern: two, pause, three, pause, one.

"Go away, sparrow." The voice through the wood was dry as old vellum. "I told you what happens the next time you bring me something shiny instead of something useful."

"It's not shiny," Tam said. "It's worse than shiny."

A long silence. Then, grudgingly: "Worse is interesting. In."

* * *

The room inside was not a room so much as a paper nest. Maps hung from every surface — pinned to walls, strung on cords across the ceiling like laundry, layered over the single table until no wood showed through. Some were beautiful, inked in colors Tam had names for but no words: the blue of deep water, the red of a wound just opened. Others were stranger. One showed a coastline that ended mid-stroke, the pen simply lifted, the rest of the country left as blank parchment. Another had been scraped nearly white, ghost-roads showing through like scars under skin.

And everywhere, everywhere, were maps of places that weren't there anymore.

Tam knew better than to touch anything. He sat on the one clear stool by the stove and watched Odile take Mirabel's measure.

She was smaller than her voice suggested, Odile Vasse — a compact woman somewhere past fifty, gray-shot hair cut short as a soldier's, ink stains up both forearms like old bruises. Her eyes did the same thing Mirabel's did: went to the exits first. Exiles, Tam thought. They all count doors.

"You're the girl from the valley," Odile said. Not a question.

"How do you—"

"Because the cold followed you in, and I have only read about cold like that in one place." Odile crossed to the window and shut the shutter, and the room went dim and close, lamplight pooling on the map-table like honey. "Kavanagh's Gate. The salt valley. The place my own teachers refused to chart, back when I still had teachers." She turned. "You're the keeper's daughter. Or the keeper. Which?"

"Neither. Both." Mirabel's hands had curled into fists at her sides. "There hasn't been a keeper since—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, flatter. "I fed the gate salt every morning for nineteen years. I kept the silence. I did everything right. And then the hunters came, and the elder gave me to them to save the rest, and the valley burned, and I ran through the door I was supposed to die guarding. And something came out with me."

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. Tam saw Odile notice; he saw her go very still, the way a deer goes still.

"Describe it," Odile said quietly.

"Cold air. A patch of it. It stays near me. It—" Mirabel's jaw worked. "When I'm afraid, it gets closer. When I'm calm, it waits. Last night it spoke to me. It used my mother's voice."

Tam watched the color leave Odile's face by degrees, like water draining from a basin. The old woman sat down slowly on her work stool, and for a moment she looked at Mirabel the way Tam had once seen a man look at a debt collector's summons.

Then she laughed — one short, harsh bark with no humor in it anywhere.

"Nineteen years of salt and silence," she said. "Nineteen years, and they never told you what you were feeding."

"They told me it was duty. They told me the hollow was empty. They told me the gate kept magic *out* of the valley." Mirabel's voice cracked on the last word. "Was that a lie too?"

"No." Odile rose and began moving along the cord-strung maps overhead, touching this one, that one, searching. "That part was true, or true enough. Magic can't cross into the valley — the gate drinks it, or eats it, or whatever word you prefer. But a seal has two faces, girl. What keeps a thing out also keeps a thing *in*. Your family didn't build a wall against the world." She pulled down a map — old, foxed at the corners, drawn in a hand so fine it looked embroidered. "They built a jar."

She spread it on the table, weighting the corners with an inkpot, a knife, a cold tea-cup, a stone.

Tam leaned forward despite himself. The map showed a country he had never seen on any other map, because no other map admitted it existed. Mountains like a spine of charcoal. Rivers in silver. And at the center, ringed in careful black, a symbol he didn't recognize — a circle bisected by a line, like an eye half-closed.

"What's that?" he asked.

"That," Odile said, "is Vessany."

"There's no such place," Tam said automatically, because everyone knew there was no such place. You learned it young, the way you learned the king's name and the shape of the crown's law: *Vessany never existed. There has never been a country east of the Thornmark. Anyone who says otherwise is a traitor or a fool.* Schoolmasters said it. Priests said it. The decree said it in letters burned into the courthouse doors.

"No," Odile agreed mildly. "There isn't. It was unwritten forty years ago. Borders dissolved, roads renamed, records burned, gravestones chiseled blank. The crown didn't conquer Vessany — conquest leaves ruins, and ruins leave questions. No, they did something cleaner. They erased it. Every map, every deed, every name. An entire country, deleted." She tapped the half-closed eye. "Except that the land itself is stubborn. Land remembers longer than paper. And some of us remember on its behalf."

"You're a traitor, then," Mirabel said. There was no judgment in it. Almost relief.

"I'm a cartographer," Odile said. "It amounts to the same thing, these days." She looked up, and her eyes were sharp as a compass needle. "Now ask me the question you actually came to ask."

Mirabel stared at the map. At the eye-symbol at its heart. Tam watched her throat move.

"The hollow," she said. "The thing in the gate. Is it the only one?"

"No."

"Where's the other?"

Odile's finger came down on the symbol, gentle as touching a sleeping animal.

"Vessany. Dead center. Under what used to be the capital, before the capital stopped existing." She straightened. "Two gates, girl. Two jars. Your family kept the western one. Someone else kept the eastern one — until the erasure reached them too, and I don't know what became of their keepers, because I don't know what became of anyone in Vessany. That's rather the point of an erasure."

"But if the crown erased the country—"

"—then they erased the keepers, or the keepers fled, or the keepers died at their posts like yours always have." Odile's voice dropped. "Which means the eastern gate may be sealed, or may be weakening, or may already be open. And your crown, which knows perfectly well that Vessany exists — you cannot erase a country without first studying everything you intend to erase — has spent forty years making sure no one alive remembers enough to go looking."

The lamp flame leaned again. Closer, this time. Tam felt the cold press against his shoulder blades like a hand, patient and vast, and he made himself breathe slowly through his nose.

"Why do they want her?" he asked, because someone had to say it. "The hunters. Why bring her alive? Everyone else gets burned."

Odile looked at Mirabel for a long moment. Whatever she saw there made her sigh, deep and tired, the sigh of a woman adding a weight to a scale already tipped.

"Because her blood opens gates," she said. "Any gate. Keeper-blood is a key, and keys are worth more than gold to men who want into locked countries. There are things buried in Vessany the crown has wanted for forty years — and things the crown is afraid of, which is worse, because fear makes kings generous and ruthless in equal measure." She began rolling the map closed with quick, precise movements. "They will follow you, girl. Not to kill you. To *use* you. And they will use your friends as leverage, because that is how crowns do arithmetic."

"I don't have friends," Mirabel said.

Tam snorted before he could stop himself. Both women looked at him.

"What?" he said. "You grabbed my wrist in an alley and dragged me across half of Corvane. Where I come from, that's practically a betrothal."

* * *

The price was negotiated over cold tea, and it was not money.

"I'll guide you," Odile said. "East, out of Corvane before the hunters finish questioning the valley survivors — and they will question them, and the elder will describe your face, and by week's end there will be sketches of you in every garrison from here to the coast. We go by smuggler roads, the ones that aren't on any honest map. I know them because I drew them." She held up one ink-stained finger. "In exchange, you carry for me."

"Carry what?"

"My maps. All of them." She gestured at the paper nest around them, hundreds of sheets, years of forbidden work. "If the crown ever finds this room, they burn it, and forty years of remembering dies with it. So the maps travel with the keeper. If I fall, you take them onward. If we all fall—" she shrugged, "—then at least the fire has to chase us further."

"That's not a price," Mirabel said slowly. "That's just luggage."

"The price is this." Odile sat back, and her voice lost its dryness entirely. "When this is done — whatever 'done' means for someone carrying what you carry — you help me redraw Vessany. Every border. Every name. Every grave with its chiseled-blank stone filled back in. The crown unmade a country, and I intend to make it exist again, line by line, even if it takes the rest of my life and theirs." She nodded at Tam. "And his. Sparrow has good hands and better ears. I'll need both."

Tam opened his mouth to object — rule three, rule three, *never bring trouble home*, and here was trouble renting a room in his future — and found, to his considerable annoyance, that he didn't want to object. Somewhere between the alley and the map-table, the flight had grown a destination, and destinations were dangerous things. Destinations made you care whether you arrived.

"Deal," Mirabel said.

They shook on it, keeper and cartographer, and Tam noticed that neither of them let go quickly — as if each recognized in the other's grip something rare and slightly desperate: a person who had been alone a very long time, holding on to the first hand offered.

* * *

They left Sable Row an hour before dawn, Odile's maps sewn into oilcloth bundles and strapped beneath Mirabel's cloak, which made her walk oddly and complain about it, which Tam privately thought was a good sign. People carrying the end of the world shouldn't also be able to complain about luggage. It humanized the apocalypse.

He took them the back way, along the drainage channel and under the aqueduct, past the tanneries where the guards never bothered walking because the smell was punishment enough. At the city's eastern sally port — a rusted gate in a wall everyone had forgotten the crown owned — Tam laid his palm flat against the lock and listened.

Locks sang differently depending on their mood. This one was old and bored and half-asleep, its tumblers dreaming of the day someone had oiled them properly, decades ago. He hummed to it, low, matching its pitch, feeling the mechanism loosen in sympathy the way a stiff shoulder loosens under a warm hand. The bolt slid back with a sound like a contented sigh.

"You could teach that," Mirabel said quietly, watching him work. It wasn't quite a question.

"Could I?" He grinned at her over his shoulder. "First lesson's free: most things that seem locked are just lonely. You have to ask them properly."

Something crossed her face — grief, maybe, or recognition — and she looked away toward the gray light gathering beyond the gate, toward the road east and whatever waited on it. The cold patch drifted after her gaze like a faithful hound.

Beyond the wall, the land fell away in long folds of dark field, and far off, where the hills went blue with distance, lay the direction Odile's maps called *remembered* and every honest map called *nothing at all*.

"Vessany," Mirabel said softly, like a word she was learning to pronounce.

"A country that doesn't exist," Tam said. "Guarded by a gate nobody admits to, chased by hunters who want you alive, hauling forty years of illegal geography on your back." He hopped down onto the packed mud of the smugglers' road and turned, walking backward, grinning at her. "I've run from bakers' wives with less cause. This is going to be the best trouble I've ever been in."

Behind them, the sally port swung shut. Ahead, the road ran east into the unnamed hills.

And somewhere on that road — though none of them knew it yet, though the dawn was still too thin to show it — a rider in crown gray was already asking questions at the first waystation, and the answers were pointing, arrow-straight, east.
