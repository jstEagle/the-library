## 5. The Hunt Decree

The village of Marrow's Bend smelled of wet thatch and woodsmoke when Captain Aldric Serevan rode into it at dawn, and by noon it would smell only of ash.

He had learned long ago to read a settlement the way a physician reads a body — the flush of health in well-tended fields, the fever-bright eyes of children who should have been at their chores, and beneath it all, if he looked closely enough, the faint discoloration that meant infection. Gift-work. It was always there, in some degree or other. A charm knotted into a doorpost against bad luck. A grandmother who could coax milk from a dry cow. A boy whose whistles made the hens lay out of season. Small things. Harmless things, most days.

That was what everyone told themselves, anyway. That was the lie that let the plague spread.

Serevan dismounted at the well and let his sergeant, Corren, bellow the decree while he stood very still and listened — not to the words, which his men had recited at forty villages already this season, but to the silence underneath them. Fear had a texture. Guilt had a texture. Somewhere in this bend of river-mud and cabbage rows, someone's hands were shaking.

"By order of His Majesty's crown," Corren read, "all bearers of unlicensed gifts are commanded to present themselves for assessment. Those assessed as harmless shall be collared and released. Those assessed otherwise—" He paused, as the script required, letting the pause do its work. "—shall be taken into custody of the Office of Purity, or burned where they stand."

*Harmless.* The word was a courtesy the crown paid to itself. There was no such thing as a harmless gift. There were only gifts that hadn't yet shown their teeth.

A woman stepped forward first, as they always did — the brave one, the one who thought honesty might buy mercy. She held up her hands, palms scarred from milking, and said her gift was bread: she could tell, by touch, whether a loaf would rise. Nothing more.

Serevan examined her hands himself. Warm, calloused, ordinary. The gift sat in her like a candle in a window — small, steady, no threat to anyone. He collared her with his own hands rather than letting a trooper do it roughly, and he saw the gratitude in her eyes, and he hated that gratitude more than he could say, because it meant she believed the collar was kindness.

It was. That was the worst part. The collar was kindness. The alternative was the pyre.

By midmorning they had found six. The bread-witch. A dowsing man. Twin girls who could warm water between their palms. An old fellow whose gift was so faded even Serevan's testing-rod barely stirred — but a gift was a gift, and the rod did not lie. And then, in the last house on the lane, a child.

* * *

The boy was seven, maybe eight, and he did not run, which was the first thing Serevan noticed about him. Most of them ran. Running was sensible. This boy sat on the floor of his mother's kitchen with his back straight and his chin up, the way his father must have taught him, and on the table before him lay his crime.

It was a charm. A child's charm — braided rushes and a crow's feather and a knot tied in red thread, the kind of thing boys made everywhere in the old days, before the old days became illegal. Serevan recognized the knotting pattern from his childhood, from a village like this one, from a girl named Yssa who had tied such a knot around his wrist when they were both nine and sworn it would keep him from drowning in the millpond.

Yssa had burned twenty years ago, in the first great purging, and Serevan had not drowned, and he had never decided whether the knot had worked.

"What does it do?" he asked the boy.

"It finds things," the boy said. "Lost things. Papa lost his knife in the river and I held the charm over the water and it pulled, sort of, toward where the knife was." His voice wobbled on the last words but his chin stayed up. "It doesn't hurt anybody. It just finds."

"No," Serevan agreed quietly. "It doesn't hurt anybody."

Corren, behind him, shifted his weight. "Captain, the rod sang when we came up the path. Strong reading for a charm-work. The boy has the gift in him proper."

"I know what the rod said, sergeant."

The kitchen was small and clean. A pot of porridge gone cold on the hearth. The mother stood white-faced in the corner with a baby on her hip, and the baby was staring at Serevan with the frank, unafraid gaze of infants, and something about that stare sat wrong in his chest, a stone swallowed sideways.

Protocol was protocol. Charm-works were confiscated and destroyed; bearers were assessed. The boy was a bearer. The collar would go around his neck, sized down, the way they sized them down now — the smiths in the capital had gotten good at it, had whole pattern-books of child collars, and Serevan tried not to think about what it meant that there were pattern-books.

But the boy wasn't looking at the collar. He was looking at the charm on the table, at the little braided rush circle and the crow feather, and his eyes were dry and fierce and full of a grief too large for his face, and Serevan had seen that exact expression once before, in a polished mirror, on the face of a nine-year-old watching soldiers carry his aunt away because she could make cuttings root in winter.

He picked up the charm.

It hummed against his palm. Not the rod's cold singing — something warmer, sillier, a puppy of a spell, all eagerness and no teeth. Find lost things. That was all. In a kingdom of collars and pyres and decrees sealed in wax the color of dried blood, this child had made a thing that found lost things, and the crown had sent armed men across three counties to take it from him.

"Captain?" Corren again, careful now. He'd served under Serevan for six years. He knew the silences.

Serevan carried the charm to the hearth and held it over the coals, because protocol was protocol and his men were watching and the boy was watching and if the captain flinched, the whole machinery of fear that kept these purgings orderly — orderly meaning bloodless, mostly, mostly — would grind loose.

The rush braid caught. The crow feather went up in a bright orange bloom. Red thread curled black.

The boy didn't cry. That was somehow worse. He watched his charm burn with the same raised chin, and when it was ash Serevan collared him — gently, gently, kneeling to do it, meeting his eyes — and the boy said, in a clear voice that carried to every trooper in the room:

"When I'm big, I'm going to find everything you lost."

No one laughed. No one moved. Serevan stood, adjusted his sword belt, and walked out into the light, and for the rest of the day he heard the sentence over and over, not as threat but as prophecy, and he could not make himself disagree with it.

* * *

The orders came that evening, at the crossroads shrine the engineers had scheduled for demolition next week — another scraped-clean holy place, another blank wall where painted saints used to watch over travelers. Serevan read the dispatch by lantern light while his men pitched camp, and he read it twice, because the third paragraph did not make sense.

*Re: the keeper of the valley called Hollowmere.*

He knew the name. Every officer in the purge knew it — the sealed gate, the salt-woman, the valley that kept to itself and bothered no one and was therefore, in the crown's new arithmetic, an anomaly. Anomalies were worse than threats. Threats you could fight. Anomalies you had to explain.

The first two paragraphs were standard: proceed to Hollowmere, assess, purge, secure the gate under Office seal. He'd written half those orders himself, in draft, weeks ago.

The third paragraph was not standard.

*The keeper is to be taken alive and delivered to the capital under close guard. She is not to be harmed. She is not to be collared. She is not, under any circumstance, to be permitted to open the gate. Lord Chancellor Ivell has a particular interest in the keeper and will receive her personally.*

Serevan read it a fourth time. Then a fifth.

She was not to be collared. In eleven years of service to the Office of Purity, Serevan had never once seen an exemption from the collar. The collar was the whole theology of the crown made iron: no gift went ungoverned, no bearer went unmarked, not for lords, not for generals, not for the king's own mad cousin who'd once charmed the tides. Collars did not have exceptions. Exceptions were how the plague had spread in the first place, back when the old kingdoms called gifts *blessings* and let the gifted marry and teach and multiply until the world was so thick with wonder that nobody noticed it rotting from the inside.

And yet here was ink on vellum, Chancellor's seal, saying this one woman — a gatekeeper, a salt-feeder, a woman whose entire life's work was keeping magic *out* — was exempt.

Someone at court knew what she was.

Not suspected. Knew. You did not spend a Chancellor's particular interest on a suspicion. Ivell wanted Mirabel Kavanagh — and the dispatch used her true name, which meant someone had dug through records that Serevan had believed were ash — for something specific. Something the gatekeeper of a forgotten valley could do that no collared gift-bearer in the kingdom could.

Serevan sat with the lantern guttering and turned the problem over the way he turned a blade, looking for flaws in the metal.

Here was what troubled him, though he would have died before saying it aloud: the decree made sense only if the gate mattered. And the gate mattered only if there was something on the other side of it. And the Office of Purity's founding doctrine — the doctrine Serevan had helped write, had taught to recruits, had watched men die believing — was that there was nothing on any other side of anything. Magic was not an invasion. It was a sickness grown native, a fever of the blood, and the cure was fire and iron and time. There were no doors. There was no elsewhere. There was only the kingdom, cleansed and clean.

So why did the Chancellor want the door-keeper breathing?

He thought of the boy in Marrow's Bend. *When I'm big, I'm going to find everything you lost.* Children always saw the shape of a thing before adults did. And the shape of this dispatch, if he squinted, was simple: the crown did not send exemptions after plagues. The crown sent exemptions after treasures.

Which meant either the doctrine was true and the Chancellor was corrupt —

— or the doctrine was the corruption, and Serevan had spent eleven years burning people for a lie.

He fed the dispatch to the lantern flame and watched Ivell's seal blister and blacken, and he felt nothing he could name, which frightened him more than any feeling would have.

"Trouble, Captain?" Corren dropped onto the bedroll beside him, unbuckling his greaves. Behind them the men sang low around the cookfire, one of the marching songs with the verses about cleansing that the Office had commissioned, all trumpets and no doubt.

"The valley," Serevan said. "Hollowmere. What do you know of it?"

Corren shrugged. "Salt-witch and a shut door. Grandmother stories. My gran said the gate keeps the weather out." He grinned. "Superstition. There's nothing behind those doors, Captain. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," Serevan repeated.

The lantern hissed. Away down the slope, in the dark beyond the pickets, the country rolled northward toward the mountains and the valley hidden among them, and somewhere in that darkness a woman was feeding salt to a door, alone, the last of a line of keepers who had died at their posts — and the most powerful man in the kingdom wanted her delivered to his hand, alive and uncollared, like a key.

Like a key.

Serevan lay back on his bedroll and did not sleep. Above him the stars wheeled, indifferent, and he found himself counting them the way he'd counted them as a boy, before the Office, before the fires, when the night sky had still been full of named things instead of cleared ground.

At dawn they would ride for Hollowmere. He would do his duty. He always did his duty.

But for the first time in eleven years, Captain Aldric Serevan rode toward a purge carrying a question he could not put down: *what does a man burn, when everything he believed turns out to be the kindling?*

The hawk appeared an hour past dawn — a dark speck circling high over the northern road, where no bird flew in a land this thoroughly swept. Serevan reined in and watched it, hand shading his eyes, and the men watched him watch it, and no one spoke, because everyone knew the rule: birds meant wildness, wildness meant gift-work, gift-work meant fire.

But the hawk did not dive. It did not scream. It simply circled, patient as a signature, as if something far ahead of them were marking the road — showing them exactly where to come.

Serevan spurred his horse north, faster than the column could follow, and told himself it was eagerness for the hunt.

It was not eagerness. He was beginning to suspect it had never once been eagerness.
