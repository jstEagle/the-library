## 6. Roadside Kin

The waystation announced itself by its absence of anything at all.

Mirabel had been watching the road for an hour from the crest of a gorse-covered hill — watching for red cloaks, for the glint of hunter steel, for the smoke that had followed her out of the valley like a second shadow — and so she almost missed the moment when Tam stopped walking, crouched, and pressed his palm flat against a stretch of road that looked exactly like every other stretch of road.

"It's here," he said. "Underneath."

"Underneath what? There's nothing underneath. There's a ditch and some nettles."

"There's always something underneath." He grinned up at her, that quick fox grin that made him look twelve instead of the fifteen he claimed to be. "That's the whole trick of it. You don't build a smugglers' station where no one looks. You build it where everyone looks and sees nothing." He rapped his knuckles twice on the packed earth, then three times, then once — a rhythm, Mirabel realized, like a knock on a door that happened to be the ground.

For a long moment nothing answered. Then the nettles shivered though there was no wind, and a seam opened in the world — not dramatically, not with light or thunder, but the way a floorboard lifts when you know which one to pull. A hatch. Below it, stairs going down into lamplight and the smell of stewed onions.

Tam's gift had led them here, as it had led them through three locked doors and one locked conversation since the night they'd met. He heard locks, he'd explained — not just metal tumblers but any kind of lock, the lock in a gate, in a purse-string, in a person's silence. *Everything that's shut makes a sound,* he'd said, tapping his ear. *You just have to listen.* And when Mirabel had asked, carefully, not looking at him, whether he could hear the thing that followed her — the cold patch of air that moved when she moved, that leaned closer whenever she was afraid — he had gone very quiet and said only: "It doesn't sound like a lock. It sounds like a door with nothing behind it. I try not to listen to that one."

Now he went down the stairs first, because he was fifteen and invincible, and Mirabel followed because the cold thing followed her, and she would rather it follow her underground than stand exposed on a hilltop under a sky where hawks might circle.

* * *

The waystation was a single low room dug into the hill's root-system, lit by oil lamps turned down to embers, and it held perhaps a dozen people who all stopped talking the instant Mirabel's boots hit the bottom stair. She felt their eyes do the arithmetic travelers' eyes always did: two strangers, one of them a girl with valley mud still dried on her hem, the other a pickpocket child. Trouble, or profit, or both.

A woman behind the counter — broad, gray-streaked, with forearms like a blacksmith's — jerked her chin toward the far corner. "Back table. Stew's a copper. Beds are two. Questions are free but nobody answers them."

"We'll take the stew," Mirabel said, "and we're not asking questions."

The woman's mouth twitched. "Shame. The interesting ones always ask questions." She ladled out two bowls without waiting for payment, which told Mirabel something about how Tam had already been working the room, or about how badly the woman wanted to hear whatever news they carried, or both.

They ate at the back table, shoulder to shoulder, and Mirabel was three spoonfuls into the best meal she'd had since the valley burned when she noticed the two people watching them from across the room.

The man was huge — not fat-huge but built-huge, a wall of a man sitting carefully on a chair clearly not made for him, with a beard going to iron-gray and hands resting on the table like they were used to holding things still. He wore no livery, but Mirabel had grown up around soldiers' daughters and knew the posture of one: spine straight even at rest, weight balanced, eyes doing constant, patient work. Beside him sat a young woman, slight and dark-haired, with ink stains on her fingers and a look Mirabel recognized from her own mirror — the look of someone carrying something heavy and pretending it weighed nothing.

The big man rose, crossed the room, and stood over their table for a moment before asking, in a voice like gravel poured slow:

"You came off the east road. Through Kellin's Ford?"

"Yes," Mirabel said.

"See anyone in red cloaks?"

"No one in cloaks at all. We kept to the trees after the ford."

He nodded slowly, absorbing this, and then did something unexpected: he pulled a stool from a neighboring table, set it down at theirs, and sat, uninvited, with the air of a man joining a family meal rather than intruding on one. The dark-haired woman followed, hesitating, until Tam kicked a stool leg out for her with his foot without looking up from his bowl.

"Brannoc," the big man said. "And Wren." He nodded at the woman. Then, studying Mirabel with those patient soldier's eyes: "You've got ash in your hair. Not woodsmoke-ash. House-ash. Thatch and timber and everything someone owned." His voice didn't soften exactly, but it lowered, the way men lower their voices at gravesides. "Valley country burned three nights back. Whole eastern sky was orange. You come from there?"

Mirabel set down her spoon. Every instinct honed by twenty years of guarding a secret said: lie, deflect, say you're a tanner's daughter traveling to an aunt. But she was so tired. And the stew was warm. And this man had asked about red cloaks before he'd asked about anything else, which meant he wasn't hunting; he was hiding.

"The Hollowvale," she said. "I was its keeper."

Something passed between Brannoc and Wren — not surprise, exactly. Recognition. The particular grief of people who have each lost a home and can name another's loss precisely.

"They took the others?" Brannoc asked.

"Some fled south. Some—" Her throat closed. She thought of Elder Maren, standing in the square with her hands folded, saying *she lives alone at the gate, take her, take her and leave us be*, and thought of the fire climbing the thatch of the house where Maren's grandchildren slept, and could not decide even now whether that was betrayal or the most terrible mercy a woman had ever performed. "Some stayed. It doesn't matter now. The decree says every gift-bearer gets taken or burned, and the valley decided I was one."

"And are you?" Wren asked quietly. It wasn't accusation. It was the question you ask when you need to know if the person beside you will be dragged away in the night.

Mirabel looked at her hands. "I keep things," she said finally. "Sealed things. Sealed things stay sealed better when I'm near them. That's my whole gift, if it is one. Salt and silence and a gate that hasn't opened in a hundred years." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And now the gate's behind me and I don't know what I am anymore. A keeper with nothing kept."

Across the table, Tam had gone very still, the way he did when he was listening to something no one else could hear. Mirabel didn't look at him. She didn't want to know what the cold thing sounded like tonight.

* * *

It was Wren who broke the silence first, and she broke it by putting her own hands palm-up on the table.

"I heal people," she said. "That sounds noble until I tell you the price. Every wound I close, I take a piece of it into myself. Not the pain — the cost. A fever I draw out takes a day of my strength. A broken bone knits, and my hair goes a little whiter. Last winter I pulled a drowning boy back from the edge and my heart has beat thin ever since, like a bird's." She turned her hands over, showing the fine white streaks threading through her dark hair like frost through a windowpane. "So I don't do it often anymore. Which means I sit beside people who are hurt, and I count what fixing them would cost me, and sometimes I pay it anyway, and sometimes I can't, and either way I carry it." She smiled, small and crooked. "The crown calls that plague. I call it arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Mirabel repeated softly.

"That's what my mother called it. She was a healer too. Died of it, eventually — spent herself down to nothing patching up a fever-camp the lords wouldn't send real medicine to." Wren shrugged, and the shrug was armor, and Mirabel recognized that too. "Anyway. That's mine. Brannoc's turn."

Brannoc cleared his throat like a landslide deciding whether to happen. "Stone-sense," he said at last. "I feel what stone feels. Where it's cracked, where it's tired, where it wants to fall. Made me useful as a siege engineer. Made me useless as a knight, eventually, because when the order sent me to collar a village of gift-bearers, I put my hand on the keep wall and the wall told me it was ashamed." He said it flatly, without drama, but his knuckles whitened around his cup. "I refused. They stripped my spurs, broke my sword in front of the garrison, and gave me a choice: the collar or the road. I took the road." He drank. "Been on it four years. Turns out there's a lot of us on it."

The lamp flames leaned in a draft from the stairwell. Somewhere above, wind combed the gorse. And Mirabel sat in the warmth of stolen light among three people who had each been unmade by the same decree, and felt something she hadn't felt since the valley burned: not safety, exactly — she doubted she'd feel safe again this side of the grave — but *company*. The specific, astonishing company of being understood without explanation.

"So," Tam said into the quiet, wiping his bowl with bread. "We're all plague, then. Nice little outbreak we've got here."

Brannoc snorted. Wren laughed — really laughed, surprised out of her, a bright sound that made half the room glance over and then quickly away. And Mirabel found her own mouth curving, achingly, like a muscle remembering an old shape.

* * *

They talked until the lamps burned low.

Not about important things, at first — or what passed for important things. Brannoc told them about the worst road he'd ever built, in the rain, for a lord who paid in promises. Wren described a midwife in the border towns who taught her that healing was mostly knowing when *not* to touch. Tam reenacted, with full voices and sound effects, the time he'd picked the wrong pocket in Drossmarket and lifted a purse off an off-duty hunter-sergeant, and narrated his own nine-block escape with such relish that Brannoc laughed hard enough to make the table jump.

And somewhere in the middle of it — Mirabel couldn't have said when, exactly; these things never announce themselves — the table stopped being four strangers and became something else. She noticed it in small ways. Wren started refilling Mirabel's bowl without asking, the way you feed family. Brannoc shifted his enormous frame to block the draft from the stair, angling himself so the cold air hit his back instead of hers, and said nothing about it, and would have denied it if asked. Tam fell asleep sitting up against Brannoc's arm somewhere past midnight, mid-sentence, the way children do when their bodies have finally decided a place is safe.

Mirabel watched the boy sleep and felt her chest ache in a way that had nothing to do with the cold thing inside her.

She had spent her whole life alone at a gate. That had been the shape of the oath: one keeper, one door, one lifetime of salt and silence. Her mother had done it before her, and her grandmother, and every keeper back into the fog of years, each of them dying at their post with the gate sealed and the hollow fed and no one, not one soul, beside them at the end. She had believed that loneliness was the price and also the proof — that if it hurt, it must be working.

But sitting here, warm, among the plague-ridden and the disgraced and the spent, listening to Brannoc describe the exact angle at which a bad bridge fails, she understood for the first time that her solitude had never been the oath. It had only ever been the shape her fear had taken. The hollow had whispered to her mother too, surely. Had offered rest. Had made the lonely post seem holy so that no keeper would ever think to ask for help.

*Power shared is power survived.* The thought arrived whole, from nowhere, like a hawk crossing a sky where no bird should fly, and Mirabel held very still around it, the way you hold still around a wild thing you don't want to startle.

"What are you thinking?" Wren asked. She was watching Mirabel with those ink-stained fingers curled around her cup, and her eyes were kind and tired and sharp all at once.

"I'm thinking," Mirabel said slowly, "that I spent twenty years believing my job was to be alone. And it took me three days and a bowl of terrible stew to wonder if that was ever true."

Brannoc grunted — his version of deep agreement. "The order teaches you that too. One knight, one oath, die alone, very noble." He turned his cup. "Took me four years to learn the noble part was the lie. The dying alone was just bad planning."

Wren reached across the table and touched Mirabel's wrist — lightly, a healer's touch, reading her pulse without meaning to. "Your heart's racing," she murmured. "Fear?"

"No." Mirabel turned her hand over and, before she could talk herself out of it, closed her fingers briefly around Wren's. "Hope. It presents similarly."

* * *

It was nearly dawn when the trouble came, because trouble keeps honest hours the way smugglers don't.

Tam woke all at once, head snapping up, hand pressed flat against the floorboards. "Horses," he breathed. "Lots. On the road above. Moving slow." His eyes were wide and unfocused, listening with his whole body. "Iron shoes. Saddle leather creaking. And—" his face went gray— "collar-chains. A lot of chains."

Brannoc was already moving, silent despite his size, dousing lamps. Around the room the other smugglers stirred like a warren going to ground, hatches opening, bodies dropping into hidden pockets beneath the floor. The gray-haired woman appeared at their table with a lamp in one hand and a short sword in the other, entirely unbothered.

"Patrol, maybe," she said. "Or a supply run. Or hunters. Can't tell from down here and I'm not opening the hatch to check."

"Then we don't wait to find out," Brannoc rumbled. He looked at Mirabel, and it was strange, she thought, how naturally he looked to her — the newest among them, the keeper of nothing — as if keeping things were simply what she did, wherever she went. "There's a back way. Old drainage cut, comes up in the stream-bed half a mile north. Stone's sound. I can feel it from here." He tapped the floor with one huge hand. "You four go. I'll hold the hatch."

"No," Wren said instantly.

"Wren—"

"You'll hold nothing alone. That's the whole point of—" she gestured at the table, at the bowls, at the night, at all of it— "*this*. Whatever this is."

Mirabel looked at the four of them in the dark: the disgraced knight with his hand on the floor listening to stone, the healer counting costs she might refuse to pay, the boy with his ear against the boards hearing chains, and herself, keeper of a sealed gate a hundred miles behind her with something cold breathing at her shoulder. None of them should have trusted the others. All of them had reasons not to. And yet here they were, arguing about who would die first for whom, and it was the most sensible argument she'd ever been part of.

"Nobody holds anything alone," she said. "Back way. All five of us. Now."

They went down through the drainage cut single file, Brannoc leading with his palm trailing along the stone like a man greeting old friends, Wren behind him, then Tam, then Mirabel — and behind Mirabel, patient as winter, the cold thing followed, and for the first time it felt less like being hunted than like being escorted.

They came up in the stream-bed as the sky went gray, and lay in the shallows while hoofbeats passed on the road above — dozens of riders, and the thin music of many chains, going south toward the capital.

When the sound faded, Tam rolled onto his back in the freezing water and started laughing, breathless and slightly hysterical. "We're alive," he said. "We're alive and we're all plague and there's five of us now."

"Six," Brannoc corrected, nodding upstream.

Mirabel followed his gaze. Standing in the shallows fifty yards off, half-hidden by reeds, was a tall figure in a travel-stained coat, silver-haired, with a leather map-case slung across her back and a look on her face like a woman who had been following them for two days and had opinions about it.

"Well," the stranger called across the water, in an accent from nowhere Mirabel knew. "If you're all going to be this loud about surviving, the least you can do is let me draw it."
