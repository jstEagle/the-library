## 8. City of Erased Names

Wren had never seen a city before. She had seen the word *city* on Odile's maps — a small black star with a name beside it, *Caldoreth*, and beneath it in the mapmaker's cramped hand: *they took the names off the doors first.*

She understood what that meant when the road crested the last hill and the capital opened below them like a wound that had learned to dress itself.

It was beautiful. That was the first betrayal of it. White walls and slate roofs going down to a river the color of pewter, spires needling a sky gone soft with evening, ten thousand chimneys breathing smoke into the amber light. Bells were ringing somewhere, three towers out of step with one another, so that the sound came in waves, overlapping, almost like singing.

"It's lovely," Tam said, and then caught himself, because he was from a city himself, a smaller one, and he knew better than to say such things aloud here.

"It's a mouth," Brannoc said quietly. "Lovely mouths have teeth."

They went down through the outer districts as the lamps were lit, four travelers among hundreds funneling toward the gates — carters, pilgrims, a woman leading geese, soldiers off a river barge laughing too loudly at nothing. Wren kept her hood up and her eyes down and did what she had taught herself to do since the valley burned: she counted exits. The postern by the tannery. The drainage culvert under the wall where the smell would cover them. The rooftops Tam could climb if it came to running.

At the gate, a bored sergeant waved them through after a glance at their faces and a longer glance at Mirabel, who had the kind of face that made bored sergeants look twice. Wren felt the hollow thing that followed Mirabel shift against her back like cold water settling, though no one else seemed to notice it anymore. She was getting used to the chill. That frightened her more than the chill itself.

Inside, the city swallowed them.

* * *

Odile had given them instructions written in her precise, joyless hand: *Enter at dusk. Leave before the second bell of morning. Do not stop in the Lantern Quarter. Do not look at the walls. If you must look at the walls, do not weep where anyone can see you.*

Wren looked at the walls.

The first mural was in a merchant square, high on a plastered gable where everyone passing could see it — or where everyone passing could see what remained of it. Someone had scraped it away with iron tools, methodically, leaving only the ghost of the image: the pale outline of figures where the paint around them had weathered differently, shadows of shapes cut out of the world. She could make out raised arms. A circle of people. Something above them that might have been wings or might have been light.

Beneath the scraping, someone had chiseled a new inscription into fresh stone:

**THE CROWN HEALS WHAT THE GIFT CORRUPTS**

"Heals," Wren said, and the word came out of her like a splinter.

"Keep walking," Brannoc murmured. His hand hovered near her elbow without touching, the way he did when he wanted to steer someone without making it obvious. He was very good at not being noticed. It was a skill, she suspected, that had been beaten into him rather than learned.

But she looked anyway. At the next square there had been a painting of a woman standing in a river with her arms spread wide, and now there was only river, scraped clean, a woman-shaped absence in the middle of the current. In the Lantern Quarter they were told not to enter, a whole street of facades had been stripped bare, every figure removed, so that the buildings wore their history like plucked chickens. And outside the great cathedral — the one whose bells rang out of step — stood a statue on a tall plinth, and Wren stopped despite everything, because the statue was wrong in a way that took her a moment to understand.

It was a king, triumphant, sword raised. But the pedestal beneath him bore older carvings, half-chiseled-away: hands. Dozens of carved hands, reaching up toward the king's boots, and each hand had been defaced just enough to make it ambiguous whether they were reaching in worship or in supplication or to pull him down.

"They didn't destroy it," Tam breathed beside her. "They couldn't be bothered to. They just changed what it means."

"That's worse," Wren said.

"Yes," said Tam, who had grown up learning which words not to say, and said this one anyway, softly, looking up at the mutilated hands. "That's much worse."

Mirabel touched the pedestal once, briefly, with two fingers, the way she touched the gate in her valley — salt and silence, feeding something. Then she walked on, and they walked with her.

* * *

They saw the first collar in a bread queue.

A man, middle-aged, hands cracked from labor, standing patiently with his ration card held in his teeth while he tied his bootlace. The collar sat snug against his throat: dull grey metal, seamless, fitted so closely it looked grown rather than made. As Wren passed, the man laughed at something the baker's wife said — a real laugh, warm, startled out of him — and the collar flared.

Just for a heartbeat. A thin line of white light along its seam, and the man's laugh died mid-breath. He swayed. Caught himself on the baker's stall. Stood there for a moment with his eyes unfocused, his mouth still shaped around the laugh that had been taken out of it, and then he finished tying his bootlace and shuffled forward in the queue like a man wading through deep water.

Nobody in the queue reacted at all. Nobody even looked up. That was the part that made Wren's hands shake — not the collar, but the queue, the practiced blindness of forty people who had all decided, together, without ever speaking of it, that this was simply what throats did sometimes.

"Suppression collars," Odile had told them, weeks ago, over her maps. "Tuned to the bearer's gift. They don't remove it — you can't remove a gift, any more than you can remove a spine. They punish it. Every time the gift stirs — joy, grief, fear, anything strong enough to wake it — pain. Until the bearer learns to keep themselves flat. Quiet. Small." She had tapped the map, at Caldoreth, at the little black star. "There are thousands in the capital alone."

"Why not kill them?" Mirabel had asked, in the flat voice she used for questions she already dreaded the answer to.

"Because dead gifts are wasted gifts," Odile said. "Collared bearers can be leased. Mines. Shipyards. Places where a stone-sense or a water-craft is useful, so long as it never grows strong enough to matter. The crown doesn't hate the gift, keeper. The crown hates that it belongs to the person carrying it."

Wren had thought, then, that she understood. Standing in the bread queue's aftermath, watching the man shuffle away with his laughter amputated, she understood that she had understood nothing.

Because Wren carried a gift herself, and hers was the cruelest kind: it worked. When she healed — truly healed, flesh knitting, fever breaking, bone setting with a sound like green wood bending — the cost came out of her own body. A year off her hair. A season off her heart. She had whitened early; there was frost in her brown hair that no dye had put there, and her pulse, when she lay awake counting it, ran thin and quick as a bird's. Every healing was a small death paid in installments.

And these collars — these grey, seamless, patient things — made gifts hurt *without using them*. They punished people for what they were, not what they did. Her sacrifice, at least, bought something. This bought nothing. This was a tax levied on existence.

She was still staring after the man when Brannoc's hand finally landed on her shoulder, heavy as a roof beam.

"Walk," he said.

* * *

They lodged that night in a smugglers' safehouse behind a chandler's shop — Odile's arrangements, paid for in coin and favors — a low room smelling of tallow and river damp, with one window overlooking a yard full of broken boats. Tam went out to listen to the city, because listening was what he did, and Brannoc slept sitting up with his back to the wall as he always did, and Mirabel sat by the window watching the dark yard with the particular stillness she fell into when the hollow-thing pressed close.

Wren couldn't sleep.

Her body wouldn't let her. It was an old companion, the restlessness — the thin blood, the quick heart, the sense that sleep was a currency she'd already overspent. So she slipped out into the chandler's yard and then into the alley beyond, telling herself she was only walking, only breathing air that didn't smell of tallow, only doing the foolish thing she always did.

The city at night was quieter and more honest. Lamplighters worked the streets with long poles. Drunkards sang in doorways. And in a court off Cooper's Row, behind a shrine no bigger than a cupboard — a shrine to some saint whose name had been chiseled off the lintel, leaving only *SAINT OF THE—* — Wren heard crying.

Not loud crying. The other kind. The kind that has learned to be quiet, which is the worst kind, the kind that comes from people who have been punished for noise.

She found the child curled in the shrine's doorway. A girl, seven or eight perhaps, in a servant's smock much too big, knees drawn up, forehead pressed to her knees. And around her neck, absurdly, obscenely, a suppression collar — child-sized, which meant somewhere a smith had measured a child's throat and forged metal to fit it, and somewhere a clerk had signed the order, and somewhere a priest had blessed it, and all of them had gone home to their suppers afterward.

"Hey," Wren said softly. "Hey, little one. What hurts?"

The girl flinched from her voice before she even lifted her head, and Wren understood — of course she flinched; kindness was a lure here; kindness was how they found you — and then the girl looked up, and Wren saw her face, and saw the collar, and saw the fine burn-blistering where the collar's edge had chafed the skin raw.

And underneath the blistering, faint as heat-shimmer, Wren saw the gift.

She couldn't have said how she knew. Gifts didn't show. But she had spent her life around the gifted and the hiding-gifted, and there was a way such children held themselves — coiled, apologetic, braced for punishment — and there was a way the collar sat on this girl like a muzzle on a dog that had done nothing but wag its tail. The girl's hands were cupped in her lap, and in the cupped hands, barely visible, a moth was glowing. Faintly. Softly. Green-gold light pulsing with the beat of its wings, like a coal wrapped in silk.

A light-bringer. A tiny one. The smallest kind of miracle there was, and they had collared her for it.

"You have to put it out," Wren whispered, kneeling. "Quickly, sweetheart, before—"

"I can't," the girl whispered back, and her voice cracked apart. "I can't make it go dark. I'm scared and it gets brighter when I'm scared and I got scared and now it won't—" The glow pulsed brighter. The collar began to sing, a thin high whine of building charge, and the girl whimpered and the glow brightened further and the whine climbed—

Wren did the arithmetic she always did. The same arithmetic, the same columns, the same merciless sums. *Cost of healing:* a month of her hair, perhaps two. A flutter of the heart that might take a week to steady. Against: a child's blistered throat tonight, and the beating — or worse — that a glowing child in a doorway would earn by morning, from watchmen who were paid by the collar-violation, who had quotas.

Against: the risk. Four fugitives in a safehouse. A healer kneeling in open lamplight, hands glowing with borrowed grace, next to a collared child. If anyone saw—

The collar discharged.

It was small, as discharges went — a warning sting, not a full strike — but the girl screamed, a tiny bitten-off scream, and the light in her hands flared like a struck match, and Wren's hands were already moving.

She had told herself, for years, that her gift was a debt she chose when to pay. Liar. She had never once chosen. Her hands chose. Her hands had always chosen, from the first time she'd closed them over her brother's broken arm in the valley and felt the ache climb her own bones like frost climbing a windowpane.

Warmth left her palms. Not light exactly, but the memory of light, the way a stone holds the sun after sunset. It sank into the girl's throat and the blisters smoothed; it sank into the collar's burn-track and cooled it; and — this was the reckless part, the part Wren would lie awake examining later — it sank into the collar itself, into the mechanism, into whatever cruel tuned-spring the crown's artificers had wound inside it, and *stilled* it. Quieted the whine. Not broke it — she wasn't sure it could be broken, wasn't sure anything could be broken that had been built this well — but hushed it, the way you hush a bell with your hand.

And the girl's gift answered.

The moth-light rose out of her cupped hands. It spread up her arms like dawn coming up over water, and the child's face — tear-streaked, terrified, six hours past a beating she'd been trying not to cry about — the child's face *changed*. Wren had seen that change before, in valley children, in soldiers dying too young, in Brannoc the one time she'd mended the old break in his shoulder that he'd carried crooked for twenty years. It was the face of a person remembering that their body belonged to them.

"Oh," the girl said. "Oh, it doesn't hurt. It doesn't hurt, it never doesn't hurt—"

"Hush now. Hush. Listen to me." Wren took the small face in her hands and made her voice into the voice she used for the dying, calm and certain and entirely false. "You're going to be brave in the boring way. Not the loud way. You're going to keep your light small, and keep it secret, and you're going to live until you're grown. Do you hear me? There are people — I know people — who help children like you get out of this city. There's a woman who draws maps of places that don't exist anymore, and she'll draw a place for you. Can you find your way back to wherever you sleep?"

The girl nodded, wide-eyed, both hands pressed flat over her quieted collar as if holding a wounded bird.

"Then go. Now. Walk, don't run. Running gets seen."

The girl went — paused at the alley's mouth to look back, and for one heartbeat her whole small body glowed like a lantern, gratitude too big to contain, and Wren's heart clenched with terror and something else, something fierce and unnameable — and then the child was gone into the dark, and Wren was alone in the shrine-court, swaying, one hand against the saintless shrine.

The cost arrived on schedule. Frost spread through her; she felt it leave her scalp in a slow tide, and knew without checking that there would be new white in her hair come morning, a stripe of it, maybe two. Her heart stumbled, caught, stumbled again, found its rhythm one beat short. She breathed through it, bent double, hands on knees, doing the other arithmetic — the one she never spoke aloud, the one where she subtracted and subtracted and tried not to ask what remained.

"Wren."

Brannoc's voice, low, from the alley mouth. Of course. Of course he'd followed her; he followed everyone; it was how he loved people, badly and constantly, by shadowing them through dangerous places.

"How much?" he asked. Not *what happened*. Not *are you mad*. Just: how much.

"A stripe," she said. "Maybe two. My heart's sulking but it'll settle."

He came and stood over her, a mountain against the lamplight, and said nothing for a long moment. Then: "The girl. Is she safe?"

"For tonight. Not for always. None of them are safe for always, Brannoc, that's rather the entire—" She stopped, because her voice had climbed, and voices that climbed got heard, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

Through the gap between the chandler's shop and the shrine, she could see down Cooper's Row to the wider street beyond, and on the far wall of that street, huge in the lamplight, stretched another scraped mural — the largest yet, covering an entire granary wall. Whole figures survived at its edges: a crowd, rendered lovingly, dozens of faces turned upward in wonder. And in the center, scraped to bare plaster, a vast absence the size of a barn, with one clue remaining at its base: a single unscraped foot, painted in gold, mid-step, as if its owner had been walking out of the picture when the erasers reached them.

Hundreds of faces, painted remembering. One absence, painted enormous. The crown hadn't even bothered to paint over it with something else. The emptiness itself was the message: *this much was taken from you, and we are not afraid to show you the shape of the hole.*

Wren looked at the golden foot. She thought about a child's throat under her hands, cooling. She thought about the queue of people who hadn't looked up. She thought about the frost in her own hair, purchased honestly, spent on purpose, and about the collars that spent nothing and saved nothing and only flattened human beings into surfaces, like walls waiting to be scraped.

"I used to think," she said quietly, to Brannoc, to the golden foot, to no one, "that my gift was expensive and theirs was cheap. That paying in years made me better than whoever built those things." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "But they've built a whole city where nobody pays for anybody. Where the only rule is: cost nothing, risk nothing, feel nothing, save no one. That's their arithmetic. Zero all the way down."

Brannoc put his hand on her head, carefully, the way he touched everything he was afraid of breaking, and found the new stripe of white without looking for it.

"Then we're the error in their sums," he said.

Behind them, in the safehouse window, Mirabel suddenly went rigid — Wren saw it, the whole line of her stiffening like a wire — and Mirabel's voice came down into the yard, low and urgent and stripped of everything but warning:

"Someone's coming. Torches — six, no, eight — they're stopping at the chandler's front. Wren, *get inside* — they're asking the chandler who lodged here tonight."

Down Cooper's Row, a whistle shrilled. Another answered it, closer.

The hunt had found the city.
