## 9. A Bargain with the Court

The palace of Corvenne smelled of lilies and lye.

Captain Serevan noticed it at once, because he had spent eleven years learning to read places by their smells. Lilies meant wealth — hothouse flowers in a season when nothing bloomed outdoors but frost. The lye meant fear. Somewhere below the marble floors, servants scrubbed blood out of stone every night, and no amount of perfume could quite bury it. He had burned enough gift-bearers to know what that particular sharpness was: ash and salt water, the smell of things erased.

He stood in the antechamber outside the Chancellor's offices for two hours before they let him in, which was itself a message. Captains who had just razed three valleys did not usually wait. He waited anyway, hands folded behind his back, watching the light move across the floor like something cautious.

When the doors finally opened, a chamberlain in silver-grey led him down a corridor lined with portraits. Every frame held a face scraped blank — not removed, not replaced, but *scraped*, the paint lifted away in long pale strokes so that each portrait showed only the suggestion of a person: a collar of rank, a shoulder, a suggestion of jaw. The plaques beneath them bore names half-effaced by time and half by chisel.

Serevan had walked this corridor once before, as a young lieutenant, and had thought it merely old-fashioned. Now he understood it for what it was. The crown did not hang portraits of its enemies. It hung portraits of its *corrections*.

"Captain." The voice came from the far end of the room, warm as banked coals. "You've made excellent time. Sit. Drink. You look like a man who has been sleeping in saddles."

Lord Chancellor Ivell sat behind a desk of black walnut so highly polished it seemed to float on its own reflection. He was not what Serevan expected. He was small — narrow through the shoulders, fine-boned, with the kind of neat grey beard that suggested it was trimmed by another man's hand. His eyes were pale hazel, almost colorless, and they did not blink as often as eyes should.

There were two cups on the desk. Only one was poured.

"Thank you, Lord Chancellor." Serevan took the offered chair but left the wine. "I'll stand on ceremony better sober."

"Of course you will." Ivell smiled as if Serevan had said something charming. "That's why I asked for you specifically. Captain Serevan. Eleven years of service to the Decree. Four hundred and six gift-bearers taken or burned — I've read your tallies, all of them, including the ones your superiors chose not to forward." He folded his small hands. "You are, by any honest measure, the most effective instrument the crown possesses."

"Instrument," Serevan repeated. The word sat strangely. He had been called many things — hound, blade, scourge, and worse by those who ran faster than their neighbors. Instrument was new. Instruments could be picked up or set down.

"A compliment," Ivell said mildly. "Instruments do not tire. They do not grow sentimental. And they do not ask questions." He leaned back. "Which brings me to why you're here. Tell me about the valley. Kavanagh's Valley. You burned it nine days ago."

"I burned it eight days ago." Serevan kept his voice level. "And I didn't finish the job. One escaped. Mirabel Kavanagh, the gate-keeper's daughter. Twenty-six years old, no recorded gift, no recorded anything. The village elder gave her up under questioning, but by the time my riders reached her cottage she'd gone through—" he paused, choosing the words carefully, "—through the sealed gate at the valley's head. The one that's been shut for as long as anyone can remember."

"And now it isn't."

"Now it isn't."

Ivell nodded slowly, and for a moment his eyes went somewhere else entirely — some private ledger only he could read. "Do you know what she is, Captain?"

"No, my lord. That's rather what I hoped to learn here."

"What she is," Ivell said, "is a key."

He rose, crossed to a cabinet against the wall, and unlocked it with a small key from his sleeve. What he brought back was not a document. It was a ring of iron, old iron, black with age, set with a stone that might have been glass. He laid it on the desk between them.

"This was recovered from the ruins of Vessany."

Serevan did not move, but everything in him went still, the way a hunting dog goes still when scent turns to sight. Everyone knew about Vessany the way everyone knew about the dark under a bed — as a shape, a story, a thing parents used to frighten children into obedience. *Behave, or you'll be taken to Vessany.* Except Vessany wasn't a threat anymore. Vessany didn't exist. It had been unwritten forty years ago, struck from maps and records and memory by decree, until most people honestly believed there had never been such a country at all — only empty moorland where the borders bent wrong.

"There is no Vessany," Serevan said.

"There is no Vessany on maps," Ivell corrected. "There is no Vessany in the census rolls, the tax ledgers, the histories. There is no Vessany in the mouths of men, because we made very sure of that." He tapped the iron ring. "But there is a Vessany in the ground, Captain. Roads under the heather. Doors under the hills. A whole country lying quiet with its breath held — and in it, hiding like a tick in the fur, the true heir to a line older than ours. The child we failed to burn, forty years ago, who is a man now. A man with a claim. A name. And, given time, an army."

The fire crackled. Somewhere in the walls, a clock ticked with the patience of institutions.

"The old king solved the problem of Vessany by erasing it," Ivell continued. "A magnificent act of will. Cartographers unmade, records burned, witnesses collared or silenced. But erasure is not destruction, Captain. It is forgetting. And forgotten things have a way of being remembered by people who shouldn't. Smugglers. Exiles. Mapmakers." His lip thinned on the last word. "We have reports of a woman called Odile Vasseur. Once court cartographer. Now a traitor who charts dead places — draws them back into the world, line by line. She has been seen traveling with a boy who hears locks and, more recently, with a woman matching Kavanagh's description."

"You want me to bring her in."

"I want you to bring in *Mirabel Kavanagh*. Alive. Unmarked. Odile Vasseur is a nuisance; the boy is a nuisance; the hedge-knight and the healer traveling with them are nuisances. Kill them if they resist — I won't mourn. But Kavanagh comes to me breathing, and preferably willing." Ivell's colorless eyes fixed on him. "Her blood opens gates. All gates. The seal on her valley was keyed to her family's blood, generation upon generation — and there were other seals, once. Other doors into Vessany, locked by the same craft, the same old compact. If her blood opens one, it opens them all. I don't need an army to march into Vessany, Captain. I need one girl to turn a lock. And then the heir, the treasury, the whole buried country rises up out of the heather — and I hand it to the crown, intact, conquered without a single battle."

Serevan looked at the iron ring on the desk. It was warm-looking, somehow, though the room was cold. "And if she refuses?"

"Then we persuade her. Her friends make excellent persuasion, I find. People will open almost any door to save someone they love." Ivell said it the way other men discussed grain prices. "You'll have authority over the garrisons along the border march. Whatever you need. Whatever you must burn."

It was everything Serevan had worked toward for eleven years. Not just a commission — a *mandate*. Resources. Rank. The ear of the Chancellor himself. He thought of the promotion list he'd been passed over for twice, the commanders who got the glory while he got the mud and the smoke and the mothers' curses. This was the door. This was the door, and here was a man handing him the handle.

So why did his skin feel too tight?

"May I speak plainly, my lord?"

"You may. Plainness is rare here. I collect it."

"Why me? You have colonels. You have the King's Own. Why send a captain who burns villages?"

Ivell studied him for a long moment, and something moved behind the colorless eyes — not warmth, but interest, which was perhaps more dangerous. "Because four hundred and six times, Captain, you have done exactly what was required and no more. I've read the accounts. You give the gifted a chance to surrender. You spare children where you can. You burn charms, not people, whenever the law allows you the distinction." He smiled faintly. "You think I don't notice these things. I notice everything. I need a man who wants the girl alive as much as I do — not because he's soft, but because he understands that a dead key opens nothing. Your conscience, Captain, is precisely what makes you qualified."

"My conscience," Serevan said quietly, "is not for sale."

"No. But it can be *used*." Ivell slid a sealed commission across the desk — heavy paper, the royal sigil in green wax. "Full authority. Field rank of Commander. And when Kavanagh stands in my audience chamber, a generalship, with lands. Think of it, Serevan. No more valleys. No more smoke. You could spend the rest of your career building instead of burning."

Building. The word landed in him like a hook finding flesh. He thought of the child's charm-work he'd burned in Harrow Fen — a little wooden bird that sang when you held it, harmless, beautiful, illegal — and how the girl who owned it had watched it curl in the flames with an expression he still saw sometimes when he closed his eyes. He had told himself for years that the burning was necessary. That gifts were plague. That mercy was how plagues spread.

He had never quite managed to believe it about the wooden bird.

"I accept," he heard himself say.

"Wonderful." Ivell extended his small hand, and Serevan shook it. The Chancellor's grip was dry and cool and precise, like shaking hands with a ledger. "Your orders are being drawn up. You ride within the week. Oh — and Captain?" Ivell's smile did not change, but the temperature of the room seemed to drop a degree. "The girl's mother kept a journal. It's in my vaults. If you find anything of hers on the road — letters, keepsakes, papers — they come to me. Unread. Am I understood?"

"Understood, my lord."

* * *

They gave him quarters in the east wing, a room with a bed softer than anything he'd slept in for a decade, and he lay awake on top of it fully clothed, staring at the ceiling, listening to the palace breathe.

He had accepted. There was no taking it back now; the commission bore his name and the green wax was already cooling around his ambition. Commander Serevan. General Serevan, if the gods were kind and the girl cooperative. A life of building. A life of never again standing in a doorway watching a family's whole world go up in sparks.

Unease walked the edges of the room like a cat.

It was the corridor, partly. Those scraped faces. He had served the Decree his whole adult life and told himself it was surgery — cutting away sickness so the body lived. But a country that scraped the faces off its own ancestors was not performing surgery. A country that needed its history blank was afraid of something in it. And men who were afraid, he had learned, did not build. They only burned, and burned, and called the ashes peace.

And it was the way Ivell had said *willingly*. Preferably willing. As if willingness were a resource to be mined, and the mining could be done with friends and love and threats dressed as bargains. Serevan had dragged gift-bearers from cellars and haylofts for eleven years, and he knew coercion down to its bones. But there was something in the Chancellor's careful, pleasant voice that made even coercion sound clean, and that cleanliness frightened him more than any screaming ever had.

Near midnight, unable to bear the soft bed a moment longer, he went walking.

The palace at night was a different animal. Servants ghosted through side passages with candles cupped against drafts. Guards nodded to him — his new commission already known, news traveling faster than horses in a place like this. He wandered without direction until he found himself, without surprise, in the records wing: long galleries of shelved documents, the archive of the realm, smelling of dust and tallow and the slow decay of truth.

Two clerks were working late by lamplight, sorting a crate of confiscated materials. Confiscations came north from the border country constantly now — smuggled goods, seditious pamphlets, and lately, if the rumors held, papers seized from a certain exiled mapmaker's abandoned waystations. Serevan slowed as he passed their table.

On the top of the pile lay a sheet of heavy rag paper, inked in a fine, sure hand.

He knew it at once, though he'd never seen the original. He'd seen copies — poor copies, circulated in secret, the kind the smugglers passed hand to hand and the Decree burned on sight. A map. But not a map of anywhere. The parchment showed a stretch of moorland rendered in loving detail — every stream, every standing stone, every fold of hill — and across the middle of it, where any honest atlas would show either farmland or emptiness, the cartographer had written, in letters both defiant and grieving:

*Here lies Vessany. Remember it for me.*

Around the words, half-finished, roads were beginning to surface under the ink like bones under skin. A bridge. An orchard, sketched in blossom. And in the corner, a compass rose unlike any Serevan had seen — its points labeled not with directions but with names. *Salt. Silence. Blood. Door.*

The clerks had stepped away to fetch more lamp oil. The gallery stretched empty in both directions.

Serevan stood looking at the map for a long time. He thought about orders. Everything of hers comes to me, unread. He thought about the green wax cooling on his commission. He thought about the wooden bird, singing as it burned.

Then he took off his glove, slid the page inside his coat, over his heart, where the cold couldn't get at it, and walked out of the gallery with the steady, unhurried stride of a man carrying nothing at all.

Behind him, the lamps guttered. Ahead of him, somewhere in the dark west, a girl with a key in her veins was running — and for the first time in eleven years, Captain Serevan found he did not know, when he caught her, whose side of the door he would be standing on.
