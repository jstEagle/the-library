## 10. River of Small Treasons

The river was called the Liss, though the smugglers who poled their flat-bottomed barge down it had a dozen other names for it — the Long Slip, the Quiet Road, the King's Blind Eye — and none of them appeared on any map Odile owned. That was rather the point.

Brannoc stood at the stern with his arms folded and his weight settled wide, the way he'd learned to stand on siege walls twenty years ago, and watched the banks slide past in the grey light of a day that couldn't decide whether to rain. Willows trailed their fingers in the water. A heron lifted off from a fallen oak with an indignant croak. Behind him, under the patched canvas awning amid crates of salted eels and untaxed brandy, his strange little family slept or pretended to: Tam curled against a coil of rope like a cat in a sunbeam that wasn't there; Wren sitting upright even in sleep, her white-streaked hair loose over her shoulders; Odile at the bow with her maps spread across an upturned crate, muttering corrections to a country that no longer existed; and Mirabel —

Mirabel sat apart, as she always did, her back against the mast-step, turning a river stone over and over in her fingers. Watching the water. Or watching something *under* the water. Brannoc had spent enough years reading stone to know when someone was listening to something deeper than stone.

He didn't ask. He was learning not to ask. It was harder than it sounded for a man whose gift had always been about asking — laying his palm against a wall and hearing what it had to say about its foundations, its cracks, its honest weight. People were worse than walls. Walls never lied about what they were carrying.

"You're doing the face," said Tam, without opening his eyes.

"What face?"

"The one where you're thinking about confessing to something." The boy cracked one eye. "You get a crease. Right there. Like a dog deciding whether to eat the roast."

"I don't have a crease."

"You have three creases and this is a fourth." Tam rolled onto his side, propping his head on his hand, all eleven years of him radiating the smug patience of someone who had heard every lock in three cities open and close and considered himself an expert on doors of every kind. "Go on, then. Whatever it is, it's eating you worse than the eel stew ate Wren."

From beneath her blanket, Wren said, "That stew nearly killed me," which proved she wasn't asleep either, which meant none of them were, which meant the whole barge was lying awake in the same grey morning pretending otherwise.

Brannoc looked at Mirabel. She had gone still, the stone stopped mid-turn, and he understood that she was awake too, and listening, and that whatever he said next would land on all of them whether he wanted it to or not.

Well. He'd carried it long enough. Stone taught you that too — that weight doesn't lessen because you refuse to set it down. It just teaches your shoulders to ache in new shapes.

* * *

The smuggler captain was a woman named Hess, broad as a doorframe, with a voice like gravel poured slowly and a tattooed forearm where the crown's brand had been burned over and covered with ink so many times the skin there looked like a palimpsest of bad decisions. She'd taken their coin without questions, which was the whole religion of the river folk, but she took Brannoc aside at dusk on the second day, when they moored in a cut willow stand to let a patrol boat pass, and said:

"You've got soldier written on you, big man. Old soldier. And old soldiers on this river are running from one of two things." She spat over the gunwale. "A debt or a decree. Which is yours?"

"Both," Brannoc said. "They're the same thing, mostly."

Hess grunted, unsurprised, and went back to her poles.

That night they made camp on a shingle bank where the river widened and slowed, a fire of driftwood burning low and orange, the smoke sweet with willow. Odile had produced a bottle of the untaxed brandy from her own cargo — "inventory shrinkage," she said primly — and passed it around. Even Tam got a thimbleful, watered, because Wren insisted and because nobody on this river wanted to argue with Wren when her healer's voice came out flat and final.

It was warm. That was the trouble with warmth. It loosened stones.

Mirabel sat across the fire from him, and the hollow-cold that clung to her sometimes — the faint wrongness of air, like a draft from a cellar that shouldn't exist — seemed quieter tonight, or perhaps Brannoc was simply getting used to it, which frightened him more. You could get used to anything. That was how walls fell. Not broken. *Accustomed.*

"There's something I should tell you all," he said.

Tam, mid-swig, lowered the bottle. Wren's hands went still in her lap. Odile raised one eyebrow over the rim of her cup, the universal gesture of a woman who already knew everything and was waiting to see if you knew it too.

Brannoc looked into the fire. Driftwood burned fast and bright and full of popping, and he watched a knot burst in a spray of sparks and thought: *here we go.*

"Before I was a hedge-knight," he said, "before I lost my shield and my name, I served the crown. Twelve years. Garrison work, then road work, then—" He stopped. Started again. His voice came out lower, rougher, like stone dragged over stone. "Then decree work. When the first Purification edicts went out, I was assigned to a compliance company out of Carrowmere. We went village to village. Registering gift-bearers. Fitting collars."

He felt them go still. All of them. Even the river seemed to hush against the shingle.

"It was legal work," he said. "That's the filthiest part of it. There were papers. Signatures. Priests to bless the collars so the families would hold still. And I told myself it was mercy, doing it gently, because the alternative was Serevan's kind of men, the burners, and at least a collar meant the person lived." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "I told myself that story for six years. Six years of holding people's arms still while iron went around their throats. And then—"

The fire popped again. He made himself go on, because a confession abandoned halfway is just a wound left open.

"There was a woman in my company. Dessa. Quartermaster's daughter, joined up same year I did. She had a gift — small thing, nothing. She could make bread rise perfect, every loaf, and keep milk sweet an extra day in summer. Kitchen magic. Charm-work, the priests called it, harmless, barely worth registering." His hands had closed into fists without his permission; he made them open. "Her brother lived out past the fens, and when the third edict came down — the one saying registered bearers' kin were to be collared too, on suspicion of inheritance — Dessa went to warn him. Helped him run."

Wren had gone very pale. Tam had pulled his knees up to his chest, making himself small, the way children do when a story turns toward cages.

"We were sent after them," Brannoc said. "Both of them. And I found Dessa first, alone, at a ferry crossing, and she looked at me — she *knew* me, we'd shared a mess table for six years, I'd carried her kit when she sprained her ankle — and she said, 'Brannoc. Please.' Just that. Please." 

He reached for the brandy bottle and found his hand unsteady and set it back down untouched.

"And I held her arms while they fitted the collar. Because it was the law, and because I told myself a collar was better than a pyre, and because—" his voice cracked, and he let it, "—because I was a coward who dressed his cowardice up in orders. Her brother got away. She was shipped to the penitent houses at Grey Ford. I requested transfer the next month. Told myself I couldn't stomach the work anymore. Truth was I couldn't stomach her face. I never learned if she lived. I never asked. Asking would have been the smallest decency, and I couldn't even pay that."

Silence. The river murmured over the stones. Somewhere upstream a night bird called, twice, and got no answer.

He looked up at last, because a man who confesses owes the people he's confessed to his eyes.

"So that's what I am," he said. "Not a disgraced knight. Disgrace implies you once had honor to lose. I'm a man who helped build the machine that's hunting us, and I only stepped off the wheel when it started grinding someone I loved. If any of you want to walk to the next crossing and find another boat, I'll understand. I'll even carry your packs to it."

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved.

Then Tam stood up, and Brannoc braced himself — for anger, for the boy's sharp tongue, for the flinch he deserved — but Tam walked around the fire and sat down heavily beside him and said nothing at all. Just sat there, shoulder pressed against Brannoc's arm, staring into the flames.

It was Wren who spoke.

"My sister," she said quietly.

Brannoc turned his head. Wren was looking at her hands, turning them over, palms then backs, as if checking them for damage.

"I've never told you why I left the healing house at Merrowdown. I let you all assume it was the gift itself — that I ran because my power costs me, hair and heartbeat, life for life." She smiled, thin and terrible. "It does cost. But that's not why I ran. I ran because the house took decree coin. Collared healers working under supervision, their gifts licensed and taxed and drained for the garrisons. And when a fever swept the collar-workshop there — the place where they forged the things — the mistress ordered us to treat only the smiths, not the collared workers who'd been burned testing the iron. I treated everyone. I spent so much of myself that half my hair went white in a single night, and when the mistress found out, she didn't punish me. She *thanked* me, and doubled my shifts, because a healer who'll spend herself for anyone is worth more than one who won't." 

She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "So don't you sit there offering us the chance to abandon you, Brannoc Oakeshield. I know exactly what institutions make good people do. I've been the good person inside one."

"The collar Dessa wore," Brannoc said hoarsely. "It might have been forged at Merrowdown."

"Then we're both stained by the same iron." Wren reached across the fire and gripped his wrist, her small hand absurd against his forearm, her grip fierce. "That's not a reason to scatter. That's a reason to stay."

Odile swirled her brandy, watching the firelight through it like an augury.

"I charted the penitent houses at Grey Ford," she said. "Two years ago. For a client who paid in secrets. There were four hundred names on the intake rolls I copied. I didn't read them. It wasn't part of the commission." She set the cup down with a click. "I have regretted that omission ever since. Perhaps tomorrow I will write to my client's contacts and ask after a quartermaster's daughter named Dessa. The river carries letters as well as brandy." She met Brannoc's eyes. "If she lives, you will owe me a map. If she died, you will owe me a grave visited. Either debt is payable."

Brannoc's throat worked. "Why would you—"

"Because I am an exile who charts dead places, knight. Do you imagine I don't understand erasure? The crown erased Vessany. It is erasing your Dessa, one unasked question at a time. I have made a profession of refusing." She picked her cup back up. "Also, you're the only one of us who can hear which bridge pylons are rotten before we cross them, and I am not drowning over your guilt. Practicality, above all."

A sound escaped Brannoc that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

Which left Mirabel.

She had been silent through all of it, and now the silence had weight, and Brannoc found he feared her quiet more than any curse. She rose, crossed the shingle, and sat down on his other side, so that he was flanked — the boy on one arm, the keeper on the other — and for a moment she just looked at the fire.

"When the hunters came to our valley," she said, "the elder gave me up. Gave up my name, my gate, everything, to buy the village an hour's grace. I hated him for a day and a night. Then I thought about what I would have done in his place, and I couldn't swear I'd have chosen differently, and the hating got heavier instead of lighter." She turned the river stone in her fingers — she still carried it, Brannoc realized; she carried it everywhere, worrying it smooth. "Guilt isn't a wall, Brannoc. I used to think it was. Something you build between yourself and what you did. But it's more like water. It finds every crack. The only thing you can do is stop pretending you're waterproof and let someone else help you carry the bucket."

"That's the worst metaphor I've ever heard," said Tam.

"It's true, though," said Wren.

"It's *true*, but it's terrible."

Mirabel almost smiled — the ghost of one, gone as fast as it came — and pressed the river stone into Brannoc's huge palm. "Hold onto this tonight. If the guilt gets loud, squeeze it. Stones can take it. That's what they're for."

He closed his fingers around it. It was cool and smooth and utterly ordinary, and it steadied him more than the brandy would have.

"I served the decree," he said again, because some things needed saying twice to be believed. "Twelve years."

"And we're keeping you anyway," Tam said, with the brutal simplicity of children. "Deal with it."

* * *

They did not sleep much, but it was a different kind of not-sleeping than before. Toward midnight the fire burned down to embers and Wren began, softly, to sing — an old healing-house lullaby, the kind sung over the dying and the newborn alike, and one by one the others went quiet to listen. Odile sketched by ember-light, quick strokes, and Brannoc caught a glimpse of the page: not a map at all, but five figures around a fire, done in a cartographer's precise hand, labeled in her tiny script like landmarks. *The Knight. The Thief. The Healer. The Keeper.* And a fifth mark, unlabeled, that he suspected was herself.

Hess came down from the barge near dawn to say the patrol boat had passed and the channel was clear, and found them all still awake, and looked at the lot of them — the knight and the thief, the healer and the mapmaker and the keeper — with an expression Brannoc couldn't read until she spoke.

"My brother wore a collar," she said simply. "Died in it. Grey Ford, eight years back." She jerked her chin toward the river. "We pole at first light. Grey Ford's two days downstream, if the letter matters to you."

Brannoc's heart slammed once, hard. "It matters."

"Then sleep on the boat, big man. You'll want your strength." She paused at the top of the bank, silhouetted against the paling sky. "River folk don't forget who helped fit the iron. But we remember who helps pry it off, too. Word travels faster than water."

She was gone before he could answer.

They cast off in the gold-grey hush of early morning, mist standing on the water in columns, the poles dipping soft. Brannoc took a turn at the stern oar beside Hess, and the shingle bank with its cold ashes slid away behind them, and ahead the river bent south through country he did not know, toward a penitent house and a name on a roll and a debt twelve years overdue.

In his pocket, his fist stayed closed around Mirabel's river stone.

And far off, at the edge of hearing — a sound only a man with stone-sense would catch, coming up through the oar-handle, through the water, through the bones of the world — the land itself hummed, low and uneasy, like a wall that has just felt its first crack.

Something was following them downriver. Brannoc had known it since the valley. But this morning, for the first time, the humming felt less like hunger.

It felt like knocking.
