## 12. Collars and Crows

The waystation smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular sourness of men who had been drinking since noon to keep their courage fed. Serevan stood in the doorway of the common room with rain running off his oilcloth cloak and pooling around his boots, and he let the silence do his work for him. It always did. A room full of guilty people made its own noise — the scrape of a stool, the clink of a cup set down too fast, a breath held just a half-second too long. He had learned, over eleven years of the decree, that fear was a language, and he was fluent in it.

"Captain," said Dree, at his shoulder, her crossbow held low and casual as a market basket. Behind them, eight of the company fanned out along the walls, cutting off the kitchen door, the back stair, the window that opened onto the dung-yard. Textbook. He'd written half the manual himself.

The innkeeper was a broad woman with flour to her elbows and a face like a closed ledger. She looked at Serevan's grey-and-white sash — the crown's colors, the hunter's colors — and something behind her eyes went very still, the way a field goes still when it hears the hawk.

"We're closed," she said.

"You're not." Sereven pulled off his hood and let the firelight find his face. He knew what they saw: a man of forty-odd years, grey at the temples, unremarkable except for the eyes, which were the pale flat grey of river ice. "You're open until I say otherwise. Names first. Then supper."

* * *

They took the smugglers one at a time into the stable, because Serevan had found early on that crowds gave cowards courage and gave liars an audience. Alone, in the straw-smell and the horse-warmth, with only Dree's lamp and his own quiet voice, men told him things they'd sworn on their mothers to carry to the grave.

The third one was called Hesk. He was maybe fifty, missing two fingers on his left hand, and he wept before Serevan even touched him, which was almost worse than the ones who held out. The ones who held out at least believed they had something left to protect.

"There was a girl," Hesk said, words tumbling now like coins from a split purse. "A keeper-girl, from the valley. Grey eyes, they said, and a scar on her wrist where the salt-rope bit her every morning. And a boy — a thief-boy, small, quick, ears like a fox. They came through the Fenmarch waystation three nights back, and old Maretta fed them, and there was others — a big man who could feel stone, a woman healer, a mapwoman—"

"Names," Serevan said softly.

Hesk shook his head. "I don't know their true names. That's the truth, captain, that's all of it. Smugglers don't trade in true names. But the route—" His remaining fingers twitched, tracing a line on the straw. "Downriver. There's a ferryman at Corlow's Bend who doesn't ask what he carries. And past the river—" He stopped, and his face did something complicated, grief and terror fighting for the same ground. "Past the river is nothing. Past the river is Vessany, and Vessany isn't real, so you can't go there."

Serevan looked at him for a long moment. Eleven years ago, he would have written that down as sedition and burned the man's tongue for it. Now he wrote it down as geography, and hated himself a little for knowing the difference.

"The boy," he said. "The thief-boy. Tell me about him."

"He heard locks," Hesk whispered. "That's his gift. Little thing, harmless, you'd think. But he stood in the yard at Fenmarch and told Maretta which of her cellar locks was going bad, and she laughed, captain. She laughed at a gift-bearer like he was her own son." Hesk's eyes came up, wet and defiant and broken all at once. "Is that a plague? Is that what we're burning the country clean of?"

Serevan didn't answer. He signaled Dree, and Dree took Hesk away to be held with the others, and Serevan stood alone in the stable for a moment, listening to the rain come off the roof in ropes.

He thought about charm-work. He thought about a child's bracelet, six weeks ago, in a village whose name he'd already let slide out of his memory like water through a fist. A little wooden bird on a string, charmed to sing when the wind came from the east. Harmless. The mother had begged; the father had held her; the child, four years old, hadn't understood and had smiled at Serevan because he wore a uniform and children trusted uniforms. He had put the bird in the brazier himself. He always did the burning himself; he never made his soldiers carry it alone. The wood had popped and flared green, and the song had gone up in smoke with a sound almost like a note cut short, and the child had cried not for the magic but for the toy, and Serevan had stood there and felt something shift in his chest, some load-bearing beam settling a fraction off true, and he had told himself, as he always told himself: *the decree is mercy. Gifts are plague. Better a burned toy than a burned village.*

He was no longer entirely sure the sentence still held its own weight. He carried it anyway. You carried the decree the way a mason carried a keystone — not because it was light, but because everything else fell down without it.

* * *

They caught the boy at Corlow's Bend two days later, and it should have been easy, and it wasn't.

The ferryman's hut sat on a spit of shingle where the river bent slow and brown between alder trees. Serevan took the place at dawn with twelve men, quiet as fog, and they found the ferryman gutting fish and the hut empty except for a cold hearth and a smell of recent bread that made Dree frown.

"Gone an hour, maybe two," she said.

But Serevan was looking at the floorboards. One of them sat wrong. Not loose — wrong, in the way a word sits wrong in a sentence you've read a hundred times. He crouched, ran his thumb along the seam, and found the hollow beneath: a crawl-space under the floor, breathing out river-damp and the ghost of a boy's sweat.

"He's still here," Serevan said. "He can't have gone far. The river's watched, the road's watched. Pull the bank apart."

It was Dree who flushed him — or rather, it was the boy's own gift that failed him, because a lock is a lock whether it's on a door or a trap, and when Dree sprang the snare-line across the alder roots the boy *heard* it, everyone heard it, a snap like a knuckle cracking in the grey morning, and then there was a flash of movement in the reeds and Serevan was running.

The boy was fast. Faster than any child Serevan had chased, faster than made sense, feet finding the safe stones in the mud as if the mud itself whispered warnings to him. He went up the shingle, through the alders, and might have made the treeline if the treeline hadn't held two of Serevan's men, and then it was over the way these things were usually over: a boy on his knees in the wet grass with a crossbow bolt quivering in the earth beside his hand and a soldier's boot on his back.

They brought him to the hut and put him in the chair, and Serevan dismissed everyone but Dree.

Up close, the boy was younger than the reports suggested. Twelve, thirteen. All elbows and sharp edges, hair the color of river-mud drying, and eyes that went everywhere — to the door, to the window, to the knife on Serevan's belt, to Dree's hands — reading the room the way other boys read picture-books. His wrists were thin enough that the rope looked obscene on them.

"What's your name?" Serevan asked.

The boy said nothing. He was listening. Serevan realized it after a moment — actually *listening*, head tilted a degree, gaze fixed not on Serevan's face but somewhere near his chest, near his hands. As though Serevan himself were a lock, and the boy was hearing what he was made of.

"I know you were at Fenmarch," Serevan continued. "I know you're traveling with the Kavanagh girl. I know the route runs downriver. What I want is the destination. Give me that, and you eat today. Give me that, and this rope comes off, and you walk out of here a free boy, because the decree has no quarrel with children who've been used by adults who should have known better."

Nothing. But the boy's listening changed quality — deepened, somehow — and Serevan had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that the child was hearing something in him that Serevan himself had spent eleven years refusing to hear.

"You think I'm a monster," Serevan said. "That's fine. Monsters are useful. Monsters keep the honest people honest." He leaned in. "But here's what monsters know that you don't: your friends are already dead or already caught, and the only variable left is how much it costs you to tell me what I'll learn anyway."

The boy's jaw tightened. And then, very quietly, he spoke — his first words, and they were not what Serevan expected.

"You're carrying something," the boy said. "In your coat. Left side. It's not a weapon. It hums."

Serevan went still. In his left coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, was the page he'd taken from Odile's camp weeks ago — a stolen fragment of the exiled mapmaker's charts, showing a border that officially did not exist. He had told himself it was evidence. He had told himself it was leverage. He had not told himself why he sometimes took it out at night, in camp, by lamplight, and looked at the inked line where Vessany should have been, feeling the same pull a man feels at the edge of a well.

"It's paper," Serevan said.

"It's more than paper," the boy said. "Paper doesn't hum. Paper doesn't *want* anything." His eyes came up and met Serevan's for the first time, and they were not afraid. That was the worst part. They were sorry for him. "You're not hunting us, captain. You're hunting the part of yourself you burned."

Dree moved toward him and the boy twisted, and it happened fast — too fast, the way a ferret goes through a hedge. He'd been working the rope against the chair's rough-sawn armrest the whole time, Serevan realized, wearing it through with a patience no interrogator credits a child with, and then the rope was off and the boy was moving, small body a blur between Dree's grab and Serevan's lunge, out the door, across the shingle—

—and the river took him.

Or seemed to. He went in where the current ran hard over the submerged weir, and Serevan reached the bank in time to see the brown water close over a splash of muddy hair, and the soldiers spread along the banks shouting, dragging the shallows, throwing lines. They searched until dark. They found his pack snagged on a root — inside it, bread, a tin whistle, a coil of waxed string, and a charm.

The charm was a small brass disc on a leather cord, no bigger than a coin, worn smooth by a thumb. When Serevan picked it up, it *hummed*. Not sound, exactly — a vibration in the bones of his hand, faint as a sleeping wasp. He knew what it was at once, because Hesk had told him and because the boy had told him with his listening: a hearing-charm. A thing that let its bearer hear locks, hear mechanisms, hear the secret speech of made things. A child's gift, pressed into brass so it could be carried, or given away, or left behind.

Left behind. On purpose. Because the boy had known — had *heard*, probably, right there in the chair, hearing the truth of Serevan the way he heard the truth of locks — that the captain would pick it up. Would turn it over. Would feel it humming against his palm like a small live conscience.

"Bag it," Dree said. "Evidence."

Serevan looked at the charm a moment longer. Then he put it in his left coat pocket, opposite the stolen map-page, where it lay against his heart like a second heartbeat, slightly out of time with his own.

"Evidence," he agreed.

* * *

That night the company made camp in the ferryman's field, and Serevan walked the perimeter alone because walking the perimeter alone was the only prayer he remembered how to say.

The river muttered over its weir. Somewhere a nightjar called, three notes, like a question asked twice and answered once. In the hut behind him, Hesk and the other smugglers waited in the cold loft for what the decree required, which was transport to the capital and the collars, and Serevan had given the order for transport rather than the pyre, and he had noticed himself giving it, and he did not examine why.

He took out the map-page first. Lamplight from the camp turned the ink soft and brown. The border ran across the parchment in Odile's sure hand, and where the official maps said *here there is nothing*, her line said *here there is everything*, and Serevan felt the old vertigo, the sense of standing at the edge of a country that had been erased so thoroughly that even the erasure had been erased.

Then he took out the charm.

It hummed against his palm. Warm, now. Warmer than it should have been, as if the boy's thumb had left heat in the brass that refused to fade. Serevan turned it over. On the back, scratched tiny with a pin-point, was a mark he hadn't seen before — a single character, quick and childish: a circle with a slash through it. The sign smugglers chalked on waystation doors to mean *this house listens, speak freely, you are among kin.*

The boy had marked his own charm. Had marked it for whoever found it. *You are among kin.* To a smuggler, it meant safety. To a hunter, it meant—

Serevan closed his fist around the brass until the edge of it bit.

Eleven years. Eleven years of the arithmetic of the decree: gifts were plague, plague required fire, fire was mercy. He had built his whole self on that sum, and the sum had balanced, more or less, the way a ledger balances when you agree not to look at certain columns. But lately the columns had been looking back. A singing bird. A laughing innkeeper. A boy in a chair who was sorry for him. And now this small warm weight in his pocket, humming its patient, accusing hum, saying in the language of made things what no prisoner had said under his hands:

*You are among kin. You simply haven't admitted it yet.*

"Captain?" Dree, at the edge of the lamplight, her crossbow slung, her face unreadable in the way of soldiers who have learned to see everything and report none of it. "The prisoners. Transport at dawn, as ordered?"

Serevan looked at the river. Downriver, past Corlow's Bend, past the fen and the moor, a girl with grey eyes was running toward a country that wasn't real, carrying whatever it was that the chancellor wanted badly enough to lie about. Ahead of him lay the hunt. Behind him lay the burning valley, the singing bird, the child crying for a toy.

"Dawn," he said. "As ordered."

He put the charm back in his pocket, where it hummed against the map-page, brass and ink, kin and country, and did not sleep, and did not pretend to.

And far downriver, in the dark, he thought he heard — though it was impossible, though charms did not carry voices, though he was a man of sums and sentences and not of signs — a boy's laugh, light as a whistle-note, skipping away over the water like a stone that refused, this once, to sink.
