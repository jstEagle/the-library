## 13. Wren's Arithmetic

Wren had always counted in costs.

It was the first thing her grandmother taught her, back before the collar-law, back when healing was still called a gift instead of a crime. *Every mending takes something from the mender,* Gran said, pressing Wren's small hands against a fevered child's brow. *So you learn your arithmetic early, girl. Know what you can afford. Know when the sum is too dear.*

Wren had been doing sums ever since.

A broken arm: a night of sleep, gone like coins spilled on a floor. A lungful of river water: two days of color leached from the world, everything tasting of ash. The child with the cleft lip in the capital: three hours of her own heartbeat, thin and fluttering as a moth against glass. She kept a ledger in her head, precise as any merchant's book, and she had never once let the columns run into debt.

Until the moor.

* * *

The ambush came at dusk, which was wrong — Serevan's hunters liked dawn, liked the flat grey light where a fleeing figure stood out against the heather like ink on linen. Dusk meant someone else had taught them patience. It meant the crown was spending real money on their deaths now.

Wren heard it first as silence: the curlews stopping all at once, the way birds do when a hawk passes over. Then Brannoc said, very quietly, "Down," and the world came apart.

The crossbow bolt took him through the chest.

She would remember it afterward in fragments, the way a dream breaks apart when you try to hold water in your hands. Mirabel screaming something — not his name, but a word in no language Wren knew, a word that made the air flinch. Tam vanishing into the bracken like the pickpocket ghost he was. The hollow-cold rolling off Mirabel in a wave that made three hunters drop their weapons and simply stand there, faces slack, as if they'd forgotten why they'd come.

And Brannoc on the ground, big hands pressed uselessly around the shaft jutting from below his collarbone, blood welling between his fingers black in the dying light.

"Stone-sense," he wheezed at her, trying for a joke because that was who Brannoc was, a man who joked at funerals including possibly his own. "Should've... felt that coming."

"Hold still." Wren knelt in the heather and put her hands over the wound, and did the arithmetic.

The bolt had missed his heart by the width of two fingers. It had not missed anything else. The head had splintered against a rib; fragments of it were walking through him like travelers lost in a dark house, opening doors that should stay shut. His lung was filling. His blood was leaving him faster than a body can spare.

A wound like this, clean, in a bed, with time — a night of herself. Maybe two.

Here, now, with iron in him and hunters regrouping beyond the rise and Mirabel's cold thing stirring in the air like a storm deciding whether to be born —

There wasn't enough in her ledger.

"Wren." Mirabel was beside her, kneeling in the blood-soaked heather, and her voice had that other sound under it, the hollow sound, the one that made Wren's teeth ache. "Tell me what you need."

"Time," Wren said. "Quiet. And a miracle."

"I can give you—"

"No." Wren looked up at her, and saw what was moving behind Mirabel's eyes, and grabbed her wrist with a blood-slick hand. "Whatever it's offering you — no. You don't know the price yet. Nobody knows the price until it's paid."

Mirabel held her gaze. Something vast and cold looked out through those brown eyes, patient as winter, and then — blessedly — blinked away. "Then spend yourself," Mirabel said, "and I'll get us out after. All of us. I swear it."

*Sworn oaths,* thought Wren grimly, *are just debts with better handwriting.* But there was no time to argue, because Brannoc coughed and the cough brought up pink froth, and arithmetic didn't care about anyone's feelings.

She closed her eyes and reached into him.

Healing, for Wren, felt like this: she imagined her own body as a house of many rooms, warm-lit, each room holding something precious. Her mother's laugh. The taste of honey on bread. The memory of rain on the schoolhouse roof. The strength in her legs for a long walk. The color of things. To mend another person, she opened a door in her house and let one of those rooms empty itself out through her hands, into the wound, into the broken place, and the room did not refill. Not ever. That was the law beneath the gift, older than crowns, older than collars: *nothing given by the giver is ever given back.*

She started small. The splinters of the bolt-head: she coaxed them out along old paths, and paid for it with the smell of her grandmother's kitchen, bread and rosemary, gone as if it had never existed. The torn muscle: the memory of her sister's face, softening even as she clung to it, becoming a stranger's face, becoming nothing. The lung, knitting: an entire summer, age nine, swallowed whole — she could feel the shape of it going, sun-warm and complete, and could not afterward remember what she had lost, only that a room in her house stood open and dark.

His heart stuttered, steadied, stuttered again.

And here is where Wren broke her own oldest rule. Here is where the arithmetic failed.

Because the bleeding wouldn't stop — the great vessel behind the collarbone had been nicked, and it was closing too slowly, and Brannoc's heartbeat was a bird flying slower and slower toward the ground — and Wren looked down at this man who carried Tam's stolen apples in his pack and hummed off-key around the campfire and had confessed his worst sin to them by river-light and been forgiven, and she opened every remaining door in her house at once.

Color went first. The moor turned to shades of grey and white, beautiful and terrible, like the world seen through frost. Then warmth — she could feel the dusk chill on her skin but not name it. Then the last of her childhood, a whole wing of the house emptying in a rush, and she heard herself make a sound she didn't recognize as her own voice.

Her hair whitened the way snow falls: not all at once, but in a spreading hush, strand by strand, from the roots outward. She felt it happen the way you feel a fever break.

The vessel sealed. The lung filled with air instead of blood. Brannoc's heart beat — steady, strong, absurdly alive — and Wren sat back on her heels in the trampled heather and looked at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger.

They were pale. Not wounded-pale. *Faded*-pale, like cloth washed too many times, like a painting left in the sun.

"Well," said Brannoc, sitting up, whole, testing his shoulder with a wince that was only pain and not death. He saw her hair and his face went grey. "No. No, girl, tell me you didn't—"

"It's fine," Wren said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "I budgeted for it."

She hadn't. They both knew she hadn't. She tried to stand and the moor tilted gently, courteously, like a host offering a chair, and Mirabel caught her before the ground could.

* * *

They ran — or rather, Mirabel half-carried Wren while Tam materialized out of the dark to take her other side, chattering rapid, frightened nonsense about how he'd led the hunters' horses into a bog and stolen a sergeant's purse while he was at it, *look, proof*, as if theft could fix anything. Brannoc walked wounded-but-whole, touching his unscarred chest every few steps as if checking that he was really there.

They made camp at midnight in a hollow ring of stones, an old shepherd's fold, and built no fire because fires drew eyes. Wren lay on Brannoc's cloak and listened to her own heart.

That was the horror of it, she thought. She could hear it. Every healer learned to hear their own pulse the way musicians hear a tuning fork, and hers was — thin. A thread of a sound. A moth against glass, again, but this time the glass was thick and the moth was tired.

Tam crouched beside her. In the starlight his face was all elbows and worry. "You look," he said, with the tactlessness of the young, "like my gran did. Before."

"Before what?"

"Just before." He fiddled with the hearing-charm at his throat, the little brass thing that whispered to him of locks and tumblers. "Can I do anything? I'm good at doors. I could steal you a door."

Despite everything, Wren laughed, and the laugh cost her more than she wanted to admit. "What would I do with a door?"

"Walk through it somewhere warmer." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "Brannoc's crying, you know. Over there. Thinks nobody can hear him. He's got stone-sense, he can feel the ground shaking when he does it, so he thinks it hides the sound. It doesn't."

"Tam."

"He keeps saying it should've been him. That he's got decades of sins banked and you've got—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "He says you spent too much. He says everyone always spends too much on him and it never balances."

Wren reached up and caught the boy's collar, gently, and drew him down close. "Listen to me, lock-hearer. Tell Brannoc the sum was mine to pay and I'd pay it twice. Tell him grief is just love with nowhere to put itself, and he should put his somewhere useful — like carrying me tomorrow, because I don't think my legs have opinions anymore." She smiled, and watched the smile work on him the way smiles worked on children who had grown up reading adults' faces for danger. "And tell him the family ledger isn't balanced by one person. It's balanced by all of us. That's the whole point of a family."

Tam nodded, swallowed hard, and went. Wren lay back and stared at the wheeling stars and did the only arithmetic left to her: counting her heartbeats, and wondering how many remained in the account.

* * *

She woke — if it was waking — to cold.

Not the moor's honest cold. This was the other kind, the hollow kind, the cold that had rolled off Mirabel during the ambush and made armed men forget themselves. It stood at the edge of the sheepfold like a person made of the space between stars, and it was looking at Wren, and it spoke with a voice like her own remembered from childhood:

*You are running low, healer. I am very deep. One word, and the rooms refill.*

Wren's heart — her thin, thread-of-a-heart — lurched. "You're not real," she whispered. "Mirabel carries you. You're her burden, not my bargain."

*Everyone's burden is someone else's bargain,* said the cold, reasonably. *Ask her what it offered her. Ask her why she hates how easy it feels. I keep accounts too, Wren of the fading hair. Mine never run dry. They only run deep.*

And it was still there, patient as winter, when Wren turned her head and saw Mirabel standing at the fold's edge, staring at the same empty stretch of darkness — and Mirabel's hands were raised, palms out, and frost was blooming across the heather in a perfect circle around her feet, and her face wore an expression Wren had seen only once before, on a woman in the capital whose collar had cracked: the expression of someone feeling, for the first time, exactly how strong they were.

"Mira," Wren whispered, and her breath fogged though the night was merely cool. "What did it offer you?"

Mirabel didn't turn. When she spoke, her voice was steady and quiet and more frightening than any scream.

"It offered me you," she said. "All of you, restored. Every room in your house refilled. Your colors back. Your summers back. Your heartbeat loud as a drum." A pause, and the frost crept another inch. "All I had to do was stop fighting it. Just once. Just open one door."

The cold thing sighed through the stones, contented, almost tender.

"And the worst part," Mirabel said, and now she did turn, and her eyes were wet and rimmed in white frost, and she was looking at Wren with a hatred that Wren slowly understood was aimed inward, "the worst part is that I wanted to say yes. For you. I wanted it so badly. And it *felt* — God forgive me — it felt *easy*."

The frost circle widened, a finger's width, hungry and gentle at once.

Wren pushed herself up on trembling arms, white hair falling around her faded face, and began — with the last clear arithmetic she had left — to calculate what it would cost to talk her friend back from the edge of a door that opened so easily.

Far off across the moor, a hawk cried once in the dark where no hawk should fly, and the cold thing laughed like ice settling on a pond, and said, *Choose soon.*
