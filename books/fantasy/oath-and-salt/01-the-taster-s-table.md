## 1. The Taster's Table

There were three rules to surviving as a royal food-taster in Vhalcrest, and Zephyrine Blackwood had written all of them herself, in the privacy of her own skull, where no Inquisitor could read them.

Rule one: never be hungry before a tasting. A hungry taster rushes. A rushing taster misses things — the almond ghost of bitter-leaf, the copper whisper of ground glass, the faint sweetness that meant someone had been generous with the wrong honey.

Rule two: never let them see you enjoy it. Enjoyment suggested appetite; appetite suggested habit; habit suggested you'd grown comfortable swallowing things meant to kill other people, and comfortable people got careless, and careless people got drowned in the harbor with their wrists tied to a paving stone. The Crown was very tidy about its metaphors.

Rule three: never, under any circumstances, use magic to do your job.

She broke rule three every single morning, but only a little, and only in the way a woman might steal a single grape from a cart piled high with them — so small a theft that even she could pretend it wasn't one.

"Her Majesty will take the barley broth this morning," announced Mistress Halloway, the kitchens' overseer, a broad red-faced woman whose voice could curdle cream at forty paces. "And the quince preserves. And the little seed cakes. Master Fenwick's broth." She said the last part with the particular relish of a woman announcing a rival's public examination.

Zephyrine bowed. "The little seed cakes," she repeated solemnly, "are my favorite duty."

"They are everyone's favorite duty," said Halloway, "which is why I trust none of you."

It was, Zephyrine reflected, the most sensible thing anyone in Vhalcrest had said in years.

* * *

The tasting chamber sat between the royal kitchens and the Queen's morning room, a narrow stone room with one long table, three chairs, and a window too high to see out of but perfectly placed to remind you the sky existed without letting you have any of it. Sunlight came down in a pale column and lit the dust motes like tiny swimming things, and Zephyrine thought, not for the first time, that even dust in Vhalcrest looked like it was drowning.

Three dishes on the table. Three tasters: herself, old Marta Voss who had survived nineteen years by being too sour for anyone to poison out of spite, and young Pell Ondrey, six months into his license, still flinching at his own reflection.

Zephyrine took her seat and laid her hands flat on the table, palms down, the way she always did. It looked like composure. It was actually camouflage. Her hands were the one part of her that had never learned to lie properly.

She began, as she always began, with the small work — the work no one could see.

Under the table, hidden by the drape of her sleeve, her ring finger brushed the wood, and she whispered the smallest charm she knew. Not a spell, really. Spells were what mages did, and mages burned or drowned depending on which the local magistrate found more picturesque. What she did was smaller than a spell. It was a listening. A charm her mother had taught her when she was small enough to be carried, back when carrying her had still been safe, back before the isles went under and the word *mage* became a sentence instead of a name.

*Tell me what is not food,* the charm said, in a language with no word for *poison*, because the people who spoke it had never needed one. The sea does not poison what it loves. It only takes.

The charm woke in her fingertips like a cat stretching. And the dishes told her things.

The quince preserves were honest — fruit, sugar, a scandalous amount of saffron, nothing else. The seed cakes were honest too, though the baker had clearly lost a war with the caraway. But the broth —

The broth was lying.

Not poisoned. Not yet. But there was something folded into it, tucked beneath the barley like a knife beneath a napkin: a bitterness with an edge to it, a green sharp taste that didn't belong in any kitchen in the palace. Bitter-leaf, she thought first. Then no. Bitter-leaf numbed. This *itched*. This was the kind of thing that wouldn't kill the Queen, wouldn't even sicken her badly — just enough cramping, enough fever, enough of a scare to have the kitchens turned inside out and three tasters questioned.

Someone wanted chaos, not a corpse. Which was, in its way, more interesting.

"Well?" said Halloway from the doorway. "We haven't got until winter."

"Ladies first," said Marta Voss, and pushed the seed cakes toward herself with the entitlement of nineteen unpoisoned years.

They ate in the usual order — sweets first, because sweet hid poison best and tradition demanded the hard thing come last. Zephyrine ate her seed cake slowly, appreciatively, and let Pell watch her do it, because half her job was teaching the boy that fear could be swallowed along with everything else.

Then the broth.

She lifted the spoon. Under the table, her other hand curled into a fist, nails pressing crescents into her palm — an anchor, a small honest pain to hold onto. Because here was the arithmetic no one else in the room was doing: whatever was in that bowl, *she* was going to eat it. That was the whole of her employment. The Queen's life was worth more than hers, and everyone present knew it, and everyone present was very polite about knowing it, which somehow made it worse.

*You could refuse,* whispered a voice in her that sounded like the sea. *You could tip the bowl. You could say the words and let the water in the broth rise up and pour itself out over the stones, and no one would ever know—*

No one would ever know except everyone, because the broth would move against nature, and nature-moving was the definition of witchcraft, and witchcraft in Vhalcrest came with a scenic walk off the Tide Steps.

She ate the broth. All of it. Spoonful by unhurried spoonful, chewing the barley like a woman with nowhere better to be, while the itch settled into her belly and began its slow, patient work.

"There," she said brightly, setting down the spoon. "Delicious. Master Fenwick has outdone himself."

Halloway sniffed, wrote something in her ledger, and left.

Marta Voss finished her portion, studied Zephyrine's face with eyes like two old coins, and said nothing, which from Marta was practically a love letter.

Pell Ondrey put down his own spoon halfway through and went grey. "Miss Blackwood," he whispered, "your hands."

She looked down. Her hands were trembling — fine, fast, bird-boned tremors, the broth's little gift making itself known ahead of schedule. The itch was climbing now, up through her middle, looking for somewhere to express itself.

Rule four, unwritten: never let them see the cost.

"Oh, that," she said, flexing her fingers and laughing — she'd practiced that laugh in front of a cracked mirror for a full month when she'd first come to the palace, and it had never once sounded real, but it sounded *confident*, which was better. "Barley disagrees with me. Always has. My mother used to say I was raised on better grain."

Which was true, incidentally. Her mother had raised her on kelp-bread and sea-sweetened rainwater, on an island that no longer appeared on maps because maps had been ordered to forget it.

She stood, curtsied to the room at large, and asked leave to visit the necessary — a request so mundane that no one would dream of reading anything into it, least of all what she intended to read into it herself.

* * *

In the privy passage, alone, Zephyrine pressed two fingers to her belly and finally allowed herself the luxury of doing something about it.

The charm was older than the city around her. Older than the kingdom, if the stories were true — and her mother had told them as though they were, in the low singing tongue of the drowned isles, where magic hadn't been a crime but a chore, like hauling nets or mending sails. *Small magics for small needs,* her mother used to say. *The sea doesn't ask whether you're worthy. Only whether you're willing.*

She whispered the words. The itch in her gut turned over, considered its options, and dissolved — drawn out along her fingers as a faint cold shimmer, invisible to anyone who wasn't looking for it, gone into the drain-water before it could become the fever and cramping some anonymous kitchen hand had seasoned the Queen's breakfast with.

She leaned against the cold stone and breathed.

This was the arrangement, then. This was the deal she'd struck with Vhalcrest five years ago, when she'd walked in off a fishing boat with a forged license and a face that looked convincingly land-born: she would spend her days eating death in small portions and her nights selling charms to people desperate enough to pay, and in exchange the city would pretend not to notice what she was, right up until the moment it decided to notice, at which point it would drown her with ceremonial efficiency and feel very good about itself.

Belonging, she had learned, was mostly a matter of agreeing to be slightly killed on a regular basis.

She straightened her collar. She checked her reflection in the polished brass plate by the door — brown hair, brown eyes, a face designed by the gods specifically to be forgotten — and gave herself the nod she gave herself every morning: *one more day, and nobody drowned, including especially you.*

Back in the tasting chamber, Pell was white as flour and Marta was eating the quince preserves directly from the jar, which was either courage or retirement planning.

"You'll want to report the broth," Zephyrine told Halloway pleasantly, re-entering. "Something in it disagreed with me rather strongly. I'd have the kitchens look at Master Fenwick's barley."

Halloway's eyebrows climbed. "Poison?"

"Or incompetence. With Fenwick it's genuinely difficult to say."

That earned her a snort, which in Halloway's economy was roughly equivalent to a knighthood. Notes were taken. Runners were dispatched. Somewhere in the palace machinery, a small investigation began grinding forward, and Zephyrine returned to her seat feeling the particular exhaustion that came from having done her job twice — once for the Crown, and once for herself.

* * *

The rest of the morning passed in the ordinary way: a second tasting at noon (the Queen's luncheon — cold fowl, bread, wine, all clean, all dull), an hour of standing about looking decorative, and then dismissal, the bell that mattered most, the bell that turned Zephyrine Blackwood, licensed taster, back into whoever it was she was after dark.

She walked home through the Saltmarket as the light went amber and the fishmongers shouted their final bargains. Her rooms were above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, two narrow floors that smelled permanently of tallow and salt, and she loved them with the fierce, defensive love of a woman who had built a life out of driftwood and knew exactly how much weather it could take.

Tonight there was work. There was always work. A sailor's wife who wanted a calm-seas knot-charm. A midwife who needed the good-luck sigil she'd been buying monthly for years and pretending was for luck. A merchant's son, poor fool, who'd heard rumors of a charm against heartbreak and was about to learn that magic couldn't fix stupid.

She unlocked her door, hung up her coat, and reached under the loose floorboard for the little tin where she kept her stock of charms — knots and sigils and folded paper prayers, each one small enough to deny, each one technically treason.

And that was when she noticed the weather turning.

The wind had come around. It was coming off the sea now, and it had a sound in it she knew — a low, dragging moan in the rigging of the city, the sound the harbor made before a storm that meant business. Rain hit the window in fat, deliberate drops, like fingers testing the glass.

Zephyrine stood in her dim little kitchen with a tin of illegal miracles in her hands and felt the storm coming in off the water like something with a purpose.

She had lived in Vhalcrest five years, and in five years the sea had never once come looking for her.

Tonight, watching the rain crawl down the glass, she had the sudden, unreasonable certainty that it had finally sent someone.
