## 2. A Knock in Low Tide

The storm came up the harbor like a debt collector.

Zephyrine felt it first in the broth she was cooling on her windowsill — a trick her mother had taught her without ever meaning to teach anything, because mothers who sing to their soup don't think of it as magic. The surface of the liquid shivered in rings before any wind touched it, and Zephyrine stood in her narrow kitchen with a wooden spoon in her hand and thought, *there it is*, the way other people thought *here comes rain*.

She had lived above a chandler's shop on Rope-Walk Lane for six years. The room was small and deliberately unlovely: one bed, one stove, one window with a view of a wall, shelves of harmless things — dried thyme, bundled candles, jars of pickled onions she actually ate. Nothing that glowed. Nothing that whispered. She had learned early that a hiding place should look like no one would bother hiding in it.

But the charms were there anyway, of course. Under the floorboard beneath the bed, wrapped in oilcloth, in a box that had once held buttons: eleven small workings, each one stitched or knotted or sung into being, each one worth her life if found. A charm against fever. A charm to find lost keys. Three charms against drowning, which sold best in Vhalcrest, though she never let herself wonder why a city built on the bones of islands needed so many of them.

Tonight the storm made the whole lane groan like a ship, and Zephyrine did what she always did when the weather turned ugly: she counted her blessings by their opposites. Not hanged. Not drowned. Not asked too many questions at the palace this morning. The Queen's broth had been clean today, for once, and Zephyrine had swallowed her ceremonial spoonful with nothing worse than boredom, and Master Quill, the steward, had even smiled at her, which meant either good tidings or a coming reshuffle of the kitchens. With Quill, it was impossible to say.

She was banking the fire when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes when they've been told not to knock at all.

Zephyrine went still, spoon in hand. Nobody knocked on her door at night. Her customers came at dusk through the chandler's yard, paid in copper, and left without names. The palace knew where she slept but sent no one after dark; the Crown's courtesy extended only as far as its usefulness. And children — children in Vhalcrest knew better than to knock on strangers' doors in a storm, the way rabbits know better than to sit in open fields.

The knock came again. Softer. Almost apologetic.

"Go home," Zephyrine said to the door, in the voice she used for beggars and drunkards, warm enough to be kind and firm enough to be final. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

Silence. Then, terribly, a sound she recognized before she understood it: a small wet cough, the kind that comes from lungs full of cold air and colder rain.

Zephyrine closed her eyes. *Don't*, she told herself. *You have a system. You have six years of careful nothing. Whatever is out there is somebody else's problem, and the city has foundlings' doors, and charity is a luxury for people whose grandmothers weren't burned—*

The knock came a third time, and this time it wasn't three knocks. It was a rhythm. Two quick, one slow, two quick.

Her mother's rhythm. The one she'd tapped on Zephyrine's shoulder when they walked through crowds, the all-clear, the *it's safe, little fish*.

Zephyrine set down the spoon very carefully, as though it might go off.

She opened the door.

The child couldn't have been more than eight or nine, though it was hard to tell, because she was wearing half of the tidal flats. Mud from her boots to her eyebrows, hair plastered flat, a coat far too big for her that smelled of kelp and tar. She stood in the doorway swaying slightly, like something the sea had tried out and decided against, and she looked up at Zephyrine with eyes so pale a gray they were nearly white.

And then the child spoke.

"*Vetha sulen mara, kest i dolen.*"

One sentence. Clear as a bell rung underwater.

Zephyrine's whole body forgot what it was for.

It wasn't Vhalcresti. It wasn't the border dialects she'd picked up in the kitchens, or the merchant pidgin of the Saltmarket, or any of the dozen tongues a food-taster learns to eavesdrop in. It was older than all of them. It was the language her mother had sung over the washbasin when she thought Zephyrine was asleep. It was the language the priests called *the tongue of traitors*, the language of the drowned isles, the language that got you a trial consisting of one question and a long walk off a short pier.

No one had spoken it aloud to her in twenty years. No one alive *could*.

"*Vetha sulen mara,*" the child said again, patiently, the way you repeat yourself to someone being very slow, "*kest i dolen.*"

"I—" said Zephyrine, who could lie fluently in four languages and currently could not manage honesty in her own. "I don't understand you."

Which was, of course, a lie, and the child looked at her with those pale eyes in a way that suggested she knew it, the way animals and children and Inquisitors always seemed to know.

*Vetha sulen mara.* Zephyrine's mind, moving without her permission, translated it the way her mouth still moved through old songs in the bath: *the water remembers what the land forgets.* It was a greeting. Or a warning. In her mother's mouth it had been both.

"You can't be here," Zephyrine whispered, glancing past the child into the hammering dark of the lane. Empty. Rain silvering everything. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog was barking itself hoarse against the thunder. "Do you understand me? There are people — if anyone saw you — where is your mother? Your family?"

The child considered this. Then she opened her mouth and produced, with evident effort, the only other sentence she apparently owned:

"*Kest i dolen.*"

*What the tide gives back.* That one Zephyrine knew from lullabies, from funeral songs, from the story her mother told exactly once about the night the isles went under. *What the tide gives back, you must take in.*

"No," Zephyrine said aloud, to the child, to the storm, to the dead. "No, absolutely not."

The child sneezed.

It was a very small sneeze, mud-flecked and tragic, and it undid in half a second what twenty years of discipline had built. Zephyrine sighed the sigh of a woman watching her own careful life spring a leak, stepped back, and jerked her head at the room behind her.

"Inside. Quickly. Don't touch anything, don't look at anything, and if anyone knocks, you're my niece and you're mute and you have a fever, do you hear me?"

The child stepped over the threshold, leaving two perfect muddy footprints on Zephyrine's clean floor — footprints that, Zephyrine noticed with a distant, rising horror, were webbed with fine channels of water that ran uphill, toward the sea, against every law of plumbing and nature — and then simply stood there, dripping, looking around the little room with an expression of profound, unimpressed assessment, as though she'd seen better hovels.

Zephyrine shut the door. Bolted it. Put the chair under the knob, then felt foolish, then left it there anyway.

* * *

Getting the mud off the child took an hour and revealed several problems.

The first problem was that under the mud and the enormous coat, the girl was thin in the way that spoke of weeks, not days, of going hungry — all wrists and collarbones, skin cold as a mussel shell. The second problem was that she had a scar on her left forearm, old and deliberate: three parallel lines, healed white, in a pattern Zephyrine had only ever seen described in books that were kept chained in the palace library. The third problem was that when Zephyrine unwrapped the sodden bundle the child clutched to her chest — expecting, given the evening so far, anything from a knife to a live crab — she found instead a smooth gray stone the size of a fist, sea-worn, hole through its middle, humming faintly against her palms like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

Zephyrine dropped it on the table. It hummed on the table. It would not stop humming on the table.

"That's not yours," she said faintly.

"*Kest i dolen,*" the child agreed.

They ate bread and honey in silence, the child demolishing hers with the focused savagery of the truly starving while Zephyrine watched and did arithmetic she didn't want to do. One extra mouth. One extra set of questions. One stone that sang. Every instinct she had honed over five years in the palace kitchens — *notice everything, explain nothing, survive until morning* — was screaming at her to feed the girl one more meal and walk her to the foundlings' door at first light and never think of this again.

But the foundlings' door asked names. The foundlings' door reported to the parish, and the parish reported to the Crown, and the Crown had a department for children who arrived out of storms speaking dead languages, and that department was staffed by men whose titles began with *Inquisitor*.

Besides. When Zephyrine had touched the girl's shoulder to steer her to the chair, something had happened — brief, unmistakable, like plunging her hand into sunlit shallows. Warmth. Salt. A vast patience, deep below. The feeling of her mother's voice, if her mother's voice had been an ocean.

The child looked up from her bread, caught Zephyrine staring, and stared back with those tide-gray eyes.

"*Vetha sulen mara,*" she said softly. Almost gently. Like a key turning in a lock Zephyrine had painted over years ago.

"Yes," Zephyrine heard herself answer, in a language she hadn't spoken since she was small enough to be sung to. "Yes, I understand you. Gods help us both."

The child nodded once, satisfied, business concluded, and went back to her bread.

Outside, the storm reached some private agreement with the night and redoubled itself, and through the rattling window Zephyrine saw, across the lane, beneath the flickering lamp at the corner of Rope-Walk and Salt — a figure standing motionless in the rain. Tall. Still. Watching her lit window with the unhurried attention of a man taking notes he already knows by heart.

By the time her heart restarted, the corner was empty, and the rain fell on nothing at all.

The stone on the table hummed on.
