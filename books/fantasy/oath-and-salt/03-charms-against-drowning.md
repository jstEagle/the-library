## 3. Charms Against Drowning

The child slept like the drowned.

That was Zephyrine's first thought on waking, and she hated herself for it — a taster's mind, always reaching for the morbid comparison first. But it was true. The girl lay in the corner of Zephyrine's bed as if she'd been set there by the tide itself, limbs slack, breathing slow and even, her mud-crusted clothes stiffening into little shells of dried riverbank. She hadn't spoken since that single sentence in the old tongue. She hadn't needed to. Her eyes did all her talking: wide, grey-green, patient as deep water.

Zephyrine sat at her little table with a cup of chicory going cold and watched the girl breathe, and tried very hard not to think of her mother.

*The sea remembers what we bury,* her mother used to sing, wringing out laundry in the pre-dawn dark, the words sliding between Vhalcrestan and something older underneath. *And the sea gives back what we owe.* Zephyrine had been seven. She'd thought it was just a song about laundry.

Now there was a child in her one-room flat above the chandler's shop, a child who had walked through a storm to her door, and who had said — in a language officially extinct for two hundred years, a language whose speakers had been given the choice of fire or drowning — *You kept the door. I found you by the door.*

She hadn't kept any door. She'd built walls, brick by careful brick, for five years. And yet here was a small wet person sitting inside them like she owned the lease.

"Right," Zephyrine said aloud, because talking to oneself was cheaper than a physician and only slightly more mad. "Rules."

The girl opened one eye.

"One: no magic in the house. Two: no magic in the street. Three—" Zephyrine paused, realizing rules one and two were identical, and pressed on with the confidence of a woman who had lied to queens before breakfast. "Three: if anyone asks, you're my niece. From... Grellick. Downriver. Nobody visits Grellick; nobody knows anything about Grellick; Grellick is the sort of place where everyone is someone's niece."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she pointed at herself.

"Yes. You." Zephyrine rubbed her face. "We'll need a name. Something dull. Marda? Petal? Gods, no. Something a Grellick family would inflict on an innocent—"

The girl touched her own collarbone, then reached down and dragged one finger through the dried mud on the floorboards. She drew a wavy line. Then another beneath it. Then another. Three lines of tide, layered like the rings of a tree.

"Brine," Zephyrine translated before she could stop herself, because that was what the three lines meant in the old tongue — salt-water rising, the taste left on skin after the sea lets you go. It wasn't a name. It was a condition. It was also, unfortunately, perfect, and dull enough to pass in Grellick.

The girl smiled. It was the first time Zephyrine had seen her do it, and it went through her chest like a fishhook.

"Brine," Zephyrine repeated, and heard the surrender in her own voice. "Fine. Brine. But if you drown anything in this house, even accidentally, I want it noted that I objected in advance."

* * *

The testing began the next evening, after Zephyrine came home from the palace with the particular exhaustion that followed a day of swallowing other people's intentions. Tonight's broth had been clean — or clean enough — but the Queen had looked at her a half-second too long across the table, and Zephyrine had spent four hours wondering which of her smiles had gone stale.

She locked the door. Drew the curtain. Lit the stub of candle she used for work that couldn't be seen from the street.

"Brine. Come here. I'm going to show you some things, and you're going to pretend you never saw them, and so am I, forever, until we die."

Brine padded over and knelt on the worn rug, folding her hands with the solemnity of a child raised by people who prayed.

Zephyrine took out her kit — a lacquered box disguised as a sewing case, because paranoia was the only heirloom she'd kept. Inside, nested in wool: six charms. Not the gutter-trash trinkets sold in the Saltmarket, which were mostly chicken bones and confidence. These were real. Small. Old. Her mother's make, or made in the manner of her mother's people, back when making them was a craft instead of a capital crime.

A knot-charm against cramp and drowning, woven of three strands of hair and red thread. A sliver of whale-ear bone drilled with a hole, for hearing what was said behind closed doors. A dried sea-bean that warmed when lies were told nearby. A pinch of grave-sand in wax, to keep dreams from following you home. A fish-scale pressed in glass, for finding your way when lost. And the last one, the dangerous one, a curl of dried kelp sealed in resin, which her mother had called *the calling-back*, and which Zephyrine had never once dared use.

Five years she'd kept these. Five years, checking them the way other people checked their coin-purses. And in five years, every single one of them had been exactly as dead as the law required.

She laid them out in a row on the rug.

"Now watch," she whispered, and took up the knot-charm, and spoke over it the way she'd done a hundred times in this room, softly, the words shaped more than voiced: the waking-words her mother had used, half prayer, half recipe.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. The charms weren't broken; they were *orphaned*. Whatever current they'd been built to catch had been dammed, diverted, executed by committee two centuries ago. The knot-charm sat in her palm like thread and hair, which is all it was.

"I know," she told Brine, feeling foolish. "I keep them anyway. Sentiment. Or spite. Both are free."

But Brine wasn't looking at the charm. She was looking at Zephyrine's hands. And slowly, with the caution of someone approaching a strange dog, she reached out and laid her own small fingers over Zephyrine's wrist.

The knot-charm lit.

Not flared — *lit*, the way a coal remembers being fire, a low orange glow threading along the red thread, up through the woven strands of hair, pulsing gently, rhythmically, in time — Zephyrine realized with a lurch — with the heartbeat under Brine's fingers.

Zephyrine didn't drop it. Tasters don't drop things; dropping is how people die. But her whole body went cold and then hot, and the cup of her heartbeat turned over.

"That's not possible," she said. "I've tried for years. There's nothing left to draw on. The wells are empty, the priests are dead, the—"

Brine tilted her head and touched the second charm. The whale-bone hummed, faintly, a note below hearing, and Zephyrine's ears popped as though she'd descended too fast in water. The sea-bean grew warm against the rug. The grave-sand charm sweated beads of wax like a frightened man's brow. One by one, without a single word spoken, the orphaned charms woke up and remembered what they were for.

Only the last one stayed dark. The calling-back, in its resin seal, sat cold and silent as a stone at the end of the row. Brine looked at it a long moment. Then she looked up at Zephyrine, and her expression was so carefully, so deliberately blank that Zephyrine understood it was deliberate — a child's imitation of an adult's composure, learned somewhere, from someone, under circumstances Zephyrine did not want to imagine.

"No," Zephyrine said. "Whatever you're asking, no. We don't touch that one."

Brine folded her hands again. Patient as tide.

Zephyrine sat back on her heels. Her heart was doing something unprofessional. Because she knew what this was now — had known since the glow, really, but knowledge arrives in stages, like grief, like dawn. The charms weren't responding to residual power in the room. They were responding to *her*. To Brine. The girl wasn't carrying magic the way a thief carries stolen goods.

She was carrying *memory*. Living memory. The old rites, the old songs, the old names of the drowned isles — everything the Crown had spent two hundred years burning out of books and bloodlines alike, everything Zephyrine's family had survived only by burying — all of it walking around in a child of perhaps nine years, wearing mud for shoes.

A living vessel. A reliquary with legs.

"They're not hunting a witch," Zephyrine said quietly, to the candle, to the room, to nobody. "They're hunting a library."

Brine reached out and patted Zephyrine's knee twice, gently, the way one pats a horse that has spooked. It was such a grandmotherly gesture from such a small person that Zephyrine laughed — a startled, cracked sound — and then put her face in her hands and laughed again, less healthily, because terror and tenderness had gotten tangled in her chest like fishing line and she could not for the life of her tell which end to pull.

* * *

It was Brine who saw him first.

Two days later, market morning, Zephyrine had the girl trailing her through the Saltmarket like a shadow with opinions, buying herring and pretending this was an ordinary errand between an ordinary aunt and her ordinary niece from Grellick. Brine carried the basket. Brine was, Zephyrine had discovered, unnervingly good at being ordinary — walking at the right pace, looking at nothing, blending into the crowd like a drop of water returning to the sea. Which should have been reassuring. It was not. Ordinary was a skill. Skills were taught.

They were at the fishmonger's, arguing in gestures about whether smoked or fresh, when Brine stopped. Went still. That stillness again — the drowned sleep, the deep-water patience — but pointed now, like a compass needle.

Zephyrine followed her gaze across the square.

A man stood at the mouth of Chandler's Row, beside the public cistern. He wore good grey wool, plain and expensive, the uniform of a man who wanted you to notice his quality and forget his office. He held no papers, carried no guards. He was simply watching the street — not the fishmonger's, not the crowd. The entrances. Counting, perhaps, the ways in and out of a certain building above a certain chandler's shop.

He was tall and spare, with the kind of face that would stay smooth no matter what it was thinking, and his stillness had the same quality as Brine's: total, economical, wrong. Water that still usually has teeth in it.

He caught her looking.

He didn't look away — men like him never did, that was the point of them — he inclined his head, very slightly, the courtesy of one professional acknowledging another across a crowded market. Then he turned and walked unhurriedly up the row and out of sight, and the crowd closed behind him like water over a dropped stone.

Zephyrine stood holding two herrings and a heart that had forgotten its trade.

"Aunt," Brine said — her first word in Vhalcrestan speech, soft, accented strangely, perfectly chosen — "who was that?"

Zephyrine watched the space where the grey man had been.

"That," she said, taking Brine's hand and steering them both, quickly, casually, toward home, "was a man who reads doors. And we, my love, have been leaving ours open."

Behind them, in the press of the market, somewhere, she could feel it — not see, *feel*, the way charms feel a change in weather — attention settling over her shoulders like a hand.

She walked faster. She did not run. Running, her mother had taught her, was how prey told everyone what it was.

But that night, after Brine slept, Zephyrine took out the sixth charm — the calling-back, cold in its resin — and set it on the table, and stared at it until the candle died, because she was beginning to understand that the door she'd kept, the one the child had found her by, had never been hers to close at all.
