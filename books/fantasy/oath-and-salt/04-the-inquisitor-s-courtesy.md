## 4. The Inquisitor's Courtesy

Inquisitor Corvane believed that all truths were like oysters: unpleasant to open, and best done slowly, with a small knife.

He had dressed carefully that morning, because the interview required care. Not his grey working coat, the one with the ash stains at the cuffs — today he wore deep blue, almost black, with a silver chain of office tucked discreetly beneath the collar so it would only show when he leaned forward. He had chosen the tea himself: a white peony from the mountain provinces, delicate, expensive, the kind of tea a man served when he wanted to demonstrate that he could afford patience.

The room he'd booked in the Gullwing House was the best in the inn — high-ceilinged, overlooking the harbor, with a fire lit against the autumn damp and two chairs set at an angle rather than opposite each other. Angled chairs said *we are having a conversation*. Opposite chairs said *this is an interrogation*, and Corvane had found over nineteen years of service that people guarded themselves against interrogations but opened, like slow flowers, during conversations.

He checked the teapot's warmth with his palm. He straightened the cups. He waited, hands folded, a mild-faced man of fifty-odd years with thinning brown hair and the patient eyes of a fisherman who has learned that the sea gives up nothing to men who tug the line.

Zephyrine Blackwood arrived at four o'clock precisely, because of course she did. The summons had been delivered that morning — *the Queen's Taster is requested for consultation regarding matters of kitchen security* — phrased with just enough bureaucratic blandness to be deniable and just enough weight to be unmistakable. She wore her good grey dress, the one without charms sewn into the hem; he knew this because his people had gone through her wardrobe on Tuesday while she was at the palace, and they were thorough men. No jewelry beyond the licensed taster's badge. Hair pinned simply. Hands quiet.

She curtsied exactly as deep as protocol required. Not one inch further.

"Master Corvane."

"Mistress Blackwood." He gestured to the angled chair. "Please. You'll forgive the informality — I find offices make people say what they think I want to hear, and I have come to value your opinions on several matters."

"Have you?" She sat. Spine straight, ankles crossed, hands in lap. A woman arranged like a still life. "I wasn't aware I had any."

"Oh, more than you know." He poured her tea first — always first, always watching how she received it. She took the cup with both hands, which told him she was cold or nervous, and her thumbnail did not tap the porcelain, which told him she was better at this than most. "You've served Her Majesty for five years now. That's remarkable tenure for a taster."

"People die of old age in other professions, Master Corvane. We die of breakfast."

He smiled, genuinely. He enjoyed her. That was the trouble with this work — the ones who lied well were always the ones worth liking. "Humor. Five years and you still have your humor. Do you know how many tasters last five years? I looked it up this morning. In the history of the Vhalcrest household: nine. And three of those were poisoned by their own families for the pension."

"How flattering to be above average."

"The average," he said, "is fourteen months. So either you are extraordinarily lucky, Mistress Blackwood, or extraordinarily careful. I have spent my career studying the difference, and I'll tell you what I've learned: luck runs out. Care compounds." He lifted his own cup. "You are the most careful woman in this city. I mean that as the highest compliment I know how to give."

For half a heartbeat — less than half, a quarter — something moved behind her eyes. Not fear exactly. Recalculation. He filed it away the way he filed everything, in the vast dim archive of his memory, cross-referenced and dusted weekly.

"I'm careful because I like living," she said lightly. "Is there a matter of kitchen security, or do you interview everyone who keeps breathing?"

* * *

The tea was excellent and she hated every sip of drinking it under his gaze, though she gave no sign of it. Corvane admired that too. He let three comfortable minutes pass — talk of the weather, of the harbor ice coming early this year, of the scandalous price of saffron — before he opened his first oyster.

"You keep a shop," he said.

"I keep a flat. Two rooms above a chandler's on Rope Walk. It's in my licensing papers."

"You keep a shop in the sense that a spider keeps a web." He said it warmly, almost fondly. "Small wares. Charms, Mistress Blackwood. Little illegal charms. Knots that won't slip. Candles that burn true. A salve for burns that heals a day faster than any salve has a right to."

The fire popped. Outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

She set down her cup. He watched her decide not to deny it — watched the decision happen, actually, which interested him greatly. Denial was the reflex of amateurs; she had weighed denial and found it too cheap a coin for a man like him.

"If that were true," she said, "it would be drowning."

"It would be drowning," he agreed pleasantly. "Slowly, in the harbor cage, at low tide, so there's quite a lot of time to think about it. I'm told the gulls are bold in winter." He refilled her cup before she could refuse. "And yet here we are, having tea, and no one has arrested you. Ask yourself why."

"Because you enjoy games?"

"Because," Corvane said, "I believe in proportion. There are charm-sellers and there are charm-sellers. A knot-charm will not topple the Crown. Half the grandmothers in the Saltmarket tie knots their grandmothers tied before the Edict, and if I drowned them all I'd empty the fish markets and accomplish nothing except teaching the survivors to hate me quietly instead of ignorantly. No. What concerns the Office of Inquiry is not *that* magic exists. It is what kind, whose, and toward what end." He leaned forward slightly, letting the silver chain catch the firelight. "So. Tell me about the tide-child."

There it was. He had wondered whether to lead with it or bury it; he had decided to bury it, because buried things surfaced with the teller's own hands.

Zephyrine Blackwood went very still. It was masterful stillness — the stillness of a deer, yes, but also of a stone, of a thing that had decided to be furniture. Most people, handed that word, flinched. *Tide-child.* The rumor had run through the lower city for a fortnight now, growing details the way a snowball grows dirt: a girl found on the mudflats after the storm, speaking no trade-tongue, walking through floodwater dry-shod. Corvane had three witnesses and one drowned informant, and he trusted the drowned one least, dead men being unreliable narrators even before they died.

"A tide-child," she repeated. Her voice was perfectly level. Perfectly. Which was itself a small confession, because innocent people ask questions. Innocent people say *what on earth is that?* They wrinkle their noses. This woman simply held the word up to the light like a coin she was checking for clipping.

"There are stories," Corvane said, settling back. "Storm-born children. Sea-marked. The old isles sent up their dead wearing new skin, that sort of thing. Superstition, mostly. But superstition is smoke, Mistress Blackwood, and smoke means fire somewhere. My men say such a child was seen near the Saltmarket. Near, in fact, to certain streets where a certain someone sells her wares." He smiled. "You would know nothing of this, naturally."

"Naturally." She sipped her tea. Her hand did not shake. He noted, with professional disappointment, that it did not shake. "Children turn up in this city all the time. Half of them can't speak the trade-tongue properly — dock brats, gutter brats. If someone saw a muddy child, perhaps it was a muddy child."

"Perhaps." He let the word sit. Then, gently: "Mistress Blackwood, may I tell you why I asked you here, past the theater of kitchen security?"

"I suspect you'll tell me regardless."

"I asked you here because I think you are the most interesting person in Vhalcrest, and because I am about to offer you something I offer very few people." He drew from his breast pocket a folded paper — not a warrant, though it was shaped like one, and he let her look at its shape for exactly as long as it took her to understand it wasn't. "Protection. Formal, signed protection. Immunity for past... commerce. Continued employment at the palace. A stipend. In exchange, information. Anything you hear, anything you see, anything that smells of salt and old blood, comes to me first. Before the rebels get it. Because they *will* get to you, you know. Odell's people have been sniffing around Rope Walk for weeks. Ex-priests are worse than Inquisitors for persistence — at least I'm paid."

"And if I say no?"

Corvane sighed, a small honest sigh, and looked out the window at the gray harbor. "Then I continue my investigation, and investigations are like tides. Patient. Indiscriminate. Everything on the flats gets covered eventually, and everything uncovered gets picked clean by gulls." He turned back to her. "I would genuinely prefer not to drown you, Mistress Blackwood. It's in no one's interest. You're useful. You're careful. And — forgive me — I think you're lonely, and lonely people eventually want to put the burden down. I am offering to carry part of it."

Silence. The fire worked at its log. Somewhere below, the innkeep dropped a tray and swore with impressive variety.

"You're wrong," Zephyrine said at last.

"About?"

"All of it. I sell thread and candles. Ordinary ones. If someone in the Saltmarket sold charms, it wasn't me, and if a child was seen, I haven't seen her." She rose, smooth as poured water, and set her cup on its saucer with a click that sounded, to his trained ear, like a door closing. "I taste food for the Queen. I go home. I sleep badly and wake early. That is the whole of my life, Master Corvane, and I'd thank you not to make it larger."

"Of course." He stood too, and bowed — a real bow, deep, courteous to the bone. "Thank you for your time. The tea is a house blend; I'll send you some. White peony. It steadies the nerves."

At the door she paused. He watched her back, the straight spine, the careful shoulders, and he said, softly, conversationally, the knife going in without any motion at all:

"Your mother was from the isles, wasn't she. The drowned ones. I've been reading the census rolls — old ones. Fascinating reading. The Blackwoods of Saltmere Isle." He let it hang. "Such a shame what happened there. Such a shame anyone survived it at all."

Her shoulders did not move. Her breath did not change. She left without another word, and the door clicked shut behind her with the same neat finality as the teacup.

Corvane stood alone in the fine warm room and allowed himself a long exhale, and then, unexpectedly, a laugh — short, genuine, almost admiring.

*Lies well,* he wrote in his little black book that evening, by lamplight. *Not perfectly. She never asked what a tide-child was.*

He blotted the page, and beneath it, in smaller script, added the line that would shape the whole terrible fortnight to come:

*Send men to Rope Walk. Quietly. Tonight.*
