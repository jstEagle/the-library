## 5. What the Rebels Want

The message came the way all of Odell's messages came: folded inside something mundane, delivered by someone forgettable. This time it was tucked into a paper cone of roasted chestnuts sold to Zephyrine by a boy with a limp and no memory of having been paid.

*The Lantern & Eel. After compline. Come alone. Bring nothing you'd miss.*

She read it twice by candlelight while Brine slept in the loft above her shop, curled around a charm made from a fishhook and red thread that Zephyrine had strung for her on the second night — not because it worked, but because the girl had looked at it the way other children looked at festival lanterns.

The chestnuts were good. That was the trouble with Odell. Even his threats arrived wrapped in kindness.

Zephyrine left Brine with Mrs. Tallow next door, who had been told the child was her niece visiting from upriver and who had accepted this explanation with the serene gullibility of a woman who believed everything except that anyone might be lying about anything important. She left her knife too, per instructions, though she kept three charms sewn into her coat hem that Odell didn't know about and never would. *Come alone* meant *come without weapons*. It did not mean *come defenseless*. A woman who survived five years as a royal taster learned the difference early.

The Lantern & Eel sat where the Saltmarket bled into the harbor district, a tavern built on pilings over black water, leaning slightly seaward like an old man listening for a name he half remembered. Inside it smelled of tar, fried eel, and the particular staleness of rooms where men made decisions they'd regret sober.

Odell was in the back booth, as always beneath the one lamp that worked. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a priest's tonsure grown out into gray stubble and hands like a dockworker's that had once, she knew, held communion bread. He'd been a chaplain of the Tidebound Order before the Crown drowned the last of them in the harbor — literally, in some cases. He still wore his old faith the way amputees wear phantom limbs: constantly, painfully, and with no useful function.

"Zeph." He smiled and pushed a cup toward her. "You look terrible."

"I've been tasting for the Queen since dawn. I'm fairly sure the pheasant was trying to kill me."

"Was it?"

"Underseasoned, if nothing else."

He laughed, and for a moment he was just Odell again, the man who'd taught her which herbs masked hemlock and which priests could be trusted and which couldn't. Then the lamp flickered, or seemed to, and his face settled into the gravity she'd come dreading.

"You've got a girl," he said.

Zephyrine kept her hands still around the cup. Stillness was the first lesson of the taster's trade: never let the poison see you flinch. "I've got a niece."

"From upriver."

"Upriver is full of nieces."

"Zeph." He leaned forward. The lamplight caught the stubble of his tonsure, the scar where his holy orders had been cut out of him along with his title. "A mud-soaked child walked out of the storm two weeks ago and hasn't said a word since. Except that Tallow woman swears she heard her singing in her sleep. Singing in a tongue nobody knows." He paused. "Nobody living, anyway."

The tavern noise went distant. Somewhere behind her a man laughed too loudly at nothing. Zephyrine drank, buying herself a moment, tasting nothing.

"Who told you this?"

"Mrs. Tallow tells everyone everything. It's why we love her." His voice gentled, which was worse than if he'd shouted. "You know what that language is. You've known since the night you took her in, because I watched your face when you carried her past my window, and you looked like a woman seeing a ghost she'd personally buried."

Five years of careful work. Five years of being nobody, of selling charms that were mostly superstition and hope, of keeping her real craft folded small enough to hide in a coat seam. And undone by a neighbor who couldn't keep her mouth shut and a man who loved her enough to pay attention.

"What do you want, Odell?"

"What we always want." He spread his hands. "What the faithful want. What the drowned wanted."

"She's a child."

"She's a vessel." The word dropped between them like a stone in a well. "You felt it. Don't lie to me, Zeph, I'll know. Dead charms wake up around her. Old words remember themselves. You've spent five years pretending your mother's craft was party tricks, and now a girl walks out of the sea carrying the whole of it in her skull, and you want me to believe you haven't noticed what she *is*?"

Zephyrine set down the cup. Her hands were steady. She'd trained them to be. "Say what you actually want."

"The network wants her." No more gentleness now; this was Odell the organizer, Odell who'd rebuilt a church out of smugglers and charm-sellers. "There are four hundred souls in Vhalcrest who practice the old ways in cellars and attics, who whisper prayers the Crown calls treason. Four hundred people who have never seen a miracle. Never seen proof that any of it was real, that the drowning of the isles wasn't just the Crown murdering a rival and calling it holy work. And then—" he snapped his fingers, softly, almost reverently— "a child who speaks the pre-kingdom tongue. Who makes dead things glow. Do you understand what she'd mean to them? Not a weapon. A *banner*. Something to gather under."

"And when the Inquisitor notices the banner?" Zephyrine asked. "Because he will. Corvane notices everything. You want to plant a living relic in the middle of four hundred frightened people and pray none of them breaks?"

"People break every day, Zeph. That's what faith is for."

"That's a terrible sermon."

"I was defrocked. My sermons improved considerably after."

It would have been easy, so easy, to laugh and let it go. But underneath the joke she could see the other thing, the thing he hadn't said yet, coiled at the bottom of his argument like a hook under bait. She'd spent years reading men across tables. She knew when there was more coming.

"And the others?" she said. "Not the faithful. The ones with muskets."

Odell's jaw tightened. "The Harbor Compact has expressed interest."

"The Harbor Compact expresses interest in anything that bleeds the Crown."

"They think a tide-child could be more than a symbol. They think—" he exhaled through his nose, a big man trying to make a small confession— "they think the old magic was real, and if it was real, it can be *used*. Against the Queen. Against the walls. Against the navy. A girl who carries the memory of the sea-mages might carry their power too, or the key to it, and the Compact would very much like to find out before Corvane does."

"No."

"Zeph—"

"*No.*" She said it quietly, which somehow made it louder. Around them the tavern went on, oblivious. "She is not a banner and she is not a battering ram. She's a child who walked out of a storm with nothing, and everyone in this city who learns what she is will try to own her, and I will not hand her to you just because you're the first ones in line and you smile when you ask."

Odell was silent a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had the weariness of a man who'd argued this with himself already and lost. "And what's your plan? Keep her hidden forever? Sell charms until Corvane kicks the door in? Because he's asking questions in the Saltmarket, Zeph. About a charm-seller whose wares are better than they should be. About a tide-child seen on a taster's street. You're not safe. *She's* not safe. We're offering protection."

"We're offering a cage with hymns painted on it," Zephyrine said. "Or a cage with gunpowder painted on it. I've seen both. I decline."

"You decline." He rubbed his face with both hands. "You understand that refusing us doesn't make you neutral. It makes you alone. There's no third side in this city, however much you'd like one. There's the Crown, there's us, and there's the harbor at the bottom of everything."

"I've eaten worse options than loneliness," Zephyrine said. "Professionally."

That startled a laugh out of him despite everything — a short, unhappy bark. Then he stood, and the lamplight made him look older, and sadder, and more dangerous than she'd ever let herself see.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I hoped you'd say yes. And I hoped you'd say no. Both at once, like a bad prayer." He laid a coin on the table — overpayment for the chestnuts, a priest's blessing disguised as arithmetic. "But Zeph — hear me. If the Compact comes for her and we stand aside, don't blame us. And if we decide she's safer with us than with you, well." He met her eyes. "Don't blame us then either."

"There it is," Zephyrine said softly. "The threat wearing its Sunday clothes."

"It's not a threat. It's a weather report."

She stood too. For a moment they faced each other across the little table, two people who had trusted each other with their lives for five years and had just discovered that trust had a shape, and the shape was a child neither of them would give up.

"Goodnight, Odell."

"Goodnight, Zeph. Mind the fog. It's thick tonight." He paused at the edge of the lamplight. "Everything's thick tonight."

* * *

The walk home took longer than it should have. Fog off the harbor had swallowed the streets whole, turning gaslamps into smudges and strangers into suggestions. Zephyrine moved through it with her collar up and her mind churning, cataloguing exits, weighing Odell's parting words against five years of knowing him.

He wouldn't betray her quickly. That was the comfort. He'd agonize first, hold meetings, pray over it in his defrocked way. She had weeks, maybe.

But the Compact wouldn't wait on his conscience. And Corvane — she'd felt him watching her street for days now, a pressure like a change in the air before a storm. Everyone wanted the same small girl, and the girl herself only wanted warm milk and the window left open a crack so she could hear the water.

Zephyrine let herself into the shop, bolted the door, and climbed the ladder to the loft.

Brine was awake. Sitting up in the dark, eyes open, watching the window where the fog pressed against the glass like something patient waiting to be let in. When Zephyrine reached her, the girl took her hand and pressed it flat against her own small chest, and Zephyrine felt it — faint, unmistakable, impossible — a pulse beneath the skin that beat in time with the tide.

Then Brine pointed, one thin finger, past Zephyrine's shoulder. Down. Toward the street.

Through the fog below, at the corner where the lamplight died, a figure stood motionless in the rain. Tall. Still. Watching the window.

Watching them.
