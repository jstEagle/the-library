## 6. Memory of Water

* * *

Cold first.

Cold is the oldest thing. Cold is before names.

The woman's hand on mine — warm-warm-warm, like a stone left too near a fire — and under her skin, water remembering it was once rain. I hold on. Holding on is how we speak. Words are for people who have forgotten how to listen.

She flinches. They always flinch.

* * *

Then: salt.

Salt in the nose, salt on the tongue, salt in the place behind the eyes where crying lives. The isles are green above me and I am small and the sea is singing through my grandmother's bones — *hush now, hush now, the tide keeps what it loves* —

Green going gray. Gray going black.

Smoke where the wind should be.

I show her this because she asked without asking. Her fingers curled around mine in the dark of her little house that smells of tallow and burnt sugar, and her pulse jumped when I said the old word, the first word, the word that means *come home*, and so. So.

I share.

That is what we were for, before. Before the burning. Before the drowning. We were the sea's memory wearing skin, and skin is such a thin coat to keep a whole ocean in.

* * *

Fire next.

Fire is loud. Fire has no manners.

They come in boats with white sails and hammers and men in gray robes who speak the new tongue, the flat tongue, the tongue with no water in it. The mages of the isles stand on the shore and the robes say *renounce* and the mages say nothing, because our words are not for bargaining, they are for keeping.

An old man walks into the waves first. He does not swim. He walks until the water takes his hat, his shoulders, his hands still raised in blessing, and the sea closes over him like a mouth being polite about it.

Then another. Then ten. Then all of them, walking in, singing, the song going down and down under the water where fire cannot follow, and the gray-robed men on the shore scream that this is not surrender, this is theft, the magic belongs to the Crown now, you cannot drown what belongs to the Crown—

But you can.

You can. That is the joke. The sea kept it anyway. Kept it all these years, in cold dark vaults, in fish-bellies and kelp-forests and the slow amber of drowned temples. The Crown burned an empty house and called itself victorious, and the sea said nothing, because the sea is patient the way stones are patient.

I feel the woman watching this inside my hands. I feel her heart go strange.

* * *

And then — this part I did not mean to show. This part leaks.

A woman on the shore who does not walk into the water.

She stands apart from the singers. She wears traveling clothes, land-clothes, clothes with buttons, and she is talking to one of the gray robes, leaning close, and the robe is writing something down, nodding, writing, and when the last singer goes under the woman with the buttons turns away before the sea finishes closing, turns away like someone leaving a market early to beat the crowds.

Her face.

Her face is the face of the woman holding my hand.

Not exactly. Faces drift, like coastlines. But the same bones underneath. The same set of the mouth, the mouth that knows how to stay shut.

Zephyrine — she told me her name is Zephyrine, a land-name, a made-up name, a name like a coat with buttons — Zephyrine makes a sound. Small. Like a cup cracking.

I didn't mean to show that part. But memory is not a cupboard you can close properly. Memory is water. It finds the crack.

* * *

More leaking. Sorry. Sorry sorry sorry.

A kitchen. A kitchen on land, in the years after, and the button-woman teaching a child — a girl, small, dark-haired — to count in the flat tongue but sing in the true one, quietly, quietly, *only when the kettle boils, only then, the noise will cover us*. And the girl learning, quick as a gull, and the girl's mother — no, aunt? No. Mother. The button-woman is the mother — pressing a finger to the girl's lips and saying in the old tongue:

*What the tide gives, the tide can take. Give it back before they come.*

And the girl promising. And the girl growing. And the girl leaving. And the girl — 

Oh.

Oh, the girl becomes the woman whose hand I am holding. The girl becomes Zephyrine. The girl buried the songs so deep even she cannot hear them anymore, packed them down under five years of broth-tasting and charm-selling and pretending, packed them like a sailor packing a hull against leaks.

But I am a leak.

I am the leak.

* * *

And the worst part. The worst part comes last, the way worst parts do.

The button-woman again. Older now. Dying, maybe, or just tired — tired looks the same. She holds the young Zephyrine's face in her two hands and says, not in the old tongue, in the flat one, deliberately, so there can be no mistaking:

*"Your grandmother's line helped them find the isles. We drew the maps. We sold the tides. That is why we lived."*

And: *"Never tell anyone. Not even yourself."*

*Not even yourself.*

So Zephyrine obeyed. All these years, obedient as a good little wave, she never told herself. She built the little house and took the taster's work and swallowed poison for the Queen and sold small safe charms at night and never once let herself remember why her hands already knew the motions of tide-craft, why the old words sat in her mouth like her own teeth.

She thought she was hiding from the Crown.

She was hiding from herself. Which is harder. The Crown only sends Inquisitors.

* * *

I open my eyes. Her eyes are open too. Wet. Land-people leak from the eyes; we never understood that, growing up. Grief with nowhere to go, so it goes out the face.

Her hand is still in mine. It is shaking. Shaking is a language too. It says many things at once, which is inefficient, but heartfelt.

"I don't—" she starts, in the flat tongue, and stops. Tries the old one instead, rusty, halting, each word like lifting a stone off a grave: "*Isles-of-my-mothers. Was-that. Was-that-us.*"

I answer in the old tongue, because she deserves it in the old tongue:

"*Your blood held the door open. Your blood walked them in.*"

She makes the cup-cracking sound again.

I put my other hand over hers. Warm-warm-warm. I am small and mud-colored and I have been walking for a long time, longer than she knows, following a pull in my chest like a fish follows current, and the pull led here, to this door, to this woman, because the sea does not do accidents. The sea does *accounts*.

The last memory is not hers to carry alone anymore. That is why I came. Not to punish her. Punishment is a land idea; we had no use for it, we just had consequences, like weather.

I came because a secret this heavy needs two backs.

Or — no. Honest accounting, Brine. You came because the secret needed a keeper and the keepers are all dead or drowned or turned traitor, and this woman, this button-blooded, map-selling, song-burying woman, is the last one left who could learn to keep it. The sea sent me to the traitor's daughter because the traitor's daughter is all that's left.

The sea forgives. The sea just also remembers. Both things, always both things, that is what land-people never understand about us.

* * *

She pulls her hand back. Wraps her arms around herself, rocking a little, and outside the window Vhalcrest goes on being Vhalcrest — bells, gulls, the rattle of carts, somebody yelling about fish prices — and none of it knows that in this small room a whole drowned nation just surfaced for air.

"They'll burn me," she whispers. "If they knew what I— if anyone knew what my family—"

"Nobody knows," I tell her. Old tongue. "Nobody living."

"Except you."

"Except me," I agree. "And I'm nobody. I'm a mud-child. I'm a stray. Nobody questions a stray."

She almost laughs. It comes out crooked, half-drowned, but it counts. Land-people laugh when they are frightened; we learned that from watching them. It is their way of shaking.

Then she goes still. That particular stillness. The listening stillness.

"What?" I ask.

"Boots," she says. "On my street. Too many boots for this hour, and stopping."

I look out the window, careful, careful, only my eyes over the sill.

Down in the lamplight, where the cobbles are still wet from yesterday's storm, a man in gray stands looking up at her door. Not hurrying. Not hiding. Just looking, the way a man looks at a page he is deciding whether to write on.

He has ink-stained fingers and a kind, patient face, and he is smiling slightly, and I know that smile. The gray robes smiled like that on the shore, right before the singing started.

Beside him, a soldier knocks on the door below us.

Knocks. Politely.

Three times.
