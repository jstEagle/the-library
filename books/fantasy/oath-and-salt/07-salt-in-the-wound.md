## 7. Salt in the Wound

The knock came at dawn, and it was wrong.

Not the soft triple-tap Odell used, nor the scratchy rhythm of the fishwife who bought fertility charms and complained about her husband. This was a fist, repeated, impatient — the knock of a man accustomed to doors opening before he finished.

Zephyrine was already awake. She had been awake since the third bell, when Brine sat bolt upright in the dark and made a sound like a gull crying over open water, and would not be soothed until Zephyrine opened the shutters and let her watch the rain.

"The market," Zephyrine said now, to no one, to the ceiling. "They've gone for the market."

Brine stood in the doorway of the little back room, wearing one of Zephyrine's old shifts belted with rope, her dark hair uncombed, her eyes fixed on Zephyrine with that unnerving stillness she had. Children fidgeted. Brine *waited*, the way water waits behind a seawall.

"Stay here," Zephyrine said. "Bolt the door. Don't open it for anyone, not even me, unless I say *low tide*."

Brine's mouth pressed flat. She did not move toward the bolt. She had opinions about being left, this child, and expressed them entirely through posture.

"*Low tide*," Zephyrine repeated, and made the sign her mother used to make over cradles — two fingers drawing a wave, then closing into a fist. Brine watched it, and something in her face said she understood perfectly well what it meant: *if I don't come back, run.*

She went anyway. That was the thing about oaths; they were heavier than fear.

* * *

The Saltmarket at dawn should have smelled of brine and blood and frying onions, of the particular sweetness of herring too long out of the ice. Instead it smelled of smoke and wet ash, and the noise was not commerce but panic — a sound Zephyrine knew from five years of listening for it the way other people listened for their names.

Soldiers in the Queen's grey-and-gold moved through the stalls in pairs, overturning baskets with the bored efficiency of men doing a job they'd done many times. And at the center, unhurried as a man inspecting his own garden, stood Inquisitor Corvane.

He was not a large man. He wore plain black without insignia, which frightened people more than any livery would have. He held a small notebook. He consulted it the way a priest consults scripture.

Zephyrine pulled her hood low and became a fishwife's widow, shoulders bowed, basket on her hip, one of forty women shuffling in the press near the fountain. It was a skill, invisibility. She had practiced it for years. You didn't hide by running; you hid by being too dull to remember.

Then she saw Odell.

He stood between two soldiers outside the chandlery where the network kept its stores, and he was not struggling. He looked — there was no kinder word for it — he looked like a man who had decided something. His coat was buttoned wrong. His hands were bound in front of him, and he was using them, absurdly, to straighten the collar of the soldier beside him, murmuring something that made the young man flush and look away.

*Oh, you fool,* Zephyrine thought, with a love that felt like swallowing glass. *You kind, reckless fool.*

Corvane approached him. They spoke. She could not hear the words over the crowd, but she saw Odell smile — actually smile — and shake his head once, gently, the way he declined a second cup of tea.

Corvane nodded. Wrote something in his notebook.

And then, without any change of expression, he said three words that carried across the square the way certain words do, pitched precisely, aimed like an arrow:

"Find the taster."

Zephyrine's body understood before her mind did. Her feet were already moving, carrying her basket, her bowed widow's shoulders, out of the press and down Fishgut Lane, walking, *walking*, because running was a confession, while her heart hammered out the truth her posture denied: *he has my name. Odell gave him my name.*

She could not even hate him for it. She had seen Corvane's notebook. Everyone broke eventually; the only question was whether they broke into pieces or into words. Odell had broken into words because Odell believed words were holy things, meant to be shared. It was the same faith that made him shelter smugglers and feed beggars and get himself taken in a raid he must have seen coming for weeks.

*Forgive me,* she thought, meaning both of them — Odell for surviving, herself for fleeing.

* * *

She did not go home. That was the first rule and the only rule: never lead them to what you love. She went instead to the tannery drain at the end of Cooper's Row, the one she'd paid a leather-worker three years' worth of charm-money to leave unlatched, and she waited inside its stinking dark with her heart going like a drum until she heard footsteps — small, bare, utterly silent — and Brine emerged from the shadows as though she'd always lived in them.

"You followed me." Zephyrine crouched, took the girl's cold face in her hands. "I told you—"

Brine pointed back the way she'd come, then at Zephyrine, then pressed her fist to her own chest with a firmness that needed no translation: *where you go, I go.* Then, softer, she took Zephyrine's hand and turned it palm-up, and traced one finger across it — a wave, curling and breaking.

Zephyrine's breath stopped.

It was the sign. The cradle-sign. Her mother's sign, drawn in salt on window sills, in flour on bread, in the air above sleeping children: *the sea keeps what it loves.*

"Where did you learn that?" she whispered, though she knew. She had known since the night on the doorstep. She just hadn't let herself know it all the way down.

Brine tilted her head, and in the dark of the drain her eyes caught light that wasn't there, a faint green-grey shimmer like the underside of a wave, and Zephyrine felt it rise in herself in answer — that old warmth behind the breastbone she had spent thirty years smothering under routine, under licenses, under the daily discipline of being nobody.

Somewhere above them, boots crossed Cooper's Row. Many boots.

"They'll check the house first," Zephyrine murmured. "Then the tannery. Then every door I've ever knocked on. We can't go up. We can't stay." She looked down the drain, into the black channel of the undercity, where the old river ran beneath Vhalcrest like a vein beneath skin, flooded these past weeks with storm-water. "So we go down."

Brine smiled.

It was the first true smile Zephyrine had ever seen her give, and it was, she thought wildly, exactly the smile of a woman in a memory she hadn't yet let herself remember — a woman walking into the sea with her chin lifted, glad.

* * *

The undercity of Vhalcrest was a drowned country that most citizens pretended not to have. Old streets, older than the kingdom, swallowed when the founders raised the seawalls and let the low quarters fill. Smugglers used the upper galleries. Nobody used the deep ways, because the deep ways were deep, and cold, and full of the particular silence that makes people think of graves.

Zephyrine waded in to her waist before she admitted what she was doing.

Her hands were moving. She hadn't told them to. They were making the passes her mother made over bathwater, over soup, over the fevered brows of neighbors — quick, economical gestures, a language of the hands older than any alphabet — and where her fingers passed, the black water *answered*. It flattened around her legs instead of dragging. It warmed, faintly, scandalously, like a bath drawn for a beloved guest. Light rose from it in slow green coils, lighting their way down the drowned street, and Brine walked beside her glowing like a lamp itself, every dead charm on Zephyrine's neck and wrists humming back to life one by one, singing in frequencies she felt in her teeth.

"Oh," Zephyrine said aloud, to the water, to her mother, to no one. "Oh, I remember how to do this."

That was the terrible thing. Not that it was hard. That it was *easy*. Five years of careful nothing, of swallowing poison and swallowing magic and swallowing grief, and underneath it all the craft had stayed whole, waiting, patient as tide. Her body had never agreed to be nobody. Only her name had.

They went deeper. Past doorways with lintels carved in the pre-kingdom script — waves, gulls, the spiral that meant *return* — past a sunken shrine where offerings still lay green with age, past windows behind which, she could not help feeling, someone might still be watching. The water sang against the walls in a register below hearing, and Brine hummed along, and Zephyrine realized the girl was teaching *her*, correcting her hand-shapes with small tugs at her sleeve, patient as a grandmother.

"I'm supposed to be protecting you," Zephyrine said, laughing, and heard her voice crack in the echo. "That was the arrangement. I feed you, you haunt my house, we pretend neither of us is anything at all."

Brine squeezed her hand. In the green light her face was grave and kind, and she mouthed something — no sound, but Zephyrine read the shape of it in the old tongue, the word her mother had used for storms, for births, for all vast and unavoidable things:

*Necessary.*

Behind them, far back along the flooded way, a light appeared. Not green. White and hard, lantern-light, and with it voices — soldiers' voices, echoing strange off the water — and one voice among them, dry and courteous even down here, even in the dark, saying something she couldn't make out but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a knife by the shape of the wound.

Corvane had not gone to her house first after all.

He had guessed the water.

Zephyrine pulled Brine close and did the last thing she swore she'd never do: she reached for the deep craft, the heavy craft, the tide-craft her mother only used twice — and the whole drowned street leaned toward her like a listener.

Ahead, far down in the dark, something answered. A light. Green-gold, vast, patient, rising through the flooded ways like a sun coming up underwater — and Brine began, silently, joyfully, to walk toward it.
