## 8. The Drowned Court

The undercity ended where the sea had decided it should.

Zephyrine felt the change before she saw it — a softening in the air, a salt-sweetness that no sewer or flood-channel could account for. Brine's small hand tugged hers forward through the last of the flooded streets, past doorways drowned to their lintels, past a chapel whose bell tower leaned like a drunk uncle at a wedding. The water here was clear. That was the wrongness of it. Vhalcrest's floodwater was always the color of regret, brown and thick with the city's leavings, but this water lay green-gold and still as glass in a church window.

"Brine." Zephyrine crouched, keeping her voice low out of habit, though there was no one left to hear them. The raid behind them had scattered like spilled coins; she'd used the tide-craft openly, felt it sing up her arms for the first time in twenty years, and now there was no pretending back. "Where are you taking me?"

The girl looked at her with those eyes the color of deep water over sand, and answered — as she always answered — in the old tongue. Three words this time. Zephyrine's mother had sung those words to her over a cradle, a lullaby about following the light down.

*Follow the light down.*

"I'm not going to like this, am I," Zephyrine said, and stood, and followed the light down.

* * *

The temple had been a temple once. Now it was a memory wearing stone.

They waded in through a breach in its western wall, and the water rose to their waists, then their ribs, warm as bathwater and lit from beneath by something that was not sunlight. Zephyrine had seen bioluminescence before, in the harbor mussels the poor scraped for soup. This was nothing like that. This light moved with intention. It gathered in slow coils and eddies around the sunken pillars, and where it touched the carved walls, images stirred — waves, boats, figures walking into the sea with their arms raised, not drowning but *arriving*.

Brine let go of her hand and walked forward, and the water parted around the child in a way that made Zephyrine's chest ache with recognition. Not dramatically. No parting of seas, no thunder. Just a courtesy, the way water makes room for a thing that belongs to it.

"Oh," Zephyrine said softly. "Oh, you're home."

She shouldn't have come. Every sensible bone in her body — and she had cultivated her sensible bones carefully, the way other women cultivated herb gardens — said turn back, find a smuggler, buy passage to somewhere the Queen's writ didn't run. But her feet kept moving, and the light kept rising to meet them, and somewhere in the flooded dark a voice that might have been memory and might have been the building itself seemed to say: *you too. You also.*

The central hall opened around them like a held breath. Pillars marched into the depths, each one carved with faces — some serene, some screaming, all of them wet-eyed even underwater, which should have been impossible. And in the middle of the hall, where an altar would have stood, the light pooled.

Not scattered. Pooled. Like oil on a puddle, like lamplight caught in a bowl, like something poured and gathering and *waiting*. It was the color of dawn on the harbor, gold shot through with green, and it turned slowly, and inside it — Zephyrine pressed a hand to her mouth — inside it were faces. Hundreds of faces. Thousands. Moving like fish under ice.

"What is it?" she whispered. She knew. Her mother's lullabies had told her, if you listened past the melody. "What is it?"

Brine reached out both hands toward the pool, and the light reached back, and for one moment Zephyrine saw the child silhouetted against it, small and straight-backed, and understood that she was looking at a vessel standing before the sea it had been filled from.

Then the memories touched her.

It was not like Corvane's interrogation, all needle and scalpel. It was not even like Brine's visions in the safehouse, which had come in fragments, sensory and half-drowned. This was a tide. It took her whole.

* * *

She stood on a shore that no longer existed, on an island green as a promise, and she watched her great-great-grandmother — no, not quite, someone older, someone whose face the family had worn and re-worn until it became this face, *her* face — stand before a council of sea-mages and lie to them beautifully.

She watched the Crown's ships come at dawn because the wards had been turned inward. She watched mages walk into the water rather than burn, singing, arms raised, arriving. She watched children hidden in hulls of ships that never sank, bloodlines carried inland like seeds in a pocket, sworn to silence, sworn to *service*, sworn to keep the memory safe by keeping it buried so deep that even they would forget they were guards and believe themselves merely survivors.

And underneath it all, patient as pressure at depth, she felt the truth the memories had been holding:

*The war did not end. The land won a battle and called it peace. The sea withdrew and called it patience. And the memory — the living, breathing memory of what was lost and what was promised — sleeps in a child who walks and eats and laughs, and every power in Vhalcrest will soon understand that whoever holds her holds the hinge of the world.*

The tide released her.

Zephyrine came back to herself kneeling in waist-deep water, shaking, Brine's arms around her neck, the girl making low sounds against her ear — not words, not quite, but comfort in the old tongue, the same notes her mother had used when Zephyrine had nightmares as a girl.

"They didn't lose," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out cracked. "My family. They didn't just survive. They *betrayed* — they opened the wards, they—" She stopped, because Brine was looking at her with an expression far too old for a child of seven or eight, an expression that said: *yes. And I am what they were guarding. The punishment and the penance, all in one small body.*

"You're not a symbol," Zephyrine said. "You're not a weapon. You're a *library*. A living library, and everyone who finds out is going to want to either burn you or shelve you, and I—"

The light in the pool flickered.

All of it, all at once, the way a candle gutters when a door opens somewhere in the house. The faces in the glow turned — not individually, but together, the way a school of fish turns — toward the breached western wall.

Zephyrine turned too.

Torches. Many torches, reflected double in the still water, coming down the flooded street in disciplined formation. And above them, taller than the soldiers carrying him, a shadow she knew the shape of the way she knew the shape of her own hands.

"No," she breathed. "No, no, we were careful, we went through the—"

"He was always going to follow," said a voice from the dark of the pillars, and Zephyrine nearly screamed. Odell stepped out of the shadows, dripping, his ex-priest's robes torn and filthy, a bruise blooming across half his face. Alive. Free. Which meant he'd bought his freedom, or traded for it, and Zephyrine's stomach dropped through the flooded floor. "I'm sorry, Zeph. They don't question me anymore. They *escort* me. There's a difference, and it's measured in chains."

"You gave them the temple."

"I gave them nothing they couldn't follow anyway." His eyes went to Brine, and there was such naked longing in them that Zephyrine stepped between them without deciding to. "But hear me, before he arrives. The faithful are coming too. All of them. If the Inquisitor takes her, the dream dies quietly in a cell. If we take her, the dream lives — loud, and burning, and *free*. Either way she leaves this place tonight. The only choice you get is which hands."

"That's not a choice," Zephyrine said. "That's a menu."

"And you've been eating alone long enough." He almost smiled. "He's here."

The torches entered the breach.

Corvane came last, as she'd known he would, wading through the water with his boots dry — actually dry, she noticed distantly, some charm of his own, some licensed and sanctified magic that the law called faith and called hers drowning-offense. Soldiers fanned along the pillars, spears leveled, and the pooled light dimmed around them like flowers closing at dusk.

"Inquisitor," Zephyrine said. Her voice was steady. Twenty years of tasting poison had taught her exactly how steady a voice could be while everything underneath curdled.

"Lady Blackwood." Corvane inclined his head with terrible courtesy. His gaze moved past her to Brine, to the pool, to the carved faces on the pillars, and for the first time since she'd known him, Zephyrine saw something in his expression that was not surgical. It was hunger. Old hunger. The kind that has waited a long time at a table. "Do you know what my order was founded to do? Everyone thinks it was to hunt mages. It wasn't. It was to *keep watch*. On this." He gestured, and the torchlight caught the pool, the faces, the sleeping tide of memory. "On places like this. My predecessors sealed nine of them. This one they thought already dead."

"It's not a place," Zephyrine said. "It's people. What's left of them."

"Yes." He smiled, thin and genuine, which was worse. "And the child is the key to it. To waking it, or ending it forever. My masters have argued about which for three hundred years." He extended one white-gloved hand toward Brine, gentle as a man offering a sweet. "Come, little one. No harm will touch you. You have my word and the Crown's seal."

Behind her, Zephyrine heard Odell's rebels wading in through the eastern arch, torches of their own, spears of their own, and the water between the two forces began — very faintly, very terribly — to tremble.

Brine slipped her hand into Zephyrine's.

And the pool behind them rose, all of it, every face in it turning outward, and the temple began to speak.
