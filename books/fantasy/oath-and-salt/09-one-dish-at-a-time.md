## 9. One Dish at a Time

The drowned temple smelled of old candles and older salt.

Zephyrine stood in the nave where the sea had come in and never left, water shin-deep and clear as glass, and watched light pool on the floor like spilled honey. That was the memories, she understood now. The last sea-mages had poured themselves out here rather than let the Crown burn what they knew, and five hundred years later they were still pouring — patient as tide, bright as a queen's table setting.

Brine stood in the middle of it, barefoot, her small hands open at her sides, and the light rose around her like something greeting its own heart.

"She's beautiful," Zephyrine said quietly, to no one, because there was no one left to say it to. Odell was in a Crown cell. Her charm network was scattered or dead. And somewhere above them, boots were finding the flooded stairs.

She'd known Corvane would follow. She'd hoped, foolishly, for one hour more.

* * *

He came down alone, which was somehow worse than soldiers.

Water streamed off his grey coat as he waded into the nave, unhurried, his lantern held high so the light could do its work. Behind him, at the top of the stair, she could see the shapes of his men — six, maybe eight, standing in the dark like furniture that hated you.

"Inquisitor," Zephyrine said, and put herself between him and Brine, which was theater and they both knew it. There was nowhere between. There was only the pool of light, and the child standing in it.

"Madam Blackwood." He inclined his head, courteous as ever, and she thought: *he has rehearsed this too.* "Or should I say whatever your true name is? I confess I've grown fond of Blackwood. It suits you. Very respectable. Very invented."

"You've had a long night," she said. "Odell talked."

"Odell sang." Something flickered behind his eyes — not triumph. Weariness, maybe. "Priests always do, eventually. They mistake confession for courage." He looked past her, at Brine, and his voice went soft in a way that made her skin crawl worse than shouting would have. "Do you know what I see when I look at her, madam?"

"A child."

"I see a war." He stepped closer; the water barely rippled. "Five hundred years asleep, and here it stands, dripping on my floor. The Crown burned the isles' magic once because it *ended kingdoms*, Zephyrine. Not because it was wicked. Because it was stronger than crowns. And you want me to believe you'll simply... keep her? Teach her letters? Let her grow up selling charms in some other market until someone less tired than me finds her?"

"Yes," Zephyrine said. "That's exactly what I want."

Something almost like respect crossed his face. "You're a better liar than Odell. But you believe it. That's the trouble."

Then the rebels came.

They poured down the far stair — a dozen of them, wet and desperate, and at their head a woman Zephyrine didn't know, scarred jaw, eyes like a storm deciding. One of Odell's people. Of course. Odell had sung, and songs travel.

"The tide-child," the woman said, and her voice cracked with something that might have been reverence or hunger. "We felt her from across the city. Every charm we ever made woke up when she walked past." She raised a hand and every rebel did the same, and Zephyrine saw the small dead charms on their wrists begin, faintly, to glow.

"Stand aside," the woman said to Zephyrine. "You've done enough. She belongs to the faithful now. She's our proof. Our banner. With her, every mage in Vhalcrest comes out of hiding, and we drown this crown in—"

"She's seven," Zephyrine said.

"She's a *weapon*—"

"She's seven," Zephyrine repeated, "and she doesn't know what any of you are saying, and if you take one more step toward her I will show you what my mother taught me, and none of you will enjoy the lesson."

Corvane laughed. Actually laughed — a short, exhausted sound. "Remarkable," he said. "Everyone wants her. The Crown wants her erased, the faithful want her enshrined, and you—" he gestured at Zephyrine with his lantern, "—you want her fed and put to bed. Do you know how rare that is? To be the only person in a room who wants nothing *from* someone?" His voice dropped. "It makes you the most dangerous person here."

"Thank you," Zephyrine said. "I've worked very hard at it."

And then Brine turned around.

* * *

Later, Zephyrine would never be able to describe it properly. The girl looked at Corvane, and at the rebels, and then at Zephyrine, and the pooled light rose up around her like a wave deciding, and she spoke — not in the tongue of the drowned isles this time, but in words that arrived inside Zephyrine's chest rather than her ears:

*Choose.*

The whole temple waited. Even the water seemed to hold still.

Zephyrine thought about running. There were tunnels; Brine knew them; the sea-mages had built doors into their own drowning. She thought about fighting, and knew the arithmetic of eight soldiers and twelve rebels against one food-taster who'd spent five years being careful. She thought about all the careful years, and how careful had meant alive, and alive had meant small, and small had meant watching a mute child eat bread across her table and knowing — knowing — that someday, someone would come for her, and all Zephyrine's care would amount to a longer or shorter head start.

Her mother had sung a rite once. A lullaby that wasn't a lullaby. Zephyrine had learned it before she'd learned what it was for, the way children learn prayers — by heart, without meaning.

The sea-mages had used it to give things away. Memories, oaths, griefs — poured into the water, sealed beyond reach, paid forward so that nothing cruel could ever dig them up again. Her ancestors had perverted it, the vision in the pool had shown her: they'd used it to *sell*. To hand the isles' secrets to the king's men in exchange for their own skins. That was the family shame. That was the face with her face, walking out of the burning surf toward the royal barges.

But the rite itself was still clean. Rites usually are. It's the hands that dirty them.

"Both of you," Zephyrine said, "are going to get what you want. Just not from her."

Corvane's eyes narrowed. The rebel woman's mouth opened.

Zephyrine knelt in the shallow water beside Brine and took the girl's small hands in hers. "Listen to me," she said, and her voice only shook a little, which after everything she counted as a professional triumph. "There's a song. My mother's. You know it — you hummed it Tuesday, badly, with your mouth full. I need you to hold it with me. All of it. Can you do that?"

Brine nodded. Then, deliberately, she reached up and touched Zephyrine's cheek — the way she did when she wanted to share something — and Zephyrine felt the question arrive wordless: *What will you pay?*

Everything, Zephyrine answered, in the privacy of her own skull, where she could be brave without an audience. Everything safe.

Because that was the trick of it, the terrible elegant trick. The rite took what you offered and sealed it — made it unreachable, unfindable, *untrue* in every way that mattered. If she gave the water her memories of safety — of home, of the little rooms she'd built her life inside, of the self who had survived by belonging to Vhalcrest — then there would be nothing left to find. No trail leading to Brine through her. No name worth torturing out of anyone. The child's secret wouldn't be hidden. It would be *unmoored*, floating free, attached to no one.

Including her.

She began to sing.

* * *

It didn't hurt, which was the cruelest part. It felt like swallowing warm broth on a cold morning — comfort going down, going *out*, going somewhere that needed it more.

She gave it the kitchen in her first lodging, the one with the crooked shelf. Gave it the taste of the Queen's breakfasts, five years of mornings, the small pride of a job done precisely. Gave it Odell's laugh, the Saltmarket at dusk, the particular squeak of her third-floor stair. Gave it the feeling of her own name fitting her mouth. Gave it safety — all of it, every version, every room she had ever been unafraid in — and watched it go out of her like heat leaving tea, and kept singing, because stopping mid-rite was the one thing her mother had warned her about in terms even a child understood.

The light in the pool flared white.

The rebels cried out and covered their eyes. Corvane dropped to one knee in the water, lantern extinguished, and Zephyrine heard him say something — a prayer, maybe, or a curse, or just *oh* — and she kept singing, because the song knew the way now and she was only the road.

And then it was done, and the temple was dark except for Brine, glowing gently, holding something vast and sealed and safe inside her small chest, and Zephyrine sat down hard in the water and thought: *well. That's that,* and could not, for the life of her, remember what *that* was.

* * *

When her eyes cleared, Corvane was standing over her.

He looked down for a long moment. Water dripped from his coat. His face did something complicated — she watched it happen the way you watch weather.

"Do you know what you just did?" he asked quietly.

"No," Zephyrine said honestly. "I appear to have ruined a perfectly good floor."

He huffed — nearly a laugh. Then he crouched, so they were eye to eye, and studied her the way he'd studied her over tea, surgical and patient, looking for the lie. There wasn't one anymore. There wasn't anything anymore. She was a woman kneeling in a flooded temple next to a strange glowing child, and she had absolutely no idea why her cheeks were wet, and it wasn't the spray.

"There was a woman here a moment ago," Corvane said slowly, "who would have died before giving that child up. I watched her pay for it." He straightened. "I don't hunt ghosts, madam. And whatever you were — you're not her anymore." He turned to his men at the stair. "Nothing down here but ruins. Move out."

"And us?" the rebel woman demanded, furious, half-blinded, gripping her glowing charms.

"Are you offering to fight eight soldiers in waist-deep water over a ruin?" Corvane asked mildly. "No? How wise." He paused at the stair, lantern relit, and looked back once — at Brine, not at Zephyrine — and said, almost too low to hear:

"Keep her fed. Whatever else you are, you seem built for it."

Then he was gone, and the rebels melted after him rather than stay in the dark with the light-child and the stranger, and the temple settled back into its long patience.

Zephyrine sat in the water. Brine waded over, climbed into her lap without asking — seven-year-olds have no diplomacy whatsoever — and rested her damp head under Zephyrine's chin.

"I don't know who you are," Zephyrine told her, and was startled to find her arms already knew how to hold her. "Isn't that funny? I don't know who *I* am. But apparently I'm yours, whoever that turns out to be."

Brine patted her twice, the way you pat someone you've decided to keep.

Above them, dawn was coming up over Vhalcrest, and Zephyrine — nameless, careful-less, lighter than she'd been in five years and grieving something she couldn't name — stood up, took the child's hand, and started the long walk back to a city that no longer had any claim on either of them.

"Well," she said, because someone had to. "I suppose we should figure out breakfast."

Brine smiled, and said her first word in the common tongue, clear as a bell over the water:

"Bread."
