## 10. Oath and Salt

*Brine*

The sea remembers everything, which is why the sea is never happy.

I learned that from the pool of light in the drowned temple, before it went quiet, before the woman with my face in its depths walked out of the water carrying nothing at all. The sea keeps every voice that ever fell into it. It keeps the songs of the isles, and the screaming of the burning year, and the small sounds a girl makes when she is learning not to cry where soldiers can hear her. The sea does not forget. That was the whole of our magic once — we did not hold power, we held memory, and memory held us.

The Crown wanted the power. The rebels wanted the power. Nobody wanted the remembering, because remembering does not win wars. Remembering only makes you carry them.

So I keep it now. I am nine years old and I am the last library of the drowned isles, and most days this feels less like a burden than like holding someone's hand while they sleep.

Because that is what she did for me. And she doesn't know it anymore.

* * *

In the mornings, Zephyrine Blackwood — who is not called Zephyrine Blackwood anymore, though she doesn't know that either; she thinks Zephyrine Blackwood is simply her name, whole and unbroken, the way you think a patched coat has always been a coat — makes porridge with honey and too much salt, because salt is the one taste she has never been able to leave behind even when she cannot say why.

"You've salted it again," I tell her.

"I have not."

"You have. You always do. Your hands remember."

She looks at her hands as if they might confess something. They don't. Her hands are very loyal. "Eat your breakfast, niece."

I eat my breakfast. I am her niece. This is the story we live inside, and it fits me well enough, the way a borrowed coat fits if you stop noticing the sleeves are long. She found me, she believes, on her doorstep during a storm, mud to the ears, unable to speak anything but gibberish. She took me in because she is kind, and because — though she would never say this, though she no longer knows it — some part of her that survived the rite recognized the language coming out of my mouth the way a body recognizes its own blood.

She calls my language gibberish. She listens to it sometimes when she thinks I'm asleep, standing in my doorway with her head tilted, and there is always a moment — I watch for it — where her face goes soft and lost, like a woman hearing music from a house she lived in before she was born. Then she shakes herself and says something practical about firewood or laundry, and the moment drowns quietly, and she never asks me what the words mean.

I could tell her. I have decided not to. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Some gifts you give back only when the person can afford the price of them, and the price of her memories is a war.

* * *

Tuesdays she goes to the palace and tastes the Queen's food, because whatever else the rite took from her, it left her craft intact, and the Crown still trusts a tongue it has watched for years. She comes home in the evening smelling of court smoke and rosewater, rubbing her fingers like a woman who has handled something unpleasant, and tells me about the nobles as though reporting on weather in a country she's glad not to live in.

"The Duchess of Vairre wore pearls today," she said last Tuesday, unlacing her boots. "Pearls at breakfast. To eat eggs in."

"Perhaps she's a mermaid," I said, in the old tongue, just to see.

Zephyrine laughed. Then stopped laughing. Then stood very still in the middle of the kitchen with one boot half off, looking at nothing, for the length of four heartbeats — I counted; I count everything about her now — before saying, "Why did I almost understand that?"

"You didn't," I said quickly. "It was gibberish."

"It sounded like—" She frowned. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "It sounded like my mother's lullaby. Which is strange, because I don't remember having a mother. I mean, I remember being born, obviously, everyone remembers—"

"Nobody remembers being born, Aunt Zeph."

"Everyone remembers *something*." But her frown was already smoothing over, the way water smooths over a dropped stone, and by the time the kettle sang she was asking me whether I wanted honey, and the stone was gone.

That is how it works, the seal she made. It isn't a wall. It's a tide. Every time the truth rises in her, something gentle pulls it back out before it can become a wave. She built it herself, kneeling in the flooded temple with Corvane's soldiers at the door and Odell's rebels at the windows, and she built it out of the best things she had: her childhood, her mother's voice, the five careful years of her invented life, the knowledge of what she'd done and why. She paid with all of it so that I could stay whole. So that the last living memory of the sea-magic could walk out of that temple in one piece, inside a child nobody would think to burn.

Corvane let us go. I saw his face when the rite finished — saw him understand, in one raw instant, exactly what she had given up, and what he had nearly made her give up more of. He is not a good man. But he is a precise man, and precision sometimes looks like mercy from the outside. He told the Queen the tide-child had drowned in the undercity floods. He told the rebels' remnant that the temple was empty. He kept both lies the way he keeps all his tools: clean, oiled, ready.

And Zephyrine came home to a house she suddenly owned outright — no debts, no history, no past at all except the papers Corvane himself signed to make her respectable — and found a child on her doorstep, and took her in, and named her Brine, because (she says) the girl smelled of the sea and it seemed fitting.

The tide brought me back to her door twice. Once as a drowning girl. Once as a gift she doesn't remember giving herself.

* * *

Some nights I sing. Quietly, in the dark, the way my mother taught me — the way *her* mother taught her, back through the drowned generations, all the way to women walking into the sea with their arms open rather than let the fires take them. I sing the isles awake in my chest. I sing the names of the dead, because a name sung is a name kept, and keeping is the whole of our magic.

And sometimes, through the wall, I hear Zephyrine humming along.

She doesn't know the words. She doesn't know she knows the tune. She hums it while washing dishes, while mending, while falling asleep, and every time she catches herself doing it she stops, puzzled, and then starts again a minute later, because the tide in her runs deeper than any seal, and seals are made of memory, and memory is made of song, and song —

Song gets through.

I think that is why I don't grieve for her. Or rather, why I grieve the way the sea grieves: patiently, in tides, certain of return. She gave up her yesterdays so I could have tomorrows. But tomorrow is a long word, and underneath it, in her, the old tongue is still humming. One day — maybe when I'm grown, maybe when the Crown rots soft enough, maybe when some granddaughter of the Duchess-of-pearls-at-breakfast asks the wrong question in the right language — I will open the seal the way you open a jar of preserves: carefully, in summer, with bread ready.

Until then, I guard the story for both of us. I am the keeper now. Keeper of the isles, keeper of the names, keeper of a woman's stolen self, wrapped in oilcloth and buried in me like treasure in a garden she'll dig up someday.

The sea remembers everything. But I have decided the sea can share.

* * *

*Zephyrine*

There is a particular peace that arrives in a house around the second month of a child living in it, when the strangeness wears off and what remains is simply the shape of another person's life fitted against yours. Zephyrine distrusted it immediately.

Peace, in her experience — though her experience, she sometimes felt, was oddly shallow, like a pond that looked deep and turned out to reach only your ankles — peace was the thing that came right before the knock at the door.

But the knock didn't come. Weeks passed. Months. The Queen remained alive and unpoisoned; the Inquisitor's black carriage did not appear on her street; the charm-buyers of the Saltmarket somehow knew not to trouble her, though she couldn't recall telling anyone she'd retired. Even the dreams had gone quiet. For years — she was sure of this, though surety itself felt like a borrowed coat — she had dreamed of water. Now she dreamed of nothing much. Porridge, occasionally. A door opening onto sunlight.

"Your broth is too salty," Brine announced one evening, pushing the bowl an inch away with the gravity of a magistrate.

"It is seasoned correctly."

"It is a small ocean."

"If you don't want it, more for me."

Brine pulled the bowl back. This was their dance, and Zephyrine loved it in a way that frightened her slightly, the way you love a thing you're certain will be taken. The girl ate like a seabird, quick and neat, and watched everything, and spoke softly in two tongues — the common tongue of Vhalcrest, learned in a season with frightening ease, and that other language, the flowing one, the one that made Zephyrine's chest ache in a place she couldn't point to on any map of herself.

"Gibberish," people called it. Zephyrine had started calling it that too, mostly to remind herself. Because the alternative was admitting that when Brine murmured it over the mending, Zephyrine's hands sometimes moved to help without being asked, stitching patterns she had no memory of learning.

Tonight the girl said something in it over the supper dishes — a short phrase, almost casual — and Zephyrine's mouth answered before her mind caught up.

"*Ael na shuir,*" she said. Then froze, dish towel dripping. "*What did I just say?*"

Brine looked at her for a long moment. There was something in the girl's face then that Zephyrine had seen before and never understood — a patience too old for nine years old, a tenderness with grief folded inside it like salt inside bread.

"You said 'the water is kind,'" Brine translated. "It's a greeting. From the old country."

"What old country?"

"The drowned isles." Brine dried a plate, set it down with a small precise click. "You knew that once, I think. Maybe you'll know it again someday. Things come back, Aunt Zeph. Tides especially."

"That's nonsense," Zephyrine said, but gently, because the girl said strange things sometimes and meant them kindly, the way other children said prayers. "I've never been near the sea in my life."

Brine smiled — that same patient, grieving, hopeful smile — and handed her the last dish.

"No," she agreed. "Of course not. But you hum when you wash up. Did you know that? You hum a song from the drowned isles. Somebody must have loved you enough to teach it to you."

Outside, far off, faint beneath the evening bells, Zephyrine thought she heard the sound of waves — impossible, this far inland — and found, to her considerable irritation, that she was humming along.
