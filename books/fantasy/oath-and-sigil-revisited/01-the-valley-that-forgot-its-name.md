## 1. The Valley That Forgot Its Name

The dead of Hollowmere began their complaints at dawn, as they always did, and Xanthe Oyelaran listened to them the way other men listened to rain against a roof—patiently, wearily, with his hands already moving at their work.

He sat in his workshop above the tannery row, where the morning fog came up off the mere like breath from a sleeping mouth, and he carved. The bone was sheep's femur today, cheap stock for cheap charms: little rounded tokens to tuck into coffin linings so the grieving could pretend their mothers had gone somewhere gentler than the cold ground. He worked a spiral into the surface with a burin older than he was, worn smooth by three generations of Oyelaran hands before his own.

*You never finished my fence,* said old Marta Cull, who had been dead eleven years and had complained about that fence every morning since. *The north rail still leans. My son leans it back up but he does it wrong, he does it wrong on purpose, I think, just to spite—*

"I'll speak to him," Xanthe murmured, not looking up. "Again."

*I know you won't. Nobody speaks to anyone in this valley anymore. That's half of why I stay.*

That was not true, exactly. Hollowmere spoke plenty. It spoke in the market square on trading days, in the chapel of the Ninefold Path where the priest droned through rites no one believed in anymore, in the two taverns whose arguments were older than the curse itself. But Marta had been dead too long to remember how living voices worked, or perhaps she simply preferred his. The dead always preferred his. That was the trouble.

Xanthe set down the burin and flexed his fingers. Through the workshop's one window, past the fog, he could see the valley walls rising on every side—the Rim, people called it, though no one living remembered who had named it that. Grey stone folded over grey stone, crowned with pines that never quite greened, and threaded through all of it, invisible unless you knew to look, ran the wards. Old magic. Dead-king magic. A net woven across the passes by Aldric the Last before his death, spun from his own dying breath, sealing Hollowmere away from the world like a hand closing over something precious—or like a lid nailed down over something the world wanted buried.

No one crossed the Rim. No letters came in; none went out. Traders had stopped coming before Xanthe's grandfather's time, and the maps—the few that survived, hidden in chapel lofts and grandmother's chests—showed the valley as blank parchment, a smudge, an absence shaped like home. The crown had struck Hollowmere from its records a century ago. Officially, the valley did not exist. Unofficially, it went on existing anyway, stubbornly, the way the dead did, because existence had never much cared what kings decreed.

*She's coming,* Marta said suddenly, her voice sharpening the way it did when she forgot herself and felt something like urgency. *The new one. She's crossing the mere now. You'll want to be kind to this one, carver. She's frightened.*

"They're all frightened," Xanthe said. "It's the dying."

*It's more than the dying,* Marta said, and then, in the peculiar way the dead had of leaving mid-thought, she was gone—not silenced, just elsewhere, drawn off toward whatever the dead did when they weren't haunting him. He sometimes imagined them gathering in the fog over the mere like herons, standing in the shallows of some country only they could see.

He didn't have long to wonder. Boots on the stair, uneven ones—a neighbor's gait—and then Tamsin Roke filled his doorway, wide-shouldered and grey-faced, wringing her apron into a rope.

"It's Wren," she said. "My sister. It's time, carver. The midwife's sent for you and she's sent for the priest and neither of them can do anything, so." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she pressed the apron rope against her mouth as if to hold herself together with it.

Wren Roke was thirty-one years old and had been coughing blood since midwinter, and everyone in Hollowmere had known for months what the coughing meant, and no one had said it aloud, because in a valley that officially did not exist, even death was a thing you agreed not to notice.

"Bring me my kit," Xanthe said gently, and rose.

* * *

The Roke cottage smelled of tallow and fever-sweat and, beneath it, the particular sweetness of a body preparing to leave. Wren lay propped on pillows by the window, her skin the color of candle-tallow gone soft, her breathing a thin whistle through fluid. The midwife hovered; the priest had come and gone, having mumbled his rites into air that didn't care. And around the bed, thick as flies in summer, stood the dead.

They were always there at deathbeds, drawn like moths. Xanthe saw them plainly—had seen them plainly since he was six years old, since the day his mother died and turned around in the doorway of the world and looked back at him instead of walking on. He had learned very young that this was not a gift you mentioned. In Hollowmere, where the curse kept all magic sealed behind the Rim, a boy who heard the dead was either a liar or a problem, and problems, in small places, got solved quietly. So he had learned to look at ghosts the way he looked at everyone: slightly past them, slightly bored, a man doing ordinary grief-work in an ordinary dying valley.

There must have been twenty of them in the Roke cottage. Grandmothers and drowned boys and one ancient shepherd whose name even he had forgotten, crowding close to watch the seam open between Wren and whatever came next.

"Wren," Xanthe said, kneeling by the bed. "Can you hear me?"

Her eyes found him. They were bright, too bright, the fever burning everything else away. "Carver," she whispered. "I want—" A cough took her, wet and terrible. "I want a charm. For after. So they know I'm still—so they know I didn't just stop."

"You didn't stop," Xanthe said quietly. "Nothing stops. It only changes rooms."

Behind him, the dead murmured agreement, and he wished, as he always wished, that the living could hear them say it. It would save so much fear. It would save him so much loneliness.

He carved while she watched, working fast, a spiral and a wren in flight, into a sliver of yew wood because she'd asked for wood, not bone—"bone is for the ones already under"—and when he pressed it into her palm her fingers closed around it with surprising strength.

"There," he said. "When you go, hold onto this. It's yours. It says you were here."

Wren smiled, and it transformed her face, made her briefly the girl she must have been before the coughing. "You're a strange man, Xanthe Oyelaran," she breathed. "Everyone says you're strange. But you're the only one who talks to us like we're going somewhere instead of nowhere."

If only she knew, he thought. If only any of them knew.

The afternoon wore down toward evening. The midwife dozed in her chair. Tamsin came and went with broth no one ate. And Wren drifted, surfacing and sinking, until near dusk she surfaced one last time and gripped Xanthe's wrist with both hands, and her eyes were not fever-bright now but clear, terribly clear, and focused on something over his shoulder.

"She's here," Wren said. "Oh—she's *here*, she's beautiful, she's—"

And then the ghost arrived.

Xanthe felt her before he saw her, the way you feel a change in weather. This was no village dead, no faded grandmother. This presence came into the room like cold water pouring downhill, vast and urgent and *new*—dying even as she entered it, still attached by a thread to the body on the bed, and therefore carrying with her the raw terrible clarity of someone who has just understood everything and has perhaps a minute to say it.

She was a woman he didn't know, tall, dressed in traveling clothes gone dark with water. Drowned, then, or drowned-adjacent. Her eyes were the color of the mere in November.

"Carver," she said, and her voice went through him like a nail through board. "*Bone-carver.* You hear us. I can see that you hear us. Listen to me—I don't have long, even for one of us."

Xanthe kept his face still. Twenty years of practice. "Grief makes shapes," he said aloud, for the benefit of the midwife, who stirred. "It's the fever talking."

"*Listen to me.*" The ghost knelt—ghosts didn't kneel, they had no knees, and yet she knelt—and seized his sleeve, and her grip was cold in a way cold had never been before. "The wardline is failing. Do you understand? The king's net is rotting, thread by thread, and when it breaks, *they will come*. The hunters. The ones with the sigil. They are already asking after the fire."

"The fire," Xanthe repeated flatly.

"The little fire." Her grip tightened. "The last flame of the old kingdom. It sleeps in this valley, carver, it has slept here since Aldric fell, and if the hunters reach it before it wakes—if it gutters out alone in the dark—then everything dies. Not just the magic. Everything. The Door seals. There will be no way out of this country, ever again, for anyone." Her voice was fraying now, unraveling like rope-end. "Warn the little fire. Find it. Keep it burning until—"

"Who are you?" Xanthe whispered. "What fire? What door?"

But she was thinning, stretched between the bed and the beyond, and the last of her poured backward out of the room like smoke reversing, and the final thing she left behind was not words but a sound—a single note, high and sweet and unmistakably the cry of something young and winged.

On the bed, Wren Roke exhaled, long and slow, and did not inhale again.

The room filled with the ordinary sounds of death: Tamsin's cry, the midwife's clucking, the priest being fetched. Xanthe knelt with his head bowed, hands folded, wearing the face everyone expected of him—the calm face, the strange gentle carver who did his work well—and inside, beneath the stillness, one thought turned over and over like a stone in a stream:

*Grief-noise. Dying women see angels and omens. It means nothing. It means nothing.*

But the dead did not lie. That was the one true thing about them, the thing he had built his whole quiet life upon: the dead could exaggerate, mourn, rage, and forget, but they could not lie. And that woman had not been a grief-shape. She had been real, and terrified, and she had known his name.

* * *

He walked home late, past the mere, where the fog had thickened into something almost solid. The valley lay silent around him, two hundred hearths banked for the night, the Rim a blacker black against the stars. Somewhere up there, invisible, the wards hummed their old dead-king hum, the same note they had held for a hundred years, the sound Xanthe had heard every night of his life and stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

Tonight, he noticed it.

Tonight, he stood at the edge of the water and listened, properly listened, the way he listened to the dead—with his whole attention, holding nothing back—and beneath the familiar hum he heard it: a wrongness. A thread pulled loose. A note gone flat where no note had ever gone flat before.

*The wardline is failing.*

His breath smoked in the cold. Behind him, the valley slept, officially nonexistent, safely sealed, and the dead crowded the fog around him in their hundreds—Marta and the shepherd and Wren Roke, new and bewildered, and the drowned woman already gone wherever the newly dead go—and for the first time in his life, Xanthe Oyelaran felt the full weight of what he was: the only living soul in a valley of the living who belonged entirely to the dead, and the only one who would hear it, when the silence finally broke, coming down through the passes.

From the direction of his own street, faint but unmistakable, came a sound he had never heard in Hollowmere before.

Hoofbeats. Many of them. Moving fast.
