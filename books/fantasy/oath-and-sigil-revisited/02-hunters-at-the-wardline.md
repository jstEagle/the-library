## 2. Hunters at the Wardline

The wards broke on a Tuesday, though no one in Hollowmere called it that anymore. They called it the day the birds went quiet.

Xanthe felt it first as a wrongness in his teeth — a high, thin pressure, like biting tinfoil, like standing too close to a struck bell. He was at his workbench, chiseling a prayer into a sheep's knucklebone for old Marta Tull's grave-goods, when every charm hanging from the rafters of his workshop began to sway at once. There was no wind. The shutters were closed against the autumn damp. And yet the bones turned together, slow and deliberate, all their carved faces swinging toward the door.

"Ah," said the dead woman who had been sitting in the corner since dawn.

Her name had been Yewna; she'd died forty years before Xanthe was born, and she haunted his workshop the way dust haunted it, patiently, without malice. She was watching the bones too, and her translucent face wore an expression he had never seen on her: fear.

"That'll be the wardline," she said. "Going down."

Xanthe set down his chisel. His hands were steady, because his hands were always steady — a carver's hands, trained to stillness over living bone — but beneath the steadiness his heart had begun to run like a rabbit through wet grass. "Wardlines don't go down," he said. "They've held for three hundred years. The king's curse—"

"The king is dead," Yewna said gently. "Dead men's promises rot same as dead men."

He wanted to argue. He wanted very badly to argue, because arguing with ghosts was the one conversation he was good at, and because the alternative to arguing was admitting what the swaying bones already knew. Instead he crossed the workshop and opened the shutters.

Hollowmere lay below him in its bowl of hills, terraced fields gone gold and grey, the slate roofs of the town steaming in the morning cold. The river Mere ran silver through the middle of everything, unhurried, indifferent. It looked exactly as it had looked every morning of his thirty-one years. But the birds — the starlings that usually wheeled over the millpond in their thousands, the crows that argued along the churchyard wall — were gone. Not scattered. *Gone*, as if the sky itself had been swept clean, and in the terrible new silence Xanthe could hear something he had never heard in Hollowmere before.

Hooves. Many hooves, on the far side of the western ridge.

"They're coming down the shepherd's road," Yewna said. "Twelve horses. Iron shoes." She drifted to the window beside him, and where her shoulder passed, the frost on the glass melted into a handprint shape. "You should have listened to the dying one, bone-carver. The little fire. I told you she was true-grieving, not grief-noise."

Xanthe did not answer. He was listening — really listening, opening the door in himself that he kept locked and bolted and disguised as ordinary mourning-work — and the valley's dead were rising around him like sediment stirred in water. From the barrow-field: unease. From the drowned mill: a low, continuous wail. From under the church floor, from the plague-pits, from the cairns on the high fells, three centuries of Hollowmere's dead were turning toward the west ridge with one collective attention, the way a sleeping body turns toward a sound.

*They're afraid,* he realized, and the realization dropped through him like a stone through deep water. *The dead are afraid.*

The dead were never afraid. That was the whole consolation of them, the secret kindness of his strange gift: whatever else happened, whatever grief the living carried, the dead themselves were finished, settled, past harm. Xanthe had built his entire quiet life on that foundation. He carved their charms, heard their small complaints about unfinished business — the widow's loom not finished, the debt unpaid, the apology never spoken — and he told himself it was grief-work, honest work, nothing more. A man could live a whole life among the dead if the dead were safe.

The hoofbeats came over the ridge.

* * *

They rode into the market square just after noon, twelve riders in crown-black, and by then half of Hollowmere had gathered to watch, because the wards had been failing for years and everyone knew it the way you know a roof leaks — theoretically, comfortably, until the rain comes through.

Xanthe stood at the edge of the crowd with his hood up and his carving satchel against his chest, and he watched the witch-hunters the way prey watches predators: without moving, without blinking, cataloguing.

They were not the ragged bounty-men who occasionally climbed the pass to chase a debtor. They were crown soldiers, drilled and silent, in lacquered black plate chased with silver filigree. Their horses were tall southern destriers, shod in iron that rang against the cobbles with a note that made Xanthe's fillings ache and made three elderly ghosts near the well flinch and scatter. At the column's head rode a woman in a white cloak — a purifier's cloak, bone-white, the only spot of color being the warrant seal stitched at her breast.

And there it was. Even at fifty paces, even half-hidden by the fold of her cloak, Xanthe saw it, and his mouth went dry as grave-dust.

A crowned stag, skull-crowned, nine-tined, standing over a broken sword.

The sigil of Aldric the Last, the Dead King, the Mourner-King — the man whose dying curse had sealed Hollowmere behind its walls of silence for three hundred years. The sigil that appeared on no living heraldry, that had been struck from every record, that existed now only in the carvings Xanthe made for graves and in the secret hearts of people who still left offerings at the barrow-field.

*On a crown warrant.* Stitched in silver thread, official, sanctioned. Which meant the crown knew. The crown had always known. The hunt for the old magic wasn't a rumor from the south, wasn't a fever of some distant king's paranoia. It was aimed here, at the valley, at the last ember of the kingdom they had erased — and someone in a palace a thousand miles away had signed their names to its extinction.

Beside him, an old man — Tam Wicker, the cooper — whispered what everyone was whispering: "Who've they come for? We've no witches in Hollowmere. Never had. The curse keeps magic out, doesn't it? Keeps everything out."

Xanthe said nothing. Because that had been the story, hadn't it? The comfortable story, the story he had wrapped around himself like a blanket for thirty-one years: the curse keeps magic out. Nothing crosses the wardline. Whatever burns in the world beyond, Hollowmere sleeps behind its wall of the dead king's grief, safe, forgotten, unimportant.

But the wards had failed. And the sigil on that warrant said the failure was not an accident.

The hunters dismounted in formation, precise as clockwork. The white-cloaked officer walked to the wellhead, unrolled a scroll, and read out in a carrying voice:

"In the name of the Crown Regent and the Purified Realm: Maren Ostwick, self-declared hedge-witch of this parish, stands accused of consorting with residual spirits, trafficking in unsanctioned charms, and harboring vestiges of the Pretender Kingdom's sorcery. Sentence is passed. Sentence will be executed."

A murmur went through the crowd. Old Maren Ostwick — everyone knew Maren. She lived up past the alder-carr in a house leaning like a tired grandmother, sold cough-salves and love-charms that were mostly honey and suggestion, buried people's dead cats with little ceremonies. Half the town owed her a favor. Half the town had drunk her elderflower tea.

"She's no witch," Tam Wicker said loudly, and there was a rumble of agreement, and for one moment Xanthe thought — foolishly, desperately — that numbers might matter, that a whole square full of neighbors might be a wall of its own.

Then four hunters walked to Maren's cottage, kicked in the door, and dragged her out by her arms.

She was small and grey-haired and she did not struggle, and that was somehow worse. She looked out over the faces of her neighbors with an expression not of terror but of terrible recognition, as if she had known this day was coming for years and had only hoped to be elsewhere for it. Her eyes found Xanthe's across the crowd — found them, held them, and Xanthe understood with a lurch that she knew what he was, had perhaps always known, the way hedge-witches know things — and her lips moved.

No sound reached him. But the dead carried it, the way they carried everything to him, whether he wanted it or not: *Run, bone-carver,* Maren's voice said inside his skull, calm as a woman telling a child supper's ready. *Don't be stubborn like me. Find the little fire. Go while the going—*

They burned her in the square.

Not at a stake — the crown had grown more efficient than that, or more obscene. They used a brazier, a portable iron thing hauled off one of the packhorses, and they lit it with a fire that burned the wrong color, a flat orange with a core of white, and Xanthe's stomach turned over because he knew that fire. Every carver of grave-goods knew that fire. It was corpse-fire, rendered from rendered things, fire with no life in it at all, fire that ate magic the way lye ate flesh.

Maren did not scream. She recited, actually, in a clear cracked voice, some old rhyme in the valley dialect that half the crowd would have known from their grandmothers' knees — and the hunters let her finish it, which was either mercy or ritual, and Xanthe did not want to know which.

When it was done, the white-cloaked officer turned to the assembled townsfolk and said, "Any household found harboring charms, relics, or persons touched by the Pretender's sorcery will share her sentence. This valley is hereby under crown audit. Cooperate, and be forgotten. Resist, and be remembered wrongly."

*Be forgotten.* The words went through the crowd like a blade through wet bread. In Hollowmere, forgetting was not an abstraction. The whole valley lived in the shadow of a king whom history had already unwritten; they knew what it was to be struck from maps. And now the crown was promising it to each of them individually, a gift and a threat braided into one.

The hunters began going door to door.

* * *

Xanthe made it back to his workshop above the tannery lane before his legs gave out. He sat down hard on his stool amid the swaying bones and put his head in his hands, and the dead crowded close around him, closer than they had ever dared, dozens of them, pressing in like mourners at a bedside.

Yewna hovered at his shoulder. "You knew," she said. "Last night. The dying woman, the warning. You thought it was grief-noise."

"I thought—" His voice came out in pieces. "I thought the wards would hold. I thought we were hidden. I thought hiding was the same as safe."

"Hiding was never safe," Yewna said. "Hiding was just dying slowly instead of quickly. You knew that. You carve gravestones for a living, boy."

He laughed — a horrible, cracked sound — and then stopped, because through the open window, drifting up from the market square, he could hear the hunters moving down the street below. Door to door. Methodical. The iron ring of a knocker. Voices, polite and lethal. And underneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the smell of that white-hearted fire beginning to spread through the lanes like fog.

His eyes fell on the workbench, on the half-carved sheep's knucklebone, on the drawer beneath it where he kept the commissions he never spoke of aloud — the stag-and-broken-sword pieces, the ones certain families asked for in whispers, the ones that would hang him twice over if a hunter's glove so much as brushed that wood.

The little fire, the ghost had begged him. Warn the little fire.

He had dismissed it. He had buried it under routine and denial and the comfortable lie of the wardline. And now the wardline was ash, Maren Ostwick was ash, and somewhere in this valley — he was suddenly, urgently certain — something small was burning down to its last ember, waiting for a warning that had come too late.

Or had it?

Below, boots on the stairs. Heavy, iron-shod, climbing toward his door.

Xanthe snuffed his lamp, seized his satchel of carving tools, and looked around the workshop that had been his whole world, knowing he would likely never see it again.

Three sharp knocks landed on the door.

"Bone-carver," said a voice, pleasant as a knife being sharpened. "Open in the name of the audit."

And from the cellar beneath his feet — faint, muffled, impossible — Xanthe heard a sound he had never heard in his life and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize your own heartbeat.

Something small, and frightened, and alive, was breathing fire.
