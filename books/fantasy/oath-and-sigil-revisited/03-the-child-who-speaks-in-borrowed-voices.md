## 3. The Child Who Speaks in Borrowed Voices

The rain had followed Sera for three days, and she had begun to suspect it was not weather but pursuit.

She crouched in the treeline above Hollowmere's lower road, Odji's small hand locked in hers, and watched the bone-carver's workshop glow amber through the shuttered windows below. Smoke curled from the chimney in a thin gray thread. Somewhere inside that house was a man who carved charms for the dead and pretended he could not hear them. Sera knew the type. She had been the type herself, once — a woman who did her work well and asked no questions, until the night the work became a child tied to a stake and her hands simply refused.

"Almost there," she murmured. "Do you understand me? Almost."

Odji looked up at her with eyes too old for a face of perhaps eight years, and answered in a voice that was not the child's own — dry, cracked, an old man's voice scraped raw by decades of pipe smoke.

*"The river remembers what the bridge forgets."*

Sera's jaw tightened. She had learned, in the weeks since she'd pulled the child from the witch-fires at Duskwatch, to stop flinching when the dead spoke through that small mouth. She had not learned to stop grieving it. Whatever voice Odji had been born with was gone now, buried under the chorus of everyone who had ever died within earshot. The child spoke only in borrowed tongues, and each one was a small funeral.

"Not helpful," she said softly. "But thank you anyway."

She moved. Low, fast, the way Veyra had trained her — and hated herself for the thought, because Veyra's training lived in her body like a splinter too deep to draw. Down through the wet pines, along the drainage ditch, across the road where the mud took their footprints and gave them back to the rain. The workshop door was oak, iron-banded, carved with small running figures she didn't have time to study. She knocked the way deserters knock: three quick raps, then silence, then two more, praying the man inside was paranoid enough to recognize the rhythm as *answer or I break this door down*.

Bolt. Footsteps. A sliver of lamplight.

Xanthe Oyelaran opened the door no wider than his own forearm. He was younger than she expected, sharp-featured, with ink-stained fingers and the hollowed look of a man who slept badly among things that talked. His eyes went from her face to her sword to the child half-hidden behind her cloak, and something in them sharpened rather than dulled.

"Huntress," he said. He said it the way other men say *plague*.

"Deserter," she corrected. "There's a difference. May I come in before your valley's wards finish dying and my former captain finishes me?"

He didn't move. Rain ran off Sera's hood and pooled at the threshold, and behind him she could see a workshop full of small bones and fine tools and shelves of charms strung on red thread, hundreds of them, each one labeled in a careful hand. A man who kept meticulous records of the dead. A man who would be very easy to track, and seemed to know it.

"You're being followed," Xanthe said.

"By Captain Veyra Solenne and six crown hunters. They crossed the wardline yesterday. Your valley's protections are failing — I felt them go like teeth loosening in a gum." She lifted her chin. "I refused to burn a child. That's my whole crime. This one—" she drew Odji forward, into the light, "—is the child I wouldn't burn. And they want both of us ash before the week is out."

For a long moment the only sound was rain and the tick of cooling iron in the forge behind him. Then Xanthe stepped aside.

"In," he said. "Quickly. And whatever you do, don't touch the bones on the third shelf. They're listening."

* * *

Inside, the workshop smelled of tallow smoke and juniper and something older underneath — dust, and the particular sweetness of long-kept bone. Sera positioned herself with her back to the wall out of habit, sightline to the door, sword loose in its scabbard. Xanthe noticed, and said nothing, which she appreciated more than any welcome.

Odji stood in the middle of the room and turned slowly, taking it in: the hanging charms, the carving bench, the little brass scale for weighing offerings. The child's face held no wonder. It held recognition.

Then Odji stopped before the hearth, where a woman's charm hung on its own nail apart from all the others — worn smooth, lovingly handled, threaded on faded blue cord instead of red — and spoke.

Not in an old man's voice this time. In a woman's voice. Warm, tired, faintly amused, with the particular lilt of the southern provinces where the accent rounds every word like a stone in a stream.

*"You've let the fire get low again, love. You always let the fire get low when you're sad."*

Xanthe went white.

He crossed the room in three strides and caught Odji by the shoulders — gently, Sera noted, so gently, even in shock — and the child looked up at him with those ancient eyes and the woman's voice came again, patient as tide:

*"Don't grab. You know I hate being grabbed. Wipe your feet, boy, you're tracking the whole world in with you."*

"Stop," Xanthe whispered. "Stop — who — whose voice—"

*"Whose do you think, my heart? Whose voice would stand in your kitchen and tell you the fire's low?"* A pause, and when it came again there was something rawer under the warmth. *"You carve such beautiful things for strangers. When did you last carve anything for me? I waited. I'm still waiting. Not for the carving. For you to say my name out loud instead of hiding it in the woodwork like contraband."*

Sera watched the man come apart quietly, the way soldiers do — no noise, just structure failing. He sank onto the bench, and Odji reached up and touched his cheek with one small hand, and the voice softened into something almost unbearable.

*"There. There it is. Grief looks the same on everyone, did you know that? Like rain on a window. Now listen to me, boy, because I only have her throat a moment longer: the little fire is real. The warning was real. Get them out. All of them. And eat something, you're skin and secrets."*

The voice left like a candle pinched out. Odji swayed; Sera caught the child before either of them registered the movement, and lowered her to the bench beside Xanthe, who sat staring at the hearth-charm as though it might speak directly if he only believed hard enough.

"That was," he began, and stopped. Swallowed. Tried again. "My mother died eleven years ago. I made her charm myself. I never — I put her name on the back where no one would read it. I thought I was protecting her." His laugh cracked in the middle. "I was hiding her. From whom? Everyone here is dead or will be."

"The crown keeps lists," Sera said quietly. "Even of the dead. Especially of the dead, if they were gifted." She unclasped her soaked cloak and hung it by the fire, and after a moment's hesitation, fed two splits of juniper to the low flames. *You always let the fire get low.* "Her name is safe on the back of that charm. For what it's worth."

"It isn't worth anything if I'm the only one who says it."

"No," Sera agreed. "It isn't. But it's more than nothing. I've seen nothing. Nothing is a pyre in a market square."

That landed between them and stayed there. Xanthe looked at her differently after it — not as a huntress anymore, or not only. As someone who had also stood in front of a fire and made the wrong choice according to her orders and the only right one according to anything else.

"Why did you bring her here?" he asked finally. "To me. You didn't know me. You knew what I was — the whole valley knows the bone-carver hears things — and you brought the most hunted child in the province to my door."

"Because you're the only man in Hollowmere who talks to the dead like they're customers instead of vermin," Sera said. "And because the child led me here. Three nights ago Odji started walking in her sleep, straight-line, compass-true, saying—" she glanced down, "—say it."

Odji looked up. An old woman's voice this time, papery, fond:

*"Follow the smell of juniper and bone. He'll grumble but he'll feed you."*

Xanthe closed his eyes. "The dead are gossiping about me."

"They're vouching for you," Sera said. "There's a difference, and given what's coming up that road behind us, I'd take the endorsement."

As if the words had summoned it, Bramble — though they did not yet know her name, only the heat — shifted somewhere beneath their feet. A faint warmth rose through the floorboards from the cellar, brief as a heartbeat, and Odji's head snapped toward it with an expression Sera had never seen on the child's face: not borrowed grief, not borrowed age, but something purely, startlingly young. Delight.

"She's still here," Odji said — and for one impossible moment it was Odji's own voice, small and clear and alive, before the dead flooded back in and the moment drowned. *"Down. Down. The little fire. She's frightened and her light is going out and if her light goes out—"*

The voices tangled, three or four at once, old men and drowned women and one that sounded disturbingly like a king, all speaking through one small mouth until the child shook and Sera knelt and wrapped her arms around her and Xanthe pressed his palms over his ears as though pressure could keep the dead out.

"—if her light goes out, the Door seals forever, and none of us ever leaves," Odji finished, in a single voice at last: quiet, level, and utterly their own.

Silence. Rain on the roof. The fire ticking.

Xanthe rose, crossed to the cellar door, and stood with his hand on the latch for a long moment. When he turned back, his face had rearranged itself into something Sera recognized from her own mirror on the morning she deserted: terror, worn like armor, over a decision already made.

"There's bread and dried plums in the crock," he said. "Eat. Both of you. Then we're going down to the cellar, because apparently I've been harboring the last fire of the old kingdom under my floor without knowing it, and I'd like to meet my co-conspirator."

From below, faint as a banked ember breathing, something warm stirred against the underside of the boards.

And far up the valley road, where the rain fell hardest, torchlight was beginning to move.
