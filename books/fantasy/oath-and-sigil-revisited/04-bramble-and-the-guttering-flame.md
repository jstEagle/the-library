## 4. Bramble and the Guttering Flame

The ghost's words would not leave him.

*Warn the little fire,* the dying woman had said, her voice thin as smoke through Xanthe's skull, and for two days he had told himself it was grief-noise, the ordinary static of a soul not yet settled. But grief-noise did not repeat itself. Grief-noise did not *insist*, the way she had insisted, with the terrible patience of the dead who know their time is short and their message shorter.

She had died in the market square, one of the first taken when the hunters came through the wardline. He had held her hand while the light went out of it, and her ghost had stayed behind, hovering over her own body like a woman who has forgotten something in the house and cannot remember what.

"Warn the little fire," she said again now, standing at the foot of his cellar stairs, translucent as breath on glass. "Before it goes out. Before they find it."

Xanthe set down his carving knife. The half-finished charm on his bench — a knucklebone of a sheep, whittled into the shape of a sleeping bird — seemed suddenly very small against the weight of what she was asking.

"I don't know any little fire," he said.

"You do." She smiled, and there was something in the smile that was almost pity. "You've always known. You just didn't know you knew."

He thought of his mother then, dead eleven years, and of how she used to say that the Oyelaran gift skipped no generations, only hid, like embers under ash. He had spent his whole life telling himself he was ash.

The ghost drifted toward the cellar door. She could not open it — the dead touch nothing — but she could stand before it the way a signpost stands before a road, and Xanthe understood that this was all the warning he was going to get.

Behind him, Sera shifted by the window, crossbow across her knees, watching the empty street through a gap in the shutters. Odji sat on the floor drawing in spilled bone-dust, small fingers tracing shapes that were not quite letters. The child had not spoken in their own voice since arriving. Every word out of that small mouth belonged to someone else — a drowned fisherman, a grandmother, once, unbearably, Xanthe's own mother, saying *he never listens, my Xanthe, he never listens until it is too late.*

"All right," Xanthe said to the ghost, though it was really to himself. "All right."

He opened the cellar door.

* * *

The stairs down into his own cellar had never felt long before. Twenty steps, worn hollow in the middle by forty years of his father's boots and twenty more of his own. Tonight they felt like a descent into somewhere older than his house, older than Hollowmere, older than the curse that kept magic from crossing the valley's border.

The lamp guttered as he reached the bottom. Not from draft. The air below was still as held breath.

And there, in the far corner, among the stacked blanks of cow femur and the jars of tallow, something breathed back.

It was smaller than he expected. Smaller than fear had made it in his imagination through two sleepless nights. A drake no bigger than a hound, curled beneath his father's old work apron like a child hiding under blankets, its scales the dull grey of cooling charcoal. It had wedged itself into the space between the foundation stone and the wall, where the cold came up through the earth, and it was shivering.

Its eyes opened when the lamplight touched it. They were the color of banked coals — orange going gold at the center, but dim, so dim, like a hearth three days after the last log.

"Oh," Xanthe said softly. All the words he had prepared — the reassurances, the careful lies about how it must have wandered in, how everything would be fine — dissolved. "Oh, little one."

The drake flinched from the light. Then, slowly, it uncurled enough to reveal what it had been protecting: the curve of its own belly, where a faint glow pulsed beneath the scales like a lantern behind cloth. The fire. Not fire in the way of torches or hearths, but the other kind — the living flame, the royal fire, the thing the old kings of Hollowmere had carried in their blood and passed down like an heirloom no vault could hold.

The last of it. Here, in his cellar, under his father's apron.

"You're what she meant," Xanthe whispered. "The little fire."

The drake made a sound. Not a growl, not quite a chirp — something between, cracked and small, the sound of a creature too young for the weight it carried. And when it opened its mouth, a thread of flame licked out, thin as candle-wick, and Xanthe saw the truth that made his chest go tight: the flame was *guttering*. Flickering at the edges. Going out.

He crouched down, slow, the way he approached frightened animals and freshly grieving widows — the same gentleness served both. "I'm not going to hurt you. No one here will hurt you. My name is—"

He stopped. Some instinct, some bone-deep caution, kept the name behind his teeth. Names had power. Names could be hunted.

The drake watched him with those coal-dark eyes, and then, impossibly, it made a decision. It shuffled forward on legs too big for its body, dragging the apron with it, and pressed its hot forehead against his knee.

The heat of it went through his trousers, through his skin, into the bone. Warmth like a wordless plea. *Help me. Help me help me help me.*

"I don't know how," Xanthe said honestly. His hands hovered over the dimming glow of its belly, not touching. "I carve bones for the dead. I listen to ghosts complain about unfinished business. I don't know anything about dragons."

But even as he said it, he felt it — the pull, the same pull he felt toward every restless spirit, except this one was alive, and alive things were not supposed to need him. Alive things were supposed to be able to save themselves.

The stairs creaked. Sera stood at the bottom, crossbow lowered, her face doing something complicated.

"Gods above," she breathed. "That's a *drake*. That's—" She stopped herself, glancing at the ceiling as if the hunters might be listening through the floorboards. "That's what they're hunting. That's what the warrant was for. Not hedge-witches. Not gifted children. *That.*"

"The last royal fire," Xanthe said. "The king's dying curse sealed the valley to keep this safe. And now the wards are failing, and the hunters are inside, and this—" his voice caught, "—this is what's left."

Sera came closer, slow, and knelt beside him. Up close, the drake's fire-glow lit her scarred face gold, and for a moment she looked younger than her years and older than them both.

"It's dying," she said quietly. "Look at the edges of the flame. I've seen fires die before. Campfires, signal fires. They flicker like that right before the dark takes them."

"And when it goes out?"

Sera was silent a moment. When she answered, her voice was flat with the particular dread of a soldier who has read orders she wishes she hadn't.

"There's a story we were told in training. Propaganda, mostly, but propaganda is built on true bones. About a Door. The Door of the Unwritten. A passage out of places that officially don't exist. They taught us it opens for the old blood, the old fire — and that it dies with the last of it. Seal forever. No way out. Ever. For anyone."

Xanthe looked down at the small creature trembling against his knee, at the guttering lantern of its belly, at the vast and terrible machinery of crown and curse and hunt that had somehow converged on his cellar, on his quiet life of bone-charms and ghost-complaints.

"So if her fire goes out," he said slowly, "she dies. And the Door dies. And everyone left in Hollowmere dies with it, slowly, on a schedule the crown finds convenient."

"Yes."

"And I'm supposed to keep a dragon's heart burning." He laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "I can't even keep my own hearth lit. Ask my neighbors. Ask the ghosts. Half of them have opinions about my housekeeping."

The drake chirped. It sounded, absurdly, like agreement.

Odji appeared at Sera's shoulder then, silent as ever in their own body, and looked down at the drake with those ancient, borrowed eyes. The child's mouth opened, and a voice came out that was neither Odji's nor any voice Xanthe recognized — older, deeper, resonant as a bell struck underground.

"*Little sister,*" the voice said, and the drake's head came up sharply, fire flickering brighter for one impossible heartbeat. "*Hold. Hold fast. The carver hears us. The carver will carry you.*"

The voice faded. Odji blinked, returned to themselves, and looked at Xanthe with an expression no child should have to wear: apology.

"That wasn't me," Odji said, in the flat tone they used for such disclaimers. "That was the mountain. Or what's left of it. The old king's fire-kin. They're... waiting. Under everything. Waiting for her."

Xanthe's hands were shaking. He noticed distantly, the way he noticed weather. He looked at the drake — at Bramble, though he did not yet know that was her name; the name would come later, in the dark, offered shyly by the creature herself like a bone placed in his palm — and he felt the full weight of it settle onto his shoulders, onto his unwilling, ordinary, ghost-haunted life.

Responsibility. Not the abstract kind he'd borne for the dead, who needed nothing but listening. The living kind. The kind that bled and starved and trusted you and could die if you failed.

"I'll need tallow," he heard himself say, practicality rising in him like a reflex, the carver's instinct to shape what cannot be shaped. "And charcoal. And every scrap of fat in this house. If her fire feeds on anything, it'll feed on that. And we leave tomorrow. Tonight, if the hunters move first."

"Leave for where?" Sera asked.

"For the Door." He said it aloud and felt the valley itself flinch around him, the dead king's curse stirring in its sleep. "If it exists. If it isn't already sealed. If we can reach it before her flame goes out or Veyra's hunters find us." He paused. "There are a great many ifs, aren't there."

"There always are," Sera said. "That's what makes it a road instead of a plan."

Bramble — for he was already beginning, without knowing it, to call her that, the way one names a thing by the sound it makes moving through brambles, low and rustling and stubbornly alive — pressed harder against his knee. Her fire pulsed once, twice, and steadied. Not strong. Not well. But steady, the way a dying man steadies when someone finally holds his hand.

Xanthe put his palm flat against her small hot skull, and felt the last flame of a dead kingdom beating against his skin like a second pulse.

"Hold on, little fire," he whispered. "I hear you. I hear you. We're going."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the tolling of the market bell — three strikes, the hunters' signal, the sound that meant another name had been struck from the valley.

They were running out of night.
