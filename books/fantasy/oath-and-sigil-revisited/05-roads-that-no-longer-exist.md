## 5. Roads That No Longer Exist

The map was a lie before Sera ever touched it.

She had stolen it from the quartermaster's tent at Marrowford, back when she still wore the crown's grey and could walk into any tent in the column without raising a hand. It was a fine map, vellum, beautifully lettered, and it was wrong. She had known the country of her childhood, and this was not it. Whole villages were simply gone — not crossed out, not marked ruined, but *absent*, as though the ink had never met them. Where Ashenlow should have been there was a blank space, a polite nothing, with the road bending around it like water around a stone.

Now, three days out from Xanthe's workshop, with Bramble riding in the sling against Odji's chest and the wards of Hollowmere far behind them, Sera spread that same map across her knees on the crest of a hill and finally let herself understand what she was looking at.

It wasn't an error. It was an edict.

"They're erasing it," she said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew how bad it was. "Not burning it. Erasing it."

Xanthe crouched beside her, bone-charm knife still in his hand, always in his hand, as if carving were the only prayer he trusted. He looked at the map the way he looked at the dead — patiently, sadly, as though expecting it to speak.

"Struck from record," he said. "That's what the heralds cry in the market towns now. My cousin's wife heard it before we fled. *Let no man name it, let no man till it, let no man mourn it.*" He tapped the blank space where Ashenlow should be. "You can't tax a place that doesn't exist. Can't conscript from it. Can't be held accountable for what happens to the people in it, either, because officially there are no people."

Sera thought of the hedge-witch in the market square, the way the crowd had been ordered not to look away and not to look directly, both at once. The crown had learned that killing was easy; the harder art was making the killing never have happened.

Below them, the road ran down toward a valley where smoke rose from chimneys in gentle, domestic threads. A village. On the map, nothing.

"We need food," she said. "And we can't buy it from nowhere."

* * *

The village had a name. That was the first thing Sera noticed, and the thing that nearly broke her. A girl drawing water at the well said it plainly when asked — *Colderharrow* — and then flinched and looked over her shoulder, because saying it aloud had become an act.

"It was struck in spring," the girl whispered, pressing a heel of dark bread into Sera's hands and refusing coin. "A man came with white gloves and a book. Read our name out and then read that it wasn't ours anymore. Took the sign off the inn with a pry-bar while we watched." Her jaw tightened. "My grandmother is buried under a name that isn't legal now. What are they going to do, dig her up?"

They fed the strangers anyway, because Colderharrow was the kind of village that fed strangers, and because the old woman who kept the bread-oven took one look at Bramble's snout poking from Odji's sling and said, "Well. About time something came back through here," as if drakes were weather, long absent and expected.

Bramble ate scorched scraps and grew briefly, gloriously vivid, her scales catching firelight like embers breathing. But Sera watched her all through supper and saw what Xanthe refused to: the flame behind the little drake's eyes guttered between bites. When she slept, curled in Odji's lap, her warmth faded so fast the child kept pulling her closer, feeding her body heat like coins into a dying hearth.

"How long?" Sera asked Xanthe quietly, by the door.

"I don't know. Weeks. Maybe less." His thumb moved over the bone knife. "The ghost told me to warn the little fire. I keep thinking — warn it of what? You can't outrun a flame going out."

"You can find what feeds it."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she realized how tired he was, how much of him had been left behind in that workshop among the dead who complained and grieved and would not stop talking to him. "And if finding it costs one of us?"

Sera didn't answer. Outside, somewhere down the valley, a horn sounded — one note, then silence, then one note again. Patrol-signals. She had blown that pattern herself a hundred times.

"We move tonight," she said.

* * *

They walked by night after that, and the country taught them its own disappearance.

Roads that the map denied were still under their feet, cobbles wearing through moss, milestones with their letters chiseled away but the chisel-marks readable if you traced them with a finger, which Sera did, again and again, learning names by their wounds. Villages appeared and vanished around bends, some abandoned, some stubbornly inhabited by people who had decided that if their home did not exist, then neither did the king's law in it. One such hamlet had hung its own door-sign back up, freshly painted, defiantly legible: *Wrenholt*. Nobody there would say who had painted it. Everybody looked at the blacksmith's wife.

Odji walked in the middle, silent in the child's own voice — Sera had never yet heard the child use it — and full of everyone else's. Dead farmers muttered harvest-songs through those small lips as they passed fallow fields. A dead shepherd counted phantom sheep. Once, horribly, a dead soldier recited patrol-orders in a voice Sera recognized, and she walked faster until it stopped.

On the fifth night they sheltered in a ruined tollhouse, and Odji sat bolt upright by the cold hearth and spoke in a voice none of them had heard before: dry, precise, delighted, the voice of a man who had loved his work.

*"Ah! Ah — you're walking my roads."*

Xanthe set down his knife. "Who is it?"

*"Merric Vael, cartographer to the Crown of Hollowmere, forty-one years in service, dead nine."* The child's hands made small drafting motions in the air, measuring, squaring corners. *"Or I was, until the new men came with their white gloves and asked me to draw the kingdom without itself in it. I refused. Well. I died around then too; the order of events is disputed."*

Sera went very still. "Merric Vael drew the boundary surveys. I learned from his maps."

*"Then you learned well, huntress, and I am sorry for it, given what they've had you doing."* The ghost-cartographer made a sound that might have been a cough or a laugh. *"Listen. They struck the names from the paper, yes, but a map is only memory written down, and memory does not live on paper. It lives in feet. In wheels. In the ruts carts wore into clay over two hundred years. They can burn every vellum in the kingdom and the road to the Door will still know where it goes."*

"The Door," Xanthe said carefully, "of the Unwritten."

*"So you've heard. Then you know it cannot be found on any chart, because I tried, gods forgive me, I tried to chart it once and my pen went dry and stayed dry for a week."* The child's fingers spread wide, sketching. *"But I walked to it. Before I was cartographer, I was a courier's boy, and my master walked me there blindfolded, as couriers were, and I cheated — I counted paces, I noted streams, I broke twigs. Old sin. Let it be of use now."*

"Where?" Sera leaned forward. "Where does the road go?"

*"North and west. Past Wrenholt — yes, the stubborn one, good, follow the painted sign — to where the river Sorrel forgets to be a river and sinks into the limestone. There is a cliff called the King's Last Word. Do you see the joke? The dead king wrote his curse and his last word was a wall, and behind the wall—"*

The child swayed. Bramble woke and crooned low, and whatever held Odji loosened its grip; the small shoulders sagged, and for a moment Odji was only a tired child again, blinking at the fireless hearth.

"He's spent," Xanthe murmured, gathering the child close. "They spend him, these voices. Like candles."

"He spends himself," Sera said. "There's a difference." And then, because she could not stop it: "A cliff called the King's Last Word. Behind it, the Door. We can do that in four days if the patrols stay south."

Xanthe looked at her across the ruined tollhouse, at the child asleep against his chest and the drake flickering like a bad lamp, and said, "You say that like four days is a mercy."

"In my experience," Sera said, folding the lying map away forever, "four days is enormous."

* * *

They followed the painted sign out of Wrenholt at dawn, and the land began, slowly, to confess.

A milestone with its face chiseled smooth — but Sera laid her palm flat against it and felt, beneath the vandalism, the ghost-grooves of letters, and Odji, passing, whispered in Merric Vael's dry voice: *"Harrowgate. Population, once, four hundred and eleven."* A bridge the map denied carried them over a stream the map had drowned. An orchard grew apples in a village square that officially contained no village, and an old man pruning the trees tipped his hat to them and did not ask their names, because names had become dangerous currency and he had clearly decided they were worth more than he was being paid.

By noon of the second day Sera understood the thing she would carry out of this country, if she carried anything out of it.

They could strike the names. They could take the signs, burn the vellums, chisel the stones, decree the people out of existence. But the road still remembered. The apples still remembered. A child counting paces past a blindfolded master sixty years ago still remembered, and spoke through another child's mouth to lead the fugitives on. The crown had confused records with reality, and reality was laughing at them in ruts and roots and riverbeds.

Memory was not paper. Memory was resistance, and it was winning, inch by inch, footfall by footfall.

She almost said this aloud to Xanthe, walking the sunken lane toward the Sorrel. She was glad afterward that she didn't, because that was the moment the wind changed, and brought with it from the south a smell she knew better than her own name: dragon-fire, but wrong — thin, chemical, hungry, a flame fed on something it should never have been fed.

The false fire. The crown's false fire, moving north.

Behind them, far off, a horn sounded the pattern she had blown a hundred times — and this time a second horn answered it, closer, ahead.

Sera put her hand on Odji's shoulder and started to run.
