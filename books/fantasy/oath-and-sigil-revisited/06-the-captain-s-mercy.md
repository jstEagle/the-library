## 6. The Captain's Mercy

The chapel had burned a generation ago, and no one had bothered to rebuild it. That was why they chose it.

Xanthe felt the dead of the place before he crossed its threshold — a congregation of ash and patience, kneeling in rows that no longer existed, their pews long since carried off for firewood. They did not clamor at him the way the market ghosts did. Burned places made quiet dead, mostly. Fire took everything at once, and there was a strange mercy in that: nothing left half-finished, nothing left to argue about. Just a congregation that had been mid-prayer when the roof came down, and had never quite finished the amen.

He could hear it still, faintly, if he let himself listen. A hundred voices murmuring the last line of an evening liturgy, over and over, like water wearing a groove in stone.

*We are not hiding here,* Sera had said, when she'd picked the chapel off Odji's whispered cartography. *We're stopping. There's a difference.* Bramble needed rest. The drake had eaten half of Xanthe's emergency rations and then looked betrayed that there wasn't more, and her flame — he checked it every few hours now, cupping his palm near her snout the way you'd check a fevered child's brow — had burned low and blue all day instead of gold. Blue was bad. Blue was a candle in a drafty room.

So they made camp in the nave, under the open ribs of the ruined roof, where the stars came down through the smoke-blackened rafters like a congregation of their own. Sera slept in snatches with her bow across her knees. Odji slept curled against Bramble's warm flank, and even asleep the child's lips moved, trading borrowed voices with someone in dreams. And Xanthe sat by the broken altar and carved.

It was what his hands did when his mind refused to be still. A rib of some long-dead sheep, a sliver of antler — small charms, the kind he'd made ten thousand of back in Hollowmere: a knot for safe passage, a spiral for grief that wouldn't end, a simple hooked shape for *remember me*. He worked them without thinking, and the chapel's dead watched him do it, and for the first time since the wardline broke, the listening didn't feel like a curse. It felt like company.

That was how he almost missed the sound of boots on old stone.

Not one set of boots. Many. Moving in the disciplined cadence he'd learned to dread since the market square: heel-toe, heel-toe, spread out in a line, closing from three sides at once. He was on his feet and reaching for Sera before his mind finished the thought, and she was already awake — huntresses slept like cats, one ear always open — her hand clamped over his wrist, her eyes wide and fixed on the chapel's western doorway.

"I know," she breathed. "I hear them."

"How many?"

"Too many." She was already moving, waking Odji with a touch so gentle it contradicted everything else about her, gathering the drake into the shadow of the altar. "Back way?"

"There is no back way." His voice came out flat and strange. "There's only the door they came through."

The moonlight in the west doorway went dark.

She stood there filling the frame the way a blade fills a sheath: Captain Veyra Solenne, in the black-and-silver of the Crown's Inquisitory Order, her crossbow held low and easy, which was somehow worse than holding it high. Behind her, in the ruined yard, torchlight moved among the trees — a dozen soldiers at least, maybe more, fanning out along the walls. Xanthe heard Sera's breath catch, heard the small animal sound Odji made, heard Bramble's growl start somewhere down in the place where her dying fire lived.

"Captain," Sera said. Her voice was steady. Xanthe marveled at it. "You found us."

"I was always going to find you." Veyra stepped into the nave, and her soldiers poured in behind her, torches raised, and the chapel's dead flinched — Xanthe felt them flinch, a hundred souls pressing back away from the flame, and something in him pressed back with them. "Desertion, harboring a proscribed gifted, theft of crown property—" her eyes flicked to Bramble, and something complicated moved across her face, too fast to name— "and conspiracy against the realm. You understand I can't let you walk out of here."

"No," Sera said. "I understand exactly what you can't let me do."

Veyra raised the crossbow.

And stopped.

Xanthe would think about that stop for the rest of his life, however much or little of it remained. The way the captain's finger rested on the trigger without pulling. The way her eyes went past Sera, past him, to the child and the drake huddled in the altar's shadow, and stayed there. The way her jaw tightened like a woman biting down on words she'd been carrying too long.

"Everyone out," she said — not to them, but to her soldiers, without turning her head.

The sergeant hesitated. "Captain, protocol says—"

"*Out.*" The word cracked across the nave like the roof beam that had killed this congregation. "Seal the perimeter. No one approaches the chapel until I give the word. That is not a request."

The soldiers withdrew, torchlight shrinking up the aisle, and then it was just the four of them and the captain and the moonlight and the murmuring dead, and Veyra lowered the crossbow all the way to the floor, which Xanthe understood, dimly, was the most dangerous thing she'd done yet. An armed enemy you could predict. This, whatever this was, had no rules.

"Sera," Veyra said quietly. "Twelve years we served together. Twelve years, and you never once asked me what happens to the gifted we take."

"You never once offered to tell me."

"They burn them." Veyra's voice didn't waver, but Xanthe heard — with the sense that never stopped listening, the sense he'd spent thirty years pretending was ordinary grief-work — the thing underneath it. Not fear. Something older than fear. "That's what the reports say. Cleansed by sacred flame, threat to the realm extinguished. Clean words for dirty work." She took a step forward, and Sera's bowstring drew taut, and Veyra stopped again, hands open. "But I've seen the wagons, Sera. I've stood at the transfer stations. The gifted don't go to pyres. They go *inward*. To the capital. To the Foundry."

The name meant nothing to Xanthe. It meant everything to Sera; he saw it land in her like a stone in still water.

"The Foundry doesn't exist," Sera said.

"The Foundry exists the way Hollowmere doesn't exist. The way your village won't exist next month. The crown has gotten very good at making things unreal." Veyra crouched slowly, setting the crossbow on the flagstones, and straightened with her hands empty and visible. "Three years ago the royal pyres started burning cold. You remember the winter the capital's forges went dark? The dragon-fire under the palace — the fire they've been feeding the realm's glory on since the old kingdom fell — it's dying. Has been dying for years. And the crown found something in the old archives, something about what dragon-fire actually *is*, and they built themselves a machine to make more of it."

Bramble's growl deepened. Odji's head came up, and the child spoke — not in Odji's voice, which almost no one had ever heard, but in the dry rustling voice of some dead archivist:

*"Fire is memory given heat. What burns must first have lived."*

Veyra went very still. "Does it always do that?"

"Always," Xanthe said. His own voice surprised him. "What did they build, Captain?"

"A furnace that burns souls." She said it plainly, the way you'd report the weather, and the plainness was the horror of it. "Gifted souls. The dead king sealed his valley because he knew what was coming — I've read his warrant, the one they gave us to justify the breach. Half of it's redacted, but the sigil work underneath, the part they didn't know I'd recognize—" her mouth twisted— "it's Foundry work. The hunt isn't about cleansing the realm. It's about *harvesting* it. Every hedge-witch, every bone-charmer, every child who talks to things that aren't there. Fuel for a false fire, so the crown can go on pretending the age of dragons never ended."

The chapel was silent except for the dead, who had gone quiet too — a hundred murmured amens suspended, listening.

"And you?" Sera's voice was very soft. "You followed orders to the market square. You were going to put a bolt in me just now. Why should I believe mercy from you isn't just another kind of order?"

For a long moment Veyra didn't answer. When she did, she looked at the broken altar, not at Sera.

"Because I burned a hedge-witch in Hollowmere's square and I have not slept since. Because I told myself the law was justice for twenty years, and tonight I cornered a deserter, a bone-carver, a child, and a dying drake, and my hand would not close on the trigger." She finally turned to face them, and Xanthe saw what the torchlight had hidden: exhaustion, deep as a well. "The law sent me here to take you. Justice — if there is such a thing outside the law — might send me somewhere else entirely. I'm trying to find out which one I serve. I haven't decided yet. But I know I won't feed you to the Foundry."

"That's not the same as being on our side," Sera said.

"No," Veyra agreed. "It isn't. Yet."

Bramble chose that moment to move — a slow, deliberate shuffle out from behind the altar, dragging her tail across the stones, until she stood in the open moonlight between the captain and the family. She was smaller than any dragon had a right to be, ribs showing through dull scales, and when she opened her jaws the flame that curled out was thin and blue and guttering, a match-flame in a cathedral. But it was gold at the heart of it. One last thread of gold.

Veyra stared at it, and Xanthe watched twenty years of certainty die in her face.

"That's real," she said hoarsely. "That's the last of it, isn't it. The last true fire in the kingdom."

"It's why we run," Xanthe said. "While it lives, the Door of the Unwritten stays open. When it dies—" he didn't finish. He didn't have to.

Veyra looked at the drake for a long time. Then at Odji, who gazed back with the patient, layered gaze of someone inhabited by multitudes. Then at Sera.

"The Door is six days east, through the Ashen Fells," she said. "My orders are to hold this valley until the second company arrives at dawn. Second company travels heavy. They'll be slow on the fells' north road — but not slow enough, not if you go the way the maps say." She bent and picked up her crossbow, and Xanthe's heart dropped — and then she slung it across her back, muzzle down, and drew instead a rolled leather case from inside her coat and tossed it to Sera. Maps. Fresh ones, crown-issue, marked in red ink. "These are tomorrow's patrol routes. Learn them, then burn them. And Sera—" her voice caught, just barely, on the edge of the word— "when you reach the Door. If you reach it. Don't look back for me. Look back for each other."

Sera held the maps like they might bite. "Why are you doing this?"

"Ask me again when I've decided." Veyra turned toward the doorway, then paused, silhouetted against the moonlit yard, and for a moment she sounded almost like the girl she might have been before the law got hold of her. "There's a thing I learned in the Order. Mercy outside the law is called treason. I used to think that made it wrong." She stepped into the night. "I'm starting to think it might be the only thing left that's right."

Her boots faded across the yard. Somewhere beyond the walls, her voice rose, giving crisp orders to her soldiers: *chapel clear, no contact, target fled north — repeat, fled north.*

A lie. A beautiful, treasonous lie, bought with her career and possibly her life.

Xanthe sank down onto the altar step, and his hands, when he looked at them, were shaking. Sera sat beside him, the red-marked maps unrolled between them, and for a while neither of them spoke. The chapel's dead resumed their murmured amen, softer now, almost tender.

"She lied for us," Sera said finally. "Veyra Solenne lied to her own company."

"Yes."

"I don't trust it."

"No," Xanthe said. "Neither do I." He touched the half-carved charm in his pocket — the hooked little shape, the one for *remember me* — and thought of a woman in a burned chapel deciding, alone, in the space of a single drawn breath, whether the law or something larger owned her soul. "But I think we just saw what justice looks like when it escapes its cage. And I don't think it's done escaping."

Across the nave, Odji sat up in the dark, eyes reflecting Bramble's faint blue-gold glow, and spoke in a voice none of the living had ever heard before — young, and frightened, and unmistakably the dead king's.

*"Hurry,"* the child said. *"They are coming for the fire sooner than she knows."*
