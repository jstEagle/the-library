## 7. What the Dead Want

Odji did not choose the voices. That was the first thing no one ever understood, and the thing Odji had stopped trying to explain, because explaining required words of one's own, and Odji had not owned a word since the night the soldiers came to the fen-village and the dead poured in like floodwater through an open door.

The voices were always there. They layered over each other the way rain layered on the roof of Xanthe's workshop — a grandmother counting stitches, a drowned boy reciting the names of fish, an old soldier rehearsing an apology he had never delivered. Odji carried them the way other children carried stones in their pockets: for comfort, for weight, proof that they were not floating away.

But tonight, in the ruined tithe-barn at the edge of the ash-orchards, the voices went quiet.

All of them. At once.

The silence was so total that Odji's hands flew to their ears out of habit, though there was nothing left to block out. Sera looked up from sharpening her knife. Bramble lifted her head from the hearth-stones where she lay curled around the last coals of her own fire, her scales dull as old pewter, and made a sound low in her throat — a question without language.

Xanthe felt it too. Odji could see it in the way his hands stopped above the bone charm he was carving, the small knife hovering, his head tilting the way it always did when he listened to the dead and pretended he wasn't listening to the dead.

"They've stopped," Xanthe said quietly. "Haven't they."

Odji nodded. Then, because the silence itself was a kind of pressure, a held breath, Odji opened the door inside — the door that had been open so long Odji could no longer remember closing it —

— and the dead came through.

Not one voice. Not a hundred. *One.*

It moved through Odji like water filling a vessel, and Odji felt their own throat shape words that were not theirs and were not any single ghost's but belonged to all of them at once, the way a river belongs to every drop and yet is something more than water:

**"We have been waiting for you to stop being afraid of us."**

Sera's knife rang against the whetstone and fell still. Bramble uncurled, firelight guttering along her spine.

Xanthe rose slowly from his stool. "Who are you?" His voice cracked in the middle, and Odji heard everything underneath the crack — the years of pretending, the charms carved for grieving families while he whispered back answers he claimed came from ritual, the loneliness worn smooth as a river stone. "What are you?"

**"We are Hollowmere,"** said the voice through Odji's mouth, and it was strange to feel it there, warm and vast, like standing inside a bell when it rings. **"We are the miller who drowned and the midwife who stayed too long at her work and the king who died cursing. We are everyone this valley buried and then tried to forget. And we are tired, little speaker, of being treated as hauntings when we are only people who have not finished."**

Odji felt Xanthe flinch at *little speaker*, felt him want to argue, felt the argument die. The dead knew him better than he knew himself. The dead had always known.

"The curse," Xanthe said. His hands had found each other, knuckles white. "The king's dying curse. It keeps magic from crossing the border. It kept us hidden. Was that — was it a prison?"

**"It was a wall,"** the dead said, **"and walls are built by people who love what is behind them. Come. Sit. You will want to be sitting."**

* * *

They sat. Even Sera, who trusted nothing, sat, drawing Odji against her side with one arm — a gesture she would have denied under torture — and Bramble pressed her warm flank against Odji's legs, and Odji felt the drake's heartbeat, slow and failing, like a drum at the end of a long procession.

And the dead told them the truth of Hollowmere.

**"You believe the valley was sealed by a tyrant's spite,"** the voice said, and Odji's mouth shaped the words while somewhere far away, behind them, Odji watched like a child watching weather through a window. **"That is the story the crown tells now, in its new histories written by men who never saw him alive. But Aldric the Last did not curse his people. He shielded them."**

Images came with the words, because the dead do not speak in words alone; they speak in the weight of memory. Odji saw a dying king in a burning hall, crown melting crooked on his brow, and around him the screams of a court being put to the sword by soldiers wearing sigils Odji recognized — the same sigil now stamped on witch-hunters' warrants, the same sigil Sera had once worn on her own shoulder before she tore it off and burned it.

**"He saw what was coming,"** the dead said. **"A kingdom that would rather burn its gifted than shelter them. A crown that feared the flame it could not hold. So with his last breath he pulled the valley's borders closed like a man pulling a blanket over a sleeping child. No magic in. No magic out. Nothing for the hunters to find. He bought us two hundred years of forgetting, and forgetting was mercy, for a time."**

"But the wards failed," Xanthe whispered. "Why?"

**"Because a wall built on forgetting can only last as long as the forgetting holds. Your families stopped telling the old stories. Your carvers stopped carving the old signs. Magic thinned like broth stretched over too many bowls. And the crown —"** here the voice darkened, and Odji felt the ghosts' grief press down like snow on a roof — **"the crown did not forget. The crown has been waiting at our borders for two hundred years, patient as rot, for the wall to thin enough to breach. They have come not for hedge-witches and bone-carvers. They have come for the fire."**

Every head turned to Bramble.

The little drake looked up at them with eyes like banked coals, and did not understand, and understood completely.

**"She is the last living flame of the old kingdom,"** the dead said gently. **"When Aldric died, the royal fire passed out of the bloodline and into the smallest vessel that could carry it — a drake hatched in the palace stables the very hour of his death. The crown's hunters carry a false fire, a stolen thing fed on suffering. They need hers to make theirs real. And when her fire dies —"**

"It doesn't just kill her," Sera said. Her voice was flat, the huntress voice, the one that meant she was more frightened than she would ever admit. "It seals the Door. Doesn't it."

**"Yes, huntress. You know of the Door of the Unwritten."**

"I deserted because of it." Sera's arm tightened around Odji. "There were orders. There was a map. There was a child I wouldn't burn, and a rumor among the old soldiers that somewhere past the fens there's a way out of a country that officially doesn't exist. A seam in the world. I thought it was a story we told to keep ourselves from despair."

**"It is a story,"** the dead said, **"in the way that rivers are stories, in the way that hunger is a story. It is real, and it is close, three days east through the ash-orchards into the cliffs the cartographers refused to draw. But hear the rest, because the rest is why we have waited so long to speak with one voice instead of many."**

The barn seemed to lean inward. The candleflames stood perfectly still.

**"The Door does not open for keys or spells or blood. It opens for a price, and the price is a name — a name freely given, whole and unwound, surrendered to the Door's keeping. Whoever gives it will pass, and those they love will pass beside them. But the name will be unwritten. Struck from every memory, every record, every grave-marker and every heart. Those who pass will carry no memory of the one who paid. They will walk into the new land whole, and free, and alone, carrying nothing of the giver but what the giver's hands have made."**

Silence.

Then Xanthe laughed — a terrible sound, small and cracked, and Odji felt through the dead's vast attention the exact shape of the thought forming in him, the thought he thought he was hiding, the thought that had been forming since the moment the dead said *a name freely given*, because Xanthe Oyelaran had spent his whole life belonging everywhere among the dead and nowhere among the living, and here, at last, was a price he was uniquely qualified to pay.

"No," Sera said, seeing it too. She stood, dragging Odji up with her, putting herself between Xanthe and — what? The idea? The future? "No. Absolutely not. We'll find another way. We'll fight. We have Veyra, half-turned, we have —"

**"There is no other way,"** the dead said, and their voice was gentle now, infinitely gentle, the gentleness of people who had watched centuries pass from beneath the soil. **"But hear the last thing, the most important thing, the thing we have died twice to tell you."**

The voice gathered itself, and Odji felt every ghost in the valley stand up straight, as if before a king.

**"We do not want silence. Understand this, all of you, before you go wherever you are going. We do not want to be laid to rest, or banished, or quieted, or mourned into forgetfulness. We want witness. We want someone living to know what happened here — the wall built out of love, the fire carried in a small scaly body, the villages struck from maps, the hedge-witch burned in the market square whose name was Maren and who sang to her bees until the end. If you cross through that Door, carry us with you. Speak of us. Write of us. That is all we have ever wanted, and it costs nothing but courage."**

Odji felt the presence begin to withdraw, the great single voice fraying back into its thousand separate threads, and at the very last, one thread lingered — small, familiar, smelling of bread and lavender soap —

and Odji's mouth, on its own, in a voice that was not borrowed from anyone, said:

"Mama?"

The candle flickered. Something warm touched Odji's cheek like a hand that wasn't there.

Then the barn was only a barn, full of ash-light and the smell of cold stone, and the ordinary murmur of the distant dead returned like tide coming back in.

Xanthe was staring at the floor. Sera was staring at Xanthe. Bramble crept to the hearth-stones and breathed her fading fire across them, and the flames that answered were smaller than yesterday, and smaller than the day before.

Outside, far off toward the west, a horn sounded — once, twice — the hunting call of the crown's advance companies, sweeping the orchards.

Three days east. One name to give.

And in the silence after the horn, Odji heard Xanthe whisper, so softly he clearly believed no one could hear it, least of all a child who spoke in borrowed voices:

"I already know which name it will be."
