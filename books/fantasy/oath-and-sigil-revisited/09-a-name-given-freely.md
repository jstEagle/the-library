## 9. A Name Given Freely

The false fire came up the valley road like a second dawn, and it was wrong in every way a fire could be wrong.

Xanthe felt it before he saw it — a pressure against his gift, the way a bad tooth presses against the tongue. The dead of Hollowmere had gone quiet all at once, the way birds go quiet before a storm, and in that silence he could hear the thing the crown had built: a great iron brazier mounted on a wagon, burning with flame that cast no warmth and gave no light to the dead. It was fire with nothing living in it. Fire as an argument. *You are not real,* it said to everything old in the valley. *You were never real.*

"They're close," Sera said. She stood at the mouth of the cliff-hollow with her bow strung and her quiver nearly empty, and she did not look at him when she said what she said next. "I can hold them at the switchback for maybe ten minutes. Maybe less. Veyra's buying us what she can, but her own soldiers don't answer to her anymore — they answer to the brazier."

"Then ten minutes is enough," Xanthe said.

It was not enough. He knew it the way he knew the weight of a half-carved charm under his fingers: exactly, and without comfort.

Behind him, the Door of the Unwritten waited in the cliff face like a seam of morning light stitched into stone. It did not look like a door. It looked like the place where someone had begun to write something and then stopped — a vertical shimmer, tall as two men, thin as a held breath. The ghosts of everyone who had ever chosen exile stood along its edges in ranks so old their faces had worn smooth, like carvings left out in rain for centuries. They did not speak. They watched. They had been watching, Xanthe understood now, for a very long time, waiting for someone to come who understood the price.

Odji sat on a flat stone near the Door, Bramble curled around the child's small body like a scarf of grey-green scales. The drake was dying by inches. Her fire had guttered to an ember that glowed behind her teeth when she breathed, a coal wrapped in flesh, and each breath came slower than the last. When she exhaled, the ember dimmed. When she inhaled, it brightened. Xanthe found himself counting her breaths the way he had once counted the breaths of the dying women whose charms he carved, and he hated himself for how practiced he was at it.

"How many?" Odji asked, in the voice of a dead shepherd named Corwin Hale, a voice Xanthe had heard complain about unfinished fences for twenty years.

"Four hundred breaths," Xanthe said quietly. "Maybe five hundred."

"That's not long."

"No."

The child looked at him then with eyes that belonged to no one living, and for a moment every ghost Xanthe had ever heard seemed to stand behind them, crowded into one small face. "They want to say something to you," Odji said, in Odji's own voice this time — the rarest voice, the one the child almost never used. "All of them. They've been waiting to say it since you were a boy carving your first charm. But they can't say it until—" The child stopped. Looked at the Door. Looked back. "Until you understand what you're about to do."

"I understand," Xanthe said.

"You don't." Odji shook his head, and the borrowed voices fell away entirely, leaving only a frightened child speaking plain words. "Nobody understands it until it's done. That's why it works. That's why it has to be given freely. If you understood, you'd be giving it out of fear, and fear isn't free."

* * *

Sera came back down from the switchback at a run, blood on her sleeve that Xanthe prayed was not her own. "Ten minutes became five," she gasped. "The brazier-thing — when it gets close, the wards die faster. It eats them. It ate the market square wards in a minute. Xanthe, whatever you're going to do—"

"I know."

"Do you?" She grabbed his arm, and her grip was the grip of a woman who had once refused to burn a child and had been paying for it ever since. "Because I've done the arithmetic too. I know what the Door asks. And I'm telling you there has to be another way — my name, take mine, I've got less to lose, I'm already struck from the rolls, I'm already—"

"Sera." He covered her hand with his own. Her knuckles were scarred; he had never noticed that before, in all these weeks of running, and now he noticed everything, because he was memorizing. That was the terrible secret of last hours: they made you see. "Your name is written in your daughter's memory if she lives, and in Odji's, and in mine while I have a mind to keep it. Mine is written in bone. In charms for the dead. In a valley full of ghosts who know me only as the quiet man who listened." He smiled, and was surprised to find the smile steady. "The Door doesn't want a name, Sera. It wants a name that will be *missed*. A name held by the living and the dead alike. That's the coin. That's why it's expensive."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "Most true things aren't."

* * *

The soldiers came around the last bend with the false fire rolling behind them like a captive sun, and Veyra came ahead of them on foot with her sword drawn and turned — turned not toward the hollow but toward her own line.

"HOLD!" her voice cracked across the valley, captain's voice, huntress voice, and for one heartbeat the line wavered. "These people are under my warrant! My seal! Any man who advances steps past me first!"

They advanced. Of course they advanced. The brazier's heatless light had eaten something out of them, some part that remembered orders were given by people and not by fire.

Veyra did not run. Xanthe watched her plant herself in the narrow throat of the path, alone, absurd, magnificent, and he understood that she had known this would happen — that her mercy had always been meant to end here, spent all at once, buying minutes instead of justice.

"Go," she shouted back at the hollow, without turning. "*Now.*"

* * *

Xanthe walked to the Door.

The old ghosts along its edge stirred as he approached, smooth-faced and ancient, and one of them — a woman in robes three kingdoms out of fashion — inclined her head to him, the way one scholar greets another across a library.

He put his palm against the seam of light. It was warm. Of course it was warm; everything false in the crown's arsenal was cold, so everything true had to burn.

He had prepared words. He realized, standing there, that the words didn't matter. The Door was not a lock to be picked with eloquence. It was a listener. And listening — that, at least, he knew how to do.

"My name is Xanthe Oyelaran," he said, and his voice carried down the cliff-hollow, down the valley, into the ears of four hundred dying breaths and one guttering ember and one child who spoke for the dead. "Bone-carver. Son of Maren Oyelaran, who taught me that grief-work is holy work. Listener to the dead of Hollowmere, who were never hauntings, only neighbors waiting to be heard."

The light beneath his palm pulsed.

"I give it freely. Not to escape — I could have hidden again, hidden forever, died unknown in a cellar among my carvings, and that would have been a kind of keeping. I give it so that these three — this huntress with scarred knuckles, this child with borrowed voices, this small dragon carrying the last true fire of the old kingdom — so that they walk out of this valley *remembered*. So that somewhere past this Door, someone will say their names, and mean them, and the old magic will live in the saying."

He drew a breath. "Take mine. Write over it. Let the maps forget me, let the records lose me, let even the ones I love reach for my name and find a shape where it used to be. Only let them keep the bones I carved. Bone remembers what ink won't."

The Door opened.

Not wide — a hand's width of light spilling along the stone, then a shoulder's width, then a doorway of pure unwritten morning, and through it Xanthe could see — or thought he saw, or would remember thinking he saw — green hills under a sky belonging to no kingdom he knew.

And then the dead of Hollowmere rose.

They came up out of the ground of the valley all at once, every ghost he had ever heard complaining about fences and debts and unsaid apologies, every soul whose charm he had carved, and they did not come as terror. They came as *witness*. They stood between the soldiers and the hollow in ranks a thousand deep, and where the false fire's light touched them it simply stopped, having nothing in them to unmake — they were already outside the record, already erased, and erasure could not touch them twice.

Xanthe heard them then, all together, for the first time not as complaints but as a chorus, and what they said was simple, and it undid him:

*We see you, Xanthe Oyelaran. We have always seen you. Go knowing that being seen is not the same as being remembered — and that we will do the one work left to us, which is to stand here, seen, until your family is through.*

Bramble lifted her head. The ember behind her teeth flared — one last time, impossibly, gloriously — and the little drake breathed out a ribbon of true fire that lit the whole hollow gold, and under that light the Door blazed wide.

"Through," Xanthe said. His voice broke on it. "All of you. Through. Now."

Sera hesitated, tears cutting through the ash on her face. Odji took her hand. Bramble limped between them, ember dimming, dimming, dimming —

—and Xanthe pushed them gently toward the light, and stepped back, and felt the first soft erosion begin at the edges of him, his name going out of the world like a lamp carried into another room.

The last thing he heard, as the Door began to close and the dead of Hollowmere stood singing against the dark, was Odji's voice — the child's own voice, small and furious and certain, calling back from inside the light:

"I'll find it! Your name! I'll find it and say it and you'll see—"

The Door sealed. The seam of light faded to a thread, then a scratch, then stone.

And Xanthe Oyelaran turned to face the valley he had saved, nameless, witnessed, and — for the first time in his life, among the thousand dead who knew him utterly — not alone.
