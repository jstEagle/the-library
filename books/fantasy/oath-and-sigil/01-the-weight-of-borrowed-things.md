## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The night market of Vell opened the way a wound opens: slowly, and with a smell you never quite forgot.

Ansel Ishikawa knew all its smells by heart—the tallow smoke from the fry-stalls, wet wool steaming off dockhands fresh from the harbor, the cloying rot of overripe quince that no one ever bought but everyone displayed, because a market stall without something spoiling on it looked like poverty, and in Vell poverty was a thing you hid even while selling it. She moved through it all in her gray cloak with the hood pulled just low enough, carrying her tray of wax charms like a priestess carrying relics she did not believe in.

Which was, in fairness, almost true. She believed in them the way a locksmith believes in locks: as instruments, not miracles.

"Charms for your troubles," she called, soft-voiced, letting the words do their small work. "Charms for steady hands and lucky dice. Charms to remember what matters and forget what doesn't."

The last part was the honest part, which is why it sold best.

Her stall was nothing—a plank across two barrels at the mouth of Cooper's Row, where the lamplight came down thin and amber through the smoke-haze and made everything look kinder than it was. On the plank sat rows of little wax discs, each one pressed with a sigil of her own devising: a spiral, a closed eye, a key with no bow. They were pale as fingernails and they caught the light like teeth. Merchants' wives bought them against bad dreams. Gamblers bought them against cold streaks. A young clerk had once bought eleven of them before his wedding, pressing them into his coat lining like a man arming himself against his own heart.

None of them knew what they were really buying. That was the whole architecture of Ansel's life: a trade built inside a secret, wrapped in a charm, sold under a lie so comfortable that no one had ever thought to question it.

Because the truth was this: every charm on her tray held a stolen memory.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way poets mean it, when they say a locket holds a memory. Actually. Literally. Somewhere in her thirteenth year—she could not say precisely when; that was part of the theft too—Ansel had discovered she could reach into another person's mind the way you'd reach into a pocket, if the pocket were willing to be reached into. It worked best on the drunk, the exhausted, the grieving-but-not-yet-broken. She would lay two fingers against a wrist or the back of a hand, murmur something soothing, and *draw*—and out would come a memory, whole and glistening, like an oyster coming up from deep water. The donor would feel only a lightness afterward, a pleasant vagueness, the sense of having set down a heavy bag and forgotten where. And Ansel would press the memory into warm wax, seal it with her sigil, and sell it to someone else who wanted to know how it felt to hold a newborn, or win at cards, or stand on a ship's prow with salt spray in their teeth.

Memories kept better than coin. Coin got spent; memories got *worn*. And worn things grew precious.

She had rules. Rules were the difference between a thief and a monster, and Ansel had decided long ago—if decide was even the word for something you learned in your bones—that she would rather be poor than monstrous.

Rule the first: never steal grief. Grief was load-bearing. Take it out of a person and the whole house of them collapsed, quietly, years later, in ways no charm-seller would ever be blamed for. She had seen it happen once, early, before the rule had a name. A fishwife in Saltrow whose dead son's memory Ansel had lifted for a rich client who wanted to understand loss without paying its price. The fishwife hadn't wept. That was the horror of it. She'd simply stopped being able to find her way home from the market, standing in the same square every dusk with her basket, looking around with the mild puzzled face of a woman who has misplaced something enormous. Ansel had given back what she could salvage—it was like returning a shattered vase in a bag of sand—and had not slept properly for a season after.

Rule the second: never steal twice from one person. Once was a lightness. Twice was a habit of lightness, and habits hollowed people out. Vell was full of hollowed people already; the Ministry saw to that in its own official way. Ansel refused to be the unofficial version.

There were other rules, smaller ones, housekeeping rules. Never take from children. Never take more than a person wouldn't miss. Never take anything you couldn't look at yourself without flinching—because the memories lived in her head until she pressed them into wax, sometimes for hours, sometimes overnight, and some of them were very hard to sleep beside.

Tonight's harvest looked to be generous. She could smell it on the crowd, the particular sweetness of a market night with money loose in it: three grain barges had come upriver unrobbed, which happened perhaps twice a season, and the merchants were drinking like men who believed the world might continue.

She marked her first mark within the hour. Master Halloway, wool-factor, pink-faced and expansive at the wine-stall, laughing too loudly at a joke about his own wife. Ansel drifted past him with her tray held at hip height, let him catch sight of the charms—men like Halloway always caught sight, there was something in the wax that snagged the eye like a hook in cloth—and smiled her market smile, the one that said *I am harmless and slightly amusing and I will listen to whatever you say.*

"You sell luck?" Halloway demanded, swaying toward her. He had rings on four fingers and none of them fit anymore, which told its own story about prosperity.

"I sell remembering," Ansel said. "Luck is just memory wearing good clothes."

He blinked at that, delighted, the way drunk men are delighted by sentences they can't quite climb. "Give us the lucky one, then. The dice one."

"The dice one costs more than the others." She said it gently, apologetically, and watched him swell. "It's a rare memory. A man winning forty crowns at knucklebones in the Silver Anchor, the night the dice ran hot. You can wear it whenever you sit down to play. It won't make you win—" honesty, always honesty, it disarmed them better than any flattery— "but it'll remind your hands what winning feels like. Hands remember. Ask any gambler."

Halloway's hands were already remembering. He paid triple and wore the charm out of the market pressed to his lips like a relic, and Ansel watched him go with the small cold satisfaction that was the closest thing to joy her work allowed.

The lifting itself took less than a minute. She waited until he was deep in his cups and deep in conversation, then brushed past close enough to touch the back of his ringed hand with two fingers, murmuring an apology about the crowd—and drew. The memory came up sweet and fizzy, all lamplight and dice-rattle and the animal joy of a man who believes the universe has finally noticed him. She held it behind her eyes the rest of the evening, warm as a coal in a cupped palm, and pressed it into wax at her stall between customers, sealing it with the spiral.

By midnight she had taken six. A shipwright's memory of his daughter's first steps—he'd offered it up so easily, half-asleep against a post, that Ansel felt like a pickpocket working a corpse. A customs officer's memory of a bribe so perfectly executed he'd replayed it for years; she took that one with less guilt than usual. A young woman's memory of dancing in the rain on the aqueduct, which Ansel pressed into wax immediately because it was making her chest hurt, and which she priced high, because somewhere in Vell was another young woman who needed to remember that her body had once been capable of joy.

And then there was the old man at the far end of the row, sitting alone at a table outside the Drowned Bell with a cup he wasn't drinking from.

Ansel noticed him the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know well. Everyone else in the market was leaning into the night, spending themselves. This man was folded inward, small and still, watching his cup as though it might confess to something. His coat had been fine once—good wool, good cut—and now hung on him like a borrowed argument.

She should have walked past. Her feet were aching, her tray was full enough, and the hour was late enough that the good memories had gone home and only the dangerous ones remained, sitting alone with untouched cups.

But she found herself drifting toward him anyway, tray balanced, hood low.

"Charm, sir? For what ails you?"

The old man looked up. His eyes were the worst kind—not empty, not dull, but *full*, brimming with something he was holding very carefully, the way you carry a bowl of water across an uneven floor.

"What ails me," he repeated, tasting the words. "That's a fair question from a stranger." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table. "My wife died in the spring."

Ansel's stomach went cold and quiet. Rule the first stood up inside her like a drawn blade.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and began to turn away—

"But here's the strange thing," he continued, not really talking to her now, talking to the cup, to the table, to the night. "Everyone keeps telling me to hold on to the memories. Hold them tight, they say. Hold them close. As if grief were a purse and someone might rob me." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "Do you know what it's like, holding them tight? Forty-one years of her, and every one of them sharp as glass. I can't eat. I can't sleep. I hold them because they tell me to, and they're killing me, and nobody—*nobody*—will take them."

The market noise seemed to recede around them, the way water draws back from a shore.

"No," Ansel said quietly. "I don't deal in that. I'm sorry."

"There must be someone." His hand shot out and caught her sleeve, and his grip was stronger than his frame suggested, desperate-strong, drowning-strong. "There are stories. Charm-wives and memory-peddlers and God knows what in the cellars of this city. Someone who can lift a thing out of a man's head. I'd pay anything. Anything. I want to remember loving her without—" his voice cracked down the middle— "without it going through me like a spear every time. Is that so wicked? Is that so wicked a thing to ask?"

It was not wicked. That was the unbearable part. Ansel could see, plain as lamplight, that this was the rare case, the exception the rule existed to protect people from tripping over—the grief that had become a wound instead of a weight, the memory that served no purpose but pain. She could lift it. Two fingers, one breath, done. He would sleep tonight. He would eat tomorrow. He would walk through the world lightly, and the bowl of water would finally be set down.

And she would carry forty-one years of a stranger's marriage in her skull, and some part of that love would live in her forever, pressed into wax and sold to some merchant's wife who wanted to feel cherished without doing any of the work of being loved.

And the rule would be broken. And rules, once broken by a good reason, stayed broken for every reason after. She had learned that lesson in Saltrow, written in a fishwife's puzzled face at dusk.

"I can't help you," Ansel said, and pried his fingers from her sleeve with a gentleness that cost her more than the grip cost him. "But there's a woman on Tanner's Lane. Widow Serrano. She sits with people. Not charms—just company. Grief shared isn't grief halved, whatever the proverb says, but it's grief *carried*, and carried is survivable."

The old man stared at her. Then he laughed again, worse than before, and released her. "Carried," he said. "Yes. Fine. Go sell your trinkets, girl. Go sell other people's lives in wax."

She walked away and did not look back, and hated herself the entire length of Cooper's Row, which was, she supposed, a kind of penance, and a kind of proof that she still had a self left to hate.

* * *

The cellar she rented—or occupied, tenancy being a fluid concept in the warren beneath Tanner's Lane—was low-ceilinged and smelled of candle smoke and beeswax and the mineral damp of old stone. She liked it precisely because no one else wanted it. In Vell, invisibility was cheaper than rent.

She lit two candles and laid out the night's harvest on her worktable: six wax discs cooling in their little clay molds, each one holding its cargo of borrowed living. The shipwright's daughter, mid-step, arms out, face astonished at her own motion. The customs bribe, all silk and sleight. The girl dancing on the aqueduct, rain silvering the air around her.

Ansel pressed her palms flat on the table and let herself feel them, just for a moment, the way you're allowed to smell bread you cannot afford. The shipwright's pride. The officer's sleek satisfaction. The dancer's rain-bright joy.

This was the part no client ever guessed, the part that made the trade what it actually was: she tasted everything first. Every charm she sold passed through her, and some residue always stayed—a silt of other people's moments settling in the riverbed of her. She was, she sometimes thought, the richest pauper in Vell. She had danced on aqueducts and won at dice and held newborns and stood on prows, all secondhand, all borrowed, none of it hers.

And hers—her own—was locked away where even she could not easily reach it. That was the arrangement, though she no longer remembered making it. There was a room in her mind with the door sealed, and behind the door was a childhood, a family, a name's full meaning, a country—she knew only that much, the shape of the absence, the way you know the shape of a missing tooth with your tongue. She had tried, over the years, prying at the edges. All she ever got was fragments that slipped away on waking: a wall the color of eggshell. A word in a language she shouldn't have known. The smell of cedar and hot iron.

The Ministry called people like her *retentives*, when it bothered calling them anything. Unlicensed retention of unregistered memory. The phrase was a masterpiece of bureaucratic violence: it criminalized having a past.

So she kept her head clean of anything incriminating, kept her charms deniable, kept her rules, and told herself the loneliness was the price of competence, and competence was the price of survival, and survival was enough. It had been enough for nine years.

She was sealing the last charm—the dancer, the spiral sigil, the wax gone smooth and pale—when the sound came.

Not from the stair. From the *wall*—the old drainage passage behind the shelving, bricked shut since before her time, except that it was not bricked shut anymore. A section of masonry shifted, ground, and sagged inward with a grinding sigh, exhaling a breath of canal-stink and colder air.

And then a man came through the hole it left, which was impossible, because the hole was barely wide enough for a cat, and the man was full-grown and bleeding from somewhere high on his torso, leaving a dark smear down the stones as he folded—there was no other word for it, *folded*—onto her cellar floor.

Ansel was on her feet with the carving knife off the table before her mind caught up with her body. "Don't," she said. "Whatever you are, don't."

The man rolled onto his back. He was young—younger than her, maybe—with a courier's lean frame and a courier's road-worn boots, and his eyes were the worst thing in the room, because they were focused entirely on her face with the ferocious precision of a man memorizing it, or recognizing it.

"You sell memories," he said. Blood bubbled at the corner of his mouth when he spoke. "They said. In the Warrens. A woman who sells memories."

"Who told you that?"

"It doesn't matter." He fumbled at his breast with a hand slicked red, and drew out a fold of oilcloth, and thrust it at her with the last of his coordination. "Take it. Take it *off* me. They can't follow what they can't find."

"Take what? Who's *they*—"

"The moon is wrong," he whispered.

It was such an absurd sentence that Ansel almost laughed, and then didn't, because his face was utterly serious, utterly certain, and terror like that is contagious.

"The moon is wrong," he said again, softer, and pressed the oilcloth bundle into her hand, folding her fingers over it with bloody strength. "Thirteen. Count them. Thirteenth moon. The Door—" His gaze slid sideways, fixing on something in the middle distance that was not her cellar, not her city, maybe not her world. Whatever he saw there, it eased him. His shoulders came down. His breathing slowed.

"Don't," Ansel said, hearing the panic in her own voice, absurdly, for a stranger. "Hey. Stay awake. Look at me."

He looked at her. He smiled—a real smile, brief and crooked, the smile of a man relieved of a great weight—and then the light in his eyes went out the way a lamp goes out, all at once, no flicker, no return.

Ansel knelt there in the candle-smell and the blood-smell, holding an oilcloth bundle she suddenly could not bring herself to open, listening to the silence where his breathing had been.

And that was when she felt it begin: the seep. His death-memory, rising in him in his final seconds, finding in her the same open door every mind keeps for a touch, a hand, a moment of contact—and pouring through it into her whether she willed it or not. Canal water. Running footsteps. A white lantern swinging. The taste of blood and the enormous, calm certainty of a mission completed, and underneath it all, rising like a sun:

*A door. A seam of light in ruined stone. Thirteen moons wheeling above it.*

Ansel sat back hard against the shelving, the bundle clutched to her chest, and understood with perfect clarity that her careful life—her rules, her invisibility, her nine quiet years—had just ended in her own cellar, on a Tuesday, between one candle and the next.

Outside, faint and far off, the Ministry bells began to ring.
