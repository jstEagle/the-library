## 2. A Stranger at the Threshold

The cellar under the tannery smelled of salt and old leather, and Ansel had spent three years teaching it to smell like nothing at all.

She came down the ladder with her night's earnings in a pouch against her ribs—eleven charms, two of them good ones, pressed from a wine merchant who had drunk himself generous and a customs clerk who wept after his third cup and never knew what she'd taken. The good ones were a first kiss on a bridge in a city that might have been Vell or might have been somewhere warmer, and a mother's voice singing through a wall, half-heard, the words worn smooth as river stones. Those would sell by week's end. People paid well for tenderness they'd never owned. It was grief they wouldn't buy, and so Ansel never stole it—not because the rule was kind, but because unsold grief went sour in the wax, curdled into something that whispered at you from the shelf.

She lit no lamp. She knew the cellar by its silences: the barrel of rainwater three steps from the ladder, the trestle where she dried her charms, the narrow cot behind the hanging sacking where she slept curled like a comma. She was halfway to the trestle when the silence changed.

There was breathing in the room that wasn't hers.

Ansel went still, one hand flat against the cool stone, and let her eyes adjust to the dark the way she'd learned to let them adjust to anything—to a market crowd, to a face mid-laugh when the memory ran shallowest. In the corner past the water barrel, something large was folded against the wall. Breathing in wet, tearing pulls, like cloth ripped slowly.

"I know you're there," she said quietly. "The door is behind me. I'll use it."

A laugh answered her, or the ruin of one. "Door's... bricked. I checked." A pause, wet breath. "You're the charm-seller. Gray shawl. You work the fountain row."

Fear moved through her, cold and specific. Someone had watched her closely enough to name her habits, and named her habits meant named her. "Who sent you?"

"No one sends me anymore." The shape shifted, and moonlight from the high slit window caught him—a man perhaps forty, courier's leathers gone dark with blood along his left side, his face gray as tallow. He had the look of someone who had been running for days and had finally, deliberately, stopped. "I looked for you. Three nights. You're hard to find. That's good. Keep being hard to find."

"Find your own cellar."

"Mine's burned." He said it without drama, the way a man reports weather. "With everything in it."

Ansel should have gone up the ladder then. Every rule she lived by said go—the rules were load-bearing, they were the whole architecture of her survival, and rule the first was *never be part of anyone's story but your own*. But he had said *you're the charm-seller* the way you say *you're the last well in a dry country*, and beneath the fear she felt the terrible pull of professional recognition: this was a man carrying something he could not put down.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"To give you something before I stop being able to." His hand moved toward his chest, and she flinched back, and he stopped, hand hovering, patient as a man feeding a nervous animal. "Not a weapon. Paper."

He drew it out folded small, folded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth, and held it out across the dark between them. Ansel didn't move.

"Take it," he said. "Please. I've carried it four hundred miles and I would very much like to set it down."

*Rule the second: never take what can't be sold or eaten.* But her hands were already moving, traitor hands, hands that had spent ten years learning to lift things gently, and the paper touched her palm lighter than any stolen memory ever had. It was warm from his body. That warmth frightened her more than the blood had.

"What is it?"

"A map." He leaned his head back against the stone. "To the Door of Thirteenth Moon."

The words landed strangely, like coins dropped down a well—she heard them, heard them echo, and understood none of their weight. "I don't know what that is."

"No," he agreed softly. "Nobody does. That's rather the point." He coughed then, deep and terrible, and the cough went on long enough that Ansel found herself crossing the room without deciding to, kneeling beside him, holding the water skin to his lips. He drank like a man remembering how. "Thank you," he whispered. "That's the first kindness in nine days."

"You need a physician."

"Physicians ask questions. Questions get answered. Answers—" another ghost of a laugh, "—get people unmade."

The word went through her like a needle finding old scar tissue. Everyone in Vell knew the word *unmade* the way children knew the word for plague: as a thing spoken low, a thing that happened to other people, to careless people, to people who remembered too loudly. The Ministry of Unmaking kept its offices above the Hall of Records, and its inspectors wore gray, and no one who went up those stairs came back down as quite the same person. Sometimes not as a person at all—as a gap in a family, a chair no one could explain missing, a name everyone stumbled over and then forgot they'd stumbled.

"You're from outside the walls," she said. It wasn't a question. He had the look of the road on him, dust in the seams of him, and something else—something she couldn't name yet, a wrongness about him that sat just below noticing.

"I'm from Cael Sarn."

She frowned. The name tickled at some locked place and found the door barred. "I don't—"

"You don't. No one does. That's how thorough they were." He closed his eyes. "Four hundred miles I've walked, and in every town I say the name and every face goes smooth as an egg. Not hostile. Smooth. They hear me say it and then they don't. It slides off. I watched a woman forget my sentence while I was still finishing it." His eyes opened, and they were very clear, and very tired. "But you sell memories. You handle the stuff raw. Maybe some of it stuck in you. Maybe that's why I found you."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Look at the map."

She almost refused. Then she unfolded it.

It was drawn on membrane, not paper—thin, veined, faintly warm—and it showed a country she did not know and somehow, horribly, almost recognized. Coastline like a broken comb. Mountains inked in fine, obsessive strokes. And at the center, a city rendered in such loving detail that the cartographer must have walked every street: walls, towers, a river delta like a silver tree. Around the whole of it, drawn in a different hand, a border—but not a line. A sigil, vast and intricate, a knot of interlocking strokes that circled the country like a ring around a finger.

And the moon. In the corner of the map, where a legend should be, thirteen circles were drawn in a row. Twelve filled in with careful hatching. The thirteenth left open, waiting.

"The Door opens when the false moon finishes its turn," the courier said. "Weeks. Maybe less. I was meant to carry word to the remnant inside the city—there are still people who remember, pockets of them, keepers—but I won't make it now, and the Ministry is close behind me. Closer than close. So it goes to you." He tried to smile. "Congratulations. You've been promoted from thief to courier."

"I'm not a courier. I'm not anything of yours. Give it to someone else."

"There is no someone else. There's you, because you happened to be the thief with clean hands in the right city, and there's the map, and there's the moon turning." He reached out—slowly, giving her time to pull away—and closed her fingers over the folded membrane. His hand was fever-hot. "When you understand it, you'll know what to do. That's all any of us ever did. Understand, and do."

"You're dying," she said stupidly. "You need—"

"I need you to listen, because I haven't got long and the bleeding has opinions." He pulled himself straighter against the wall, and his voice dropped, urgent, running now like water downhill. "Three things. One: never show the map under an open sky. Two: the Ministry hunts it—they've hunted it since the Unmaking, since they sealed the country inside its own forgetting—and their hunters wear stolen memory like armor, so if one looks at you kindly, run. Kindness is the trap. Three—" he caught her wrist, grip shockingly strong, "—when the moon is wrong, when you see the thirteenth rise, remember that doors only open for people willing to be someone on the other side. Names matter there. Names are the coin."

"I don't have a name worth spending."

"Everyone says that," he murmured. "Everyone who's hiding."

Then the strength went out of him all at once, like a tide withdrawing, and he sagged, and Ansel caught him—caught a stranger's full weight in her arms in her own hidden cellar, breaking rule the first, rule the second, and whatever rule came third, all in the space of a quarter hour.

"Stay awake," she ordered. "Tell me your name."

"Hallo," he breathed. "My name is Hallo. Remember it. Remembering is the whole war."

His eyes fixed on the high slit of window, on the thread of moonlight lying across the floor.

"The moon is wrong," he whispered. "Look at it. Look at it and count."

She looked. Through the slit, a sliver of moon hung over the tannery roofs, thin as a paring. And beside it—or near it, or maybe she imagined it, maybe it was a cloud, a tear in her eye, a trick of the lamplight she hadn't lit—there was a second light. Faint. Silver-gray. Shaped like nothing.

She blinked and it was gone.

Hallo smiled at whatever he saw in her face. "There it is," he said. "Thirteen." And died between one breath and the next, quietly, the way a lamp goes out, still holding her wrist.

* * *

Ansel sat with the body for a long time.

She told herself it was practicality: she needed to think, and thinking was easier than moving. There was a dead courier in her cellar. There was a map made of skin in her hand. There was blood on her floorboards that she would have to scrape and salt and scrub before dawn, and a body she would have to carry up four ladders and out through streets she knew blindfolded, to the lime pits beyond the tanners' quarter where the city's unwanted went, and all of it before the fish carts rolled and the market woke and the world resumed its business of not noticing things.

Practicality. Yes. She sat because of practicality.

But the truth, which arrived slowly and settled over her like snowfall, was that she couldn't stop touching his hand. It was cooling under her fingers, and she kept hold of it anyway, because letting go felt like a second death and she had caused enough of those tonight.

*I stole from him,* she thought. *Even now. Even dead, I'm stealing.* Because already—she could feel it, the way she felt the charms in her pouch, a faint pressure behind the eyes—his last hour was seeping into her. The four hundred miles. The burned safehouse. The woman whose face went smooth as an egg. The taste of ditchwater drunk from cupped hands at dawn. It came into her unbidden, unliftable, a memory with no owner anymore, and it pooled in her mind like rainwater in a boot print.

She had never taken a death-memory before. She hadn't known they took themselves.

And in the flood of him she found the thing he'd saved for last, the thing he hadn't had breath to say: a face. A woman's face, seen across a burning field, mouth open in a shout he couldn't hear over the roar—shouting a warning, or a name, or both. And beneath the image, lodged like a splinter, a single certainty that Hallo had carried four hundred miles and handed to her with the map:

*They know the map survived. They will follow it the way blood follows a wound.*

Ansel closed her eyes. Behind them, uninvited, the burning field played again.

When she finally moved, dawn was still hours off. She worked in the dark with a tanner's efficiency: scraped the boards, salted them, wrapped Hallo in the spare sacking she used for winterizing the charm racks. He was heavier than he'd looked, the way the dead always were, and she apologized to him twice under her breath, absurdly, for the indignities of the carrying. At the top of the fourth ladder, in the coal-cellar that opened on the tanners' walk, she paused with his weight across her shoulders and looked back down into the dark of her home.

Her eyes had adjusted. They always adjusted. And in the adjusted dark she saw, plain as writing, the place where the map had lain on her trestle: a faint discoloration in the wood, shaped like a ring of interlocking strokes, already fading even as she watched—leaving its mark, or taking one.

Outside, the sky over Vell was graying toward morning. She shifted Hallo's weight and stepped out into the streets, and above the tannery roofs, low on the horizon where no moon had any business being, the thirteenth light hung pale and patient, waiting for her to count it.
