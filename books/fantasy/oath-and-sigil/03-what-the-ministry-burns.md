## 3. What the Ministry Burns

The morning the Ministry came to the Night Market, Ansel was three charms from finishing her week's rent and one lie away from a comfortable life.

She had learned long ago that Vell kept two markets. The day market belonged to the lawful: fishwives shouting over silver-scaled catch, spice sellers with their brass measures, children running errands between the legs of honest commerce. But the night market—the one that bloomed in the drowned light of paper lanterns along the Sump Canal, where the cobbles still smelled of last century's tanneries—belonged to people like her. People who dealt in the intangible. People who had made themselves small enough to slip through the cracks of the city's attention.

She had set up her stall an hour past dusk, as always, wedged between Maretta's fortune-wax and the tooth-puller who worked out of a wheelbarrow. Her wares sat in their usual rows: little wax discs the color of old honey, each one pressed with a thumbprint sigil and holding, folded inside it like a moth in amber, somebody else's memory.

A charm of first snow, taken gently from a shepherd's daughter who'd sold it to buy her mother medicine. A charm of a wedding dance, borrowed from a widower who swore he didn't want it anymore but wept when Ansel lifted it anyway—which was why she never took grief, only joy gone soft with age. A charm of the sea, which sold well in landlocked Vell because half the city had never seen water wider than the canal and hungered for it without knowing why.

Business was good. Business was always good on nights when the fog came off the canal thick enough to make strangers of everyone.

She was wrapping a charm of a mother's lullaby—for a young man who'd been raised in a foundling house and wanted, just once, to know what it was like to be sung to sleep—when she felt it.

Not heard. Felt. The way you feel a storm before the first drop lands: a pressure behind the ears, a hush moving through the crowd like a hand smoothing bedclothes. The lantern-light seemed to lean. Somewhere down the row, Maretta stopped mid-sentence, her fortune-teller's patter dying in her throat.

Ansel looked up from her wax and saw the gray coats coming.

* * *

There were six of them, walking abreast down the market lane with the unhurried gait of people who had never once been told *no*. Ministry inspectors wore no badges; they didn't need them. You knew them by the gray—coats the color of ash, the color of fog, the color of a page after the words have been scraped away—and by the way the crowd parted around them without being asked, the way water parts around a keel.

They carried iron braziers on poles, coals already glowing, and behind each inspector shuffled a clerk with a ledger and a long-handled rake for raking fire through whatever needed burning.

"Charm-sellers," said the lead inspector, and his voice wasn't loud, but it carried the way cold carries through a wall. "By order of the Ministry of Unmaking, all mnemonic wares are hereby declared contraband. Sellers will present licenses or present themselves."

The market did not run. That was the terrible thing about it, Ansel thought later. Nobody ran. Running was a confession, and confession was how people got unmade. Instead the whole night market went still and quiet as a held breath, hundreds of vendors and buyers standing among their little illegal comforts while the gray coats moved down the rows.

Ansel's hands were steady. She noted that about herself with a kind of distant pride, the way you might note the weather. Her hands were always steady. It was the rest of her that wanted to shake apart.

Think. *Think.* The inspectors would work the near end of the lane first—that was procedure, she'd seen sweeps before, though never one this size, never one aimed at the whole market at once. She had perhaps ten minutes. Her stock was in the tray under the counter, wrapped in oilcloth. The wax would burn; wax always burned; that was rather the point of wax. But the memories inside wouldn't burn. They'd scatter, loose in the air, homeless, and loose memories were exactly what Ministry clerks collected with their long-handled rakes. Every memory she'd ever lifted, every charm she'd ever pressed, would end up in a Ministry ledger, catalogued, and somewhere in those ledgers would be the thread that led back—

No. Not back to her. She had been careful. Eleven years careful. Never stealing twice from the same person, never stealing grief, never letting anyone see her take, keeping her own past locked so deep behind her own eyes that even she sometimes doubted it existed. No one remembered Ansel Ishikawa well enough to describe her to a clerk. That had been the whole architecture of her life: to be forgettable by design.

But the map.

The map was sewn into the lining of her coat, against her ribs, where it had lain for six days now like a coal that wouldn't cool. The dying courier's blood was still stiff in its folds. And the courier had died in her cellar, and death left traces, and the Ministry could smell a trace the way sharks smelled blood in water.

She hadn't connected it until this moment. She should have. Six days ago a man had bled out on her floor whispering about a wrong moon, and she'd buried him under the flagstones and told herself the matter was closed, and all week the market had felt normal, and normal was a drug. Normal made you stupid.

The sweep was coming. Ten minutes. Maybe less.

"Maretta," she said quietly, not looking up from her wax. "How much for the reading?"

Maretta's eyes flicked toward the gray coats and back. "Girl, are you mad? Now?"

"Now is precisely when I want my fortune told." Ansel slid two coins across the counter. "Tell me something true."

Maretta took the coins because taking coins was older than fear. She turned her palm up, and Ansel laid her hand in it, and the old woman leaned close as if divining, though what she actually whispered was: "Back tunnel, past the tooth-puller, through the tanner's yard. It floods at high tide, so go *now*, girl, and don't come back tonight."

"I can't leave my stock."

"Your stock is wax." Maretta's grip tightened, surprisingly strong. "You are not wax. Go."

But Ansel had already done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said otherwise. Under the counter, wrapped in oilcloth: forty-one charms. Forty-one stolen moments belonging to forty-one different people, none of whom could be traced to her individually—but gathered together, burned together, raked together into a single clerk's ledger? Forty-one threads. Threads could be woven. She had spent eleven years making sure no one held more than one thread of her. She would not hand the Ministry a whole tapestry.

Besides—and here was the thought she refused to examine too closely—those weren't just wares. That was a shepherd's daughter's snow. That was a widower's wedding dance. That was a lullaby some foundling boy had paid good coin for and hadn't yet opened. If the Ministry burned them, those moments wouldn't return to their owners. They'd be confiscated. Filed. Erased twice: once from the rememberer, once from the world.

Ansel Ishikawa had rules. Never steal grief. Never steal twice. And—new rule, invented on the spot, the best rules always were—never let the Ministry have anything of yours, even if it was already somebody else's.

"Thank you for the reading," she said to Maretta. "It was very accurate."

* * *

What followed took four minutes and cost her everything, and she would remember the cost far longer than the four minutes.

She worked fast and low, below the counter line, where the lantern shadows pooled thickest. Wax melts at the warmth of a determined palm; that was the craft of it, the secret she'd never taught anyone. You cupped a charm in your hand and thought at it—not words, nothing so tidy, just the shape of *release*—and the seal softened and the memory rose out of the wax like breath off warm water.

She couldn't put them back. That was the other law of her trade, the one nobody advertised: a memory, once lifted, didn't return to its old home. It could only be given somewhere new. So she gave them to herself.

Forty-one charms. She pressed snow into her mind and it fell, brief and blinding, across a hillside she'd never walked. She pressed the wedding dance into her bones and felt an old man's hands on an old woman's waist, felt the fiddle scratch and the barn boards bow, felt love so worn-in it had become a kind of silence. The lullaby she saved for last, and when it settled into her she had to stop, just for a moment, forehead nearly touching the counter, because it was someone's mother's voice, low and cracked at the edges, singing words in a language she almost—*almost*—recognized, and there was no reason on earth a foundling's charm should feel like a door opening in a wall she'd bricked up decades ago.

"Next," said a voice directly above her.

Ansel looked up into the face of a Ministry clerk.

He was young. That was the worst of it—young enough that the gray coat hung on him like a borrowed future, young enough that his eyes still did what eyes were supposed to do. He was looking at her stall, at the empty display rows, at the single charm she'd left sitting out as bait because a stall with nothing to sell drew questions and a stall with one item drew none.

"The mnemonic ware," he said. "Surrender it."

"This?" Ansel picked up the last charm—a nothing-charm, a filler, a stranger's memory of a hot meal on a cold day, worthless, anonymous—and turned it in her fingers. "It's a keepsake. My grandmother's. There's hardly anything left in it."

"Hardly anything is not nothing." He held out his hand, palm up, and the gesture was so bureaucratic, so absurdly gentle, that she almost laughed. Behind him, three stalls down, the first brazier was doing its work. She could hear the wax crackling, smell the sweetish reek of burning memory-husks, hear Maretta answering questions in a voice sanded flat with terror. The rake-clerk stood by with his long pole, ready to gather whatever drifted loose from the flames.

Ansel put the charm in the young clerk's hand.

Their fingers touched around it, briefly. And because she was who she was—because lifting was as natural to her as breathing, because eleven years of discipline apparently meant nothing when a door presented itself—she took something in the same instant she gave.

Just a sliver. A hair-thin thread off the surface of his mind, the kind of lift so shallow the victim never missed it, never even felt the tug. She hadn't planned it. It was reflex, the thief's equivalent of flinching.

What came across was an image: a courtyard. Gray walls. A line of children in gray coats reciting something in unison, and the young clerk among them, mouthing words he didn't understand, while an instructor wrote a name on a slate and then, deliberately, slowly, wiped it away with a damp cloth. Wiped it and wiped it and wiped it until the slate was clean, and all the children nodded as if they'd learned something, and the young clerk had felt—this was the part that lodged in Ansel's chest like a splinter—the young clerk had felt *relief*.

She blinked. The vision folded itself away into her crowded, borrowed interior, and the clerk was already turning away, dropping the charm into a collection tin, calling for the next seller.

Ansel packed her empty trays with shaking hands. Not fear-shaking. Something else. Because she had just looked through a window into the Ministry's nursery, and she had seen where hunters came from, and the map against her ribs suddenly weighed as much as a country.

* * *

She didn't use Maretta's tunnel. Tunnels had exits, and exits had watchers, and watchers had ledgers. Instead she did the thing she was best at in all the world: she became forgettable.

Shoulders down. Eyes soft. Gait adjusted to the rhythmless shuffle of a woman with nowhere to be and nothing worth noticing. She joined the thin stream of vendors packing up early—there was always a stream; sweeps cleared markets the way weather cleared streets—and drifted toward the canal stairs with the crowd's slow current carrying her along.

At the top of the stairs she allowed herself one look back.

The night market was burning. Not the stalls—the Ministry didn't burn stalls, stalls were taxable—but the wares. Braziers glowed at intervals down the lane like a string of malignant lanterns, and around each one clustered a knot of gray coats and weeping sellers. She watched a fortune-teller's cards go into the fire, watched a bone-reader's rack of relics, watched a man beg—not for mercy, for his merchandise, clawing at a clerk's arm and being pried off with the patient firmness of men handling livestock. The smoke rising off the braziers had a strange quality she'd noticed once before, years ago, from a distance: it didn't rise straight. It curled, hesitated, moved against the wind in little spirals, as if the memories cooking out of the burning wax were trying to find their way home and couldn't.

Loose memories. Homeless ones. The rake-clerks swept them up like leaves.

And then, because the universe was not finished teaching her lessons, she saw *him*.

He stood at the far end of the lane, apart from the sweep, not directing it, not participating in it. Just watching. Tall, gray-coated like the others but wearing the gray differently—the way a sword wears a scabbard. He wasn't looking at the fires. He was looking at the crowd, at the faces, moving along the fleeing vendors one by one with the unhurried attention of a man reading a book he'd read many times.

His gaze reached the foot of the canal stairs.

Reached her.

Ansel had been looked at by dangerous men before. She knew the taxonomy: lust, greed, suspicion, the dull appraisal of a buyer sizing up goods. This was none of those. This was recognition. His eyes passed over fifty faces and stopped on hers the way a compass needle stops, and even across the length of the burning market she felt the contact like a finger laid flat against her sternum.

He tilted his head. Slightly. Almost courteously. As if to say: *there you are.*

Then he turned and spoke to one of the inspectors, and the inspector turned and began, unhurriedly, to walk toward the canal stairs.

Ansel went down the steps into the dark.

* * *

The Sump Canal at night was a black ribbon stitched with garbage and secrets, and she ran along its towpath with her heart slamming and the forty-one stolen memories churning inside her like ballast in a storm-tossed hull. Snow, dance, lullaby, sea, hot meal, first kiss, a dog's wet nose, a father's shoulders, sunlight through beer, the smell of a print shop, rain on a tin roof—they knocked against each other in her mind, forty-one lives jostling for room in a skull built for one, and beneath them all, quiet and patient as sediment, the dead courier's last hours lay where she'd buried them six days ago.

*The moon is wrong,* he'd whispered. *Thirteen. There were always thirteen.*

She reached her cellar entrance—an unlocked hatch behind a derelict boathouse, hidden under rotting netting—and paused with her hand on the frame, listening the way animals listen, with her whole skin.

Nothing. No footsteps on the towpath. No gray coat descending.

But she knew—knew with the cold certainty of a woman whose entire life had been an education in being hunted—that knowing the way here was no longer safe. The hunter at the market's end had *seen* her. Had marked her out of a crowd of hundreds with a tilt of his head. Whatever careful architecture of anonymity she'd built over eleven years, whatever walls of forgettability, whatever locks on her own past—he had looked through all of it in one glance, the way she could look through a cheap charm and see the memory inside.

Which meant he had something of hers already. Some thread. Some fragment. Enough to recognize her on sight in a crowd, in fog, in the dark.

Enough to hunt her.

Ansel slipped down into her cellar, barred the hatch, and lit no lamp. In the darkness she pulled open the floor cache and took out the map, unfolding it by touch alone, feeling the courier's dried blood crackle along its folds like autumn leaves.

Six days she'd told herself the matter was closed. Six days she'd pretended a dying man's desperation was somebody else's problem, that a map to a door out of an erased country was a curiosity, a burden she might sell or bury. Tonight the Ministry had burned her livelihood to ash and sent a hunter whose gaze had found her like a hook finding flesh, and the pretense was over.

The map had marked her. Not with ink—with consequence. And there was nowhere in Vell, nowhere in the Sumps, nowhere in the whole gray breadth of the city where a marked thing could hide from the Ministry of Unmaking.

Except, perhaps, the place the map led to.

Ansel sat in the dark with the bloodstained map spread across her knees, and outside, faint through the earth, she could hear the canal lapping at its stones, and farther off, the bells of Vell ringing midnight in their usual twelve—twelve strokes, twelve moons, twelve tidy lies—and she understood, finally and completely, that her invisibility was dead, and that the only road left to her was the one drawn in a dead man's blood.

Tomorrow she would need help. Someone who could read what she couldn't, carve what she couldn't, see what she couldn't. Someone forgotten enough to be safe and strange enough to be useful.

She knew exactly one such person.

Osk the letter-carver was going to be very unhappy to see her.
