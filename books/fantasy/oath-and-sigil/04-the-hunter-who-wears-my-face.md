## 4. The Hunter Who Wears My Face

The Ministry of Unmaking kept its hunters in a building that had no address.

Corvane had worked there for eleven years, and still, when he walked to it each morning through the fog off the Sarnwater, he had to find it by feel—the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. The street was called Nothing Row on no map at all; the door was iron, unmarked, warm to the touch in winter as though something behind it breathed. He had long ago stopped asking what warmed it. Curiosity was a debt the Ministry collected with interest.

Inside, the halls were lit by lamps that burned without oil, and the walls were hung with sigils of containment rather than decoration—grey geometry that hummed faintly, like wasps dreaming. Corvane passed them every day and felt them pass over him in turn, weighing him, finding him light enough to keep.

He was light enough to keep because he carried almost nothing of his own.

That was the bargain every hunter made. The Ministry did not trust men who remembered themselves; a man with a past had loyalties, and loyalties bent like green wood under pressure. So when Corvane had signed his oath—ink that drank into the skin and never surfaced again—he had surrendered his history into Ministry keeping, and in exchange they had issued him other men's memories, curated, licensed, fitted to him like armor plates. A soldier's courage, so he would not flinch. A magistrate's patience, so he would not rush. A fisherman's knowledge of tides, a locksmith's feel for tumblers, a mother's tenderness—for those rare occasions when tenderness was the more efficient tool.

He wore them the way other men wore coats. And like a coat worn long enough, they had begun to fit him so well he sometimes forgot they were not his skin.

* * *

The summons came before dawn, delivered not by messenger but by the lamp above his desk turning the color of an old bruise. Corvane set down the report he had been reading—a charm-seller in the Tallowmarket, arrested with forty stolen griefs sewn into her hem—and went down three flights to the Vault of Holdings.

The vault-keeper was a woman named Sereth who may once have been beautiful and now was simply precise. She kept the Ministry's confiscated memories in wax and glass, row upon row of small amber lights, and she knew Corvane's file better than he did, which was to say she knew it entirely, since he knew none of it.

"You're being issued a supplement," she said, without greeting. "For the Ishikawa pursuit."

"Ansel Ishikawa." He said the name carefully, tasting its edges. Three days since the courier had died in a cellar off Cordwainer's Stair, and the name had already crossed four desks. "The charm-thief."

"The map-thief." Sereth drew a case from beneath her counter—black lacquer, sealed with a sigil that squirmed when looked at directly. "What we recovered from the courier's effects included certain... resonances. Memories he'd been carrying. They've been cleaned and pressed." She slid the case across. "You'll want them integrated before you go into the field. The quarry is skilled. She moves through Vell the way water moves through cloth."

Corvane turned the case in his hands. It was heavier than it should have been. "Whose memories?"

Sereth's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Does it matter?"

It should have mattered. He knew it should have. But eleven years in the Ministry's service had taught him that questions were doors best left shut, and so he signed the receipt, and took the case upstairs, and did what hunters do.

* * *

Integration was done lying down, in a chair shaped like a confession booth, with a crown of thin silver needles resting against the scalp. The Ministry's technicians called it *fitting*. The older hunters, the ones who had begun to fray at the seams, called it *dressing wounds that aren't yours*.

The first memory came in like weather.

Rain on a tin roof—but not rain, sugar-salt, someone shaking a paper cone of candied nuts over a brazier, and a woman laughing, and the laugh was—

Corvane's hands closed on the arms of the chair.

The laugh was *familiar*.

Not familiar the way a stranger's voice is familiar, the way all laughter rhymes. Familiar the way your own name is familiar. He knew the exact pitch of it. He knew that if he heard it from across a market square, through crowd-noise and cart-rattle and the cry of gulls off the Sarnwater, he would turn his head.

The technician droning somewhere above him: "Breathing steady, pulse elevated but within tolerance. Continue."

The memory unfolded. A courtyard behind a dyer's shop, sheets of indigo cloth hanging like captured sky. A girl of perhaps nine years crouched at the edge of a rain barrel, and a boy beside her—both of them watching a beetle swim its desperate circle. The girl reached in and lifted the beetle out on a leaf, and set it on the stones, and said, with the absolute gravity of childhood: *"It wasn't doing anything wrong. It just got lost."*

And the boy had laughed—that same laugh, high and bright—and Corvane realized with a lurch that went through him like cold water down a well:

*I am the boy.*

Not in fact. Not in flesh. Some other child, some licensed donor whose history the Ministry owned outright. But the memory had been *worn*, worn long and deep by whoever had held it before it came to Corvane, worn until the edges softened and the wearer's presence soaked in like sweat into a collar. Someone had lived inside this afternoon so thoroughly that their attention remained, a ghost-shape pressed into the scene: the warmth of the sun on a particular shoulder, the smell of indigo and wet stone, the fierce private joy of sitting next to this girl, this specific girl, as though proximity to her were itself a kind of wealth.

And the girl—

The girl grew up. The memory rippled forward the way dreams do, seasons folding into seasons, and the girl became a young woman leaning over a workbench pressing wax charms by candlelight, her fingers quick and sure, her face in profile lit gold, and Corvane thought, with a certainty that arrived whole and terrible as an egg dropped on stone:

*Ansel Ishikawa.*

She had been a child in someone's memory. She had been a child in *his* memory, or near enough to his that the difference was a legal fiction. Somewhere in the Ministry's holdings there was a donor file, a number, a man or woman who had grown up beside Ansel Ishikawa in a courtyard behind a dyer's shop—and the Ministry had taken that afternoon, cleaned it, pressed it, and buttoned it onto Corvane's chest like a medal.

"Integration complete," the technician said. "How do you feel?"

Corvane sat up slowly. The needles withdrew with a sound like rain ending.

"I feel," he said, "like I've known her my whole life."

The technician wrote something down. It did not occur to the technician that this might be a problem.

* * *

Vell in the rain was a city of grey silk, and Corvane moved through it the way he had been trained to move through everything: without disturbance. He wore a porter's coat and carried a porter's load—brass fittings, harmless—and he walked the Tallowmarket with his head down and his borrowed eyes up.

The market smelled of tallow and frying onions and the river's iron breath. Charm-sellers lined the arcade under awnings beaded with rain, their wares laid out on velvet gone bald at the corners: little wax seals, each one holding a pressed memory, sold to buyers who wanted an hour of someone else's courage or a stranger's first snow. Illegal, all of it, since the Edict of Wholeness—but Vell had always understood that law was a tide, and tides could be waited out.

Corvane was not here for the sellers. He was here for the shape of one seller's absence.

He stopped at a stall where an old man sold charms pressed into river pebbles instead of wax, and bought one—a cheap thing, a memory of standing in a wheat field at dusk—and while he paid he let his gaze travel the way porters' gazes traveled, nowhere in particular.

"She's not here tonight," the old man said, unprompted, because old men in markets read coats better than coats read them. "The girl. The good one. Three nights now."

"I don't know any girls."

"Everyone knows some girl." The old man wrapped the pebble in paper. "Ansel. Best hand in the market. Never steals grief, never steals twice. You can tell a thief's character by their rules, and hers are good rules." He leaned close, conspiratorial, breath like chicory. "They say the Ministry's after her. Say she's carrying something that burns."

Corvane pocketed the pebble. "What do you say?"

"I say I liked her father," the old man said, "and I say whatever she's carrying, she didn't ask for it. There's a difference between guilt and weight." He waved Corvane off with a wet rag. "Go on. Deliver your brass."

Corvane walked on. Rain ticked on the awnings. And in his chest, in the newly fitted memory, the girl in the courtyard lifted the beetle out of the barrel on its leaf and said, *it wasn't doing anything wrong, it just got lost,* and the words landed in him now with a different weight than they had in the fitting chair. Before, they had been charming. Now they were evidence. Now they were a verdict being drafted against her, and he was the clerk of it.

*It wasn't doing anything wrong.* The Ministry would not care. The Ministry did not deal in wrongness; it dealt in *excess*. A person who remembered too much was not guilty, merely overfull, and the remedy was not punishment but reduction. Like bleeding a fever. Like pruning a tree.

Like taking a beetle out of a barrel, he thought, and hated himself for the analogy, and could not stop making it.

* * *

That night he worked from the safehouse on Bellfounder's Lane, spreading out the courier's recovered effects on a table under a single lamp: a boot heel worn at the outer edge, a stub of red sealing wax, a scrap of oilcloth that had once wrapped something folded and important. The map itself was gone—taken, according to the file, by persons unknown within hours of the courier's death. Persons unknown. As if the cellar had been empty except for a dying man and the dark.

But memories leave residue the way fire leaves smoke, and the Ministry's instruments were exquisitely tuned to smoke.

Corvane laid two fingers on the boot heel and closed his eyes and let the resonance rise.

A road. Night. The particular ache of feet that have walked too many hours. A city wall ahead, black against a sky where the moon hung wrong—not full, not crescent, but *thirteenth*, a moon that existed only in the erased country's old reckoning, and the courier's heart hammering with a fear that had nothing cowardly in it, only urgency, only *I must reach her before they reach me*—

Her. The courier had been carrying the map to a her. To a charm-seller with good rules and quick hands, because she was the only person in Vell who could press a memory into wax without cracking it, and the map was a kind of memory, and it needed preserving, and—

Corvane opened his eyes. His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with detached interest, the way one watches another man's hands.

There was a protocol for this. When a hunter's issued memories began generating unauthorized responses—grief, loyalty, recognition—the protocol was to report to Sereth for adjustment. Adjustment meant sanding down the offending plate of armor until it fit flat again. Adjustment meant losing the courtyard, the indigo sheets, the beetle on its leaf, the girl's grave voice saying lost things deserved carrying home.

He did not report it.

He told himself it was tactical. The resonance was useful; a hunter who understood his quarry's childhood understood her patterns, her haunts, her likely refuges. That was sound doctrine. That was even true.

It was also a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and he filed the lie away with all the others, in the drawer where he kept the things he could not afford to look at: the question of whose memories these had been before they were his; the question of why the Ministry had chosen *this* donor for *this* hunt, out of all the courtyards in all the cities of the world; the question, vast and cold as the sea, of whether the choice had been an accident at all—or whether somewhere in the Ministry's grey geometry, someone had decided that the most efficient way to catch Ansel Ishikawa was to send a man who loved her.

He blew out the lamp. In the dark, the thirteenth moon seemed to hang behind his eyelids, wrong and patient.

* * *

He found her trail on the fourth night, and lost it on the fifth, and found it again on the sixth, and each time it was the same strange experience: pursuing a woman through the streets of Vell by the light of a childhood he had never lived.

She left traces the way memory leaves traces—faint, deliberate, deniable. A charm-seller's network was a web of favors, and favors leave fingerprints. A wax-worker buys her stock somewhere; a fugitive eats somehow; a girl raised behind a dyer's shop knows which cellars flood and which stay dry. Corvane followed her not with hounds or informers but with the accumulated tenderness of strangers' recollections, stitching together her route from the memories of everyone she had ever touched: a baker who remembered a pale girl buying bread with coins counted twice; a ferryman who remembered a passenger who paid in silence and stared at the water as though it owed her an apology; a child on Cordwainer's Stair who remembered a woman who fixed his broken spinning top and told him, gravely, *things that fall aren't broken, they're just resting sideways.*

Each fragment fit the courtyard. Each fragment fit the girl lifting the beetle free. And each fragment, logged properly, was another entry in the ledger that would end with her name struck from every record and every mind in Vell—including, if the Ministry had its way, the mind of the hunter who had assembled it.

On the sixth night, from a rooftop across the Tallowmarket, he saw her.

Only for a moment. She moved along the arcade's shadow-line with a bundle under her arm and her hood up, and the lamplight caught her profile for the space of a single heartbeat—and Corvane's borrowed memory rose in him like a tide meeting its moon, and he knew the exact angle of that jaw, knew how it would look gold-lit over candle-wax, knew the pitch of the laugh that lived one inch behind her closed lips.

He could have signaled. He had a whistle in his pocket that would bring six men out of the dark. One breath, one note, and the hunt would end in the arcade among the awnings, clean and bureaucratic.

Ansel Ishikawa paused at the mouth of the arcade—paused as though she felt his attention on her back, the way skin feels weather coming—and looked out at the rain, and pulled her hood lower, and slipped away into the city that was trying to forget her.

Corvane stood on the rooftop with the whistle silent in his fist, rain running off his chin, and admitted the thing he had been refusing to admit since the fitting chair:

He did not want to catch her.

He wanted to warn her.

And far below, in the drowned lamplight, the girl who had once said *it just got lost* vanished around the corner of Cordwainer's Stair, carrying a map to a door that should not exist—and behind her, patient as arithmetic, the Ministry began to wonder why its best hunter's reports had grown so strangely, so uselessly, gentle.
