## 5. Thirteen Turns

The map had not let her sleep.

Ansel had tried everything she knew—wrapping it in oilcloth and burying it beneath the loose flagstone by her cot, singing the small dulling song charm-sellers used to quiet restless goods, even, in a moment of desperation, holding it over a candle flame to see if it would burn like an ordinary thing. It would not. The paper drank the fire's heat and gave back nothing, cool as river stone, and when she snatched it away the flame had bent *toward* it, hungry, as if the map were the more alive of the two.

So at grey dawn, with Vell still wrapped in the particular silence that came before the fish-carts and the temple bells, she climbed the crooked stairs out of her cellar and went looking for Osk.

* * *

Osk lived above his shop on Tallow Lane, in a room that smelled of cedar shavings and hot wax and the faint iron tang of chisels kept too long against skin. He was already awake—Osk was always already awake, though he claimed he never slept so much as *waited*, lying in the dark listening to the letters settle into their shapes. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, sixty if he was a day, with a beard like untended moss and eyes that had gone white as skimmed milk twenty years ago. The Ministry had taken his sight. That was the story everyone told, anyway: that he had carved a name they didn't want carved, and the punishment had been elegant—they hadn't blinded him for what he'd done, but for what he might do again, since a man who couldn't see letters couldn't carve them.

Except Osk had learned to read with his fingers, and then to read without fingers at all, the way a spider reads a web.

"You're breathing wrong," he said before she had finished knocking. "Sit down before you fall down, girl."

"I need you to look at something."

"I don't look at anything. That's rather the point of me." But his hands were already out, palms up, waiting, and Ansel crossed the little room and laid the folded map into them as gently as if she were setting down a sleeping animal.

She watched his face while he read. She had watched a hundred faces take in a hundred stolen memories—the drunk merchant's slack wonder, the widow's terrible gratitude—and she thought herself fluent in the language of faces. But Osk's face did nothing. His fingers moved over the paper in slow spirals, tracing lines she could not feel from where she stood, and his expression stayed smooth and closed as a pond in winter.

"Where did you get this?" he said at last.

"A man gave it to me. He was dying."

"Men who are dying give away many things. Most of them are worthless. This—" His thumb stopped at the center of the page and pressed there, hard, until the paper dimpled. "This is worth dying for too. Whose blood is on it?"

She hadn't noticed the blood. Now that he said it she could smell it, old iron under the wax smell of her cellar. "His. I think. Osk, what is it?"

He didn't answer right away. He turned the map over, ran his fingers along its edges, held it up near the window as though light might tell him something his hands could not. Then he set it flat on the table between them and said, in the voice he used for prices and other unpleasant truths:

"It's a letter, Ansel. A very large one. Written very small."

* * *

He made her tea while he gathered himself, which was Osk's way of being kind—he could not bear to hand someone bad news with empty hands. The tea was bitter, oversteeped, the color of strong regret. Ansel wrapped both palms around the cup and did not drink, and Osk sat across from her with the map between them like a third guest neither wanted to speak to.

"The paper," he began, "isn't paper. Or it is, but it's been treated—steeped in something, ash and tallow and I'd guess grave-dirt, though don't ask me whose. That's why it won't burn. Things soaked in memory don't burn easy. You know that better than most."

"I know that," she said quietly. Her charms were pressed from the same principle: memory made solid enough to hold a shape.

"And the ink isn't ink. Run your finger over it—no, lightly, *lightly*, girl, this isn't a thing you want soaking into your skin." He guided her hand himself, caught her wrist, drew her fingertips across the map's surface. Under her touch the lines rose faintly, like veins under skin. Warm. Faintly warm, the way a cheek is warm.

"What am I feeling?"

"Writing. Real writing, underneath the picture. What you can see with your eyes—that's the top layer, the decoy. Roads, rivers, a border drawn where no border's been drawn in thirty years. Pretty nonsense. But underneath, pressed into the fibers themselves, there's text. Hundreds of lines of it. Thousands." His white eyes fixed on her with unsettling accuracy. "It's a petition, Ansel. Or a plea. Or a list. I can't read all of it—not here, not fast—but I can read enough."

"Enough for what?"

"Enough to know why the man who carried it was bleeding." Osk leaned back. Somewhere below them, Tallow Lane was waking; a cart rattled past, and the smell of frying onions climbed the stairwell. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary smells. Ansel clung to them the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood. "This map doesn't lead anywhere. It *argues*. Every line of it is a name—names of streets, names of people, names of things that officially do not exist. And at the center, written largest, repeated thirteen times like a bell being struck—"

He stopped. His jaw worked.

"Say it," Ansel whispered.

"Cael Sarn," Osk said. And the word went through the room like cold water through cloth. "The capital. The country. The whole swallowed thing, all of it, named over and over, because whoever wrote this believed that a name said often enough becomes a door."

Ansel's mouth had gone dry around the bitter taste of the tea. She had grown up in Vell hearing Cael Sarn spoken of the way one speaks of a scandalous relative—in whispers, in jokes that weren't jokes, in the careful silences of older people who sometimes started a sentence with *back when* and then, catching themselves, poured tea instead. The Ministry called it nothing. Officially, geographically, legally, there was a stretch of empty land east of the mountains, sparsely settled, of no strategic interest. There had never been anything else there. To say otherwise was to invite a visit from men in grey coats with kind voices and ledgers full of your neighbors' names.

And now she was sitting in a blind carver's attic holding a document that said otherwise in every fiber of its being.

"Why give it to me?" she asked, and hated how small her voice came out. "I'm nobody. I sell other people's yesterdays to drunks. Why would a courier die to put this in my hands?"

Osk was quiet a moment. Then he reached across the table and found her hand, his fingers rough as bark, and turned her palm up, and pressed the map's corner into it.

"Because you're a keeper," he said. "That's what you are, whether you admit it or not. You've spent years keeping things safe that didn't belong to you. Someone knew that. Someone looked at the whole city of Vell and picked the one person whose whole trade is *not losing what she's given.*" He almost smiled. "Terrible luck, being good at the wrong thing."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and into the afternoon, and the light moved across the attic floor in a slow gold bar while Osk taught her how to listen to a piece of paper.

It was not reading, exactly. It was closer to the way she lifted memories—a matter of attention, of leaning close without touching, of letting the object speak first. Osk showed her how to warm the map against her cheek (it grew warmer the longer she held it, and once, briefly, it seemed to beat), how to find the places where the hidden text thickened, how to follow a line of names with one fingernail and feel where the nail snagged, because important words were written deeper, cut into the paper like scars.

"There," Osk said, when her nail caught at the map's heart. "There. That's the Door."

"There's nothing there. It's blank."

"Blank to the eye. Press harder. Read it with your skin."

She did. And under her fingertip, faint as a pulse, she felt it: a circle of dense, interlocking script, ring within ring within ring, thirteen rings in all, each one finer than the last, and at the very center a single word worn nearly smooth by handling—handled so many times, she realized, by so many desperate fingers, that the writing itself had begun to fade, loved half to death.

"What does the center say?"

Osk's hand covered hers. Together they traced it, letter by slow letter, and he spoke the syllables aloud like a man walking on ice.

"*Thirteenth Moon,*" he said. "The Door of Thirteenth Moon. And around it, the conditions. Listen, because I'll only say this once and you will not write it down." His grip tightened. "The Door exists. It has always existed—it's older than the Ministry, older than the erasure, maybe older than Vell itself. But it only opens when the moon completes its thirteenth turn. Not the twelfth. Not any turn a calendar will admit to. There's a moon that comes after the last moon, girl. Small, wrong-colored, visible only from certain ground, and the Ministry has spent thirty years pretending it isn't there. The couriers used to call it the Liar. When the Liar rises full—when the thirteenth turn closes—the Door opens. For one night. Perhaps less."

"How long?" Ansel asked. "Until it turns?"

Osk tilted his head, listening to some inner almanac. "The Liar runs slow. Last cycle, the smugglers on the east road spoke of it in autumn. If the rhythm holds—" he counted on his fingers, lips moving, "—nineteen days. Twenty, if it stumbles."

Nineteen days. Ansel looked at the window, at the ordinary gold light, at the smoke rising off Tallow Lane from a hundred ordinary chimneys, and felt the number land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. Nineteen days in which she had to cross a country that did not exist, following a map she could not read with her eyes, carrying a dead man's purpose and a dead man's blood, while the Ministry—which had already burned her market stall, already questioned her neighbors, already, she was sure, begun asking after a charm-seller with clever hands—looked for exactly her.

"They'll be watching the roads," she said.

"They'll be watching everything. They always are." Osk gathered the map, folded it along its soft-worn creases, and pressed it back into her hands. "But here's what they can't watch, Ansel. Here's the part that should frighten you worse and comfort you more, both at once." He leaned in, and she smelled the cedar on him, the wax, the long patience of him. "A door that opens for a name. That's what this says. The Door doesn't open for keys or coins or bloodline. It opens for *claiming*. Anyone who stands before it on the thirteenth night and speaks their true name—their whole name, the one the Ministry's lists never got, the one their mothers used—walks through. That's the whole of the lock. That's the whole of the key."

"And the Ministry knows this?"

"The Ministry's whole empire is built on making sure no one remembers they have a true name to speak." Osk sat back. "You've seen what they do to people who remember too much. You've sold charms to the ones who escaped with fragments."

She had. She had pressed those fragments into wax and sold them back to their owners, who wept to hold even a splinter of themselves. She had told herself it was commerce. She had told herself a great many things.

* * *

She stayed until dusk. Osk fed her bread and hard cheese and talked, in his sideways way, about the old days—the days before, when Tallow Lane had run all the way down to a river gate and the river gate had opened onto a road that went east, and the road had gone somewhere, and somewhere had a name. He spoke of it without drama, the way a man describes weather, and that undid her more than weeping would have. Grief worn smooth by decades is heavier than fresh grief. It has had time to settle.

At the door, as she pulled her hood up, he caught her sleeve.

"One more thing. The text at the center—it repeats. Thirteen times, I said. But the thirteenth repetition is different. Shorter. Older handwriting, I think, though my fingers aren't what they were." He recited it slowly, giving each word its weight:

"*What was taken together cannot be returned alone.*"

"What does that mean?"

"If I knew, I'd be younger and probably dead." He smiled, but it didn't reach his voice. "Means what it says, likely. Means the Door opens for one, but whatever's behind it—whatever was erased—it takes more than one to bring back. Go carefully, girl. And go soon. The Ministry burns what it can't read, and it's been burning a lot lately."

* * *

The walk home took her past the market square, and she could not help slowing, could not help looking.

They had left the scorch marks. That was the cruelty of it—the stalls were gone, the awnings, the little folding tables where she and a dozen others had laid out their wares in rows of wax and bone and ribbon, but the stone flags still bore the black outlines of where the fires had been, like chalk drawings of absence. A few sellers stood at the edges of the square, talking low, pretending to shop. No one was selling. No one dared.

And at the far side, posted on the notice board beside the curfew edicts, a new proclamation, fresh ink, still shining:

*By order of the Ministry of Unmaking: information concerning false geography is sedition. Possession of seditious documents is unmaking. Citizens observing unusual lunar phenomena are reminded that there are twelve moons.*

Twelve moons. Ansel read it twice. Around her, other citizens read it too, and she watched them do the arithmetic she was doing—the counting backward, the furtive glances at the sky, the quick dropping of eyes. The Ministry had just told the entire city, in the act of denying it, that there was something in the sky worth denying.

*They're afraid,* she realized. Standing in the scorched square with a dead man's map folded against her ribs, she felt the thought arrive whole, like a memory she had stolen from someone braver. *They've been afraid for thirty years. All that power, all that grey machinery of forgetting—and they are afraid of a moon.*

Nineteen days. Twenty, if it stumbled.

She pulled her hood lower and walked, and for the first time since the courier had died in her cellar whispering about the sky, the dread in her chest had a companion: a small, stubborn, dangerous warmth, like a coal cupped in bare hands. Purpose. She barely recognized it. It fit badly, the way borrowed clothes fit badly.

Behind her, at the edge of the square, a man in a grey coat unfolded a ledger and began, politely, to ask questions.

Ahead of her, east beyond the walls, past the checkpoints and the empty land that officially contained nothing, a drowned village waited on the road she would have to take—and Ansel Ishikawa did not yet know its name, but the map did, and tonight she would press her fingers to its fibers and make it tell her.
