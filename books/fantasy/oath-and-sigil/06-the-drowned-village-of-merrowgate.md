## 6. The Drowned Village of Merrowgate

The map told her the name before the road did.

Ansel had walked half the night through the empty country east of Vell—the land that officially contained nothing, where the Ministry's cartographers left blank paper and the Ministry's inspectors left burned farms—and when dawn came up grey as dishwater, she stopped at a crossroads marker to rest her feet and unrolled the courier's map across her knees.

The ink had shifted again. It did that; she'd learned it did that, the way a sleeping animal shifts its weight. The road she'd followed was drawn in careful strokes, but beneath it, fainter, like writing seen through wet cloth, another line ran north by northeast toward a smudge of coastline. And beside the smudge, in a hand older than the map's own, someone had written: *Merrowgate.*

She said the word aloud, just to feel it in her mouth, and the wind off the flats seemed to lean away from her, as if even the air had been instructed not to remember.

*Merrowgate.* Not on any chart sold in Vell. Not in any ledger she'd ever lifted a memory from. A village that existed only because a dead man's map insisted on it—and because, three hours later, cresting a low ridge of gorse and broken wall, Ansel saw it with her own eyes.

It lay in a shallow bowl of land that sloped down to what should have been sea but wasn't. The water had come partway and then, apparently, thought better of it. Half the village stood above the tide line, roofs sagging, chimneys leaning like drunks holding each other up. The other half stood *in* the water—whole streets submerged to their second stories, windows dark under a hand's depth of green glass, a church spire tilted at an angle that hurt to look at, rising out of the shallows like a needle stuck in the world's flesh.

And the water itself was wrong. Not seawater. It was too still, too clear, too warm-looking for the season, and it had no smell. Water that has drowned a village smells of rot and salt and old wood. This smelled of nothing at all, the way a room smells after the Ministry has visited it. Scrubbed. Unmade.

Ansel crouched at the ridge top and watched, and her thief's eye did what it always did: it read the place like a mark reading a crowd.

Smoke from two chimneys in the dry half. Laundry on a line—a woman's shift, a child's small coat, both mended many times. A boat, absurdly, tied to a fencepost forty yards from any water, as if waiting for a flood that had either already happened or never would. And figures moving: three, maybe four, going about the slow business of survival, hauling nets that came up empty, stacking driftwood that wasn't driftwood—it was door timber, she realized, planks with hinges still attached, salvaged from the drowned houses and burned for heat because there was nothing else.

They were living inside the evidence of what had been done to them, burning it to stay warm.

Her chest did something complicated and painful. She named it hunger, because naming it grief would have required admitting she had anything to grieve for, and Ansel Ishikawa had spent eleven years building herself out of other people's memories precisely so she wouldn't have to.

She went down anyway. The walls of Vell were behind her, and Corvane—or whatever the man in the grey coat was called—would not stop at the checkpoints forever. A drowned village full of people who remembered wrong was as good a hiding place as the empty land offered, and better than most.

* * *

The first person she met was mending a net on the shingle above the tide line, and he asked her a question that made her blood run cold in the ordinary way, and then a second question that made it run cold in an entirely new one.

"Come about the fish?" he said, not looking up. He was old, brown as rope, fingers quick despite their knots of arthritis. "We don't have fish. Haven't since—" He stopped. Frowned. His hands kept working without him, looping twine in patterns worn into the muscle. "Haven't since the flood."

"There was a flood?" Ansel asked carefully.

"Course there was a flood." He looked up then, mild and certain. "Nineteen years back. The sea came up the valley in a single night, quiet as a held breath. Took the lower town. Drowned the church with the congregation still at evening prayer." He said it the way you'd recite your own birthday. Then his brow creased, and something flickered behind his eyes like a candle in a draft. "Except," he said slowly, "there wasn't any flood."

Ansel went very still.

"I mean—" The old man's hands had stopped. He stared at them as if they'd betrayed him. "I mean there *was*, I watched it, I carried my mother up the hill on my back, I heard the bells ringing underwater for three days afterward, everyone heard them—" His voice climbed, cracked. "But there's no sea there. Look at it. There's no sea. There's never been a sea. Merrowgate is a farming village, it's always been a farming village, we're nine miles inland, the sea is—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "The sea is somewhere else. The sea is a story. Why do I remember a flood?"

He lowered his hands and looked at her, and his face was the loneliest thing she had ever seen outside her own mirror.

"You're a charm-seller," he said suddenly. "I can tell. You've got the look. They all had the look, the ones who used to come through before the inspections started." He grabbed her wrist with shocking strength. "Tell me which one is true. You steal memories—you'd know. Tell me if I'm mad."

And here was the thing Ansel Ishikawa had rules about. Never steal grief. Never steal twice from one person. Never, ever, tell a mark the truth about what they were feeling, because the truth was worth money and money was survival.

She knelt down on the shingle next to him, and broke every rule she had except the ones that mattered.

"Both are true," she said quietly. "That's how they do it. They don't take the memory out. They put a second one in beside it, one that contradicts, and let the two of them grind against each other until people stop trusting their own heads. It's cheaper than erasure. Erasure leaves holes. Holes get noticed." She had learned this in fragments, over years, from the minds of frightened officials she'd skimmed in the night market—one of the few categories of knowledge her trade gave her that she'd have paid to forget. "You're not mad. You're *occupied*."

The old man stared at her. Somewhere out in the still green water, or in the memory of water, a bell began to toll—faint, arrhythmic, coming from no direction. Three days, he'd said. The bells rang for three days.

"They did this to all of us?" he whispered.

"All of Cael Sarn," Ansel said, and only after she'd said it did she realize she'd spoken the country's name aloud, the forbidden one, the one that got mouths washed out with lye in Vell's schoolrooms and got adults a visit from men in grey coats. It felt enormous in her mouth. It felt like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

The old man didn't flinch. He closed his eyes and breathed the name in like a man breathing the smell of bread after a fast.

"Cael Sarn," he repeated. "Yes. Yes, that's—I knew that. I knew that this morning and I'll know it again tomorrow and every day in between I'll lose it and find it and lose it." He opened his eyes. "Who are you, girl?"

"Nobody," Ansel said, out of habit, and then, because the word tasted different here, "Ansel. My name is Ansel."

* * *

They fed her barley soup and smoked eel—which, the old man, whose name was Tam, admitted cheerfully, also existed and didn't exist, depending on which history you were standing in—and she learned the shape of Merrowgate's ruin.

There were perhaps thirty residents left. Once there had been four hundred. Some had drowned in the flood that never happened; some had simply walked out one morning toward Vell and never come back, and their neighbors couldn't agree whether they'd died or been taken or had never existed at all. The Ministry's inspectors came twice a year, in spring and autumn, and the villagers had learned the choreography of survival: hide the boats, hide the bells, hide the children who remembered too loudly, and answer every question with whichever history the questioner seemed to prefer. They had gotten very good at it. Good enough that it had begun to eat them.

"We keep two of everything now," said a broad-shouldered woman named Maren, who ran what passed for authority in the village and had the eyes of someone who hadn't slept properly in a decade. "Two calendars. Two sets of graves—we dig graves on the hill for the flood-dead, and we tend the farm plots for the families that supposedly moved to Vell. Two histories, side by side, like a house with two kitchens. You cook in whichever one isn't being watched." She laughed, briefly, horribly. "My daughter thinks the drowned church is beautiful. She goes out in the shallows and puts flowers on the roof. Her brother says she's mourning nothing. They fight about it. They're twins, Ansel. Born the same hour, and one of them grew up grieving and one of them grew up scoffing, and neither of them will speak of it anymore because there's no winning."

Ansel sat with the soup cooling in her hands and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down her spine, exactly what she was looking at.

This was what the Ministry did. Not fire, not knives—those were for emergencies, for people like the courier who bled out in her cellar clutching a map. For everyone else, the work was slower and kinder and infinitely worse. They let you keep everything. They just made sure none of it agreed.

And the grief—she set the bowl down because her hands had begun to shake—that was the cruelest cut of all. In Vell, grief for Cael Sarn was illegal, so nobody grieved, and the not-grieving had built up over nineteen years like pressure behind a dam. But here, grief was permitted and *pointless*. Here you could mourn openly, and the object of your mourning sat in front of you every day, half-drowned and half-denied, so that your sorrow had nowhere to go. You couldn't even have the clean pain of loss. They'd taken that too.

A whole country, taught to stand at its own funeral and argue about whether anyone had died.

"I'm sorry," Ansel said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded.

Maren shrugged, a big tired motion. "Sorry doesn't drain the water. Sorry doesn't fix the bells." She glanced toward the window, toward the still green expanse. "Though they've been louder lately. The bells. Last few weeks. Tam says it means the flood is trying to finish. I say it means something else is coming." Her eyes came back to Ansel and sharpened. "You're running from something. Don't insult us by saying you're not—we know the look of hunted better than anyone alive. What's coming?"

Ansel weighed the truth against the habit of concealment, and found, to her surprise, that the scales had shifted. These people lived inside the Ministry's worst work. They had earned the truth more than anyone in Vell.

"A map," she said. "To a door. The last way out of Cael Sarn—for those of us who still remember there's anywhere to get out *of*."

The silence that followed had texture. Tam's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. From the next room, a child's voice—singing something, a skipping rhyme, and Ansel caught the words and felt the floor tilt: *thirteen turns and the moon comes home, thirteen turns and the door is stone—*

Children's rhymes survived everything. Even rewriting. Especially rewriting; the Ministry had never figured out how to search a nursery song.

"The thirteenth moon," Tam whispered. "My grandmother sang that. Said the Door opens when the false moon finishes its turn. We thought it was a story to make children sleep."

"It's on my map," Ansel said. "Weeks yet. Maybe more."

Maren leaned forward, and for the first time her exhaustion burned off and something harder showed underneath, something that had been waiting nineteen years for a reason to exist. "Then you'll need help getting there. The flats between here and the capital ruin are watched, and the bridge—" She stopped herself, glancing at Tam, some shared caution passing between them. "There are things you'll need to know. Things we remember that the maps don't show."

"And why would you help me?" Ansel asked. It was her rule: never trust kindness without knowing its price. "What do you want?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Outside, faint and patient, the drowned bell tolled again, and this time Ansel could swear she felt it through the floorboards, through the bones of her feet, a vibration with no source in the physical world.

"We want to be mourned properly," Maren said. "One time. Before the end. If your door opens—if there's a world past the edge of the blank paper—then somebody, somewhere, will remember Merrowgate as it actually was. Both histories. All of it. That's our price. Carry us out with you." Her voice roughened. "Because we can't leave. Believe me, we've tried. The road won't hold us. Whatever they did to the land here, it won't let us cross the border of our own village. We are *rooted*, girl. So you'll be our legs."

Ansel looked around the low room—at Tam with his two histories grinding behind his eyes, at the mended laundry, at the boat tied to a fencepost waiting for a sea that was a story—and felt something crack open in her chest that she'd welded shut eleven years ago in a cellar much like her own.

She had spent her whole life taking memories. Pressing them into wax, selling them cheap, keeping her own locked in a box so deep she'd sometimes wondered if it was even there anymore.

These people were handing her theirs freely. Asking only to be carried.

"I can't promise—" she began.

"We know," Maren said. "Promise anyway. It's what oaths are for."

So Ansel Ishikawa, who dealt in borrowed things, who had never given anything away in her life, took the village's grief into her hands like a charm of solid gold, and swore.

* * *

She slept that night in a dry attic bed that smelled of lavender and brine, and dreamed—not of her own past, which stayed obediently locked, but of the dead courier. He was walking ahead of her through waist-high water that didn't wet him, and every few steps he turned and pointed, patiently, the way you'd guide a child, and though his mouth never moved she understood him perfectly:

*Not far now. Keep the bells on your left.*

She woke before dawn to shouting.

For one merciful second she thought it was the twins arguing about the church. Then she heard the word they were shouting—heard Tam's voice cracking across the shingle, calling her name, calling *Ansel*—and she was up the ladder and out the door with her knife drawn before her mind caught up with her body.

The eastern sky was doing something impossible.

Where the horizon should have been flat and grey, a light was rising that was not the sun—pale, silver-green, deliberate, climbing with the unhurried confidence of something that owned the sky. And beneath it, out in the still water of the flood that never happened, the surface had begun to glow from below, street by drowned street, window by drowned window, as if the village under the water had lit its lamps for the first time in nineteen years.

Tam stood at the tide line in his nightshirt, pointing with a shaking hand, and when he turned to her his face was streaming and radiant and terrified all at once.

"The bells," he said. "Listen."

And Ansel listened, and heard them clearly now, dozens of them, pealing out from under the water in joyous, reckless, unmistakable celebration—and understood, with the certainty of a thief recognizing another thief's work, that the thirteenth moon had risen early.

Something had changed the count. Something—or someone—was hurrying the door open.

Or hurrying the hunt to its end.
