## 7. Company of the Forgotten

The road out of Merrowgate was a road only in the way a scar is skin. It ran along the drowned fields where the Ministry's rewriting had broken the land's memory of itself—half the wheat standing gold and upright, the other half dissolved into grey reed-water that whispered when the wind crossed it. Ansel walked the dry half. She had learned that much from the dead courier lodged behind her eyes: walk the remembered ground, never the forgotten.

Osk walked beside her, his carved staff tapping out the world in small knocks. The blind letter-carver had refused to stay in Merrowgate the moment she'd told him what the map meant. "I've spent forty years cutting names into stone," he'd said, shouldering his pack of chisels before she'd even finished asking. "You don't get to tell me there's a country I'm not allowed to have cut them into."

Behind them came the boy.

Pip had simply appeared at the village edge as they left, barefoot on the cold causeway stones, carrying nothing but a dead sparrow he cupped in both hands like something that might still be persuaded to live. He was perhaps nine, thin as a reed, with the enormous unblinking attention of a child who has learned that adults lie about important things.

"You're going to Cael Sarn," he said. Not a question.

Ansel stopped. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody." He shrugged. "The water remembers it. When the tide comes up over the churchyard at night, you can hear the old names under it. Merrowgate used to be called something else, before. Everyone here pretends they don't know, but they hum it when they think no one's listening. It goes—" and he hummed three notes, low and round, and Ansel felt the hair rise on her arms because she knew the tune. She had never heard it before in her life, and she knew it the way you know your own heartbeat.

"You shouldn't know that," Osk said quietly. His blind face turned toward the boy with terrible gentleness. "Child, if the Ministry heard you humming that—"

"They already took my mother," Pip said, matter-of-fact, the way children report weather. "She remembered too much. They came at dawn and she wasn't there anymore, but her shoes were, so I kept them." He lifted the sparrow. "Can I come? I remember things. That's useful."

"It's not useful," Ansel said. "It's fatal. There's a difference."

"Then I'll be fatal with you." He fell into step behind them, small feet slapping stone, and did not ask again, because he understood—as she had not, at nine, as she still barely did—that some questions are doors you don't knock on twice.

* * *

They made camp that first night in the shell of a tollhouse whose roof had been unmade but whose walls stubbornly remained, standing in perfect outline like the idea of a house rather than a house. Rain came through where the roof wasn't. Ansel built a fire anyway, in the corner where the walls still held their memory of shelter, and the smoke rose straight and strange, as if the absent beams were still guiding it upward.

Pip slept instantly, curled around the sparrow, breathing in the deep rhythm of a child who trusts the world more than it deserves.

Osk sat sharpening a chisel against a whetstone, the sound soft and endless as surf. "You didn't say no to him," he observed.

"I said no."

"You said no and then you let him follow us for six miles."

Ansel fed a stick to the fire. "He'd have followed anyway. Children like him—" She stopped. She had been a child like him once; she had been exactly him, memorizing forbidden things in a city that punished memory, learning to hide what she knew inside a smile. She had survived by becoming a thief of other people's pasts so no one would ever look for her own. "Children like him die alone," she finished. "It seemed wasteful."

Osk's whetstone paused. "That's the coldest kindness I've ever heard."

"It's the only kind that holds."

But she looked at the sleeping boy a long time after that, and the firelight moved on her face, and whatever she felt she filed away in the locked room behind her eyes where she kept everything that could hurt her.

* * *

Brava found them two days later, or let herself be found—Ansel was never sure which, and with Brava it mattered.

They heard her before they saw her: a single note of a bone whistle, high and clean, from the ridge above the old canal road. Then a woman dropped down the scree with the casual violence of someone who had spent years treating gravity as a suggestion. She landed in front of them, rolled, came up standing, and grinned.

Brava was broad and sun-browned, with a deserter's cropped hair growing back in uneven tufts and a Ministry-issue sword worn wrong—slung across her back like a farm tool, hilt toward her left hand, which told Ansel she'd killed men with it and hated every time. Her uniform coat had been dyed brown and then, apparently, attacked with mud again for good measure.

"Deserter," Brava announced cheerfully, before anyone asked. "Fourth Cohort, Unmaking Auxiliary. Ran eight months ago. You're the charm-thief from Vell, that's the carver, and that's the doomed child. Yes?"

"How—" Ansel began.

"The whole border country talks about you. A woman walking east with a map the Ministry would burn a city for. You're about as subtle as a funeral." Brava's grin faded into something sharper. "I want in."

"No," Ansel said.

"Yes," Brava said.

"She can't come," Ansel told Osk flatly. "She's Ministry-trained. She knows how we move, how we camp, how we think. If they've turned her—"

"They haven't turned me. They *hunted* me." For the first time the grin dropped entirely, and underneath it Brava's face was older and much more tired. "Eight months, madam thief. Do you know what an Unmaking Auxiliary does? We're the ones who go door to door after the inspectors. We find the people who hid during the sweep—the grandmothers who kept letters, the fathers who taught their kids the old songs. And we take them somewhere, and afterward there's one less chair at the table, and everyone in the house agrees there was always one less chair, because we make sure of it. We make *sure* of it." Her hand had drifted to the sword without her seeming to notice. "I ran because I made sure of it four hundred and eleven times, and I counted, because counting was the last part of me they hadn't figured out how to use."

The wind moved through the reed-water below the road, whispering its two histories.

"I can fight," Brava said. "I can read Ministry sign, I know their rotation schedules, I know which officers are corrupt and which are worse. And I know what happens to deserters they catch, which means I have precisely nothing to lose, which makes me the most reliable person you will ever meet." She spread her hands. "Or I follow you anyway, at a distance, being annoying. Your choice."

Ansel looked at Osk. The old man's blind face was unreadable, but he said, mildly, "We'll need someone to stand between the child and trouble. I carve letters. The lady steals. Neither of us is any good in a brawl."

"We're not a company," Ansel said. "We're not anything. We're four people walking the same direction until it stops being convenient."

"Sure," Brava agreed easily, falling into step. "Whatever helps you sleep."

* * *

It was Pip, of all of them, who broke the friction open—and it happened because of the sparrow.

On the fifth evening, in the ruin of a waystation chapel, the boy buried it. He dug a small grave in the dust with his hands, laid the stiff little body in, and covered it, and then he stood over the mound with his fists at his sides and said, furious and wet-eyed, "It deserved a name. Everything deserves a name. Even sparrows. Even—" and his voice cracked, "—even my mother, who doesn't have one anymore because they took it, and nobody will say it, and I'm the only one who remembers it and if I forget it she'll be gone *twice*—"

He stopped himself, clamping his mouth shut, remembering—always remembering—that saying the name aloud was dangerous.

The silence in the ruined chapel was total. Even the rain seemed to hold off the broken roof out of respect.

Then Brava knelt beside the little grave. "What was the sparrow's name?" she asked gravely.

Pip blinked at her. "...Sergeant Bramble."

"Sergeant Bramble," Brava repeated, solemn as an oath. "A fine soldier. Died with his boots on." She saluted the mound. Osk, feeling his way over, laid a hand on the boy's head. "I'll cut him a marker," the old man said. "Small stone. 'Sgt. B. He sang at dawn.' Every name wants a stone, lad. That's the whole of my trade, when you boil it down."

Pip looked at them—these three strangers kneeling in the rain-dust around a sparrow's grave—and something in his face unclenched for the first time since Merrowgate. He didn't quite smile. But he breathed.

And Ansel, standing apart by the doorway with her arms crossed, felt the locked room behind her eyes strain against its hinges. *This is how it starts,* she thought. *This is exactly how it starts. First a grave for a bird, then a fire shared, then a name given freely, and then they can hurt you, and then they can be taken from you, and then you are the woman in the market again pressing grief into wax because you swore you'd never carry your own.*

But she stayed in the doorway. She didn't leave. She told herself it was because leaving would mean crossing the rain, and that was a lie, and she let it be one.

* * *

Trouble found them on the seventh day, as trouble does, wearing the Ministry's grey.

They were crossing the flats near a place the map marked only as *the long silence*—a stretch of country where the rewriting had been so thorough that even the reeds wouldn't grow—when Brava suddenly whistled the alarm note and pulled them all down into a drainage culvert. Through the crack above, Ansel watched a Ministry patrol pass: six inspectors in grey, and behind them a caged cart, and in the cart—

Charm-sellers. Three of them, hooded, wrists bound. One was Maren, who worked the fish stalls in Vell, who had bought stolen memories from Ansel a dozen times, who had once traded her a memory of the sea for a memory of snow because each wanted what the other had.

The cart rattled past. Maren's hood slipped for a moment, and her eyes swept the culvert crack, the flats, the horizon—and Ansel would have sworn, would have staked her life on it, that the woman looked directly at her hiding place for one long second.

And said nothing.

The patrol passed. The sound of wheels faded into the long silence.

In the dark of the culvert, Pip was shaking—not with fear, Ansel realized, but with rage. "They're going to unmake her," he whispered. "She saw you. She *saw* you and she didn't tell, and they're going to unmake her for a stranger."

"That's not how it works," Brava said softly. "That's exactly backwards, lad. She didn't tell because you're not a stranger. Nobody in this country is a stranger anymore. That's the one thing the Ministry never understood." She looked at Ansel in the culvert's gloom, and her voice went quiet and hard. "Four hundred and eleven chairs. I keep telling myself I'm done counting. But I count her now too. So we move fast, we move quiet, and we get where we're going before they give her a reason to regret her silence. Understood?"

Ansel understood. What she hadn't expected—what she was still turning over hours later as they moved through the dusk—was the warmth of it. Four people moving together, watching each other's blind spots, a deserter ranging ahead and a child riding on Osk's shoulders asking questions about constellations that the old man answered with the old, forbidden names of stars. Somewhere in the last seven days, without her permission, the walking had become a *we*.

That night they made camp early, in the lee of a fallen boundary stone whose inscription had been chiseled away—but Osk ran his fingers over the gouges and smiled his terrible gentle smile.

"This was a border marker," he said. "Cael Sarn's eastern bound. They chiseled the country's name off the stone, but stone keeps the shape of what was cut from it. Feel—" He guided Pip's small fingers into the scars. "There. Letters under the letters. The Ministry can burn paper and unmake people, but stone remembers slowly. Stone is patient."

"What did it say?" Pip asked.

Osk's mouth worked silently for a moment, reading the negative space. "It said," he pronounced at last, and his voice caught on it, "*Here begins Cael Sarn, and all who enter are home.*"

No one spoke. Above them, through the torn clouds, the false thirteenth moon was rising—a pale smear of light where no moon should be, weeks yet from full, but climbing.

Pip pointed at it. "It's getting brighter," he said. "Every night. My mother said—" He stopped, jaw tightening, guarding the name again.

"Say it," Ansel heard herself say.

Everyone turned. She hadn't meant to speak. But the words were out, standing in the firelight like a stray animal she couldn't call back.

"Your mother's name," she said. "Say it. We won't tell. That's the point of us now, isn't it? That's why you're here." Her voice was rougher than she intended. "Somebody should remember her out loud."

Pip stared at her. The fire popped. Far off, the reed-water whispered its two histories, and the false moon climbed, and the boy opened his mouth—

—and Brava's hand closed gently over his shoulder, and her eyes, fixed on the darkness beyond the fire, had gone very still.

"Hold that name a moment longer, lad," she murmured. "We've got company coming. A lot of it. And I think—" she drew her sword with a sound like a held breath released, "—I think it already knows we're here."
