## 8. Charms and Confessions

The fire was small because small fires could be lied about.

Ansel had built it in the cupped hands of a ruined dooryard, three days out from Vell's walls, where a farmhouse had been unmade so thoroughly that only its kindness remained—a hearthstone, a chimney breast, the ghost of a herb bed still breathing thyme into the dark. The Ministry's rewriting had taken the walls and the roof and the family that had lived there, but it had left the thyme. Ansel did not know whether that was mercy or oversight, and she suspected the difference did not matter to the thyme.

They had walked all day through country that remembered wrong. A river ran where the map showed none; a road ended in a wheat field that no one had planted, the grain growing in rows too straight for accident. Twice they had passed milestones carved with letters that slid away when looked at directly, like fish under ice. Pip had read one aloud anyway—"Cael Sarn, four leagues"—and then clapped his hands over his mouth as if the words might be taxed.

Now the boy slept against Brava's side, his head tipped back, his mouth open, his fingers still curled around the stub of chalk he carried everywhere. The deserter sat with her sword across her knees, not because she expected trouble but because her hands did not know what to do without it. And Osk, blind as a closed book, sat closest to the fire, his scarred palms open toward the warmth, reading it the way he read everything.

"It's time," Osk said.

Ansel looked up from the map—or from the folded square of leather that had been a map before Osk had shown her it was also a sigil, and would be again. "Time for what?"

"Payment." He turned his face toward her. Firelight moved across the burn-scars on his cheeks like water. "You said passage. You said you'd take us to the Door of Thirteenth Moon. Well. Nothing moves toward that Door for free. Not even us."

"I'm not charging you."

"No." Osk smiled, and there was something old and sad in it. "That's exactly what I mean."

* * *

She should have seen it coming. She saw everything coming; that was her trade, her craft, her whole careful architecture of survival. She watched hands, not faces. She counted exits. She noticed when a drunk merchant's gaze went soft and inward, when his grief rose close enough to skim. But she had not been watching tonight. She had been watching the fire, which was a mistake, because fire made everyone look honest.

"Here's how it goes," Brava said. She spoke the way she did everything—flatly, efficiently, like loading a crossbow. "Osk explained it while you were scouting the road back there. We each give one true memory to the journey. Freely given, freely held. The land takes what's offered and lets us pass. That's the old law, isn't it? Before the Ministry. Before the rewriting."

"That's superstition," Ansel said.

"Everything true is superstition now," said Osk mildly. "It's why they burned the word."

"The land doesn't take payment. The land doesn't take anything. It's *gone*, most of it. Rewritten down to the stones." She heard her own voice sharpening and hated it. "The bridge we crossed this morning only stood because four people believed in it. Do you know what happens when the fifth person doubts? I do. I watched Pip nearly fall through the middle of a miracle."

"And yet," Osk said, "here we are. Past the border wards. Past the first of the drowned villages. Past things I can hear breathing in the hedges at night that have no business breathing." He spread his hands. "Something is letting us through, Ansel Ishikawa. Something is counting."

The use of her name landed like a dropped coin. She flinched before she could stop herself—her name, spoken aloud, in open country, under an open sky—and Osk heard the flinch the way he heard everything.

"You don't have to give yours," he said gently. "No one's asking for your name. Just a memory. One truth, freely surrendered, to bind the company. That's the custom. That's the *oath*. The old carvers used to say a company without shared truths was just a crowd waiting to scatter."

Brava snorted. "I've marched in companies bound by oaths. They scattered too. But fine." She shifted, and the sword slid off her knees onto the ground with a sound like a verdict. "Me first, then, since everyone's staring."

* * *

Brava stared into the fire for a long time. Ansel realized, watching her, that the deserter was doing what Ansel herself always did before a theft: choosing. Selecting the thing to be lifted with care, testing its weight.

"When I was nineteen," Brava said at last, "I stood guard on the wall at Kel Doreth. Night watch. Rain coming down so hard the torches drowned in their brackets, and I remember thinking—I remember this exactly—that the rain sounded like applause. Like the whole sky had come to watch me do my duty and was pleased."

Pip stirred against her side but didn't wake.

"There was a woman on the far road. Just walking. No lantern, no pack, walking through that rain like it was weather happening to someone else. Orders were orders. Challenge, then warning shot, then arrow. I challenged. She kept walking. I fired the warning shot and she kept walking, and I remember thinking, *she's deaf, or she's mad, or she knows something about these arrows that I don't.*"

Brava's voice didn't change. That was the terrible thing about it. It stayed level as a ledger line.

"So I put the third arrow into her. At eighty paces, in that rain. I was the best shot in the garrison; that wasn't pride, it was arithmetic." Her hand found the sword hilt on the ground beside her and gripped it. "She fell. And when we went out at dawn to collect the body—for the report, for the tally—there was nothing there but a bundle of washing. Someone's laundry, blown off a line. Blue cloth. I told myself it was blue cloth the whole way home and for six years after, until the night I walked out of the garrison gate and kept walking, no lantern, no pack, through weather that was happening to somebody else."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark a nightbird called, or something shaped like a nightbird's call.

"So that's my payment," Brava finished. "That's the truest thing I own. Take it. Carry it to the Door if you like. It weighs less than the arrow did."

Nobody spoke. Pip, half-waking, pressed his face into her sleeve and slept again, and Brava let her hand rest on his hair as though it had always meant to be there.

* * *

"My turn." Osk turned his scarred palms over, up to the light, as if showing them empty. "Mine's smaller. Most true things are."

He had been a letter-carver in Vell for thirty years, everyone knew that much. What no one knew—what Ansel felt settle into the circle now like a stone into a well—was why he worked blind.

"I wasn't born blind. I was born seeing, in a house behind the tannery, fourth of six children. And when I was nine years old I had a brother, Tammas, who was eleven, who could whistle any song after hearing it once and who once convinced the entire street that the moon had fallen into the reservoir and that the water glowed because of it. Half the neighborhood came down with lamps and buckets. My father was furious. My mother laughed until she cried." Osk's voice was steady, unhurried, a man reading an inscription he had carved himself long ago. "Tammas died of the fever when I was twelve. And I loved him so much that every morning, waking, the first thing I did was look for him. The empty bed. The silence where the whistling should be. Grief is mostly looking, did you know that? Looking and not finding. Thirty times a day, for years, I looked for a dead boy."

He drew a slow breath.

"So when my eyes started failing—young, too young, some wasting of the nerve—the physicians offered their tonics and their leeches, and I thanked them, and I went home and I did not use them. Because I understood, even then, what I'd lose and what I'd be rid of. Blindness took the looking from me. Took the empty bed. Took the doorway where he wasn't. Now I carry him whole instead—in sound, in whistles I can't stop remembering, in the glow of a lie about the moon that half a street believed." He folded his hands. "People think I gave up my sight for my grief. I traded my grief for everything else. It was the best bargain I ever struck, and I have never once told anyone, because it is a shameful thing to say out loud: that losing a sense can be a kind of mercy."

The thyme breathed in the dark. Pip was fully awake now, sitting up, watching Osk with the fierce attention of a child who hoarded other people's truths the way other children hoarded marbles.

"You gave it away easy," Pip said. "Both of you. Like it doesn't cost."

"Costing's the point, lad," said Osk.

"No." Pip pulled his knees up to his chin. He was small for ten, small enough that the firelight made him seem younger, except for his eyes, which were old the way wells are old. "No, because then it has to be fair. If everyone gives a real one." He looked at the fire. Then, quickly, before he could lose his courage—the way children dive into cold water—he said:

"I remember my mother's face."

Silence. Brava's hand stopped moving on his hair.

"They took my mother when I was five. Ministry men, gray coats, gray carts, and afterward nobody in our building remembered her—not the neighbors, not the landlady, not my father, who woke up the next day asking why there was a child in his house and whose he was. Everyone. Except me. I was too little for them to bother with, or I was lucky, or whatever I am that I don't got a name for. And I remember her. I remember her face. I have remembered her face every day for five years and I never told anyone, because the one other boy who remembered his sister got taken away too, and I figured—" his voice cracked, and he pushed through it, furious at the crack— "I figured remembering quietly was staying alive. So. There. That's my payment. My mother's face. Freely given." He swiped his sleeve across his eyes. "And it costs *everything*, and I'm sick of carrying it alone, and if the land wants it, the land can bloody have it, except I hope it doesn't, because it's mine."

Then he burst into tears, and Brava gathered him in without a word, and over the boy's bowed head her eyes met Ansel's—flat, level, merciless as a ledger line—and asked the question the whole circle was asking.

* * *

Ansel looked at the fire.

She knew every trick of this moment. She had built this moment, sold it a hundred times in a hundred forms: the circle, the warmth, the confession, the sweet ache of giving something true to strangers. She had harvested confessions by the basketful. Drunk merchants weeping over dead fathers. Wives telling fires their husbands' secrets. She knew better than anyone alive that a memory given freely was worth ten taken, because the giver polishes it beforehand, turns it over, wears a groove in it—and a grooved memory carries better, sings truer, binds tighter than any stolen thing.

She knew all of this. And knowing it, she still could not make her mouth open.

Because her memories weren't grooved. They weren't worn smooth by handling. They were locked—sealed behind sigils of her own devising, wax and iron discipline and thirty-year-old terror—and the one time she had gone near the vault door, even in thought, even now, she felt it: the pressure behind it, the weight of everything she had buried to survive. Her childhood in a country that officially did not exist. Her mother's voice saying words in a language the Ministry had outlawed. The night the gray coats came to her street in Cael Sarn, and the cellar, and the smell of the wax she had pressed over her own memories like a charm against herself.

If she opened that door—even a crack, even one memory—one thread would pull loose, and then another, and she had spent her whole life being the woman with no past precisely so that no hunter could find a handle to grip.

"I don't pay," she said.

The words came out flat. She made them flat on purpose. Flat was armor.

"This isn't Vell market," she said. "There's no toll here. I'm not selling passage; I'm leading it, because I'm the one holding the map, and the map chose me, and that's payment enough. You want to give your memories to the fire, give them. They're yours. But mine are mine, and I'll thank you not to stare at me like debtors."

The silence that followed was worse than argument. Brava looked away first, jaw tight, and pulled her cloak around Pip. Osk simply sat, hands open, face tilted toward her with that terrible gentle attention, the way a man listens to a lock he is learning to pick.

"You misunderstand the custom," he said softly. "It isn't the land that needs feeding, girl. It's the company. Four people who know each other's truths will walk into hell holding hands. Four people with locked doors will scatter at the first shadow, each certain the others will sell them." He paused. "We're not asking to *take* anything from you, Ansel. We're asking to trust you. And trust, unlike charms, cannot be bought. Only given."

"I know how trust works."

"Do you?" It was Pip—red-eyed, furious, five years old inside and ancient outside. "You steal memories for money. You keep 'em in wax like *pickles*. You won't say one true thing. You walk ahead of us and you won't even turn around. How are we supposed to follow somebody we don't know? How's that different from the Ministry? They erase people too. They just use different wax."

"Pip—" Brava began.

"He's right," Ansel said.

She said it before she could stop herself, and hearing it aloud startled her more than the boy's accusation had. She looked at the fire, at the small dishonest warmth of it.

"He's right. I keep doors locked. All of them. Even the ones that aren't load-bearing." Her laugh came out wrong, dry as ash. "You should understand—it isn't distrust of you. It's that I don't know what's behind my own doors anymore. I sealed them so long ago. Some of what's in there... I sealed it because opening it might kill me. Or worse." She met Osk's sightless eyes. "Might unmake someone else."

"Whom?" Osk asked.

And she almost answered. The word rose in her throat—*everyone, all of us, my whole country, that's what's behind the door, that's what the map is*—and she swallowed it, and the swallowing cost her something visible, because Brava saw it, and Brava, deserter, liar-by-trade, murderer-of-laundry, nodded slowly as if a figure had finally come right in a column she'd long suspected.

"Keep your doors," Brava said. "But know this much: when yours opens—and it'll open, doors like yours always break before they're opened—we'll be standing there. Whether you want us or not."

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Ansel sat apart with the map-sigil on her knees and did not sleep.

The fire had burned to a red eye. Above the ruined chimney breast the stars were wrong—not wrong like the moon the courier had whispered of, but rearranged, constellations shifted a hand's width from where any chart in Vell placed them, as though the sky here had been rewritten too and only imperfectly. She found herself tracing the sigil's lines with one fingertip, not reading it, just touching it, the way you touch a scar.

One truth, freely given. The old law. The company bound.

Her hand drifted, without permission, to the inner pocket of her coat—to the place where she kept not the map but the courier's ring, the plain iron band she had taken from his finger before the ground took the rest of him. In her mind, unbidden, his death-memory stirred: the wrong moon rising over a white seam in the air, and his voice saying *tell them the moon is wrong, tell them to count again*—

Hooves.

Far off, back along the road they had traveled, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall: horses. Many. Moving at a walk, unhurried, the pace of hunters who already knew where their quarry slept.

Ansel closed her hand over the ring and listened, and in the space between one hoofbeat and the next she understood two things at once: that the fire's light had carried farther than she'd believed—and that somewhere out in that rewritten dark, wearing a stranger's face and her own stolen childhood, Corvane had finally stopped being tender.
