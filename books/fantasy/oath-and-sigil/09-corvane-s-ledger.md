## 9. Corvane's Ledger

The Ministry of Unmaking kept its prisoners the way it kept its records: in rooms with no windows, filed beneath the earth, catalogued by what they could no longer prove.

Corvane descended into the holding cells of Vell's inspection house at the hour when the lamps above were lit and the city forgot itself into evening. The stairs were worn stone, slick with condensation, and each step took him further from the smell of woodsmoke and frying onions that drifted down from the street kitchens—further from the world of ordinary appetites that he had long ago been excised from, or had excised himself; he was no longer certain which, and the not-certain was a splinter he carried everywhere, working deeper.

He carried a ledger under his arm. Black leather, brass corners gone green with damp, pages ruled in the Ministry's precise hand. It was not an official document. Officially, his work required no ledger at all—the Ministry preferred its hunters to hold their quarry whole in the mind, unmediated, the way a hound holds a scent. But Corvane had learned, across eleven years of unmaking others, that memory was a liar with excellent posture. It stood up straight and told you beautiful stories about itself. So he wrote things down. Names, dates, fragments lifted from witnesses, contradictions flagged in red ink. A hunter who trusted only his own mind, he had decided early, was a hunter waiting to be rewritten without noticing.

The first cell held a woman named Maret Doss, charm-seller, third stall from the fountain in the night market. She sat on the plank bed with her hands folded in her lap like a schoolchild awaiting correction, and when the door opened she flinched at the light before she flinched at him.

"Ministry," said the warden behind him, as if the word were a sentence being passed.

"I know what you are," Maret said. Her voice was dry. "Everyone knows what you are. That's rather the point of you."

Corvane set his stool down across from her, opened the ledger to a fresh page, and dipped his pen. He did this slowly, deliberately, because fear made memories slippery and patience made them pool. "You sold charms in the night market for six years," he said. "You'll tell me about a thief who worked your row. Small woman. Dark hair cut short. Moves like she's apologizing for taking up space."

Maret's mouth twisted. "You want me to remember her for you. That's the trade, isn't it? You take it out of my head and put it in your book, and then I don't have it anymore, and neither do you, really—you have ink."

"Ink lasts."

"So does scar tissue." But she was already talking; they always were, eventually, because the alternative was the quiet room upstairs where the Ministry's inspectors performed their gentler surgery, and everyone in Vell knew someone who had come back from the quiet room smooth-faced and pleasant and wrong. "Her name—I never knew her name. We called her the moth. Because she worked the lamps, see? Always near the lights. You'd catch her out of the corner of your eye and then she'd be gone and something would be missing from you, some little bright thing, and you wouldn't even know what until days later."

"What did she take?"

"Nothing worth prosecuting." Maret laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "That's the joke of it, hunter. She took *evenings*. A merchant's memory of his daughter's wedding—he sold it to her willingly, did you know that? He came to her stall and paid her two coppers to carry it for him because remembering it hurt too much, the girl married off to a fisherman up the coast, and then—" Maret stopped. Her hands unfolded. Refolded. "And then last winter his daughter died, drowned when the ferry went over, and he wanted the wedding back. He went to her stall every night for a week, weeping, begging her to sell it home to him. And she did. For nothing. Gave it back free. I watched her do it. Pressed the wax charm into his hand and told him—" Maret's voice did something complicated. "Told him *grief is heavier than joy, but it's yours*. Those words. I've carried them a year and I don't know why I've carried them, except that they were true."

Corvane wrote: *Returned stolen memory without payment. Reason unknown.* His pen hesitated over the word *unknown*, which was a word the Ministry did not love. He wrote it anyway.

"The Ministry's file says she is a parasite," he said. "A hoarder of other lives. A threat to civic coherence."

"The Ministry's file wasn't there," Maret said, "the night the ferry went down."

* * *

The second cell held a boy of perhaps nineteen, a runner for a charm-broker called Half-Face Tam, and he was terrified in the way of the young, which is to say thoroughly and without any talent for concealing it. His name was Jules Fen. He had been arrested carrying forty-one charms in a false-bottomed satchel, and he confessed to everything immediately and then, when that produced no mercy, began confessing to things he could not possibly have done, hoping volume might substitute for value.

"Sit still," Corvane said, "and tell me about the moth."

Jules told him about the moth. Or rather, Jules told him about a dozen moths, a swarm of contradictory women, and Corvane wrote them all down because that was the discipline—that was the whole discipline, the entire art of hunting a person through other people's memories of them: you gathered every version, however incompatible, and you looked for the shape that survived the contradictions.

To Jules, she was cold. All business, eyes down, never a wasted word, paid fair and expected fair, once turned away a baron's factor for trying to haggle and didn't even look insulted while doing it. To the wine-seller in the fourth cell, she was warm—a woman who bought mulled wine on the coldest nights and always asked after the seller's lame brother, remembered his name, remembered which leg, year after year. To the fence called Half-Face Tam, whose interrogation Corvane conducted personally and at length, she was *dangerous*: the only thief in Vell who had never once been caught, who had walked out of a sealed warehouse with a memory locked in a lead box, who had looked Tam in the eye—Tam, who feared nothing—and said, *I know what you buried under your mother's floor, and I will never say it, and that is why you can trust me.*

Four women. One shape.

Corvane sat in the warden's office afterward with the lamp turned low and read back through his own notes, and the shape that survived was this: a woman who stole grief and gave it back. Who kept rules—never steal sorrow unasked, never steal twice from the same mind—who enforced those rules on herself with a severity that suggested they were not professional ethics but wounds. Who was, by every account, alone. No lover mentioned. No friend named. The wine-seller's lame brother had been the closest thing to intimacy anyone could describe, and that was a transaction conducted across a market stall in four-word sentences.

*Alone by design,* Corvane wrote. *Alone the way a man stands apart from a bridge he does not trust.*

He should have felt satisfaction. The portrait was assembling beautifully. The Ministry wanted a monster and he was delivering them a precise, documented, three-dimensional monster, cross-referenced and initialed. He closed the ledger and heard, in the silence of the office, his own voice saying the words Maret Doss had repeated: *grief is heavier than joy, but it's yours.*

He had never met Ansel Ishikawa. That was the official truth, and official truths were load-bearing; remove one and the whole structure groaned. He had never met her. And yet the phrase lived in his head with the particular texture of a thing once spoken aloud in his own hearing, in a voice he almost recognized, in a season he could not name—summer, or the end of summer, dust and late light and—

He put the ledger away and went up into the night, and walked, because walking was the only prayer the Ministry had left him.

* * *

Vell at night was a city pretending to be whole. Corvane moved through the night market's aftermath—stalls shuttered, ash from the inspectors' burn-barrels still ghosting the cobbles, the smell of charred wax and burnt rosemary where confiscated charms had fed the flames—and he found himself reading the city the way he read the ledger, gathering fragments, testing them against the file.

The file said Cael Sarn had never existed. This was the founding kindness of the Ministry, the mercy at the root of everything: a country had broken itself so badly, in the wars before the walls, that the only cure was forgetting, and the Ministry administered the cure daily, patiently, generation after generation, scraping the wound clean whenever it scabbed. Every citizen of Vell lived inside the treatment. Every citizen was better off. This was not propaganda; Corvane believed it the way he believed the weight of his own boots. He had made hundreds of people better off. He had watched their faces go smooth and easy as he drew the excess out of them—the griefs, the treasons, the unbearable particulars—and he had told himself, every time, *this is the kindest knife.*

But the file also said the moth was a simple parasite, and the moth returned weddings for free.

He stopped at the corner of Tanner's Row, where the burn-barrel had been tipped and swept, and crouched, and found in the gutter seam a scrap of wax no larger than a thumbnail—dregs of a melted charm, half a pressed impression still legible. Not a memory-wax; the color was wrong, too pale, too fine. Sigilwork. He knew sigilwork the way a locksmith knows locks, and this fragment was old work, careful work, the kind of carving the Ministry itself had not produced since—

Since. Since when? The sentence would not finish. There was a door in his mind with no room behind it, and he had walked past that door for eleven years without once putting his hand to the handle, because hunters who opened their own doors ended up in the quiet room, and worse than the quiet room was the discovery that there had never been anything behind the door at all, that the sense of a sealed space was itself the wound, the scar tissue where something had been cut out.

Corvane stood. His knees ached. He was forty-one years old and he could not remember being thirty, could not remember twenty-five, could not remember anything before his induction with the vividness of a thing lived versus a thing told. They had given him his history in a leather folder on his first day: born in Vell, orphaned young, recruited at nineteen, exemplary service. He had read it the way one reads another man's letters. Even then. Even at the beginning.

In the ledger, by lamplight borrowed from a shrine alcove—the shrines were tolerated, empty-eyed gods, worship without memory—he wrote:

*Witnesses describe subject with affection disproportionate to her crimes.*
*Subject returns stolen grief voluntarily. File offers no explanation.*
*File offers no explanation,* he wrote again, and underlined it, and hated the neatness of his own hand.

Then, because the discipline demanded completeness, he added the last entry, the one he had been circling for three days the way a tongue circles a broken tooth:

*Note: witness M.D. reports subject speaks with regional accent inconsistent with Vell districts. Soft vowels. Northern. Consistent with coastal provinces north of the Salt Reach.*
*No such provinces appear in the census.*
*The census is complete.*

He looked at those five lines for a long time. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, the thirteenth moon—the false moon, the Ministry's moon, the pale stitched thing that crossed the sky on its wrong schedule and that no citizen ever remarked upon, because remarking was the first symptom—rose over the wall of Vell like a coin flipped by a god who had already decided the outcome.

*The census is complete.* That was the sentence every child learned before letters. The census is complete; the map is whole; the country is Vell and the Wall and the sea, amen. Corvane had written the sentence himself, in reports, a hundred times. It was a true sentence. It was a stone.

And yet.

He thought of the moth giving back a dead girl's wedding day to a weeping father, and the northern vowels, and the wax fragment in his coat pocket that predated the Ministry's own seal-styles, and he thought—carefully, the way one steps onto ice—one thought:

*A country erased leaves edges. Someone cut from a country remembers the shape of the scissors.*

He closed the ledger. In the morning he would request the deep files, the sealed annexes, the records of what the Ministry had unmade in the founding years—and the request itself would be logged, and the logging would mark him, and he knew that too, and wrote it down as well, because the ledger did not flinch and he had built it precisely so that he could borrow its courage on nights when his own ran short.

Far to the north, past the Salt Reach, past the drowned villages and the bridges that were not there, a woman with soft northern vowels was moving toward a border that did not exist, carrying a dead man's map and a dead man's final hours lodged in her skull like a splinter of moonlight.

Corvane did not know this. But he opened the ledger one last time, and on the inside cover, where he kept the questions he did not dare ask upstairs, he added a line in small, exact script:

*What am I not allowed to remember about the north?*

The ink dried. The false moon climbed. And somewhere in the sealed dark beneath the Ministry, in a drawer no living hand had opened in thirty years, a folder bearing a single burned-off name waited, patient as scar tissue, for the man who had once been a boy next door to come looking for it.
