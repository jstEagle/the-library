## 10. The Bridge That Isn't There

Brava had crossed a hundred bridges in her soldiering years, and she had learned to read them the way other people read faces. A bridge told you what it was made of, how long it had stood, whether it would hold a wagon or only a man on foot. She could hear rot in the creak of planks before she ever put weight on them. She could smell floodwater in the grain of old timber. Bridges were honest things. They held you up or they didn't, and either way they told you so plainly.

The bridge in the gorge below Merrowgate was not an honest thing.

She saw it first from the ridge, and her body refused it before her mind did—a full-body flinch, like stepping into cold water. The gorge was deep, maybe forty paces down to a river that ran white and loud over black stone, and where a bridge should have been there was nothing but air. Then there was something. Then nothing again. It flickered at the edges of her vision the way heat shimmers above summer road-stone, except this was autumn and the morning was cold enough to frost the heather.

"Stop staring," Osk said. He couldn't see it anyway—couldn't see anything—but his carved walking stick tapped ahead of him with the certainty of a man who trusted the ground under his feet more than any god. "You'll make it nervous."

"Bridges don't get nervous."

"This one does." He smiled without humor. "It's made of nerves."

Ansel stood at the lip of the gorge with the map open in her hands, though Brava noticed she wasn't reading it so much as holding it, the way a soldier holds a standard in battle—not for information, but for courage. Pip was pressed against her side, and for once the boy was quiet, which frightened Brava more than anything else that morning. Pip talked when he was comfortable. When he went silent, it meant the remembering had started, the flood of things no child should carry, and he went somewhere inside himself that none of them could follow.

"It's real," Ansel said finally. "It's as real as anything else here."

"That's not comforting," Brava said. "Nothing else here is real either."

They had been three days out of Merrowgate, following the drowned village's contradictory roads into hill country that shouldn't exist. Brava had served in the border garrisons before she deserted; she knew these hills, or she had known them, and the hills she remembered were bare pasture and sheep-walks. These hills had ruins on them. Not broken ruins—*unfinished* ones, walls that stopped mid-course as if the masons had been called away mid-sentence, orchards of trees that bore blossom and dead fruit on the same branch. The Ministry's rewriting hadn't erased Cael Sarn cleanly. It had left the country half-built, like a house abandoned during construction, and everything here existed in the tense between *was* and *wasn't*.

Including, apparently, its bridges.

"The map says cross here," Ansel said. "There's no other way around. The gorge runs twenty miles to the south, and north it goes up into the fells where even the Ministry doesn't go, because—" she paused, checking herself, "—because there's nothing there anymore. Nothing left to unmake."

"So we walk across a bridge made of," Brava gestured at the flickering air, "*opinion*."

"Made of memory." Osk corrected her gently, the way he always did, as if precision were a kindness. "There's a difference. Opinion changes with the weather. Memory holds its shape if enough people carry it. This bridge was built by masons, Brava. Real ones, with real stone and real mortar, and it stood for two hundred years, and thousands of people crossed it, and every one of them carried a piece of it away in their heads. The Ministry burned the bridge. They can't burn what people remember of it. So long as enough of us believe hard enough, the memory holds the shape the stone used to hold."

"And if we stop believing?"

Osk turned his blind face toward the gorge. The wind moved his white hair. "Then we find out what the bottom looks like."

* * *

Brava made them wait until midday, because that was the kind of careful she was. She walked the rim of the gorge for an hour, testing the wind—it gusted up the chasm in irregular breaths that could shove a man sideways—and studying the far side, where the road resumed, plain and gray and mercifully solid-looking. She counted their supplies. She checked Pip's boots, laced them double herself while the boy watched her with those too-old eyes. She did all the small soldier's rituals that kept fear in its cage, and then she ran out of rituals, and there was nothing left to do but cross.

Ansel went first, because it was her map and her errand, and because—Brava was beginning to understand—she punished herself constantly, quietly, in ways she probably thought nobody noticed. She stepped off the solid rim onto the first plank of nothing, and the plank held. It looked like a plank. It sounded like one, a low wooden note under her boot. But Brava could see through it if she didn't look directly at it, could see the river churning far below through the ghost-grain of the wood, and her stomach climbed into her throat and stayed there.

"Keep your eyes on the far side," Ansel called back. "Don't look down. Don't look at your feet. Look where you're going."

"That's not how bridges work," Brava muttered, but she stepped on.

The strange thing—the thing she would think about for the rest of her life, in whatever life she had left—was that it *worked*. The bridge held her. Not the way stone holds you, indifferent and absolute, but the way a hand holds you: actively, with effort. She could feel it thinking. Every plank was a decision someone had once made, a crossing someone had once survived, a mason who had once set that timber and gone home to his supper believing it would hold tomorrow too. The bridge was built out of all of them, layered like lamplight, and it bore her weight the way a story bears a listener—as long as she gave herself to it completely.

The moment she doubted, she felt it thin beneath her.

That was the terror of it. There was no railing. There was nothing between faith and the fall except the quality of her own attention, and Brava had spent six years in the army learning to doubt everything—her officers, her orders, the war itself, until doubting was the only thing she trusted. Doubt had kept her alive through two campaigns. Doubt had made her desert. And now doubt was a hole opening under her feet, plank by plank, every time her mind wandered toward *this isn't real, this can't hold, I am standing on a thought.*

Halfway across, the wind came up the gorge in a long cold gust, and the bridge swayed.

Bridges sway. She knew that. Timber flexes; suspension takes strain. But this bridge swayed like a thing *startled*, like a sleeper flinching from a nightmare, and Brava heard Osk behind her say, very quietly, "Hold it steady. All of you. Hold the shape of it. Stone, gray stone, mortar between, railings on both sides—give it back its railings—"

And the railings came. Brava felt them arrive under her hands, cool and solid, worn smooth by generations of palms, and she nearly wept with relief at touching something—even a remembered something—that behaved like the world was supposed to behave.

Pip was behind Osk, and Pip was singing.

He sang under his breath, some skipping rhyme from a country that officially had never existed, and Brava realized after a few lines that it wasn't nonsense. It was a counting song. Children had sung it crossing this bridge, she understood suddenly, children for two hundred years, clapping time on the railings, and the song was part of the bridge's bones now, part of what held it. Pip wasn't singing because he was scared. He was singing because he was *helping*. He was the strongest of all of them at this, the boy who remembered everything he shouldn't, and his small voice was laying plank after plank of pure belief across the gorge.

Then, near the far side, he stopped singing.

Brava heard it stop—the little tune cut off mid-word—and heard the quality of the silence change, and knew before she turned that something had gone wrong.

* * *

Later, trying to explain it, Pip would say that he hadn't meant to doubt. He'd just looked down. Everyone looks down eventually; the gorge asks you to, politely, patiently, the way a card sharp asks you to pick a hand. And looking down, he had seen the river, really seen it, white water on black stone forty paces below, and a thought had come into his head that was not his own—or maybe it was, that was the horror, maybe all the worst thoughts were your own all along:

*If the bridge is made of memory, whose memory? Mine? I never saw this bridge. I'm not from here. I don't have any stones to give it. What's under my feet is other people's remembering, and other people are dead, and dead people's memories are just stories, and stories don't hold up eight-year-old boys.*

One doubt. One small, reasonable, well-earned doubt.

The plank under his right foot went out.

Not broke—went *out*, like a candle, like a word forgotten on the tip of the tongue, and his foot swung through empty air, and the next plank softened under his left like wet bread, and he dropped to his knees on a bridge that was forgetting itself beneath him, planks guttering out in a spreading ring around his body, and he screamed—not from the falling, he hadn't fallen yet, but from the *unbeing*, the awful specific feeling of a thing ceasing to be believed in while you are standing on it.

Brava moved without deciding to. Six years of drill lived in her legs; she dropped flat, spread her weight, and grabbed for the boy's wrist as the railing beside him dissolved into cold air. Her fingers closed on him. Her whole body slid forward on planks that were going soft as snow going to rain, and the gorge roared up at them, hungry and patient, and she shouted the first true thing she could find:

"Look at me! Not down—*at me!* You're real! I'm real! I've got you!"

But it wasn't enough, she could feel it not being enough, the bridge dying under them both, because her belief was a soldier's belief, rough and rationed, and Pip's doubt was a child's doubt, total and absolute, the way only children and philosophers can doubt.

Then Osk's voice came across the gap, calm as a letter-carver reading an address:

"Pip. Son. Tell me about the door of your grandmother's house."

Pip sobbed. "I—I don't—"

"You do. Blue door. Chipped paint on the left side, where the dog scratched it. Tell me the color of the latch."

"Copper," Pip whispered. His voice shook. "Copper latch. Green where her thumb wore it."

"Green where her thumb wore it," Osk repeated, warm as bread. "That's it. That's exactly it. Now the kitchen floor. What was under your feet there?"

"Flagstones. Cold in winter. There was a loose one by the stove that rocked—"

"Rocked when you stepped on the corner. I know that stone. Every house has one." And as the boy spoke, Brava felt the miracle happen under her own hands: the planks firmed. Not because anyone was thinking about the bridge—they weren't, they were thinking about a blue door and a copper latch and a rocking flagstone in a kitchen that the Ministry had burned out of the world—but because Pip was remembering *something real*, wholly, with a child's totality, and the strength of that remembering poured into the bridge like water into dry ground. The planks came back plank by plank. The railing rose under Brava's elbow, smooth and certain.

Pip crawled the last ten feet on his hands and knees, and Brava hauled him up over the rim onto honest dirt, and held him, and found she was shaking worse than he was.

* * *

They sat on the far side a long time, the four of them, backs to a drystone wall that was mercifully, boringly real. Ansel checked Pip for injuries with hands that weren't quite steady. Osk refilled the water skins. Brava drank tea that tasted of smoke and pine needles and watched the bridge behind them, still standing, faint as breath on glass, spanning a gorge that had no reason to be spanned.

"I keep thinking," she said finally, to no one in particular, "that's what the Ministry never understood. Or maybe it's what they understood best, and that's why they do it. You don't need to burn a country down. You just need everyone to stop believing in it at the same time. One generation that won't teach the songs. One generation that looks down."

Ansel was quiet a moment. "That's why the map matters," she said. "It's not directions. It's a *remembering*. Someone wrote down everything they refused to forget, and drew it, and hid it, and carried it bleeding into my cellar." She folded the map away with the care of a priest handling relics. "We're not carrying a map to a Door. We're carrying proof the bridge can be rebuilt."

Osk laughed softly. "Careful, soldier. Next you'll be saying we're on a pilgrimage."

"Don't tempt me." But Brava looked at the bridge—at the ghost-arch holding its shape over the roaring water because four tired, frightened people had given it their attention and their grief and a child's counting-song—and felt something shift in her chest that she hadn't felt since the day she deserted. Purpose was a dangerous thing to feel. It got people killed. It had gotten her whole company killed, following a purpose handed down from men in clean uniforms.

This purpose was different. This one she'd chosen, plank by plank, with her own feet.

They shouldered their packs as the light went long and gold across the fells. Ahead, the road climbed toward a line of hills where, according to the map, the ruins grew thicker and the remembering grew stranger—and where, Ansel said quietly, studying the next page, someone had recently passed. The marks on the parchment were fresh. Charcoal, not ink. Drawn within days.

Someone else was looking for the Door. And by the neatness of the hand, and the little sigil sketched in the margin—a seal Brava didn't recognize, though it made Osk go rigid and whisper a word he hadn't spoken in thirty years—

whoever it was, they weren't pilgrims.
