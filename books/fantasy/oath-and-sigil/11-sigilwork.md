## 11. Sigilwork

Osk had carved letters for sixty years before his eyes failed him, and for eleven years after, which was the joke he never told anyone: that a man who made his living by sight went blind and kept working anyway. His fingers knew the alphabet better than his eyes ever had. They knew the weight of each stroke, the grain of the wood, the moment when a chisel found the heart of a letter and the letter became true.

So it was not strange to him that he understood the map before anyone else did.

They had stopped that night in the shell of a granary outside Merrowgate, one wall standing, three walls drowned to their shoulders in the black water that had swallowed half the village. The company arranged itself around a fire built on a floor of old threshing stone—Pip asleep with his head on Brava's pack, Ansel sitting cross-legged with the courier's map spread across her knees, her face lit orange from below like something out of an older story.

Osk sat apart, as he always did, listening to the fire eat.

"Read it again," he said. "Out loud. Every mark."

Ansel glanced up. He heard the small hesitation—the habit of someone who'd spent years deciding what to hide before she decided what to say. But she obeyed. She traced the map with one finger, describing each line, and Osk followed her voice into the thing itself.

A border of mountains drawn as a row of small triangles. A river rendered as a doubled curve. Thirteen circles along the southern edge, twelve filled in with ink and the thirteenth left open, waiting. And through the center of everything, so fine Ansel had taken it for a crease in the paper, a single unbroken line.

"There," Osk said. "The long line. Does it stop anywhere?"

She was quiet a while. He heard her breathing change, heard paper turn under her fingers as she followed the line's whole length.

"No," she said at last. "It runs edge to edge. It goes straight through the Door."

"Read me the line again. Exactly how it moves."

She described it: the way it bent at the mountains, hooked once around the river, crossed itself twice near the thirteenth circle, and ended—or did not end—at the Door itself, where it coiled into a knot no bigger than a thumbnail.

Osk's hands had gone still in his lap. He noticed this the way a man notices his own heartbeat going wrong.

"Girl," he said, "put the map away."

"Why? What is it?"

"It isn't a map." He heard the fire pop; heard Pip stir and settle; heard Brava's hand move toward her knife and stop, the way soldiers' hands do when they've decided not to decide yet. "It is also a map. That part's true enough—it will take you where you're going. But underneath the cartography there is a sigil, and I have been carving sigils since before your mother was born, and I know one when it's described to me even blind, because a sigil is just a letter that got too big for its word."

* * *

He made them bring it to him. Ansel knelt close, guiding his fingers across the paper, and he read it the only way he could now: by touch, by pressure, by the faint ridges of ink dried thick where the pen had paused. The world narrowed to his fingertips, and in that narrowness the truth of the thing opened up like a door.

"The Ministry doesn't erase people one at a time," he said. "That's what they let you believe, because it keeps everyone afraid of being *chosen*. But erasure on that scale—a whole country, every record, every grave, every memory in ten thousand heads—you can't do that with writs and burnings. You do it with geometry. You draw the country once, correctly, completely, and then you draw over it. You seal it. One line, drawn through the true shape, and everything inside the line forgets itself. Not gradually. All at once, everywhere, forever."

"That's madness," Brava said. "No one can forget a country all at once. There are—were—millions of us."

"And you remember Cael Sarn?" Osk asked gently. "Truly? Name its capital. Name its king. Name the song your grandmother sang when she hung the washing."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that settles on the shoulders.

"I rest," Brava said finally, and her voice was flat and careful, "and I know I come from somewhere, and somewhere means nothing anymore. It's like a tooth gone bad. The ache is there but the tooth is gone."

"Yes," Osk said. "That's the seal working. That ache—that's the shape of your country pressing against the inside of the lie."

Ansel's fingers had tightened on his wrist. "You're saying this line"—he felt her tap the paper—"is doing that. Right now. Still."

"It was drawn to be permanent. Seals don't sleep." He turned his face toward her though it meant nothing, toward the sound of her. "But here is what the Ministry's geometers feared, and why they burned every letter-carver who worked in the old style. A seal is a sentence. And every sentence can be *unwritten*—but only from a specific place, in a specific way, with a specific payment."

"The Door," Ansel said.

"The Door. The one gap they left, because a seal without a gap strangles the hand that drew it. The knot at the end of the line—that's the lock. And a lock, girl, is just a promise written in a circle. Break the promise and the sentence comes undone."

"How?"

Osk drew breath and held it. Sixty-one years of letters lived in his hands, and he had never hated his own knowledge until this moment, because he already knew the answer and knew what it would cost the woman kneeling in front of him.

"A name," he said. "Freely surrendered. At the threshold. Spoken by the one who owns it, given up entirely—not hidden, not traded, not stolen, *given*—and the lock drinks it and the sentence breaks and everything the seal holds shut comes flooding back into the world."

* * *

The fire had burned low. Pip had woken without any of them noticing—he did that, surfaced out of sleep like a fish rising—and sat hugging his knees, listening the way children listen when adults forget they have ears.

"But that's just passage," Ansel said. Her voice had gone very controlled, and Osk, who had spent a lifetime reading people through his ears, heard the control like a crack in ice. "Give my name, walk through, be free. That's what the courier died for. That's what the map is *for*."

"No." Osk said it softly, and the softness made them all go still. "Listen carefully, because this is the part the dying man couldn't have known, and the part the Ministry will kill to keep unknown. The lock takes a name either way. But a name given at the Door only buys *passage*—one body through one seam, out of the sealed country, free and safe and forgotten by the land behind you. That's the mercy option. The trap option. Because here is the other use."

He took her hand and set it flat on the map, over the knot.

"You don't spend the name walking *through*. You spend it *on the seal itself*. Speak your name into the knot—give it to the lock instead of the door—and the lock breaks. All of it. The whole sentence unwritten. Every erased memory returns to every erased mind. The drowned villages rise or at least are mourned properly. The country remembers itself."

The silence afterward lasted so long that the fire cracked twice.

"And me?" Ansel said.

Osk's throat closed. He had buried two wives and a son, and he had thought himself finished with the particular pain of saying true things to people he loved. He had been wrong about being finished.

"The name is the payment," he said. "All of it. Whoever gives their name to the seal doesn't pass through anything. They stay—what's left of them. A person without a name isn't dead, exactly. Dead is a place. Nameless is... nowhere. You'd remain. Walking, breathing, perhaps. But no one could hold onto you. No memory would stick. You'd fade the way a letter fades when the ink runs out—not torn away, just... thinner. And thinner. Until the paper forgets it was ever written on."

"That's not sacrifice," Brava said harshly. "That's just a slow way of being unmade. Same as the Ministry does. Why would we trade their knife for ours?"

"Because theirs takes *everything from everyone*, and this takes one thing from one person, freely." Osc turned his ruined eyes toward her. "I know the difference between a knife and an oath, deserter. I've carved both."

* * *

Later, when Brava had taken first watch and Pip had been wrapped in blankets and the granary had gone quiet except for water lapping at drowned walls, Osk heard Ansel cross the stones toward him. She sat down close. He smelled woodsmoke and the wax she used for charms, that faint sweetness that clung to her like a second skin.

"You knew," she said. "Before tonight. When we deciphered the thirteen turns—you already suspected what the map was."

"I suspected. Suspecting is cheap. Tonight I *read* it, and reading makes it true, and I'm sorry for that. Truth is heavy freight and I've just loaded it onto you."

"Say the rest. Whatever you're not saying. I'm good at hearing what people don't say—I've made a living off it."

Osk smiled without joy. He reached out and found her hand, folded it in both of his, feeling the fine scars across her fingertips from years of chisel work and charm-pressing. Hands like his own hands. A maker's hands.

"When I was young," he said, "in the years before the Unmaking, I apprenticed in Cael Sarn itself. In the capital. I carved door-lintels and grave markers and the names above shopfronts. And there was a law among carvers, older than any guild: *never carve a living person's name into a seal*. Because a name in a seal is a life in a lock, and locks are meant to hold things forever. We broke that law exactly once that I know of. The geometers came to us with their commission and their gold, and my master—wept, actually, the whole time he worked, and I held the lamp and watched him weep and didn't understand."

"He carved the original," Ansel whispered. "The seal on our country."

"With a name freely given at its center. Someone volunteered, back then too. Someone who believed they were saving something. And the geometers took the name and locked it in the knot and erased the giver along with everything else, and my master lived another nine years and never carved another letter, and drank himself blind before his eyes went." Osk's grip tightened. "I am telling you this so you understand: the Ministry did not invent this price. They inherited it. Which means it can be paid differently than they paid it—but it cannot be unpaid. There is no clever third door, girl. There is the Door, and the seal, and a name, and you."

Ansel was quiet for a long time. Water moved against the drowned walls. Somewhere out in the dark, a night bird sang three notes that might once have been the opening of a song from a country that no longer officially existed.

"You said freely surrendered," she said at last. "What if the name isn't mine to surrender? What if the name I'd give isn't the one I was born with?"

Osk went cold. Of all the questions she might have asked, this was the one that told him the most—and confirmed the thing he had guessed weeks ago, listening to her lie fluently about small things and tell the truth awkwardly about smaller ones. A woman who sold memories and kept none of her own. A woman whose name fit her like a borrowed coat.

"That," he said slowly, "is a question I cannot answer. The old law says a seal knows its own. A false name given freely might break the lock anyway. Or it might shatter the giver instead of the sentence. Or it might simply refuse, and leave the Door closed forever." He shook his head. "I carve letters, Ansel. I don't write laws. Ask me again at the Door, when it's too late for answers to help."

"That's a cruel thing to say to someone."

"I know." He squeezed her hand. "Forgive me. Cruelty is the honest form of love, sometimes. Kindness lies more often."

* * *

He did not sleep that night. Blind men learn to rest without sleeping, drifting in the dark with their ears open, and so he heard everything: Brava pacing her watch like a caged animal, stopping too long at the edges of the ruin, looking south—Osk had learned to hear the direction of a person's looking by the turn of their breath. He heard Pip whispering to himself in the dark, reciting lists of names, dozens of names, names of streets and ships and dead men, hoarding them the way other children hoarded marbles. And he heard Ansel, near dawn, unfold the map one more time and lay her fingers on the little knot of lines at its heart, and stay that way for a long, long time, the way a person touches a wound to check whether it's still bleeding.

When gray light came through the one standing wall, Osk rose and began packing his tools, and his fingers found the smallest chisel in the roll—the one he had not used in forty years, the one reserved, by the old law, for carving names into seals.

He had sworn never to touch it again.

He packed it anyway.

They broke camp as the sun came up over the water, turning the drowned village briefly to gold, and Ansel stood facing east with the map rolled tight in her fist, and Osk heard her say, quietly, to no one—to the country, maybe, or to herself:

"Then we go to the salt flats. And after that, whatever's left."

Brava checked her rifle with a small metallic click, and said nothing, and looked south again.

Osk walked beside them into the morning, carrying a tool he had promised never to carry, toward a choice none of them had fully agreed to make.
