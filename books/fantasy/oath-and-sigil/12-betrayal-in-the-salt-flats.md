## 12. Betrayal in the Salt Flats

Brava had learned to count her sins the way soldiers counted rounds: by weight, not number. The letter in her coat pocket weighed almost nothing. A single page, Ministry paper, sealed with grey wax that smelled of cold iron. She had read it forty times since Merrowgate, and each reading it grew heavier, until she felt it dragging at her hip like a sabre she could not draw.

*Amnesty for four,* it said. *Full pardon for desertion. Restoration of rank and pension. In exchange: deliver the bearer of the map to the Ministry road at the salt flats, unharmed if possible.*

Unharmed if possible. She had laughed at that, alone, in the dark, with her back against a drowned fencepost while the others slept. Unharmed if possible. As though the Ministry had ever left anything unharmed in its long career of kindness.

She told herself the old lie again as they walked out onto the flats: that she was doing it for them. That amnesty would cover Pip too, cover Osk, cover even Ansel, who was not a deserter but a thief and therefore forgivable in the arithmetic of empires. That a cage with soft walls was better than a border that might not exist.

The lie fit her like an old uniform. It still had her shape in it.

* * *

The salt flats began where the drowned country gave up pretending. Merrowgate's last fields lay behind them, silvered with brine, and ahead the land stretched white and featureless to a horizon so flat it looked sawn off. Nothing grew there. Nothing remembered there. The Ministry's rewriting had passed over the flats like a scythe over stubble, and what remained was ground without history—ground on which a person could be taken and no ghost would trouble itself to object.

They walked in single file through the crust, which crackled underfoot like thin ice, like old parchment, like bones being counted. The sun stood high and merciless and threw their shadows down beneath their feet, small dark stains they carried with them everywhere, as if the land insisted they drag their own silhouettes behind them.

Pip walked at the front because Pip always walked at the front, his satchel of forbidden memories bumping against his spine, his head full of other people's yesterdays. He had found a salt-crusted doll two miles back and refused to leave it, and now he carried it tucked under one arm like a comrade. Brava watched him and felt the letter shift against her ribs with every step, and hated herself in the methodical way she did everything—in ranks, in files, in proper order.

Osk walked with his carved stick clicking against the crust, reading the ground by sound. "Salt's thinner here," he said. "Water under. Keep to the ridge of it."

"You can hear water under salt?" Pip asked, delighted.

"I can hear everything," Osk said. "That's my tragedy. I never asked for so much listening."

Ansel said nothing. She had said very little for three days—not since the night by the fire when everyone had traded a true memory for passage and she had sat with her hands closed in her lap like a woman holding coins she would not spend. Brava had given up a memory then, freely, the first thing she had ever given away without orders attached. She had given them the morning she deserted: the smell of the barracks bread, the sergeant's voice cracking on the word *Cael Sarn*, the door of the armoury standing open like a mouth. And Ansel had listened and thanked her and offered nothing back, and Brava had understood, and resented understanding, and understood the resentment too. It went around like that. Everything about love went around like that.

"Two days to the far ridge," Ansel said now, consulting the map without opening it fully, her fingers shielding its edges out of habit. "There's a well at the halfway mark. Old army survey point. We'll water there."

"A well," Brava repeated. Her voice came out strange, flattened by the white light.

"You know it?" Ansel glanced back at her.

"No," Brava said. "I don't know it." Which was true, in the way that mattered least. She knew it from the letter. Grid reference, depth, condition of the pump. The Ministry wrote beautiful letters. They left nothing out.

* * *

They made the well by late afternoon, when the light had gone the colour of brass and the salt threw it back up into their faces from below, so that they seemed to walk inside a lamp. The well was exactly as the letter described: stone lip cracked, iron pump furred with rust, a low wall built around it by hands that had believed in walls. Brava worked the pump while the others drank, and the water came up brownish and warm and tasting of minerals and old grief, and Pip pronounced it the best water he'd ever had, which was a lie, but a kind one.

It was there, watching Pip drink, that Brava finally admitted what she had known since Merrowgate: that she was not going to do it. That whatever the letter promised, she could not walk these people to the Ministry road and stand aside while grey-coats took them. Not Pip, who slept clutching his satchel like a child clutching a bird. Not Osk, who heard everything and complained beautifully. Not Ansel, whose closed hands haunted her more than any open wound she'd ever dressed.

And having admitted it, she made the second decision, the worse one, the one that would follow her the rest of her life: she would warn them. Tonight. At the ridge camp. She would take out the letter and lay it on the ground between them all and say, *I was going to sell you, and I couldn't, and here is the proof of both halves.* Honesty as penance. Confession as a kind of payment. She had been a soldier too long; she still believed you could settle accounts.

She should have known better. Accounts were the Ministry's language. Every debt she tried to pay, they had already marked paid in someone else's blood.

* * *

The ambush came at dusk, which meant it came early, because dusk on the flats lasted only minutes—the sun dropped like a coin into a slot and the dark came up out of the ground instead of down from the sky.

Brava saw it first because she had been trained to see it: the wrongness of the horizon. Salt flats have no features, and so any feature is a wound. There was a line out on the western white that did not shimmer correctly, a straightness where straightness should not be. Wagons. Grey canvas. Men standing in the open with the patience of people who have been told exactly when.

"Pip," she said, and her voice did something she had never heard it do—it broke in the middle, like green wood. "Pip, get down."

Then the lanterns lit along the line, six of them, ten, twelve, and the flats turned the colour of an old bruise, and Brava understood that the letter had never needed her at all. They had tracked the map itself. Or tracked her. Or simply known, the way the Ministry always seemed to know, the way a spider knows what has landed in its web without bothering to look. The amnesty had not been an offer. It had been a leash, and leashes are pulled, not consulted.

Greycoats came out of the dark in a line, unhurried, boots crunching the salt in unison, and the sound was the worst thing—the sound of order, of boots that had once been hers.

"Run," Brava shouted, and this time her voice held, held the way a wall holds, and she put herself between the line and the others because that was the only prayer she knew. "Run for the ridge—Osk, take his hand—"

The world came apart in the ordinary ways. A whistle. A shout of command. The flat crack of a signal pistol, red starburst hanging over the salt like a wound in the sky. Brava drew the knife she had sworn off and put her body low and wide, a barricade of one, and the first greycoat reached her and she took his wrist and turned him into the second, and for eleven seconds—she counted, she always counted—she was magnificent.

In the twelfth second someone hit her across the back of the knees with a stave, and the flats rose up and struck her face, and salt filled her mouth, and through the blur she saw the thing she would see every night afterward:

Pip, running the wrong way.

Not toward the ridge. Back toward the well, back toward her, because Pip was eight years old and loved clumsily and completely, and children do not run from fires when their people are inside them. He was running straight back into the lantern-light with his satchel bouncing and the salt doll still under his arm, and two greycoats were turning toward him with the smooth economy of men doing a job.

Brava got one knee under herself. Then the other. She did not remember standing; she remembered only motion, the lurch of her body like a cart taking a ditch. She crossed the distance between herself and the boy in a time that could not be measured in seconds, only in the closing of a gap, and she hit the first greycoat shoulder-first and felt something in her own shoulder give, and then she had Pip by the collar and the arm both, lifting him, swinging him, throwing him—there was no gentler word for it—throwing him bodily toward the dark beyond the lanterns, toward Ansel's shape rising out of the night like something summoned.

"Take him," she screamed. "Ansel—take him and go—"

And Ansel took him. Ansel, who trusted no one, who kept her hands closed and her past locked, caught the child out of the air the way you catch something holy, stumbling, folding around him, and for one moment her eyes found Brava's across the burning salt, and what was in them was not forgiveness. It was worse than forgiveness. It was recognition. She had known. Some part of her had known since the well, maybe since Merrowgate, maybe since the fire where Brava traded her desertion-memory and Ansel had listened too carefully—and she had said nothing, had let Brava carry it, because that was Ansel's whole religion: everyone keeps their own weight.

Then the map.

It happened fast and it happened slowly, the way disasters do. Ansel turned to run with Pip, and a greycoat officer—tall, gloved, faceless in the lantern-shine—stepped out of the dark precisely where the Ministry's arithmetic said she would be, and there was a moment of grappling, brief and brutal, and then the officer was holding something pale and folded, and Ansel was holding nothing, and the officer was already retreating backward into the line with the map raised in one hand like a relic or a warrant.

"No—" Ansel's voice, raw, stripped of all its market-charm, just a woman's voice losing something irreplaceable. Two greycoats closed on her and she went down under them with Pip screaming in her arms, and Brava, face-down in the salt with a boot on her neck, tasted blood and brine and understood the exact shape of what she had done.

She had not led them to the slaughter. That would have been simple. Instead she had hesitated, and delayed, and planned a confession for a night that never came, and her hesitation had delivered them to the same place on schedule anyway. The Ministry had not needed her sin. It had only needed her slowness.

* * *

They bound her wrists with wire and set her kneeling on the salt, and an officer crouched in front of her with a lantern held close, studying her face the way you study a form filled out incorrectly.

"Deserter," he said. "Third Regiment. Brava Kess." He said her name like a serial number, and it sounded, for the first time in years, like someone else's name entirely. "Your letter is in your coat. We'll want it back. It's evidence."

"Evidence of what?" she said. Her mouth was full of salt and blood.

"Of your good sense," the officer said mildly. "You'll be credited. Amnesty stands—for you. You brought us within reach of the courier's map. The child and the thief will be processed. The blind man escaped into the dark; we'll have him by morning." He stood, brushing salt from his knee. "You did the right thing, soldier."

"I didn't do anything," Brava said. "That's what I did."

The officer paused, and something flickered behind his face—recognition, perhaps, of a species of honesty the Ministry had no form for filing. Then he turned away, and the lanterns began to move, and the company of the forgotten was loaded into wagons like evidence, like charms, like things to be burned.

Brava knelt in the salt and watched the dark where Ansel had vanished with Pip, and found she could not pray, and could not count, and could not make the numbers come out in anyone's favor. Somewhere out in that blackness Osk was alone, listening to everything, carrying the only knowledge of the sigil that any of them had. Somewhere the map rode west in a gloved hand toward the Ministry, toward Corvane, toward whatever wore stolen memories for armor. And somewhere inside Brava's chest, beneath the wire and the shame and the taste of salt, a thought assembled itself with parade-ground precision, the one clear order she had ever given herself:

*I lost them. So I will follow. To the Ministry's own door if I have to. And this time I will not hesitate, and this time I will not confess—I will collect.*

Far off across the flats, past the last lantern, something answered her. Not a voice. A sound like chalk on slate, faint and rhythmic, coming from the darkness where no man stood: the sound, she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, of someone carving letters into the air.
