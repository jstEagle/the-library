## 13. Memory of Water

The map was gone.

Ansel had turned out her pack three times on the cold stones of the ruined mill, shaking it upside down until a button and a dried plum and half a spool of thread lay scattered like the contents of a small life. No map. Brava had taken it, or the ambushers had, or it had burned in the salt grass when the Ministry lanterns swept the flats — it didn't matter which. What mattered was that she stood on the far side of the salt flats with a child asleep against her shoulder, an old blind man feeling his way along a wall that wasn't there anymore, and a deserter who wouldn't meet her eyes, and no map at all.

"You could go back," Osk said quietly. He had heard her searching. Blind men heard everything; that was the whole of their terrible gift. "Back to Vell. Surrender the girl's name to some registry, buy yourselves new ones. People do it."

"People do it because they've forgotten there was ever anything else to do," Ansel said.

She sat down among the buttons and the plum and put her head in her hands, and that was when she felt it — not the absence of the map but the presence of something else, something she had been carrying for weeks without letting herself look at directly, the way you don't look at a splinter working its way toward your heart.

The courier's memory. Still lodged behind her eyes like a stone at the bottom of a well.

* * *

She had taken it the night he died. Not deliberately — that was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing that shamed her more than any theft. His hand had closed around her wrist as the life went out of him, and memory, which is only another kind of blood, had poured across the threshold of her skin before she could stop it. She had felt his last hours settle into her like silt into still water: a road under rain, a folded paper against his ribs, a woman's voice saying *take it to someone who remembers how to read the old signs*, and beneath all of it, steady as a heartbeat, one word repeated so many times it had worn smooth as a river pebble:

*Cael Sarn. Cael Sarn. Cael Sarn.*

She had spent six weeks avoiding that stone. Now she walked to the edge of herself and looked down into the well.

"Osk," she said. "I don't need the map."

He turned his face toward her. Even blind, he had a way of looking at people that made them feel mapped themselves, charted from crown to heel. "No?"

"I have the man who carried it." She pressed two fingers to her temple. "He's still in here. Every mile he walked. Every turn he took. He *was* the map, Osk — he'd memorized it before he ever left Vell, in case they took the paper from him. And they did take the paper. But they never got the walking."

Osk was silent for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the mill's broken door, Pip murmured in his sleep, and Brava's fire cracked and spat.

"That's a hard road," Osk said at last. "Walking inside a dead man's head. I've carved letters for forty years and I'll tell you what I know about names: the dead keep theirs better than we do. But they keep other things too. Everything they couldn't say while living."

"I know," Ansel said. "I've been keeping it for him."

* * *

She waited until the others slept. This was work for solitude; she had learned that long ago, back when stealing memories was a trade and not a survival. You didn't open a memory in company. A memory opened was a door opened, and doors let things out as well as in.

She built the fire up, wrapped her cloak around her, and closed her eyes.

The well-water rose to meet her.

* * *

Rain. Always rain first — that was how the courier's memory began, every time: rain on a road outside Vell, the kind of gray drizzle that doesn't fall so much as hang in the air like doubt. His name was Sefren Calloway, though everyone called him Sef, though most people called him nothing at all, because couriers were the furniture of the world, always present, never seen. He was thirty-one years old and he had a crooked tooth on the left side of his mouth that caught his lower lip when he smiled, and he smiled often, because he was the sort of man who found the world mostly amusing and occasionally heartbreaking and almost never worth despairing over.

Ansel knew all of this the way she knew her own hands. That was the trouble with stolen memories: they came with the person attached.

In the memory, Sef was walking. The road ran west out of Vell through country that grew stranger with every mile — fields that ended mid-furrow as if a hand had lifted the plow away, hedgerows that doubled back on themselves, a milestone whose carved inscription was worn smooth not by weather but by *erasure*, by decades of the Ministry's slow sanding of the world. He passed a shrine where the saint's face had been chiseled off, and he touched the blank stone as he went by, the way you'd touch the shoulder of a friend who'd been crying and didn't want to talk about it.

*I know,* the touch said. *I remember anyway.*

And here was the thing that stopped Ansel's breath, there in the dark of the ruined mill with the fire burning low: Sef remembered. Not fragments, not smuggled scraps like hers — he remembered *whole*. He had been born in Cael Sarn, in a town called Lowmere where the river bent like a drawn bow, and he had been nine years old when the Ministry came with their ledgers and their sigil-ink and unmade the country around him, and he had spent twenty-two years carrying that town inside himself intact, undimmed, refusing every amnesty and every mercy and every comfortable forgetting the Ministry offered. Twenty-two years of being a foreigner in his own skin, of answering questions about his origins with the careful lies of a man whose hometown officially did not exist.

Twenty-two years, and then a chance to carry the map home, and he had taken it knowing what it would cost him.

"Why?" Ansel whispered into the memory, and the memory answered, because memories answer when you ask them truly: a kitchen in Lowmere, bread on the table, a mother's hands dusted with flour, a sister laughing at something he'd said — and then the door opening, and the gray coats, and the sound of his mother saying his name one last time before the sigil-ink took even the word *mother* out of her mouth. He had run. Nine years old and he had run, and he had hated himself for running for twenty-two years, and the map was his way of walking back through that door at last, even if he arrived too late to find anyone waiting.

*You can't save a country,* his father had told him once, in a memory within the memory, a voice like gravel in honey. *But you can refuse to be the one who forgets it.*

Ansel opened her eyes. The fire had burned down to embers. Her face was wet.

She hadn't cried for Sefren Calloway when he died in her cellar. There had been no time — there was never time — and after that there had been fear, and flight, and the flat grey panic of being hunted, and grief is a luxury that hunted people defer. But now, alone in the ruin with the embers breathing, she grieved him properly at last: for the crooked tooth and the easy smile, for the mother with flour on her hands, for the boy who ran and the man who walked back, for twenty-two years of remembering in a country that paid people to forget.

"I'll get us there," she told the embers, which is to say she told him. "Every mile you walked, I'll walk. You're not done yet."

The ember-light shifted, and for half a heartbeat she would have sworn the fire threw his shadow on the wall — broad-shouldered, easy, one hand raised in a lazy salute, the way he'd saluted her in the cellar before the light went out of him.

Then it was just firelight. But she took it as agreement.

* * *

They walked west at dawn, and Sef walked with her.

Not visibly — she was not mad, whatever the Ministry might have written in its ledgers about charm-thieves — but as a pressure, a pull, a compass needle behind her eyes. When the track forked at a leaning cairn, she stood a moment with her eyes unfocused and felt his certainty settle over her like a coat: *left, always left at the cairn, the right-hand path drowns in spring.* They went left. By midmorning the right-hand path, glimpsed through the trees, lay under a foot of black water.

"He does that on purpose," Pip said, trotting beside her. "The dead man. He shows you things."

Ansel glanced down at him. Pip was seven or eight, nobody had ever bothered to record which, and he remembered everything he shouldn't — every erased street name in Vell, every vanished festival, every neighbor the Ministry had unmade. The Ministry wanted him very badly. Ansel wanted him alive even more.

"What do you mean, shows me?"

"He walks ahead of you sometimes. I can see him." Pip said it simply, the way children say impossible things, because impossibility is just a fact they haven't been taught to fear. "Wet boots. He leaves little puddles."

Ansel looked at the track ahead. It had rained in the night; the mud held water everywhere, in every hoofprint, in every hollow. But there — she made herself see it — there was a line of prints smaller than a man's stride should make, spaced evenly, going exactly where she had been about to go.

She decided not to decide whether they were real. Some things you carry better unexamined.

Behind them, Osk tapped his carving-stick along the track, and Brava brought up the rear with her hand never far from her knife, and none of them spoke of the salt flats or the ambush or the reason Brava flinched whenever a bird went up out of the grass. There would be a reckoning about that. Ansel was saving it, the way she saved everything, for a moment with room enough to hold it.

* * *

On the third day the land began to remember water.

It came gradually — a marsh where the map of the world had a wrinkle in it, then a stream running the wrong direction, uphill toward some vanished sea, and then, on the morning of the fourth day, they crested a low ridge and looked down into the drowned village of Merrowgate's sister-town, a place Sef's memory called Brenholt and the Ministry's records called nothing at all, because officially it had never existed.

Half of it stood above the waterline. Half of it stood below, perfectly preserved, streets and doorways and a well with its bucket still hanging, all of it visible through water so clear it seemed less like submersion than like preservation — a town kept, not lost. And the water was full of light that didn't come from the sky. It rose from below, soft and gold, as if the drowned half of Brenholt had never agreed to the drowning and kept its lamps lit regardless.

"The rewriting," Osk said, when they had picked their way down to the shore. "When the Ministry unmade Cael Sarn, they rewrote the rivers too. But water has a long memory. It runs where it used to run, no matter what the ledgers say. So the Ministry drowned what it couldn't erase." He tilted his blind face toward the golden light under the surface. "This was a valley. Now it's a lake. The houses are still down there. The people—" He stopped.

"The people are still down there too," Ansel finished quietly.

Sef's memory stirred, and gave her the village as it had been: market day, gulls, a fiddler on the bridge, his sister buying ribbon. Ansel stood at the water's edge and let the two versions lie over each other like transparencies — the living gold-lit town and the drowned one — and understood, finally, in her bones rather than her mind, what the Ministry had actually done. Not conquest. Not even cruelty, exactly. Something worse: *editing.* They had taken a country the way you take a sentence out of a letter, and the hole where it had been still ached on every page of the world.

Pip crouched at the shoreline and put his hand in the water.

"There's a woman down there," he said. "By the church. She's waving."

Brava made a sound low in her throat. "Kid. Don't."

"She's not sad," Pip said, frowning, listening to something none of them could hear. "She's saying hurry."

* * *

That night they camped in the shell of a chapel on the shore, in the dry half, under a roof that was half slate and half open sky. Ansel took the middle watch, and while the others slept she let Sef walk her further along his road — past Brenholt, into hills she had never seen, along paths he had taken in the last weeks of his life, hiding by day, moving by night, sleeping in barns and bell towers with the map against his ribs and the Ministry's hunters somewhere behind him always.

And in the memory, on the last night before Vell, he had spoken aloud to the empty dark, the way lonely men do, and the memory had kept his words:

"If I don't come back, I hope whoever finds this is stubborn. Not brave — brave runs out. Stubborn keeps. I hope they're stubborn as riverwater. Riverwater gets where it's going if it takes a hundred years."

Ansel sat in the ruined chapel with the dead man's voice in her skull and laughed, once, wetly, at the sky through the missing roof.

"Stubborn as riverwater," she repeated. "All right, Sef. Message received."

* * *

On the fifth day the golden light of Brenholt's drowned lamps appeared behind them in the dawn mist, impossibly, though they had walked a full day east-to-west since leaving the shore — and Ansel understood that the drowned village was becoming a landmark that moved, or that landmarks in a rewritten country obeyed different rules, or that she was tired enough to stop asking why. Ahead, the hills rose into mountains that Sef's memory knew by names the maps no longer printed: the Cinder Teeth, the Weeping Pass, and beyond them, three days' hard walking, the high plateau where the capital of Cael Sarn had stood and still, in some stubborn sense, stood.

They made the foothills by evening. And it was there, in the blue shadow of the first great peak, that Ansel felt the change in Sef's memory — a tightening, a coldness, the way a dog goes stiff before the storm arrives.

She stopped on the track. "What is it?"

The memory answered with images, quick and urgent: gray coats on a mountain road. Lanterns with the Ministry's sigil burning green at their centers. And a figure at the head of the column, tall, unhurried, wearing a hunter's long coat — and wearing, beneath the coat, in the set of the shoulders and the tilt of the head, something horribly familiar. Sef had seen him from a distance, three nights before he died, and had hidden in a culvert with his heart hammering while the hunter paused beside the culvert, so close, and stood there a long moment as if tasting the air.

Corvane. Coming up the same road. Days behind them, perhaps hours now.

"Pip," Ansel said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Osk. We need to move, and we need to get off this road."

Below them, far down the valley, a point of green light flickered in the dusk. Then another. Then a third, like eyes opening.

Osk's blind face turned toward them with terrible accuracy. "How many?"

"I can't tell. More than three." Ansel pulled Pip close and scanned the slopes — scree, pine, and above the treeline a dark seam in the rock that Sef's memory identified without being asked: an old pilgrim path, steep, narrow, invisible from the valley floor. *Riverwater gets where it's going,* she thought. *Even if it has to go straight up.*

"Off the road," she said. "Up. Now."

The green lights in the valley began, unhurriedly, to climb.
