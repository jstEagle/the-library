## 15. What Corvane Forgot

The rain in the Salt Flats did not fall so much as arrive, sideways, like a rumor no one wanted to repeat. Corvane stood at the edge of a drowned orchard—trees that had been dead for thirty years and alive for twenty, depending on which ledger you consulted—and felt the first wrongness of the day settle into his bones the way cold water settles into boots.

He had lost her trail twice since the salt ambush. The first time was her doing; she was skilled, and he had always respected that the way a chess player respects an opponent's patience. The second time was his own doing, though he could not yet name it as such. He had stood at a crossroads marked with a Ministry waypoint—a brass pin driven into the earth, humming with directive—and he had looked down the eastern road instead of the western one, and told himself it was instinct.

Instinct. That was the word the Ministry gave him for everything he could not explain about himself.

*The hunter who wears my face.* He had heard her say it once, through a wall, in Vell, before the burning of the market. He had let her hear him laugh. It seemed, in retrospect, the cruelest thing he had ever done, and he had done crueler things by any honest measure: men made to forget their children mid-embrace, women returned to houses that no longer contained their lives, smiling vacantly at furniture they could not remember buying. The Ministry called it kindness. Corvane had carried out eleven hundred kindnesses and kept a ledger of them in a notebook he was not permitted to own, written in a shorthand he had invented himself, because someone inside him—some stubborn tenant who refused eviction—insisted that erasure should at least be *recorded*.

Tonight the ledger was useless. Tonight there was only the orchard, and the rain, and the small fire burning in the ruin of a farmhouse that appeared on no map because the Ministry had unmade the family that built it, and where a house's memory went, its walls were not far behind.

She was alone at the fire.

That was wrong too. She traveled with the blind carver and the deserter and the impossible child; he had tracked their passage across three provinces like a man reading a novel he hated but could not put down. But tonight the fire held only one figure, hood up, hands extended to flames that guttered green in the rain—salt-fire, the kind that burned without warmth, the kind that grew in places where the Ministry had rewritten too much. Even the elements here were confused about what they were.

Corvane drew his sigil-blade. It woke against his palm with the familiar ache, the blade that cut memory before it cut flesh, and he began to move down through the dead orchard, quiet as a debt coming due.

*Eleven hundred and one,* he thought, and something in him flinched from the number, though he could not have said why. *This is what you are. This is all you are.*

---

He was ten steps away when the memories surfaced.

They came the way floodwater comes—not gradually, not fairly, but all at once, with the pressure of years behind them. Later he would understand that proximity had done it, that some fragment of her had been waiting in him like a seed in dry ground, and her nearness was the rain. But in the moment there was no understanding. There was only—

There was only a garden behind a blue door, and a girl of perhaps nine years sitting on the wall with her legs swinging, eating stolen plums with the concentration of a scholar and the guilt of a saint.

*"You'll get caught,"* said a boy's voice, his voice, high and missing two teeth.

*"I never get caught,"* the girl said, and smiled with plum-stained lips, and the smile was Ansel Ishikawa's smile, older now and armored, but the same architecture beneath—the same tilt, the same defiance arranged carefully over loneliness. *"And even if I did, no one remembers anything long enough to punish me. That's the trick of this whole country, Corrie. Nobody holds on."*

*Corrie.*

Corvane stopped walking.

The blade trembled in his hand. Rain ran down the back of his collar, cold as fingers. And the memories kept coming, patient now, orderly, as if they had spent decades folded in a drawer and had merely been waiting for permission to unfold.

A street called Lantern Row that did not exist. A mother who sang off-key on purpose because perfect pitch frightened her. A neighbor woman, Mrs. Vess, who sold charms at the market and winked at children who couldn't afford them, slipping honey-memories into their pockets free of charge. A city with two moons over it, the second one thin and silver and *wrong*, and everyone pretending it wasn't, because pretending was the national art of Cael Sarn.

*Cael Sarn.*

The word arrived in him like a bell struck inside the skull. He knew that name. He had been ordered not to know that name. There was a file with his designation on it—Hunter Third Class Corvane, Ministry of Unmaking, Directive Nine—and somewhere in that file was the account of how a boy named Corrin Vael of Lantern Row had been flagged for retention anomaly, had remembered too much, had been scheduled for gentle correction, and how the correction had taken *almost* completely.

Almost.

That was the flaw in the Ministry's great machine, the flaw he had spent fifteen years not looking at directly: erasure left edges. You could burn a page out of a book, but the binding remembered the weight. You could take a boy's childhood, but some nights he woke with the taste of plums in his mouth and wept without knowing why, and reported himself for emotional irregularity in the morning, and was praised for his vigilance.

The girl on the wall turned her head in the memory and looked at him—looked at *him*, across thirty years and an ocean of forgetting—and said:

*"Promise me you won't forget me either, Corrie."*

And boy-Corvane, earnest, gap-toothed, hopeless, said: *"Never. Not even if they take everything else."*

---

Ansel must have heard him—his stillness breaking, or his breath, or simply the way the world leans when a man's entire self is collapsing—but she turned, and the hood fell back, and the firelight found her face.

For a moment neither of them moved. Rain threaded between them like a curtain half-drawn.

"You," she said. Not fear. Something stranger. Recognition arriving out of order, the way it does when you meet someone in a dream and then again in daylight.

"Ansel Ishikawa," he said. His voice came out ruined, scraped. "Lantern Row. Blue door. Your mother sang flat on purpose."

She went white. He watched her hand drift toward her knife and stop, hover, fall—watched her decide, in real time, that whatever was happening here was not happening in the grammar she knew. "How do you—" She stopped. Started again. "Those are *my* memories. I locked those. I locked them so deep I sometimes doubt they're real."

"They're mine too," Corvane said. "That's the horror of it. They were never only yours. I was there. I was—" His knees decided things without consulting him. He sat down in the mud of the dead orchard, blade falling from his grip, and the sigil-metal hissed in the wet earth like something dying. "I was your neighbor. I was the boy who promised."

"That's not possible." But her voice cracked on *possible*, and he saw her doing the same arithmetic he had done for fifteen years without knowing it: counting the edges of herself, feeling for the seams. "The Ministry doesn't leave pieces. They pride themselves on—"

"On thoroughness. Yes. I've read the manuals. I've written some of them." He laughed, and the laugh was terrible, and somewhere in the farmhouse ruin the green fire leaned toward him as if listening. "Do you know what I am, Ansel? Officially? Hunter Third Class. Eleven hundred corrections executed. Commendations for emotional stability under doctrinal stress." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "Unofficially, I am a filing error. I am the page they burned and the ash that stayed in the binding. Every night for fifteen years I have dreamed of a garden I could not find on any map, and a girl on a wall, and I reported the dreams. I *reported them*. Do you understand? I handed them the last of you myself, over and over, every quarter, in triplicate, and they thanked me for my honesty."

Ansel crouched in front of him. Slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal—or a wound that has learned to speak. "What did they do to you, Corrie?"

The name undid him more than the question. He made a sound that was not quite a word.

"Correction is gentle," he recited, and heard the doctrine in his own mouth like a worm in fruit. "Correction is kindness. Correction returns you to yourself." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious. "It's none of those things. It's amputation dressed as healing. They took Lantern Row. They took my mother—they took her *from both of us*, do you see, because when they unmade her street they unmade everyone who remembered it, and I was assigned—" His breath hitched. "Three years after my correction, they assigned me to her file. My mother. She was flagged for retention anomaly. She kept a drawing I made when I was six. A house. A blue door. She hid it in a flour tin for nine years, and when they found it, she was scheduled."

Ansel's hand rose, hesitated, and settled on his shoulder—a weight light as ash and heavy as a continent.

"And I performed the correction," Corvane said. "My own mother. Because the alternative was refusal, and refusal is unmaking, and I was a coward, and I told myself that at least *I would remember her*, that someone should, that my remembering would be a kind of—of grave." He stared at his own hands. "Two days later I couldn't recall her face. I could recall the *file*. Subject retained image-based contraband. Correction successful. I sat in my quarters trying to grieve and discovering I'd forgotten what for, and the emptiness was so clean, so *tidy*, and I understood in that moment that tidiness was the whole of the Ministry's promise. No grief, because nothing to grieve. No love, because nothing loved. No *country*, because—"

"Because Cael Sarn," Ansel finished quietly.

The name hung in the rain between them.

"How do you know that word?" he whispered. "I know the penalty for knowing that word. I've *administered* the penalty for knowing that word."

"I've been carrying a dead man's map for a month." She said it simply, without drama, and somehow the simplicity made it worse. "And a dead man's memories. He died in my cellar telling me the moon was wrong, and he was right, Corvane. Look up."

Corvane looked up.

Through the torn clouds, above the drowned orchard, the sky held two moons. The first rode high and full and lawful, fat with borrowed light. The second hung lower, thinner, a silver paring, a fingernail of a moon, and it was *moving*—drifting against the stars with a slow, deliberate patience, like something keeping an appointment.

"The thirteenth moon," Ansel said. "Osk says it isn't a moon at all anymore. It's what's left of the seal. The Ministry didn't erase Cael Sarn, Corvane. They *sealed* it—stitched the whole country shut and hung the stitch in the sky where everyone could see it and no one could name it. Everyone in Vell looks at that moon every night of their lives. And no one asks what it is." Her grip tightened on his shoulder. "Because asking is remembering, and remembering is death."

"I asked once," Corvane heard himself say. "As a child. I remember asking. I remember my father going gray and putting his hand over my mouth and saying, *some doors stay shut so the house stays standing.*"

"Your father was right about the mechanism and wrong about the moral." Ansel almost smiled. "The house is already fallen. We've been living in its shadow and calling it shelter."

---

They stayed like that while the green fire burned down to embers—hunter and quarry, predator and prey, kneeling together in the mud of a country that officially did not exist, and Corvane felt the last load-bearing wall of his life come down brick by brick.

Everything reassembled itself around new architecture. Fifteen years of service became fifteen years of hostage-taking—his own mind held for ransom by men who called the ransom mercy. The ledger in his pocket, eleven hundred entries of recorded erasure, transformed in his hands from a professional habit into something else entirely: testimony. Evidence. A list of names that someone, someday, might read aloud.

"I can't go back," he said. It was not despair. It was cartography—the simple mapping of a border he had crossed.

"No," Ansel agreed. "You can't."

"They'll send others. When I don't report. They'll send hunters who weren't careless enough to keep their edges." He thought of his colleagues—Vessa with her humming, Dorn with his collection of confiscated wedding rings—and felt a grief for them that was almost worse than grief for himself, because they were good people walking around with holes where their hearts should have filed their objections. "They'll find your company. The carver. The deserter. The child."

"Pip," Ansel said, and her face changed, and he saw it—the motherless ferocity of her, banked but not buried. "Yes. Which is why I need to know, Corvane, right now, with the rain coming in and my knife closer than yours: what are you going to do?"

He considered the question honestly, which was more than the Ministry had ever asked of him. He considered the blade in the mud, the directive humming faintly in his blood like a second heartbeat, the eleven hundred names, the flour tin, the blue door, the promise made by a boy who had not known that promises were the one thing erasure could not reach—because a promise was not a memory, exactly. It was a *debt*, and debts lived somewhere deeper than the archives.

"I'm going to ask a question," he said finally. "I've been a hunter for fifteen years and I have never once been permitted to ask one. So I'm going to ask it now, to the sky, to the seal, to whatever stitched this country shut." He rose from the mud, slow, joints aching with the weight of recovered years. "*Why?* What was worth doing this to a nation? What did Cael Sarn have that terrified them into sewing the sky?"

Ansel looked at him for a long moment. Rain slid off her chin.

"There's a ruin east of here," she said. "The capital, Osk thinks. Buildings standing as outlines, like chalk drawings the rain hasn't dared touch. Pilgrims have been scrawling names on the doorframes for thirty years—names of the unmade, I think. Names of the remembered." She picked up his sigil-blade from the mud and held it out to him, hilt-first, and the gesture was so ordinary and so enormous that his throat closed. "Come and see for yourself, hunter. Come and ask your question at the source."

He took the blade. It did not wake against his palm. It lay inert, cool, almost humble—as if the metal itself had understood something its wielder was only beginning to learn.

Behind them, the thirteenth moon drifted another degree along its patient arc. Three nights, perhaps four, until the cycle closed. Somewhere south, the Ministry would be noticing his silence, spinning up its tidy machinery, dispatching its tidy men.

Somewhere east, a city of chalk outlines waited with its doorframes full of names.

Corvane slung the blade across his back—the first weapon he had ever carried pointed, in spirit, the other direction—and followed the girl from the blue door into the dark, toward the ruin of the only home either of them had ever truly had.

And in his ledger, that night, beneath eleven hundred entries of erasure, he wrote a twelve-hundredth line in his private shorthand. It read:

*Entry twelve hundred: subject remembered me. Correction impossible. Recommend the opposite.*

*Recommend everything.*
