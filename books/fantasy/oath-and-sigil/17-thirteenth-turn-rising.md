## 17. Thirteenth Turn Rising

The sky over the ruin of Cael Sarn had been wrong all day, though only Pip seemed to notice at first.

He stood on a broken colonnade with his arms crossed and his chin tipped back, watching the pale smear where the sun should have been. "It's hiding," he said. "The sun's hiding because it knows."

"Because it knows what?" Brava asked, though her voice was rougher than usual. She had not slept well since the salt flats; none of them had. She sat sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen, the whetstone whispering against steel in long, even strokes, as if rhythm itself could keep her from thinking.

"That it's going to be replaced," Pip said simply.

Ansel felt the words settle into her chest like a stone dropped into still water. She looked up too. The light was thin and gray, the color of old linen, and it made the ruin stranger than usual—the buildings that stood only as outlines, walls like pencil sketches of themselves, doorframes with names scrawled inside them in a hundred hands: *Maren was here. We were the Vess family. I remember the baker on Cord Street.* The names caught the failing light and held it, faintly luminous, like embers under ash.

Osk sat nearby with his carving knife and a length of soft pine, working without looking at it. His blind eyes were turned toward the sky as if he could read it better than any of them. "The thirteenth moon," he said. "It comes tonight. I can feel it in the stone. The whole country is holding its breath."

"The Ministry will come with it," Brava said. It was not a question. She had seen their signal-fires on the ridgelines since dawn—three fires, then five, then a ring of them like a crown of red eyes around the valley. "They've known about this place longer than we have. They let us walk here."

"Why?" Pip asked.

"Because they couldn't stop what happens tonight," Osk said quietly. "So they mean to be here when it does."

Ansel said nothing. She had spent the day doing what she always did when fear came: making herself small, making herself useful, checking the map-sigil one more time against the memory of the dead courier's final journey. But there was nothing left to check. The map had led them here. The dead man's last steps ended at the heart of the ruin, before a wall that showed no door at all—only a blank stretch of outlined stone, smooth as an unwritten page.

She walked to it now, alone, while the others argued softly behind her about watches and sightlines. Up close the wall hummed. Not sound exactly—a pressure, a feeling in the teeth and behind the eyes, like standing near a struck bell that has not yet finished ringing. She pressed her palm flat against it. The stone was warm.

*Thirteen turns,* the courier had whispered, dying in her cellar with his blood cooling between the flagstones. *The moon is wrong. Count them. Count them and you'll see.*

And she had counted. Every night since Merrowgate, she had counted, and every night the moon had been a hair wrong—a sliver too wide, a shade too copper, rising a finger's width out of true. A moon no almanac admitted to. A moon the Ministry had written out of the sky along with everything else, but which the sky itself, being older than ink, had never quite agreed to forget.

Tonight it would finish its turn.

Behind her, Osk spoke again, softer, meant for her alone. "You feel it, don't you. The wall."

"It's warm," Ansel said.

"It's waiting." He set down his knife. "A sigil is a held breath, Ansel. That's all magic ever is—something held so long it becomes solid. The Ministry has been holding this country's breath for thirty years. Tonight someone lets it out."

"And then?"

He smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle smile. "Then we find out who we are."

* * *

Dusk came down like a lid.

They ate cold food in the shadow of the nameless wall—hard bread, dried fish, water from a spring that tasted faintly of iron and, strangely, of roses. No one talked much. Brava checked the approaches twice, three times, moving through the ruined streets with a deserter's silent economy, and each time she came back more grim.

"They're not hurrying," she reported. "That's what worries me. An army that hurries is afraid. This one isn't. They're coming the way tide comes."

Pip, who could not sit still and never had, climbed the colonnade again and went rigid. "Lights," he breathed. "On the north road. Lots of lights. And—" he squinted, and his face did something complicated, the face of a child remembering something he shouldn't, "—and they're singing."

"Singing?" Brava's knife stopped mid-stroke.

"Ministry hymns. The kind they play at unmaking ceremonies." Pip shivered. "I heard one once, in Vell, through a wall. You don't forget it. It's designed so you can't."

Ansel rose. Far off across the darkening valley, a line of lanterns wound down from the ridge, orderly as a rosary being told. And beneath the wind she heard it now too: a low, sweet, terrible chanting, hundreds of voices in unison, a melody with no bottom to it, a song shaped like forgetting. Her skin crawled. She had sold memories for years; she knew what stolen things sounded like. That song was made of stolen things, ten thousand voices' worth, worn smooth by use.

"They'll reach the outer streets by full dark," Brava said. "We have maybe two hours. Maybe less."

"Then we hold the wall for two hours," Ansel said.

"With what?" Brava laughed, short and bitter. "Four people and a blind carver? Ansel, I've seen what the Ministry brings to an unmaking. There won't be a fight. There'll be an erasure. They'll sing us out of existence and go home to supper."

"Maybe," Ansel said. "But the Door opens tonight whether they like it or not. All we have to do is be standing here when it does—and make sure that when it opens, it's us at the threshold, not them."

Osk had begun, very quietly, to carve.

* * *

The thirteenth moon rose at the hour when even owls doubt themselves.

It did not rise like other moons, up from behind the hills. It rose out of the ground—or so it seemed—swelling up through the ruin itself, through the outlined streets and the scrawled names, a great copper disc the color of old blood and new coin, hanging low and huge and utterly wrong. Its light fell sideways. Shadows pointed toward it instead of away. Where it touched the ruin, the outlines deepened: ghost-walls gained ghost-color, a faded fresco of a harvest moon flickered briefly into gold wheat and laughing faces, and along the doorframes the scrawled names began, very faintly, to glow.

"Oh," Pip said, in a small voice. "Oh, it's beautiful."

And then the wall opened.

There was no roar, no thunder. The blank stretch of stone simply became a seam—a vertical line of light, thin as a knife cut, widening slowly, patiently, the way dawn widens. Through it came a light that was not copper and not white but something older than either, a light like the moment before remembering. Warm air breathed out of it, smelling of rain on dust, of bread, of a summer none of them had lived but all of them suddenly grieved for.

The Door of Thirteenth Moon.

No one moved. Even Brava had forgotten her knives. The seam widened, a hand's width, a shoulder's width, and through it they could see—not another country, not freedom, exactly, but a kind of depth, a distance, a road going somewhere real.

"It's open," Ansel whispered. "Osk, it's actually open."

"Yes." Osk's voice shook. He was facing the Door with his carved sigil held against his chest, and tears ran freely down the old map-lines of his face. "Thirty years, and it's open. Now comes the hard part."

"What part?" Pip asked.

"The part where everyone wants it," Osk said.

The hymn had stopped.

In its place: silence, and then boots—hundreds of boots, in step, coming up the ruined avenue with the patience of tide. Lantern-light bloomed street by street. And at the head of the column, walking alone, unhurried, came a figure in a hunter's gray coat, and even at that distance Ansel knew the set of those shoulders, the tilt of that head, the way the lantern-light slid off him like rain off oiled leather.

Corvane.

He carried no drawn weapon. He carried something worse: a ledger, open in one hand, its pages fluttering. And as he walked he was reading aloud—not orders, not demands, but names. Names of charm-sellers, of deserters, of a blind letter-carver, of a child. Their names, assembled from a hundred interrogations, spoken into the night air like a summons.

"He knows we're here," Brava said. "He's known the whole time."

"He's not looking at us," Ansel said slowly.

He wasn't. Corvane walked with his eyes fixed on the Door, on the seam of impossible light, and his face—she could see it now as the lanterns closed the distance—his face was the face of a man reading his own death warrant and finding, in the fine print, something unexpected. Something that looked almost like hope.

Behind him the Ministry column fanned out through the ruin, gray coats filling gray streets, and officers began shouting the ritual words of unmaking, and the singers took up their hymn again, closer now, loud enough to shake dust from the outlined walls—

—and the scrawled names on the doorframes began, all together, to answer.

A sound like a thousand voices exhaling rose out of the ruin itself. The pilgrims' names lifted off the stone and hung in the air, glowing, refusing the hymn, and the Ministry's song faltered, cracked, wavered—for a hymn built on forgetting has no defense against remembering.

Corvane stopped twenty paces from the Door. From Ansel.

He closed the ledger.

"Ansel Ishikawa," he said, and his voice was strange, stripped of its hunter's polish, raw underneath. "Or whatever your name is tonight. The Ministry ordered me to bring you back alive. The rest—" he glanced at Osk, at Brava, at Pip crouched on the colonnade with his fists clenched, "—the rest are to be unmade on site."

"And you?" Ansel asked. "What do you order yourself to do?"

For a long moment he said nothing. The Door breathed its summer light between them. Behind him, the hymn swelled; around them, the names burned brighter in answer; overhead the false moon hung fat and copper and patient, waiting to complete its turn.

"I brought you something," Corvane said finally, and reached into his coat. "I stole it from you years ago and I've worn it like armor ever since. I think—" his hand trembled, and the hunter who never trembled trembled, "—I think it was never mine to keep."

The Ministry's front rank raised their lanterns. The hymn reached its crescendo. And the thirteenth moon, directly overhead now, began—to the naked eye, unmistakably—to move.
