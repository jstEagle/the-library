## 18. The Price of the Door

The thirteenth moon did not rise so much as *arrive*—the way grief arrives, the way a debt arrives, all at once and long expected.

Ansel stood in the heart of the ruined capital with her face turned up to it. It hung low over the broken city, a pale coin of light that cast no shadows, or rather cast them in the wrong directions, so that every outline of Cael Sarn seemed lit from within. The buildings stood as they had stood for years: walls without substance, doorways opening onto nothing, streets drawn in silver lines like the scaffolding of a house not yet built. But tonight the outlines trembled. Tonight they breathed.

"It's answering it," Pip whispered beside her. The boy's hand was wrapped around hers, small and hot and gripping hard enough to hurt. He was staring at the moon with his mouth open, and Ansel remembered—she would always remember—the first time she'd seen him do that, in the market at Vell, at a conjurer's trick worth less than a copper. Pip remembered everything he shouldn't. Right now he looked like any child looking at any impossible thing: undone by wonder.

"Answering what?" Brava asked. Her voice was rough. She had not slept since the salt flats, since the ambush, since the word *traitor* had settled on her like a coat she couldn't take off. She stood apart from the rest of them, arms crossed, as if she might still walk away if she decided to. She wouldn't. Ansel knew that now. People who meant to leave didn't stand so close.

"The Door," Osk said.

The old letter-carver had gone ahead of them through the ruin, his carved stick tapping the ghost-stones, reading the city the way he read everything—by touch, by absence, by the shape of what was missing. Now he stopped at the center of the great square, where once—Pip said, breathlessly, remembering—a fountain had stood, and where now there was only a ring of pale stone and a seam of light.

It ran through the air itself. A vertical line, taller than a man, thin as a knife's edge, glowing faintly gold, like the crack of dawn under a door left unlatched. It made no sound. It cast no warmth. And yet Ansel felt it against her skin the way you feel a held breath in a quiet room.

"Thirteen turns," Osk murmured. "Thirteen moons, thirteen seals, thirteen names struck from the ledger." His blind eyes turned toward her, and though they saw nothing, Ansel felt seen more thoroughly than she ever had. "It's waiting for someone to knock."

* * *

They had come through the drowned villages and the memory-haunted roads; they had crossed a bridge that existed only because enough people believed in it; they had lost the map and found their way anyway, guided by a dead man's last journey lodged in Ansel's mind like a splinter of light. They had buried Brava's shame halfway between blame and forgiveness, which is the only place shame can be carried. They were five when they had set out from Merrowgate. They were four now, and the fourth was a boy who should never have been allowed to come, and none of them would have sent him back for anything.

And now the Door stood before them, and it was smaller than Ansel had imagined. That was the strange part. All this way—all these weeks of fear and hunger and running, all this weight of country and history—and the way out was a seam of light no wider than her hand.

"Ansel." Osk's voice dropped low. "Before anyone touches it. Before anything. Listen to me."

She turned. The old man's face was grave in the shadowless light, every line of it carved deep, the way he carved letters into wax and bone and the lintels of houses that no longer existed.

"I told you the map was also a sigil," he said. "I told you the Ministry's seal sits over this whole country like a lid on a pot. What I didn't tell you—what I only understood when I touched the stones here—is how the seal was made."

"Made?" Brava frowned. "Seals are stamped. You press them down and—"

"They're paid for." Osk's voice was very quiet. "Every sigil costs something. The Ministry's inspectors pay in other people's memories, stolen and spent. But a seal this size—" he spread his hands, taking in the pale outlines, the trembling air, the whole erased shape of Cael Sarn—"a seal this size needed a name. A true one. Freely given."

Ansel felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs.

"Whose?"

"No one remembers," Osk said simply. "That was the price. Whoever gave it isn't in anyone's memory anymore—not even their own. They gave their name to close the country, and the giving took them out of the world's accounting entirely." He paused. "But sigils can be unmade the same way they're made. Same currency. A true name, freely surrendered—at the threshold, under the thirteenth moon."

The wind moved through the square, and it smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet, and of woodsmoke from fires that hadn't been lit in twenty years.

"And passage?" Ansel asked. Her voice came out steady. She was proud of that, later. "What does passage cost?"

Osk's silence answered before he did.

"The same," Pip said.

They all looked at him. The boy stood with his chin up, the way he did when he was about to say something everyone else already knew and no one wanted spoken.

"The same thing. Don't you see? It's a door out of an erased country. Nothing gets through for free—nothing real. You want to walk out and live in Vell and buy bread and be nobody special, you have to give the Door your name forever. Not a charm-name. Not a market-name. Your true one. The one your mother called you." His voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and he pushed through it. "That's why the Ministry doesn't guard it. That's why they let it exist. Because almost no one will pay."

* * *

Ansel walked toward the seam of light.

She didn't decide to. Her feet decided, the way they had decided things all her life—slipping through night markets, stepping over thresholds, always moving, always keeping the weight of herself distributed so no single step could betray her. Twenty years of practice at being light-footed, and now her feet carried her to the one place in the world where lightness was the whole price.

Up close, the Door hummed. Not sound exactly—more a feeling in the teeth, in the bones behind the ears, the way a struck bell feels before the note reaches you. She raised her hand. The light did not burn. It passed over her fingers like water, like the memory of water, and in its glow she saw—

Herself. Reflected. But wrong.

The reflection wore her face, but younger. Eight years old, maybe nine. Standing in a kitchen that didn't exist anymore, in a house in a city that had no name, with flour on her hands and her mother's voice calling from another room—calling a name.

*Ansel.*

Not Ansel Ishikawa. Just—*Ansel.* The way you call a child. The way a name sounds when it's not written anywhere, not owned by any registry, not a brand burned into a citizen's papers. Just a word someone loved, spoken across a warm room.

Her mother's voice. She had sold a hundred memories of mothers' voices to drunk merchants who wept into their cups and thanked her for it. She had never once kept one of her own.

"Ishikawa," she said aloud, testing it. The Door drank the words. "Ansel Ishikawa, of Vell, of nowhere, thief of small sorrows, seller of borrowed joys." The litany of her trade, her armor, her careful constructed self. "That's what they know me by. That's what the Ministry hunts."

The reflection-child looked at her with her own eyes and waited.

*Give it,* the Door seemed to say. Or perhaps that was only her own longing, reflected back. *Give me the false name and walk free. Forget the country. Forget the seal. Forget the dead courier whose last journey lives in your head like a splinter. Forget the boy holding your hand an hour ago. Walk out into Vell and be no one, safely, forever. Isn't that what you've practiced your whole life?*

Isn't it?

Ansel's hand trembled in the light.

Because here was the truth she had built her rules around, the truth under the truth: she had spent twenty years making herself forgettable on purpose. Never steal grief. Never steal twice. Never stay. Never be known. Every rule a wall, every wall a brick in the fortress of her anonymity, and she had told herself it was craft, it was survival, it was wisdom—but it was also fear. Fear of being seen. Fear of being taken. Fear that if anyone ever truly knew her, they could take her the way the Ministry took people: wholly, cleanly, leaving nothing but a smooth place where a person used to be.

And now the Door offered her the same bargain the Ministry offered, dressed in gold instead of gray. Surrender yourself and be safe. Be no one, and no one can hurt you.

She thought of the vision they'd shown her in the Ministry's kindness-chamber, weeks ago—the painless life, the soft blank morning, the tea that tasted of nothing because nothing was required of it. She had nearly said yes. She had opened her mouth to say yes. And then she had heard, somewhere inside her, the dead courier's whisper: *the moon is wrong, the moon is wrong,* and understood that some wrongness is worth dying inside of, because the alternative is a rightness with nothing in it.

"Ansel?"

Brava's voice, behind her. Rough. Afraid—for her, Ansel realized. For her, specifically. The deserter who had betrayed them and been forgiven stood three steps back like a soldier guarding a retreat that hadn't happened yet.

"If you go," Brava said, "we go with you as far as the edge. That's not nothing. That's—"

"That's everything," Ansel said. "I know."

She looked at the reflection again. The child in the kitchen. The flour on her hands. Somewhere beyond the frame of that memory, a whole country waited—fountains and markets and mothers calling names across warm rooms, all of it pressed flat under the Ministry's seal, all of it held in the dark by one stolen name and twenty years of enforced forgetting.

And here was the arithmetic, sudden and terrible and clear:

One name could buy her freedom. One name could also break the seal. The Door did not care which. The currency was the same. The question was only what it purchased.

A life. Or a country.

*You could pass through alone and free.* That had been the promise she'd made herself at the beginning, back in her cellar with a bleeding man's blood cooling on her floor. Get out. Survive. Let the world burn behind you; you've only ever saved yourself, and look how well that's worked.

But she thought of Merrowgate, half-drowned, its people carrying two histories in their heads like stones in each hand, unable to put either down. She thought of the bridge that existed only because people believed together—that was the lesson of the bridge, wasn't it, the lesson she'd felt in her bones while Brava screamed at Pip to keep believing: *nothing holds unless it's shared.* She thought of pilgrims' names scrawled on doorframes in the ruin, the mourning that had gone on for twenty years in secret, in chalk, in whispers, because no one was allowed to grieve out loud.

She thought of Pip, who remembered everything he shouldn't, standing behind her with his hand empty now because she'd let go of it to walk here, and who would grow up in a country that officially did not exist, carrying a country inside him that no one else was permitted to carry.

And she thought of the nameless one who had built the seal. Someone had stood here, once, under this same wrong moon, and paid the same coin—and chosen, or been made to choose, the closing. Chosen the erasure. Maybe they'd been promised safety too. Maybe they'd been promised mercy. Maybe they'd stood exactly where Ansel stood, hearing exactly this arithmetic, and the Ministry's kindness had worn a gentler mask that day.

Someone had to choose the opening.

"Osk," she said. Her voice didn't shake. Later she would wonder at that. "The counter-sigil. If I gave my name to breaking it instead of passing through—could it work? Truly?"

The old man came forward slowly, his stick tapping the ghost-stones, and stopped beside her at the edge of the light. For a long moment he said nothing. Then he reached out—not toward the Door, but toward her, and took her hand, and turned it palm-up, the way he read everything.

"You have ink on you," he said. "Old ink. Map-ink. Courier's blood. And underneath all of it—" his thumb traced a line across her palm that she couldn't see, that perhaps only he could feel— "underneath, the shape of a name that has never once been written down. Do you understand what that means?"

"That it's worthless?"

"That it's *whole.*" His grip tightened. "Everyone else in this country has had their name handled—registered, taxed, stamped, filed in the Ministry's ledgers. Their names are worn coins. Yours has never circulated. You kept it locked away even from yourself." His blind face turned up toward hers. "A name like that, freely surrendered at the threshold under the thirteenth moon—it wouldn't just break the seal, girl. It would shatter it. It would ring through every erased street in Cael Sarn. It would be the loudest thing this country has heard in twenty years."

"And me?"

The silence stretched.

"And me?" she asked again. "If I spend it on the seal instead of the Door—what's left of me?"

"I don't know," Osk said honestly. "No one knows. No one has ever done it. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps—" and here his voice went strange, almost tender— "perhaps the opposite of nothing. The seal was closed with a name, and the closing ate the namer. But an opening—" he shook his head. "An opening might work differently. Memory given freely restores what was taken. That's the oldest law of sigilwork, older than the Ministry, older than the moons. They stole. You would give. Those are not the same act, whatever their ledgers say."

Behind them, Pip made a small sound. Not quite a word. Ansel didn't turn around. If she turned around and saw his face, she wasn't sure what she would do, and she needed, desperately, to know what she was going to do before she saw anyone's face.

*Pass through alone and free.* The old promise. The old wall. The old brick.

Or.

She looked at her reflection one last time—the child in the kitchen, the flour, the voice calling her name across a warm room—and understood, finally, what her rules had been protecting all along. Not her life. Her name. The one thing she'd never sold, never traded, never pressed into wax for a drunk merchant's coppers. She had hoarded it like a dragon hoards, without knowing why, without knowing what for.

Now she knew what for.

"Brava," she said quietly. "When Corvane comes—and he'll come, he's coming, I can feel him the way you feel weather—he'll need to hold the square. Hold it long enough for Osk to carve."

Brava went very still. "Carve what?"

Ansel turned at last. Pip's face was wet and fierce. Osk's blind eyes were wide. Beyond them the ruin of Cael Sarn breathed in its silver outlines, and above it the thirteenth moon hung like a seal about to break, and far off—too far off to see yet, but coming, coming at the speed of certainty—rode the Ministry.

Ansel smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile she wore lifting memories from drunk merchants: competent, calm, and absolutely certain of the theft she was about to commit.

"My name," she said. "All of it. Every letter. We're going to steal the country back."

* * *

Far across the plain, where the road bent down from the hills, the first lanterns of the Ministry's column flickered into view—dozens of them, then scores, a slow river of cold light flowing toward the ruin. And at its head rode a figure on a gray horse who carried, folded against his heart like armor, a scrap of childhood that did not belong to him.

Corvane felt the thirteenth moon rise, and every stolen memory in his ledger turned over at once, like pages in wind.
