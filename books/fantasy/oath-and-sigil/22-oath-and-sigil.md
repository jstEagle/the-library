## 22. Oath and Sigil

The shop on Candlemaker's Row had been a coffin-maker's once, and the smell of cedar still lived in the walls, faint as an old grief. Ansel had chosen it for that. A place where death had been handled honestly, with care and good wood, seemed the right kind of room to sell true things in.

She opened the shutters each morning to the sound of Vell unlearning its walls.

That was how she thought of it—not the walls coming down, which had happened in the first furious weeks after the seal broke, when hammers rang along the Ringway from Gate of Ashes to the Salt Post and the Ministry's grey stone came apart like bread—but the city unlearning them. For two hundred years Vell had taught itself that the world ended at the ramparts, that beyond lay only fog and the Ministry's mercy. Now carts rolled out through gaps in the masonry wide enough to drive a hay-wain through, and they came back heavy with things no one in Vell had tasted in living memory: river salt from Merrowgate, honey from the orchards above Cael Sarn's ruin, apples with names—actual names, varietals with histories—that made old women weep into their cups in the taverns.

The thirteenth moon still hung up there on clear nights, thin and silver and wrong-shaped, but no one flinched from it anymore. Children counted it aloud in their counting games: *one moon, two moons, three...* all the way to thirteen, and then laughed, because saying it was nothing now. Saying it was easy. Saying it was just air leaving your mouth, carrying a name.

Ansel swept the floor. She was very good at sweeping; she had learned patience from years of waiting for drunks to sleep deep enough to be robbed, and patience translated well to keeping a shop.

The stock was honest, which is to say it did nothing. On the shelves behind the counter sat river stones from the drowned streets of Merrowgate, dried and clean, each tagged with the name of the street it had come from. Bundles of letters written by returned citizens to relatives who had forgotten them, sold at cost to anyone who wanted to read what courage looked like in ink. Wax charms—yes, she still pressed wax, some habits were bones—but these held nothing stolen. A mother could press her thumb into warm wax beside her child's, and the charm would hold exactly that: two prints, side by side, and nothing else. No memory in it at all. Just proof. *We were here together.* It turned out that was what people had always wanted from charms, underneath everything. Not borrowed lives. Proof.

The bell over the door rang mid-morning, and Pip came in sideways, the way he came in everywhere, all elbows and momentum, a satchel of other people's correspondence banging against his hip.

"Post," he announced, though he delivered no post. He was eleven now by his own count and thirteen by Brava's, and he had grown half a head taller since the salt flats, which he mentioned daily. "Corvane says there's one for you he wouldn't let me carry. Something about it being *yours*, capital-Y yours."

"Where is he?"

"Doing the Ringway round. He'll be by." Pip dumped the ordinary mail on the counter, then lingered, eyeing the shelf of thumbprint charms with the expression of a boy conducting inventory. "You're out of the blue wax."

"I know."

"You said Tuesday."

"It is Tuesday."

"It was Tuesday *yesterday*," Pip said with the gravity of a magistrate, and left before she could argue, bell clattering, off down the Row toward whatever mischief the open city offered children who remembered too much and had finally been allowed to.

Ansel smiled at the empty doorway. She caught herself doing that more often now—smiling at nothing, at air, at the plain fact of a morning. It was a habit she distrusted slightly, the way one distrusts a limb that has healed stronger than before the breaking.

* * *

Corvane arrived at noon, as he arrived every day at noon, and Ansel had stopped pretending she didn't watch the street for him.

He wore the courier's grey still, though the Ministry livery was long gone from it—re-dyed, patched at the shoulder where Brava's knife had opened it in another life, or another version of this life. He carried the letter satchel the way he had once carried his ledger of stolen memories, carefully, both hands, as if it might spill. He had never told her directly what it felt like to have the memories taken back out of him—the ones the Ministry had fed him, her own childhood among them, the red door, the well, the girl who climbed the plum tree to count moons. When the seal broke, everything the Ministry had hoarded went home to its owners, flooding back across the country like water finding its level. Corvane had stood in the ruin of Cael Sarn holding his own head as if it belonged to a stranger, and what remained inside him afterward was mostly questions.

He had made a profession of delivering them ever since. Letters, that is. The questions he saved for her.

"The blue wax situation has reached the ears of the postal service," he said, setting the satchel on the counter. "I'm instructed to report it."

"Instructed by whom?"

"A small person with a satchel who outranks us both." He drew out the last letter, the one he'd kept back, and held it a moment before offering it. He did that sometimes—held a thing a moment first—and Ansel had learned not to ask why. Some habits were bones. His had been built in a Ministry that taught its hunters to weigh everything before letting go of it.

The letter was sealed with plain tallow, no sigil, no mark. Her name was written on it in a hand she knew.

"Merrowgate," she said.

"Brava carried it as far as the bridge. The part that exists." He leaned against the doorframe, arms folded, and watched her break the seal. He was better at watching now than he used to be. The hunger had gone out of it. Or perhaps it had simply changed direction, the way a river changes course and leaves the old bed green.

The letter was short. Brava wrote the way she fought: direct, economical, with the tenderness hidden in the structure rather than stated. She had stayed in Merrowgate through the rising of the waters' memory—when the drowned streets had drained themselves, house by house, as the land remembered being dry—and she had found work there, of all improbable things, training the village watch. Former deserters, it turned out, made excellent trainers of village watches. They knew every way a person could run.

*I am staying,* the letter said, in so many words. *I know you'll understand. I spent so long running toward a border I forgot some people are the border. The village needs someone who remembers how to be afraid properly. Come see us when the shop can spare you. Bring the boy. He'll want to see the church stand up out of the water. It does it at dusk. You won't believe it even when you're looking at it.*

P.S. *I've stopped dreaming about the amnesty papers. Thought you'd want to know.*

Ansel read it twice. Then she folded it along its creases, precisely, and put it in the drawer under the counter where she kept the letters that mattered, next to one from Osk that was really a carving—a small wooden moon, thirteen-sided, with a note burned into the flat of it: *Went north to re-carve the boundary stones. Someone has to write the country's edges by hand or the mapmakers will do it badly. Your name is on the stone at the Door. I checked. It reads clean.*

Her name was on the stone at the Door. That had been Osk's doing, and Pip's, and Brava's, and the hands of strangers whose faces she would never know—people speaking her into existence again, freely, the way she had once spoken other people's memories into wax. She had spent her name to break the seal, poured it out like the last of a flask, and for three days she had been no one at all, a woman-shaped absence standing in the returning color of her country while everyone she loved shouted her back into being.

She had thought, in those first months, that she understood the price. Survival's price paid. But the truth was stranger: the price had been paid *for* her, mostly, by other people, and the debt of that was not a weight but a practice. You paid it daily. You opened the shutters. You ordered the blue wax. You answered the door.

"How is she?" Corvane asked.

"Staying." Ansel looked up. "She's staying, and she's stopped dreaming about the papers."

Something moved through his face—relief, and beneath it the older sediment of a man who had hunted that same woman through three provinces and now carried her letters. "Good," he said. "That's—" He stopped, the way he still sometimes stopped, reaching for a word and finding the Ministry's vocabulary rotted out of his mouth. He'd been fluent in erasure once. It took time to learn plain speech again.

"That's grace," Ansel supplied quietly.

"That's grace," he agreed, and looked embarrassed, and looked at the shelves instead.

* * *

The afternoon brought customers, and Ansel loved the afternoon customers best because they were the hardest cases.

A young man came in wanting a charm for his grandmother, who had been Unmade in the old days—half-Unmade, the way the Ministry sometimes botched it, leaving a body that breathed and a mind with holes in it like moth-eaten cloth. Since the seal broke, the holes had been filling, slowly, randomly, the country's returned memories seeping back into the damaged minds like rain into dry ground. Sometimes what came back was hers. Sometimes it wasn't. The grandson wanted something to anchor her.

"She keeps calling me by my father's name," he said. "And then she cries because she knows it's wrong, but she can't find the right one."

Ansel took down a blank wax disc and warmed it over the little lamp. "Tell me about her hands," she said. "What did they do? Before."

"Baking. Bread, mostly. She had a scar here—" he touched his own wrist, "—from the oven door, when she was young."

"Put your hand here. Press hard." She guided his thumb into the softening wax, then held the disc out to him. "Take this to her. Don't explain it. Just put it in her hand and say, *Grandmother, feel how strong your thumb is.* Let her find the rest herself."

He stared at the charm. "It doesn't hold anything."

"It holds the shape of your trust," Ansel said. "In my experience that's the strongest thing there is."

He paid too much and she gave change too generously, and after he left she stood a moment with her palms flat on the counter, feeling the cedar-smelling wood, the day's warmth in it, the whole improbable fact of a legal shop selling honest nothing on a street in a city with no walls.

There were days the enormity of it ambushed her. Two hundred years of erasure, undone by a name given away at a door. And the country hadn't come back perfect—it came back *arguing*, villages disputing old boundaries, families discovering they'd been split by the rewriting and didn't entirely like each other, historians weeping over contradictory records that were suddenly all true at once. Memory, it turned out, was not a treasure. It was a country. Heavy, quarrelsome, worth dying for, impossible to keep clean.

But the bread in Vell was better. That was the thing nobody wrote ballads about. The bread was better because the wheat came from fields that remembered being fields, and the bakers remembered being bakers, and nobody had to pretend the grain had always come from the Ministry's grey granaries. Small mercies, stacked like cordwood. A life built out of them.

* * *

At dusk she closed early and walked.

This was her other practice, the private one. She walked the Ringway, or what remained of it—a promenade now, the wall's spine stripped of its teeth, planted with plum saplings by some civic committee with a sense of humor that Ansel suspected included Osk. From the high stretch near the Salt Post you could see west, past the fog-line, all the way to the hills where Cael Sarn's outline stood in the evening light: the capital rebuilt not in stone but in *tending*. The pilgrims still came, still scrawled names on the doorframes of houses that existed only as outlines, but now the outlines were roofed with glass to keep the rain off the writing, and lamplighters walked the ghost-streets at dusk lighting lamps in doorways that led nowhere, because the dead of Cael Sarn deserved light on their thresholds like anyone else.

Corvane fell into step beside her without either of them arranging it. They had stopped arranging it months ago. The body learned the hour the way it learned any appointment.

"Pip tells me you're teaching him the trade," he said.

"I'm teaching him the *ethics* of the trade. There's a difference."

"He lifted my watch yesterday. Gave it back before I noticed. Said he wanted me to know he *could*."

"That's the ethics lesson," Ansel said. "Rule one: never steal twice from one person. Rule two: never steal grief. Rule three—" she paused at the gap in the wall where the gate had been, where the evening came through unhindered, smelling of river and cut grass and someone's dinner, "—rule three is new. Never steal what someone would give you if you asked."

Corvane was quiet a moment. "The Ministry never had rules like that."

"The Ministry had one rule. Forget." She watched the last light go amber on the western hills. "It turns out forgetting is the only theft that costs the thief as much as the robbed. Everything they took, they had to carry. Everything they erased, they had to remember how to erase. They built themselves into a prison of other people's losses." She glanced at him. "You know. You lived in it."

"I lived in it wearing your face," he said. "Your red door. Your plum tree. I used to dream about climbing it before I knew what it was." He shook his head, but gently, at himself. "I still don't fully understand why you didn't hate me. At the Door. With the Ministry coming and me standing between you and the seal."

"Because you stepped aside," Ansel said. "Everything else is detail."

Below them, lamps were coming alight along Candlemaker's Row, and somewhere a fiddle had started up, playing one of the returned songs—the ones that had come back with the land, melodies no one in Vell had heard in two centuries that everyone somehow knew the words to. Memory given freely restores what was taken. She had learned that at the Door, in the worst hour of her life, held up by voices shouting her name. She built her days on it now, the way the bridge in the white flats had been built on belief: not a foundation you could point to, but one that held as long as everyone kept faith together.

"Come to Merrowgate with me," she said. "When the shop can spare us. Brava wants to see Pip. And I want—" she searched for it, and found it easily, which was itself still a small miracle, "—I want to see the church stand up out of the water at dusk. I want to stand on dry ground that used to be drowned and feel it under my boots."

"When?"

"Soon. After the blue wax comes in." 

Corvane laughed—one short breath of it, real—and they stood together in the gateless gateway while the fiddle played and the thirteenth moon rose thin and silver over the country that remembered, and Ansel Ishikawa, who had once survived by selling other people's lives in wax, went home through the open dark to her own.
