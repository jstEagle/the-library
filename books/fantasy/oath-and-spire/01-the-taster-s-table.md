## 1. The Taster's Table

The pheasant arrived under a silver dome, and beneath the dome, as always, was the question: is this the night?

Tamsin Blackwood had asked it so many times that the asking had worn smooth, like a river stone carried in a pocket for years. He stood at the king's left hand in the long amber light of the Great Hall, and he lifted his spoon, and he did not let his hands shake.

Twelve years of practice will do that. Twelve years of swallowing small deaths and walking away from them.

The pheasant smelled of juniper and roasted plum, of butter worked through the breast, of the faint char where the spit had kissed the skin too long. His tongue catalogued all of it before the first swallow — that was the discipline, the litany every taster learned before they were permitted to stand at a monarch's table: *smell first, then the rim of the sauce, then the flesh, then wait.* The waiting was the part no one saw. The court saw a man dip a spoon and nod and step back, and they thought the whole art lived in the dipping. It didn't. The art lived in the ninety heartbeats afterward, when Tamsin stood very still with his hands folded and counted, and listened to his own blood for anything that sounded like an argument.

He swallowed.

Juniper. Plum. Butter. Salt — a hair too much salt, which meant Cook Aldern had been distracted tonight, which meant something had happened in the kitchens, which meant Tamsin would want to know what before he tasted the soup.

Ninety heartbeats. Sixty. Thirty. Nothing climbed his throat. Nothing prickled along his gums or pooled cold behind his eyes. The pheasant was only dinner.

"Your Majesty," he said, and stepped back from the table, and the hall exhaled around him the way it always did — that small collective loosening, the rustle of silk as nobles remembered they were hungry. King Osric the Third, grey-bearded and gout-ridden and suspicious of everything except the man who tasted his food, gestured for the serving to begin.

Tamsin withdrew to his station against the eastern pillar, where a single chair and a small table waited, because protocol demanded the taster remain visible through the meal. Protocol also demanded he be invisible, which was a contradiction he had made peace with long ago. A good taster was furniture that could die. He folded his hands. He watched the king eat.

And while he watched, he did what he always did during the second course: he ran the evening's inventory behind his eyes.

Arsenic presented as a bitter almond ghost on the breath — none tonight. Wolfsbane numbed the lips before it stopped the heart; his lips were warm. The grey mushrooms that grew along the north wall of Greyhollow's cemetery turned wine to fire in the veins; he'd had a splash of the cellar red with the fish course and felt nothing but the tannin. Belladonna dilated the pupils, and he had checked his own reflection twice in the polished silver of the dome. Nightshade, henbane, the sweetish rot of hemlock water — all absent. All quiet.

His body was a ledger, and tonight every column balanced.

It hadn't always been so simple a thing to say. There had been a time — he kept the memory in a locked room at the back of his skull, and he did not open the door often — when his body had been an open wound instead of a ledger, when every meal had been a gamble and every gamble had been someone else's idea. But that was boyhood. That was another country. Here in Greyhollow, in the amber light, he was thirty-one years old and the most carefully poisoned man in the kingdom, and if you cut him — though no one ever would, he was too valuable, too *functional* — you would find a man built in layers, like lacquer. Dose upon dose upon dose, twelve years deep, until the things that killed kings slid off him like rain off oiled cloth.

Immunity was not a gift. Immunity was a debt paid in installments, and the interest was everything else.

"The soup," murmured the steward, and Tamsin rose, and went, and dipped his spoon into the pale gold of it — leek and almond, cream, a whisper of mace — and swallowed, and counted again. Ninety heartbeats. The salt problem persisted. Something had happened in the kitchens.

He would find out after. He always found out after.

* * *

The scullery smelled of lye and cold fat and the green bite of discarded herb stems, and it was, by unspoken agreement between Tamsin and everyone who worked below stairs, the one room in the palace where a man could speak without being overheard by marble.

"You look terrible, Marta," he said.

Cook Aldern's youngest kitchen girl looked up from her basin of dishes, and her face did the thing faces did around Tamsin when they wanted something and hated wanting it — a flinch, a hope, a shame, all in the space of a blink. She was maybe nineteen. Her hands were cracked from winter water even though it was high summer outside, and there was flour in her eyebrows, and she had been crying recently enough that the evidence still sat at the corners of her eyes.

"My mum," she said. "She's — it's her breathing. The physician says her lungs are going soft, like wet paper, and there's nothing—" Her jaw set. "I heard. From Bess, who heard from the laundry girl. That you can get things. Charms."

The word dropped between them like a coin down a well.

Tamsin leaned against the doorframe and let the silence do its work. This was the other half of the discipline, the half that never appeared in any guild manual: the waiting. Let them say it plainly. Make them own it. Because if the wrong person ever heard the word *charms* in this city — and the wrong people heard everything, the Lord Protector's grey-cloaked men had ears like burrs — then the girl who said it needed to have said it clearly, and the man who sold it needed to have been somewhere else.

"I don't know what you've heard," Tamsin said quietly, "but magic is outlawed in Greyhollow. Has been for sixty years. Anyone caught selling charms hangs. Anyone caught buying them hangs beside him." He held her gaze. "So I'm going to walk out this door now, and you're going to finish your dishes, and we're both going to forget this conversation."

Her face crumpled. And then — because she was braver than she looked, braver than most of the courtiers upstairs whose courage extended exactly as far as their titles — she said, "Please."

Tamsin closed his eyes briefly.

This was the part he had never learned to make painless. Twelve years, and it still went through him like a splinter working toward the bone: the *please*, the desperation dressed in kitchen smocks, the way the ban on magic had not made magic disappear but had only driven its price up and its sellers underground, until the desperate bought their mothers' breath from a man who tasted poison for a living because he was the only seller they'd ever heard whispered about.

"I'll come by the Lantern Row entrance at the change of watch," he said. "Don't ask me for anything else. Don't tell anyone. Not Bess, not the laundry girl, no one. If my name comes out of your mouth in connection with mine in a way I don't like, I will deny you, and I will keep denying you, and you will hang alone. Do you understand me?"

"Yes." It came out small.

"And Marta." He softened, just slightly, because fear made bad customers and worse liars. "What does your mother take for the pain?"

"Willow tea. When we can afford the willow bark."

"Then she takes too little. Steep it stronger, and add honey and a spoonful of rendered goose fat — don't make that face, it coats the lungs, the old wives knew what they were about. Half the charm-sellers' stock is old wives' knowledge wearing a better coat." He pushed off the doorframe. "The rest is what I'll bring you."

He left her scrubbing dishes with her head down, and he took the servants' stair up and then, at the third landing, the narrow window-passage that let out onto the roof of the buttery, because he needed air and he needed not to be seen having feelings about it.

Greyhollow at dusk, from above, was a city holding its breath. The mountain reared up behind the palace like a sleeping animal — the Spire, they called the peak, though no road went to it and no map in the kingdom marked what was under it, and the snow on its crown never melted, not in June, not ever, and everyone agreed this was simply how mountains were. The city spilled down its flank in tiers of slate roofs and chimney smoke, and beyond it the river ran brown-gold with summer meltwater, and the lamplighters were moving through the lower streets with their long poles, lighting the city one flame at a time against the coming dark.

Somewhere down there, a girl named Marta was terrified for her mother. Somewhere down there were dozens more like her — he'd stopped counting at forty — who knew the taster only as a rumor with kind eyes and a locked box. Somewhere down there, the Lord Protector's men were walking their rounds, and if any one of them followed the right rumor home, Tamsin Blackwood would end his life on a gallows between a hedge-witch and a midwife, and the king would have a new taster within the week, because that was the arithmetic of power: every man was replaceable except the ones nobody noticed replacing.

Tamsin breathed the cooling air and thought, as he did most evenings, about the ledger of himself. Twelve years of doses. Arsenic, acquired drop by drop in the guild cellars until his body shrugged at it. Wolfsbane. Hemlock. Nine varieties of mushroom toxin, three mineral poisons, the distilled essence of a river eel's spine that the southern kingdoms favored. His blood was a museum of everything that had ever tried to kill a king, and he was its curator, and curators did not get to leave.

That was the bargain he'd struck at fifteen, starving, in a guild cellar that smelled of vomit and candle smoke, signing his name to a document he hadn't been able to read all of. *Serve the crown. Swallow its fears. In exchange: wages, lodging, and the slow construction of a body that death couldn't get a grip on.*

They had never mentioned, in that document, the loneliness. Perhaps it had seemed self-evident.

A taster could not drink with friends — one careless cup of unlabeled wine, and twelve years of careful work meant nothing. A taster could not eat at another man's table, could not accept so much as a honey cake from a market stall without running the same litany, smell first, rim, flesh, wait. A taster learned to hold his spoon like a shield and his tongue like a lockpick, and he learned that every human gesture of trust — *here, try some of mine* — was, for him, a small ambush. So he stopped going to tables. He stopped being invited. The courtiers called him *the Pale Spoon* behind his back, half in awe and half in pity, and he pretended not to hear, and he went home each night to his narrow rooms above the chandlery and ate food he had cooked himself from ingredients he had inspected himself, and he was alive, magnificently, unprecedentedly alive, and some nights the aliveness felt less like a prize than a sentence served in excellent conditions.

*One day,* the guild master had told him, the day he earned his chain of office, *one dish will find you. Some poison we haven't dosed you for. Some new thing out of some far country. You cannot build immunity to everything, boy. You can only build it to everything that has already tried.*

Tamsin had nodded, fifteen and invincible-feeling, and thought: *not me. I'll be careful forever.*

Now, thirty-one and tired, watching the lamplighters work, he understood what the old man had actually been saying. It wasn't a warning about poison. It was a warning about time. Everyone's dish found them eventually. The trick — the only trick — was to be so busy counting heartbeats that you never noticed yourself dying of something slower.

He climbed back inside. He had a delivery to prepare.

* * *

The charm he made for Marta's mother took twenty minutes and cost him more than it would cost her, though she'd never know it.

He worked at the little table in his rooms, by candlelight, with the shutters closed and a wool blanket hung over the door to keep the light from leaking into the alley. On the table: a saucer of river water collected at dawn. A sprig of coltsfoot, bought from an herb-wife who asked no questions. A length of blue thread. And a sliver of beeswax, into which — this was the illegal part, the hanging part, the part that made his palms sweat even now, twelve years in — he pressed his thumb and *asked*.

Not aloud. Never aloud. Asking was a feeling, a shaping, a leaning of the mind against the world the way you'd lean against a door that might or might not be locked. The hedge-folk called it knocking. Mother Hessel, who had taught him, back when teaching it to anyone was worth both their necks, had called it *the oldest courtesy*: you knock, and sometimes the world answers, and what comes through is small and warm and smells faintly of summer, and you catch it in wax or thread or bread before it dissipates, and you give it to someone whose lungs are turning to wet paper, and it holds the wet paper together a little longer.

It wasn't much. It wouldn't cure anything. It was a candle in a drafty room. But the woman would breathe easier for a season, maybe two, and her daughter would sleep, and the world would contain slightly less suffering than it otherwise would, and if that was worth a gallows then Tamsin supposed he'd been volunteering for the rope one charm at a time for years.

He sealed the wax around the coltsfoot and the thread, murmured the closing — a wordless closing, a shape of gratitude — and wrapped the finished charm in brown paper, plain as a parcel of buttons.

Then he sat back, and rubbed his eyes, and allowed himself, as he did every night, sixty seconds of honest accounting.

Assets: one body proofed against nearly everything. Wages sufficient. A trade, illegal, that kept his heart from calcifying entirely. A routine that fit him like a well-worn glove fits a hand — snug, familiar, and shaped by years of use into exactly the form of his own confinement.

Debts: one childhood he preferred not to revisit. One friend whose name he did not say anymore. Forty-odd customers whose survival depended on his continued freedom. And a fear he kept in the same locked room as the rest of it: that the ledger would not balance forever. That some night a dish would arrive under a silver dome carrying something new, something patient, something from across the sea or out of the old stories, and his layered, lacquered, magnificent body would look at it and have nothing on file.

He blew out the candle, tucked the parcel inside his coat, and went down into the lamplit streets to make his delivery, past the curfew bells, past the grey-cloaked patrol who nodded to him — the king's taster was known, respected, waved through doors other men were stopped at — and he did not see, because no one in Greyhollow ever saw it, the fine white sifting of snow that had begun, impossibly, at the crown of the Spire.

In June.

It fell without sound. It fell on unmelted snow that had never once, in living memory, grown thinner. And far beneath it, in a court that had no name on any map, something ancient turned a page in a book a century old, and read a line aloud in a voice like frost forming:

*"The taster and the cartographer shall come to us by winter's door."*

The ink was still wet.
