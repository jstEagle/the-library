## 2. A Cartographer's Hands

The pastries were not stolen so much as borrowed with intent.

Tamsin had learned the distinction early. A stolen thing announced itself — in the hands, in the walk, in the way a man's eyes flicked toward every guard in the square. A borrowed thing walked out of the palace kitchens tucked under an arm like it belonged there, wrapped in a cloth that smelled of rosemary, carried by someone whose whole posture said *I am doing my job and my job is dull.* Twelve years of tasting for the crown had taught him that most of survival was theater: you did not sneak past people, you made them decide not to see you.

So he walked out of Greyhollow Keep at dusk with six sugared plum tarts under his arm and the unhurried gait of a man who had somewhere tedious to be.

The city received him the way it always did, with smoke and noise and the smell of the river. Greyhollow in summer was a kettle left on the coals — heat rising off the cobbles in visible waves, offal carts and fishmongers shouting each other down, the mountain above the city wearing its perpetual cap of cloud like a crown no one had asked it to hold. Tamsin moved through it all the way water moves through cloth: present, unnoticed.

He was heading for the bridge steps — the ones where the lamplighters started their rounds and the watch changed shift and nobody who mattered ever looked twice at anyone — when he saw the man drawing the city.

That alone was enough to stop him. Not the drawing; Greyhollow attracted draftsmen the way a wound attracts flies, all of them hired by some guild or other to make the streets look tidier than they were. But those men worked from towers and rooftops, squinting through brass instruments, rendering the city as a diagram of itself. This man was sitting on the lowest step of the bridge with his boots nearly in the river muck, sketchbook balanced on one knee, drawing *down* — the underside of the bridge, the waterline stains on the pilings, the way the moss grew greenest where the laundry water drained from the dye-houses. He drew the city as it actually was: stained, sagging, alive.

And he was laughing. At nothing. At the moss, maybe. There was no one near him, and still his shoulders shook with quiet amusement, as though the world were a joke he was politely letting himself in on.

Tamsin should have kept walking. He had tarts to sell — or rather, charms disguised among tarts to deliver to three different desperate households, which required a certain amount of unremarkable movement through the evening streets. Instead he found himself stopped, tart-wrapped arm going slowly numb against his ribs, watching a stranger find the bottom of a bridge worth laughing about.

"You're dripping on your work," Tamsin heard himself say.

The man looked up. He had ink on his fingers, ink on his jaw where he'd pushed his hair back without thinking, ink on the cuff of a coat that had once been good and now was merely honest. His eyes were the grey-brown of river water over stones, and they went to Tamsin's face with an attention that felt, absurdly, like being tasted.

"Am I?" He lifted the page. A drop of river spray sat at its corner, blooming soft into the paper. "Well. The bridge weeps. I'm only transcribing." He said it lightly, but then his eyes dropped to Tamsin's arm, to the shape of the cloth bundle, and sharpened. "Is that — forgive me. Is that a plum tart?"

"It is several plum tarts."

"From the keep?" The man's voice climbed with genuine wonder, the way another man might have spoken of relics. "They do a plum tart in autumn that I've thought about for two years. Two years. I was mapping the northern passes and I caught myself sketching it instead of the ridge lines."

"That seems like a professional failing."

"I'm Aldric Vane," the man said, offering an ink-stained hand, and then, before Tamsin could take it or decline it: "Commissioned by the crown, before you ask why a grown man sits in the mud. They want the low town mapped properly. Every stair, every arch, every drain. Apparently the last surveyor got lost between the tanneries and the Shambles and was found three days later having drawn the same alley four times with increasing despair."

Tamsin took the hand after all. Ink transferred immediately to his own fingers, and he found he didn't mind. "Tamsin," he said, and then, because a name alone invited questions: "I work in the keep."

"What house?"

"The kitchens."

It was the safest lie he owned. It fit like an old glove: kitchen staff came and went, carried things, stood in doorways, were beneath notice by design. The truth — *I put poison in my mouth for the king's benefit and sell small illegal charms to the desperate* — was not a thing one said on a bridge step at dusk.

"A kitchen man with a scholar's delivery stride," Aldric Vane said, and smiled, and Tamsin felt the smile land somewhere below his ribs where he kept nothing, where he was careful to keep nothing.

"They're only tarts," he said.

"Everything is only something," Aldric said, closing his sketchbook. "Bridges are only stone. Cities are only crowds. And yet here we all are, drowning in meaning." He stood, brushing mud from his coat with limited success, and looked at the bundle again with naked, unembarrassed longing. "Would it compromise your professional dignity terribly if I begged one? I'll trade you. I know where the lamplighter's boy hides the good honey wine, and I owe him a favor I've been saving."

Every instinct Tamsin had honed over twelve years said: *walk away. Men who laugh at bridges want something. Men who look at you like that are either trouble or bait, and in this city the two are sold side by side.*

He heard himself say, "One tart. And the wine better be good."

* * *

It was good. It was, in fact, excellent — sharp and dark and faintly smoked, decanted into two tin cups that Aldric produced from a satchel containing, as far as Tamsin could tell, an entire profession: compasses, dividers, rolled vellum, stubs of charcoal wrapped in waxed cloth, a folding rule worn silver at the joints, and a small tin of candied ginger that he offered around like communion.

They ended up on the upstream parapet of the bridge, legs dangling over the slow brown water, passing the cups back and forth while the lamplighters worked their way along the embankment and the first true dark of evening settled into the streets. Tamsin ate his tart slowly, making it last. Sugared plum, butter thick enough to leave a shine on the fingers, pastry that shattered rather than crumbled. He ate like a man who knew exactly what food could carry, because he did — he'd watched kings' suppers carry hemlock and arsenic and once, memorably, ground glass sifted into a honey cake meant for a visiting duke. Food was never only food. But this tart, eaten beside a stranger who laughed at moss, came as close to innocent as anything he'd touched in years.

"Tell me if I have this right," Aldric said. He'd turned his tin cup in his fingers, studying Tamsin with that same attentive weight. "You carry pastries out of a royal kitchen at sundown. You walk like a courier and eat like a man saying grace. You flinch — just slightly, don't argue, I draw for a living, I notice edges — when the watch went past, and you didn't flinch at the drop under us, which means you're comfortable with heights but not uniforms." He paused. "And when I said *crown-commissioned* earlier, something behind your eyes did arithmetic."

Tamsin kept his face pleasant and his heart very still. "You see a great deal for a man who draws bridges."

"I see everything for a man who draws bridges. It's the seeing I'm paid for; the drawing is just how I prove I wasn't dreaming." Aldric shrugged, and let it go — genuinely let it go, Tamsin realized, with a strange little shock. No probing follow-up, no clever trap. He simply refilled both cups from the bottle and said, "You don't have to tell me what you are. Half the people in this city are somebody's secret. It's why the light here is so interesting. Everything's lit from the side."

The wine was loosening something in Tamsin that he had nailed shut a long time ago. He watched the river carry a fallen blossom under the bridge and thought about how long it had been since he'd sat anywhere with anyone without calculating exits. Three years? Five? There had been the widow Maren, who bought fertility charms until her husband grew suspicious; there had been old Fen, the ratcatcher, dead now. Conversations, yes. Transactions. But sitting — idle, purposeless, drinking stolen-adjacent wine on a public bridge with a stranger's shoulder a hand's width from his — that was a country he'd emigrated from so long ago he'd forgotten the language.

"Why drains?" he asked, to keep from thinking. "Why would the crown pay good coin for the low town's drains?"

Aldric's expression shifted — the humor staying in place but something more serious arriving underneath it, the way current runs under a calm surface of water. "Because of the snow," he said.

"There's no snow. It's midsummer."

"Mm." He drank. "There was snow on the Spire's shoulders yesterday morning. Above the cloud line, where there shouldn't be anything but bare rock in July. And the wells on the north side are running cold — cold enough that the dyers are complaining their vats won't set color." He turned the cup again. "The Lord Protector's office doesn't say *we're worried about the weather.* They say *map the drains,* because drains are how a city freezes from underneath, if a city were going to do such a thing. Which it isn't. Obviously." He smiled his easy smile, and it didn't quite reach whatever was behind it. "Obviously."

Something moved through Tamsin then, cold and thin, like the first thread of draft under a winter door. He set it aside. He was practiced at setting things aside; it was half his profession.

"You take your commission seriously," he said.

"I take maps seriously. A map is a promise that the world will stay where you left it." Aldric glanced sideways at him, and the lamplighter's oil-flame caught the ink smudge on his jaw and made it look like a beauty mark. "That's the whole trade, really. Cartography and trust."

"And what happens," Tamsin asked, hearing his own voice go quieter than he intended, "when the world doesn't stay put?"

Aldric was silent a moment. Downriver, a barge slid past with its lanterns doubled in the black water, going nowhere fast. "Then you redraw," he said finally. "That's the other half of the trade. You grieve the old lines and you draw new ones. My mother taught me that. She lost our house to a flood when I was nine — the river took the whole lane — and she stood on the hill with the neighbors weeping, and then she handed me charcoal and said, *draw it, love, before you forget it, so we can miss it properly.*" He laughed softly, not the bridge-moss laugh but something older. "First map I ever finished. Our own drowned street. I've been redrawing the world ever since, trying to get it to sit still."

The warmth in Tamsin's chest was becoming a problem. He identified it clinically, the way he identified bitter almond in a sauce — *there it is, note it, prepare accordingly* — but identification did nothing to slow it. The evening was soft, the wine was good, the man beside him drew drowned streets so they could be missed properly, and Tamsin Blackwood, twelve years a function, was for one hour feeling like a person. It was the most dangerous thing that had happened to him since his first immunity trial, and he recognized the danger perfectly well, and he stayed on the bridge anyway.

"Here." Aldric flipped to a fresh page in his sketchbook, licked his thumb, scrubbed at a corner of ink, and began to draw quick and sure: the bridge, the parapet, two figures seated with cups. In forty strokes they existed — hunched, laughing, unmistakably themselves. He added the lamplight as short radiating dashes, the river as three curved lines, the keep on its crag above as a shadow with lit windows. Then he tore the corner free — a barbarism that visibly pained him — and held it out.

"For the kitchen man with the courier's stride," he said. "Proof you weren't dreaming either."

Tamsin took it. The paper was warm from Aldric's knee. Two figures on a bridge, rendered in forty strokes of another man's certainty. He looked at them for longer than was reasonable.

"This isn't accurate," he said. "I wasn't laughing."

"You will be," Aldric said, "if you come back Thursday. Same step. I've been promised access to the undercrofts beneath the Shambles, and no sane person should go down there alone, and I suspect you're not entirely sane."

It was a bad idea. It was such an obviously bad idea that Tamsin could have written a treatise on it: attachments were liabilities, liabilities were leverage, leverage got people killed, and the people who got killed were never the careful ones — they were whoever the careful one had failed to push away. He had a friend's grave behind that lesson, and a boy's hunger, and twelve years of small poisons building walls around whatever this was trying to become.

"Thursday," he heard himself say.

Aldric grinned, packed his satchel with the cheerful violence of a man who organized nothing, and walked backward a few steps along the embankment, raising the tin cup in salute before turning away. Tamsin watched him go until the lamplight gave him up.

Then he sat alone on the parapet with the torn corner of paper in his hand and the last of the honey wine going flat, and told himself the arithmetic he always told himself: *nothing given, nothing owed, no name given truly, no trail left behind.*

But his fingers, traitorously, had already folded the little drawing with care — corners matched, crease pressed flat — and tucked it into the inner pocket of his coat, the one over the breastbone, where he kept the only things he'd ever bothered to protect.

Above the city, high on the mountain where July had no business reaching, the cloud around the Spire's shoulders glowed faintly white, like frost catching moonlight.

Tamsin noticed it. He noticed, and filed it, and forgot it — the way a man forgets the taste of something bitter when he's busy learning the taste of sugar.

Thursday, he thought, and walked home through the warming streets, humming a tune he didn't know he knew.
