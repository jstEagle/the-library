## 3. What the Archive Kept

The royal archive of Greyhollow smelled of cold stone and older paper, a scent Aldric Vane had loved since boyhood—the smell of the world before it was drawn, waiting patiently for someone to come along with ink and intention. He climbed the spiral stair each morning with his satchel of pens, a flask of chicory that had gone bitter hours ago, and the particular joy of a man paid by the crown to do exactly what he would have done for free.

His commission sat folded in his breast pocket: *Aldric Vane, cartographer, is engaged to survey and catalogue all maps, charts, and geographic folios held in the Royal Archive of Greyhollow, that they may be properly indexed against future need.* The words *future need* were vague in a way he found charming rather than ominous. Bureaucracies always wrote like that, as if the future were a room down the hall someone had simply forgotten to furnish.

Three weeks in, he had reached the third gallery, the one where the light came down through windows of bottle-green glass and everything it touched turned the color of deep water. Dust hung in the shafts of morning sun like slow golden snow. He worked alone up here; the archivists kept to the lower floors where the ledgers lived, and the king's scholars rarely climbed past the second gallery unless summoned by some crisis of succession or grain tariff. The silence was total except for the scratch of his pen, the whisper of vellum, and—on windy days—a low moan in the chimney flues that sounded almost like a voice practicing speech.

He catalogued as he read. That was his indulgence: no map passed through his hands without him actually reading it, tracing coastlines with one finger, murmuring place-names to himself like a man greeting old acquaintances. There was an *Atlas of the Salt Marches* with marginalia in six hands, three of them arguing about the same estuary across two hundred years. There was a chart of the northern passes so old its ink had gone brown as dried blood, marking a road through the mountains that no modern map admitted existed. He set that one aside with special care. Roads that vanished from the record interested him more than roads that stayed.

It was on the ninth day of the third gallery that he found the book without a title.

It was shelved spine-inward, which archivists did only with damaged volumes, wedged between a tax census from the reign of Aldous the Fat and a treatise on well-water that someone had clearly used as a doorstop for decades. The boards were oak, scarred at the corners, and when he drew it out into the green light, dust rose off it in a slow bloom.

Inside, the first page bore no title either—only a line of verse in a hand so fine it looked printed:

*What is written is written, and what is written waits.*

"Cheerful," Aldric said aloud, because talking to books in empty rooms was one of the privileges of solitude, and turned the page.

He expected almanac. Farmers' verses, weather lore, the usual bones of century-old handbooks. Instead he found prophecy—or what wore prophecy's clothes. The entries were dated, but not by any calendar he knew; they counted down rather than up, years falling away like leaves off a tree, and each entry described something small and strange in language that circled without landing.

*A woman will sell her shadow for bread and buy it back at double cost.*

*A bell will ring in a tower that has no tower.*

*Two brothers will quarrel over a field, and the field will outlast them both.*

Folk-prophecy, then. The kind sold at fairs next to love charms and cures for toothache, the kind that said nothing until afterward, when believers combed the verses to find their own lives hiding in them like faces in firelight. Aldric had catalogued a dozen such books already. He smiled, settled onto his stool, and prepared to be gently entertained.

Then he reached the last quarter of the book, and the dates stopped being a hundred years gone.

They began counting toward now.

* * *

He noticed it the way you notice a stair that isn't there—one moment of ordinary descent, then the lurch. The entries had been moving forward through the decades, each verse a little closer to the present, and he had been reading them the way you drift downstream, hardly aware of the current. Then the handwriting changed. Not decayed—refined, as if the same pen had grown surer, more urgent—and the verses stopped being riddles and started being directions.

*In the year the summer snows begin, look to Greyhollow.*

*The taster will sit at a table of enemies and live.*

*The cartographer will draw a road he does not know exists.*

Aldric's pen hovered over his catalogue sheet and left a small blot, the first of the day.

Coincidence. He wrote the word mentally, firmly, the way one presses a thumb on a loose thread. There were tasters in every court in the world; it was an ancient trade, older than the crown itself. And cartographers—well. The archive held perhaps forty volumes touching on maps. Any prophecy-writer wanting to seem mysterious need only reach for the trades of the court and let chance do the rest. He had argued precisely this point with a colleague once, over dinner, gesturing with a spoon: prophecy was astrology for people who liked poetry. It told everyone what they already feared in language vague enough to fit anyone.

But these verses weren't vague anymore. They had names.

He saw the name before he finished deciding to see it. It was simply there on the page, in that fine brown-black ink, in the middle of a stanza, and his eye went to it the way a compass needle goes north—without permission, without thought.

*The taster and the cartographer will walk together into the dark beneath the mountain,*

*and there the bargain will be broken or the bride will be taken,*

*and the snow will have its answer.*

*Tamsin, who has swallowed death and made it familiar.*

*Aldric, whose lines are true and whose road ends below.*

The archive's silence, which had seemed companionable all morning, became enormous. He could hear his own pulse in his ears. He could hear the wind working at the flues, that almost-voice moaning somewhere above him in the dark of the chimneys, and for the first time in three weeks of solitary labor he wished there were another person in the room.

Because the name was his. Not a near-name, not a cousin of his name spelled oddly by some provincial scribe. *Aldric*. His mother had chosen it from her grandfather, an uncommon name, a name he had never shared with anyone in Greyhollow—he'd checked, idly, years ago, the way people check such things. And the ink under it was brown-black and flaking at the edges, and the board covers of this book were scarred oak, and the volume had been shelved spine-inward between a tax census and a treatise on wells in a gallery nobody visited, in a book written—

He turned carefully to the flyleaf. No date. But the watermarks in the paper, the style of the letterforms, the particular way the scribe formed his ampersands—all of it belonged to the last century. He had spent enough years handling old documents to date paper in his sleep. This book was old. Old the way ruins are old. Old enough that a boy named Aldric Vane had not yet been born when someone dipped a pen and wrote his name in a stanza about walking beneath a mountain and not coming back.

"That's not possible," he said to the book, conversationally, reasonably, the way one reasons with a dog caught on the table.

The book said nothing, which he appreciated.

He laughed. Actually laughed—a short, startled sound that bounced off the stone and came back thinner. Of course it wasn't possible. Someone had written this recently and aged it, or written it as an elaborate joke, or—and this was the explanation he clung to, hauling it up like a rope—someone long ago had simply been lucky. A century of prophecy, hundreds of verses, and somewhere in all that noise a pair of names would land right eventually, the way a blindfolded dart-thrower hits the wall eventually. Names were small targets if you threw enough darts.

Except the verses didn't stop at names. He read on, hunched over the book now, his chicory forgotten and cold beside him.

*The taster carries his deaths inside him like coins in a purse, and counts them nightly.*

*The cartographer's hands remember every country, and will forget his own.*

*What was sold will be sought. What was bought will burn.*

*Beware the ferryman who asks for less than you can spare.*

Each line landed somewhere under his ribs with a soft weight, like snow sliding off a branch. *Will forget his own.* His own what? Country? Name? He thought of his mother, dead four winters, and felt an irrational flare of protectiveness toward the memory of her, absurd as guarding a lit candle with your bare hand.

And underneath the laughter he was still wearing, unease settled in and began unpacking its bags.

He was a practical man. Maps taught you practicality: the world was large, your ink was finite, and sentiment did not hold a bearing. So he did what a practical man does with an impossible document. He examined it.

The ink, under his loupe, was iron-gall, properly made, aged uniformly—not brushed on to fake antiquity but sunk in, part of the fibers, the way real age sinks into skin. The parchment had been scraped and rewritten in places, earlier verses ghosting up beneath later ones when the light struck sideways, which meant someone had kept this book for a long time and kept writing in it, correcting it, refining it—as if the prophecy were a draft. As if whatever spoke through it was still learning the language.

That detail frightened him more than the names. Books don't revise themselves. People revise books. Which meant someone had been tending this thing, generation after generation, the way you tend a fire you're afraid to let go out.

*What is written is written, and what is written waits.*

He copied the page.

He told himself it was scholarship. That was the story he wore over the fear, buttoned up neat: a cartographer cataloguing an uncatalogued text should make a faithful copy for the record; the original was too fragile to move; duplication was procedure. All true. None of it was why his hand shook slightly as he cut fresh paper and chose his finest nib, or why he reproduced not just the stanza with his name but the whole final quarter of the book, every verse, every correction, even the ghost-lines that surfaced under angled light—because some instinct older than his skepticism whispered that the parts that didn't make sense yet were the parts that would matter most.

He worked through the afternoon while the green light moved across the floor and climbed the shelves and died. His copy grew precise, loving, exact—his hand doing what it did best, remembering lines, holding shapes. Twice he stopped, pen lifted, because he'd caught himself reading a verse aloud under his breath like a prayer, and both times he laughed again, quieter, and bent back to work.

By lamplight he finished. He blotted the last page, weighed the stack of papers in his hand—it was heavier than paper should be, he thought, though he knew that was nonsense—and considered, seriously and for a long moment, leaving the copy in the archive with the rest of his work. Filing it. Letting it become someone else's problem in fifty years.

Instead he folded the pages small and put them in his breast pocket, opposite the commission, where they lay against his heart like a second, colder contract.

* * *

He was late to supper because of it, hurrying down through streets going blue with evening, and so he was still slightly out of breath, still rattled, when he collided—nearly literally—with Tamsin Blackwood at the corner by the baker's, and forgot the whole thing for one entire hour.

That hour matters, though Aldric wouldn't know until much later how much. Over stolen pastry—Tamsin had charmed the baker's wife with a jar of honey wine and Aldric had done the stealing, laughing, both of them giddy with the small crime—they talked about nothing. Rivers. The way the city smelled after rain. Whether the mountain really hummed on certain nights or whether everyone who claimed so was drunk. Tamsin had a laugh Aldric wanted to hear again the way a man wants a song stuck in his head to keep playing, and eyes that measured everything twice, and when he smiled it was like watching a door open in a wall Aldric hadn't known was there.

Walking home afterward through the lamplit fog, Aldric touched his breast pocket once, feeling the folded pages, and felt the two halves of his day press together: the warm hour and the cold book, the living voice and the waiting ink.

*Tamsin, who has swallowed death and made it familiar.*

He had laughed at a prophecy that afternoon. He was not laughing now. Now, standing in his doorway with the fog curling around his boots, he was a scholar again, and scholars know the oldest rule of their trade better than anyone: things get copied down for a reason, and the reason is rarely comfort.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would show him. Not the whole book—just the page. Just to watch his face. Just to hear what a man who tasted poison for a living had to say about a hundred-year-old verse with his name in it.

Somewhere far above the city, in the flues of the archive, the wind found a chimney and made it moan, almost like a voice, almost like a name.
