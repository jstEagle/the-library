## 4. Ink That Remembers

The charm stall did most of its business in the hour between the lamplighters and the closing of the market gates, when the honest folk had gone home and only the desperate remained, hooded, hurrying, coins clutched like contraband. Tamsin had learned to read desperation the way he read a dish: by scent first, then texture. The widow with salt-reddened knuckles wanted a charm for her son's cough. The apprentice with ink-stained cuffs and a bruise he didn't explain wanted one for courage. The old soldier who came every month without ever quite meeting Tamsin's eyes wanted sleep that didn't dream.

He sold them all something. Not magic—never quite magic, though the line was thinner than any of them knew, thinner than he let himself remember. Knots tied in horsehair at certain hours. Salt sewn into linen with words murmured over it that were half prayer and half recipe. His mother's people had called these things charms; Greyhollow's law called them witchcraft; Tamsin called them what they were: small comforts, priced fairly, wrapped in brown paper so no one could see themselves buying hope.

He was tying off the last knot of the evening—the soldier's dreamless sleep, three strands of hair from his own head because the strongest knots used the maker's—when the curtain across the stall's back entrance twitched, and Aldric Vane stepped through it as if he'd been invited, which he hadn't, which was somehow worse.

"You said you'd come to the archive at dusk," Aldric said.

"I said I might."

"I've decided to interpret that optimistically." He set a paper-wrapped bundle on the counter, right on top of the dream-charm, and Tamsin moved it aside with two fingers before it could absorb bad luck. "Meat pastries. From the place near the fountain. The one you pretended not to love while eating four of them."

"Three."

"Four, and you folded the last one's corners so precisely I assumed it was part of your profession." Aldric leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, and the lantern light caught the ink stains on his fingers—the permanent ones, ground into the skin around the nails, the mark of a man who had spent his life holding pens the way other men held tools. "I need to show you something. And I'd rather do it here than in my rooms, because here there are knives within reach and you strike me as a man who knows how to use them."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Twelve years in the king's service taught a man to weigh every visitor: threat, fool, or customer. Aldric Vane sat strangely across all three categories, and had since the night they'd shared stolen pastry crumbs on the archive steps and laughed until a guard told them to move along, and Tamsin had walked home through the rain without his cloak because he'd given it to a shivering mapmaker and hadn't regretted it once.

"That's an odd thing to say," Tamsin said, "to a man whose job is tasting poison."

"It's an odd thing I have to show you." For the first time since Tamsin had known him, Aldric wasn't smiling. That frightened Tamsin more than anything the page might contain, though he didn't yet know it would contain anything at all. "Please, Tamsin. Before I talk myself out of thinking it's nothing."

* * *

They went up. Tamsin's rooms above the stall were narrow and smelled of dried herbs and lamp oil and, lately, faintly of another man, because Aldric had begun leaving traces the way weather leaves traces—a second cup that never quite got put away, a pencil sketch of the harbor on scrap paper, a coat hook that now held two coats. Tamsin had told himself the traces meant nothing. He had told himself this while straightening the second cup so it sat exactly beside his own.

Aldric sat on the stool by the window and unwrapped his satchel with the care of a surgeon. Out of it came a leather folder, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with archive cord. Archive cord. Tamsin's stomach tightened before the knot was even undone. He knew what the royal archive sealed its documents with; every taster learned the seal-cords too, because food sometimes traveled with paperwork and paperwork sometimes traveled with poison.

"This stays between us," Aldric said. "If the Protector's office learns I copied it—"

"Copied *what*?"

Instead of answering, Aldric unfolded the oilcloth and laid the contents flat on Tamsin's table: a sheet of heavy rag paper, browned at the edges, covered in Aldric's precise cartographer's hand—but the hand was wrong tonight, hurried, pressed too hard, letters slanting where they should have stood square. A copy. Beneath it, wrapped separately, a rubbing taken in black wax of what was clearly a much older page, the original text ghosting through like bones under skin.

"The archive has a sealed section," Aldric said. "Sub-basement, behind the old ordinance vault. Most clerks never get keys. But I've been cataloguing the collection for the crown for eight months, and I'm thorough, and thoroughness opens doors." He turned the copy toward Tamsin. "Three nights ago I found a book. No title. Bound in hide I couldn't identify. The catalogue says it entered the collection a hundred and twelve years ago, bequest of some minor noble house, provenance unknown. It reads like prophecy. The real kind—not the fortune-teller kind, the kind that makes archivists lock things in sub-basements."

"Aldric."

"Read it," Aldric said quietly. "Read it and tell me what you see."

Tamsin looked down at the copied page.

The verse was in Old Court script, the formal hand used for charters and coronation oaths, but the language itself was older, stranger, with the compressed, rhyming weight of nursery rhyme gone wrong:

> *When snow falls ripe in summer's keeping,*
> *And kings grow cold while courts are sleeping,*
> *The taster and the cartographer meet*
> *Where mountain swallows river-street.*
>
> *The taster's tongue has swallowed death*
> *Twice six years, and twice six breath;*
> *His name is Blackwood, born of briar,*
> *Called Wren in childhood, marked for hire.*
>
> *The cartographer draws the way*
> *With hands that sold what hands can't pay.*
> *Vane his name, his soul the fee,*
> *Bought beneath the spire-tree—*
> *And one shall break the winter's vow,*
> *Or wear the frost-ring as a bride.*

The room went very quiet. Outside, a cart rattled over cobblestones; somewhere below, the market gate groaned shut for the night. Tamsin became aware of his own heartbeat the way he became aware of bitterness in a broth—suddenly, totally, with the whole body listening.

*Called Wren in childhood.*

Nobody called him Wren anymore. Nobody living. His grandmother had called him Wren, before the hunger year, before the guild, before he learned that names were things you surrendered like coats at a door. She'd died when he was nine. There was no record of the nickname anywhere—no guild document, no census entry, nothing official. It had lived only in her mouth and then, briefly, in his own memory, private as a scar in a place clothing covers.

And this book had entered the archive **a hundred and twelve years ago**.

"That's—" His voice came out steady, which surprised him. Trained steadiness. The same steadiness he used to say "the soup is clean" while his palms sweated. "That's not possible."

"No," Aldric agreed. "It isn't. I've checked the accession ledger myself, three times. Ink analysis, if you're wondering—I know how the paper was sized, I can date the watermark on the original folios. A century old, at minimum. Possibly older." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, smearing a fresh streak of ink across his brow, and Tamsin's chest did something complicated and painful at the sight. "I laughed. In the archive, alone, at midnight, I actually laughed, because someone wrote a prophecy about us, Tamsin. Us. You and me. A century before either of us was born. And then I stopped laughing, because I kept reading, and the details are—" His jaw worked. "*Wren.* Who calls you Wren?"

"My grandmother." Tamsin hadn't said the name aloud in twenty years. It tasted strange, like a dish from a cuisine he'd eaten as a child and never since. "She's dead. There's no record. How does a book that predates my birth know what she called me?"

"That's rather the question, yes."

"And 'twice six years.'" Tamsin counted without meaning to, the way he counted everything—doses, intervals, grains. Twelve. "I've been tasting for twelve years. Exactly. My immunity trials started when I was eleven. The verse knows that too."

Aldric was watching him with an expression Tamsin recognized and hated: the careful neutrality of a man trying not to alarm another man who was already alarmed. "There's more," Aldric said. "The last stanza. Read it again."

*And one shall break the winter's vow, / Or wear the frost-ring as a bride.*

"One of us ends it," Tamsin said slowly. "Or one of us marries it. Whatever 'it' is." Something cold slid down his spine, and he identified it professionally, distantly: the feeling of detecting a poison too late to spit it out. "What's 'the winter's vow'?"

"I don't know. The pages after are water-damaged. The pages before are worse—someone cut them out. Deliberately. With a razor." Aldric spread his hands. "Someone, a hundred years ago, read this book and decided the world was safer without parts of it."

Tamsin crossed to the window. Below, Greyhollow lay in its ordinary evening grid of lamplight, smoke, and shadow. Somewhere in the citadel the king was eating a supper Tamsin had already tasted, spoonful by spoonful, safe because Tamsin's body was a wall built of twelve years of small poisons. Magic was outlawed in Greyhollow. It had been outlawed for three generations. The ban was written into law, carved over the city gates, preached against from every pulpit—and yet.

And yet.

His charm-seller instincts, the sense he'd never had a better name for—the one that told him a knot was tied true or false, that told him which desperate customer would pay and which would betray him, that had kept him alive through dishes that should have killed him—those instincts were screaming now, quiet and total, the way they screamed when he lifted a spoon and knew, before touching it to his tongue, that the cook had used the wrong mushroom.

This was real. This page was real the way arsenic was real. Not metaphor. Not coincidence. Magic, old and patient and horribly specific.

"There's one more thing," Aldric said. He sounded like he was walking on ice. "The verse about me. '*With hands that sold what hands can't pay.*'" He laughed, short and unamused. "I assumed it was poetry. Then I remembered—when the crown commissioned the survey of the northern passes, the contract had a clause I never understood. An unusual payment schedule. Part of my fee deferred to... I can't even describe it properly. Some instrument held in trust. My solicitor called it an annuity. I signed. Mapmakers sign absurd contracts all the time; we're flattered to be asked." He looked up at Tamsin, and his eyes in the lamplight were the color of weak tea, warm and worried. "What if I signed away something I don't know I owned?"

"Don't," Tamsin said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't finish that thought. Don't follow it down." Tamsin heard his own voice go flat and hard, the guild voice, the voice he used in the scullery when a junior taster panicked. "You start asking what you've sold and the next question is who bought it, and the answer to that is never good. I've seen contracts like yours. Not yours exactly, but—the shape of them. Desperate people sign shapes like that."

"Tamsin." Aldric rose. Crossed the small room in two steps. Took Tamsin's hands, ink-stained and warm, and Tamsin let him, which was itself alarming, because Tamsin did not let people hold his hands; hands were tools, and tools stayed sheathed. "Look at me. Whatever this is—prophecy, prank, some archivist's century-old joke—we face it the way we apparently face everything according to this book. Together. 'The taster and the cartographer meet.' That's us. We've met."

"You don't understand what you're saying." Tamsin pulled his hands free—gently, but he pulled them free—and turned back to the window because looking at Aldric while thinking clearly had become impossible. "You don't know what I am. What I've done to survive. You think the tasting is just a strong stomach. It isn't. It's—" He stopped. Swallowed. The truth sat in his throat like a bone. "Every immunity I have was bought. Every single one. And the currency was never mine alone."

"Then tell me."

"Not tonight." Because if he told Aldric tonight, with the prophecy page lying on the table like an open wound, the telling would tangle with the dread and he'd never untangle which fear belonged to which thing. "Tonight I need you to take this copy and the rubbing and put them somewhere no one will find them, and tomorrow I need you to go about your day as though nothing has changed, and I need—" His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and he hated himself for it with a professional intensity. "I need to think."

"All right." Aldric gathered the papers with those careful hands, rewrapped the oilcloth, tied the archive cord. At the door he paused, and for a moment Tamsin thought he would reach out again, and braced himself for it, wanting it, dreading it, the twin pulls balanced as precisely as scales. "Tamsin. The nickname. Your grandmother—what else did she call things?"

"What?"

"Wren. Small bird. In the old herb-lore, wrens were messengers. They carried word between worlds." Aldric smiled, faint and crooked. "Scholar's habit. Everything connects. Sleep well."

He left. The stair creaked under him, the street door sighed, and Tamsin stood alone in his narrow room with the lamp burning low and the ghost of warmth still on his palms.

* * *

He did not sleep. He did the opposite of sleep: he worked.

He took down the locked box from under the floorboard beneath the bed—the box that held his ledgers, his recipes, his hoard—and he began, methodically, to inventory his immunities the way a miser inventories coin. Arsenic, acquired gradually over four years. Monkshood, the hardest-won, three near-deaths to build tolerance. Nightshade family, various. Hemlock. The bitter metals. The unnamed thing he'd swallowed at nineteen that had stopped his heart for the length of a prayer and started it again on its own, which the guild physicians had recorded as "anomalous recovery" and which Tamsin had privately filed under other names entirely.

Twelve years. Twice six. The verse knew.

He thought about the phrase *marked for hire*. Born of briar—his grandmother's cottage had grown wild roses up its eastern wall, briars that caught his sleeves and drew blood he'd licked off his thumb without thinking, because children do. Marked for hire: apprenticed, sold to the guild by a starving mother for the price of a winter's bread. The verse even knew that. The verse knew everything, and it had known everything for a hundred years, sitting in a sub-basement waiting for two specific men to be born and find each other.

Prophecies didn't work that way. Coincidence didn't work that way. Contracts, though. Contracts worked exactly that way. Someone had made an arrangement, long ago, with terms this precise, and terms this precise always had a collector.

And the collector had Aldric's name.

Tamsin closed the box. Sat on the edge of the bed with his head in his hands. And here was the thing he had been circling all night, the thing worse than the dread, worse than the prophecy, worse even than the possibility that Aldric had signed away his soul in a clause about deferred fees:

Somewhere between stolen pastries and a lent cloak and a second cup that never quite got put away, Tamsin Blackwood had made an error no professional should ever make. He had let someone matter. Twelve years of perfect discipline, of keeping every human heart at arm's length the way he kept every dish at knife's distance—and one cartographer with ink-stained fingers and a crooked smile had walked straight through walls Tamsin hadn't even known he'd built.

And the prophecy said one of them would lose.

*Break the winter's vow, or wear the frost-ring as a bride.* Either ending cost. Either ending, and Tamsin already knew with the certainty of a man who has tasted enough deaths to recognize the flavor of one brewing, that he would pay whatever the breaking cost, and that if it came to wearing the ring instead—if it came to choosing between his own survival and Aldric Vane's—he would choose with no hesitation at all, and that terrified him more than any poison ever had.

Because he had spent twelve years building a body that could not easily die.

And in one evening, a hundred-year-old page of ink had shown him a thousand ways to lose something worse than his life.

Dawn was graying the window when he finally blew out the lamp. Below, the market would be stirring; the honest folk rising; somewhere in the citadel, cooks lighting fires for a breakfast he would taste as he always did, calm and steady, the wall that walked.

But first—he had an errand. There was a woman at the edge of the Ropewalk district who had once been a hedge-witch before the ban, who owed him a debt for a charm he'd sold her granddaughter, and who would know what *the court beneath* meant, because Tamsin had finally admitted to himself what his instincts had been screaming since midnight:

The winter in that verse wasn't weather. And whoever had bought whatever Aldric signed away would not stay bought quietly.

He dressed in the dark. And if his hands shook slightly as he buttoned his collar—if the great immune Taster of Greyhollow, the man who swallowed death daily, trembled at nothing but a thought—it was nobody's knowledge but his own, and he carried it down the stairs into the waking city like a coal cupped in bare palms.
