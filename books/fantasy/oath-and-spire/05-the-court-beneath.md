## 5. The Court Beneath

The archive's references led nowhere and everywhere at once.

Tamsin spent three days following them the way a man follows a scent through a market — one thread of ink, then another, each breaking off in some clerk's careful marginal note or a page eaten to lace by damp. *Beneath*, the old texts kept saying. *Beneath the mountain, beneath the law, beneath the ice.* A surveyor's journal from eighty years past described a door in the roots of the Spire that "opens only for those who have made peace with dying," which was either poetry or a warning, and Tamsin had learned the hard way that in Greyhollow it was usually both.

A customs ledger from 1147 recorded a tithe paid not in coin but in *names*. A bishop's private diary, sealed until his death, mentioned "the cold court" exactly once before the rest of the entry had been scraped away with a knife — someone had wanted it forgotten badly enough to ruin the vellum doing it.

And every trail, every half-erased line, circled back to the same word: *Hessel*.

He heard the name first from a fishwife who bought fertility charms off him twice a year and talked too much while she counted out coppers. Then from a lamplighter with a cough he'd sold a lung-charm to at a loss, because the man had children and Tamsin was weak in ways he pretended not to be. *Old Mother Hessel. Down past the tanneries, where the water runs grey. She'll see you if she wants to. She doesn't want to, mostly.*

Tannery Row stank the way only Greyhollow could manage — lime and rot and old blood boiled into the very stones, a smell that got into wool and hair and stayed for days. Tamsin walked down it in the blue hour before dawn, hood up, hands in his sleeves, feeling naked without a basket of charms to justify his presence. The ban on magic was twelve years old and enforced unevenly; the rich bought licensed alchemists and called it medicine, while women like Hessel were stripped of their names in the guild rolls and left to rot by the water. He had built a small quiet business in that gap between law and mercy. He had never once thought of himself as brave for it. Standing before Hessel's door, he thought maybe he should have.

The house leaned into its neighbors like a drunk leaning on friends. Salt-crusted shutters. A roof patched with boat tar. And over the lintel, so faint he almost missed it, a smell that stopped his breath: rowan and iron and something older underneath, the scent of a charm made properly, made whole, the kind he could only counterfeit.

He knocked.

Nothing. He knocked again, and a voice came through the wood like gravel poured slowly. "I don't buy, I don't sell, I don't heal, and I don't care. Go home."

"Tamsin Blackwood," he said. "Royal food-taster."

A long silence. Then, dry as old paper: "Then you're already dead, dear, you just haven't been told. Come in before someone sees you being stupid."

* * *

Inside smelled of smoke and dried yarrow and, beneath it all, that same true-magic scent, thick enough now to taste. The single room was crowded the way a nest is crowded — bundles of herbs hanging from every beam, jars of cloudy liquid ranked on shelves, a hearth banked to coals that glowed a color coal shouldn't glow, a soft greenish white like foxfire trapped in stone. An old woman sat in a chair by the fire with her feet wrapped in three layers of wool, and she looked at him with eyes gone pale as frost but sharp as needles behind the paleness.

"So," said Mother Hessel. "The king's spoon. They say you've swallowed more deaths than a plague doctor."

"They say right."

"And yet here you stand, hale and sour-faced. You build your immunities." It wasn't a question. She'd seen through the trick that had kept him alive and employed for twelve years, the slow accretion of doses, the poison taken like medicine. "Guild-trained, then. Or worse — self-trained. Self-trained is worse. Guild men die clean."

"Self-trained," Tamsin admitted. "I need to know about the Undercourt."

Something moved across her face — not surprise. Recognition, the way a scar recognizes weather. She turned back to the fire and was silent long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer at all.

"I knew a girl once," she said finally, "who went looking under the mountain for her brother's soul. This was before the ban, when folk still remembered why the ban would someday be needed. She came back with snow in her lungs in July and her brother's laugh in her mouth, and it wasn't her laugh and it wasn't his either, and she wept whenever she heard music for the rest of her life. Sit down, taster. Pour yourself something. This is a long story and it goes downhill the whole way."

He sat. He didn't pour anything; he'd stopped drinking other people's liquids decades ago, and she noticed and smiled without warmth, the smile of one professional acknowledging another.

"The mountain," Hessel said, "is hollow. Not empty — hollow. There is a difference, and the difference has teeth. Under the rock, under the roots of the Spire they built their palace on top of like a lid on a pot, there is a court. Old things. Older than the kings, older than the names of kings. Fae, if you want the pretty word. The Winter Court, the Cold Court, the Undercourt — call it what you like, they answer to none of the names because they gave up answering to anything before this city had walls."

"What do they want?"

"Warmth." She spat the word like a seed. "Not heat. Warmth. There's a distinction, and the distinction is everything. Fire they can make themselves — they have fires down there, great cold-burning ones, blue and beautiful and useless. But warmth — living warmth, soul-warmth, the thing that makes a person *person-shaped* inside — that they cannot make. That they must buy."

"Buy from whom?"

"From us, dear. From everyone. From anyone desperate enough." Her voice had gone flat and careful, a woman walking on ice she knew was thin. "Why do you think Greyhollow has the debt-laws it has? Why do you think the crown forgives certain debts in certain winters, and certain families simply... stop? Every generation, the Lord Protector's office keeps a list. People whose debts cannot be paid in coin. People who will sign anything. And once a year — sometimes twice, if the winter below runs hungry — a clerk comes to them with a contract written on paper that never yellows, and they sign away the only thing they own outright, and they walk home lighter and colder and they never know quite why the world tastes of ash after that."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. Twelve years of tasting had taught him every flavor fear could take; this was the oldest one, copper at the root of the tongue. "Souls."

"Candles," Hessel corrected. "That's how they keep them. A soul won't sit still in a jar — it gutters, it grieves, it goes out if it isn't fed. So they render them down, the way we render fat. A soul becomes a candle, and a candle burns, and the burning feeds the winter. Their winter is endless because our souls are endless, or were, once. Now—" she shrugged, and the shrug carried forty years of disgrace in it, "—now the court below buys faster than the city breeds, and they've begun taking contracts from people who aren't even desperate yet. People with futures worth stealing."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside a cart rattled past toward the tannery gates, an ordinary sound in an ordinary city, and Tamsin felt the ordinary city tilt beneath him like a deck in rough water.

"Why Aldric?" he asked, and hated how raw his voice came out. "He's a mapmaker. He draws coastlines. What possible use—"

"Because a mapmaker sees everything and belongs nowhere." Hessel turned her frost-pale eyes on him. "Because the court below has no maps of itself — did you think fae nobility bother with cartography? They know their halls the way a tongue knows its teeth, but the deep places, the places even they have let fall dark — they want eyes for those. Fresh eyes. Mortal eyes, that see mortal-wise and can be sent where fae pride forbids them to go. And most of all—" she leaned forward, and the foxfire coals lit her face from below, hollowing it into something ancient, "—most of all, because a soul that has been *named in prophecy* burns brighter than any common candle. A hundred times brighter. A soul the old books have already claimed is worth ten thousand ordinary ones. Whoever sold Aldric Vane's contract knew exactly what they held."

"Sold by whom?"

"That I can't tell you. Contracts are sealed against divination — believe me, I tried, back when my art still worked well enough to try. But ask yourself, taster: who in this city knows your cartographer's value? Who benefits from a bright new candle going down the mountain? Who has been trading with the cold court since before your grandfather's grandfather learned to walk?" She let the question hang, and it hung there, enormous and obvious, wearing a crown.

The Protector's office. Generations of Protectors. The crown he had fed, dish by dish, for twelve years, had been selling its people down the mountain like cordwood, and he had stood at the king's table every night tasting for treason in the soup while the real treason sat enthroned above him signing contracts.

"Aldric signed nothing," Tamsin said. "He'd have told me. He tells me things. He—" He stopped, because the sentence forming was *he loves me*, spoken aloud to a stranger, and he couldn't carry its weight yet.

"He may have signed nothing," Hessel agreed, not unkindly. "Debts pass down bloodlines. Debts pass through guilds. Debts pass through *names* — did you know that? If your family ever swore anything to the crown, the crown holds lien on the blood. And debts can be transferred. A creditor may sell what he is owed." She watched him absorb it, and something in her expression softened by a degree, frost thinning over still water. "You love him."

It wasn't a question either, and Tamsin found he hadn't the strength to deny it. Twelve years of denial was all he had, and he'd spent it all getting down Tannery Row before dawn.

"Then listen, because I'll say this once." She gripped the arms of her chair and pulled herself upright, and standing she was taller than sitting suggested, straight-backed as a blade. "The court below honors bargains the way a knife honors flesh — absolutely, and without mercy, and always cutting along the lines drawn for it. If Aldric Vane's soul-candle sits on their shelf, it can be reclaimed. Reclaimed by payment, by theft, or by bargain struck better than theirs. But the gates open twice a year, at the turning, and the last turning before the deep winter seals everything is—" she calculated, lips moving, "—nine days hence. Nine days, taster. After that, the snow closes the passes and the doors seal and whatever happens below happens for another full season without witnesses."

"Nine days." His voice sounded far away to himself.

"And understand what entry costs." She crossed the room to a shelf, took down a jar of something dark, and set it on the table between them with a click that rang like a verdict. "The Undercourt does not admit the living out of charity. Everything that crosses their threshold owes toll, and their tolls are paid in what you *are* — memories, years, loves, protections. Everything you've hoarded, taster. Every immunity you built dose by dose, every poison your blood has learned to laugh at — down there, those aren't skills. Those are currency. They will strip them from you like coins from a dead man's purse, gladly, one by one, and each one will hurt worse than the poison that taught it to you."

"Then I'll pay," Tamsin said.

Hessel laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of someone who had watched girls come back with snow in their lungs. "Of course you'll pay. That's what makes it tragic instead of merely sad. Sit. Drink nothing. Tell me the boy's full name and his mother's mother's name if he knows it, and tell me every detail of that prophecy page, word for word, in the order the words stand, because words in prophecy are load-bearing and move one and the whole verse falls differently." She lowered herself back into her chair, and for a moment, just a moment, the frost in her eyes looked less like age and more like grief worn smooth by handling. "You'll want to write nothing down. In this city, paper remembers longer than people, and paper talks."

So he told her. The verse, the nickname no one living should have known, the line about the taster and the cartographer descending, the two endings braided together like rope — *breaker or bride* — and as he spoke, Hessel's face changed by increments, the way a sky changes before snow, until by the end she was looking at him the way he imagined one looks at a man already halfway down a gallows ladder.

"Bride," she repeated softly, when he finished.

"It means servant," Tamsin said. "Consort. Someone bound to serve the court forever. I looked through the archive references. In the old usage, a bride of the court is—"

"Is property that smiles," Hessel said. "Yes. I know the usage too. And you should understand, taster — the prophecy doesn't predict. Prophecy is not weather. Prophecy is a *contract*, the oldest kind, drafted by the court below and filed in the world above where some fool scholar would eventually copy it out and set the terms moving. It names you because you were always going to read it. It offers two endings because a contract needs consideration on both sides. Breaker or bride." Her pale eyes held his. "They wrote it a century ago, which means somewhere down in the dark, something has been waiting a century for you specifically. And things that wait a century are never impatient, because they have never once lost."

The foxfire coals settled with a sigh. Outside, far off toward the harbor, a bell rang the hour, ordinary and oblivious.

Tamsin sat very still in the herb-hung dark and did the arithmetic of his own heart. Nine days. One soul, priced. One immunity, spendable. One choice that wasn't a choice at all, because he had already made it twelve years ago in a guild cellar when he decided that surviving alone was not the same as living, and had been pretending otherwise ever since.

"Mother Hessel," he said. "How much of your art is left?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she began, quietly, to laugh again — and this time, low and rusty as an unused hinge, there was something in it that might have been hope.

"Oh, you *are* going to be trouble," she said. "Good. I've missed trouble. Ask me, boy. Ask me before I remember I'm supposed to be afraid."

* * *

He left Tannery Row an hour past sunrise with a charm warm against his sternum — Hessel's last true work, she said, woven from the dregs of her own remaining magic, and he had refused to take it until she threatened him with a curse that would make his hair fall out in the shape of an insult.

The city was waking around him, ordinary and golden in the morning light. Bakers' chimneys breathing. Gulls quarreling over the fish-market scraps. On the corner by the cooperage, Aldric stood waiting with two hot buns and ink stains on his fingers and a smile that broke across his face when he saw Tamsin coming, easy and unguarded as a door opening onto summer.

Nine days, said the arithmetic in Tamsin's chest, and the price tag hanging off his lover's soul, and somewhere beneath the mountain something patient counting down the hours.

He took the bun. He smiled back. And he began, for the first time in his life, to plan a betrayal.
