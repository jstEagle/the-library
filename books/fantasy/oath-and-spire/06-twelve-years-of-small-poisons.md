## 6. Twelve Years of Small Poisons

The past did not come to Tamsin gently. It came the way poison came—slipped into something sweet, swallowed before you knew to be afraid.

He was standing in the scullery, washing the king's tasting spoons, when the smell of tallow and ash pulled him under.

* * *

Greyhollow had been colder then, or he had been smaller, which amounted to the same thing. He was nine years old and starving in the way that city children starved: quietly, invisibly, with a full view of other people's dinners. His mother had been dead eleven months. His father had been dead longer than that, though no one had ever told him exactly how, only that the river took what the winter didn't. Tamsin had learned to eat the parts of fish that honest people threw away, and after that, the things that weren't fish at all.

The Guild of Tasters found him outside a bakery on Corvess Street, watching bread through glass like it was a window into another country.

"You look like a boy who'd eat anything," said the woman in grey.

"I would," said Tamsin.

"Even if it might kill you?"

Tamsin had considered this with the gravity of a scholar. "How much might it kill me for?"

Her name was Mistress Ondrey, and she laughed—a short, barking sound that startled pigeons off the bakery roof—and that laugh was the first warm thing he could remember in a year. She bought him a roll. He ate it so fast he barely tasted it, which, she said, was the whole problem with him, and also the beginning of his education.

The Guild of Tasters was not a guild in any official sense. Officially, there were no tasters; officially, the crown's food was protected by sworn kitchens and sealed pantries and the fear of God. Unofficially, kings had been dying of poison since before Greyhollow had walls, and someone had to stand between the fork and the grave. The Guild lived in a crooked house behind the chandlery on Tanner's Row, and its business was simple: it sold immunity.

Not protection. Immunity. There was a difference, and Ondrey made him learn it before she taught him anything else.

"Protection is a wall," she said, tapping the table with a knuckle scarred like tree bark. "Walls fall. Immunity is a door you've walked through so many times it can't surprise you anymore. We don't keep the poison out. We teach the body to yawn at it."

"And how do we do that?"

She slid a thimble of something amber across the table. It smelled of almonds, faintly, sweetly—the smell every child in Greyhollow was raised to fear.

"Small doses," she said. "Small poisons. Every day, a little more. Twelve years, if you're careful. Fifteen if you're lucky. And then you can sit at a king's table and smile while death knocks at your teeth."

"What happens if I'm not careful?"

"Then we bury you in the yard behind the chandlery, and I find another starving child." She said it without cruelty, the way one says *it will rain tomorrow*. "That's the trade, boy. Nobody's forcing you."

He drank the thimble. It burned going down and worse coming back up, and he spent the night curled against the privy wall of the Tanner's Row house, sweating and shivering while an older boy named Wren sat beside him and held a bucket and talked nonsense until dawn.

"That's the first gate," Wren said. "There are forty more. Arsenic. Belladonna. Foxglove. Hemlock, if you're brave. The Queen's Tears, if you're mad. Each one you pass, they write your name deeper in the book."

"What book?"

"The book nobody reads out loud." Wren grinned. He was maybe fifteen, all elbows and freckles, with a gap in his teeth that whistled when he spoke. "You'll see."

* * *

The trials took twelve years because Tamsin was careful, and careful was slow.

He learned to hold still while his body argued with him. He learned the difference between a poison that killed and a poison that merely wished you dead—how arsenic settled in the bones like an unpaid debt, how belladonna made the world bloom and blur, how foxglove slowed the heart until you could count the spaces between beats and wonder if there would be another. He learned to eat small and chew slowly and never, ever let them see him flinch, because the Guild's clients paid for calm, and calm was the product.

But the thing he learned best—the lesson stitched into him deeper than any antidote—was this: immunity is not free.

Every dose he survived had been measured out by someone who had watched others die measuring it. The Guild's yard behind the chandlery held more graves than the house held beds. Ondrey kept the ledger herself, in her own hand, and once—only once—Tamsin saw her writing in it late at night, and understood that each line was a name, and each name was a dose that had gone wrong.

"We're not murderers," she said, without looking up. She had known he was in the doorway; she always knew. "We're honest about arithmetic. The crown eats safe because somebody swallows the risk. That's true whether the somebody is a guild or a slave or a fool who loves his king. At least we write our names down."

"Does that make it better?"

"No." She capped the ink. "It makes it *counted*. There's a difference, and the difference matters, but it isn't better. Don't let anyone tell you otherwise—not the Protector's men, not the priests, not yourself at three in the morning. What we do costs. That's the whole truth of it. Everything in this city that keeps people alive is built on somebody else's loss, and the only sin is pretending yours doesn't stink."

He was fourteen, and he hated her a little for saying it, and he believed every word.

* * *

The first charm he sold was a coward's charm, and he was proud of it anyway.

He was sixteen, and by then he had a small talent that had nothing to do with swallowing. The Guild house kept its own remedies—bitter infusions, clay tablets, little sewn sachets of charcoal and herb—and Tamsin had begun, without quite deciding to, making his own. Better ones. A taster learns chemistry the way a soldier learns geography: intimately, and with his life. He knew which binders slowed absorption, which oils carried toxin away from the gut, which bitter roots made the stomach clench shut against harm. He began folding these into paper twists no bigger than a thumbnail and selling them for coppers to the desperate.

This was illegal. Everything worth doing in Greyhollow was illegal; magic had been banned for two generations, and the law drew no fine line between hedge-craft and chemistry, between a blessing and a bitter pill. A charm was a charm. Sell one, and if the wrong ears heard, you burned—or worse, since the Protector's men had gotten creative.

So he sold quietly. Scullery doors. Laundry yards. The corner behind the fish market where the watch never bothered walking because the smell defeated them. His customers were washerwomen whose husbands drank things they shouldn't, dockhands afraid of rivals' knives, a girl from the silk row who was being poisoned by inches by a jealous sister-in-law and paid him in buttons and secrets. He charged what they could pay, which was rarely what they should have paid, and he told himself the arithmetic balanced somewhere he couldn't see.

The first twist he ever sold went to a baker's widow on Corvess Street—the same street, though she didn't know it, where a woman in grey had once bought a starving boy a roll. The widow's son had enemies among the coal carters, and someone had already tried the flour bin twice.

"It won't stop a knife," Tamsin told her. "It's not for knives. If they come at him with steel, nothing I sell will help."

"But the flour—"

"The flour, yes. For a while. Poisons change. You'll need to come back."

"How much?"

He looked at her hands, cracked from oven-heat and lye, and named half of what he'd meant to. She paid it in small coins counted out one at a time, as if each one hurt, and he took them and hated himself and slept well that night anyway, which confused him for years afterward.

Ondrey found out, of course. She always knew.

"You're selling outside the Guild," she said.

"I'm selling *better* than the Guild."

"Yes." She turned his sample twist over in her fingers, sniffed it, wet the corner and touched it to her tongue with the casual courage of a woman who had been poisoning herself for thirty years. "Yes, you are. That's the problem. The Guild sells survival. You're selling hope, and hope has a way of making people bold, and bold people get noticed, and noticed people hang." She set it down. "Do it anyway. But do it smart. Never the same corner twice. Never a name you can't forget. And never—" she caught his chin, forced his eyes up to hers, "—never believe your own immunity makes you safe. You are immune to forty poisons, boy. You are not immune to rope."

* * *

Wren died in the autumn Tamsin turned nineteen.

It was not dramatic. That was the obscenity of it. No coup, no assassin, no grand betrayal—just a bad batch. The Guild had taken delivery of new stock, a consignment of powdered Queen's Tears from the southern passes, and the strength of it ran hotter than the assay suggested. Standard trial dose. Same as a hundred times before. Wren took it at the long table with the rest of them, joking about supper, and an hour later he was grey, and two hours later he was gone, and the gap in his teeth never whistled again.

Tamsin had sat beside him. Held the bucket, same as Wren had once held it for him. Talked nonsense. And none of it mattered, because some gates don't open twice, and the body does not care how much you loved the man going through it.

They buried him in the yard behind the chandlery. Ondrey wrote his name in the ledger. Tamsin stood in the cold rain and understood, finally and completely, what she had tried to tell him at fourteen: that every immunity in his blood had been bought with someone else's loss. The doses he survived were calibrated on the ones who hadn't. His calm at the king's table was paved with the yard's quiet graves. He had drunk twelve years of small poisons, and every swallow had a name attached, and most of those names belonged to boys and girls he had eaten beside, laughed beside, bled beside.

He stopped sleeping in the Guild house after that. He took rooms above a cooper's shop near the castle ward, close to the kitchens, far from the ledger. He kept selling charms—smarter now, quieter, never the same corner twice—and he kept tasting, because tasting was the only trade he had and the crown paid in coin and safety both, and because some part of him had decided that if the swallowing had to mean something, it would mean *this*: that no one at that table would die of a poisoned dish while he sat there breathing.

Grief, he learned, does not shrink with time. It just gets a smaller room to live in. He gave it the room beside his heart and locked the door, and for twelve years he did not open it, and he told himself that was the same as healing.

* * *

Now, in the scullery, with the king's spoons clean and racked and the kitchen singing its low evening song around him, Tamsin dried his hands and thought about Wren's whistle and the widow's counted coins and Ondrey's ink-stained fingers closing the ledger over names.

Twelve years of small poisons. Forty gates walked through. A body so steeped in death that death had grown bored of him.

And for what? So he could stand in warm rooms full of people who needed him but did not know him. So he could sell hope in thumbnail parcels and go home alone to a bed that stayed cold on both sides. So he could be a function wearing a man's face, immune to everything except the one thing he had never dosed himself against—

Which was, he realized with a jolt that had nothing to do with chemistry, wanting to stay alive for someone.

Aldric Vane had laughed at his joke about the oyster course three nights ago, and Tamsin had thought about it every day since, the way you worry a loose tooth. Dangerous. That kind of warmth was more dangerous than anything in the Guild's cabinet, because there was no trial for it, no graduated dose, no way to build tolerance. You either let it kill you or you locked the door and kept walking.

He was still deciding when the scullery door banged open and a kitchen boy skidded in, breathless, eyes too wide.

"Tamsin—Master Blackwood—you'd best come. The archive's sent down for you. Says there's a mapmaker asking after you by name." The boy swallowed. "Says he won't leave till you come."

Outside the high windows, over the grey roofs of Greyhollow, the first light of evening was turning strange—a pale, unseasonal silver, like frost forming in the middle of summer.

Tamsin did not notice it yet. But it noticed him.
