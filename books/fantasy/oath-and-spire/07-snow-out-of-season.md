## 7. Snow Out of Season

The snow came at noon, in the middle of the plum harvest, while the market stalls still smelled of sun-warmed fruit and the cobbles held the day's heat like a banked oven.

Tamsin Blackwood was crossing the palace yard with a tray of tasting spoons when the first flakes fell. He stopped. Everyone stopped. A hundred people in that yard, and every one of them went still as painted figures, watching white drift down through golden afternoon light, landing on hot stone and dying there in small wet stars.

"Snow," someone said, stupidly, the way a man says *fire* or *blood*.

Tamsin had tasted for the king for twelve years. He knew what wrongness smelled like — bitter almonds under honey, rot beneath a glaze, the sweetish chemical note of metals that had no business near food. Wrongness had a texture. It made the back of his teeth ache.

This was that. The air itself was wrong, the way a dish is wrong before you can name the ingredient. Warm day, high summer, and snow falling anyway — not cold snow, either. He caught a flake on the back of his wrist and it did not melt. It sat there, perfect and six-pointed, cold as a coin from a dead man's pocket.

Around him the yard erupted into noise. Servants ran. A guard captain started shouting orders about the granaries. Somewhere a bell began to ring, arrhythmic, panicked, the bell-ringer clearly having abandoned any pattern in favor of making as much sound as possible.

Tamsin stood very still and let the flake sit on his skin, and thought, with the strange calm that twelve years of poison had taught him: *this is aimed at something.*

Snow out of season was not weather. Weather did not have a target. But this fell like a message falls, like an arrow falls — with intent behind it, with somewhere it meant to land. And Tamsin had spent enough years around the court to know that when something impossible happened, the first question was never *how* but *who paid*.

He looked up, past the spires, past the grey bulk of the mountain that shouldered the sky north of the city, and felt — distinctly, absurdly, the way one feels a draft from a shut window — that he was being watched by something patient and old and very, very hungry.

The flake on his wrist finally melted. It left no water. Only a small patch of skin colder than the rest, which stayed cold until evening.

* * *

By nightfall the city had gone mad in the orderly way cities do, which is to say everyone was shouting and no one was listening.

The king appeared on the balcony at dusk, wrapped in furs though the air was mild, and blamed the saboteurs. Of course he did. Kings always blame saboteurs; it is cheaper than blaming gods, and gods are harder to arrest. He stood in the torchlight with his jowls trembling and declared that agents of a foreign power had "cast unnatural frost upon the realm," that the guilds would be searched, that anyone found with charms or hexes or "witch-traffic" would be tried under the old statutes.

Which meant, translated out of royal grammar: the magic ban just got teeth.

Tamsin heard it from the scullery, where he was burning his week's stock of charms in the bread oven — three luck-knots, a fever-thread, half a jar of salt charmed against nightmares. Twelve years of careful inventory, feeding the flames one piece at a time while the king's voice echoed down the service stairs. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; that was the first thing the guild had trained into him, before immunity, before anything. *Steady hands, taster. Your fear is the last thing that reaches the king's mouth.*

But his chest felt like the inside of that oven.

Because the raids would come tomorrow, and the people who bought his charms were the ones who'd suffer for it — the laundress with the failing heart, the ferryman's daughter who couldn't sleep since her brother drowned, the old mason who just wanted to see his dead wife's face again in dreams. They hadn't cast frost on anything. They'd bought a knotted thread from a tired man in a cellar because the world was cruel and the crown offered them nothing else.

And underneath all of it, quiet as ash settling, was the other thing. The page Aldric had copied. *The taster and the cartographer.* The verse that ended beneath the mountain. Snow in summer fit that page like a key fits a lock, and Tamsin had spent two days telling himself it was coincidence, and now the sky itself had called him a liar.

He raked out the oven ashes, ground the charm-scorch fine, and went to find Aldric.

* * *

He found him at the mapmaker's hall, and Aldric did not know him.

Not entirely. Not at first. That was the shape of it — Aldric looked up from his drafting table with the polite, faintly puzzled expression of a man approached by a stranger, and for four full seconds Tamsin stood in the doorway feeling the floor tilt under him, before Aldric's face broke open into recognition and relief and that crooked smile.

"Tamsin! Gods, sorry — I was miles away. Deep in the northern passes." He gestured at the table, where a map of the mountain country lay half-finished, its contour lines drawn in Aldric's precise, confident hand. "You look terrible. Sit down. There's wine."

"You didn't know me," Tamsin said. "Just then. When I came in."

Aldric laughed, but it came out slightly wrong, slightly too quick. "I was concentrating. You startled me."

"Aldric." Tamsin sat down across from him, slowly, the way you approach a spooked animal. "What's my name?"

"What?"

"My name. Say it."

Aldric opened his mouth. Closed it. Tamsin watched him reach for it — watched the actual reaching, the way a man pats his pockets for keys he is certain are there — and the silence stretched one second too long, two, and then Aldric said, "Tamsin. Tamsin Blackwood. Don't be absurd, I've known your name since—" and stopped, because he could not remember *since when*, and they both heard the stop.

The lamp between them hissed softly. Outside, the wrong snow was still falling, silent as sifted flour, gathering on windowsills that would not hold it.

"I lost an hour yesterday," Aldric said quietly. He wasn't laughing anymore. His hand had gone to the edge of the drafting table, gripping it, as if the room might slide. "Walking home from the archive. I came to myself three streets from my own door with no idea how I got there. And this morning I couldn't—" His jaw worked. "My mother's name. I sat at breakfast reaching for my mother's name and it wasn't there. Not hidden. *Gone*. Like a tooth pulled clean. There's just a smooth place where it used to be."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. He was a man who made his living detecting poison by instinct before evidence, and every instinct he owned was screaming now, quietly, steadily, the way a kettle screams before it boils.

"When did it start?" he asked.

"After the archive." Aldric's eyes came up, and there was fear in them now, real fear, the naked kind men like Aldric almost never let show. "After I copied that page. Tamsin, I keep telling myself it's exhaustion, a fever, some— some wasting of the nerves. But I can feel it. Something is *taking*. Bit by bit. Like snow falling inside me, filling me up, and every flake lands where a memory used to be." He laughed, hollow. "Listen to me. I sound like a hedge-witch's patient."

"No," Tamsin said. "You sound like a man describing exactly what's happening to him."

He reached across the table and took Aldric's hand — the drafting hand, ink-stained, warm, human, *his* — and held it, and did not say what he was thinking, which was: *the prophecy names us both, and the court beneath the mountain has already begun to collect.*

Instead he said, "You're coming home with me tonight. And tomorrow we go see Mother Hessel."

"Hessel? The witch they burned out of Tanner's Row?" Aldric blinked. "She's alive?"

"She's alive. She's also the only person in this city who'll talk to me about what's in that book without calling the guard." Tamsin stood, pulling him up. "And Aldric — write your mother's name down. Tonight. Write down everything you're afraid of losing. Every street you love, every friend, the way home from anywhere. Fill a book if you have to."

"Why?"

"Because paper remembers what flesh forgets." Tamsin's voice came out rougher than he intended. "Trust me. I deal in remembering things for people. It's most of what a charm is."

Aldric searched his face for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and turned to his desk, and began to write — and Tamsin stood in the lamplight watching the cartographer map the country of his own mind before the borders closed, and understood with cold, absolute clarity that whatever was coming had already arrived.

The snow outside kept falling. It did not melt on the warm stones. By morning, Greyhollow would wake to streets gone white in July, and the king would blame saboteurs, and the guards would drag charm-sellers into the square.

And somewhere beneath the mountain, something old had opened its ledger, and was reading down the page of names — and had reached theirs.
