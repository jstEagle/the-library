## 8. The Crown's Bargain

The summons came at dusk, folded inside the mouth of a silver spoon.

Tamsin found it during the evening service, when he lifted the tasting-spoon from its velvet cradle and felt the wrongness of weight against his palm. He had learned every legitimate weight in that tray — the fish fork, the honey knife, the little gilt blade for citrus — and this was none of them. A rolled slip of paper, no larger than a thumbnail, tucked where the bowl of the spoon met its stem, invisible to anyone who had not spent twelve years learning to read silver the way priests read scripture.

He did not open it at the table. That was the first rule of being a taster: nothing surprised you in front of the king. He tasted the white soup, the lark pie, the candied violets, holding each morsel on his tongue for the counted sixty heartbeats while the court chattered about the snow — the impossible snow, falling in high summer, lying grey on the palace roofs like ash from a fire nobody had lit. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady. It was the rest of him that had begun to shake.

*Come alone. The west stair. When the candles are snuffed.*

No seal. No signature. But the paper smelled faintly of cold iron and old incense, and Tamsin had sold enough charms in the scullery shadows to know what it meant when power tried to smell like nothing at all.

* * *

The west stair was the oldest part of Greyhollow's palace, older than the dynasty that ruled it, older than the ban on magic that the dynasty had written into law with blood and bonfires. The stones there were black basalt, quarried — if the whispers were true — from the roots of the mountain itself, and they drank candlelight the way sand drank rain. Tamsin climbed with his hand on the wall and his breath held shallow, counting steps out of habit. Two hundred and eleven. At two hundred and twelve, the stair ended at a door of banded oak with no lock he could see.

It opened before he could knock.

The room beyond was round, windowless, and warm in a way that made his skin crawl, because warmth underground — warmth where no hearth stood — was a thing he had only ever heard described by Mother Hessel, in her crooked shop of banned remedies, in the voice she saved for the worst truths: *the Undercourt keeps no fires, taster. They do not need them. Their heat is borrowed, like everything else they own.*

Lord Protector Cassian Vael stood at the center of the room with his back to the door, studying a map pinned to a frame of antlers.

Tamsin had served the man's table for twelve years. He knew the Protector the way he knew all the great lords of Greyhollow: as an appetite. What wine softened him, which dishes soothed him, how his jaw tightened when the salt was off. He had watched this man eat ten thousand meals and never once seen him look at food the way he was looking at the map now — with hunger, yes, but also with something worse. Recognition.

"You came quickly," Vael said without turning. "I thought you might run."

"I considered it." Tamsin kept his voice level. "But I've never been summoned to a room that opened itself before. Curiosity is cheaper than cowardice, my lord. Most nights."

"Most nights." The Protector turned. In the lightless air his face seemed carved from the same basalt as the walls, all planes and shadow. "Do you know what this room is?"

"A place where the architecture misbehaves."

"It is a *threshold*. One of nine in this city, though the other eight have been sealed since your grandmother's grandmother was young." He gestured, and Tamsin saw now that the map was not a map of Greyhollow at all. It showed the mountain — Mount Ceren, the Spire, the frozen crown of rock above the city — and beneath it, rendered in ink so pale it was nearly breath, a second kingdom. Roads. Halls. A court drawn upside down, hanging from the underside of the world. "You've been selling charms out of the scullery for six years, Blackwood. Did you think we didn't know?"

The floor tilted. Or perhaps it didn't; perhaps it was only the room reminding him whose ground he stood on. Tamsin had survived poisonings, purges, three changes of royal physician who each wanted their own man in the tasting chair. He had survived by never assuming he was hidden. And still the words landed like a blade between the ribs.

"The scullery," he said carefully, "is where the kitchen waste goes. I go there because I am often ill after service. A known occupational hazard—"

"Spare me." Vael crossed to a table Tamsin hadn't noticed — furniture in this room appeared the way thoughts appear, already present, waiting to be looked at — and lifted a small object from it. A charm. *His* charm. Birch bark and red thread and a sliver of river-iron, one of the protection knots he'd sold to the laundress Marta only last week, priced at two coppers because her boy coughed blood in winter. "Twelve years of loyal service," the Protector said, turning the charm in his fingers, "and six years of treason. You must be very tired, taster. Living two lives wears a man down to wire."

"I could say the same of you, my lord. Though I imagine your second life has better furnishings."

Something flickered across Vael's face — surprise, or the ghost of respect. He set the charm down with exaggerated gentleness, the way a man sets down a live coal. "Yes. My second life. Let us speak of it plainly, since we are both men who deal in plain things behind closed doors." He drew a breath, and when he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, wearier, stripped of the court's lacquer. "How much do you know about the winter?"

"That it shouldn't exist. That it began eleven days ago, in summer, and the king blames saboteurs. That the granaries will hold four months if the cold doesn't worsen, and everyone in this palace knows it will."

"And beyond that?"

"Beyond that," Tamsin said slowly, "I know that Mother Hessel told me about a court beneath the mountain that buys souls to feed an endless winter. I know she named it the Undercourt. And I know that eleven days ago, the name of a cartographer appeared in a book a century old, and eleven days ago, the snow began." He held the Protector's gaze. "I do my reading, my lord. Poisoners and charm-sellers both survive by knowing what's in the cup before we drink."

Vael laughed once — a short, bitter sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man hearing his own secret spoken back to him in a scullery accent. "Hessel. Of course. The old woman should have been burned decades ago; instead we let her sell fever-cures to the poor because burning her would have cost more than it bought." He moved to the threshold-door and pressed his palm flat against the oak. "Then let me tell you what your hedge-witch left out, taster. She told you the Undercourt buys souls. She did not tell you *who sells them*."

The warmth in the room deepened. Tamsin felt it settle against his skin like a hand.

"The crown of Greyhollow has traded with the Court Beneath for three hundred years," Vael said. "Before the ban. Before the dynasty. The first king of this city was crowned in a hall under the mountain, and the price of the crown was simple: the Undercourt keeps its winter small and its hunger fed elsewhere, and in return, the crown sends down... payments. Souls. Not many. A handful each generation. Criminals, mostly, at first. Then debtors. Then—" his jaw worked, "—then whoever the ledger required. Every Lord Protector learns the truth on the day he takes office. Every one of us signs the continuation. I signed mine nineteen years ago. My predecessor signed his. And his. Back and back, an unbroken chain of oaths, each man telling himself the alternative was war, famine, a winter that would swallow the whole coast." He turned from the door. "Tell me, taster. You've swallowed death daily for twelve years. Do you find the habit gets easier?"

"No," Tamsin said. "You just get better at keeping your face still."

"Exactly that." Vael smiled without warmth. "So keep your face still for what comes next."

* * *

The Protector took a ledger from the table — a real book, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, its pages furred at the edges like something alive and aging. He laid it open before Tamsin without ceremony.

The columns were written in ink that shifted color as Tamsin looked at it: brown, then blue-black, then a faint arterial red. Names. Hundreds of names, in dozens of hands across three centuries. Some entries were struck through. Some were marked with a small symbol like a snowflake, or a star, or — Tamsin leaned closer and his stomach dropped — a candle.

"Payments received," Vael said quietly. "And here—" he turned forward through the pages, past decades, past generations, to the newest leaf, still smelling of the reed pen that had written it, "—is the current account."

*Aldric Vane,* the ledger said. *Cartographer. Commissioned by the Crown, Third of Highsummer.* And beneath the name, in that shifting ink: *Purchased in full. Consideration rendered. Delivery due before first frost.*

Tamsin read it twice. Three times. The words did not change, which was somehow worse than if they had. Mother Hessel had told him, and he had believed her — belief was different from this, from seeing the price of the man he loved entered in a government ledger in the same bureaucratic hand that recorded grain shipments and border garrisons. *Delivery due.* As if Aldric were a shipment of wool. As if the crown of Greyhollow had commissioned his maps, paid his lodging, arranged his introduction to the royal archive — arranged, Tamsin realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the mountain, *his introduction to me* — all as the wrapping around a gift already sold.

"The commission," Tamsin heard himself say. His voice sounded far away, someone else's voice. "The archive work. The prophecy book he found. That wasn't chance."

"No."

"He was led to it. So that he'd learn what he was. So that he'd run, or freeze, or come willingly — any of it ends the same way, doesn't it. The prophecy is the delivery mechanism." He looked up. "Who wrote the entry? Who signed for the crown?"

"I did." Vael said it flatly, without apology, and that was its own kind of confession. "Nineteen years ago, when I took office, the ledger showed a debt maturing this generation. The Undercourt names its purchases a century ahead, sometimes more. Your cartographer was chosen before his parents met. All the crown had to do was make sure he was in Greyhollow when the frost came." He closed the ledger. "You are wondering why I am telling you this. Ask."

Because Tamsin was wondering. Because a Lord Protector did not confess treason to a servant unless the servant was either about to die or about to be used. He catalogued the exits — one door, which had opened itself and would presumably close itself against him — and the weapons — none — and the odds — none — and asked.

"Why am I here, my lord?"

"Because the ledger has developed a complication." Vael walked to the antler-frame and touched the pale map of the Undercourt, at a point deep beneath the mountain's root. "Eleven days ago the Queen Beneath stopped accepting scheduled deliveries. She wants something specific now. The snow is her... impatience, made weather. And what she wants—" he turned, "—is written in a book your cartographer was kind enough to find. *The taster and the cartographer.* She wants the pair of you. Delivered together. Before the last snow seals the gates."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below the floor, or above it, or inside the walls, Tamsin heard — or imagined he heard — a sound like distant singing, thin and sweet and utterly without mercy.

"She knows about me."

"She has always known about you. Why do you think a starving guttersnipe with no family and no patron was elevated to the most sensitive post in the palace? Why do you think the guild's immunity trials, which kill half the children who take them, were waived in your case? Why do you think you survived twelve years of a job that kills its men in five?" Vael's voice was almost gentle now, and the gentleness was the cruelest thing in the room. "You were cultivated, Blackwood. Raised like a rare orchid in a hothouse of poisons. The crown built you into exactly what the prophecy required. I confess I did not believe it until the snow started. I have spent nineteen years hoping the whole ledger was superstition. It is not superstition. It is *bookkeeping*."

Tamsin's hands were shaking. He put them behind his back, the way he did at the king's table when a dish smelled wrong and he needed a moment before his tongue touched it. Twelve years of practice, and it held. Barely.

Every meal he had ever tasted. Every immunity he had hoarded like a miser's coins. Every friend lost, every charm sold in the dark, every night he lay awake cataloguing the day's toxins in his blood like a prayer — and it had all been *arranged*. He was not a survivor. He was a *product*, ripened on schedule for a buyer beneath the mountain, and the crown had played gardener the whole time.

He fed these people. He had taken poison into his body ten thousand times so that their king could eat in safety. And they had spent those same twelve years fattening him for slaughter.

"There it is," Vael said softly, watching his face. "That's the look. I remember wearing it, my first week in office." He returned to the table and stood with both hands flat upon the grey ledger, a magistrate delivering sentence. "Now. The choice, taster, since you've earned the courtesy of having one — which is more than most souls in that book received."

"Deliver Aldric willingly," Tamsin said. "Or die as a witch. You'll want me to say it aloud, I suppose. Confession makes it binding."

"Something like that." Vael produced a document from his coat — heavy paper, ducal seal, the works — and set it beside the ledger. "Cooperate, and you will be rewarded. Lands. A pardon covering your charming little side business. A title, if you want it — *Baron Blackwood* has a certain ring. The crown will even intercede with the Queen Beneath on the matter of your own soul; a cooperative bride is worth more than a purchased one, and the Undercourt honors value." He tapped the paper. "Refuse, and tomorrow morning the city guard finds witch-charms planted in your quarters — I have them already, made by the same hand that made yours, ironically — and you burn in Founder's Square by noon. Publicly. Spectacularly. The city needs someone to blame for the snow, and a traitor-taster who poisoned the season itself will do beautifully. Your cartographer will still go down the mountain either way. The only variable is whether you burn first."

There it was. The whole machine of the state, laid bare in a windowless room: not law, not justice, just a ledger and a threat, dressed in seals.

Tamsin looked at the document for a long time. Then he looked up at Lord Protector Cassian Vael — the appetite he had fed for twelve years, the man whose wine he had tested, whose bread he had broken — and he felt something in his chest go very still and very clear, the way it went clear in the sixty heartbeats after he swallowed a suspect dish, when the outcome was out of his hands and all that remained was to observe.

"You're right that I'm tired," Tamsin said. "Twelve years of two lives. Six years of treason. Ten thousand poisons." He stepped closer to the table, close enough to see his own reflection ghosted in the ledger's shifting ink. "But you've made an arithmetic error, my lord, and I'd hate to let it reach the final column. You think my choice is between reward and fire. It isn't." He smiled, and knew it was the smile the kitchen boys hated, the one that meant the soup was fine and the soup was deadly and only he could tell the difference. "My choice is between betraying the only person who's ever made me feel like a man instead of a function — and betraying the institution that spent twelve years growing me like a crop. That's not a hard sum. I did it before you finished talking."

Vael studied him for a long moment. Whatever he saw, he did not flinch from it. "Then you understand the consequences."

"I understand that you'll plant the charms tonight. I understand that hunters ride within the hour — they're probably riding already; you wouldn't leave a variable like me unwatched while you fetched me here. I understand that the gates will be sealed and the roads watched and every ferryman on the Greywater paid to look for a taster and a cartographer." He straightened his cuffs, a small domestic gesture, absurd and deliberate. "And I understand that the last snow seals the gates, which means you have a deadline too, my lord. Deadlines make people sloppy. I've built a career on noticing what's wrong with a dish before anyone else tastes it. Give me a head start and I promise you'll be amazed what slips past a hungry court."

For the first time, the Lord Protector's stillness cracked — a hairline fracture, quickly smoothed. "You intend to run."

"I intend," Tamsin said, "to deliver exactly what the prophecy promised. Just not to whom you sold it."

He turned for the door. Behind him, Vael's voice followed, quiet and almost kind:

"The Undercourt always collects, Blackwood. Three hundred years of oaths say so. You cannot outrun a ledger."

"No," Tamsin agreed, and the door swung open onto darkness and the long fall of the west stair. "But I've spent twelve years learning what's poison and what isn't. And I know a rotten bargain when I'm forced to taste one."

He descended into the dark, two hundred and twelve steps, counting them out of habit, and at the bottom the palace spread before him — torchlit, feasting, oblivious, the institution he had fed and that had fed on him — and somewhere above, in a mapmaker's room that smelled of ink and cedar, Aldric Vane was working late by candlelight, unaware that the candle had a buyer.

Tamsin climbed toward the light with the taste of iron in his mouth and one thought repeating like a heartbeat:

*Run until the map ends.*
