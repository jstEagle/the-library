## 9. Run Until the Map Ends

The rain had not stopped since the summer snow began to fall.

Aldric Vane sat at the corner table of the Lamplighter's Rest with his hands around a cup of tea he had not drunk and watched Tamsin Blackwood lie to him for the last time. He could tell it was a lie because he had spent three days learning the architecture of the man's face — the small muscle beside his eye that flinched when a guard walked past, the way his jaw set before he said something true. Right now the jaw was set like masonry, and what came out of it was: *It's nothing. A misunderstanding at court. We should leave Greyhollow for a while.*

"You're doing the thing," Aldric said quietly.

"What thing."

"The thing where you decide what I can bear without asking me." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "I found a prophecy with my name in it, Tamsin. Written before my grandfather was born. I catalogued four hundred books in that archive and exactly one of them knew me. You think I'm going to sit here and accept *a misunderstanding at court*?"

Around them the tavern did what taverns do: argued about the snow, complained about the price of coal, pretended the world was still round and warm and ordinary. Outside the window, flakes drifted down through lamplight in midsummer, fat and unhurried, settling on the cobbles and refusing to melt. A child ran past with her arms out, catching them, laughing — and then stopped laughing when an adult pulled her inside by the collar.

Tamsin looked at the window too. Something moved behind his face like weather behind glass.

Then he told him everything.

He told it badly and completely, the way a man empties a drawer by turning it upside down. The taster's guild. The immunity trials — milk of mercury in drops, then spoons, then cups; arsenic bread; the slow schooling of his blood into a fortress. Twelve years of the king's supper passing through his mouth before it reached the royal tongue. And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, the other trade: charms sold out of a scullery for coppers, little knots of outlawed craft for the desperate — a charm against fever, against grief, against the dreams that come after watching someone die.

"I'm a witch," Tamsin said, and the word came out of him like a splinter that had been in there so long the flesh had grown over it. "By their law. By any law. And the Lord Protector called me in yesterday and offered me a choice between delivering you and dying as one."

"Delivering me *to whom?*"

"To the mountain." Tamsin's hands were flat on the table now, steadying himself or the table, Aldric couldn't tell. "There's a court under Greyfell. Old things. They bought your soul, Aldric. Bought it — someone signed for it, generations ago, a bargain the crown's been paying interest on ever since, and the debt has come due, and the payment is you. Your winter is already starting." His voice cracked on the next words, and he hated it, Aldric could see him hating it. "The snow. The forgetting. It's them collecting."

Aldric thought about his mother's name. He had gone looking for it two nights ago, reaching for it the way you reach for a stair in the dark, and found — not nothing, but a smooth blank place, a wall where a door used to be. He'd told himself it was exhaustion. He'd told himself a great many things were exhaustion.

"My maps," Aldric said.

Tamsin blinked. "*What?*"

"The road out. You said we should leave, and every gate out of this city will have the Protector's men on it by tomorrow, because men like him always move faster than they need to. But I've been surveying the old drover roads for the crown for eight months." He was already pulling the leather tube from beside his chair, already unrolling paper across the sticky tabletop, pinning the corners with the salt cellar and Tamsin's untouched cup. "There's a shepherd's track off the north wall. It doesn't exist on any map the garrison owns, because it doesn't exist on any official map at all — only mine, because I walked it in March with a compass and a bad knee and no reason to tell anyone. If we go tonight—"

"*Tonight?*"

"Aldric." Tamsin put his hand flat on the map, over the blank space beyond the city walls, as if he could hold the whole world still by pressing down on it. "They took your mother's name. Whatever's left, they'll take slower, and then all at once. Every hour we stay is an hour they own."

The candle between them guttered in a draft that shouldn't have been cold, and was.

"All right," Aldric said. "Tonight."

* * *

They went out through the tanneries, where the stink covered them like a cloak and even the guards' dogs sneezed and lost the scent. Tamsin moved through the back ways of Greyhollow with the ease of long habit — down stairs that weren't stairs, along a wall-top only wide enough for goats and smugglers — and Aldric understood, walking behind him in the dark, that he had never once asked how a food-taster knew these paths. Some knowledge a man keeps like a knife under his shirt. You don't ask to see it. You just hope, if it comes to it, it's pointed away from you.

The north wall was older than the rest of the city, built by people who had feared different enemies, and in its footings there was a postern gate so forgotten that moss had eaten the hinges. Tamsin had brought oil. Of course he had brought oil. There was probably a charm sewn into his coat hem; there were probably six. Aldric was beginning to build a private catalogue of him, the way he catalogued archives: *the man who oils gates. The man who counts exits first and eats second. The man who touched my wrist in the archive doorway like it cost him something.*

The track beyond was a rumor of a path, climbing through bracken silvered with impossible snow. Above them Greyfell stood against the sky, blacker than the night, wearing a crown of cloud. Somewhere inside it, Aldric's soul sat in a vault like a deed in a strongbox, and he found that he could almost feel it — a faint coldness behind his sternum, a draft blowing through a house he lived in alone.

"Tamsin," he said, after an hour of silence and switchbacks. "The prophecy. The part you didn't read me. I saw your face when you skipped it."

The silence went on long enough that he thought it would hold.

"It says a taster breaks the bargain or weds it," Tamsin said finally, not stopping, not turning. "Breaker or bride. Those are the words. I've been trying very hard to be the breaker."

"And which are you?"

"That depends," Tamsin said, "entirely on whether we outrun the people currently hunting us."

Which was, Aldric reflected, both an evasion and, in its way, the most honest sentence anyone had spoken to him in weeks.

* * *

The hunters found their trail at dawn, or rather — Aldric realized later — they had never lost it at all. From the ridge above the second valley they saw them: four riders moving up the drover road below with the patient, terrible economy of men who are paid by the day and know the fugitive has nowhere to be. Behind the riders, the snow fell thicker, as if the mountain itself were leaning toward them.

"They'll catch us on open ground by noon," Tamsin said. He was breathing hard, and not only from the climb.

"No," Aldric said. He unrolled the map against a boulder, weighted the corners with stones, and put his finger on a wrinkle of contour lines west of the track. "Here. The land folds. There's a hanging valley the shepherds use in lambing season — it's invisible from below and from the road unless you know the fold exists. My map knows. Theirs won't."

"And if they follow us in?"

"Then they spend three hours finding a path I spent three days finding, and we spend those three hours being somewhere else."

Tamsin looked at the map, then at Aldric, and something in his expression shifted — the wariness thinning, just slightly, into something warmer and more frightened. "You drew a way out," he said. "Before you even knew there was anything to escape."

"I draw ways everywhere. It's the whole job." Aldric rolled the map up with hands that were not entirely steady. "I just never expected to need one for myself."

* * *

The bothy sat in the fold of the valley like a stone dropped in cloth — low, turf-roofed, older than the sheep it sheltered. Inside it smelled of peat smoke and lanolin and decades of weather kept politely outside. There was a hearth, a stack of cut peat left by some considerate shepherd against the day he'd need it, and a single window that framed the hanging valley like a painting nobody had hung.

Tamsin got a fire going with a competence that made Aldric smile. "What?"

"Nothing. It's just — twelve years tasting for a king, and you strike a fire like a man who's slept in worse places than palaces."

"I've slept in worse places than this," Tamsin said, without rancor. "This one has a roof and no rats with opinions." He fed the fire another block of peat, and the light moved over his face, and Aldric sat down across from him and let himself look — really look, the way he looked at coastlines, taking bearings.

Outside, the snow came down soft and ceaseless. Inside, the fire cracked and settled. And the strangeness of it struck Aldric all at once, so hard he laughed out loud.

"What now?"

"We're fugitives." Aldric spread his hands. "Witch and debtor, fleeing the crown, hunted by the Protector's riders, hiding in a hole in a mountain that wants to eat my soul. This is objectively the worst week of my life." The firelight caught Tamsin's eyes; the wariness was back, braced for the blow. "And it's the first time in years I haven't been lonely. Isn't that obscene?"

For a moment Tamsin didn't answer. Then he said, "Yes," in a voice like a door opening onto a room he'd sealed long ago.

They ate hard bread and hard cheese and a heel of dried sausage Tamsin produced from some bottomless pocket, and drank melted snow that tasted of stone and clean cold. And because the fire demanded conversation the way fires do, they talked — really talked, for the first time, with nowhere either of them needed to be pretending to be.

Aldric learned that Tamsin had been hungry enough, once, to eat candle wax. That he kept a list of everyone whose poison he'd swallowed, written in code, in a book hidden under a floorboard — *so I remember what each immunity cost, and who paid it.* That he had not been touched gently in eleven years until Aldric's hand brushed his in the archive, and that he had gone home afterward and sat in the dark and been furious about how much it had undone him.

In exchange Aldric gave up his own hoard: the father who measured love in miles and left to find more of them; the year he'd drawn maps of places he invented because real ones hurt; the day the crown's commission arrived and his mother said, *you'll see the whole kingdom now, love,* and he'd thought, *no — I'll see the edges of it, which is better.* He gave up the memory of his mother's name being *Maren*, and discovered, telling it aloud, that he still had it — still, for now, tonight, by firelight — and held onto it with both hands.

The fire burned down to a red heart. The wind changed and the snow thickened against the window like static. And at some point they were sitting shoulder to shoulder against the bothy's one bench, wrapped in the horse blanket that smelled of hay and travel, and neither of them had moved away, and the silence had stopped being empty.

"If I lose it," Aldric said quietly, to the fire, "if they take it all — I want you to know where I keep the important ones. So you can tell me. When it matters."

"Don't talk like that."

"Maren. My mother's name is Maren. She smells of rosemary and ink. She sings flat. My first map was of our street, and I drew our house too big because I loved it too much to make it honest. The toll bridge — " he stopped. There was a toll bridge coming; Hessel had warned them; the river crossing ahead would take a memory per passenger, and he had been turning over his inventory all day, deciding what he could live without. "When we get there, whatever I give them — come find me after, and give it back. That's all. That's the oath. Come find me and give it back."

Tamsin turned. In the firelight his face was stripped of every defense Aldric had watched him build all week — the taster's calm, the seller's smile, the witch's watchfulness — and what was underneath was so young and so afraid that Aldric's chest ached with it.

"I'm not good at oaths," Tamsin said. "Oaths get people killed. I've read the ledger."

"This isn't an oath for the crown. It's just for me."

"I know." Tamsin's hand found his in the dark between them — found it and stayed, thumb moving once across his knuckles, a touch so careful it was almost prayer. "Aldric. There's a version of this where I say something now that I can't take back, and then tomorrow the mountain takes you anyway, and I spend whatever's left of my life knowing the last true thing I ever said was to a man who couldn't—" His voice failed. He tried again. "I've survived twelve years by never letting anything matter enough to bleed. And then you laughed at my joke about the fishmonger, and I've been bleeding ever since, and I don't know how to stop it, and I don't want to."

The fire popped. Snow whispered at the window. Aldric turned his hand over and laced their fingers together, and felt Tamsin tremble — this man who swallowed death daily for a living, trembling at a held hand.

"Then don't say it," Aldric said. "Not yet. Say it somewhere they can't hear. Say it somewhere with spring in it. I'll wait. I'm a cartographer; waiting is half the trade. But Tamsin—" he leaned close, forehead nearly touching the other man's temple, voice dropping to the register reserved for truths and prayers— "know that I'm keeping count of the days. Know that when you finally say it, I intend to have a better one ready."

Tamsin laughed — wet and broken and real — and pressed his face into Aldric's hair, and for a long time neither of them spoke, and the storm wrote its white sentences against the window, and the little fire held its ground against all that dark the way they held theirs.

* * *

Aldric woke once in the grey hour before dawn, cold at his back and warm along his front, Tamsin's breath slow against his collarbone, and lay still and memorized it: weight, warmth, the smell of peat smoke and wool and the man beneath it. Memorized it the way he memorized coastlines. *If they take everything else,* he thought, *they cannot take what I've already drawn.*

Then he heard the sound that ended the world's tenderness.

Hooves. Not on the drover road — closer, higher, wrong. He slid out from under Tamsin's arm and to the window, and the sight below froze him where he knelt: lantern-light bobbing along the ridgeline above the hanging valley, five points of it, descending. Someone had found the fold. Someone was reading his map.

"Tamsin." His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "They've found us."

Behind him, Tamsin was already awake, already reaching for his coat, and in the same motion — Aldric saw it clearly in the pre-dawn grey — his hand went to the charm-seller's knot at his belt, the one stitched in red thread that he'd never once explained, and closed around it like a man closing his hand around the last coin he owns.

"There's a river," Tamsin said, "two valleys east. We can reach it by dark if we run."

"And if we run, they follow."

"They'll follow. But the river has a ferryman." Tamsin met his eyes across the dying fire, and his face was grim as the mountain above them. "Hessel told me what waits at the crossing, and what it charges. We're out of time to choose our prices, Aldric. Now we only get to pay them."

Above the bothy, the first lantern cleared the ridgeline, and the light it threw was the wrong color — pale blue, winter-colored, falling like snow out of season across the green of the hanging valley.
