## 10. The Toll Bridge

The river found them before the road did.

They had been walking since the grey hour before dawn, following a track that was barely a track—Aldric's own cartography made flesh, a line he'd once inked between two mountain folds and labeled *drover's path, disused*. Now his boots were making it used again, one careful print at a time in the frost-brittle grass. The snow that had fallen out of season still clung to the high shoulders of the valley, but down here the world was only cold, not impossible. Cold I could work with. I had spent twelve years learning the exact temperature at which fear becomes manageable.

Aldric walked half a step ahead, which he always did when the ground grew uncertain, as if the land might apologize to its mapmaker for being difficult. He'd been quiet for an hour. Not the easy quiet of the pastry shop, or even the frightened quiet of our first night out—he'd slept in my arms by the shepherd's fire with his heart hammering like something trying to get out of a locked room—but the quiet of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"You keep touching your temple," I said.

"Do I?" He dropped his hand as if it belonged to someone else. "It's nothing. A headache. It's been there since—" He stopped. Frowned. "Since the snow," he said, but slowly, the way you say a word in a language you're relearning.

*Or since before the snow,* I thought, and said nothing. Mother Hessel's warning rode my shoulder like a second cloak: *The court takes what it's owed, taster, and it always sends a bill ahead.*

The river announced itself first as sound—a low, muscular muttering under the wind—and then as smell: wet stone and iron and something green and ancient underneath, like the inside of a forest's mouth. When we came around the last spur of hill, there it was, wide and slate-grey and moving fast despite the season, unchoked by ice where every other water in Greyhollow had frozen solid weeks ago.

And there was the bridge.

I use the word loosely. It was more suggestion than structure: two stone piers rising from the water, connected by planks that looked less built than *arranged*, as though the river itself had grown tired of separating things and grudgingly permitted passage. No rail. No tollhouse. Just a figure standing at the near end, wrapped in a coat of layered grey that moved wrong—not with the wind but against it, hem lifting into the breeze instead of away from it.

"Ah," Aldric said softly. "That's not on any of my maps."

"No," I said. "I imagine the Undercourt prefers its doors unmapped."

He turned to look at me then, really look, and I saw him assemble the pieces—the archive page, Hessel's crooked cottage, my twelve years of small poisons, this flight through country only he could read—and accept, again, the shape of the truth we were running along. He'd been better about it than I'd dared hope. There was a steadiness in him that had nothing to do with bravery and everything to do with precision: Aldric Vane met the world the way he met a blank sheet, measuring first, drawing second.

"Ferryman?" he asked.

"Ferryman," I agreed. "Hessel said the river crossings belong to them. She said—" My throat tightened around the memory. *She said pay what they ask and ask nothing back.* "She said they take payment. Always payment."

The figure hadn't moved, but as we drew closer I understood that it had been watching us for some time. Its face was smooth as river-washed bone, features present but worn, the way lettering wears from a coin passed through too many hands. When it spoke, its voice came not from its mouth but from the water beneath the bridge, from everywhere the river touched, which meant, suddenly and horribly, from everywhere.

"TWO TRAVELERS," the river said. "ONE CROSSING."

"We'll swim," Aldric said, in exactly the tone he used when a client tried to haggle over a commission.

The ferryman's head tilted. The river chuckled—it was unmistakably a chuckle, a deep rolling amusement that set the water shivering in rings. "SWIM, CARTOGRAPHER. THE RIVER IS OLDER THAN YOUR KINGDOM AND HUNGRIER THAN YOUR WINTER. IT HOLDS WHAT IT TAKES. YOU WOULD REACH THE FAR BANK AS A SONG SUNG BY SOMETHING ELSE." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "THE BRIDGE IS KINDER. THE BRIDGE ONLY BARTERS."

"What's the price?" I asked, because someone had to, and because I had spent my whole life being the one who swallowed first.

The ferryman raised one hand. Its fingers were long and pale as birch roots, and between them it held—or seemed to hold—a thread of light no thicker than a hair, glowing faintly gold, pulsing with a rhythm I knew. I had felt that rhythm under my ear pressed to Aldric's chest. Under my own.

"A MEMORY," the river said. "PER PASSENGER. ONE EACH. FREELY GIVEN OR NOT AT ALL." The light-thread swayed. "WE DO NOT STEAL. STEALING MAKES THE MEMORIES SOUR. WE ONLY TRADE."

My mouth went dry. Twelve years of tasting had taught me every flavor of dread: the metallic tang of arsenic anticipation, the almond ghost of bitter cyanide, the slow vegetable rot of aconite. This was none of them. This was the taste of being asked to open your own veins and pour.

"One each," Aldric repeated. His voice was level. His hand had found mine without either of us deciding it, cold fingers laced through colder ones. "And if we refuse?"

"THEN YOU STAND ON THIS BANK UNTIL THE SNOW COMES FOR YOU, OR THE HUNTERS, OR THE HUNGER. THE RIVER DOES NOT MIND WAITING. THE RIVER HAS NEVER LOST A PATIENCE CONTEST." The ferryman's worn face managed, somehow, to smile. It was the worst thing I had seen all week, and the week had included watching summer fall as snow on a city full of screaming children.

I stepped forward.

"Tamsin." Aldric's grip tightened.

"It's fine." It wasn't fine. Nothing had been fine since a book of prophecies printed his name in ink older than his grandfather. But I had learned long ago that *fine* was a flavor you could fake well enough to swallow. "Let me go first."

I walked to the bridge's edge. Up close, the ferryman smelled of deep places—cellars, wells, the underside of ice—and its coat resolved into layers of grey feathers, or scales, or pages; I genuinely could not tell which, and decided I would rather not know.

"WHAT WILL YOU PAY, TASTER?"

Here is what no one tells you about memory: you don't get to inventory it. Standing at the edge of that bridge, I reached inward expecting a shelf, a ledger, a tidy archive like the one Aldric catalogued—and found instead a flood. Every kindness anyone had ever done me, every meal, every morning, all of it tangled together like yarn pulled from a ruined sweater. And threaded through it, bright as brass, the memories I could least afford to lose:

My mother's hands kneading bread. Gone if I paid it—no, wait, was it tied to that? How would I ever know what I no longer knew?

Aldric laughing with flour on his nose. If I gave that up, would I still follow him across this bridge? Would I still be running toward him, or just running?

And underneath everything, oldest and heaviest, the one I had carried longest:

*Wren.*

I hadn't spoken his name aloud in eleven years. A guild boy with a gap-toothed grin who'd held my hand through the third immunity trial, who'd sworn we'd both live forever on our carefully cultivated poisons, who'd taken the dish that should have been mine when the guildmasters tested us with something new—something they hadn't trained into us—because he'd seen my hands shaking and swapped the plates with a smile, thinking himself immune enough, thinking we were safe, thinking—

Thinking wrong.

Everyone thought I'd survived that trial. Only I knew I'd survived *Wren*, wearing his courage like a stolen coat. Every immunity I'd built since, every poison I'd swallowed and outlasted, I'd built on the foundation of a dead boy's mistaken generosity. The guild called me their miracle. I called myself his debt.

If I paid with anything else—if I paid with my mother's bread, or Aldric's laugh—I would walk off this bridge lighter, luckier, still carrying the weight of Wren's death. Still owing. Still flinching every time someone was kind to me at their own cost.

But if I paid with Wren—

I would finally sleep a full night. I would lose the guilt that had shaped me like water shapes stone. And I would forget, entirely, why I flinched. Why I never let anyone close until a cartographer with ink-stained fingers made closeness feel like coming home. Everything Wren's sacrifice had taught me—that love is a plate-swap, that protection is a thing you do for people, not a wall you build against them—all of it gone, and I wouldn't even know it was gone. I'd just be a man with no reason left to be good.

"That's the trap, isn't it," I said quietly. "You let us choose. And choosing is the price."

The river said nothing, which was answer enough.

I thought of Aldric asleep against my chest, heart hammering. I thought of Hessel saying, *the outlawed magic of Greyhollow lives in its people, boy, and grief is the strongest spell we've got.* I thought of a gap-toothed grin swapping plates in the dark.

"I'll pay," I told the ferryman. "But I choose what."

"THAT IS THE ARRANGEMENT."

I closed my eyes. I went down into myself, into the flood, and found Wren's memory where it had always lived: not a single bright thread but a braid, woven through everything. I took hold of the braid with both hands, metaphorical and otherwise—my actual fists clenched hard enough to ache—and I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than swallowing a king's poisoned supper, harder than betraying the crown whose bread I'd eaten for twelve years.

I offered it up.

"Wren," I said aloud, so that somewhere, somehow, it would be witnessed. "His name was Wren. He died saving me. I'm paying with him so I can save someone else. I think—" My voice cracked. "I think he'd have liked that trade. He always liked a plate-swap."

The thread of light in the ferryman's fingers brightened, hungry, waiting.

I let go.

There was no pain. That was the obscene part. There was simply a moment of holding—of *knowing*—and then the knowing drained out of me like warmth from a cooling cup, smooth and total, and I stood on the bank of a grey river with wet eyes I couldn't explain and a hollow in my chest shaped like a name I no longer possessed.

I remembered that someone had died for me once. I remembered *that much*—the fact, stripped bare, like a bone washed clean by current. But the boy was smoke. The grin was smoke. Eleven years of guilt and grief and the strange, crooked goodness they'd carved into me: paid, spent, traded across a bridge in the mountains.

I wiped my face with my sleeve. "Your turn," I said to Aldric, and my voice came out steady, which felt like theft.

* * *

Aldric stood very still at the bridge's edge for a long time.

The wind had died. The river muttered. Somewhere above us, on the peaks, the unnatural snow gleamed like a held breath. And Aldric Vane—who measured everything, who trusted paper and ink and the honest arithmetic of distance—stood weighing the unweighable.

"I've been forgetting things," he said at last, not looking at me. "Since the snow started. Small things. My mother's maiden name—I sat down three days ago to write her a letter and couldn't—there was just a blank where it should be. The way home from the archive. Which drawer I keep my good nibs in." He laughed, short and terrible. "I thought I was tired. Then I thought I was sick. Then Hessel looked at me the way she did, and I stopped thinking anything at all."

"The court's already collecting," I said quietly. "On the sale. Before you're even delivered."

"Yes." He flexed his fingers, ink-stained even now, even fugitive. "So here's my question, Tamsin, and I want an honest answer, because you're the only person I've ever known who gives those." He turned. His eyes were grey as the river and just as fast underneath. "What happens if I pay a memory while the debt's already being collected? Am I spending coin I don't have? Am I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Am I feeding it? Making the candle burn faster?"

The river answered before I could, which should have warned me.

"CLEVER CARTOGRAPHER," it rumbled, with something that might have been approval. "YES. EVERY MEMORY SPENT HERE IS SPENT FROM WHAT REMAINS. THE COURT'S CLAIM AND OUR BRIDGE DRAW FROM THE SAME WELL." A ripple of that terrible chuckle. "BUT REFUSE, AND THE WINTER TAKES ALL OF IT ANYWAY, SLOWLY AND WITHOUT RECEIPT. WE AT LEAST GIVE CHANGE."

"Change," Aldric repeated. "Meaning?"

"MEANING THE BRIDGE IS FAIR, AND FAIRNESS IS ITS OWN CURRENCY. PAY, CROSS, AND THE COURT MAY NOT TAKE WHAT WE HAVE BOUGHT. PAID MEMORIES ARE BRIDGE-MEMORIES. THEY BELONG TO THE WATER NOW." The ferryman's worn face tilted toward me. "YOUR TASTER UNDERSTOOD THIS. WHY DO YOU THINK HE CHOSE SO CAREFULLY?"

I hadn't understood it. I had chosen with grief and instinct and a dead boy's example. But I kept my face still, because apparently I was the kind of person who understood rivers now.

Aldric looked at the far bank. Then at me. And I watched him do the thing he always did—measure, calculate, commit—except this time the equation had no solution, and he knew it, and he squared his shoulders anyway.

"All right," he said to the river. "One memory. Freely given."

"CHOOSE WELL."

He closed his eyes. And here is what I will carry to whatever grave eventually takes me, the image burned into the backs of my eyelids: I watched Aldric Vane decide what to forget, and I saw him pass over the archive, over his maps, over his mother, over the road home—and settle, with a tenderness that broke me, on something that made his mouth soften even as his fists clenched.

"No," I said. "Aldric, no—"

He opened his eyes. They were already wet.

"If I give up a map or a name, I'm still me, just poorer. But Tamsin—the winter is eating me from the edges inward. Whatever I don't spend here, the court spends there. So I'm going to choose the thing I most want to keep, and pay it here, where it's *safe*—where it belongs to the river instead of the Queen." His voice shook on the last words. "Where maybe—maybe someday—it can be bought back."

"That's insane."

"It's *cartography*," he said, and smiled, and it was the smile from the pastry shop, flour-dusted and hopeless and mine. "You mark the thing you can't afford to lose so you can find it again later. Even if finding it costs everything."

"Aldric—"

"Pay attention," he whispered. "Someone should remember it, if I can't."

And before I could move, before I could shout or grab him or offer to pay double, triple, anything—he leaned toward the ferryman's waiting thread of light and gave it up.

He didn't say what. He didn't speak a name, didn't frame it in words the way I had. He simply exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a pack carried too many miles, and the light-thread drank him in, flaring briefly gold, and the river sighed with satisfaction, and it was done.

Aldric blinked. Straightened. Looked at me with polite, distant attention—the look of a man regarding a stranger he's been told he's traveling with.

"Well," he said pleasantly. "Shall we cross?"

* * *

The planks held. Of course they held; the river wanted us across, wanted its payment banked in deep water. But I crossed every step beside a man who no longer knew why his hand kept drifting toward mine, and each time it happened, each time his fingers brushed mine and withdrew with courteous apology, something in me tore a little further.

He remembered the mission. I could see it in how he walked: purposeful, oriented, checking the sun and the peaks, already sketching in his mind the route beyond the river. He remembered the prophecy, the flight, the hunters, the mountain door we sought. He remembered *me* the way you remember a landmark—useful, named, plotted.

He did not remember the pastry shop. He did not remember the fire in the bothy, his heartbeat under my palm. He did not remember that three nights ago he had almost said a word he never finished, and that I had waited for it like a man waits for dawn.

Whatever he'd chosen, he'd chosen *us*. He'd paid the river with the memory of what we were to each other, banking it in fair waters against the Queen's thieving winter, gambling that love kept in trust could someday be withdrawn.

He'd made himself a lantern with the flame removed and hidden.

Halfway across, the wind returned, and with it a smell that did not belong to any river: woodsmoke and dried herbs and old, stubborn magic. I turned toward the southern ridge and saw, impossibly, a small bent figure descending the switchbacks with a staff and a satchel, unhurried, unmistakable.

"Hessel," I breathed. "She followed us."

Behind us, faint but gaining, came another sound: hoofbeats on the frozen road. The Protector's hunters had found the pass.

And ahead of us, on the far bank, the mountains opened into dark, and somewhere inside that dark, a candle with Aldric's name on it was burning low.
