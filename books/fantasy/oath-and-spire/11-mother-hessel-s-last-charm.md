## 11. Mother Hessel's Last Charm

The pass above Greyhollow had three names, and Mother Hessel knew them all: the shepherds called it the Lamb's Throat, the cartographers called it Vane Col after some dead surveyor who had frozen there with his instruments in his arms, and the old women—the ones who remembered what magic was before the ban made it a whisper—called it the Place Where the Wind Counts Your Bones.

She was counting her own as she climbed. Sixty-one years of them, and most of them aching now, and the last one—she could feel it in her knees, in the ache behind her eyes that was not weather but warning—the last one would be spent here.

Good, she thought. Better here than in a bed in Salt Lane, listening to the neighbors pretend not to hear me coughing.

She had watched them leave the city two nights past from her rooftop, where she kept a garden of things no one was supposed to grow: moonwort in a cracked washbasin, hellebore gone feral and beautiful, a rowan sapling she'd raised from a berry smuggled up from the southern valleys in her cheek for three days like a woman hiding contraband, which was exactly what she'd been doing. Magic was outlawed in Greyhollow. Growing it was outlawed. Remembering it, near enough. But Hessel had been born before the ban hardened into law, back when hedge-witches still hung their shingles out openly, and she had decided long ago that the law could catch up to her or go on pretending she didn't exist, whichever suited it better.

Two figures on the north road, moving fast, keeping to the ditches. One tall with a walker's stride and a mapmaker's habit of glancing at landmarks even in the dark—one glance at the ruined mill, one at the lightning-split oak, cataloguing, always cataloguing. The other smaller, quicker, with the particular careful way of moving that Hessel recognized because she'd seen it in soldiers and in animals and once, heartbreakingly, in a boy of nine years being led through the guild gates to have poison fed to him on a spoon.

Tamsin Blackwood. She'd sold him his first counter-charm when he was fourteen and already carrying more death in his blood than a battlefield surgeon. He'd paid with a silver button off a lord's coat and hadn't met her eyes. She'd known then what he was building himself into—a fortress with every wall load-bearing—and she had pitied him the way you pity any structure that cannot come down without killing everyone inside.

Now he was coming apart for love, which was either the saddest thing she'd ever witnessed or the first honest thing he'd ever done.

* * *

She caught them at the shepherd's bothy below the col, where the wind came down off the high snowfields even in summer and made the stones sing a thin, whistling note. They were asleep—or pretending to be—with a fire burned down to its red arithmetic of coals, and she stood at the door and let them feel her before they saw her, because courtesy cost nothing and surprise killed witches.

Tamsin came up off the floor with a knife already in his hand, which pleased her. Aldric Vane woke slower, blinking, with the lost look of a man surfacing from deep water, and that did not please her at all.

"You're supposed to be halfway to the river by now," she said.

"We stopped." Tamsin's voice was rough with sleep and something else. "He needed—" He stopped. Looked at Aldric. Looked away.

"He needed," Aldric said, smiling, easy, "a fire, and less walking. My companion drives me like a mule."

Hessel looked at him a long moment. He was handsome in the way maps were handsome—all clean lines and confident borders—but underneath the handsomeness there was a wrongness she could taste on the air between them, faint as frost on a window in June. A coldness sitting in the man like a coin dropped down a well. Something missing. Something *taken*.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. I've walked all night on these knees and I'll have tea before I save anyone's life."

* * *

They gave her the good seat by the fire because she bullied them into it. She brewed her tea in a battered tin pot, and while it steeped she studied them the way she studied patients, which was to say without mercy.

Aldric Vane laughed at her jokes a beat too late. He called Tamsin *my companion* twice and *the taster* once, and each time the word landed wrong, like a key that fit a lock but wouldn't turn. When Tamsin handed him a bannock, their fingers touched, and Aldric flinched—not away from Tamsin, but inward, a small private wince, as though something inside him had knocked against a wall it didn't remember building.

And Tamsin saw it. That was the worst part. Hessel watched the boy—man, she corrected herself, though to her he would always be the boy with the poisoned spoon—watched him see the flinch and fold it away behind his face like a letter he couldn't bear to read yet. Twelve years of tasting had taught him to swallow everything.

"Well," she said, setting down her cup. "You want to know how bad it is. So I'll tell you. It's very bad."

"Hessel—" Tamsin began.

"The Undercourt doesn't sell souls back, Tamsin. It doesn't rent them out and it doesn't lose track of them. Every soul in that mountain is a candle on a shelf, and candles are *fuel*. The Queen has burned through four counties' worth of bought souls in the last century alone. Every bad winter Greyhollow ever blamed on God, she lit herself." She let that sit in the coals between them. "Your man there is a stub of tallow with maybe three weeks of burn left. Less, if she's cold."

"How do you know all this?" Aldric asked. There was no suspicion in it, only the flat bright curiosity of a scholar, and that absence of suspicion frightened Hessel more than anything the Protector's hunters could do.

"Because I was married to it for thirty years, dear. My husband sold our daughter's name to a gentleman with grey gloves who paid in never getting sick again. I went looking for the buyer. I found the seller. I found the mountain." She turned her cup slowly on her knee. "I found my daughter's candle, too, if you're wondering. It was half-burned. I couldn't take it. You can't take your own candle—it gutters the moment you touch it. I sat in front of that little flame for an hour and then I walked out of the Undercourt alive, which almost nobody does, and I have spent forty years since then being angry about it in useful ways."

The wind counted bones outside. Inside, Tamsin had gone very still.

"You said *almost* nobody," he said.

"I did."

"And you're going to tell me how."

* * *

She took the charm out last, after she'd told them about the toll bridge, the ferryman, the price of passage; after she'd told them about the door in the mountain that opens only for a throat that has learned to welcome death daily; after she'd watched Aldric listen to all of it with polite attention and understood, finally and completely, what had been taken from him at the river, and watched Tamsin understand it a heartbeat later, and seen the boy's face do something she would remember for the rest of her short remaining life—which was not long, but she meant to make it count.

He didn't cry. Of course he didn't. He was a Blackwood; they metabolized grief like everything else, quietly, and built immunity.

But his hand found Aldric's shoulder and stayed there, and Aldric leaned into it without knowing why, the way a plant leans toward light it has never seen, and Hessel thought: *there. That's the thread. Cut every other one, burn the map, take the name—that thread holds.*

Love is the one thing they can't buy, she thought, because they can't make it. They can only steal it, and stealing isn't having.

"Now," she said, and reached into the inner pocket of her coat, where she kept the things that mattered wrapped in oilcloth against the damp.

The charm was small. That was the joke of it, the cruel joke she'd been carrying up a mountain in her pocket: forty years of hoarded power, a lifetime of small transgressions, every stolen berry and whispered word and midnight working, and it all came down to a knot of braided thread no bigger than a thumb. Rowan bark at the center, red thread and white, nine knots, each one tied on a different night of her life—her wedding night, the night her daughter was born, the night her husband died and she cursed him gently and forgave him before dawn, the night the ban passed and she buried her shingle under the rowan tree and went on practicing anyway.

It was warm. Charms made of nothing are cold; charms made of something are warm as a held hand, and this one had been warming against her heart for six days, drinking from her.

"What is it?" Tamsin asked.

"It's everything I have left." She said it lightly, because heaviness helps no one. "When the ban came, they didn't take magic out of Greyhollow, boy. They drove it underground and it went feral and it pooled, in cellars and rooftops and stubborn old women, and I have been a reservoir for sixty-one years. This is the water. All of it. What's left after a lifetime of leaks."

"I can't take that."

"You can and you will, and we haven't time to argue, so I'll talk over you." She pressed it into his palm and closed his fingers over it with her own two hands, which were spotted and strong and colder than they used to be. "One knot for the ferryman—he'll take it as payment if your memories run short, and don't spend it there unless you must. Two knots for the door in the mountain; it likes to be asked nicely and it has forgotten how anyone asks nicely anymore. Three through seven are strength, and they'll feel like sunrise, and they won't last, so use them fast. Eight is a door closing behind you. Nine—" Her voice caught, just barely, and she covered it with a cough that fooled neither of them. "Nine is mine. Nine is a memory I've been keeping warm for someone, and you'll know whose when you need it. Trust the ninth knot, Tamsin Blackwood, the way you trust your own tongue."

He was staring at the little braid in his palm like it might bite him, or like he might bite it. "What memory?"

"That's between me and the knot." She smiled at him, and let the smile soften into something truer. "You were nine years old the first time I saw you. They marched you into the guild hall in your dead mother's coat sleeves and you ate what they gave you and you didn't cry, and afterward you asked me—for a charm, you'd heard I sold them—you asked me for a charm to make the taste go away. And I told you there wasn't one, because there isn't, taste is memory and memory is the self, and you nodded like a little soldier and you paid me your button anyway and said *then hold it until you find one.*" She laughed, wet-eyed. "I held it forty years, you impossible boy. Here's your refund."

* * *

They argued with her, of course. She'd expected that. Tamsin argued with facts—the hunters were a day behind, maybe less; the Protector wanted them alive or dead, preferably dead and deniable; a woman of her age couldn't hold a pass against six armed men. Aldric argued with maps, spreading them on the bothy floor and showing her the alternate routes, the goat tracks, the old Roman stair that bypassed the col entirely, and it was strangely painful to watch him do it—to watch him give her, with perfect generosity and total ignorance, the gift of a way to survive, when surviving was the one thing she had decided not to do.

But it was Tamsin, in the end, who stopped arguing. Because Tamsin looked at her across the dying fire—at her spotted hands, her bent spine, her face with its sixty-one winters—and saw, because he was a taster and tasters see what's actually in the dish, that she had already swallowed her decision and there was no antidote.

"When?" he said quietly.

"Tomorrow dusk. They'll reach the mouth of the pass at dusk—I've laid a little something on the road to slow them, a confusion, a tiredness in the legs. It buys me an hour of their patience. Then I stand at the narrows where the rock walls pinch together, and I remind the mountain that it used to belong to people like me." She shrugged. "Stone remembers, Tamsin. The ban made everyone forget, but stone is older than bans. Give me an hour and a hillside and I can be very persuasive."

"They'll kill you."

"They'd have done that in Salt Lane eventually, slower, with more indignity." She reached over and cuffed his ear, gently, the way you cuff someone you love. "Listen to me now, because I'll only say this once. I had a daughter. I had a husband. I had a whole streetful of neighbors who brought me their sick children in secret for forty years and called me *just old Hess* so the wardens wouldn't look twice. Every one of them is gone or scattered or pretending, and I used to think that meant I'd failed—that magic in Greyhollow would end in a cellar with me and die there." She looked at him, and then at Aldric, sleeping again by the fire with the frost-coin cold behind his handsome face, and then back. "And then a food-taster walks into my kitchen with prophecy ink on his fingers and a fugitive cartographer at his side, going up a mountain to break a bargain a hundred years old, and I thought—*oh.* Oh, you foolish girl. It didn't die in the cellar. It packed a bag and grew up and got itself a job poisoning kings. That's inheritance, boy. Not land. Not gold. You hand what you have to whoever's still walking toward the thing you couldn't finish."

She took his chin in her hand and turned his face to hers, the way she had once turned the faces of sick children toward the light.

"You were never a function," she said. "Whatever the guild told you, whatever you told yourself with all those little poisons—you were always a person, and persons are what the Undercourt eats, and that is why you're going to win. Not because you're immune. Because you're *breakable*, and you're going anyway."

His jaw worked. His eyes were bright and terrible and dry. "Come with us instead. Please, Hessel. Come down the mountain with us."

"And leave the pass open behind you? Let the Protector's men follow your trail straight to the Undercourt's front door, with winter sealing in three weeks?" She shook her head. "No. This is my knot, child. Ninth knot. Mine."

* * *

She left them at first light, before full daybreak, because partings go better in the grey hour when faces are half-dream and the words don't stick so hard.

Tamsin walked her to the edge of the bothy's shadow. At the boundary line where the firelight died he stopped, and she stopped, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the wind counted their bones together, kindly, like a nurse taking inventory.

"Hessel," he said. "The ninth knot. If I use it—if it's what I think—"

"Then you'll owe me a visit to a grave on a hillside above Greyhollow, and you'll bring a candle, and you'll light it, and you'll complain about the walk, because I'll have earned the complaining." She smiled. "That's all the immortality I ever wanted, Tamsin Blackwood. Somebody remembering to show up."

Then, because sentiment is a luxury and she had work to do, she kissed his forehead—tasting, faintly, absurdly, of honey and arsenic and twelve years of survival—and turned, and climbed.

The path to the narrows was steep and the morning was cold and her knees sang their old complaint, and she let them, because pain was proof and she intended to arrive at her work with every proof intact. Behind her, far below, the bothy smoke rose thin and straight into a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Ahead, the pass narrowed to a throat of grey stone, and beyond it, invisible over the ridge, the mountain waited with its warm dark and its shelves of little flames.

She rolled up her sleeves, which she had not done in public in forty years, and set her palms against the living rock.

"All right," Mother Hessel told the mountain, in the language the old women used before the ban made them whisper. "Let's remind them whose ground this is."

Far below, at the mouth of the pass, six riders crested the rise—too soon, hours too soon, the Protector's hounds running ahead of schedule—and the foremost of them raised a horn to his lips.

Hessel smiled, cracked her knuckles, and began to pull.
