## 12. Descent

The door was not where the map said it would be.

Aldric had drawn the pass three times in his life—once as an apprentice copying his master's plates, once for a survey commissioned by a duke who never paid, and once, two winters ago, from memory, because he could not sleep and because mountains were kinder company than dreams. All three versions agreed: the old pilgrim road ended at a shrine to Saint Verity, its altar cracked, its bell long since stolen, and beyond it nothing but scree and the white shoulder of the mountain called the Spire.

But the door was there anyway, when we came down through the last of the pines at dusk, breathing steam, our boots crusted with the snow that had fallen out of season since midsummer. It stood in the cliff face like a wound that had learned patience—a seam of black stone taller than two men, narrower than a coffin, with no handle, no hinge, no carving except a single line sunk deep into the rock above the lintel:

*Only the daily dead may enter.*

The letters were not written. I understood that at once, the way you understand a smell before you can name it. They had grown there, the way frost grows on glass, the way scar tissue grows over a wound. The stone had been told something once, long ago, and had never forgotten how to say it.

Aldric stopped six feet away. He had been quiet since the pass—quieter than the man I'd met over stolen pastries, quieter even than the fugitive who'd laughed with me in a shepherd's bothy while hunters' torches crawled along the valley floor below us like slow fireflies. But this silence was different. This was the silence of a cartographer standing at the edge of his own competence.

"I can't map this," he said. Not bitterly. Almost gently, the way you tell someone a truth you've been carrying for them. "There's no survey. No pilgrim ever came back to draw what's down there. Everything past this line is—" he turned his hand over, palm up, empty. "Blank."

"Then we'll be the first ink," I said.

He looked at me then, and something crossed his face too fast for me to catch it—grief, maybe, or its shadow—and was gone before I could ask. He'd been doing that since the river. Small flickers. A name almost spoken. His hand rising toward my sleeve and stopping midair, as if he'd forgotten halfway through why he'd been reaching.

I told myself it was exhaustion. We had both paid the ferryman; I knew what my own payment had cost me, the shape of the hole where Merrin's laughter used to live. Whatever Aldric had surrendered, he hadn't wanted to say, and I hadn't pressed. Love, I was learning, meant letting people keep their own ledgers.

*Love.* When had I started using the word without flinching?

I took off my pack and knelt in the snow.

* * *

Mother Hessel's charm sat in the inner pocket, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, warm as a sleeping bird. She had pressed it into my hands at the mouth of the pass with fingers that shook—not from age, I understood now, but from spending. She had unwound her own remaining magic into the weaving, thread by thread, the way a poor woman unpicks her last good dress to make a shroud.

"It holds one thing," she'd said. "One memory, mine or yours or anyone's. Don't spend it cheap. And Tamsin—" she had caught my wrist, her grip still strong as a trapline, "—when you meet her, remember she was a girl once. That's not mercy. That's tactics."

Now, kneeling before the door, I did what I had done every day for twelve years.

I reached into the satchel at my belt.

Twelve years ago, a guildmaster with kind eyes and a ledger heart had put a thimbleful of gray powder in a starving boy's cup and told him it was medicine. It was not medicine. It was monkshood and rot, arsenic ground fine, a distillation of everything the city's enemies might slip into a king's wine. The boy drank because the alternative was hunger, and the guild watched him sweat and shake and vomit into a bucket, and wrote numbers in a book, and the next morning they gave him a little more.

That was the secret no one tells about immunity: it isn't armor. It's a debt. Every dose taught my body a new way to die and then, barely, how not to. Twelve years of small poisons, each one a coin paid into a bank I could never close. The tasters who washed out—the ones who shook too hard, whose livers quit, who woke one morning unable to swallow—they didn't fail the trials. The trials collected them.

I had survived by becoming a ledger myself.

And now the door wanted proof.

I drew out the vial I'd been saving since Greyhollow—the one I'd decanted from the Protector's own stores the night he gave me his choice: deliver the cartographer or hang as a witch. I had chosen neither, and run, and stolen from him on the way out, which felt appropriate. Inside the glass swirled something colorless that caught the dying light and refused to shine. I didn't know its name. That frightened me more than anything since the prophecy page. A taster who knows his poisons is a craftsman. A taster holding an unnamed poison is a gambler.

"Tamsin." Aldric crouched beside me. Close enough that I could feel the cold coming off him—which was wrong, I realized; he should have been warm from the climb, and instead the chill rolled off him like he was the mountain's kin already. "What are you doing?"

"Paying the toll."

"You already paid a toll."

"The ferryman takes memories. Doors take blood." I worked the stopper free. "Or whatever this is."

"If it kills you—"

"It won't kill me." I almost smiled. "That's the whole point of me. I'm the one thing in Greyhollow that poison keeps missing. Twelve years, Aldric. I've swallowed death so many times it knocks first now."

He didn't laugh. He took my free hand instead, and held it, and his fingers were ice, and I thought: *the winter is inside him already, and I am running out of time to say the things I keep not saying.*

"Drink fast or don't drink," he said quietly. "Don't make me watch you choose slowly."

So I chose quickly, the way I did everything that mattered. I tipped the vial back and swallowed.

For a moment—nothing. The snow ticked softly against the black stone. Somewhere above us a bird made a sound like a hinge.

Then my heart forgot itself.

Not pain. Worse than pain: *absence*. My pulse stumbled like a man on stairs in the dark, missed, missed again, and my vision went silver at the edges, and I heard Aldric say my name from very far away, twice, three times, and I thought with strange professional calm: *so this is what the ledger closes like.* My hands went numb. My knees found the snow. The world tilted toward a darkness that was, insultingly, warm—

—and stopped.

My heart caught. Caught again. Found its rhythm the way a horse finds its footing on ice, and then steadied, and then, unbelievably, *strengthened*, each beat landing harder and cleaner than the last, as if the poison had swept through me finding nothing to burn and left in frustration. Sweat poured down my spine and steamed in the cold air. I knelt in the snow shaking like a wet dog, alive, alive, alive.

And the door opened.

No grinding. No thunder. The seam of black stone simply became a doorway, the way a face becomes familiar—suddenly, irreversibly, as if it had always been open and we had only just learned to see. Warm air breathed out of it, smelling of loam and candle smoke and, beneath that, faintly, of summer. Real summer. Cut grass and hot dust and ripening fruit.

It was the most horrifying thing I had ever smelled.

"A taster's key," I rasped. My voice came out scraped. "It only opens for someone who's died enough times to know the taste. The Undercourt doesn't want the living walking in uninvited. And it doesn't want the dead, either. Just us. The ones in between."

Aldric hauled me upright, and his arms were steady around me, and for a moment I let my forehead rest against his shoulder and simply breathed.

"You absolute idiot," he murmured into my hair. There was tears in it somewhere, or nearly. "You brilliant, reckless, self-keeping idiot."

"Flattery will get you mapped."

"Nothing will get me mapped. You heard me. Past this door there's nothing to draw." He pulled back and looked at the open dark, and I watched a man who had spent his whole life giving the world edges confront a place that refused to hold still. Cartographers hate that. The world is supposed to consent to being known. "Tamsin. If we go in there and I lose the way out—"

"Then I'll get us lost together. I'm very good at surviving places I don't understand. It's the only skill I have."

"It's not the only one." He said it strangely, formally, like a sentence he'd rehearsed. Then he frowned, faintly, as if the sentence had surprised him too, and shook it off. "Rope first. Then light. Then each other. In that order."

* * *

We went down.

The passage behind the door descended in long shallow ramps, cut smooth as river stones, and the dark was total within twenty steps—but the warmth. Gods, the warmth. After a week of summer snow, after the pass where Hessel had stayed behind with her last magic and her straight back, after frost in our beards and ice in our bootlaces, the mountain's interior wrapped around us like a bath. My frozen fingers ached as they thawed. Steam rose off our shoulders. Within the hour we had stripped our outer coats and tied them around our waists, and still the heat climbed, gentle and patient, the heat of a thing that had all the time in the world.

"It's wrong," Aldric kept saying, every few hundred steps, like a prayer he couldn't finish. "Caves run cold. Deep rock runs cold. Geothermal vents run *hot*, but then you'd hear water, you'd see mineral staining, sulfur—there's none of it. This heat has no source. It's not coming from anywhere. It's just *here*."

"Where do you think the winter goes?" I said. "All those souls they buy. All that fire they take out of people. It has to be kept somewhere." I ran my hand along the wall as we walked. The stone was blood-warm. "We're walking through the furnace room, Aldric. Everyone they've ever bought is burning in these walls so the Queen can wear winter like a gown."

He was quiet for a long time after that. Our footsteps made no echo. That was another wrongness I had stopped cataloguing and started simply enduring: the passage swallowed sound the way it exhaled heat, and we moved through a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse, strong and steady and newly grateful, and beneath it—only when I held my breath—something else. A hum. Vast and low, below hearing, more felt than heard, like the note a cathedral bell keeps humming after the rope goes still.

The candles began at the third hour.

At first I thought they were stalactites—pale drips of wax hanging from the ceiling, thousands of them, tens of thousands, stretching back into the dark farther than our lantern reached. Then Aldric raised the light and I saw that they hung from nothing. No fixtures. No chains. Each candle floated at its own height, burning with a flame that did not flicker, and the wax that melted off them fell upward, back into themselves, so that none of them ever grew shorter.

They were not candles. I knew what they were. I had known since Mother Hessel's kitchen, since she'd told me what the Undercourt does with what it buys. But knowing and seeing are different countries, and crossing between them hurt.

Souls. An entire cavern of them, burning quietly in the warm dark, lighting the road deeper in.

"Don't look at them," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "Aldric. Don't look at them too long."

Too late. He had stopped walking. He stood with the lantern raised, and the light lay across the nearest flames, and his face had gone the color of the wax.

"They're singing," he whispered.

I listened. Beneath the hum, beneath the bell-note, if I opened some door in myself I hadn't known I owned—I could hear it. Not words. Not quite. More like the memory of words, the way a shell holds the memory of the sea. Thousands of voices reduced to their smallest possible selves, humming the shape of songs they used to love.

"One of them knows me," Aldric said.

"What?"

He turned, and his eyes were wet and wide and confused, and he pointed with a hand that wasn't steady. "That one. Third row. The small one. When it—when the flame bent toward the light, I felt—" He pressed his fist to his sternum. "I felt it *here*. Like a hook behind my ribs. Like something reeling in a line." He laughed, short and awful. "That's madness. That's the dark getting to me. Right?"

I looked at the small flame he'd indicated. It burned a little apart from its neighbors, lower than the rest, and its light was thinner, guttering at the edges the way a candle gutters when the wax runs low.

When we moved on, it leaned after us.

I saw it. I would swear on every saint I'd ever sold charms against, I saw it lean, straining on some invisible wick, following the sound of Aldric's footsteps until the distance took it.

I didn't tell him. Some mercies are lies, and I had decided, somewhere in the last mile, that I would carry all of them from here on. He had lost enough at the river. He had lost things he wouldn't name and things he didn't know he'd lost, and if the small flame was what I feared it was—if the Queen had already begun to spend him, to burn him down in installments—then he would learn it soon enough, in a market where such things were priced and shelved.

Instead I took his hand as we walked, and he held on, and the warm dark carried us down.

* * *

The passage ended at the top of a stair.

Not carved—grown, I think, or accreted, a spiral of pale stone descending into a glow that came up from below like the memory of a sunset. The air here smelled richer: bread, and woodsmoke, and rain on hot cobblestones, and under all of it that impossible summer again, thick as perfume. Sounds drifted up. A crowd's murmur. A vendor's call, high and sweet and slightly wrong, the way a song played a half-step flat is wrong. Coins, somewhere, ringing against a counter.

A market. Of course. Every kingdom has one, even a kingdom of stolen fire.

Aldric stood at the stairhead and stared down into the glow, and I watched him try to do the thing he always did—fix the place, frame it, give it north and south and scale—and I watched him fail. His jaw tightened. His hand flexed around nothing, around the ghost of a pencil.

"There's no down," he said finally. "I mean it, Tamsin. I've been counting turns. By my count we've descended four hundred feet, which puts us under the valley floor, under the river, under the limestone shelf—it should be flooded down here. It should be *impossible* down here." He exhaled. "This place isn't in the world. It's underneath the world's agreement to have places."

"Then no one's ever drawn it," I said. "Which means no one's ever claimed it. Which means, for once in your life, nobody owns the truth of where you're standing." I squeezed his hand. "Go make it yours."

He laughed—really laughed, brief and startled, the first true laugh since the pass—and kissed me, quick and hard, tasting of salt and the road, and then we started down the stair together into the light of ten thousand burning souls.

Halfway down, the small flame from the cavern caught up with us.

I don't know how. It came drifting along the ceiling like a moth, guttering, straining, and it hovered above Aldric's head for three heartbeats, four, five, pouring its thin light down over his hair, and the hum of it resolved—for just a moment, just for me—into something almost like a word.

Almost like a name.

*Aldric.*

Then a bell rang somewhere in the market below, deep and cold as the far side of winter, and the flame fled back the way it had come, and the Queen's country closed around us like a hand.
