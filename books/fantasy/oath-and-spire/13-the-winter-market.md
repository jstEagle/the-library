## 13. The Winter Market

The dark was warm.

Aldric had expected cold — had braced for it through every mile of the descent, imagining the Undercourt as a heart of ice, all glittering frost and breath that smoked. Instead the air grew softer as they went down, thick as wool, smelling of candlewax and turned earth and something sweeter beneath it, like fruit left too long in a closed room. The warmth was the worst part. Warmth meant fuel. Fuel meant burning. And everything down here, he would learn within the hour, was burning.

Tamsin walked ahead of him with the lantern held low, the way he did everything — carefully, economically, as if light were a coin he might be asked to account for later. His shoulders were set in that particular way Aldric had catalogued a dozen times now: the taster's posture, weight balanced, ready to spit out what the mouth received. He hadn't spoken since the door sealed behind them. He kept touching the inside of his coat, where Hessel's charm lay wrapped in grey linen, and each time he touched it his jaw tightened like a man biting down on something bitter.

"Aldric." His voice came back to him strangely in the tunnel, flattened by the warm dark. "When we get there — whatever you see — don't touch anything. Don't taste anything. Don't *answer* anything."

"I remember the rules," Aldric said.

Something crossed Tamsin's face then, quick as a fish under ice — a flinch, or almost a flinch — and was gone before Aldric could name it. It had been happening for days, these small flickers, these half-second hesitations whenever Aldric spoke too easily, too confidently, as though Tamsin expected him to say something else entirely. As though he were waiting for a word that never came.

"You remember the rules," Tamsin repeated softly. Not a question. Almost a prayer.

The tunnel opened.

* * *

Aldric had mapped cities his whole life. He had drawn Greyhollow from its spire-tops to its sewer-grades, had charted coastlines no crown ship had ever sailed, had once spent a feverish month reconstructing the street plan of a city that burned down two hundred years before his birth, working only from a merchant's diary and the rings of a felled oak. He believed — had staked his whole self on the belief — that any place could be known. That any place, given enough attention, given enough honest lines, would yield.

The Winter Market refused to be known.

It filled a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor, a darkness overhead that might have been stone or might have been sky. Light came from everywhere and nowhere: thousands upon thousands of flames, none of them quite the color of fire. Some burned blue-white as starlight on snow. Some were gold gone green at the edges. Some were colors Aldric had no name for, hues that seemed to arrive in his eyes slightly after the rest of the world, as if they were being remembered rather than seen.

And the stalls — the stalls stretched away into that impossible distance in crooked avenues, hundreds of them, thousands, canopied in grey silk and hung with bells that made no sound, and every stall was selling light.

Not lamps. Not torches. *Light itself.* Light in jars. Light wound on spools. Light braided into ropes and hung like onions. Light pressed flat between panes of glass, light folded into paper lanterns, light dripping slow as honey from the lips of clay amphorae. Vendors moved among their wares with long tapers, trimming flames the way a gardener trims hedges, and the trimmed pieces fell not as sparks but as snow — fine, grey, unhurried snow that never reached the ground, that simply thinned into the warm air and vanished.

"Souls," Tamsin said. His voice had gone very quiet, very flat, the voice Aldric imagined he used when the king's broth smelled of bitter almonds. "That's what I told you they'd be. Souls."

"They're *candles*," Aldric said, because his mind, his poor cartographer's mind, needed the geometry first. "They're shaped like candles."

"That's what a soul looks like when someone's bought it." Tamsin didn't look at him. He was watching the nearest stall with the fixed stillness of a man watching a snake. "Burned down to whatever's left. Trimmed to make the winter. Every flame you see — every single one — that's somebody. Somebody who sold themselves, or got sold, or traded themselves for passage or pardon or a child's fever broken. The court doesn't burn coal, Aldric. It burns people."

Aldric looked at the market again and this time he saw it properly, and wished he could unsee it.

Because once you knew, you couldn't stop knowing. The jar of golden light labeled *SUMMER, 61 YEARS, GENTLY USED* — that was a grandmother, an entire grandmother, her kitchen and her humming and the specific way she'd said a grandchild's name. The spool of blue thread-light that a weaver-woman was selling by the inch — that was a girl who had loved someone once, the love spun out and sold off until only the workmanlike blue remained. A choir of voices hung in a birdcage near the central avenue, singing a harvest song over and over, and the song was beautiful, and the song was *all that was left*, and the thing that had once been a person stood beside the cage wearing its body like an empty coat, smiling at customers, remembering nothing of why the melody made its hands shake.

He thought of his mother's name and reached for it and — nothing.

Not nothing exactly. Worse than nothing. A shape where the name should be, smooth and blank as an unlabeled region on an old map, *here be dragons*, and around the shape a terrible certainty that something had been written there once. He had noticed the gaps for days now. Small things. The name of the street he'd grown up on. Which hand he'd taught himself to write with. The reason he flinched at the smell of rosemary. He had told himself it was fear, exhaustion, the thin air of the high passes. Standing in the warm dark of the Winter Market, watching souls burn, he understood with a lurching, seasick clarity that fear did not take things from you in clean-edged pieces.

Something had taken them. Something was still taking.

"Aldric?" Tamsin's hand was on his arm. When had Tamsin's hand gotten onto his arm? "You've gone white. What is it?"

"I can't—" Aldric heard his own voice come out wrong, too high. "There are things missing. In my head. There have been things missing for days and I keep telling myself it's the road, it's the fear, but Tamsin, I can't remember my mother's *name*, I can't remember—" He stopped, because the next one wouldn't come either, the biggest one, the one that sat in the center of his chest like a hole in a roof letting the rain in. There was a person-shaped absence somewhere in him and he could feel the draft coming through it.

Tamsin went very still. In the blue-white light of the nearest stalls his face was stripped of everything except a grief so old and so controlled that it looked carved.

"What else is missing?" Tamsin asked. Carefully. So carefully.

"I don't know. That's the hell of it. You can't inventory what isn't there." Aldric laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I keep reaching for — there's something, someone, there's a *name*, I can nearly say it, it's right at the edge—"

"Don't." Tamsin's grip tightened. "Don't chase it. Not here. If it's at the edge, chasing it might push it over." His thumb moved against Aldric's wrist, once, an unconscious gesture of such tenderness that it caught in Aldric like a hook — familiar, achingly familiar, a gesture his body knew and his ledger of memories had somehow lost the entry for. "We find your candle first. We get it back. Everything else comes back with it. Do you understand me? Everything comes home."

"And if it's already burned?"

Tamsin didn't answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

They moved into the market because standing still marked you. That much Aldric's instincts still supplied, even with holes in his head: in any crowd, motion is anonymity. He let Tamsin take the lead and studied the place the only way he knew how, the way that had kept him sane his whole life — he mapped it.

The avenues radiated from a central square where a spire of black ice rose out of the floor and up past sight, humming faintly, shedding a frost that melted before it landed. Off the avenues ran alleys of specialist trade. One entire lane sold nothing but songs — voices in bottles, harmonies in loaves of dark bread that the vendor swore tasted like the year they were sung. Another lane dealt in seasons: jars of summer stacked to the ceiling, autumn pressed into leather-bound volumes, one stall offering *a single morning in April, 40 years ago, dew included*, priced beyond anything Aldric had ever seen. The buyers drifted through in clothes too fine for any human court, tall and lovely and wrong, their beauty arranged rather than grown, and their eyes when they turned them on Aldric were patient as glaciers.

And everywhere, everywhere, the candles.

Some stalls organized them alphabetically. Some by year of purchase. One vast establishment near the square had them racked like wine, tier upon tier vanishing upward into the dark, tens of thousands of small flames, and a placard at the entrance read in letters of silvered frost:

**EVERY FLAME FEEDS THE WINTER. EVERY WINTER NEEDS FUEL. BE GRATEFUL SOMEONE BURNED FOR YOU.**

Aldric read it three times. On the third reading he understood that it wasn't advertising. It was theology.

"The king knows," he said. The words came out of him before he decided to say them. "Greyhollow's crown. This is what the bargains buy. All those generations of treaties, the Protector's sealed ledgers — they've been sending fuel down here. People. Our people."

"Since before the ban on magic," Tamsin said. "Maybe the ban *is* the bargain. Keep magic out of human hands, keep the supply moving underground." He said it evenly, but Aldric had spent enough evenings learning the geography of Tamsin's face to see the fault-line running under the calm. Twelve years tasting poison for a crown, and the crown had been drinking something worse the whole time. "Later. Grieve later. Find yourself first."

"How?"

Tamsin stopped at a corner where two avenues met, and for a moment he simply breathed, and Aldric watched him do the thing he had watched him do a hundred times across a hundred dinners: assess, decide, commit. The taster's discipline. The whole self bent to a single swallow.

"Names," Tamsin said. "Everything down here is catalogued. They don't burn chaos; they burn *inventory*. Somewhere there's a rack with V-A-N-E on it. We walk the racks."

* * *

It took hours. Or what felt like hours; time moved strangely in the warm dark, stretching like taffy, and Aldric suspected afterward that the market itself was bending it, keeping customers lingering, keeping them browsing. They walked the lettered aisles while vendors smiled at them with too many teeth and offered samples — *just a sip of springtime, sir, just a taste of your own wedding day* — and Tamsin walked them past every offer without breaking stride, though twice Aldric saw his hand drift toward his coat and the charm inside.

They found the Vs in a narrow aisle near the market's edge, where the candles were older, the flames smaller, the frost on the racks thicker. The labels here were handwritten, the ink brown with age. Vagran. Valcourt. Vance. Vane.

Aldric saw his own name and the world went quiet.

Not silent — the market hummed and murmured and sang its stolen songs — but *quiet*, the quiet inside a wave before it breaks. The candle stood on the rack at eye height, in a niche of its own, newer than its neighbors, the wax still pale and unyellowed. It was a good-sized candle. It had been a generous soul; even now, even sold, even burning, you could tell. The label beneath it read, in careful clerk's script:

**VANE, ALDRIC. CARTOGRAPHER. PURCHASED BY THE COURT OF UNDERMOUNTAIN, FULL TITLE, IN PERPETUITY. REMAINING: SEE FLAME.**

He looked at the flame.

It was low. That was the first thing, the thing that stopped his breath: it was *low*, guttering in a way the neighboring candles weren't, burning down faster than a soul should burn, as if something about it — some hollowness in the wick, some leak in the wax — made it consume itself greedily. It burned blue at the base and gold at the tip and it threw a light that Aldric recognized with a sick, swimming jolt, because he had seen that exact quality of light before, in his own study, in the hour before dawn, reflected in the inkwell while he worked. It was the color of his own concentration. His candle burned the way he burned.

He reached for it.

His fingers stopped a hand's width from the flame. Not because anything held them — there was no glass, no barrier, no ward he could see — but because the moment he crossed that last hand's width, something in the flame *looked at him*. He felt it. Attention, ancient and small and terribly tired, turning toward his hand the way a starving animal turns toward a door it has learned opens onto nothing.

And the flame shrank from him.

It leaned away, a fraction, the way a beaten dog leans from a raised hand, and Aldric understood — knowledge arriving whole, the way map-knowledge arrives, all at once and forever — that it knew him. That it was him, the sold remainder, the part of himself he had carried unknowing all his life, and it had been burning alone in the dark for however long the bargain had stood, and it did not trust the hand that reached for it, because every hand that had ever touched it had been a buyer's hand.

"Oh," Aldric said. Just that. Just *oh*. Because there was nothing else. Because language had been built for weather and bread and the names of streets, and there was no word for standing in a warm dark market looking at your own soul, priced and burning and afraid of you.

"It's fast," Tamsin said behind him, and his voice had gone taut as wire. "Aldric — look at how fast it's going. Candles like the others, they'll burn decades yet. Yours is—" He stepped closer, and Aldric saw him do the arithmetic, the same arithmetic Aldric was doing, the same terrible sum. "Weeks. Maybe less. Whatever they paid for you, they're spending it quickly. Spending it on the winter. On the snow in summer. On—"

"On the forgetting." Aldric's hand was still raised, hovering, unable to advance, unable to retreat. "The gaps. My mother's name. The street I grew up on. The—" the person-shaped absence, the name at the edge of his tongue, the draft through the hole in his roof — "it's not the mountain taking them. It's *me*. I'm burning them. I'm burning myself to stay lit, and the parts going into the flame are—" His voice cracked. "What am I losing, Tamsin? What's already gone?"

Tamsin was silent for three full seconds. Aldric counted them against the flicker of his own guttering soul.

"Nothing that matters," Tamsin said at last, and it was the worst lie Aldric had ever heard anyone tell, because it was told with such care, such craftsmanship, such obvious and desperate love that it could only have been built to hide something enormous. "Nothing that matters. We're going to take it back. Tonight, tomorrow, however long it takes — we take it back, and we light it ourselves, and nothing else gets fed to the winter. Do you hear me?"

The flame leaned toward Tamsin's voice.

Aldric saw it happen. Saw his own sold soul, mistrustful of his hand, turn toward the sound of the taster's voice like a plant toward sun — lean, brighten, steady — and in that instant the flame burned gold all the way down, and for one heartbeat it cast a shadow on the rack behind it, and the shadow was the shape of two people sitting close together over a plate of stolen pastries, laughing.

Then it guttered again, and the shadow was gone, and Aldric stood in the warm dark with his hand still raised, shaking, understanding that his soul trusted Tamsin more than it trusted him, and having absolutely no idea why.

"Tamsin," he said slowly. "Why does it do that?"

Tamsin's face did something complicated and broke, very slightly, along the fault-line. "Do what," he said, in a voice that begged him not to ask.

But before Aldric could press — before the question at the edge of his tongue could reach the flame of itself — a bell rang. Not one of the soundless market bells: a real bell, deep and silver and cold, tolling from the black spire in the central square, and every flame in the Winter Market bowed at once, ten thousand souls bending together like wheat before wind, and a voice made of frost came rolling down the avenues:

"*The taster is come. The taster is come. Let the bride be brought to audience.*"

Every vendor turned to look at them.

Every flame turned to look at them.

And Aldric's candle — his own low, frightened, guttering soul — turned its light full upon Tamsin Blackwood's face, and burned, for one clear instant, like a wedding candle.
