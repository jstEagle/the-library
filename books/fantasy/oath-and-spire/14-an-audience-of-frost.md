## 14. An Audience of Frost

They came for him at what the Undercourt called morning, though no sun had ever reached this deep.

Tamsin knew it was morning only because the candles in the guest chamber guttered all at once, the way candles do when a door opens somewhere far above and a draft finds them. Then the frost-heralds were in the room—three of them, tall as doorframes, their bodies like the inside of an icehouse, blue-white and faintly luminous, faces smooth as river ice with nothing behind it but cold light.

*The Queen will taste you now,* they said, in a voice like a blade drawn slowly across silk.

*Taste.* The word was chosen, he was sure, to remind him where he stood. He had been tasting things for other people's safety since he was eleven years old. He rose from the narrow bed of grey wool, dressed in the clothes he'd been given—black wool, fine as anything woven in Greyhollow, cut for someone slightly taller—and followed the heralds out into the corridors of the mountain.

The Undercourt did not sleep, but it dreamed. That was the impression the halls gave him as he walked them: vast galleries of pale stone veined with silver, hung with banners that had not moved in centuries because there was no wind, lit by candles that burned without shrinking. And everywhere, everywhere, the sense of attention. Of being watched by the walls themselves, or by whatever the walls were made of. He had spent twelve years learning to read a court—the angle of a serving girl's glance, the weight of silence around a particular chair—and this place read like a court that had forgotten it was one. Or never known. A court that had become its own ceremony so completely there was nothing left underneath.

Aldric walked beside him. They had not been separated since the vault—since Tamsin had pressed the candle-flame against his own chest and felt it sink into him like a swallowed coal—but Aldric had barely spoken since. He looked at the halls with his cartographer's eyes, cataloguing, measuring, and when Tamsin caught his hand briefly in the dark between one gallery and the next, Aldric squeezed back and then let go, and said nothing, and Tamsin told himself that fear made people quiet. Fear made people quiet. It did not have to mean anything else.

He was lying to himself and he knew it. Something had gone out of Aldric at the river. Some warmth, some small habitual thing—the way he used to hum when he inked coastlines, the way he said *we* instead of *I*. Tamsin had been too busy surviving to name it. Now, walking toward the throne of the creature who had bought his lover's soul, he found himself naming it over and over, the way you probe a broken tooth with your tongue.

*Here,* said the heralds, and the doors opened.

* * *

The throne room of the Winter Queen was a cavern of black ice, and the ceiling of it was a sky.

That was the first thing Tamsin understood, and understanding it took the breath out of him. Far above—two hundred feet, three hundred—stars burned in a frozen firmament, real stars or perfect copies, wheeling so slowly he could not see them move. Snow fell from that false heaven in long, unhurried spirals, and before each flake touched the ground it stopped, mid-air, and hung there. Thousands of them. Hundreds of thousands. A snowfall paused forever, filling the great dark hall like dust motes in a shaft of light, and Tamsin realized with a lurch that every suspended flake was a soul. He could feel them the way he could feel poison in a cup: a wrongness, a crowding, a whisper just below hearing. The whole room was full of the bought and the bound, hanging in the air like weather that had been told to wait.

At the far end, on a dais of layered frost, sat the Queen.

She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, which is to say: entirely without mercy, and aware of it. Her gown was grey as old ice; her hair was white, but not with age—with stillness. Her skin held the faint translucence of thin ice over dark water. She did not wear a crown. She *was* one, somehow; the air around her brow glittered and cracked with cold, and looking directly at her made Tamsin's eyes ache the way snow-blindness aches.

Beside the dais, on a low stool like a servant's, sat something that made his stomach drop through the floor.

It was a table setting. A single place: plate, cup, knife, spoon, all of black iron furred with frost. Set for one. Waiting.

"Sit," said the Queen, and her voice was the sound a lake makes freezing—slow, cracking, beautiful. "Or stand. You are a taster. I am told you prefer to stand near doors."

Tamsin did not sit. He also did not go near the doors, because the doors had closed behind him with the soft finality of a book shutting, and because Aldric had been guided to a position three paces to the Queen's left, flanked by two heralds, and Tamsin would not put distance between them.

"You know my name," he said.

"I know many names." The Queen regarded him with eyes the colour of deep glacier ice, blue shading into green shading into the dark. "I have had a century to learn them. Yours arrived recently. Tamsin Blackwood. Taster to the House of Corvane. Seller of charms in the scullery markets." A pause, delicate as frost forming on glass. "Swallower of deaths."

"You've been reading my mail."

"I have been reading your *prophecy*." She lifted one hand, and from the frozen air beside her throne a book drifted down—old, leather gone grey, its spine cracked. Tamsin recognized it with a sick lurch. The archive volume. The one Aldric had copied from. Here, in the heart of the mountain, at the Queen's elbow, like a household ledger. "You have read it too, I think. The relevant passage."

"The taster and the cartographer," Tamsin said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. Twelve years of practice at sounding steady while everything in him screamed. "'One shall break the winter, or wed it.'"

"Yes." The Queen smiled, and her smile was the worst thing he had seen yet in this place, worse than the hanging snow, because it was warm. It was the smile of someone remembering warmth. "And here is the difficulty, taster, the small cruel joke of it: the prophecy does not say *which*. It never said which. I have had a hundred years to wonder which, and I wrote the wretched thing myself."

* * *

The words landed in him slowly, the way cold lands in bones.

"You wrote it," he said.

"I was a girl in a village called Marrowdale, before your city had walls. Before your guild had guildhalls." The Queen rose from her throne, and the suspended snow shivered, all of it, a million flakes trembling in unison. "I was a kitchen girl. And then I was a taster—for a lord whose enemies were patient, whose wine was often kind, whose bread was rarely safe. I learned immunity the way you learned it. Dish by dish. Death by death, in doses small enough to survive." She descended the dais step by step, and the frost spread outward from her hem across the black floor like a blooming flower seen in reverse. "I was very good at it. I survived everything they gave me. And when the Undercourt came hungry in the long dark years, and my lord could not pay, he paid with me. He gave them the one thing he owned outright."

"Your soul."

"My *protection*," she corrected softly. "My immunities. All those little deaths I had swallowed and mastered—they were currency, child. The court bought them out of me one by one, and what was left when they finished was cold enough to be useful. Cold enough to be crowned." She stopped ten feet from him, close enough that his breath fogged and hung in the air between them, refusing to fall. "The prophecy came later. I wrote it in my last human year, when I still remembered what hope felt like, and I wrote it badly, and I have regretted its ambiguity for a century. *One shall break the winter, or wed it.* I meant it as a warning. It has functioned as a job description."

"And the brides before you?" Tamsin asked. "There were others. Hessel said—" He stopped himself. Did not give away how much Mother Hessel had told him, or how much she'd paid for knowing it.

"There were four," the Queen said. "Each one a taster. Each one brought below by a love they could not abandon, exactly as you were. Each one offered the same choice I am about to offer you. Three chose service." Her gaze flicked, brief and unreadable, toward the hanging snow. Somewhere in that frozen blizzard, Tamsin understood, were three women who had once stood exactly where he stood. "One chose breaking. She broke nothing but herself, and the court sealed the gates for a decade after, and the winter above lasted seven years beyond its term. Your Greyhollow histories call it the Famine of the Grey Summer. You may have heard of it."

Tamsin had heard of it. Every child in Greyhollow had heard of it. It was the story mothers used to make children finish their soup.

"So you understand," the Queen said, "that I am not offering you cruelty. I am offering you the only ending to this story that has ever worked."

* * *

She gestured, and the table setting beside the dais floated down and arranged itself on the floor between them: plate, cup, knife, spoon, all gleaming with rime.

"Taste for me," the Queen said. "Not for a season. Not for a lifetime. Forever. My court eats, taster—it is one of the few human habits we kept, because ceremony is the last thing to die—and my food comes from a hundred desperate bargains, a hundred bitter vassals, and half of it arrives wishing me dead. For a century I have relied on servants whose tongues went numb a generation ago. I need a tongue that remembers. I need someone who has swallowed twelve years of small deaths and learned to hear them coming." She tilted her head, and the starlight from the false sky caught in her hair like frost in fur. "In exchange: the cartographer goes free. Unbound. His candle returned whole, his debts dissolved, his memories restored—every one, even the ones he has already paid away. He walks out of this mountain into the spring that is coming, because spring *is* coming, taster, whether I like it or not, and he lives the mortal life you apparently want for him. Children, if you're sentimental. Old age. An unremarkable grave in a country churchyard. Everything you would choose for him."

"And you get me."

"I get the finest palate beneath any sky, and I get it eternally. You will not age. You will not sicken. You will not die. The poisons of this court cannot touch you—I will make certain of it personally; it is within my gift. Time itself will pass over you like weather over stone." She stepped closer, and the cold of her was not painful; that was the horror of it. It was *comfortable*, the way numbness is comfortable, the way the second hour in a snowbank stops hurting. "Think what that means, Tamsin Blackwood. No more counting doses. No more scullery charm-stalls, no more watching desperate people weigh their children's fevers against your prices. No more fear. You have spent twelve years buying survival one mouthful at a time, and I am offering you the whole purchase at once, paid in full, forever. Survival without end. Is that not the dream? Is that not what every dose was *for*?"

Tamsin looked at the table setting. At the knife, furred with frost. At the empty plate, waiting for the first of an infinite number of meals.

He thought about the boy he had been, kneeling in the guild cellar with the first poisoned cup shaking in his hands, and the oath he'd sworn to himself afterward: *never again will I be the one who can't afford to live.* Twelve years of discipline. Twelve years of hoarding. Every immunity a coin in a purse he'd sewn into his own flesh. And here was a queen offering to fill the purse to bursting.

That was the trap, and he saw it clearly, and seeing it did not make it less bright.

Because it *was* the dream. That was what made her dangerous—not the cold, not the hanging souls, but the fact that she had read him correctly. She had read him the way he read a suspect dish. She knew exactly which note of bitterness to balance with sweetness.

"And if I refuse?" he said.

"Then the gates seal early. Tomorrow, perhaps. The winter above continues past its term—as long as I require, and I will require a very long time, because I will be angry, and my anger has always been seasonal." She said it gently, almost kindly, the way you tell a patient a prognosis. "The cartographer stays. Bound. His candle burns in my vault until it gutters, and souls gutter slowly down here, taster—decades, sometimes a century—and what remains of him at the end is what remains of everyone you see hanging in my hall. And you walk out alone into a Greyhollow that hates you, having failed, having lost him twice: once to me, once to your own pride." A pause. "Or," she said, "you can keep him. Alive, free, whole, and warm, for the price of standing at a table. It is not even a difficult arithmetic."

"No," Tamsin said. "It isn't. That's what frightens me."

The Queen smiled again, that terrible remembered warmth. "Honesty. How refreshing. My previous tasters lied beautifully right up until they signed."

"I'm not signing."

"Not yet."

"Not ever." He made himself look at her, made himself stand in the comfortable killing cold, and said the true thing, the thing he had carried since the vault, since the river, maybe since the first stolen pastry in a Greyhollow market: "You're offering me forever. But forever isn't the prize. I thought it was, for twelve years. I built my whole life on the idea that surviving was winning. And then a man with ink on his fingers laughed at my jokes over burnt pastries, and I found out I'd been starving in a pantry." His voice cracked and he let it. "You want a bride who'll serve winter forever. I'd rather have thirty bad years and one good one with him than eternity at your table. So take your arithmetic and freeze it."

Something moved behind the Queen's eyes. Not anger. Something older and more tired—recognition, maybe, the look of a woman hearing an echo of a girl she had drowned a century ago.

"That," she said quietly, "is what the fourth one said."

* * *

The word *fourth* had barely left her lips when the hall changed.

The suspended snow—all of it, every soul-flake hanging in the frozen firmament—shivered again, but differently this time. Not the tremble of the Queen's passing. A stir. A leaning. Tamsin felt it against his skin like a crowd drawing breath, and the whisper that lived below his hearing rose half a register, and among the thousand indistinct voices he caught—clearly, unmistakably—one that was *Aldric's.*

He turned.

Aldric stood where the heralds had placed him, three paces from the throne, and he was looking at Tamsin with an expression Tamsin had never seen on his face. Polite. Attentive. Slightly puzzled—the look of a man watching a stranger argue passionately on his behalf.

"Tamsin," Aldric said, and his voice was level and cool and terribly courteous, "why does the Queen keep calling you *my*—"

He stopped. Blinked. Put a hand briefly to his temple, as if a page had fluttered in a wind only he could feel.

"—as if we know each other well?" he finished. "I only meant—we've travelled together, yes, but you speak as though—" Another blink. The puzzlement deepened, and behind it, for half a heartbeat, something else surfaced: fear, raw and drowning, the look of a man going under. "Why can't I remember—" he began, and then the heralds shifted, and the frost-light in the hall thickened like fog, and the moment closed over him the way water closes over a dropped coin.

The Queen watched Tamsin watch it happen. She did not gloat. That was somehow worse.

"Now you understand the full shape of my offer," she said softly. "Ask him, taster. Ask him what he paid at the river. Ask him what he traded to keep his candle burning until you could reach it. And then ask yourself whether you can carry enough love for two." She turned back toward her throne, her gown whispering across the frosted floor, and paused with one hand on the armrest. "You have until the last snow seals the gates. Choose your cage, Tamsin Blackwood. They are both lined with him."

Above them, in the false and frozen sky, the hanging snow stirred like a held breath about to break—and somewhere in that vast white multitude, a voice that was almost Aldric's whispered a word Tamsin couldn't hear, and would spend the rest of the night trying to.
