## 15. What Aldric Paid at the River

Aldric woke to the smell of cold iron and wet stone, and to the certainty that he had forgotten something enormous.

It was not the ordinary forgetting of a man surfacing from deep sleep—that soft erosion where dreams dissolve before they can be named. This was structural. He could feel it the way a cartographer feels a gap in a coastline: there was a shape to his life, and somewhere in that shape a piece of land had been cut away, and the map of himself now showed open water where solid ground should have been.

He was lying on his side on a shelf of rock, and the rock was warm.

That was the first wrong thing. The air down here—wherever *here* was—was warm as a held breath, warm as a kitchen at midnight, and it pressed against his skin like something alive. Above him, no ceiling he could see; only a darkness so thick it seemed less like an absence of light than a substance, a black felt stretched over the world. Somewhere far off, water moved. Not dripping. Moving. A river underground, going about its business with the patience of things that have never once seen the sun.

He sat up. His coat was still buttoned. His satchel was still slung across his body, and when he pressed his hand against it he felt the reassuring crackle of paper—the maps, the copied prophecy page, the charcoal stubs, all of it intact. His fingers were ink-stained. There was a smear of graphite along the outside of his right palm where he always smudged it, drawing.

So: he was Aldric Vane. He was a cartographer. He had been commissioned by the crown of Greyhollow, and then he had run from Greyhollow, because—

Because the crown had sold him. Because there was a court beneath the mountain. Because a book written a hundred years before his birth had described him by name.

The knowledge came back in order, tidy as a legend's key. What did not come back was anything attached to it. He knew *why* he had fled the city. He could not remember who had made him flee. There had been someone. He was sure of it the way you are sure of a door in a dream—you never see it open, but you know someone has just gone through it, and the latch is still swinging.

He said the name aloud, testing the dark: "Tamsin."

Nothing. No face rose to meet the word. It was like saying the name of a city he'd never surveyed—he understood that it existed, that it meant something, that other people carried it around inside them like a coin in a pocket. But it bought him nothing. It was blank currency.

And yet his chest hurt when he said it. A clean, specific ache, just beneath the sternum, like a bruise on an organ he couldn't locate. Grief without a photograph. Mourning with the portrait cut out of its frame.

"All right," he said quietly, to the warm dark, because talking kept the fear at arm's length. "All right. I'll map it anyway."

That was the whole of him, he realized. That was what remained when everything else was stripped away: a man who, faced with the unmapped, took out his instruments.

* * *

The stranger found him—or Aldric found the stranger; afterward he would never be certain which—and the stranger already knew his name.

"Tamsin Blackwood," the man said, stepping out of a seam in the rock with a lantern held low, and Aldric felt that clean ache again, sharper, like a key turning in a lock whose mechanism had been removed. "You're awake. Good. We have to keep moving—the gates seal at first snow, and down here snow isn't weather. It's a decision."

He was slighter than his voice suggested, quick-moving, with the wary economy of a man who had spent years being paid to swallow danger and had learned to make his body small. His eyes were remarkable—not the color, though they were a dark amber, but the way they moved over Aldric: a full inventory, top to bottom, done in half a second and then deliberately, visibly, put away. As if he had caught himself looking for something and decided not to find it.

"You know me," Aldric said.

"I know your name." Tamsin lifted the lantern, and the light climbed Aldric's face. Something crossed the taster's expression—there and gone, a fish under ice. "We traveled together. From Greyhollow. You don't remember."

"No." Honesty cost less than pride down here; pride was a luxury of places with doors and daylight. "I remember the mission. I remember why we came. I remember—" He stopped, because this part arrived strangely, arriving at all. "I remember that I paid a toll. At a river. I remember the ferryman's hands. They were like birch bark. And I remember choosing what to give him." He frowned. "But I don't remember what I chose. Isn't that strange? I remember the *choosing*. I remember it mattered. I remember thinking—I remember thinking, better this than the candle. Better the candle keep its flame."

Tamsin had gone very still. Lantern-light trembled once across the rock walls, and in that trembling the shadows looked briefly like a crowd of witnesses.

"What candle?" Tamsin asked. His voice was careful the way ice is careful—a surface holding weight, one misstep from giving.

"I saw it. In the market below—I've seen it, haven't I? Or I will." Aldric pressed two fingers to the bridge of his nose. The memories were not missing so much as misfiled; he kept reaching for tomorrow and finding yesterday. "My soul. They keep it as a candle. I know that the way I know the coast of Vellmark—because I've drawn it. And at the river, the ferryman said the flame was guttering, that it needed feeding, that a soul sold twice burns thin, and I could pay in memory to—" He gestured vaguely, a cartographer's gesture, indicating a region rather than a point. "To shore it up. To bank the wick. One memory per passenger, he said. You paid too."

"Yes," Tamsin said.

"Which memory?"

The silence went on long enough that Aldric looked up from his own confusion and actually saw the man in front of him. Tamsin Blackwood stood in the lantern light with his jaw set and his eyes fixed slightly past Aldric's shoulder, and his face was doing something Aldric had no training to read but every instinct to recognize: it was the face of a man standing in a burned house, finding one wall still standing, and touching it.

"A friend," Tamsin said at last. "From when I was young. Someone I saved. It's gone now. I know that I saved them; I can't remember them." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Aldric had ever seen, because it was competent. Twelve years of tasting poison had taught this man to smile through anything. "The toll takes what hurts most. That's how they price it. So—we're even, you and I. Two passengers, two payments."

"We're not even," Aldric said slowly. "You remember *me*. You said my name like—like you'd been carrying it a long way. I don't remember you at all. I remember the road, the hunters, the bothy, the pass. I remember the mission. I don't remember the man."

Something in Tamsin's posture closed, gently, like a shop shutter at dusk. "Then the ferryman took well from you," he said. "Come on. The gates."

* * *

They walked.

The passage descended in long switchbacks, and Aldric, because he could not help himself, began to map it. Charcoal on the backs of his charts, in the margins, anywhere. The tunnel walls were veined with something pale that caught the lantern light and gave it back slowly, like a glow remembered rather than emitted. Warm air rose past them from below, carrying scents he catalogued automatically: beeswax, old paper, frost, and under all of it something floral and faintly rotten, like lilies left too long in a sealed room.

"The warmth," Aldric said, after an hour or a day—it was impossible to measure time where no sun came. "Why is it warm? I'd have guessed a winter court would be cold."

"It is cold." Tamsin didn't look back. "The cold isn't temperature. It's appetite. Everything down here wants heat—wants summer, wants life, wants time—and it pulls. The warmth you feel is the pull. You're feeling yourself being wanted."

"That's a horrible sentence."

"It's the truest thing anyone's told you all week."

Aldric laughed—short, surprised at himself—and heard Tamsin's breath catch ahead of him in the dark, small and involuntary, quickly mastered. The ache under Aldric's sternum flared at the sound of his own laugh, which made no sense, unless grief could be triggered by joy, which he was beginning to suspect was exactly how grief worked.

They stopped at a landing where the passage opened into a gallery, and the gallery was hung with candles.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They lined both walls from floor to the unseen ceiling, each in its own niche of black stone, each burning with a flame that gave light but no heat—or rather, that gave heat and took it back again, a slow tidal breathing Aldric felt against his skin. Some flames burned tall and steady. Some guttered. Some were almost out, crouched on their wicks like animals in a burrow, and as Aldric passed them he felt each one notice him.

"They're souls," he said. Not a question. He had drawn this gallery. He had drawn it in a book a century dead, or someone had, and the line had passed through him like current through wire.

"Bought and paid for," Tamsin said quietly. "Farmers who sold a bad harvest. Mothers who sold a child's fever. Kings. Cartographers." He said the last word lightly, too lightly. "Everyone the court ever fed on. The winter runs on them."

Aldric walked the gallery slowly, and the candles turned their flames toward him as he passed, the way flowers turn—not toward light, but toward the last light they remember. Near the gallery's end, in a niche set slightly apart, burned a candle that made his teeth ache to look at. Its flame was low. Thin. Blue at the base, the way a fire burns when it is eating its own wax to survive.

He knew it was his the way he knew his own signature.

"There you are," he murmured, and raised his hand, and his fingers stopped a finger's width from the flame—stopped, because some law older than law would not let them cross. He could feel the heat of it on his fingertips. It felt like a hearth. It felt like a specific hearth, in a specific room, and he almost—almost—had the room, had the window, had the voice that had talked to him across that room while he drew—

The flame leaned toward his hand. Desperate. Hungry. Recognizing.

"Aldric." Tamsin's hand closed around his wrist—not pulling, just holding, warm through the shirt sleeve, and his voice had frayed down to the thread. "Don't. It can't be touched, and it shouldn't be fed, and—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "It knows you. That's enough. Come away."

Aldric looked at the taster's hand on his wrist. At the man's face, close now, lit from below by a thousand borrowed flames. And he watched Tamsin understand, in real time, that he had been caught—that some look or word or simple fact of proximity had given him away—and watch him decide, with visible cost, to say nothing.

"You loved someone," Aldric said. "Before the river. Whoever it was—the ferryman took them from me, didn't he. That's what I paid. That's what paid for the candle." His own voice sounded far away to him, level, professional, a surveyor reporting findings. "I paid with a person. And you've been walking beside me since, knowing that, saying my name like it was yours to carry, and I keep—" The ache under his sternum cracked open another degree. "I keep grieving you without knowing why."

"Don't," Tamsin said.

"Why not?"

"Because if you start asking why, you'll start digging, and what the toll takes stays taken, and all you'll do is bleed over the scar." He released Aldric's wrist, one finger at a time, as if each release were a small expense. "Save it. Save everything you have. We're nearly at the court, and the Queen will offer you something that looks like mercy, and you'll need every scrap of yourself you've got left to refuse her."

"And what will she offer you?"

Tamsin's face did the thing again—the fish under the ice, the flicker of a whole drowned country visible for one instant beneath the surface.

"Immunity," he said. "She'll offer me immunity. She always does."

"*Always?*" 

But Tamsin had already turned, lifting the lantern, moving off down the descending passage, and the dark folded itself around him like a closing book. Aldric stood a moment longer before his own guttering soul, close enough to feel it strain toward him, thin blue flame bending like grass in wind, like a hand reaching from a window.

"Hold on," he whispered to it—to himself, to whatever the ferryman had left unspent in the wax. "Whoever I was to him. Hold on until I can earn it back."

The flame bent lower. It did not answer. Souls, Aldric suspected, were like maps in that way: they recorded everything, and explained nothing, and it was the living who had to read them.

He followed the lantern down.

* * *

The gates of the Undercourt were carved from a single slab of glacier ice that had never known a sky, and they opened at Tamsin's approach without being touched—which meant, Aldric thought, watching, that the mountain had been expecting this man for a very long time. Beyond them, corridors of black stone branched like veins, and from somewhere deep in the body of the world came a sound that stopped Aldric's charcoal hand mid-note:

Singing. Hundreds of voices, thin and sweet and terribly patient. The candles, singing in their niches. Or the souls in the candles. Or the winter itself, humming over its food.

Tamsin paused at the threshold. For a moment—half a heartbeat, no more—he looked back at Aldric with an expression stripped of all its competence, all its armor, naked as a man at a graveside. Whatever he was about to say, Aldric watched him decide against it, swallow it, pocket it the way he seemed to pocket everything.

"Stay behind me in there," Tamsin said instead. "Whatever you see. Whatever she shows you. However real it looks." A breath. "Especially if it looks like me."

Then he stepped through the gates, and the singing swelled to meet him like water closing over a stone, and Aldric Vane—cartographer, fugitive, amnesiac, the man who had once been loved by someone worth a soul's ransom—followed him into the court of winter, carrying nothing but his maps, his mission, and a grief shaped exactly like a stranger.
