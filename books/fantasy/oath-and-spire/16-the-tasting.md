## 16. The Tasting

They dressed him for it.

That was the first obscenity — that the Undercourt had clothes for a taster, that somewhere in the frost-hung wardrobe of the Winter Queen's household there hung garments cut and fitted for the man whose only office was to die first. The seamstresses were wights, grey and patient as moths, and they worked in silence, lacing him into a doublet of white fox-fur over grey silk until he looked, in the tall mirror of ice, like something carved rather than born.

*You could refuse,* said the part of him that had survived twelve years in Greyhollow by knowing exactly which refusals were survivable.

He looked at his own reflection and did not believe it. Refusal here was not a door you walked through; it was a wall you were buried behind. And Aldric — Aldric, who had stood at the Queen's left hand two nights past with another man's eyes, polite and distant and *empty*, watching Tamsin as one watches a stranger who has taken too long at the market stall — Aldric needed him alive and useful and close.

So he let them dress him. He let them lead him down corridors of black ice where the torches burned blue and cold, where the walls were thick with frozen faces — souls pressed into the stone like flowers pressed into books, their mouths open on words no one would ever hear again. He had stopped looking at them after the first day. He had not stopped hearing them. At night, when the mountain breathed its long slow breath of winter, they whispered through the ice like wind through a keyhole, and every whisper was a name someone above ground still spoke to an empty chair.

The great hall opened before him like the inside of a bell.

It was lit by ten thousand candles, and every candle was a soul. He understood that now, walking the length of it on stone so polished it showed his reflection walking beneath him, upside down, a second Tamsin descending into a second hall. Some of the candles sat in silver sconces along the walls, tall and steady. Some floated, small flames adrift in the air like fireflies caught mid-season. Some were short, guttering, nearly done — and beside each of those, a courtier sat very still, very pale, listening to themselves burn.

At the far end rose the dais, and upon the dais the Winter Queen, and beside her throne —

Aldric.

They had given him a cartographer's sash, blue and silver, and set him at a table drawn with maps of countries that had not existed for a hundred years. His hands moved over them, precise and lovely and unburdened. He did not look up when Tamsin entered. That was mercy, probably. It did not feel like mercy. It felt like the worst dish Tamsin had ever been served, and he had been served nightshade in a honey-cake once, and arsenic folded into a bride's wedding soup, and all of those he had swallowed with a steady hand because that was the work.

This he could not swallow. This sat in him whole.

"Kneel," said the chamberlain, a tall wight whose voice sounded like snow sliding off a roof.

Tamsin knelt. The stone was warm. It had been warm since the day they came down, and the warmth was worse than cold had ever been, because cold was honest. Warmth underground, under winter, meant something was being fed.

"Taster." The Queen's voice filled the hall the way frost fills a window: slowly, beautifully, from every edge inward. She was beautiful the way the archive's oldest maps were beautiful — accurate about things that should have stayed unknown. "Tonight you will serve me as you served your king. Dish by dish. You will tell me what is in them."

"And if I find nothing?" Tamsin kept his eyes lowered. Twelve years of practice. You never looked a sovereign full in the face while your mouth was near their food; it read as accusation.

"Then you will kneel there until you do." A pause, delicate as a hairline crack. "My court has grown... inventive. I wish to know how inventive."

They brought the table to him.

That was the second obscenity. In Greyhollow, the taster went to the table; here, the table came to him, borne by four wights, laden with the Queen's supper, and set down at his knees so that the whole court could watch him work. And the court *did* watch. Hundreds of faces turned toward him, candlelit, hungry in ways that had nothing to do with food. He recognized some of them now — he had been three days among the dead-hearted and already he catalogued faces the way Aldric catalogued coastlines. The lady with the moth-wing collar. The old lord whose beard was rime. The young knight who wept without tears whenever the choir sang.

All of them bought. All of them burning.

The first dish was soup, white as bone broth, smelling faintly of sweetgrass. Tamsin took the spoon. Steadied his breath. Did what he had done four thousand times before.

Here is what tasting is, and no one who has not done it knows: it is not eating. It is reading. The tongue is a page and every ingredient leaves its script — fat writes one line, salt another, and poison writes in a hand all its own, a cramped and bitter signature that Tamsin had learned to recognize the way a mother learns her child's cry in a room full of crying children. Bitter almond. Hemlock's green rot. The mineral tang of arsenic, like licking a coin. The sweet-then-numbing lie of belladonna. He had a library of deaths in his mouth and he opened it now, spoonful by spoonful, and read.

Soup: clean. Sweetgrass, cream, a salt harvested — he suspected — from tears. Nothing.

Fish course: river trout, pale as the mountain's memory of rivers. Clean.

Bread: black bread, dense, warm. Clean, though the grain tasted old, older than any harvest, grown somewhere the sun had not reached in living years.

He announced each verdict aloud, because the Queen required it, and his voice held level, and somewhere in the middle of the fourth dish he realized that this — the steadiness, the ritual, the terrible competence — was the closest thing to home he had felt since the gates closed behind him. That was its own kind of horror. They had taken everything else and left him his skill, and his skill fit here, in this hall of the stolen, as neatly as a key fits the lock it was cut for.

The seventh dish was a roast, dark and glistening, and it was poisoned.

Not crudely. Not with anything from his library of deaths. This was subtler — a bitterness so fine it hid beneath the fat like a knife beneath a coat, and underneath the bitterness something stranger, something that tasted the way the air tastes before a storm, charged and wrong. Tamsin chewed, held it on his tongue, and did not swallow.

His heart did not speed. Twelve years had taught it better. But his mind went very quiet and very fast, the way it went in the half-second before he named a poison aloud and changed the fate of everyone at the table.

Because he knew this taste. Not the substance — the *intention*. He had tasted intention before, in Greyhollow, in the year the grain merchants' guild decided the king's new tariffs were theft and hired a cook with a grudge and a knowledge of herbs. Poison made in anger has a flavor. Poison made in grief has another. And this — this was poison made in despair, made by hands that had nothing left to lose, seasoned with a desperation he knew intimately because he had been seasoning his own charms with it for twelve years.

Someone at this table wanted the Queen dead. Someone who had already died, in every way that mattered.

"The roast," Tamsin said, and laid down the fork, and turned his palm upward in the guild-sign for *halt*, a gesture so old his body made it without asking his permission. "Is death."

The hall went silent in the way only the dead can be silent — total, attentive, without even breath to break it.

The Queen leaned forward on her throne of ice. "Name it."

"I cannot name it." Honesty was survival; he had learned that in his first week as a boy-taster, when he lied about a dish and a chamberlain spent three days dying and Tamsin learned that lies in this trade kill the innocent faster than the guilty. "It is no herb I know, no mineral. But it is poison, and it is strong, and it was not meant for you alone."

"Explain."

"The dose is in the whole roast. Not the carving — the *roasting*. Whatever killed it was worked into the fat, into the basting, into every part. If I am meant to taste for you alone, then this poison wastes itself on me. No one poisons a single taster with a whole roast." He heard his own voice go flat and professional, the voice he used in the scullery when he told a client *this charm will cost you more than coin*. "This dish was meant for the entire court. Every plate carved from it. Everyone."

Silence. Then, from somewhere among the hundreds of seated figures, a sound — small, involuntary, almost like laughter, almost like sobbing. Tamsin did not look toward it. Looking would be accusation, and accusation was a weapon you saved.

The Queen rose. Frost bloomed outward from her feet across the warm stone, racing along the floor in feathering white veins, and the temperature of the hall dropped so fast that the nearest candles guttered — and the souls inside them cried out, faintly, in voices like paper tearing.

"Which of you," she said, softly, which was infinitely worse than loudly, "has seasoned my supper?"

No one answered. Of course no one answered. But Tamsin watched the court the way he watched the king's table — not at the faces but at the *hands*, and the hands told him everything. The old lord with the rime-white beard had gone rigid. The lady with the moth-wing collar had tucked both hands beneath her shawl. And the young knight who wept without tears — the knight was looking not at the Queen, not at the roast, but at *Tamsin*, with an expression he had seen exactly once before, twelve years ago, on the face of a friend in the guild infirmary who knew the draught in his cup was the last kindness anyone could offer him.

Recognition. Gratitude. *Finally.*

*Oh,* thought Tamsin, and the understanding arrived whole, the way the worst understandings always do. *Oh, you poor bastards. You're not trying to murder her. You're trying to end the winter. Even if it unmakes you.*

Because that was the arithmetic of this place, wasn't it? He had seen it in the vaults, in the bazaar, in the ten thousand candles. The Undercourt's endless winter was fed by bought souls, and the bought souls *were* the winter — their years, their warmth, their summers poured into the Queen's long night. Every soul that burned out made the frost a little deeper. Every candle that guttered gave the mountain another decade of cold. The courtiers were not prisoners of the winter.

They were its fuel. And fuel, eventually, learns what it is for.

The knight wanted the fire to go out. All of them did, maybe — or enough of them, enough to slip hemlock-that-was-not-hemlock into the roasting fat and trust that whatever came after the Queen could not be colder than what was.

And they had used a taster's own craft against her. Worked the poison into the whole, so that no single tasting could catch it — except that they had not counted on a taster from above, a taster who read intention as well as substance, a taster who had spent twelve years learning that the difference between medicine and murder is only dosage and desire.

"Taster." The Queen had descended the dais. She stood over him now, and the cold coming off her was absolute, a cold with no memory of summer anywhere in it. "You will find who did this."

"Will I?" Careful. Careful. He kept his eyes on the roast, on the steam rising from the carved-open flesh. "I can find *what* did it. The *who* — that requires your court to speak. And your court, Majesty, does not strike me as a place where people speak freely."

Something moved behind her face. Not anger. Something older and wearier, glimpsed for less than a heartbeat — and gone.

"You are either very brave," she said, "or very foolish, to negotiate with me."

"In Greyhollow they say the same of hedge-witches." He lifted his head at last and met her eyes, and it cost him more than any poison ever had. "Majesty. I have tasted for a king for twelve years. I know what it sounds like when a household wants its master dead. Your court doesn't want you dead." He paused, and chose the truth, because the truth was the sharpest blade he had left. "Your court wants to *stop*. There is a difference, and I think — I think somewhere under all that frost, you know it."

The hall's silence changed texture. The candles flamed higher, all together, all at once, as if a thousand souls had leaned in — and Tamsin felt it, physically, a pressure against his skin like standing too close to an open oven. The whispers in the ice walls rose. For the first time he could pick out words.

*Tell him.* / *The taster knows.* / *We put it in the fat, we put it in the fat, we put it—*

"Quiet," said the Queen, to the room, to the candles, to the century.

But they did not quiet, quite. And the knight — the young knight with the dry weeping eyes — rose from his seat, slowly, the way a man rises when he has decided the moment for hiding is over, and began to walk toward the dais with his empty hands open at his sides.

Two wights moved to intercept him. The Queen raised one finger, and they stopped.

"Majesty," the knight said, and his voice was rust and riverbed, a voice long unused to being its own. "Let him taste us. Let him taste all of us. Let him tell you what he finds."

The Queen looked at the knight. Then at Tamsin. And Tamsin saw — with the same clarity with which he read poison, the clarity that had kept him alive through twelve years of other people's attempts at regicide — that she was afraid.

Not of the knight. Not of the coup. Of *tasting*. Of being read, the way she had just been read, by a mortal with a spoon and a steady hand.

"Very well," she said at last, and the word fell into the hall like a stone into a well. "Begin with him. Taste him, taster. Tell me what my knight carries."

The knight knelt before him, and offered his wrist, and Tamsin took it — because refusing was a wall to be buried behind, and because Aldric was watching now, Aldric's borrowed eyes fixed on him from the map-table with an expression Tamsin could not read and could not stop trying to.

Under the knight's skin, beneath the cold, Tamsin found it at once: the same storm-before-lightning taste as the roast, but vaster, layered through the man's whole body like ink through water. And beneath it, threaded through it, something else — a residue, a ghost of flavor that stopped Tamsin's breath.

Summer. Actual summer. Hay and hot stone and river-water, the taste of a soul that had once been warm, preserved in the dead heart of this frozen man like a flower pressed in a book of ice.

They were all of them like this. He understood it suddenly, kneeling in the warm dark with a dead knight's pulse under his fingers. Every courtier in this hall was a jar of summer, hoarded, sealed, slowly spending itself to feed a winter that would never end. And the poison in the roast had not been malice.

It had been mercy. The only mercy left to buy.

"Well?" said the Queen.

Tamsin released the knight's wrist. Rose. Faced the throne and the ten thousand candles and the woman who had been buying summers for longer than Greyhollow had had a name — and made, in the space of one heartbeat, the kind of decision that a taster is built for: quick, total, and impossible to take back.

"There is no poison in this man," Tamsin said clearly, so that every candle in the hall could hear. "There is a cure. And I know where to find more of it — if your Majesty is wise enough to want one."

The nearest candle flared white.

And across the hall, at the map-table, Aldric Vane stood up — slowly, one hand pressed to his chest, as if something inside him had just remembered its own name.
