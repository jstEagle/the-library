## 17. Candles in the Dark

*Tamsin*

The vault door was iron, and the iron was singing.

Tamsin had heard it since they descended past the third landing of the Undercourt's spiral stair—a thin, keening note like a wet finger circling a glass rim, felt more in the teeth than the ears. Aldric walked beside him with a stranger's careful distance, map-case slung across his back out of habit, hands cold as river stone. He did not know why he had come. He had been told the mission, and the mission was enough. That was the cruelty of it: the man beside him would follow Tamsin into the dark for reasons written on a page, while every reason that lived in his own heart sat burning somewhere below them, in a jar, on a shelf.

The candle-flames had followed them.

They did not walk as flames walk. They drifted along the walls of the stairwell like moths made of memory, guttering when Tamsin moved too fast, steadying when he slowed. He had begun to feel them the way one feels a crowd at one's back—not hostile, but *attentive*. Hundreds of attentions. Thousands. The whole long winter of the court, pressed close to the walls of its own prison, watching the taster come.

"You hear that?" Aldric said quietly.

"The singing."

"It's coming from the door." Aldric pressed his palm flat against the iron. His brow furrowed—the particular furrow Tamsin knew from archive work, the scholar's confusion meeting something older than scholarship. "It's not a song. It's... names. Someone is reciting names behind this door. Or—" he paused, and his voice dropped, "—or the door is made of names."

Tamsin set his hand beside Aldric's. Through the metal came the whispering he had been hearing all night, resolved now into words: *Maren. Maren Ostell. Maren Ostell, weaver's daughter, Greyhollow.* Then another name. Then another. Each voice small. Each voice patient. Each voice saying its name over and over the way a man lost in snow says his own name to remember he exists.

"They're remembering themselves," Tamsin said. "It's all they have left."

He thought of Mother Hessel's charm against his sternum, warm as a held bird. He thought of twelve years of swallowing death by the spoonful so that other men could eat safely. He had spent his whole life believing that survival was a thing you built alone, plate by plate, poison by poison, wall by silent wall. But here were the souls the Undercourt had bought, and they had survived a century of being *spent*—burned down to warm a winter that never ended—and what they had built in that darkness was not a wall. It was a chorus.

"Hello," Tamsin said to the door.

The whispering stopped.

Every flame in the stairwell turned toward him at once—not physically, but the way a room turns when someone speaks an unexpected word. The silence had weight. It pressed against his eardrums like deep water.

"I can't promise much," he said. His voice sounded absurdly human in that vast cold dark, scraped raw, ordinary. "I'm a food-taster. I've never broken anything bigger than a fast. But there's a prophecy that says I'm either the breaker or the bride, and I have decided—" and here his hand found the charm at his chest, Hessel's last magic, her whole remaining self woven into thread and wax, "—I have decided to be the breaker. If you'll lend me the strength. When you're free, you can go wherever souls go. I won't ask you to stay. I won't ask you for anything after tonight. Just—lend me your light until I reach the vault."

The silence stretched so long that Aldric shifted beside him, uneasy.

Then the nearest flame—the smallest, no bigger than a birthday wish—drifted forward and touched Tamsin's cheek.

Warmth went through him like summer going through a window left open too long. Not heat exactly. *Season*. He tasted cut grass and rain on hot slate and his mother's kitchen, a smell he had not known he remembered. Other flames followed, dozens, hundreds, pressing close without burning, pouring themselves into him the way light pours into glass. His veins felt full of July. His breath came out golden.

Aldric stumbled back, staring. "You're—you're *glowing*."

"I'm borrowing," Tamsin said, and found he was smiling, and found the smile hurt. "Hold my hand. This is going to be bright."

* * *

*Aldric*

Aldric Vane had mapped coastlines in storms. He had charted the Glasswater reefs by dead reckoning and starlight, drawn passes no surveyor would enter, and he had always trusted his hands: steady, precise, incapable of panic. Now those hands were wrapped around the fingers of a man who burned like a lantern, and they were shaking, and Aldric could not remember why that frightened him more than the dark did—only that it did, only that some part of him screamed at the loss of it even though he had nothing to lose.

*Nothing to lose,* said the screaming part. *Then why does it scream?*

"Tamsin." He said the name because it was the only word he was sure of. "Tamsin, tell me again what I am to you."

Ahead of him the taster's shoulders stiffened. For three steps he said nothing. The borrowed light poured off him and painted the stairwell walls with moving shadows, and in that shifting gold Aldric watched the man he was following decide how much truth he could carry.

"You're the reason I came down here," Tamsin said at last, not turning. "That's what you are. Everything else is detail."

"And before?"

"Before doesn't matter."

"That's a lie," Aldric said. "Cartographers know lies. A false line on a map is still a line. It tells you where the truth runs by running alongside it."

Tamsin stopped walking.

For a moment the flames dimmed, as if they too were holding their breath, and Aldric saw the taster's face in profile: exhausted, terrified, lit from within by other people's summers. "Before," Tamsin said carefully, "you used to steal pastry with me in the market on Thursdays. You drew maps on napkins and gave them away like flowers. You laughed at your own jokes before you finished telling them." A pause. The light flickered. "And you loved me. I think. You were going to say it properly, and then the snow started falling, and then everything happened too fast, and now you don't remember, and I am going to get it back for you if it costs me every immunity I ever bought. That's the before. That's the map. Don't ask me again—I'll tell you wrong on purpose next time, because it hurts less."

The flames roared back up around him, brighter, furious, tender.

Aldric's throat ached. "I believe you," he said, which was all he had, and it was not nothing—it was the first true thing he had given anyone since the river.

They came down the last flight together, hand in hand, a lantern and a map with no territory, toward the vault where Aldric Vane kept himself.

* * *

*Tamsin*

The vault was smaller than the singing had promised. That was the horror of it: not a cathedral but a cellar, low-ceilinged and dry, lined floor to ceiling with shelves, and on the shelves, ten thousand candles in jars of frost-glass, each one burning with a flame that had once been breakfast arguments and wedding dances and the specific weight of a child asleep on a shoulder. Labels in silver script named their owners. Some labels were very old. Some were very new—one jar near the door still bore water stains, and Tamsin recognized the ferryman's ledger-hand, and beside it, in fresh ink:

**VANE, ALDRIC — cartographer, Greyhollow. Balance outstanding.**

The candle inside was half gone.

Tamsin lifted it from the shelf with both hands, the way one lifts a sleeping infant, and the flame bent toward him through the glass like grass bending toward sun. Up close he could see it wasn't fire at all. It was weather. Small warm winds moved inside the jar; he caught the scent of charcoal and cedar shavings, of pastry sugar, of ink. Of Aldric, rendered into light.

Behind him, Aldric stood in the doorway and looked at his own soul with the mild interest of a stranger examining someone else's luggage. "Is that mine?"

"Yes."

"It's very small," Aldric said, apologetic.

"It's half of what it was," Tamsin said. "They've been spending you. Every snowflake that fell out of season, every name you forgot—that was them, dipping their wick into you." His voice cracked on the last words and he let it. There was no one left to perform calmness for. "But the rest is here. And I'm taking it."

He reached for the lid.

The cold hit him like a wave hitting a seawall. It came up through the floor and down through the ceiling and out of the jars themselves—ten thousand flames flinching away from him at once as the Queen's will arrived in her absence, filling the vault the way pressure fills a drowned bell. Frost raced across the shelving. The label on Aldric's jar grew new letters beneath the old ones, silver script crawling like ice on a pond:

**Property of the Winter Court. Removal constitutes theft. Theft constitutes forfeit.**

"Forfeit of what?" Tamsin asked the empty air, and his borrowed summer answered for him, blazing up around his fists. "Oh," he said. "Of me. Right."

He wrenched the lid off the jar.

* * *

*Aldric*

Later—much later, in a life he would have to be re-taught—Aldric would try to describe what happened in the vault, and he would fail, because cartography has no symbol for it. What he remembered was this:

The flame rose out of its jar like a bird released from a cage it had forgotten hating. It hung in the air between the shelves, trembling, neither flying nor falling. And Tamsin Blackwood—who had spent twelve years making himself unkillable, who had armored himself in small poisons the way other men arm themselves in iron—took off his shirt, pressed the flame to his own bare chest, and held it there.

"Light me," he said.

The flame entered him.

Aldric heard himself shout—heard his own voice crack apart on a name he did not consciously know but his mouth remembered anyway, *Tamsin, TAMSIN*—and lunged forward, and was stopped, not by force but by warmth, a wall of gentle summer standing him in the doorway like a courteous guard. Through it he watched the taster burn.

It did not look like dying. It looked like dawn happening inside a man. Light moved under Tamsin's skin the way light moves under ice when the thaw comes, tracing rivers and tributaries, and where it passed, Aldric saw scars fade—old guild-marks, old trial-scars, the pale lattice of twelve years' swallowed deaths—all of it dissolving into gold, all of it *spent*, poured out willingly the way he had once refused to pour anything at all.

The frost on the shelves shrieked.

New letters fought across the jar's surface—**FORFEIT**, **THIEF**, **RECLAIMED**—and each word froze, cracked, and slid off the glass like dead leaves. Somewhere above them, far up through the mountain's bones, the Winter Queen felt her property walk out of the vault inside a mortal chest, and the whole mountain groaned at the sound of her rising.

The wall of warmth lowered itself. Aldric stumbled through it and caught Tamsin as he swayed, and the man in his arms weighed almost nothing, and he was glowing, and he was laughing—actually laughing, a ragged disbelieving sound.

"What did you do?" Aldric demanded.

"Gave myself a job." Tamsin's grin was white in his shining face. "Your soul can't be reclaimed while it's lit. So I lit it. Against my own heart. Congratulations, cartographer—you're currently living inside a lantern, and the lantern is me." He pressed Aldric's hand flat against his sternum, and beneath the skin, impossibly, unmistakably, something beat in time with the candleflame. Warmth spread up Aldric's fingers, into his wrist, and for one dizzy moment he smelled pastry sugar and cedar shavings and ink, and grief arrived in him for a home he could not find.

"Can you carry a map?" Tamsin asked.

"I *am* a map."

"Good." Above them, down the stairwell, the cold began to descend like an avalanche learning patience. Around them in the vault, ten thousand flames rose off their shelves at once, drifting toward the door, toward the two of them, forming a river of small lights that wanted, with one borrowed will, to go *up*. "Because I promised these people spring," Tamsin said. "And I intend to deliver it by morning."

* * *

*Tamsin*

They climbed with the river of souls flowing around them, and the mountain fought them the whole way.

Doors slammed where no doors had been. Stairs lengthened underfoot, each step arriving a fraction later than the last, so that climbing felt like wading through syrup. The cold came down in waves, and each wave broke against Tamsin's borrowed light and cost him something—he could feel it now, the summer draining out of him flame by flame as the souls spent themselves shielding him, spending themselves gladly, the way he had spent his immunities for strangers' suppers. It was, he thought wildly, the fairest trade he had ever been part of.

Halfway up the great stair, the smallest flame—Maren, the weaver's daughter, he knew her voice now among the chorus—pressed close to his ear.

*She's coming,* the little light whispered. *The bride-bride. She's bringing the long night with her.*

"Let her come," Tamsin said.

His chest burned steadily. Inside it, Aldric's soul had settled against his heartbeat like a bird against a warm window, and he could feel it dreaming—could feel, faintly, absurdly, images that were not his: a coastline at dawn, a napkin-map folded like a flower, a Thursday market stall, a laugh finishing its own joke. He carried the man he loved inside his own ribs, and the carrying hurt, and he would not have put it down for a kingdom, or a crown, or a thousand years of safe tasting.

At the top of the stair, the door to the Queen's hall stood open, spilling moonless silver light onto the steps.

Beyond it, seated on a throne of black ice, the Winter Queen waited. She was beautiful the way glaciers are beautiful, and she was looking directly at Tamsin's chest—at the light burning there—with an expression he would spend the rest of his mortal life trying to forget.

Not rage. Recognition.

"Well, little brother," she said, in a voice like the first frost on a grave. "You're wearing his soul where I wore mine. Come in. Let us discuss the fitting."

Behind Tamsin, ten thousand flames gathered at his back like a congregation, and Aldric's hand found his in the dark, and together—for whatever together was worth, with one of them a lantern and one of them a map—they stepped into the Queen's long night.
