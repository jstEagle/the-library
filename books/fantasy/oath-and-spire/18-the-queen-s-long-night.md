## 18. The Queen's Long Night

The throne room of the Undercourt was not a room at all. It was the inside of a held breath.

Tamsin walked toward the Queen across a floor of black ice so clear he could see down through it—down and down, past strata of frozen light, past shapes that might have been furniture or bones or the roots of the spire above, until the dark simply gave up pretending to be anything but dark. His boots made no sound. Nothing here made sound unless it wanted to. Even his own heartbeat seemed to have been asked politely to wait outside.

Aldric walked beside him. That was the wound of it, the wound he had to fold away into some cold back room of himself if he meant to survive the next hour: Aldric walked beside him like a stranger assigned the same errand. His eyes went to the walls, the vaulted frost, the chandeliers of hanging icicles that dripped upward instead of down—and not once, not once, to Tamsin's face.

*He remembers the mission,* Tamsin told himself for the hundredth time since the market. *Not the man.* He had paid the memory at the river to keep his soul-candle lit, and Tamsin had watched him do it without knowing what coin he handed over, and now the man who laughed over stolen pastries, who drew coastlines with ink-stained fingers, who said *stay for breakfast* in a voice like an open door—that man was somewhere inside this walking stranger, folded up small, waiting.

*I'll buy you back,* Tamsin thought. *I've spent twelve years learning what things cost.*

Ahead, the Queen waited on a throne grown from the ice itself, as if the room had decided, long ago, what shape its mistress should take and simply obliged. She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful: precise, patient, and utterly uninterested in whether you survived admiring it. Her gown moved when she did not, hemmed in slow snowfall. Her crown was a ring of black iron furred with frost—the only iron in all this ice, and Tamsin understood suddenly that it was not decoration. It was a tether. Something bound her here, something old, and the crown was the knot.

Behind them, at a respectful distance, the court had gathered. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Nobles of the Undercourt in their glacial finery, faces like the moon seen through different windows—and among them, Tamsin realized with a lurch, others. Faces too warm. Faces too tired. Bought souls wearing their borrowed bodies badly, standing very still, the way people stand when they have learned that stillness is the price of staying.

And everywhere, everywhere, the candles. They lined the walls in their thousands, each one a soul, each flame burning at its own pace, and the light they cast was not gold but the pale silver of memory, and the whole vast hall breathed by their light, and the whole vast hall was *cold*, and Tamsin finally understood where the endless winter came from. It was not weather. It was appetite. Every candle burned and gave off warmth, and the court drank the warmth and stayed cold anyway, because nothing bought can ever be warm enough.

"Come closer," the Queen said. Her voice was the sound of a lake freezing—one long crystalline crack, beautiful and final. "Taster."

"You know what I am," Tamsin said. He stopped ten paces from the throne. Close enough to be heard. Far enough, he hoped, to think.

"I know what you are better than you do." She rose. The snowfall of her gown settled around her like a decision being made slowly. "I knew it the moment your name crossed my threshold. I have been waiting a long time for another taster to walk down my mountain. A century, give or take a hard winter." She smiled, and the smile did not reach anything. "You are late."

"A century," Tamsin repeated. His mouth had gone dry, which was absurd, down here, in all this water. "The prophecy is a century old."

"The prophecy is a *rota*," she said. "A duty roster. Did you think it was fortune-telling? We do not guess, taster. We schedule." She descended the last step of the dais and stood before him, close enough that the cold coming off her raised the fine hairs on his arms through his coat. "Every hundred years, the gates soften. Every hundred years, someone comes down who has swallowed death daily and lived. And every hundred years, I make the same offer, and every hundred years, someone accepts."

Beside him, Aldric shifted. "Then the book in the archive—"

"Was written by the last one," the Queen said, without looking at him. "Before she forgot how to hold a pen. Before she forgot there had ever been hands." Now she looked at Aldric, and Tamsin's whole body went tight as a drawn wire. "Your cartographer has lovely handwriting, by the way. So did she."

"What are you saying?" Tamsin asked, though the cold was already telling him. The cold had been telling him since the throne room doors opened. Some part of him had known since Mother Hessel first said *the bride*, since the word *bride* sat wrong in his ear like a key that almost fit.

The Queen turned back to him. And for just a moment—less than a moment, a flicker, a candle guttering—the beauty cracked, and underneath it he saw a face that was tired. Not ancient. *Tired.* The particular tiredness of someone who has been awake far past any night that should have ended.

"I am saying," she said quietly, "that I know exactly what I am offering you, because I took it myself. I was a taster. In a city with a different name, under a king with a different face, a hundred years and more ago. I swallowed death daily and learned its flavors the way you learn a lover's moods. I came down this mountain for someone I loved, because the book said I could break the bargain or wed it, and I stood where you are standing, and I looked at the winter, and I chose to live." Her voice did not waver. That was the worst of it. It was perfectly, professionally steady, the voice of a woman who had given this speech so many times it had worn smooth as river stone. "I chose the tasting. Immunity to poison, immunity to age, immunity to time itself. And my beloved went home alone, and grew old, and died, and I stood at this window—" she gestured, vaguely, at the black ice, at nothing— "and watched the winters, and after a while I could not remember why I had thought living was the prize."

The hall was silent. Even the candles seemed to lean in.

"They call me Queen," she went on. "I did not ask to be. The court needs a center, and a taster's palate is a fine thing to build a court around—I can taste intention, now. I can taste a lie three rooms away. I taste the souls as they burn; I know which of my subjects are thinking of leaving. It is a very useful gift and I would give it back in a heartbeat if anyone were fool enough to take it." She tilted her head, and the frost on her iron crown caught the candlelight. "So. The offer stands, taster, as it has always stood. Serve the court. Taste for us until the mountains wear down. Your cartographer walks free with his soul restored, his memories intact, his little mortal life returned to him—and you will never grow old enough to lose him. You will watch him die from a great height, safely, the way I did. Or—"

"Or I refuse," Tamsin said, "and you keep him, and the winter seals the gates, and the world above forgets spring."

"Or that," the Queen agreed pleasantly. "Though I confess I hope you won't. It gets very quiet down here between centuries."

* * *

Tamsin had faced poisoned dishes with more steadiness than he felt now. But he had learned something in twelve years at the royal table, something no guild trial had taught him directly: that fear and poison have the same architecture. Both arrive dressed as something else. Both must be identified before they are swallowed. And both, once named, lose half their power.

He made himself look at her—not the beauty, not the crown, but the tiredness underneath—and he named what he saw.

"You're not offering me a choice," he said. "You're offering me a loop."

Her expression did not change. But the snowfall around her hem slowed, very slightly, the way breath slows when someone is listening harder than they want to admit.

"A century ago you stood here and chose survival," Tamsin said. "And survival made you the thing that offers survival. And whoever comes next will stand here, and choose, and become the next offer, and the next, forever. The prophecy doesn't predict a bride. It *manufactures* one. You're not the Queen of this place. You're its most successful dish." He paused. "They fed you to the court, and the court liked you, and now you feed it to everyone else."

Something moved behind her eyes. Frost does not flinch—but the light behind frost can shift.

"You presume a great deal," she said, "for a man whose heart is currently trying to exit through his ribs. I can taste it from here. Fear, and under it—" her nostrils flared faintly, delicately, the connoisseur's gesture— "grief. Old grief, layered like lacquer. Twelve coats of it, at least. You have been swallowing loss for years, taster. One more will hardly notice."

"That's the second time someone's offered to add to my collection," Tamsin said. "The first person who tried is dead, and I helped put her there, and I've regretted it every day since. So let me tell you what I've learned about surviving." He took a breath. Here it was. The whole hand, played at once. "Survival isn't the prize. I used to think it was. I built my whole life on it—every poison, every charm, every immunity, hoarded like a miser. And then a mapmaker laughed at one of my jokes and I discovered I'd been keeping myself alive for absolutely no one." He glanced sideways at Aldric—at the stranger wearing Aldric's face—and made himself look back at the Queen. "You've been alive a hundred years. Tell me honestly. What did you save?"

For a long moment the Queen said nothing. The candles burned. Somewhere high above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone and ice, something groaned—a sound like a ship in heavy weather, like a mountain remembering it was once soft.

"Do not mistake me for a woman who can be reached," she said at last. "I was reached a century ago. It did not take."

"No," Tamsin said. "It didn't. Because they didn't reach *you*. They reached what was left after you'd traded everything away." And then he reached into his coat.

The court stirred. Along the walls, the bought souls leaned forward, and the candles flickered in a wind that had no source, and Tamsin felt the attention of hundreds of trapped things settle on him like snowfall. In his hand was the charm Mother Hessel had woven from the last of her magic—knots of red thread and river-clay and a sliver of birch bark, ugly as a beggar's knuckle, warm as a sleeping bird. She had pressed it into his palm at the pass and told him: *it holds one true thing. I didn't look. I couldn't bear to spend it on anything small.*

She had stayed behind to hold the hunters. Tamsin did not let himself think about what that had cost. Not yet. There would be a grave above, someday, and a candle on it. First: this.

"This is hedge-magic," he said. "Outlawed in Greyhollow for longer than your winter. Ugly, small, desperate work—the kind of magic that exists because someone poor needed it to. It holds a memory. Not mine. Hers." He held it out on his open palm, between himself and the Queen. "But it isn't hers anymore. She gave it to me to spend, and I'm spending it here."

The Queen looked at the charm the way a starving woman looks at bread she has sworn off.

"What could a hedge-witch possibly hold," she said, "that would interest me?"

"Your name," Tamsin said.

The word dropped into the silence like a stone into a well, and the well went on echoing long after it should have stopped.

"Not your title. Not the name the court gave you when it crowned you with iron. Your *human* name. The one you had before you ever tasted poison. The one your mother called you across a kitchen. The one you traded, or buried, or lost somewhere between the door of this mountain and that throne." His voice was steady. He was surprised by that. "Hessel's magic remembers what the court makes people forget. She never knew what it carried. But I've been thinking about it since the pass, and I think—I think the wheel turns because the bride forgets she was ever a maker. You can't break a cycle you don't remember starting. So here it is. Not a bargain. Not a trade. I'm not buying anything and I'm not selling anything." He stepped closer, one pace, two, until the cold coming off her skin met the warmth rising off the charm, and where they met the air shimmered like heat over summer roadstones. "I'm giving it back."

"You are a fool," the Queen whispered.

"Probably," Tamsin agreed. "I've been reliably informed. By kings, by witches, by the man I love, who currently doesn't remember loving me, so his opinion may be biased." He almost smiled. "Take it or leave it. But know this first: I'm not doing it for you. I'm doing it because somewhere under that crown is a taster who once stood exactly where I'm standing, and somebody should have done this for her a hundred years ago. Consider it—" and here he did smile, thin and crooked, the scullery smile, the charm-seller's smile— "a professional courtesy. Between colleagues."

The Queen stared at the charm.

And the hall changed. It was subtle at first—the candles steadying, their flames standing up straighter, as if called to attention. Then the bought souls along the walls began, one by one, to weep. Silently, without moving, tears tracking down faces that had forgotten the mechanics of grief, and Tamsin understood: they could feel it too. Whatever slept in Hessel's knots of thread, whatever small desperate human thing she had woven with her last strength, it was calling roll. It was reading names.

The Queen raised her hand. Stopped. Raised it again.

"If this is a trick," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle like the freezing lake it had always resembled, "if this is some taster's sleight, some poison of the mind—"

"Hessel didn't deal in tricks," Tamsin said gently. "Only in last resorts."

The Queen closed her fingers around the charm.

For a heartbeat, nothing. The iron crown glittered. The candles burned. Aldric stood beside him, breathing, present, absent, and Tamsin ached so badly he thought the ache might actually kill him, which would have been, he reflected, a fitting end for a food-taster: slain at last by the one flavor he'd never learned to spit out.

Then the Queen opened her hand, and the charm was gone—spent, unraveled, its single true thing delivered—and she made a sound.

It was a small sound. A human sound. The kind of sound a woman makes in a kitchen, over a burnt loaf, at nothing, at everything. And with it the frost began to fail. Not explosively—not yet—but the way ice fails on a river in the first week of thaw: quietly, structurally, everywhere at once. The snowfall of her gown fell faster. The black-ice floor fogged with a breath of warmth rising from below, from deep below, from places that had been waiting a century for permission.

"*Maren*," the Queen said.

Just that. Just the name. And she said it the way you say a word in a language you dreamed you'd forgotten, tasting it, testing it against a hundred years of titles, and her knees buckled, and Tamsin caught her—caught the Winter Queen of the Undercourt by the elbows, her weight shockingly slight, shockingly human, the weight of a tired woman at the end of a very long shift.

"It's mine," she said. Wonderingly. "It was mine the whole time."

"It was always yours," Tamsin said. "That's how names work. That's how *all* of it works. That's what they never told you, down here—you can't own a soul, Maren. You can only delay the return."

Around them, the court was coming apart—or coming together; from inside, Tamsin suspected, it would be impossible to tell the difference. The noble faces were flickering now, frost and flesh alternating like a signal fire. The candles along the walls had begun to burn fast and bright, flames leaping, and the warmth they released rolled through the hall in visible waves, and the bought souls were weeping openly, laughing, calling out names of their own—names, names, a hundred years of names arriving all at once like letters delayed in a dead season's post.

Above them, the mountain groaned again, louder, and this time it did not stop groaning. Dust sifted from the vaulted dark. Somewhere far overhead, Tamsin knew, the spire was beginning to move—the great frozen engine of the Undercourt, its fuel no longer willing, its machinery meeting its first honest spring.

"Maren." He kept his voice low, steady, the voice he'd used on frightened scullery boys and dying friends. "Listen to me. This place is coming down, and everything in it is coming up. Including a few thousand souls that need carrying home—including one that belongs to a cartographer standing right behind you. I can't carry them alone. But you spent a century learning to taste intention." He met her eyes—human eyes now, brown, ordinary, exhausted, magnificent. "So here's my intention, plain as an open ledger: help me break it the rest of the way. All of it. Every candle out of the dark. Every soul sent home. The whole rotten bargain, unwritten."

Maren, who had been a taster, who had been a bride, who had been a queen against her will for a hundred frozen years, looked up at the failing ceiling of her prison and began, very quietly, to laugh.

"You're going to need a bigger table," she said.

Far above, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, the spire began to fall.
