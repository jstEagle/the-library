## 19. Oath and Spire

The name came out of her like a tooth.

Tamsin felt the moment it broke loose — a shudder through the floor of the Undercourt, through the black stone that had been warm as flesh since they'd descended, through the thousand candles guttering in their niches. The Winter Queen stood at the center of her hall with Hessel's charm open in her cupped hands, and what rose from it was not light but *scent*: woodsmoke, bread, river water in July. A summer she had not touched in four hundred years.

And under all of it, a name.

*Marisol.*

She said it once, testing. She said it twice, weeping. By the third time she was on her knees on ice that was no longer ice, and Tamsin watched frost crawl backward off the walls like a tide going out, watched centuries of winter peel away from stone that remembered being warm.

"No," the Queen said — to him, to the hall, to the girl she had been before some starving court made her taste for them forever. "No, I was careful. I was so careful. I never let them take—"

"You let them take everything except the one thing you hid," Tamsin said. His voice cracked down the middle. He was holding Aldric's candle against his chest with both hands, and its flame burned through his shirt, through skin, and he did not flinch because he had spent twelve years learning not to flinch. "You hid your name where no bargain could reach it. That's how you survived. That's also how you became this."

The hall groaned. Above them — impossibly above, through miles of mountain — something vast shifted its weight.

* * *

Aldric heard the mountain crack before he understood what he was hearing.

He had been standing at the edge of the great hall, empty-handed, hollowed out, watching a man he did not know hold a candle that sang with a voice Aldric recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting. He knew the mission. He knew the maps. He knew the way back to Greyhollow and the toll bridge and the pass. He did not know why his chest hurt when the taster looked at him, only that it hurt like a room in his house he had been told never to open.

Then the Queen wept her name, and the whole architecture of the dark began to come apart, and Aldric Vane did the only thing he had ever been good at: he read the ground.

Cracks spidered up the pillars. Meltwater — actual water, bright and cold and *moving* — sheeted down the far wall where frost had reigned since before his grandmother's grandmother. The candles were flaring, all of them, thousands of bought souls burning high and glad, and their light showed him what his feet already knew: the floor was tilting toward the spire's root, and the spire's root was failing.

"The ceiling," he said, and his voice carried the way a map carries — flat, precise, impossible to argue with. "This whole vault is the underside of the peak. When it goes, everything goes. We have minutes."

Tamsin turned to him. There was blood on the man's mouth — the Queen's bargain had required a final tasting, and Tamsin had tasted, and Tamsin had named the poison before it could touch a single soul in the court — and there was candlelight shining *through* him, out of him, as if his ribs were lantern slats.

"Go up," Tamsin said. "There's a stair behind the throne. It follows the meltwater. You know the way; you've always known the way; you drew it before you ever saw it."

"I'm not leaving without—" Aldric stopped. He didn't have the word. He had never had the word. He gestured instead at the candle, at the fire eating through the taster's shirt, at the terrible calm on his face. "What is that? Why do I know your hands?"

"Because you paid for me at the river," Tamsin said gently, "and I'm here to steal you back."

* * *

The Queen did not stop them.

That was the thing Tamsin would remember afterward, in the years when he told this story to no one and everyone: she did not stop them. She knelt in her own thawing hall with water running silver through her white hair, saying a name over and over like a prayer she'd been keeping in a drawer for four centuries, and when Tamsin passed her with the burning candle held to his chest, she looked up at him with eyes that were, for one moment, a serving girl's eyes, a taster's eyes, eyes that had counted every dish and trusted none of them.

"Take the stairs," she said. "The meltwater goes up faster than fear does." And then, quieter, almost lost under the groaning of the mountain: "It doesn't get better, little brother. Being immune. You just get very good at being alone." Her hand found his wrist — cold, then warming, then human-warm — and pressed something into it. A key of black ice, already melting. "For the door at the top. In case spring is slow."

He ran.

* * *

Burning immunities is not like burning anything else.

Tamsin had twelve years of small deaths banked inside him — hemlock and nightshade, arsenic layered on arsenic, the slow tuition of a hundred poisoned dishes, each dose survived and stored in marrow and liver and the secret rooms of the blood. The guild had called it an immunity. Mother Hessel had called it what it was: a hoard. And a hoard, she had told him once, lighting her last charm from her own remaining magic, is only wealth if you're willing to spend it.

He spent it all.

He did it on the stairs, three turns up, when the candle against his chest flickered low — Aldric's soul, guttering, its wax worn thin by whatever the Undercourt had fed it through the long winter — and Tamsin understood that flame needed fuel, and the only fuel he had left was himself.

So he opened the doors of his body the way you open a house to wind.

It went through him like the first swallow of a feast after famine, except backward, except wrong — every poison he had ever survived waking at once and *leaving*, streaming out of him in a bright corrosive torrent that the candle drank like a starving thing. Hemlock first, the taste of his first trial at nine years old, the guildmaster's hand steady on his shoulder. Then arsenic, green and metallic, twelve years of it. Then the rare ones, the ones whose names he'd learned from dying men. Each one tore loose from wherever his body had walled it away, and each one took a piece of the wall with it.

By the fourth turn of the stair he could feel dust settling into his lungs — ordinary, mortal dust, the kind that would sicken him now, the kind that could kill him. By the sixth turn his hands were shaking with a fever he had no armor against. He had not been vulnerable since boyhood. Vulnerability, it turned out, felt exactly like being born.

The candle blazed.

It blazed so bright that Aldric, climbing ahead of him through falling water, turned around and had to shield his eyes, and in that unshielded moment — light pouring through the taster's translucent skin, the man inside it smiling like someone setting down a heavy load — Aldric remembered.

Not everything. Not yet. But a shape of everything: a scullery, stolen pastries, laughter in an archive, a page of prophecy annotated in two hands. A name arriving like weather.

"*Tamsin.*"

"Yeah," Tamsin said, still climbing, still burning. "Hi."

* * *

They came out of the mountain's throat into a night that was ending.

The spire — the black needle that had crowned the peak for a thousand years, that had drunk the winters of the world down into the Undercourt's furnaces — was coming apart above them in total silence. That was the worst of it, the silence: a structure that size should have screamed. Instead it simply softened, frost going to water, water going to stream, the whole impossible edifice collapsing upward into cloud and downward into rivers at once, spring meltwater in every direction, in midsummer, in a world that had forgotten the season.

Aldric stood at the mouth of the cave with the dawn coming up pink over the pass and said, in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant and finding it signed in flowers:

"That's my map. The one I drew at fourteen. The pass in summer. I drew it from a dream and thought I was mad."

"You weren't mad," Tamsin said. He swayed. Aldric caught him — caught all of him, the whole lightened mortal weight of him, a body that now weighed exactly what it weighed and nothing more, no hoard, no armor, no twelve years of purchased survival. Just a man. Just bones and hunger and a heart beating fast and unprotected.

"The candle," Aldric said. "Where—"

Tamsin opened his mouth and breathed out, and the flame left him — gently, the way a bird leaves a branch — and went into Aldric's chest, and Aldric made a sound that Tamsin would carry longer than any scar: half gasp, half sob, the sound of a house refurnishing itself. Of a soul coming home to find the windows open and the tables set and one chair worn smooth by someone who had sat in it waiting.

"Oh," Aldric said. "Oh, *you*. It was you the whole time. At the river I gave them—" His hand flew to his mouth. "I gave them you. To keep myself lit. Tamsin, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I didn't know what I was—"

"You knew exactly what you were doing," Tamsin said. His legs went then, properly, and Aldric went down with him onto the wet stone, and they knelt together in the meltwater like two men at a baptism. "You bought us both time. It worked. I'm not even slightly angry. Ask me again in a year and I'll lie about it, but right now — right now I'm not angry at all."

Above them the last of the spire came down as rain. Warm rain. It fell on Tamsin's bare head and he did not taste it first.

That was the moment he understood what he had done — not the grandeur of it, which meant nothing, but the smallness, which meant everything. Somewhere in Greyhollow tonight there would be food he had not tested. Water he had not smelled twice. A door he had not checked. He was going to spend the rest of his life flinching at meals, and he was going to do it anyway, every day, gladly, because the alternative was the Queen's hall: warm forever, safe forever, alone forever, tasting for a court that loved nothing it could not consume.

Twelve years he had bought his life one dish at a time.

He had finally found something worth the whole purse.

"Aldric," he said. The word cost him breath he could no longer spare easily, and he spent it anyway. Everything cost, now. Everything was worth it. "When we get down the mountain — when we're home — cook for me. Badly. Burn something. I want to taste it and live."

Aldric laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle, and he pulled Tamsin's forehead against his own, and the rain came down warm on both of them, and far below — so far below that no map would ever chart it — a hall full of candles burned free.

* * *

They were halfway down the pass when the snow on the far peaks began to slide — not the deadly roar of avalanche but a long soft sighing, winter letting go all along the range at once, and Aldric stopped and pointed with a cartographer's helpless precision at a road emerging from beneath ten feet of old ice: cobbles, milestones, a bridge, a shrine. A highway no living person had seen.

"There's a town under there," he whispered. "There are *towns* under there. Tamsin — the maps are all wrong. Every map anyone has ever drawn of the north is wrong, and I'm the only one who knows it, and I have to—"

"Tomorrow," Tamsin said.

"But the survey, the crown will want—"

"Tomorrow," Tamsin said again, and took his hand, and this time Aldric laced their fingers together like a man signing something.

Behind them, at the summit, where the spire had been, the morning sun hit wet stone and made it shine like a candle flame seen from very far away. And somewhere on the road below, small and patient, a figure in patched robes was climbing toward them through the thaw, leaning on a staff, carrying in her pocket a charm that had done its final work and a question she had walked a long way to ask:

*Well, taster. What will you build with the winter you broke?*
