## 20. Thaw

The first thing Aldric Vane knew was the sound of water.

Not the polite water of gutters or wells, but a vast, patient working — snow giving itself up all at once across the whole shoulder of the pass, a thousand small streams threading through rock, the mountain exhaling after a century of held breath. He lay on his back in wet grass that should not have been possible at this altitude and listened to it, because listening was easier than thinking, and thinking was easier than remembering, and remembering—

Remembering was a door he could not find.

He opened his eyes to a sky so blue it looked newly made. Clouds moved. Wind came down off the peaks carrying the green smell of things growing, which was wrong, which was *wonderful*, which was — his brow furrowed — familiar in a way he couldn't account for, like recognizing a face in a crowd before you know why you know it.

He sat up. His body catalogued its complaints: bruised ribs, a burn along his chest like a sun-scald shaped suspiciously like a hand pressed flat over his heart, boots ruined by meltwater, an ache behind his eyes as though he had been crying for a very long time and had only just stopped. His coat was soaked through but still buttoned, and inside it, against his ribs, something crackled.

Paper.

He drew it out with fingers that trembled and did not know why they trembled.

It was a single page, copied in a careful cartographer's hand — his own hand; he knew his own hand the way he knew his own gait — from some old book. The ink had run where the meltwater had found it, but the words were legible still, and they were about him.

*The taster and the cartographer,* the old verse said. *The one who swallows death daily and the one who draws the way down. They will come beneath the mountain when the last snow seals the gate, and there the taster shall break the bargain — or wed it.*

And beneath the copied verse, in margins gone soft with damp, annotations. Two hands. One was his own — cramped, slanting, the letters leaning east like trees in wind. The other was smaller, more controlled, a hand that wrote the way a person moves when they are used to being watched: neat, economical, betraying nothing until you looked closely at the pressure of the strokes and saw how hard they were pressed into the paper, as if the writer were afraid the words might otherwise escape him.

He read the annotations. They began simply enough — dates, cross-references, a note about the archive's catalogue numbers — but by the third margin they had become something else. A conversation. A correspondence conducted sideways across a page, two people writing to each other in the spaces around a prophecy, and the second hand — the careful one, the watched one — grew less careful as the notes went down the page. By the twelfth annotation it had stopped pretending to be scholarly at all.

*You laughed when I said I didn't believe in fate,* the careful hand had written. *I want you to know I've stopped laughing.*

*I haven't,* his own hand answered. *But I've stopped wanting to.*

Aldric read it twice. Three times. The page told him a story in fragments: a city called Greyhollow, a court beneath the mountain, souls bought and burned like candles, a winter out of season, a man who tasted poison for a living and sold charms in the dark to people who needed them. A name recurred in the margins the way a refrain recurs in a song, appearing and reappearing until it seemed less like a word than a place:

*Tamsin.*

He said it aloud, testing it against the air, and the air did nothing, and neither did his heart, and that absence frightened him more than anything else had. Because the handwriting loved this name. Anyone could see it. The careful hand loosened every time it wrote *Tamsin*, the letters spilling past their margins like water finding its level, and whoever had written those words had been writing about someone they —

Someone they what?

He searched himself the way he would search a map for a landmark that the surveyor had somehow omitted. There was Greyhollow — he remembered Greyhollow, remembered the guildhall, the commission, the king's surveyors, the smell of the archive dust. There was his mother's name — *Maren*, it surfaced suddenly, whole, and he nearly wept with relief at having it back. There were roads, coastlines, the shapes of mountains he had drawn a hundred times. All of it intact.

And where a person should have been — where twelve chapters of marginalia insisted a person had been — there was nothing. A blank space on the map, neatly bordered, unlabelled. Cartographers hated blank spaces. They were the one thing in the trade considered unforgivable: not error, but omission. You could be wrong about a coastline and be forgiven; you could not simply leave it out.

Someone had left a person out of him.

* * *

He got up because staying down felt like surrender, and followed the water downhill, because water always knew the way. The pass was unrecognizable and yet he recognized it — that was the maddening thing. His feet chose paths before his mind approved them, skirting a scree slope, cutting below a shelf of rock, and each time he arrived safely he understood that some version of himself had walked this route before and had left instructions his body had kept even as his mind lost them.

Toward midday he found the bothy.

It was a shepherd's shelter, low and stone, half-swallowed by the thaw, and the door stood open. Inside: a cold hearth, a straw pallet, and on the pallet's edge a clay charm strung on a leather cord, the kind street-sellers hawked outside guildhalls in cities that had outlawed the making of them. It was crude work, amateur work — and yet when he picked it up, warmth moved through his palm like a pulse, deliberate and unmistakable, and somewhere very deep in him something turned over, the way a sleeper turns toward a window when light first enters.

He put the cord around his neck. He did not ask himself why. Some decisions, he was learning, lived lower than memory.

In the hearth's ash he found the remains of a fire and, beside it, a tin cup and a knife and the stub of a candle burned down to almost nothing — burned, he thought, the way a person burns a candle when they cannot sleep, when they are keeping watch, when they are afraid that the dark will take something if they stop looking at it. He crouched there a long time with the cup in his hands, feeling the shape of a night he could not recall: two people, one fire, a storm outside or a fear inside or both, words spoken that one of them had paid dearly never to say again.

The blank space in him ached. That was the truest description he could find for it. Not emptiness — emptiness is painless. This was the ache of a socket, of a missing tooth the tongue returns to obsessively, of a coastline the map insists upon while the land itself has forgotten it was ever sea.

*What did you pay at the river?*

The question arrived fully formed, and he did not know where it came from, and it terrified him, because it implied a river, and rivers in stories of this kind took payment, and payments of that kind were always —

He stood up too fast and had to brace against the doorframe.

* * *

He walked for two days, or what he judged to be two days, following the meltwater down out of the high country into a valley that turned green as it went, absurdly, extravagantly green, as though a hundred years of stored spring had been released all at once and the land was drunk on it. Wildflowers he had no business knowing the names of introduced themselves anyway: *eyebright, thrift, mountain avens.* His own knowledge kept arriving like weather — frontally, without warning, sometimes gentle and sometimes knocking him flat.

On the evening of the second day he crested a ridge and saw Greyhollow below, and stopped as though he'd struck glass.

The city lay in its bowl with the snow gone off it entirely, roofs steaming in the low sun, the river running high and brown and free. And above the citadel, where the great white glacier-spire had stood for as long as any map in the kingdom had recorded it — he knew this, he had *drawn* it, he had inked that spire onto forty commissions with the patience of a monk — there was now nothing. A bare peak. Wet rock. A waterfall pouring from the wound of it, falling a thousand feet into mist.

The spire was gone. The winter was gone. And the city, instead of mourning, was — he squinted — festooned. Garlands on the bridge towers. Lanterns being hung in daylight. Smoke from celebration fires. Even at this distance he could see figures moving in the streets with the loose, quick gait of people who have recently been afraid and are not anymore, and do not entirely trust it.

He descended into the city as the light failed, and no one stopped him, and everyone stared. He understood why when he passed a public fountain and saw his reflection: a gaunt, hollow-eyed man in a ruined coat, frost-burned at the collar, with a scald-shaped mark glowing faintly pink at his chest like a brand half-healed. He looked like a man returned from somewhere no one returns from. Which, the margins of the page in his coat suggested, was exactly what he was.

He did not go to the guildhall. He did not go to the citadel, though he could feel the pull of it, the professional curiosity of a mapmaker confronted with a landscape that had erased its own most famous feature. His feet, keeping their own counsel, took him down through the terraces, past the tanneries, into the warren of lanes below the castle rock where the poor lived stacked like books, to a crooked street he had never seen and knew intimately, and stopped before a stall.

It was a charm-stall. A folding table under an awning, hung with cords and knots and little sewn sachets, and behind it sat a man mending a broken luck-knot by lamplight.

Aldric's heart did not leap. That was the horror of it. He had expected — the margins had taught him to expect — some catastrophic recognition, the world tilting, love arriving like a landslide. Instead his heart did nothing at all, and the man looked up, and his face performed a whole tragedy in the space of a breath: hope, recognition, and then the swift, practiced closing of it, like a shopkeeper hiding the good stock when the wrong customer enters.

"You're alive," the man said. His voice was steady. His hands were not; the knot slipped through them twice. "Good. That's — good."

"You're Tamsin," Aldric said. "From the page."

Something crossed the taster's face then that Aldric had no map for at all — a flinch, quickly mastered, of a man struck in a place that had already been struck many times. "Yes," he said. "I'm Tamsin Blackwood." And then, carefully, politely, in the voice of a stranger serving a stranger: "Do you need a charm?"

"I don't know." Aldric's own voice came out rough. "I don't know what I need. I remember everything except—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a space where you're supposed to be. I can feel the shape of it. Twelve chapters of your handwriting and mine, and I can't remember a single morning of you. What happened at the river, Tamsin? What did I pay?"

The lamplight moved. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked and was shushed. Tamsin set down the knot with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something fragile when his hands want to shake, and when he looked up his eyes were wet and absolutely level.

"You paid the ferryman," he said, "the one thing that would keep your candle burning. You paid the memory of me." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Aldric had ever seen, brave and ruined and offered anyway, like a gift wrapped in torn paper. "And it worked. You're here. Your soul is yours. So the price was fair, and I won't insult either of us by wishing it hadn't been."

"But the annotations—"

"Are yours. Every word. Whatever the candle kept, the ink kept too." Tamsin's mouth twisted. "Turns out a man's hand remembers what his head lets go. Hessel would have liked that. She'd have said it proved magic was just love that learned to write."

Aldric stood in the lamplight with the page in his hands and read it again, right there, all twelve chapters of it, while the man who had written half of it watched him read. And this time he read it not as a scholar but as a cartographer reading a map of a country he had once known by heart — and slowly, horribly, wonderfully, the coasts began to return.

Not as memory. As weather.

First a pressure, then a wind, then rain: stolen pastries on a guildhall stair, laughter over a map of harbors that didn't exist. Then a downpour: the archive, the verse, the moment his own childhood nickname — *little wren, wren of the west stairs* — had surfaced in century-old ink and the floor had seemed to tilt. Then a storm entire: summer snow falling on Greyhollow, a mother's name dissolving on his tongue, a road north by night, a bothy, a fire, a confession half-made and then — the river, the grey water, the ferryman's patient hand, and himself choosing, choosing freely, choosing *well*, trading the face of the man he loved for the soul that loved him, because a soul without its love could be given back someday, but a soul burnt to ash could never be given at all.

He had chosen correctly. He knew it the way he knew contour lines meant elevation. And knowing it broke him open anyway.

"Tamsin." His voice went to pieces on the second syllable. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I didn't — I would have told you, I would have asked you, I wouldn't have made you carry it alone—"

"You'd have done exactly what you did," Tamsin said quietly. "I read your handwriting for twelve chapters, Aldric. I know exactly what you'd have done." He came around the table then, slow, giving Aldric every chance to step back, and stopped a hand's breadth away, close enough that Aldric could see the new lines grief had drafted onto his face, the mortal fragility of him — and Aldric's body, which had forgotten everything, remembered this: his arms knew the distance. His arms closed it.

They stood in the lane holding each other while the city celebrated around them, lanterns rising, someone singing badly three doors down, the mountain dripping spring into every gutter in Greyhollow. Aldric pressed his face into the taster's shoulder and breathed him in like a coastline rediscovered, and felt the memories keep arriving, weather front after weather front, joy and grief in the same rainfall, indistinguishable, inseparable, both of them true.

"I'll earn it back," he said into Tamsin's collar. "Every day. Every morning you remember and I don't. I'll learn you again until there's no blank space left."

"There was never a blank space," Tamsin said. His hand found the scald-mark on Aldric's chest, over his heart, and rested there — and Aldric realized, with a jolt, that the burn was shaped like a hand because it *was* a hand's print, because Tamsin had held a lit soul-candle against his own skin to keep it from being reclaimed, had used his own body as a lantern, had burned himself to carry Aldric home. "You carried me the whole way down. In your coat. In your handwriting. You just didn't know whose map you were reading."

Above the rooftops, the bare peak where the spire had been caught the last of the light, and the meltwater sang in every channel of the city, and down in the lanes the first lanterns of the thaw-festival drifted upward past them like slow bright fish.

Then, from the direction of the citadel, a bell began to ring — not the festival bells, but the council bell, urgent, arrhythmic, calling the city together — and Tamsin lifted his head, and Aldric saw him go still in a way that twelve chapters of marginalia had taught him to recognize as fear wearing competence like a coat.

"They've been asking for me," Tamsin said. "The new council. Since the thaw. Every day." He looked at Aldric, and there was something in his face that was almost — almost — a request. "They want the witch who broke the winter to help decide what happens to magic now. And I've been putting them off. But they've stopped sending polite messages."

The bell rang on. Down the lane, a runner was already moving door to door, and the runner had stopped at the charm-stall's neighbor, and was pointing.
