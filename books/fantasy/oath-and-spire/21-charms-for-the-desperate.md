## 21. Charms for the Desperate

The snow on Greyhollow's rooftops went soft in a single night, and by morning the city smelled of wet slate and woodsmoke and something older underneath—something green, like a cellar of forgotten apples turning toward spring.

Tamsin stood at his window in Aldric's narrow house above the map-shop and watched the thaw run down the gutters in silver ropes. For twelve years he had measured every season by what it did to food: how fast milk soured, how long a fish kept, which poisons woke in warmth and which slept in frost. Now he watched the meltwater simply because it was beautiful, and the strangeness of that nearly undid him.

His hands still shook sometimes. Not from withdrawal—Hessel's charm had burned clean when it burned—but from the absence of the thing it had carried. For twelve years he had been a fortress, wall behind wall behind wall, each poison swallowed another stone laid against death. Now the walls were down. He could feel everything: the cold draft under the door, the ache in his knees from the mountain road, the terrifying openness of a body that could be hurt.

He had not realized how much of himself he had spent being unbreakable until there was nothing left to spend.

Aldric was downstairs already, ink to the elbows, drawing the mountain road as it actually was now—a road, passable, mapped in daylight instead of rumor. Tamsin could hear him talking to the paper the way he always did, low and half-amused, arguing with a river's course. The sound filled the house the way heat fills a room.

*You came back to me twice,* Tamsin thought, watching the meltwater. *Once out of the mountain. Once out of your own forgetting.* He did not know yet which return had cost more. Perhaps he never would. Perhaps that was what a mortal life was: costs you carried without ever seeing the ledger.

He dressed, pocketed the little bundle of charms he'd made before dawn, and went down into the city that no longer quite knew what to do with him.

* * *

Greyhollow had cracked along with its winter.

Not all at once—the ban did not shatter so much as go soft at the edges, like ice in a stream. But everywhere Tamsin walked that morning he saw the small evidences. A hedge-witch's sign hung openly over a door on Tanner's Row, painted with a mortar and pestle wreathed in rowan leaves, where a year ago such a sign would have meant the pyre. Two women argued cheerfully in a doorway about whether a luck-knot should be tied left-handed or right, and neither of them lowered their voices. In the market square, where the witch-posts had stood—black iron hooks for those the crown had judged—someone had wound the posts in ivy, and someone else had hung them with ribbons and drying herbs and one defiant pair of red boots, and nobody had taken any of it down.

Children chalked spirals on the cobbles. Old women sold *actual charms*, out in the open, haggling like fishwives. A man played a fiddle on the church steps and the tune had a shape to it that made the air shimmer faintly, and the priest came out and listened, and tapped his foot.

Magic coming back to Greyhollow was less a flood than a thaw—seeping, tentative, finding old channels. And everywhere it seeped, people looked up when Tamsin passed.

He hated it. He had spent twelve years perfecting invisibility: the taster's gray face, the servant's bowed head, the charm-seller's forgettable stall in the scullery shadows. Now they knew his name. They knew what he'd done under the mountain—or the version of it that had come back with the survivors, embroidered by telling—and they looked at him the way you look at a landmark. *There goes the man who ended the winter.*

Nobody said *there goes the traitor who fed the king poisons' worth of secrets to a hedge-witch.* Nobody said *there goes the man whose lover forgot him to save his own soul.* Grief, Tamsin was learning, made a poor spectacle. Heroism made an excellent one. The city had chosen which story to tell, and he had to live inside their telling of it.

At the corner of Cordwainer Street, a baker pressed a warm roll into his hands and refused payment. "For the taster," she said, beaming. "My boy was one of the bought souls, did I tell you? Came home with the thaw. Came home *whole*."

"He did," Tamsin said, because it was true; the candles he had freed had mostly gone home to their bodies, waking in beds and ditches and one memorable case in the fountain outside the Protector's hall, blinking at the sunrise like cats. "I'm glad."

"You gave us back our winter-dead." She pushed the roll harder against his chest until he took it properly. "Eat. You're thin as a Lenten sermon."

He ate it walking. It was good bread. That was the strange grace note of his new life: he could taste things now without cataloguing them for danger. Almond, salt, the char of a hot oven. Just bread. He had to stop in the middle of the street, once, overcome by the sheer unguardedness of it.

* * *

They came for him on the fourth day after the thaw began—three of them, in good wool, with the careful faces of people about to ask for something enormous.

He recognized the woman first: Magistrate Corren Hale, who had spent the winter's last weeks quietly refusing to sign execution orders, and who now wore the sash of the Provisional Council. Beside her, a young man Tamsin didn't know, sharp-eyed, with ink stains to match Aldric's. And third, to Tamsin's surprise, Mother Hessel's old neighbor Wren, the herbalist, gray-braided and unsmiling, whom Hessel had trusted enough to leave her cat with.

They found him at his stall.

Because that was the other thing. Three days after the thaw, Tamsin had done something he had been circling for years without daring: he had set up his charm-stall in daylight. Not in the scullery shadow, not in the market's blind corner, but out on Cordwainer Street itself, on a trestle table borrowed from the cooper, with a striped awning Aldric had insisted on ("If we're doing this, we're doing it *well*, Tam") and a hand-painted sign in Aldric's precise cartographer's lettering:

**CHARMS FOR THE DESPERATE**
*Fair prices. No questions. No curses sold here, whatever you've heard.*

Business was, frankly, overwhelming. Half the city wanted luck-knots for their newly legal trades. Mothers wanted fever-charms. A fisherman wanted something for his nets that Tamsin had to explain, gently, three times, was not how magic worked. An old soldier wanted a charm against nightmares and paid in stories instead of coin, sitting on the cooper's bench for two hours telling Tamsin about a war Tamsin had only ever seen from the king's table, and by the end both of them were quieter and easier in themselves, which was, Tamsin supposed, also a kind of charm.

So the council found him mid-morning, elbow-deep in knotwork, a queue of six customers, flour on his sleeve from the baker's roll.

"Master Blackwood." Corren Hale inclined her head. Behind her, the queue exchanged glances and, with the instinctive courtesy of people who wanted their luck-knots, drifted a polite distance away.

"Magistrate." He wiped his hands on his apron. "If this is about the awning permit, the cooper says—"

"It is not about the awning." Almost, almost, she smiled. "It is about the laws."

She explained while her colleagues stood in the thaw-light: the Provisional Council, nine seats, charged with rewriting a century of statutes in a season. The magic ban was dead in practice and alive on paper, and paper, unchecked, had a way of growing teeth again. They needed people who understood the craft—not the court's version, the real one—to write what would replace it. What would be licensed. What would be forbidden. Who would answer for harm done, and how, and to whom.

"We want you on the council," Hale said. "Or failing that, heading the commission on magical practice. You'd have a title. Rooms in the Protector's hall, if you want them. A salary that would make your cartographer weep."

Wren snorted softly at *cartographer*, in the way of someone who had heard exactly how much Aldric already earned.

Tamsin looked at his hands. There was red thread under the nails. On the table between them lay half-finished a fever-charm for the fisherman's daughter, woven from rowan and a word Hessel had taught him, and beside it a nightmare-knot waiting for the old soldier to come back, and a luck-bracelet for a bride, and a jar of salve for burns that he made from a recipe written in Hessel's crabbed hand, which he had copied out forty times now and given away free to anyone who asked, because that was what she had done, all her life, outlawed and disgraced and generous as weather.

"May I tell you what I was doing twelve years ago?" he said.

Hale waited.

"I was standing in a scullery, selling a fever-charm to a laundress whose son was sick, and I was charging her a week's wages because I was hungry, and I hated myself for it, and I did it anyway. And the whole time I was doing it, upstairs, I was tasting the king's supper, dish by dish, ready to die for a man who would have burned me if he'd known what my hands could do." He turned the thread over in his fingers. "I learned everything I know about power in that building, Magistrate. Mostly how it uses people. If I sit in that hall—in those rooms—I'll be useful to you for a year, maybe two. And then I'll be furniture. Ornament. *The taster who saved us*, trotted out to make the laws smell honest while the same old hands write them."

"That's cynical," the young ink-stained one said.

"It's observational," Tamsin said. "I was a professional observer for twelve years. Ask me what the Lord Protector drank with dinner."

Hale studied him for a long moment. She had a magistrate's patience; he'd seen her use it on the worst winter of the city's memory.

"What do you want, then?" she asked. "Truly. Name it."

And Tamsin, who had spent a lifetime wanting nothing out loud because wanting was dangerous, heard himself say it plainly:

"Make the craft legal. All of it, or as much as can be borne—hedgerow work, charms, midwifery-craft, the small magics. License it cheap, or don't license it at all; license fees are just the ban wearing a nicer coat. Write protections for practitioners, so no hedge-witch hangs because a lord dislikes her. And put Wren here—" he nodded at the herbalist, who raised her chin, "—and people like her, actual practitioners, on whatever commission writes it. Not me. I'll advise. I'll read your drafts and tell you where they'll bite. But I won't sit in the hall."

"Why not?" the young one asked. "Honestly. Most men would kill for—"

"Because I've already had my fill of being the crown's instrument," Tamsin said. "I'm told the cure is community."

He picked up the fever-charm and finished the last knot while they watched, and tied it off, and held it up to the light the way Hessel had taught him—checking the weave, checking the intention, checking that the thing was *kind* at its center, because a charm without kindness at its center was just a pretty trap.

"This," he said, "is the office I want. Right here. Six customers deep, flour on my sleeves, fair prices, no questions." He smiled, and it felt strange on his face, and then it didn't. "Charms for the desperate. It's even on the sign."

Corren Hale looked at the sign. At the queue, which had crept back within earshot and was pretending very badly not to listen. At Wren, who was already pulling a stool up to the trestle table as though she intended to stay.

"Well," the magistrate said at last, and there was something in her voice that might have been relief—relief, Tamsin thought, that at least one hero of the winter had declined to become a statue. "We'll take the advice, then. And the drafts. Expect a messenger." She paused. "Master Blackwood. The council is grateful. More than the record will ever show."

"The record shows enough," Tamsin said. "Believe me. I've seen what records do to people."

* * *

Spring came on the way spring does in Greyhollow—all at once, apologetically, as if it had overslept and was trying to make up for it.

The mountain road opened in the second week of April, and the first carts came down from the high passes carrying cheese and wool and news, and among the news was this: in the villages around the mountain, the snowmelt had uncovered things. Old doors in the rock, sealed since the spire fell, now standing open onto passages full of light. Wells that ran sweet where they had run bitter for a century. A chapel in the pass, long buried, with a fresco of a queen with a human face, weeping, and beneath it a name carved in letters that scholars were already arguing about.

Aldric mapped all of it. He came home each evening smelling of pencil shavings and pine, and spread the day's work across the kitchen table, and Tamsin would bring tea and lean over his shoulder and watch the world get bigger sheet by sheet.

"You know what they're calling the pass road?" Aldric said one night, tapping a fresh survey. "*The Taster's Way.* There's going to be a milestone. With your name on it. I've seen the stonemason's draft."

"I'm going to deface it," Tamsin said. "Every night. With charcoal. Until it just says *this way to the shepherd's bothy, mind the toll bridge, tip your ferryman*."

"You can't deface a monument to yourself."

"I have betrayed a king, broken a fae court, and burned twelve years of immunities in a single night, Aldric Vane. I can absolutely deface a milestone."

Aldric laughed—that laugh, the one from the first evening over stolen pastries, the one Tamsin had nearly lost twice—and pulled him down into a chair, and the maps slid, and neither of them cared.

But it was true, what the city had decided to make of him. He saw it in small ways. The guildmaster of cooks sent a groveling letter asking whether the Taster's Guild might be rechartered under his patronage (he wrote back: *dissolve it, feed people well, pay your sculleries*). A playwright was mounting a drama called *The Winter Court* in which a character named "Tamlin the Bold" delivered speeches no living person had ever heard Tamsin say. Children played at breaking the winter in the lanes, taking turns being the Queen and the brave taster, and the brave taster always won, and always got the girl, and Tamsin overheard one small girl correct another with great authority: "No, silly, he gets the *mapmaker*. Everyone knows that."

Everyone knows that. He turned the phrase over some nights like a coin he couldn't quite believe was legal tender. Twelve years of being a function, a gray face, a secret in a scullery—and now he belonged to a city the way a bell belongs to a tower: publicly, audibly, impossible to unsay.

It should have frightened him more than it did. What frightened him, if he was honest, was how little it frightened him. How easily he had set down the weight. How the body, even a mortal body, even a body that had swallowed death daily for twelve years, apparently remembered how to be light.

* * *

On the last morning of April, Tamsin opened his stall early, before the queue, and did the thing he did every morning now: he lit a candle in the little lantern at the corner of the trestle table.

It was a plain candle, beeswax, nothing enchanted—he had checked, twice, because he would never again sell or light anything whose flame he hadn't verified—and it burned for Hessel. Customers had learned not to ask. Some of them left offerings beside it, unasked: a sprig of rowan, a copper bit, once a perfectly good sausage, which Wren confiscated for lunch.

He was trimming the wick when he noticed the girl.

She was standing at the edge of the awning's shade, maybe ten years old, barefoot despite the chill, with the particular stillness of a child who has learned that being noticed is dangerous. Tamsin knew that stillness. He had invented it, at her age, in a starving year he rarely spoke about.

"Are you lost?" he asked, keeping his voice easy, the way you talk to a stray cat.

She shook her head. Then, after a moment: "Mam says you buy things. Odd things. Things nobody else wants."

"Sometimes. What have you got?"

She came forward slowly and opened her fist. In her palm lay a scrap of parchment, soft with age, folded many times, edges gone furry. "Found it in our wall. When the thaw came the wall went damp and the plaster fell off, and there it was, stuck behind a brick. Mam says burn it, walls shouldn't have writing in them, it's unlucky. But—" her chin lifted, defiant, "—it's *old*. Old things should go to someone who keeps them."

Tamsin took it carefully. Unfolded it on the table, weighting the corners with spools of thread.

The hand was crabbed, hurried, familiar in a way that stopped his breath. He knew that hand. He had forty copies of recipes in that hand pinned above his workbench, and a grave in the melting hills with that hand's name on it, and a charm's worth of borrowed magic still warm somewhere in his chest on cold nights.

It was a page in Mother Hessel's writing. Not a recipe. Something older—the ink browned, the language archaic, the margins crowded with her later annotations in different ink, added decades apart like rings in a tree. And at the top, in the oldest hand of all, a title:

*On the Breaking of Courts, Being a True Account of the First Bride*

Below it, the first line, and Tamsin read it twice because the first time his eyes refused it:

*Every hundred winters the court beneath buys itself a bride, and every hundred winters a taster must choose what she will swallow...*

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"Where exactly," Tamsin said, and had to start again, gentler, "in your wall? Which street? Which brick?"

The girl told him. And it was Hessel's old street—her house, or the house that had replaced it—and Tamsin sat down slowly on the cooper's bench while the morning queue began to gather behind the girl, patient, curious, watching their charm-seller hold a page of his dead friend's handwriting like it might bite.

Which, knowing Hessel, it probably could.

"Aldric is going to want to see this," Tamsin murmured. He folded the page with the care owed to relics and live coals alike, and looked up at the girl, and dug in the till for a coin far too large for the purchase. "Tell your mother the wall was right to keep it. And tell her—" he almost laughed, grief and wonder arriving in the same breath, "—tell her the person who keeps old things says thank you."

Outside, the thaw-water ran bright in the gutters, running downhill toward the river, toward the pass, toward the open doors in the mountain where the light got in.

And in Tamsin's chest, where twelve years of immunities used to live, something that was not immunity at all—something softer and far more dangerous—began, cautiously, to grow.
