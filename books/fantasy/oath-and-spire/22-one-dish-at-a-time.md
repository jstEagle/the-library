## 22. One Dish at a Time

*Tamsin*

The bread is dangerous.

Not poisoned — nothing in Greyhollow has been poisoned for him in over a year — but dangerous the way a high ledge is dangerous, the way deep water is. He kneads it on the scarred table by his window while morning comes up grey-gold over the rooftops, and every so often he tears off a corner of raw dough and eats it without thinking, and every time he does, some old sentry in the back of his skull stands up and shouts *untested, untested, you fool* — and then remembers there is no one left to test for, and sits back down.

He is learning to be careless. It is the hardest thing he has ever done.

Twelve years he spent making himself into a fortress: the small poisons taken nightly in measured doses, the antidotes brewed and drunk bitter, the body built like a lock with a hundred tumblers all turned inward. He could eat nightshade and shrug. He could drink from a well that had killed three men. And now — now his hands shake if he cuts an onion too near the knife's edge. Now a stomachache keeps him up all night wondering. Now a bee could sting him and he might swell and die like any ordinary man on any ordinary street, and Aldric would find him on the floor among the flour sacks, and that would be the whole story.

Mortality sits on him like a coat cut too small. He wears it anyway.

The loaf goes into the oven. He sets two places at the table because there are always two places now, and while it bakes he opens the shutters onto the street where his charm stall leans against the wall below, its awning faded to the color of weak tea. Business has changed since the winter broke. Half his customers used to come hooded, whispering, sliding coins across the boards like they were passing contraband. They still do — habits die slower than laws — but others come in daylight now, openly, asking after charms for colicky babies and rheumatic knees, and one memorable widow who wanted a luck-knot for her henhouse and haggled for twenty minutes.

The council had offered him more than a stall. *Help us write the laws,* they said. *You know what magic costs. You've paid it.* Witch, traitor, hero — the titles still got swapped around depending on who was talking, though "traitor" had gone quiet since the Lord Protector retired to his estates in the south and the snowmelt records came out and everyone learned exactly what the crown had been trading away for four generations. But Tamsin had stood in front of men with power before, tasting their food, watching them decide the fates of people who couldn't reach the table, and he had no appetite for becoming one of them.

"I make charms," he'd told the council. "Small ones. For desperate people. That's the whole trade."

It was enough. It had always been enough; he'd just needed twelve years and one apocalypse to see it.

The smell of baking bread drifts out the window and down into the street, and Mrs. Corvel from the dyer's calls up, "That smells like sin, Tamsin Blackwood!" and he laughs, actually laughs, leaning on the sill with flour on his knuckles. A year ago he hadn't laughed in so long the sound had surprised him, coming out rusty and strange. Now it comes easy as breathing, which is to say imperfectly, which is to say it can stop at any time, which is the whole point of everything.

The oven door opens. The loaf comes out golden-crusted, cracked along the top like a healing wound, and he carries it to the table where the second place waits, and he cuts it while it's still too hot, burning his fingertips, swearing cheerfully, feeling every degree of it.

Aldric comes up the stairs on the hour, smelling of cedar shavings and ink, and steals the heel of the loaf before Tamsin can plate it, which is traditional now, which is law.

"Tastes safe," Aldric says, mouth full.

"It should," Tamsin says. "I tested it."

"On whom?"

"Me. I'm the taster." He says it lightly, but Aldric hears what's under it, the way he always does — the way a cartographer reads contour lines, all the hidden shape beneath the surface. He crosses the room and kisses Tamsin's floury cheek, and his hand lingers at the back of his neck, warm and callused, steady.

"Only your own cooking," Aldric says softly. "Those were the terms."

"Those were the terms," Tamsin agrees. "I'm holding myself to the contract. I hear the Undercourt is very strict about contracts."

"They're not around to enforce it anymore." Aldric pulls out his chair. Through the window, far off, the mountain stands bare-headed in the morning light, its spire gone, its gates propped open, meltwater shining down its flanks in long silver threads like the mountain itself is crying with relief. "Sit. Eat. You're mortal. Act like it."

Tamsin sits. He eats. It tastes like bread, just bread, warm and plain and impossibly precious, and he thinks — not for the first time, and surely not for the last — that survival was never the prize. Anyone can survive alone in a locked room. This, this burnt tongue and shared loaf and second chair worn smooth by the same hips every morning — this is the thing he'd spent twelve years buying protection against ever wanting, because wanting could be used against him.

It had been. It had nearly cost everything.

And he'd pay it again. That was the joke of it. That was the grace.

*Aldric*

The mountain road runs from the north gate of Greyhollow up through the pass in eighteen switchbacks, and Aldric Vane has walked every one of them eleven times since the thaw, and intends to walk them until the map is perfect or he dies, whichever comes later.

The council commissioned the survey in spring: *the reopened road must be charted before pilgrims and merchants break their necks in it.* What the council didn't say — what everyone understood — was that the road needed mapping for another reason too. People needed to see it drawn. Needed proof, in ink, that the pass was passable, that the dark under the world had ended, that you could walk toward the place where the winter lived and come back out the other side with your soul intact and the sun on your face.

Aldric's maps did that work better than any proclamation. He drew the switchbacks fine and exact. He drew the shepherd's bothy where they'd once slept pressed together listening for hunters, and marked it, after some deliberation, with a tiny symbol meaning *shelter* — though privately he thought of it as meaning something else entirely, something he'd almost said that night and then said properly a hundred times since. He drew the river and the crossing where the ferryman's barge had rotted midstream the moment the court died, its tolls uncollectible, its cargo of stolen memories drifting free like leaves on water. Some of those memories had even found their way home, the archivists reported. Not all. Never all. That was the wound the Undercourt left that would never fully close, and Aldric carried his own piece of it in his coat pocket everywhere he went.

But most of all, he drew the mountain itself, the hollowed-out halls where the Winter Queen had reigned over a thousand years of bought souls, and he drew them empty. Because they were. Because the candles had guttered free, and the court had wept itself into meltwater, and the great spire above had come down in a rush of spring that drowned the valley roads for a week and made the farmers bless it.

Today he works at the seventh switchback, sitting on a flat stone with his board across his knees, sketching the view down over Greyhollow — the smoke rising straight in windless air, the charm stalls opening their awnings, the cathedral spire catching sun. His hand moves and the world arrives on paper, and this is the oldest joy he knows, older than love, maybe: that the hand can hold what the eye sees and give it to someone who isn't there.

Footsteps on gravel behind him. He doesn't turn. Only one person climbs this road with that particular rhythm, half-stride and half-lope, a man who spent years moving quietly through kitchens and never quite lost the habit.

"You're supposed to be at your stall," Aldric says.

"Hessel's day," Tamsin says. "Closed the shutter. Left a sign. 'Gone up the mountain, back by dark.'" He comes around into view, carrying a satchel and a wrapped candle, squinting in the light. "You're nearly done?"

"This stretch, yes." Aldric looks down at the page. In the corner, where most mapmakers put their crest, he has drawn a small loaf of bread. Nobody at the council has asked about it yet. He hopes someone eventually will. "Come look. Tell me if the pass looks like home."

Tamsin leans over his shoulder. Aldric feels his breath, feels the weight of him, solid and warm and *breakable*, and the knowledge of it lands in his chest the way it does every single day now — not as fear anymore, mostly, but as attention. He had forgotten Tamsin once. Had paid for his own soul with the memory of the man he crossed mountains for, and woken in the snow knowing the mission and not the man, and walked beside a stranger who looked at him like the end of the world. Then he'd found the prophecy page in his own coat pocket, annotated in two hands across twelve chapters, and memory had come back like weather, slow and total, rain filling a dry riverbed until suddenly there was a river again and it had always been a river.

Some nights he still wakes reaching for the shape of that absence. Some mornings he watches Tamsin eat and thinks: *I chose you twice. Once with the memory and once without. Once knowing everything and once knowing nothing but that my hands turned toward you like compass needles.*

He has told Tamsin this exactly once, on this road, at this switchback, three months ago. Tamsin had gone silent for a long moment and then said, "Then I'm not a memory to you. I'm a direction." And Aldric had kissed him with the map ink still wet on his fingers, and smudged the pass beyond repair, and redrawn it worse the next day, and counted it a fair trade.

"The pass looks like home," Tamsin says now. "Home looks better than the pass, though. Less scree."

"Flatterer."

"Cartographer. Come down when the light goes. I'll cook."

"Poison me gently."

"Only ever gently." Tamsin grins, and starts back down the switchbacks, quick and sure on the loose stones, a mortal man with mortal footing, and Aldric watches him go all the way to where the road bends, because watching is free, and he intends to spend extravagantly.

*Tamsin*

Hessel's grave sits on the shoulder of the pass where she made her stand, in a fold of hillside the hunters' arrows never reached past. There is no body under the cairn — what the pass took, the pass kept — but the cairn is there, and the marker, and the view. She'd have approved of the view. The woman had opinions about real estate like she had opinions about everything, loud and correct.

They climb in the gold end of the afternoon, the two of them and a dozen others strung out along the road at intervals — the widow with the lucky hens, a pair of archive clerks, a young hedge-witch in training whose charm-work already has Hessel's messy precision, because Tamsin teaches what she taught him and she taught him to teach badly and often. Word of the anniversary had gone round the way word does now, openly, passed shop to shop instead of hand to hidden hand. Last year they'd come alone. This year the cairn grows a small crowd, and someone has left wildflowers wedged between the stones, and someone else a chipped clay cup filled with honey, because Hessel had liked her tea sweet enough to stand a spoon in.

Tamsin sets the candle on the flattest stone. Beeswax, good wax, bought from the chandler on Candle Street who'd survived the winter and cried when Tamsin came in and asked for the best he had. He lights it with a match — matches, ordinary fire, no charm in it at all — and shields the flame with his palm until it steadies.

"For the witch of Greyhollow," he says. His voice cracks on *witch* and he lets it. "Who stayed behind at the pass so two fools could keep walking. Who gave me the last of her magic and told me—" He stops. Swallows. Aldric's hand finds his.

"Tell them what she told you," Aldric murmurs. "You tell it every year."

"She told me the charm wasn't for keeping me alive." Tamsin looks at the flame. Inside it, if you knew how to look — and he knows, now, he'll always know — there is a warmth that is hers, the last ember of a life spent illegally healing strangers. "She said it was for reminding me what I was keeping me alive *for*. Said survival's just a room, boy. Love's the door."

Around the cairn, the little crowd is quiet. The young hedge-witch is crying and pretending not to. The widow says, loudly, "Well, she was right, wasn't she," and everyone laughs wetly, and the ceremony becomes what Hessel would have insisted on: not solemnity but company.

They stay while the light goes long and amber. Aldric sketches the cairn, quick and loving, adding it to the map of the road — *memorial site, elevation 1,240 paces, keep to the left of the stones* — and Tamsin watches him do it and thinks about how a man can be a map of everything he's survived. The blank spaces. The routes burned through. The annotations in two hands.

When the candle burns low they leave it lit — that's the tradition, let it burn itself out under the stars, let her have her light — and start down together in the blue dusk, shoulders bumping, arguing amiably about whether the bothy roof needs new thatch before winter.

Winter. The word has lost its teeth. Winter is just a season now, with a date on the calendar and boots by the door.

At the bend where the road looks back, Tamsin stops once, the way he does. Far below, Greyhollow is lighting its lamps one window at a time, and somewhere among them his stall stands shuttered with its honest sign, and tomorrow there will be dough to knead and charms to sell and a man to feed, and none of it is guaranteed, and all of it is his.

"One dish at a time," he says, to the mountain, to the witch, to himself.

Behind them on the hillside, small and stubborn in the gathering dark, the candle goes on burning.
