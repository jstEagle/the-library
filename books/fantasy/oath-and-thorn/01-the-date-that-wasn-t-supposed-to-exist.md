## 1. The Date That Wasn't Supposed to Exist

The workshop smelled of dust and tallow and the faint sweet rot of old ivory. Oscar Sandoval had been breathing that smell for thirty-one years, ever since his uncle first set a chisel in his hands and told him the bones would tell him what they wanted to be.

He did not believe it, not exactly. Belief was a luxury for people who hadn't spent their whole lives listening. What he believed was simpler and stranger: that the dead left something behind in their bones the way a fire leaves heat in a hearthstone, and that if you held the bone still enough—carved slow enough, careful enough—you could feel it. Not words, not at first. A pressure. A leaning. The way water leans toward a drain.

Tonight's work was a memorial plate: the sternum of Aldous Merrick, weaver, dead eleven days, to be mounted above the door of his daughter's house on Larkspur Row. Oscar had already roughed out the border of wheat sheaves—the Merrick family mark—and now he sat under the single lamp with the fine graver, cutting the letters one by one into the polished face of the bone.

*ALDOUS MERRICK. WEAVER. FATHER. HE KEPT THE LOOM SINGING.*

Outside, Thornvale was doing what Thornvale always did after dark: settling. The wind came down off the western ridge and moved through the pines with a sound like slow breathing. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it. The valley was a bowl of darkness ringed by mountains, and in all his life Oscar had never once seen anything enter it or leave it that wasn't born here or buried here.

And beneath everything, as always, the murmur.

He had stopped calling them voices years ago. Voices implied conversation, and there was no conversing with what he heard. It was more like weather—a low, ceaseless stirring at the floor of his hearing, fragments rising and dissolving like silt in a stream. *—the blue dress, the one with the—* *—tell him I'm sorry about the fence—* *—cold, it was so cold that winter, we ate the—* Names half-finished. Regrets without owners. The last sentences of people long dead, worn smooth by time until they were less than words, just the shape of words, the way a river stone is the shape of a word no one speaks anymore.

Every carver in Thornvale's history had worked in silence. Only Oscar heard the murmurs, and he had learned early never to mention it. His mother, before she died, had caught him once, age nine, standing in the churchyard with his head tilted like a bird's, and asked him what he was listening to. He'd said *the wind.* She'd looked at him a long moment with an expression he could never afterward describe—not fear, exactly, and not sorrow either. Something older than both. Then she'd kissed his hair and said, *Good. Keep it that way.*

She'd been dead nine years now. Her voice was in the murmur too, sometimes—or something that wore her voice, which amounted to the same thing and hurt worse. Fragments mostly. *—the garden needs—* *—you were such a small—* Once, only once, on the anniversary of her burial, clear as a bell across still water: *Don't be afraid of it, little owl.*

He didn't know why she'd called him that. He'd never asked anyone. Some things you keep folded away where the light can't reach them, because unfolded they might mean more than you can carry.

The graver whispered through the bone. *S-I-N-G-I-N-G.* The last letter needed a deeper cut; the grain of the sternum dipped there, a flaw like a frozen ripple, and Oscar slowed, letting the tool find its own way as he'd been taught. Feel the bone. Let it speak.

And the bone spoke.

Not the murmur. Something else. Something that rose out of the ordinary static the way a hand rises out of dark water—one voice, singular, close, immediate, as though someone had leaned their mouth against the back of his neck.

*"Oscar."*

The graver skipped. A thin bright line scored across the finished *G*, ruining it. Oscar sat very still in the lamplight, his breath loud in the quiet workshop, every hair on his arms lifted.

The murmurs never named him. In thirty-one years, not once. They were echoes, residue, the shed skins of other people's endings. They had nothing to do with him. That was the whole architecture of his life, the load-bearing wall he'd built himself against: *the dead are not speaking to you. They are simply leaking. You happen to be standing where they leak.*

"Say it again," he said aloud, to the bone, to the room, to whatever had spoken. His voice sounded strange to him, flattened with effort. "Say it again."

Silence. The ordinary murmur resumed underneath, indifferent, silt-like. *—so cold that winter—* *—the blue dress—*

But then, fainter, from somewhere that felt less like the bone and more like the walls themselves, like the valley:

*"Oscar Sandoval. Moonset. Moonset. Before the moon sets—"*

And gone. Folded back into the stream. He listened until his neck ached, tilting his head the way he had in the churchyard at nine years old, but nothing else surfaced. No name again. No date. Just the usual fragments, and one new fragment that had not been there before and would not leave: *moonset, moonset,* repeating somewhere far down in the noise like a stone rolling over and over at the bottom of a well.

He finished the plate. He had to—he'd promised Aldous Merrick's daughter, and the dead did not excuse a man from his debts to the living. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; they were the steadiest thing about him. But when he blew the bone dust from the finished letters and held the plate up to the lamp, he saw that his ruined *G* looked less like a mistake than like a scar, and he thought, absurdly, *I'll remember this one. This is the night something changed,* and then he thought, *no, don't be a fool, you're a bone-carver in a valley that smells of pine and turnips, nothing changes here, nothing has changed here in a hundred years.*

That was the phrase that snagged, later. *A hundred years.* He turned it over as he banked the lamp and walked home through the dark lanes, past shuttered windows and the black shapes of the loom-houses, and he couldn't have said why it snagged. Except that the voice had said *moonset,* and tonight the moon was three days from full, and setting late, and he found himself walking home slower than usual, watching it climb over the eastern ridge like a pale eye opening, and thinking: *three days.*

Three days until full moon. Four until it began to wane. And moonset on a waning night comes near dawn, and dawn was when the mist came off the river and the valley looked, briefly, like a place that belonged to no one.

He locked his door. He did not sleep.

* * *

The chapel archive stood behind the church of Saint Brannoc-the-Quiet, a squat stone building that had been a tithe barn before it was anything else, and it smelled the way all old buildings in Thornvale smelled: cold stone, mouse, and the particular dry sweetness of paper that has given up expecting to be read. Oscar had keys to it because the carvers kept their guild records there—apprenticeships, burials, fees, grievances going back generations—and because Father Ellery had long ago stopped caring who came and went, so long as they shut the door against the damp.

He told himself he was coming for the guild ledger. He even took down the guild ledger, and wrote the morning's date neatly beside the entry for the Merrick commission, and stood there holding the pen for a long moment, doing nothing, before he put it down.

Then he went looking for the other thing. He didn't know what the other thing was. He only knew that the voice had said *moonset,* and that his grandmother—Ilsa Sandoval, vanished into the mountain before he was born, a woman spoken of in his family the way you speak of weather that killed someone—had been, according to his aunt Maren, *a keeper of papers.* And that the archive had a shelf, high up behind the vestment chests, of books no one touched.

He climbed the ladder. The top shelf held perhaps forty volumes, most of them parish records, tax rolls, a water-damaged hymnal swollen to twice its size. And at the end, bound in cracked green leather gone grey along the spine, a book with no title on its cover.

It was heavier than it should have been. When he opened it, the first page took his breath in a way he couldn't account for: the handwriting was beautiful. Not clerk-hand, not priest-hand—something older and more deliberate, letters like planted seeds, each one set with care, the ink brown-black and faded at the edges of every stroke as though the words themselves were growing old.

*A Book of the Valley's Keeping. Begun in the year the mountain last spoke.*

One hundred and four years ago.

He turned pages. Prophecies, mostly—that was the word used, though they read more like warnings. Passages about the boundary of the valley, about the line where magic ended, about *what waits without shall hunger for what sleeps within.* A century of careful, frightened handwriting, each entry initialed at the bottom in a small looping monogram he didn't recognize.

Until he reached the entries from sixty years back. And there the monogram changed.

*I.S.*

His hands went cold. He set the book down on the reading table, opened it flat, weighted it with the brass page-rest shaped like a sleeping fox, and made himself go slowly. Ilsa Sandoval had kept this book. Had added to it. Had written, in that same seed-lettered hand, entries that grew stranger as the years passed—entries about the mountain, about bargains, about *the court below Cindrel,* a phrase Oscar had never heard spoken aloud in his life and yet which settled into him with horrible familiarity, like a word from a dream everyone agrees to forget.

And then, forty-one years ago—the year before his father married, the year before any of them were born—an entry unlike the others. Shorter. Written faster, the seed-letters loosening, hurrying.

*The debt is struck. One soul, held in trust, against the sanctuary of the vale. It cannot be recalled while the vale stands whole. It will come due when the marked one reads these words.*

Below that, in different ink—older, browner, pressed hard enough to emboss the page—a single line:

*The marked one will know the day. It is written in the margin.*

Oscar turned the page. And stopped breathing.

There, in the outer margin of the following leaf, faded almost to nothing, a circle drawn in ink the color of dried blood. Inside the circle, small and cramped and unmistakably the same hand:

*The twenty-ninth of Harvestmoon. At moonset, the debt wakes. Let none of my blood read past this page before that day, lest the reading itself complete the marking.*

And beside the circle, in letters so faint he had to tilt the page toward the window's grey light to catch them, catching the fibers of the paper like frost catching grass:

*Sandoval, Oscar.*

He read it four times. Five. He counted the letters of his own name with the point of a pencil, as though repetition might dissolve it, as though it might turn out to be someone else's name wearing his shape. It did not. It was his. Every letter. The middle initial he'd always hated—*O.S.,* the initials carved into the handle of his first graver, the initials his uncle had chosen because they were also the initials of the guild's founder, a coincidence he'd laughed about at fourteen—

*O.S.*

He sat down on the reading stool because his legs had quietly resigned. The archive was silent around him except for the tick of the case clock and, beneath the clock, beneath the stone, beneath everything—the murmur. Present even here. Even in this room of dead paper, the dead were murmuring. And now, for the first time in his life, he strained to hear them differently. Not as weather. As testimony.

*Moonset,* something said, far down. *Moonset. Before the moon sets.*

Today was the twenty-seventh of Harvestmoon.

Two days.

He checked the almanac chained to the reading desk, because checking facts was the only ritual he had against panic. Full moon fell tomorrow night. On the twenty-ninth, the moon would rise late and set in the hour before dawn—at 4:51 in the morning, the almanac said, in its flat printed certainty, as though the exact minute of a soul's waking were a matter of public record.

He closed the book. He closed it gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping, because some animal part of him had decided the book was alive, was listening, was *waiting*—had been waiting a hundred and four years, and forty-one of those waiting specifically, patiently, for him.

*Let none of my blood read past this page before that day.*

Too late. He had read past the page. He was reading past the page right now, standing in the margin of his own life like a man standing in wet cement.

He thought about putting the book back. About climbing the ladder, slotting it between the swollen hymnal and the tax rolls, climbing down, locking the archive, walking home, carving Aldous Merrick's replacement letter, living. The thought was seductive and total: he could feel its whole shape, the ordinary life continuing inside it like a fly in amber. Nothing had to change. Nothing *ever* changed in Thornvale. That was the valley's whole promise, the promise his family had paid—God, had his family *paid*—to keep.

But the voice in the workshop had known. Before he'd ever touched the book, something had said his name. Which meant the knowing had not come from the reading. Which meant the reading had not caused this. Which meant—

Which meant the marking had been done long ago, and today was simply the day the seal broke, and whether he read the book or burned it or carried it to the top of Mount Cindrel and threw it into the crater, the debt was already awake, already turning over in its sleep, already opening one pale eye.

He wrapped the book in the linen cloth kept in the reading desk for maps, tied it with the cord, and tucked it under his arm. Taking it was theft, technically—guild property, archive property—but the alternative was leaving his own name sitting in the dark on a high shelf, circled in fading ink, waiting. He could not do it. He could no more leave it than he could leave a child crying in a well.

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at the room: the cold shelves, the sleeping fox, the clock ticking off the hours of the twenty-seventh with perfect indifference.

"Moonset," he said to the empty air, quietly, testing the word in his own mouth for the first time. "Before the moon sets."

Underneath him, beneath the flagstones, beneath the hill, beneath the roots of everything—the murmur shifted. He felt it the way you feel a current change under a boat. A hundred fragments rearranging themselves, and among them, distinct now, patient, horribly gentle, a voice he knew better than any voice in the world, a voice that had been dead for nine years and had apparently been waiting just as long as the book had:

*"Come home, little owl,"* it said. *"You need to see your aunt."*

He stood in the doorway a moment longer, the wrapped book against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

Then he stepped out into the grey morning, and went to find Maren.
