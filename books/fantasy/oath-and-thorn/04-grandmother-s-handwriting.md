## 4. Grandmother's Handwriting

Oscar did not sleep.

He sat at his workbench with the prophecy book open before him, the candle burned down to a stub that guttered every time the draft slipped under the chapel door, and he told himself he was being careful. Methodical. The way he was with bone—measuring twice before the first cut of the saw, because bone forgave nothing and neither did he.

But the truth was simpler and worse: he was afraid to touch the page again.

The book lay where Wren Ashgrave had left it on the archive table, its cover the color of old ash, its spine cracked in three places like a riverbed in drought. *The Vellum Hours*, someone had written across the flyleaf in a hand so faded it seemed less ink than memory. A century old, Wren had said. A hundred years of sitting in the dark beneath the chapel, waiting for a date that hadn't existed until this morning, when Oscar had opened it and found today circled in brown ink beside his own name.

*Sandoval, Oscar.* Not even his full name. Just enough to find him.

The dead murmured in the walls. They always murmured—the chapel had been built over the old burial ground, and the bones below were never entirely quiet—but tonight their voices had changed register. Softer. Closer. Like people leaning in through a window to watch something happen.

He made himself turn the page.

* * *

The handwriting was the first thing he noticed properly, now that he was looking for something other than his own name. It slanted left, against the grain of most hands, and the letters were tall and narrow, crowded together as though the writer resented the space each one required. The ascenders were sharp. The descenders hooked like talons. Where the ink had pooled at the ends of strokes, it had dried into small dark stars.

Oscar knew that hand.

Not from letters—Ilsa Sandoval had never sent a letter in her life, or if she had, none had survived her going. He knew it from somewhere humbler and closer: the inside covers of cookbooks, the margins of hymnals, the backs of seed packets pinned to the kitchen wall of the house where Maren still lived. Ilsa had annotated everything. Recipes with corrections. Prayers with objections. *Too much salt. This verse is a lie. Plant after the frost, not before, no matter what the packet says.*

His grandmother had been dead to Thornvale for thirty years—not dead dead, which would have been easier, but gone, walked out of the valley one autumn morning with a satchel and her good boots and never come back. The official story, the story Maren told with a closed face, was that she'd gone to live with cousins in the lowlands. Children had been told that for decades. Children had grown up and told it to their own children.

Oscar looked at the tall, narrow, left-slanting hand crowding the margins of a hundred-year-old prophecy, and felt the floor tilt under him.

"No," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange in the empty chapel, too loud, too young.

He went to the archive's back shelf, where the parish kept its oldest records behind a curtain of mildewed velvet, and pulled the ledger of household births. His own family's entries went back six generations. And there, in the column for annotations—because Ilsa had been the one who recorded the births, in the years before she left, because she'd been the sort of woman who could not leave a margin blank—

There it was. The same hand. The same hooked descenders, the same sharp ascenders, the same little dark stars where the ink had pooled.

*Born under a waning moon,* she had written beside his father's entry. *This will matter later.*

Oscar sat down on the cold stone floor with the ledger in his lap and laughed once, a sound with no humor in it at all, and then he carried both books back to the table and began, very carefully, to read everything his grandmother had ever written in the margins of a book that should not have known his name.

* * *

The prophecy itself was nonsense—or rather, it was the kind of nonsense that only revealed its sense too late. Verses about a valley without magic, a mountain that hungered, a debt written in blood before blood existed. Wren had read them aloud in the archive earlier that evening in a flat, careful voice, watching him the whole time, and Oscar had heard only the shape of madness. But Ilsa's marginal notes were another language entirely. They were practical. Furious. Increasingly afraid.

In the margin beside the verse about the mountain's hunger, she had written: *It is not a metaphor. It eats. I have seen the ledger.*

Beside the verse about the valley's sanctuary: *Bought, not blessed. There is a difference, and the difference has a price, and the price is coming due.*

And beside the circled date—today's date, a hundred years ahead of her own hand—she had written, in ink so fresh-looking it might have dried yesterday:

*If you are reading this, Oscar, then the debt has found you, and you have found this book, and both of those things mean there is still time.*

Oscar's hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the table until the trembling transferred to the wood, to the candle, to the small pool of light around him, and the shadows beyond it leaned in like an audience.

"How," he whispered. "How could you know my name. You left before I was born."

The dead in the walls stirred. One voice, older than the others, dry as leaf-mold, said: *She knew all of your names. She wrote them down before she went. She said the mountain takes what is promised, and promises are made of names.*

"Which dead are you?" Oscar asked, because it mattered, because knowing whose whisper it was had always been the first courtesy of his trade.

*A carpenter,* the voice said. *I built the shelves she hid this book behind. She paid me in bread and questions. She asked me what I remembered of the old bargain, and I told her, and she wept, and then she stopped weeping and started writing.*

Oscar closed his eyes. In the dark behind his lids he could see her—vaguely, the way you see someone you met when you were four: a tall woman, gray braid, hands stained with ink instead of bone-dust. She had lifted him once. He had a single memory of being held above her head while she laughed, and the memory might not even be real; memories that young are often built later, from photographs and other people's stories. But the laugh—he thought the laugh was real.

"You knew," he said to the margins, to the hooked letters, to the woman who had walked out of the valley thirty years ago and left this behind like a letter mailed with a century's delay. "You knew what she'd done. What *I* was."

He turned pages faster now, past verses, past her annotations, hunting. Somewhere in this book there had to be more than dread. Dread was cheap. Dread was what you felt standing at a graveside. His grandmother had not been a woman who stopped at feeling things.

Near the end of the book, on a page whose verse described the court beneath Mount Cindrel in words that made the candle flame shrink, he found it.

* * *

*The debt can be voided,* Ilsa had written, and the letters here were larger, pressed harder into the paper, as though she had needed the pen to shout. *Not paid. Paid only feeds it. Voided. A contract dies when its terms are broken by the party who holds the greater claim, and the court's claim on a soul is great but not greatest. Read the clause. Read the clause they hope you never reach.*

Below that, smaller, hurried:

*Moonset on the marked date. You must be beneath the mountain before moonset on the marked date, and you must be reading, and you must be willing. Willingness is the hinge everything turns on. They cannot take what is given freely, and they cannot refuse what is offered freely—it is the one law older than their law. Remember this, Oscar. Whatever they offer you, whatever they threaten, the answer is in the giving, not the keeping.*

And below that, in ink that had gone brown and thin, almost an afterthought, almost a whisper:

*I tried to go myself. Thirty years ago. I got as far as the gate and my heart failed me—not with fear. With arithmetic. I calculated what my death would buy and what it would cost the ones I loved, and love made a coward of me, and I turned back, and I have hated myself every day since with a cleanliness that is almost a comfort.*

*I am going again. If these pages end here, then I did not reach the court in time, or I did not reach it at all, and the debt passed down the blood to you. Forgive me. I did not sell you. I bought you a valley, and the valley was worth it, and I would do it again, and that is the worst thing about me.*

Oscar read the last line eleven times.

Then he read it aloud, because some things need air to become true: *"I did not sell you."*

The candle gutted. The dead murmured assent, or pity, or simply the restless shifting of voices that have waited a long time for a living man to understand something.

He understood, and understanding hurt in a way he had no craft for. He could mend a cracked femur, could carve a name into a skull-plate so fine it would outlast the stone that covered it, could ease a dying man's last hour with the right words gathered from the right dead. But there was no tool in his kit for this—for learning that the grandmother who abandoned his family had actually spent thirty years trying to un-abandon them, that her vanishing had been a campaign, that her silence had been strategy, that the wound his mother had carried to her grave—*she left us, she chose the road over us*—had perhaps never been a wound Ilsa meant to give at all.

Or perhaps it had. Perhaps love and abandonment had worn the same boots that morning. He would never ask her. That was the arithmetic grief always came back to: never, never, never.

Unless.

His eyes went back to the clause. *Read the clause they hope you never reach.* He paged forward, past Ilsa's last note, into the legal matter at the book's end—the dense, cramped text of the original covenant, copied out in a different hand, a clerk's hand, patient and terrible. Most of it was archaic formulae, invocations of powers whose names had been scratched out by some cautious priest a lifetime ago. But near the bottom, half-hidden by a blot of wax, ran a passage in the clerk's hand that made Oscar's blood move strangely:

*...and should the pledged soul pass, prior to settlement, beneath the eaves of the court in willingness, bearing knowledge of these terms, then the contract shall stand open to renegotiation between the bearer and the crown of Cindrel, and no power of the valley, nor oath, nor ward, nor blood, shall bar the bearer's passage either down or up.*

Open to renegotiation. Not freedom. Not safety. Just—a door. A door that swung both ways, and stood open only until moonset, tonight, and led down into a court that ate souls the way his valley grew apples.

Wren had told him what waited below. She had told him in the flat voice of a woman reciting her own crimes: the veined courtiers with their marble skin, the tolls paid in memory, the Regent on a throne of fused vertebrae, the holding dark where the pledged waited out their centuries. She had told him and then she had said, quietly, "You don't have to go. There's no shame in letting the valley keep you. That's what it was bought for."

But the valley had been bought with *him*. With his soul, signed away before his birth by a woman who had then spent her remaining years trying to claw the signature back—and failed, and failed, and finally walked into the mountain to try in person, and never come out.

Oscar looked at the circled date. The ink had faded to the color of dried blood, but the circle itself was drawn twice, firmly, the way you circle a thing when you intend to keep an appointment.

Outside the chapel windows, the sun was setting over Thornvale, gilding the orchards, the slate roofs, the bone-gate with its carved saints. Somewhere out there Maren was lighting lamps and telling herself her nephew was safe behind the boundary line, where magic died at the edge of the fields like frost at a windowpane. Safe. Kept. Bought and paid for.

"I'm going," Oscar said—to the margins, to the dead, to the woman with the gray braid whose laugh he might be remembering wrong. "You drew me a map. It's a terrible map. Half of it's warnings and the other half's apologies. But it's a map."

The candle chose that moment to die, and in the sudden dark the dead spoke clearly, many voices at once, and for the first time in his life they were not fragments. They were a chorus, and the chorus said:

*Hurry.*

A knock fell on the chapel door—three blows, unhurried, deliberate. Through the wood, in a voice roughened by long roads and older regrets, Wren Ashgrave said:

"The moon's already climbing, carver. We leave within the hour, or we don't leave at all."
