## 5. Leaving Thornvale

He did not sleep.

Oscar lay on his cot in the workroom above the chapel, listening to the valley breathe. That was the thing about Thornvale that no visitor ever understood—it breathed. The pines exhaled in long slow sighs along the ridgeline. The river muttered to itself over its stones like an old man rehearsing a grievance. Even the earth had a voice here, low and mineral and patient, the sound of roots drinking in the dark.

And the dead—of course—the dead murmured through all of it, soft as ash settling.

He had spent twenty-six years learning to live beneath that murmur the way other men learned to live beneath rain. You stopped hearing it after a while. Or rather, you heard it so constantly it became a kind of silence, a texture, the way a man born by the sea stops noticing the waves. He carved bones to their whispers. He ate supper to them. He had courted a miller's daughter once with the dead humming through every word he said, and when she left him—when she said, *Oscar, you're never entirely here*—he had not even blamed her. How could he be entirely anywhere, with a hundred voices threaded through his skull like weevils in grain?

But tonight the murmur would not settle into texture. Tonight it was *loud*, and getting louder, and it was not the usual fragments. Not the old complaints of grandfathers who wanted their headstones straightened, or the small griefs of mothers who'd died before saying what they meant to say. Tonight the dead were saying his name.

*Sandoval.* It came up through the floorboards like water through stone. *Sandoval, Sandoval.*

They had been saying it since moonrise. He knew now why. He had read the page. He had put his finger on the faded circle of ink around today's date, beside his own name, in a book a century older than himself, and something in the act of reading had been like turning a key in a lock he hadn't known was in his chest.

The prophecy book sat on his workbench where he'd left it, wrapped in oilcloth because he could not bear to look at it and could not bear to put it down. His aunt Maren had forbidden him to go to the boundary line. She had stood in her kitchen with flour to her elbows and told him, in the voice she used for stubborn goats and stubborn nephews alike, that he was not to set one foot past the standing stones, and that was final, and she would hear no argument.

She had been crying while she said it. She thought he hadn't seen.

Oscar rose before the sky did. He dressed in the dark, taking only what a carver takes: his knife roll, a skin of water, bread wrapped in cloth, the bone-handled awl his mother had given him at fourteen when his talent first showed itself. And the prophecy book. He hesitated over it a long time, hands hovering, then took it anyway. A wound you carry is still a map.

Maren slept in the back room, breath whistling faintly through her nose, one arm flung out of the blankets the way she'd slept since childhood. Oscar stood in her doorway for the length of ten heartbeats. He memorized her: the gray coming in at her temples, the deep lines the valley had written into her face, year by patient year, like a ledger of its own.

*I'm sorry,* he didn't say. *I have to know.* He said it silently instead, and hoped the dead would carry it to her someday, gently, when she was ready to hear.

Then he went down the stairs, past the workshop with its racks of half-finished memorials—a shepherd's femur awaiting its inscription, a child's small finger-bone polished smooth as a river pebble—and out into the blue-black cold of the last hour of night.

* * *

Thornvale in the hour before dawn was a country made of breath. Mist stood in the fields like congregations. Frost furred the fence rails silver. Oscar walked the cart road east with his collar up and his boots striking sparks of sound off the frozen ruts, and all around him the whispering swelled with every step, as though the whole valley were leaning in to watch him go.

The standing stones stood where they had always stood, at the eastern lip of the valley where the land climbed toward the pass. Twelve of them, granite, older than any record, each taller than two men. The oldest folk called them the Seam. Oscar had played among them as a boy and never once wondered why the moss grew thick on their western faces and thin as memory on their eastern ones. Why the birds went quiet within thirty paces of them. Why, on the day his father died, the whispers in the chapel had gone suddenly, mercifully muffled, as though someone had drawn a curtain between Oscar and the world of voices—and he had understood, even through grief, that the curtain was the valley itself, holding the noise of everything else at bay.

Magic could not enter Thornvale. Everyone said it plainly, the way you'd say *the well is sweet* or *the winters are hard*. Pilgrims came sometimes from beyond the pass, hedge-witches and charm-sellers and the occasional gray-cloaked oath-keeper, and they all said the same thing with wonder or frustration: past those stones, their arts simply stopped. Candles would not divine. Cards would not speak. The gift went out of their hands like heat out of iron.

No one had ever asked what it cost to keep such a place.

Oscar stopped three paces from the stones. His breath hung before him. Beyond them the road climbed through frost-dead grass toward the pass, toward the world, toward Mount Cindrel, whose distant peak was just beginning to catch the first gray light—a smudge of smoke-colored stone against a pearl sky, so far away it looked harmless.

The whispers were screaming now.

Not literally—he knew the difference; he'd heard literal screaming twice in his life and still woke some nights remembering—but the dead pressed against him from every side, dozens of voices braided into one insistent rope, and underneath them, for the first time, he could feel the *other* voices. The ones beyond the seam. Faint, straining, like voices heard through a wall. Like voices calling through deep water.

They were louder than anything inside the valley. They were louder than anything he had ever heard.

And they were all saying his name.

His feet had carried him forward without his permission. Two paces. One. He stood between the third and fourth stones, close enough to touch the granite, and the air here felt strange against his skin—thinner, somehow, the way air feels at the top of a stairwell, charged and waiting.

*Turn back,* whispered the valley dead, and there was love in it, real love, the accumulated tenderness of everyone he had ever carved and mourned for. *Turn back, carver. Stay where it is quiet. Stay where you are safe.*

And for one long moment he almost did.

That was the terrible thing, he thought later. Not the fear. Fear he could have managed. It was the *temptation*—the sheer animal sweetness of the valley, the way it wrapped a man in hush and routine and the small warm certainties of bread and work and Maren's kitchen, so that leaving felt less like courage than like tearing your own skin off. Twenty-six years of protection pressing down on him like snow on a roof, and he could feel how easy it would be to simply let it hold him. To walk home. To carve the shepherd's femur. To grow old listening to gentle ghosts and telling himself the prophecy was ink, only ink, a madwoman's scribble in a dead woman's hand.

Except it wasn't a dead woman's hand. That was the whole horror of it. Ilsa Sandoval had walked out of this valley forty years ago and never come back, and her handwriting waited in his pack like a debt collector's letter.

*You sold me,* he thought at her, at the mountain, at whoever was listening. *Before I drew breath, you sold me, and I'm going to find out why.*

He stepped through the stones.

* * *

It hit him like cold water.

There was no gentler way to describe it, though he tried for years afterward to find one. One stride he stood in the valley's held breath, and the next he was through, and the world *poured* into him—not magic in the way the stories told it, no fire leaping from his fingers, nothing so kind—but everything else. Every voice. Every whisper within a hundred miles, suddenly uncurtained, unfiltered, arriving all at once like a wave breaking over a drowning man.

He went to his knees in the frozen grass.

The dead were deafening. They were everywhere. They were in the ground under him and the wind above him and the road ahead of him, centuries of them, travelers who'd died on the pass and farmers whose names no ledger kept and soldiers from wars Thornvale had never heard of, and none of them were murmuring anymore. Inside the valley they had been ash-sounds, softened, domesticated. Out here they were raw. They were *hungry*. They crowded against the walls of his skull demanding to be heard, demanding to be remembered, and Oscar clutched his own head and made a sound he was ashamed of, high and thin, a boy's sound, while the frost soaked through his trousers and the sun crept up behind Mount Cindrel and turned the whole eastern sky the color of an open wound.

*Stop. Stop. Stop.* He said it aloud, uselessly, and then—because he was a carver, because his whole life had been an education in listening—he did the only thing he knew how to do with overwhelming voices.

He listened *properly*. He opened himself the way he opened himself over a fresh bone, letting the whispers sort themselves, finding the grain of them, the structure. And slowly, agonizingly, the roar resolved into layers. Near voices and far voices. Old griefs and new. Underneath it all, a vast low drone he suspected was the mountain itself, Cindrel humming to itself like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

He knelt in the grass until the sun cleared the ridge and the frost began to smoke off the fields below him, and by degrees he learned to stand inside the noise the way a man learns to stand in a river—braced, bent, but standing.

When he finally looked up, wiping his face with a sleeve that was more ice than wool, the pass stretched away before him, narrow and stony, climbing toward a saddle of bare rock. Somewhere beyond it, days away, the bone courts waited with his soul in their ledgers.

He should go back. The thought arrived calm and reasonable, wearing Maren's voice. Go back, tell her everything, let the valley keep him. Let the debt come due however debts come due. At least he would be warm. At least he would be *quiet*.

He was already turning when he heard her.

*Oscar.*

Not a whisper. Not one voice among hundreds. It cut through the chorus like a blade through ribbon, clear and close and unmistakable, and every other voice fell silent as if a room had emptied for it.

His mother had been dead eleven years. Fever, the winter he was fifteen. He had carved her memorial bone himself, kneeling in this same workshop, weeping onto the sanded surface until Maren had taken the tools from his hands and finished the inscription for him, because his tears were warping the letters. In eleven years he had never once heard her among the murmurs. He had told himself that was mercy. He had told himself the dead who loved you best stayed quietest, so as not to make the living ache.

*Mama?* He said it aloud, into the empty morning, and hated how young he sounded.

*Look at me,* she said—or the memory of her, or whatever the dead keep of themselves when the body is long gone. He looked, though there was nothing to see but frost and stone and light. But in the way of these things, in the way of the gift he had never asked for and never understood, he *did* see her: a woman with flour on her hands and her sleeves rolled up, standing in a kitchen full of morning, smiling at him with the particular smile she saved for when he'd done something brave and stupid at the same time.

*You're going the wrong direction,* she said.

*Everyone says turn back.*

*Everyone in there loves you,* she agreed, and he felt her glance fall backward, toward the valley, toward the seam and the stones and the sleeping aunt he'd slipped away from. *Love makes poor directions. Your grandmother loved this valley enough to sell you for it. Look how that turned out.*

*I don't even know if it's true. The prophecy. The debt. Maybe it's—*

*Oscar.* Gentle. Firm. The voice that had ended a thousand childhood arguments with a single word. *I died in that valley. I was born in that valley, and I will lie in its ground until the ground itself ends, and I am telling you: go. Whatever waits on that mountain, it has been waiting for you longer than you've been alive. You cannot outrun a thing by standing still.*

*What if I can't do it? What if I get there and I'm not—*

*You listen better than anyone I ever knew,* she said, and there was no doubt in her at all, which undid him more than any comfort could have. *You listened to me for fifteen years and heard things I never said out loud. Now go and listen to the dead, my love. All of them. Even the ones under the mountain. Especially those.*

The presence thinned like mist in sun. He felt her going and reached for her the way a drowning man reaches, and caught only the last of it, fading, warm:

*Take the left fork at the shrine. The right one floods.*

And then she was gone, and the chorus rushed back in around the space she'd left, and Oscar Sandoval knelt alone on the far side of the seam with tears freezing in his beard and the sun full on his face.

He got up. He brushed the frost from his knees. He took out the bread and ate it standing, because his mother had never allowed self-pity on an empty stomach, and then he drank, and then he shouldered his pack with the prophecy book riding against his spine like a second heartbeat.

The pass waited. The mountain waited. Somewhere in the galleries of the world below it, a ledger waited with his name in it.

*Left fork at the shrine,* he reminded himself, and set his boots on the road out of Thornvale, walking away from the only quiet he had ever known, toward the deafening morning—with his mother's voice going with him, the one voice the dead could not take back, steady as a lamp in all that noise.

Behind him, so faint he might have imagined it, the valley sighed.

Ahead, on the wind off the pass, something answered—a sound too deliberate to be echo, too patient to be bird. A knock, almost. Stone on stone. As if something far up the trail had heard him coming, and was counting his footsteps home.
