## 6. The Toll Road

Wren had forgotten how loud the world was.

Not the sound of it—wind in the pines, gravel under boots, the distant argument of crows. She had lived with that kind of noise for thirty years and knew its grammar by heart. It was the other noise she'd lost: the hum of magic threading through everything beyond the valley line, the way it pressed against her skin like a second atmosphere, thick and sweet and faintly rotten, like fruit left too long in the sun. Thornvale's silence had spoiled her. Three days of walking beside the hearing carver through country where nothing listened back had made her almost comfortable.

She caught herself humming an old court-song on the morning of the fourth day and stopped so abruptly that Oscar stumbled into her back.

"What?" he asked. He carried a carver's satchel slung across his chest, bone-handled tools clicking softly against one another with every step. He touched them often, she'd noticed. The way other men touched talismans.

"Nothing." She adjusted the strap of her own pack and kept walking. "It's an old song. Not fit for open roads."

"Is anything?"

He said it lightly, but Wren heard the edge beneath it—the same edge that had been sharpening since they crossed out of Thornvale at dawn three days ago. The boy had walked into the world expecting silence and found instead a roar. He hadn't slept well since. She'd watched him lie awake by their fires, hands pressed flat over his ears, jaw tight, listening to whatever the dead outside the valley were saying to him. Out here there were more of them. So many more. Battlefields and plague pits and nameless graves along the roadside, all of them murmuring, and only one boy alive who could hear every word.

"You need to eat," she said. "And you need to listen to me while you do."

"I'm always listening."

"To them. Now listen to me instead." She found a flat stone beside the road—a pilgrim marker, once carved with blessings, worn to a smooth grey tongue—and sat down on it, unpacking bread and dried plums. The pilgrim road ran straight ahead of them through the high passes, a pale scar winding toward the black shoulder of Mount Cindrel, which stood on the horizon like something waiting. "We have maybe two days before we reach the shadow of the mountain. Before that, there are tollmen on this road, and before *that*, you're going to learn the two rules that keep people alive below."

Oscar sat across from her. Up close, in daylight, he looked younger than his years and older than his blood—there was something in his face of his grandmother Ilsa, though he didn't know it yet. Or perhaps he suspected. He'd spent the whole walk turning the prophecy book's pages in his mind; she could see him doing it, the way his lips moved sometimes without sound.

"Rules," he said. "Go on."

"One." She held up a finger. "Never give your true name. Not to a courtier, not to a gatekeeper, not to a beggar who looks too long at your mouth when you speak. In the courts beneath Cindrel, a name is a handle. Give them yours and they can lift you by it. They can turn you, carry you, set you down wherever they please. You become theirs in the way a cup becomes theirs—something owned, something *used*."

"And what do I give instead?"

"A use-name. Something small and dull. I traveled under *Sparrow* for six years and no one ever lifted me by it." She almost smiled at the memory, then remembered why she no longer had a name worth protecting, and the smile died where it started. "Two. Never accept a gift. Nothing. Not a coat against the cold, not a crust of bread, not a compliment offered as though it were one. A gift freely given creates a debt, and debts are the only law the mountain recognizes. They will offer you things precisely so you'll owe them. The Regent's entire court is built on the architecture of obligation. Every soul in those halls is chained by some kindness someone did them forty years ago."

Oscar chewed his bread slowly. "My grandmother," he said. "The book says she traded my soul. Was that a gift or a bargain?"

Wren looked at him. He met her eyes steadily, the way he did everything—without flinching, which was either courage or a carver's professional detachment from the dead, and she suspected it was both.

"It was a bargain," she said. "A bad one, but a bargain. There's a difference. A gift is a hook dressed as kindness. A bargain, at least, lets you see the hook." She paused. "Though I'll tell you now, and don't take it as kindness because I won't have you owing me—Ilsa Sandoval got a better price than anyone I've ever known to walk that road. A whole valley's sanctuary for one soul not yet born. The court thought it was robbing her. I've begun to wonder if it was the other way around."

"The dead say things about her." Oscar turned a plum in his fingers, not eating it. "Since we crossed the line, they say more. Fragments. *The woman who wrote.* *She went down singing.* Do you know what that means?"

"No," Wren lied, and hated how easily the lie came, thirty years of practice making it smooth as river stone. She did know. Everyone who had served the court knew what came down the mountain singing, and what came back up silent, if they came back at all. But telling him would mean answering questions she couldn't answer yet—not until they reached the monastery, not until he understood enough to bear it. "Eat your plum. We move in ten minutes."

* * *

They made the toll bridge at midafternoon, and Wren smelled it before she saw it.

Not smoke, exactly. Not rot. Something between—old smoke soaked into old wood, the smell of a house fire that happened generations ago and never fully ended. She stopped at the crest of the hill above the river crossing and put a hand out to halt Oscar, though he had already gone rigid beside her.

Below, the pilgrim road narrowed onto a stone bridge spanning a brown, fast-moving river. On the far side stood a tollhouse, and before the tollhouse stood four men.

They looked, at first glance, like any tollmen on any pilgrim road: rough-cloaked, stave-armed, positioned where the road gave travelers no choice but to stop. But Wren had been a soldier of the mountain's court, and she knew the look of its property when she saw it. These four stood wrong. Too still. Their cloaks hung as though no wind dared touch them. And their eyes—

Their eyes were the worst part. Hollow wasn't the right word; hollow implied emptiness, and these men's eyes were full of something. Full the way a well is full. Dark, deep, and reflecting nothing.

"Hollow-eyed," she murmured. "Servants of the mountain. Don't run. Running marks you as carrying something worth taking."

"What do they want?" Oscar whispered. His hand had drifted to his satchel again, touching bone through leather.

"Toll. Coin, if you have it. But they'll take more than coin if they can." She pulled her hood lower. "Stay behind me. Say nothing. And whatever happens—whatever you hear—do not let them hear you hear it. Do you understand? If they learn what you are, we're finished. Worse than finished. We're inventory."

They descended to the bridge together, boots crunching, and Wren felt the wrongness thicken around them like fog. The river ran loud beneath the stones but the air above it was silent, dead-silent, the way the air inside the valley had been silent—as if the tollmen carried a pocket of the mountain's hunger with them everywhere they went.

"Halt." The nearest tollman raised a stave. His voice was a strange thing, layered—one voice speaking over another voice speaking over another, a chord of voices jammed into one throat. "Toll. What carries? What crosses?"

"Two pilgrims bound for the healing shrines," Wren said, pitching her voice low and bored. She'd rehearsed the accent on the road—lowland, weary, unremarkable. "We carry little. Bread, bandages, prayer ribbons."

The tollman looked at her. Then past her. At Oscar.

And Oscar made a mistake.

It was a small thing—he stepped half forward, the instinctive courtesy of a man raised in a polite valley town, offering himself to be inspected rather than hiding behind a stranger. But the movement brought his face into the light, and Wren saw the tollman's dark-well eyes fix on him and *dilate*, the way a cat's pupils widen in the dark, drinking in something invisible.

"*Hears,*" the tollman said. The layered voice cracked apart on the word, splitting into four throats saying it at once, then eight, then a chorus bleeding out of all four bodies standing on the bridge. "*Hears. Hears. The hearing one walks. The ledger calls. Bring the hearing one home.*"

"Move," Wren breathed, and drew her knife.

What happened next took eleven seconds, and she would spend years replaying every one of them. The tollmen moved fast—faster than men should, joints bending in sequences that made her stomach lurch—and the first stave came down toward Oscar's head. Wren caught it on her forearm, felt the crack of wood against bone, twisted, and drove her knife into the tollman's thigh. He didn't bleed. That was the detail that haunted her afterward: no blood. Just a thin wisp of grey vapor rising from the wound, like breath escaping a sealed room.

Oscar screamed—but not from the blow, and not from fear.

Wren heard it too, then. Once the skin of the world had been broken open by violence, the sound poured out of the tollman like water from a split jug. Screaming. Not the man's voice—the voices *inside* him, the stolen ones, packed into his hollow body like corpses in a mass grave. They screamed through his mouth, through his wounds, through his staring eyes, a sound with no direction and no mercy, and Oscar dropped to his knees on the bridge stones with his hands clamped over his ears and *listened*, because that was the curse of him, he couldn't not listen—

"There were—" Oscar's voice tore. "There are *people* in him. Four—no, nine—Wren, there are *nine souls* sewn into this man, they're screaming, they've been screaming for—"

"Get up." She hauled him by the collar as the second tollman closed in. "Get UP—"

She fought the way the court had taught her, ugly and efficient, elbow and heel and knife-edge, and it should have been enough—it *was* enough, she broke the second man's knee and sent the third sprawling off the bridge edge into the river—but the fourth simply walked through her guard and seized Oscar by the throat, lifting him off the stones, and looked deep into his face with those well-deep eyes, and spoke in the layered chorus:

"*Name.*"

"Don't," Wren gasped, blood running from her forearm. "Don't tell it your name—"

But Oscar, hanging in the creature's grip, choking, did something Wren did not expect and would never forget.

He opened his mouth, and instead of screaming or pleading or giving his name, he *spoke to them*. To the nine souls inside the hollow man. His voice came out cracked and strangled but clear:

"You're seen. All nine of you. I hear you. You're not alone in there."

The tollman shuddered.

Every muscle in its stolen body locked at once, and the chorus-voice fractured, and for one impossible moment nine distinct voices rose out of the single throat—nine voices sobbing, crying out, whispering *he hears us, he hears us, after all these years, someone hears us*—and the grip on Oscar's throat went slack.

Wren did not waste the mercy. She dragged Oscar backward off the bridge, up the far bank, and ran.

They ran until the light failed, until the tollhouse was a smudge behind them and the road climbed into pine-dark hills, and then Wren pulled them both off the path into a hollow beneath a lightning-split oak, and only then let herself collapse against the trunk and shake.

* * *

Oscar knelt in the leaves, still shaking, hands pressed over his ears even though the screaming had fallen away with distance—or had it? Wren watched his face and understood, with a slow cold dread, that it hadn't. That somewhere inside him the nine souls were still sounding, faint as a bell heard through a mountain, and would keep sounding forever, because he had heard them and hearing, once given, could not be taken back.

"They weren't men," Oscar said finally. His voice was raw. "Or they were, once. Men. The bodies are men but the men are... gone. Used up. Something wears them."

"The court does that." Wren wrapped her forearm with a strip torn from her cloak. The bone wasn't broken, she thought. Bruised to the marrow, maybe. She'd had worse. "When a soul comes into their keeping—through debt, through bargain, through theft—they don't destroy it. Souls are currency, and currency circulates. They pour harvested souls into servants. Prisoners in living walls. It keeps the souls... fresh. And it makes servants who cannot die until every soul inside them is spent." She tied the knot with her teeth. "I told you the cost of the world outside your valley. This is what I meant. Your Thornvale has no magic, no wonders, no courtiers granting wishes at crossroads. It also has no *this*."

Oscar was quiet a long moment. In the blue dusk, his face was unreadable, and Wren realized she was afraid of what he'd say—that he'd turn back, that he'd demand answers she couldn't give, that he'd break the way the pledged broke.

Instead he said: "Nine souls. In one body. For how long?"

"I don't know. Years. Decades. Some of the hollowed have carried their cargo for a century."

"And they knew I heard them." He looked down at his own hands, turning them over as though seeing them new. "They called me *the hearing one*. They said—the ledger calls. Wren, the tollmen knew my name before I said a word. Not my true name—something else. Something written down." He lifted his eyes to hers, and in them she saw the thing she had feared most since Thornvale's boundary: not fear, but purpose, hardening like cooling metal. "The mountain has been waiting for me. My whole life, it's been waiting."

"Yes," Wren said. There was no point lying anymore; the road itself had testified. "The contract names you. The court feels you coming the way a spider feels the thread go taut. From here to the Gate of Teeth, everything that serves the mountain will know what walks its roads. We have to be faster than their messages. We have to be ghosts."

"Can we be?"

"I was," she said. "Once."

He crawled closer and sat beside her against the split oak, shoulder nearly touching hers, and for a while neither spoke. Somewhere upslope an owl asked its question of the dark. Below, far behind them, the river kept talking to its stones.

"Wren." His voice had changed—quieter, careful. "When that thing grabbed me. I heard the nine. But underneath them, deeper, there was something else. Something the tollman itself was saying, under all the stolen voices. It said, *"so hungry, so hungry, the master is so hungry."*" He turned to look at her. "What's starving down there, Wren? What's under that mountain that eats souls and is still hungry?"

The question landed in Wren's chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling toward a depth she had sworn never to measure. Because she knew. She had stood in the audience hall twenty years ago with an oath-ring burning on her brow and watched the Pale Regent receive the tithe of souls, and she had seen—not clearly, not then, glamour had veiled it—but she had *felt* the hunger radiating off that throne like heat off a forge, vast and patient and bottomless, and she had told herself it was majesty.

It was not majesty.

"I don't know," she said.

This time the lie tasted different. Not smooth. Like ash.

Oscar studied her in the dying light, and she saw—hated—that he heard the untruth in her voice the way he heard everything, effortlessly, helplessly. But he didn't press. He only leaned his head back against the oak and closed his eyes, and after a moment he said, very quietly:

"They're still singing. The nine. Far away now, but... they're not screaming anymore. Since I spoke to them. They're singing." His throat worked. "Nobody ever taught me what to do with that. In Thornvale, the dead just murmur. Out here they *sing*."

Wren looked at him—at this carver of bones, this heir to a bargain struck before his birth, this boy who had freed nine prisoners with five words while a monster held him by the throat—and felt the old guilt shift in her chest, reshaping itself into something sharper and more useful.

Desperation had carried her this far. Purpose would carry her the rest.

"Sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we reach the ruined monastery of Ashfall, and there's something there you need to see. Something your grandmother left behind."

His eyes snapped open. "You *knew*—"

"Tomorrow," Wren said, and lay back against the oak, and stared up through the branches at the first stars, and did not sleep at all.

Far to the north, Mount Cindrel stood black against the stars, and she could have sworn the mountain was listening too.
