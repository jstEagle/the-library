## 7. Ink and Ash

The monastery announced itself as a suggestion rather than a structure. From the road it was only a darkening against the dusk—a shoulder of stone crouched beneath a bald hill, half-swallowed by ivy gone black with the season. As they drew closer, the suggestion became walls, and the walls became ruin: a bell tower snapped off at the waist like a broken finger, its remaining arches open to a sky already filling with cold stars.

"Saint Vessal's," Wren said. "Or what the mountain left of it. Pilgrims used to stop here on the way down to make their confessions before the court heard them." She said it lightly, but her hand had found the knife at her belt again, the way it did whenever she spoke of the mountain's business.

Oscar stood in the doorway and listened.

He had learned in three days on the road that listening was not one thing but many. In Thornvale the dead came to him thin and far away, voices through water. Beyond the valley line they were nearer—closer every mile they walked toward Cindrel—and now, standing in this roofless nave, he could hear them layered like sediment. Old deaths at the bottom, murmuring their worn liturgies. Newer ones above, sharper, still surprised.

And beneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat through stone, something vast and patient that was not a voice at all. The mountain itself, perhaps. Or the thing that lived in it.

"It's empty," Wren said, moving ahead of him through the nave with her lamp raised. "Empty enough. The tollmen won't follow past dark—they don't like holy ground, even ruined holy ground. There's a logic to it even now, see?" She tapped the floor with her boot. "The stones are laid in the old pattern. Warding geometry. Half of it's crumbled, but ruins keep their habits."

Oscar knelt and pressed his palm flat against the floor. Cold seeped up through his skin, and with it a whisper—not words, just the memory of kneeling, ten thousand knees' worth of it, prayer soaked into limestone like tallow into bread. He lifted his hand.

"They prayed here," he said. "For a long time."

"That they did." Wren set the lamp on the altar slab, which alone among the wreckage remained whole, its surface scoured pale by centuries of wind through the broken roof. "There should be cells along the east wall. We'll take the abbot's—it'll have the last of the roof. And there may be a scriptorium."

"A what?"

"Where the monks wrote. Copied books, kept records." She glanced back at him, and in the lamplight her face was careful, too careful. "You said your grandmother came this way. Down the pilgrim road, forty years ago. If she stopped anywhere, she'd have stopped here."

Oscar's chest tightened. He hadn't said it aloud since morning, but Wren had a way of hearing the things he didn't say—the disgraced oath-keeper's habit, he supposed, of reading oaths in the spaces between words.

They found the abbot's cell under a ceiling that still held, most of it, and while Wren built a small fire in the hearth—dry gorse from the hillside, burning fast and sweet—Oscar went looking for the scriptorium.

* * *

It was a long room off the cloister walk, its arched windows filled now with ivy and night. Desks of black oak stood in rows, dusted grey. Along one wall, the book presses had been smashed open long ago by hands looking for anything of value; leather bindings lay split on the floor, their pages fanned out like the wings of drowned moths. Rain had gotten in over the years. Much of it was pulp now, ink bleeding into grey blooms.

But one press, in the corner furthest from the broken window, had survived intact—its iron lock rusted shut, its wood still sound. And when Oscar knelt before it and put his ear against the grain, the way he'd done all his life with bone, he heard something he had never heard from wood before.

Not a voice. A *holding*. The way a reliquary holds. Something inside that door had been loved so fiercely, guarded so deliberately, that the intention had soaked into the timber like smoke into rafters.

"Wren," he called, and his voice came out strange to him.

She appeared in the doorway with the lamp, saw him kneeling, and understood—she was learning to read him almost as fast as he was learning to read the world. She fetched an iron bar from the collapsed refectory and together they broke the lock, the crack of it enormous in the silence.

Inside were six books. Five were psalters, their gilt gone dull, their vellum spotted with mold. The sixth was wrapped in oilcloth tied with wire, and it was not a psalter at all. It was smaller, sewn by hand into covers of grey leather, and when Oscar unwound the wire with fingers that would not quite obey him, the smell that rose from it was ash and lavender and something older underneath—ink, and time, and a woman's life pressed flat between boards.

The first page read, in a hand he knew:

*I left on the morning of the frost. If you are reading this, then either I am dead, or I failed, or both. Forgive the handwriting. My hands have never been steady and they are less so now.*

He knew the hand because he had spent two nights hunched over it by lamplight in chapel archives and roadside inns, comparing it against the prophecy book's cramped marginalia—the same forward lean, the same crossed letters slashed hard as if written quickly or angrily, the same peculiar *g* with its tail curled under like a hook. His grandmother's writing. Ilsa Sandoval, née Voss, who had walked out of Thornvale one autumn and never come home, and whose name no one in the family spoke above a murmur, and whose face Oscar knew only from a single cracked daguerreotype where she sat rigid as furniture beside a man whose eyes were kind.

Wren lowered herself onto a broken desk across from him and said nothing. The fire crackled in the next room. Somewhere in the walls, the dead of Saint Vessal's murmured their old prayers, and Oscar bent his head over his grandmother's words, and read.

*The valley elders told me the bargain was made and could not be unmade. They told me a soul was already promised—that mine would serve, or my daughter's after me, or hers after that. Debts of this kind do not expire. They only wait.*

*I have come to speak with them myself. Not the Regent—I am told the Regent does not receive petitioners. But there are others below, lesser courts, clerks of the ledger. Someone keeps the accounts. Someone must know if a debt can be paid early, or paid differently, or paid in full such that nothing remains to inherit.*

*Maren is nine months old. She sleeps in the crook of my arm as I write this, and I am ashamed to say I write faster when she does, because when she wakes I cannot think of anything but the weight of her, and how the whole of the world's arithmetic seems obscene measured against it.*

Oscar stopped. Read the sentence again. Maren. His aunt Maren, who had barred the door of the house when Wren first knocked, who had known about the prophecy all his life and carried it alone, who had gripped his arms at the valley line and said *you come back, you hear me, you come back*—Maren had been an infant in Ilsa's arms when Ilsa walked down this road.

She had been walking toward the mountain holding the knowledge that her own granddaughter might be taken for it.

"You didn't know," Wren said quietly. It wasn't a question. "About Maren being mentioned in it."

"No." Oscar's throat was tight. "Aunt Maren never—" He stopped. Started again. "She never talks about her mother. When I asked as a boy, she said she died in the low country. Fever." He looked down at the journal. "This says she meant to come back."

*Keep reading,* said a voice near his knee—one of the monastery dead, patient, curious. *Keep going, carver. We remember her. She sat where you sit.*

He flinched. Wren saw him flinch and leaned forward. "What is it?"

"She was here." He touched the desk beneath him. "The monks—whatever was left of them, whatever passed through—remember her sitting right here. Reading. Writing." He laughed, and it came out wrong, wet. "Forty years, and the room remembers her better than her own family does."

* * *

They read by turns as the fire burned down. Wren took the middle passages while Oscar rested his eyes and fed the fire, and her voice—low, roughened by exile, made for swearing oaths and reciting law—was strangely gentle with the words.

*Third day. The tollmen took my boots and my ring but let me keep the journal when I showed them the ward-mark on the cover. Even thieves fear the written word down here. I begin to understand why. In the low country everything is owned. Every field, every face, every breath has a lien against it somewhere in a ledger I cannot see.*

*A woman at the well in Harrow's Ford asked me for a blessing. I gave her bread instead. She wept. I did not understand until W—until someone explained that kindness freely given is so rare below the mountain that it reads as madness.*

Oscar looked up sharply at the dash. "*W*," he repeated. "Someone whose name started with W."

Wren had gone very still. The firelight moved on her face and she let it, saying nothing, and after a moment Oscar turned back to the page—but he filed it away, the way he filed everything now, the way the dead taught him: carefully, in order, waiting.

The later entries darkened. The handwriting changed too—the forward lean becoming a lurch, the angry slashes deepening until they tore the paper in places.

*Ninth day. I have seen what they take. Not metaphor. I saw a man in the archive galleries carrying coals with no face at all—just smooth skin where a face should be, like an egg. The clerks call them the Hollowed. They were people once. People who owed.*

*Eleventh day. I asked a clerk directly: can a debt be voided? She smiled at me the way a spider is smiling when it waits. She said: all debts can be voided, madam. The question is whether the payer survives the payment.*

*Fourteenth day. They have told me what the court wants. Not my soul. Souls are currency to them, common coin. What the Pale Regent covets is rarer. The clerk would not say what. She only looked at my ears when she said it. My EARS. I have not slept. I keep thinking of Maren's small ears, how they go pink when she cries.*

Oscar's blood went cold. He thought of the Pale Regent's offer, whispered through the dark of the holding cells three nights from now—or rather, he couldn't think of it yet, because it hadn't happened, because he was still two days from the gate of teeth—but he knew, somehow, with the certainty of the dead, that the clerk's smile and the Regent's covetous gaze and his own cursed gift were all one thread, and that his grandmother had felt the first pull of it forty years before he was born.

"What did they want from her?" he whispered. "Her hearing? Did she have the gift too?"

"I don't know," Wren said. But she said it too evenly, the way people say things they have decided not to know.

*Sixteenth day. I understand now why the valley was founded magic-free. It is not sanctuary FROM the mountain. It is a blindfold. Inside Thornvale, the mountain cannot smell us. Cannot count us. The bargain was this: the court takes no one from the valley, and in exchange the valley stays deaf and dumb and blind, generation upon generation, so that no gift will ever wake there that the court might covet.*

*Except one woke in me. Small, half-formed—I only ever caught whispers, and I told myself they were grief. But the clerk heard it. The clerk SMELLED it. And now the bargain is at risk, because I brought the very thing the sanctuary was built to prevent, down the pilgrim road, straight to their door.*

*So I have made them a new offer. Let the old bargain stand, and let my granddaughter's line bear the mark instead—*

Here the entry stopped mid-sentence. Not ended. Stopped. The ink trailed off into a blot where the pen had rested too long, pressing a small dark crater into the page.

Below it, a new entry began, in handwriting gone suddenly calm, suddenly neat, as if written by a different person entirely—or by the same person after passing through some country from which one returns changed.

*Last entry. To whoever finds this—I hope it is family—I have done a foolish and possibly unforgivable thing, and I have done it for love, which is the only excuse anyone down here ever gives, and the only one the mountain accepts.*

*I have signed. The details are in the ledger below and I will not write them here, because the paper of this journal travels with me and I do not trust any surface under this sky. But hear me, whoever you are: if a date is ever named, if a debt comes due, there is a way to break it. Not to pay it—to BREAK it. The court fears one thing above all things, though they would starve before admitting it. Give them that thing, freely, and the contract burns.*

*What the thing is, I cannot say. Saying it names it, and naming it gives it to them. You will know. If it comes to you—if you are the one reading this on the appointed day—you will KNOW, the way I knew, and you will be more afraid than you have ever been in your life, and you will do it anyway.*

*Tell Maren I am sorry. Tell her the fever story was kinder than the truth. Tell her I chose her over coming home, and I would choose it again, and that choosing it broke me in a way I do not regret.*

*The rest is torn out. I tore it out myself, this evening, by candlelight. Some pages are not for keeping. Some pages are instructions, and instructions can be stolen.*

*Go home. Go home. Go ho—*

The final word cut off at the edge of the page, the last stroke dragged long, as if the pen had been pulled away mid-letter.

* * *

Silence held the scriptorium for a long time. The fire in the next room had burned to embers; neither of them had tended it. Outside, wind moved through the broken arches of Saint Vessal's, playing the ruined tower like a flute, and high above the bald hill the moon was rising—two days from full, two days from setting on the appointed hour.

Oscar closed the journal with hands that trembled and did not understand why, until he realized it was grief. Plain, ordinary grief, arriving forty years late—for a woman he had never met, whom he now knew the way you know someone through a wall: by the shape of what passes between.

She had chosen Maren. She had chosen the valley. She had signed herself into the dark so that a baby girl would never be taken from her cradle, and she had torn out the pages that explained the breaking of the bargain, and she had died down there—somewhere below, in the galleries of the Hollowed—with her last written word unfinished.

"She knew," he said at last. His voice was gravel. "Whatever the Regent wanted—she figured it out, and she protected it anyway, even from this book. Because she knew the book could be stolen." He looked up at Wren. "The pages. Torn out. Where would she have kept them?"

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had the flat care of an oath-keeper stating fact.

"If she was smart, somewhere the court would never search. Somewhere sacred to them, or worthless to them." Her eyes went to the reliquary press, to the five mold-spotted psalters, and something moved behind her expression—calculation, dread, and beneath it a current of old guilt running fast. "The archive below holds everything the court has ever seized. Everything taken from travelers. Everything surrendered by the dead."

"The ledger hall," Oscar breathed. The place his own name waited in ink, one soul, held in trust. "She hid them in the archive. With her signature. With the contract."

"Maybe." Wren stood, joints cracking, and picked up her knife from where she'd laid it. "Or maybe she hid them somewhere on this road, and we've already walked past them. Either way—" She looked at the torn stubs of pages deep in the journal's spine, ragged-edged, deliberate, and her jaw tightened. "Either way, we're going to have to go down and find them. Into the archive. Past the clerks. Before moonset tomorrow night."

Oscar looked down at the journal in his lap—at his grandmother's hooked *g*, at the crater where her pen had pressed too hard, at the unfinished word *go* reaching off the edge of the world.

*We remember her,* whispered the monastery dead, gentle as ash falling. *She read by this light. She wept by this light. And when she left, carver, she left something behind in the altar stone—something small, something she said was for whoever came next.*

Oscar's head came up.

"The altar," he said, already rising. "Wren. She left something in the altar."
