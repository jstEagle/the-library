## 8. The Court of Small Mercies

The town announced itself before we saw it.

First there was the smell—woodsmoke and tallow and something underneath both, a faint sweetness like fruit going bad in a cellar. Then the sound, which was the absence of one. No dogs barking at our approach. No children shrieking in the lanes. Just the creak of a mill turning somewhere out of sight and, beneath it, so low I might have imagined it, a hum that lived in my teeth.

"They call it Mercy's Rest," Wren said, reining her mule to a stop on the last rise before the valley opened below us. "The Court of Small Mercies, some say. It sits on the mountain's borderland, close enough to feel its breath but not close enough for the court to bother ruling it outright. They govern themselves." She said *govern* the way a person says *rot*.

I looked down at it: a scatter of grey roofs around a market square, ringed by fields that grew more stone than grain. Above it all, Mount Cindrel shouldered into the clouds, its summit lost in a permanent bruise-colored haze. The mountain did not look like a place. It looked like a presence—a thing crouched over the town with its chin on its paws, watching.

"The whispers are louder here," I said.

"Of course they are. We're outside Thornvale now." Wren glanced at me sidelong. "How loud?"

I didn't answer right away, because the honest answer would have frightened her, and I wasn't sure I wanted to see what fear looked like on a face that had been nothing but flint since the toll road. Since the tollmen. Three days had passed and I still heard them at night—the stolen souls screaming inside those hollow-eyed men, screaming without mouths, without lungs, without end. I had learned to sleep by wrapping my cloak around my head as if cloth could muffle the dead. It couldn't. Nothing could. In Thornvale, the whispers had been a brook murmuring behind a wall. Out here they were a river in flood, and every soul that had ever died unquiet within fifty leagues seemed determined to tell me about it.

"Loud," I said finally.

Wren nodded slowly, and I saw her file it away, the way she filed everything—her face shuttering, her hands going still on the reins. She'd been different since the tollmen. Quieter. Watching me the way you watch a wound you're afraid has started to fester.

"Then keep your hood up," she said, and nudged the mule down the slope. "And whatever you hear in that town—however much you hear—don't let it show on your face. The pledged can smell pity. So can their masters."

*Their masters.* I turned the phrase over as we descended, and it sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

* * *

Mercy's Rest smelled worse up close. The sweetness was everywhere—in the gutters, in the doorways, rising off the people themselves—and it took me half a morning to understand what it was. It was the smell of lives being drained. Not blood; something subtler. A soul, I thought, must have a scent the way a candle does when it gutters, all smoke and spent tallow.

The market square was busy enough, in the way a funeral is busy. People moved through their errands with their heads down, buying turnips, mending awnings, hauling water, and none of them spoke above a murmur. Not from fear, exactly—or not only. There was a flatness to them, a worn smoothness, like stones in a riverbed. I watched an old man argue with a fishmonger over the price of eels, and even his anger was quiet, rounded at the edges, as if he'd been arguing for so long that the argument had stopped meaning anything.

And then I saw the children.

They were playing in the shadow of the church—not playing, really, but arranged near each other the way children do, pushing a hoop and chasing it with motions that never quite became laughter. Five of them, maybe six. And around the throat of each one, where another child might wear a ribbon or an amulet, was a thin band of black iron, plain and seamless, fitted so perfectly it might have grown there.

"Wren," I said. My voice came out strange. "The children."

She followed my gaze and her jaw tightened. "Pledged bands," she said. "Iron from the mountain's roots. It doesn't hold them—it marks them. Everyone can see what they owe."

"Owe?" One of the children stumbled; another caught her without a word, without looking, the way you catch someone you've caught a hundred times. "Owe *what?* They're children."

"They were born owing." Wren kept her eyes moving along the rooftops as she talked, low and fast, a merchant's patter meant for no listeners. "Soul-debt runs in the blood here. Grandmother pledged, mother pledged, child born already spent. When they come of age, the court collects. Some go up the mountain to serve in the galleries. Some just—" she made a small gesture with one hand, opening her fingers like a bird taking flight, "—empty. The Regent takes what it needs and leaves the body walking. You've seen the elders here. Now you know why."

I had seen them. All morning I'd been seeing them and calling it old age: the men and women who moved through Mercy's Rest with that terrible smoothness, faces like unsalted bread, eyes that tracked motion without following it. I'd assumed they were tired. Everything about this land was tired—the fields, the sky, the light itself, strained through Cindrel's haze until it fell grey as dishwater.

But tired was a thing that ended in rest. This was different. This was a lamp burning after the oil is gone, fed on something else, something that was never meant to burn.

One of the hollowed elders passed within arm's reach of me, carrying a basket of washing. And because I am what I am—because Thornvale carved the habit into me deeper than bone—I listened.

There was nothing. That was the horror of it. In the valley, the dead whispered, but these people were alive, breathing, walking, and inside them there was only a vast and wind-scoured silence, like a house stripped to the joists. Except—no. Not quite nothing. At the very bottom of that silence, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, something was still whispering. Still *them*. A woman's voice saying a name over and over, *Tomas, Tomas,* as if the name were the last plank of a wrecked ship and holding it was all that kept her from sinking out of herself entirely.

I stepped back. My heel caught a cobblestone and I nearly went down.

"You heard something." Wren had appeared at my elbow, sharp as a blade drawn. "What did you hear?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's what I heard. There's almost nothing left in her."

Something moved behind Wren's eyes—guilt or grief or both wearing the same mask. "Now you understand what your soul is worth," she said quietly. "Not to you. To *them*." She nodded toward the mountain. "Come. We need rooms, and we need them before dark. The pledged are gentle, mostly. But the collectors come at dusk, and they don't like strangers asking questions."

* * *

We took a room above a tavern called the Kindly Hearth, which was neither. The innkeeper took Wren's coin with hands that trembled slightly, and asked no questions, which told me everything: in Mercy's Rest, questions were a luxury taxed higher than beds.

I meant to stay in the room. I want that recorded somewhere, if anything of me is ever recorded—that I meant to keep my hood up and my mouth shut and my gift folded away like a knife I'd promised not to draw. Wren had been clear. The dead here were louder than anywhere she'd seen, she said, because the town sat in the lee of the mountain where every unquiet soul for a hundred years drifted like snow against a fence, and if I opened myself to them even a crack, I'd drown.

But the drowning found me anyway.

It happened near evening, when I went down for water and found the tavern common room filling with the day's last customers. The noise of the living was ordinary enough—mugs, benches, a fiddle tuning in the corner. It was underneath that the other noise began, the way rot begins under paint. Souls. Dozens of them. The tavern stood near the churchyard, and the churchyard was full, and the fullness pressed against the walls like floodwater against a door.

A grandmother complaining that her grandson planted his beans too shallow, year after year, and nobody listened, nobody ever listened. A drowned well-digger describing, patiently, eternally, the exact color of the light at the bottom of the well. A soldier—Cindrel's soldier, veined skin and marble voice still clinging to him like pond water—who simply repeated *the gate remembers* over and over in a cadence that made the hair on my arms rise. And threading through them all, thin as wire: the pledged ones. The emptied ones. The name-sayers and plank-holders, each clutching the last scrap of themselves the way a freezing man clutches a coal.

I gripped the edge of the bar until my knuckles went white. The innkeeper's boy asked if I wanted ale. I heard him through three layers of the dead, and my answer came out wrong, or maybe I didn't answer at all, because the next thing I knew a hand was on my arm—an old woman's hand, light as paper—and an old woman's face was tilted up toward mine.

Her eyes were the worst part. Not empty. *Fighting.* Two little fires in two deep wells, guttering, refusing.

"You're the hearing carver," she said.

The common room went on around us—mugs, benches, fiddle—but I felt the words pass through it like a cold current through warm water. Nearby conversations faltered. Faces turned.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"Yes, you do." Her grip tightened with surprising strength. "The stranger with the valley look. The one who flinches at the churchyard. Word walked ahead of you down the toll road—word always walks here, it's the only thing that moves fast." She drew a breath, and the effort of it bent her forward; I saw then how far gone she was, how little was holding her upright besides will and fury. "My name is Sera Voss. I am dying. Tonight, most likely, or tomorrow. And I have last words, carver. Everyone does. But no one here can hear them." Her voice cracked, and the crack was the truest sound I'd heard in days. "No one has been able to hear anyone for a hundred years."

Behind me, I heard Wren come down the stairs. Heard her stop. Heard the silence where a warning should have been—and the silence itself was a warning, and I ignored it.

"What happens to last words here?" I asked Sera Voss.

"They die with us. Unheard. Unpassed." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, proud and broken at once. "Do you know what that makes a whole town of deaths, carver? It makes them a hundred years of letters written and never delivered. My mother's last words died in her mouth. Her mother's too. And mine will—" her hand spasmed on my arm, "—unless."

The dead in the walls had gone quiet. All of them. Every whisper, every complaint, every patient description of underwater light—stilled, listening, the way a forest goes silent when a wolf crosses the trail. Even the hollowed ones in the corners had lifted their heads, those scraps-of-coal voices straining toward me like plants toward a window.

"Carver." Sera Voss pulled me closer. Her breath smelled of the sweet rot that hung over the whole town. "I have six coppers and a hairpin. It's all they haven't taken. Will that buy me a minute of your gift?"

And the anger came then—not hot, but cold, rising from somewhere below thought, below the marrow. Because she was offering to *buy* it. She had been robbed her whole life—robbed of her neighbors' voices, her mother's blessing, her own death's dignity—and her first instinct, her trained and beaten-in instinct, was to pay. Six coppers and a hairpin for the last words of a human life. The mountain had taught this town to beg for what should have been free as rain.

"No," I said.

Her face began to fold, and I caught her hands—both of them, papery and strong—and held on.

"No," I said again, louder, so the whole room could hear, so the dead in the walls could hear, so the mountain itself could hear if mountains have ears. "You don't pay. Nobody pays. Sit down, Sera Voss. Tell me your last words, and I will carry them wherever they need to go, and I will not take one copper for it, and may God and the Regent both witness that some things in this world are still given and not sold."

I heard Wren exhale behind me—one long, slow breath that might have been despair or might have been relief.

Sera Voss stared at me. The two little fires in her eyes flared, and for one moment she looked young, decades young, a girl standing in an unmown field before the mountain ever cast its shadow across her family.

Then she sat down, and she began to talk.

She talked about her son, who had gone east years ago and never written, and whether that meant anger or forgetfulness, and she needed someone to know she forgave both. She talked about a debt of four silver owed to the Hendersons, hidden under the third floorboard by the hearth, and she would not meet her Maker owing four silver. She talked about the taste of June, actual June, the specific green smell of it, because she feared that after tonight she would be the last person alive who remembered what June tasted like before the haze thickened. She talked for the length of a candle and past it, and the common room stayed silent, the living leaning in though they could hear nothing, and the dead pressing closer against the walls like children at a keyhole, and I wrote it all in the place inside me where the dead live, word by word, sealing each one like wax.

When she finished, she was crying without noise, the way people cry who learned long ago that noise costs extra.

"Deliver them," she whispered. "All of them. Especially the June."

"I swear it," I said. "On my craft. On the bones I've carved."

She smiled then, truly smiled, and lay back against her chair, and I felt—there is no other word for it—I felt her soul settle, the way a house settles at night, creaking once and going still. Around the edges of the room, the dead began to murmur again, but differently now. Not complaints. Not repetitions. Something almost like thanks, passing from mouthless mouth to mouthless mouth: *he hears, he hears, the carver hears.*

I looked up and met Wren's eyes across the room. She stood by the stairs with her arms crossed and her face unreadable, but her knuckles were white where her nails bit into her sleeves, and when she spoke, it was barely a breath.

"You shouldn't have done that."

"Probably not."

"Oscar. The collectors come at dusk. They watch for exactly this—for gifts given freely, for power that isn't theirs. If they learn what you are—"

"Let them learn." The cold anger was still in me, steady now, banked like a forge fire that would burn all night. "Someone should tell them. Someone should stand in their grey-faced town and shout it. Their souls aren't currency, Wren. Their deaths aren't debts. And I am done pretending otherwise."

For a long moment she said nothing. Outside, the light was failing; somewhere in the streets, iron-shod boots had begun to ring against cobblestones, unhurried, inevitable, coming nearer.

Then Wren unfolded her arms, crossed the room, and sat down beside me in the space the dying woman had warmed.

"Before those doors open," she said quietly, "there's something you need to know. About what I did. About why the court exiled me." Her eyes flicked to the door, then back, and in them I saw for the first time not flint but fear—and under the fear, something older and rawer that she had been carrying since the toll road, maybe since longer than that.

"It's about a child," she said. "And it's about you."

The boots stopped outside the Kindly Hearth.
