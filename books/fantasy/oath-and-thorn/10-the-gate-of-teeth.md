## 10. The Gate of Teeth

The mountain did not grow larger as they walked toward it. That was the first wrong thing.

For three days it had hung on the horizon like a bruise, and for three days it had refused to come closer, as though the road itself were a treadmill beneath them, unspooling without progress. Wren said nothing about it, but she ate less each evening, and by the third night she stopped building a fire altogether, sitting instead with her knees drawn up, watching the dark in the way of someone listening for footsteps.

On the fourth morning, the mountain simply *was* there.

Oscar woke with frost on his eyelashes and Cindrel filling half the sky, its slopes black and glassy where ancient fires had fused the stone, its summit lost in cloud that did not move. The pilgrim road ended at its base, or seemed to—ran straight as a plumb line into a wall of shadow that was not shadow at all but the mountain's mouth, a cavern opening wide enough to swallow a cathedral.

And set into that mouth, catching the grey dawn light like something wet, were teeth.

"They're real," Wren said quietly, coming to stand beside him. She had not slept; her eyes had the raw, scraped look of someone who had spent the night arguing with herself. "The gate. People always think the stories exaggerate."

"What was it?"

"No one knows. It died before the court built the mountain's halls—before the mountain was even a mountain, some say. The court found the carcass and made a door of it. They do that." Her voice flattened. "They take what is dead and make it useful. It's the only mercy they know."

Up close, the teeth were each taller than Oscar himself, curved like sickles, yellowed ivory shot through with veins of grey and gold. Between them stretched a portcullis of black iron, and before the iron, on a shelf of bone worn smooth by centuries of kneeling pilgrims, sat a basin.

The basin was carved from a single skull.

*They kneel here,* whispered a voice at the edge of his hearing—one of the road-dead he'd been carrying since the tollmen, the ones whose stolen souls screamed when the wind blew through the hollow men. *Everyone kneels here. Everyone pays.*

"How much?" Oscar asked. His voice came out smaller than he intended. "The toll. What does it cost?"

Wren didn't answer at once. She was looking at the basin the way a sailor looks at weather on the horizon—calculating, dreading, unable to look away.

"It doesn't cost coin," she said finally. "Coin is nothing to the court. It takes memory. One memory, freely given, of your choosing—or not of your choosing, if you hesitate too long. The gate chooses for you then, and the gate has no taste. It will take your name from your mother's mouth. It will take the color of your childhood house. It will take whatever you can least afford." She turned to face him fully, and in the flat light of the mountain's shadow her scarred face looked almost gentle. "So you must choose first, Oscar. That's the trick of it. The only power you have left at this door is the power to decide what you lose."

* * *

They made camp within sight of the gate, because Wren insisted they arrive at full waking strength—"You don't bargain with a door while your mind is soft with sleep"—and because, though neither of them said it aloud, they both needed one more night of being people who still owned everything they owned.

Oscar could not sleep. He sat against a leaning slab of stone and turned his mother's face over and over in his mind, the way a man counts coins he cannot bear to spend.

She had been dead nine years. Nine years, and the valley being what it was—magicless, silent, sealed—he had never heard her voice among the whispers. The Thornvale dead went quiet, went down into whatever silence lay beneath the sanctuary, and their sons were left with memory alone. Memory alone. He had built his whole life out of what the dead left behind, carving their bones into shapes that held their names, and his mother's bones were in the chapel yard under a stone he had cut himself at nineteen, and all he had of her was—

Her hands, flour-dusted, pressing dough flat on the kitchen table while she hummed off-key. That was real, that was certain. But a face—

He closed his eyes and summoned it, and it came, and this was the terror: it came *fuzzily*. He knew she had brown eyes, deep-set, with laugh lines already cut deep by the time he was born. He knew she had a scar through her left eyebrow from a childhood fall from the pear tree. He knew these facts the way he knew the dates of old wars. But the whole of her—the living arrangement of her, the thing you would know anywhere, in any crowd, across any room—that lived somewhere below knowing, and every year since the funeral it had sunk a little deeper, and every year he reached for it a little later, and told himself a little more firmly that he still had it.

He still had it. Didn't he?

"Stop," Wren said.

He hadn't realized his breathing had changed. He opened his eyes. She was sitting across the dead fire-pit from him, wrapped in her threadbare oath-keeper's cloak, watching him with an expression he was beginning to recognize: guilt, worn smooth by years of handling.

"You're testing it," she said. "Checking whether it's still there. Don't. Whatever you choose to give, don't rehearse losing it. The gate smells rehearsal. It takes the things you've been practicing parting with—it finds the wound you've already picked open." She pulled her cloak tighter. "I watched a man give away his wedding day once. He'd spent the whole climb up grieving it in advance, telling himself he could live without it, and the gate heard him, and it took the wedding, and the marriage, and the woman's name, all three, because he'd loosened the roots for it. He walked through that door with a hole in him shaped like forty years."

"And what did you give?"

The question was out before he could weigh it. Wren's jaw tightened. For a long moment he thought she would refuse, and he would have deserved it.

"I gave the oath," she said at last. "The words themselves. I stood before the Pale Regent's gate with my oath-keeper's vows in my mouth—I could recite them in my sleep, I'd taken them at fourteen—and I gave them up so I'd have room in me for the breaking." A thin, terrible smile. "It seemed clever at the time. Empty yourself of the vow so you can't be bound by it. But a man is made of his vows, carver. I walked through that gate lighter than I have ever been, and I have never once gotten the weight back."

The wind moved through the pass behind them, and far away, faint as breath on glass, the stolen souls of the tollmen keened. Oscar had learned to sort them now—to hear which voices screamed and which merely murmured, which ones had been taken whole and which had been worn down to nubs of themselves. He thought of the hollow-eyed men on the toll road, and how they had counted his coin with fingers that trembled, and how one of them, the youngest, had wept without knowing why while his companions searched Oscar's pack.

"The court eats memories," he said slowly. "Souls. Everything. And it's still hungry."

Wren's head came up sharply. "What did you say?"

"The Regent. The court. You've said it feeds on souls—harvests them, keeps them. But a thing that eats and eats and is never full isn't strong. It's starving." He heard the words as though someone else were speaking them, some cold clear voice that sounded a little like the whisperers and a little like himself. "That's why the toll is memory. That's why everything is a toll. It isn't greed, Wren. It's famine."

For a moment she stared at him as if seeing him for the first time—as if, perhaps, she were seeing the boy the prophecy had promised rather than the carver she had collected from Thornvale's edge. Then she looked away, toward the teeth gleaming in the dark, and said nothing at all. Which was, he was learning, how Wren Ashgrave said the loudest things.

* * *

They approached the gate at first light, when the mountain's shadow lay longest across the road and the teeth burned pale as moonlight.

Up close, the beast's jaws were worse than they had seemed from a distance. The teeth were not set in clean rows; they were crooked, broken, ground down at the tips by centuries of use. The gums had been carved into friezes—scenes of tribute, of kneeling figures, of processions winding down into darkness—and the carving was old enough that generations of hands had polished the figures featureless. Somewhere in that stone, Oscar understood, were the faces of everyone who had ever paid. Worn away. Made anonymous. Filed among the nameless.

The basin sat on its shelf of vertebrae, and as Oscar knelt before it—for there was no question of standing; his knees bent as if the floor remembered every pilgrim before him—he saw that the skull-basin was lined with a film of something like water and something like mercury, and that the surface moved, very slightly, as if breathing.

"Do I say anything?" he asked.

"There are no words," Wren said. She stayed standing, three paces back, her fists clenched at her sides. Whether she could not cross closer or would not, he couldn't tell. "You lean over the basin. You find the memory. And you let it go—truly let it go, not pretend, not hoard a copy in some corner of yourself. The gate knows the difference. If you cheat, it takes until you haven't."

"And if I have nothing it wants?"

"Everyone has something it wants." Her voice cracked on the last word. "That's the whole architecture of the place, Oscar. The court runs on the truth that everyone has something it wants."

He leaned over the basin.

His reflection looked back at him—thin, travel-worn, twenty-six years old and older around the eyes than his father had ever been—and beneath his reflection the silver surface swirled, patient, expectant, hungry. The dead in his ears had gone quiet. All of them. The tollmen's stolen souls, the road-dead, the murmuring chorus that had followed him since the boundary line—all silent, all listening, as if even the dead wanted to know what he would pay.

*I'm sorry,* he thought—not to the gate, not to Wren, but to her. To the flour-dusted hands. To the off-key humming. To the brown eyes he could no longer assemble into a whole.

He reached for his mother's face.

Not the facts—he kept those, or believed he did; the scar, the laugh lines, the way she'd squinted when she laughed too hard—but the *face*, the living whole of it, the thing he had carried for nine years without once showing anyone because there was no one left alive who shared it. He gathered it the way he had learned to gather a dying man's last words: gently, completely, holding nothing back, because a gift given in fragments was no gift at all.

The last time he saw her. He had not known it was the last time—no one knows, that's the mercy and the horror of it—and she had stood in the doorway of the workshop with the afternoon light behind her, and she had said his name, and she had smiled with one side of her mouth higher than the other, and the light had caught the grey just starting at her temples, and he had looked up from his chisel and thought, idly, fondly, *there she is*, and gone back to work.

*There she is.* Three words and a face. The smallest possible treasury. The largest thing he owned.

He let it go.

There was no pain. That was the obscene part. The memory lifted out of him like heat off summer stone—a soft inward rush, a lightening, a door swinging open in a house he had thought sealed—and the silver surface flared bright and drank, and for one instant Oscar Sandoval saw the basin filled not with mercury but with afternoon light and a doorway and a woman smiling with one side of her mouth higher than the other, and then it was gone, and the basin was only a basin, and he was kneeling on cold bone trying to remember what he had just lost.

He knew he had lost it. That was the cruelest architecture of all: the gate took the treasure but left the receipt. He knew his mother's face had been the most precious thing he possessed. He knew he had given it freely, and why, and that the giving proved something the gate required proving. But when he reached for her now—

Flour on her hands. Humming, off-key. Brown eyes, deep-set. A scar through the left brow. Facts. Dates. A list where a face had been.

He knew she had stood in a doorway. He could not tell you the doorway. He knew she had smiled. He could not see it.

A sound came out of him that he did not recognize as his own voice, and strong arms took him by the shoulders—Wren, who had crossed the distance after all, who hauled him back from the basin's edge with the desperate grip of someone who had watched men kneel there until they forgot how to stand.

"It's done," she said, low and fierce, her scarred cheek nearly touching his. "It's done, and it's paid, and you chose it, which means it's still yours—you chose what it meant. Do you hear me? They can take the memory. They cannot take what you did with it. Get up, Oscar. Get *up*."

The iron portcullis was rising.

It rose without sound, without strain, the great black bars lifting into the beast's upper jaw like a throat swallowing, and beyond it the mountain's mouth breathed out air that smelled of cold stone and candle smoke and, beneath it all, faintly, sweetly rotten—like flowers kept too long in a closed room. From somewhere deep inside came a sound that was not wind and not water: a vast, slow rhythm, regular as a heartbeat.

The mountain's heart. Or something wearing one.

Wren got him to his feet. He swayed; she steadied him. His face was wet and he did not remember crying, and he suspected he would not remember this either, in time—grief, too, was a kind of memory, and the mountain had a long reach.

"You'll get it back," Wren said quietly. It was not a comfort; it was a vow, and she spoke it like one, in the cadence of her lost oaths. "Whatever happens in there, however this ends—I swear it on what's left of my name. You'll walk out of that mountain remembering her face."

"You can't swear that," Oscar said. His voice was wrecked. "You told me. Nothing gets out of the ledger."

"Nothing has ever walked out of that mountain carrying what you carry." She nodded at his ears—at the silence where the chorus should have been, and the silence, he realized suddenly, was ending: one by one, faint and far below, the dead were beginning to speak again. Not the road-dead. Not the tollmen's stolen ones.

Voices from underneath. Voices rising through the stone like water through chalk, hundreds of them, thousands, a city of the dead beneath the mountain's heart—and every one of them, as the portcullis finished rising and the dark mouth of Cindrel waited open before them, was saying the same thing:

*Come down. Come down. We remember her face. Come down, hearing carver—we have been keeping it for you.*

Oscar looked at Wren. Wren looked at him.

Neither of them asked what the dead had said. But she took his arm, and together they walked through the teeth.
