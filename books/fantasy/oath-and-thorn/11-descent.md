## 11. Descent

The mountain swallowed them at the Gate of Teeth, and Wren Ashgrave learned, in the first hundred steps, that she had forgotten nothing.

That was the cruelty of exile. The court took her name, her rank, the pale fire of her oath-magic—but not the knowledge. Her feet still knew the rhythm of the descent stairs, worn concave by ten thousand years of courtiers' heels. Her lungs still knew when to breathe shallow, because the air below grew thick with something older than dust. And her heart—her traitor, coward heart—still knew how to be afraid in exactly the way the mountain wanted: quietly, obediently, with her chin lifted and her hands steady, so that fear looked like composure and composure might pass for innocence.

She walked three paces ahead of Oscar Sandoval and did not look back. Looking back was a courtesy she could no longer afford him. If she saw his face—if she saw the boy who had paid a memory of his mother's face to a gate made of jaws, and had done it without flinching—she would lose the discipline she had spent seven years forging out of shame. So she listened instead. His footsteps behind her were uneven but persistent, a lame bird's rhythm against the black stone, and every few dozen steps he made a small sound, half breath and half word, like a man trying to hold a door shut against a flood.

The dead were loud down here. She could not hear them herself—her hearing had gone with her magic, or perhaps her magic had only ever been the hearing's shadow—but she could see what they did to him. Each time they passed one of the wall-niches where the old bones were set like candles, Oscar stumbled, caught himself on the stone, and pressed his palm flat against it as though steadying a horse.

"Talk to me," she said, without turning. "Not to them. To me."

"It's—" A breath. "It's not whispers anymore."

"I know."

"You don't." His voice cracked on it, young and furious and frightened all at once. "In Thornvale they're fragments. Scraps. A name, a date, someone humming a lullaby under everything. Down here it's—" He made the sound again, the door-against-the-flood sound. "It's a choir, Wren. They're *singing*. All of them. At once. They've been singing for centuries and nobody ever told them to stop."

Wren closed her eyes for one step, two. *Nobody ever told them to stop.* Of course not. That was the design. The court below Mount Cindrel did not harvest souls the way butchers harvested pigs, swiftly and without ceremony. It kept them. It tuned them. A soul in anguish sang louder than a soul at peace, and the Regent had built an entire architecture of grief for exactly that reason—the galleries, the niches, the bone-lamps fed on residue—and Wren had once walked these halls as a keeper of oaths and had called it *order*.

"We're close to the third landing," she said. "There's a gallery there where the acoustics change. When we reach it, stop walking until I tell you. Can you do that?"

"Yes." Then, quieter: "How do you know where you're going? You said you'd never been past the archive."

"I lied," Wren said. "I was born down here."

That landed between them like a dropped stone. She heard him stop walking. She stopped too, because a guide who outpaces her charge in the dark is a guide who has already left him.

"You said you were exiled from the mountain court," Oscar said slowly. "I thought—I assumed that meant you came from outside. Like a pilgrim who took service."

"The court doesn't take pilgrims. It takes children." Wren turned at last, because some truths deserved a face. The bone-lamp light caught her and gave her back to herself in pieces: the scarred knuckles, the oath-brand long since burned over and grown back wrong, the gray in her hair that had arrived at thirty-one and stayed. "I was pledged before my birth, same as the tollmen, same as the hollowed elders in the border towns. The court raised me in the nursery galleries. I served eleven years as an oath-keeper before I broke mine. Everything I know about this place, I know from inside its ribs."

Oscar stared at her. Behind his eyes, she could see the chorus moving—his pupils tracking things that were not in the corridor, his head tilting toward sounds she would never hear. But he held her gaze, and after a moment he nodded, once, the way a man nods when he decides to keep trusting someone who has just admitted to being a stranger.

"Then you know the way to the Regent," he said.

"I know the way to the audience hall. Whether the Regent is there is another matter." She started walking again. "Keep your hand on the wall. Left side. The stone remembers footsteps, and if you touch it, it will remember yours too, and that matters later. Trust me on that."

* * *

The third landing opened around them like the throat of something patient.

Wren had forgotten the scale of it—or rather, she had spent seven years deliberately misremembering, shrinking the galleries in her memory to the size of a chapel, a crypt, anything a human mind could hold without tipping over. The truth was worse. The gallery rose in ribbed vaults of veined marble, white shot through with slow-moving gray threads, and every vein pulsed faintly, dimly, in a rhythm Wren refused to count. Bone-lamps hung in their thousands from chains that vanished upward into darkness, each lamp a skull capped with a perforated silver crown, each burning with a light that cast no heat and no shadow. The floor was a mosaic of interlocking vertebrae, polished by centuries of bare feet, and it gave slightly, springily, beneath each step, like walking on the roof of a living thing's mouth.

Beside her, Oscar made a noise she had only heard once before—from a dying woman in the border town whose last words he had carried for free.

"They're *louder* here," he whispered. "The ones in the walls. They're not singing anymore. They're listening."

"To us?"

"To *me*." He pressed both hands over his ears, which Wren knew from long observation helped him not at all; the dead did not speak through the ear. "They know what I am. Word's traveled faster than we have. They're saying—oh. Oh, no. They're saying *the carver's come, the carver's come, the one who hears.*"

"Good," Wren said.

Oscar lowered his hands and stared at her. "Good?"

"If they're talking about you, they're not talking about us to anyone else. News travels down here by mouth, and mouths belong to the dead, and the dead love a story. Let them carry it. A rumor is a fog, and fog hides footprints." She was already moving along the gallery's edge, counting arches under her breath. Seven years, and the counting still lived in her body like a second heartbeat. "Third arch past the broken lamp-chain. There's a service stair behind it. We go down, not across. The open galleries have patrols."

"Patrols." He said the word carefully, tasting it. "You mean courtiers."

"I mean what the court made of its favorites." Wren reached the arch and put her shoulder to the false panel, feeling for the seam she had last touched as a girl of twenty, in another life, under another name. The stone resisted, then sighed, then swung inward on hinges of fused vertebrae. Cold air breathed out of the dark beyond, smelling of chalk and iron and something sweetly rotten, like lilies kept too long in a sealed room. "Courtiers are what happens when a soul is harvested *well*. No screaming. No hollowness. Just—polish. Marble skin, veins of the mountain running through them, beautiful and obedient and empty as a struck bell. They walk the galleries in threes. They don't speak unless spoken to. And if they find you, they will not kill you. Remember that, Oscar. It's important. They will *escort* you. Politely. Gently. All the way to wherever the Regent wants you, and you will want to go, because their voices are made of the thing you miss most. That's how they hunt. Not with teeth. With longing."

Oscar was quiet for a moment. In the bone-light his face had gone the color of the marble overhead, but his jaw was set, and Wren recognized the expression because she had worn it for eleven years: terror, disciplined into focus, the way a smith disciplines fire into a blade.

"My mother's voice," he said. "That's what they'd use. The gate already took her face. They'd give me her voice and ask me to follow it."

"Yes."

"And you'd let them take me, if it meant reaching the ledger?"

The question hit her in the place where her broken oath lived. Wren let the silence stretch—one breath, two—because a lie offered quickly is a lie believed slowly.

"No," she said finally. "No. I broke one oath refusing to deliver a child to this place. I will not complete the delivery by another road. If it comes to choosing, I'll choose badly, and loudly, and we'll both probably die. But I won't hand you over. Now move. The stair won't stay hidden."

He moved. And Wren stood a moment longer in the mouth of the hidden stair, looking back up at the vast ribbed dark of the gallery, at the thousands of crowned skulls burning their cold light, and allowed herself three seconds of honest terror—three seconds, no more, the allowance she permitted herself each time she descended, a tithe of fear paid to the child she had been—before she folded the terror away and followed him down.

* * *

The service stairs were narrow, spiral, cut for servants and the pledged, and the walls here were not marble but packed earth threaded with root-systems of bone—thin white filaments branching like frost ferns, drinking from the stone. Oscar trailed his fingers along them as they descended, and Wren watched him flinch with every touch.

"What are they?" he asked.

"Root-bone. It grows toward grief. The whole mountain is threaded with it—that's why the valley above stays clean. Cindrel drinks everything that leaks downhill." She paused on a landing barely wide enough for two. Below them, far below, a light moved: a soft, veined glow swinging gently, like a lantern carried by someone walking with perfect, unhurried grace. "Patrol," she breathed. "Three of them, by the spacing of the light. Service stair lets out into the under-gallery ahead of them. We wait here until they pass the junction, then cross behind them. Understood?"

Oscar nodded. Then, in a whisper: "Can you hear them?"

"No. I told you—my hearing went with my oath."

"I meant—" He hesitated, and Wren realized with a small cold shock that he was embarrassed, this boy who had faced a gate of teeth, embarrassed to offer her a kindness. "I could listen for you. Tell you what they're saying. What the dead down here say about you."

The offer sat in the dark between them. Wren found that her hands had gone still on the rail of fused finger-bones, and that she did not want to know, and that she wanted it more than she had wanted anything in seven years.

"Not yet," she said. "When we're out. If we're out. Some doors you only open once, and you want daylight on the other side when you do."

He accepted that with a nod, and for a while they simply waited in the dark, shoulder to shoulder on the narrow landing, listening to the patrol's light swing closer. Wren counted its rhythm out of habit—courtiers walked in triple time, always, a metronome of ownership—and felt the old discipline settle over her like armor being buckled on piece by piece. Breathe shallow. Chin level. Hands loose. Fear is fuel; burn it slowly.

Below, the light rounded the junction. Three figures passed beneath them, and even from the landing Wren could see what exile had let her forget: the courtiers were lovely. Tall and smooth-limbed, their skin true white marble veined in slow gray, their faces serene as unfinished sculpture, they moved without the smallest waste of motion, and the bone-lantern they carried swung in time with their steps. One of them turned its head as it walked—not toward any sound, but upward, along the spiral of the stair, directly at the niche of dark where Wren and Oscar stood frozen.

Its face did not change. Its eyes, which were seams of dull silver, lingered for the length of one full step. Two.

Then it faced forward again, and the three of them swept on into the dark, and their light shrank to a coin, a spark, a memory.

"They saw us," Oscar whispered.

"They saw movement. They don't *see*, not the way we do—they feel warmth and weight and grief. And they didn't stop, which means whatever they felt wasn't worth reporting." Wren let out a breath she had been holding since the gate. "Or means they already know exactly where we're going and are letting us come. Both possibilities end at the same door. Move."

* * *

The under-gallery led them, over hours that Wren measured in landings and not in time—for time ran strangely below, pooling in the deep places like water in a cellar—to the edge of the archive district, and there she called the halt she had been dreading.

Beyond the last arch lay the Hall of Ledgers' approach: a colonnade of pillars carved as stacked figures, generation upon generation of the pledged rendered in bas-relief, each figure holding the hem of the one above, rising into the dark. At the colonnade's far end, visible even from here, stood the bronze doors of the archive, tall as chapel spires, worked with the faces of every oath-keeper the court had ever made—and among them, third row from the top, left of center, a face Wren had spent seven years not thinking about. Her own. Younger. Unbroken. Smiling, because the sculptor had insisted oath-keepers smile; it reassured the pledged.
