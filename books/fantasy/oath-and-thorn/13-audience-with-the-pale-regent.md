## 13. Audience with the Pale Regent

They came for him without footsteps.

Oscar heard them first as a silence—a hole torn in the chorus of the dead that had been his constant, deafening companion since the Gate of Teeth. The holding cell where Wren had left him was a niche of black stone behind a curtain of hanging ribs, and in it the voices pressed close, hundreds of them, murmuring their fragments: *the river was higher then, tell her the loom is mine now, I never meant to leave the dog.* Then, all at once, nothing. A silence shaped like two figures.

He knew before they drew back the bone curtain. He had learned, in the galleries above, what the court's servants sounded like—or didn't. The veined courtiers moved through the dead like stones through water, and where they passed, the whispers went quiet, as though even ghosts were afraid to be overheard.

"Carver." The taller one spoke in a voice like dry reeds. It did not open its mouth so much as permit sound to leak from it. "The Regent will see you now."

"I'd rather not," Oscar said.

The second figure laughed. It was a horrible sound, thin and delighted, like ice cracking in warm water. "It asked to *rather not*," it said to its companion. "The mountain has not heard such wit since the last age."

Hands like polished marble closed on his arms—not cruelly, which was somehow worse—and he was drawn out of the niche, through the curtain of ribs, into the bone-lit dark of the deep courts.

* * *

The way down was long, and Oscar counted it because counting kept him sane. Forty-one steps of white stone veined with red, like something half-formed still deciding whether to be flesh. Then a gallery of arches carved from stacked vertebrae, each one hung with lamps of amber light that burned without heat. Then a bridge across a chasm where the dead did not whisper but *sang*—a low, wordless drone that rose through the soles of his feet and settled behind his eyes like a headache made of music.

The voices were louder here than anywhere he had been. Since crossing the valley line three days ago, his gift had gone from a gift to a flood; every soul that had ever died within reach of the mountain seemed to press against him at once, all of them talking, none of them waiting. In Thornvale he had spent thirty years learning to sort them, to cup his hands around a single voice and let the rest wash past. Here there was no sorting. There was only the ocean.

But beneath the ocean, threaded through it, he could hear something else. Something that was not dead.

A hunger. Vast and patient and old, coiled at the bottom of everything, the way a root is coiled at the bottom of a hillside. He had felt it first at the gate and told himself it was his imagination. He did not tell himself that anymore.

"The Regent does not like to wait," said the reed-voice courtier.

"The Regent has waited a very long time," said the other. "What is one more carver?"

They descended a final stair into a hall so large that Oscar's eyes refused, at first, to accept it. The ceiling was lost in darkness overhead; the walls were lined floor-to-vault with niches, millions of them, each holding a skull or an urn or a folded shroud, and from each niche came a voice, and all the voices together made the drone he had been hearing—the hall itself was singing, or rather the hall was full of the dead who could not stop singing, because no one had ever laid them properly to rest.

At the far end, on a dais of black basalt, sat the Pale Regent.

Oscar had expected a monster. He had spent the climb down building one in his mind: something fanged and vast, a thing of teeth and shadow, the kind of creature grandmothers invented to keep children from wandering. What sat on the dais was worse, because it was almost beautiful. Tall beyond reason, robed in grey that might have been silk or might have been ash, its face pale and smooth and fine-boned, with eyes like chips of moonstone set too deep. It wore a crown of interlocking finger-bones, delicate as lace, and its hands lay folded in its lap with the composure of someone who had never once been interrupted.

Around the dais stood more courtiers, veined marble skin and patient eyes, arranged like chess pieces mid-game.

The silence around the throne was total. Not one whisper reached it. And Oscar understood, with a lurch, that the Regent itself was the hole in the world he had felt below—that the hunger he had sensed was sitting right there, wearing a crown, watching him with an expression of mild, courteous interest.

"You are the hearing carver." The voice was soft. It did not echo in the vast hall; it simply arrived inside Oscar's ears, as though the air between them had agreed to carry it personally. "I have wanted to meet you for some time."

Oscar's mouth was dry. His legs ached from the descent, and the chorus hammered against his skull, and every instinct he owned screamed at him to run. But he had carved the bones of the dead for thirty years, and if there was one thing a bone-carver learned, it was how to keep his hands steady when the work mattered.

"Then you know why I've come," he said.

"I know why you *think* you've come." The Regent tilted its head, and the finger-bone crown caught the amber light. "You believe you have come to bargain. To reclaim what was taken from you. You believe you are a debtor arriving to negotiate terms." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down. "But you are mistaken about the nature of the transaction, Oscar Sandoval. You are not here to buy back your soul. You are here because your soul has already been sold, and the seller was very thorough, and the buyer—" the moonstone eyes glinted, "—is feeling generous tonight."

* * *

The Regent rose, and the whole hall seemed to lean toward it—the courtiers, the lamplight, even the drone of the dead bending like grass in wind. It descended the dais steps slowly, robes whispering, until it stood close enough that Oscar could see that its skin was not skin at all but something like porcelain stretched over stone, hairline-fine cracks webbing the cheeks and throat, faintly glowing from within.

"Do you know what I am offered, in a century?" it asked. "Do you know what comes down the pilgrim road to my door? Pledged children, delivered by parents who weep and sign anyway. Hollowed elders who gave themselves in trade for a good harvest or a healed child. Souls by the handful, souls by the basketful. And do you know what they taste like?" It smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near its eyes. "Fear. They all taste of fear. A hundred years of fear, seasoned with desperation, and I have grown so *tired*, carver. So tired of fear."

It circled him once, slow, and the courtiers watched with expressions of perfect blankness, and somewhere in the niches a dead woman was murmuring *the loom is mine now, tell her the loom*—

"But you," the Regent said, stopping before him again. "You grew up in the silent valley. You were raised on the bones of the willing dead, who gave their last words freely because you listened. Do you understand what that means? You have fed your gift on consent. On grief freely shared. On love." Its voice dropped lower, and the hunger underneath it rose like a tide. "Your grandmother promised me a soul. She delivered instead a valley sealed against me and a child raised outside my reach—and I have waited a hundred years for the debt to walk down my road on its own feet. But a soul, Oscar Sandoval, is the least of what you carry."

"No," Oscar said.

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"I don't need to. Whatever it is, the answer is no."

The Regent laughed softly, and the sound rippled through the hall, and for one terrible moment every niche flickered as though the dead themselves had flinched. "There it is. There is the spine. Your grandmother had one too, you know. Right up until the end." It drifted closer, and the cold rolled off it like air off a winter river. "Here are the terms, then, since you insist on hearing them. Your soul—held in trust these hundred years, signed away before your birth, accruing interest you cannot imagine—I return to you. Freely. No price. No clause. Walk out of my mountain whole, unclaimed, free as the day you were born. All I ask in exchange—"

"—is my hearing," Oscar finished. His voice came out flat. "Wren told me the court would want it. Everyone keeps telling me what the court wants."

"And everyone keeps being right." The Regent spread its hands, and the gesture was almost humble. "Think about what I am offering, carver. A soul is a single note. Yours, alone, would feed me for—well. A year, perhaps two, given what you are. But your *hearing*?" The moonstone eyes burned. "Every death in this mountain, every last word, every unfinished grief—you could gather them for me. Willingly given, freely spoken, sweetened with trust instead of fear. You would not be my servant. You would be my *cupbearer*. And in exchange, you keep everything else. Your name. Your hands. Your art. Your little valley keeps its sanctuary. Everyone lives. Everyone is *fed*. Is that not kinder than anything else this mountain has ever done?"

The hall was silent except for the drone, and Oscar stood in the middle of it, and he thought about the dying woman in the border town who had begged him to hear her last words, and how he had done it for free, and how her face had eased at the end like a knot coming loose.

He thought about his mother's face—which he no longer remembered, having paid it to a gate of teeth, and which he grieved now as a second loss, a ghost of a ghost.

He thought about Ilsa's handwriting in the margin of the prophecy book: *a debt can be voided, but only by giving what is not owed.*

"It's a generous offer," he said quietly. "That's what frightens me about it."

"Generosity is not frightening."

"From you it is." He lifted his chin. "You want my hearing because it's the only thing in the world that feeds you without costing anyone else. And once I gave it, you wouldn't need bargains anymore. You wouldn't need consent. You'd have a wellspring, and wellsprings get *owned*." He shook his head slowly. "My grandmother bought this valley with a lie and a signature, and you've starved a hundred years on the interest. I'm not going to hand you the meal you've been dreaming of just because you asked nicely and threw in my soul for free."

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the drone of the dead seemed to hold its breath.

Then the Pale Regent sighed—a long, weary, ancient sound, the sigh of something that had hoped, one more time, against all evidence, that this one would say yes.

"So be it," it said. "Keep your ears, carver. Keep your defiant spine. Keep your soul, for now—it is mine regardless, written in ink older than your mother, and ink does not care what you refuse." It turned back toward the dais, robes pooling, and its voice cooled to formal frost. "The debt stands. Moonset has passed the point of negotiation. You will remain in the mountain until you reconsider. They all reconsider eventually. Your grandmother nearly did."

Something in Oscar cracked at that—something hot and furious. "She died before she could finish whatever she was trying to do. I read her journal. I read what you *did* to her."

"I did nothing to her." For just a moment, something moved behind the Regent's porcelain calm—something that might, in a living face, have been pain. "She ran out of time. That is all this mountain ever does to anyone. It runs out of time." It settled onto its throne and folded its hands, and the courtesy returned like a mask settling into place. "Take him to the holding dark. Gently. He is family, after all."

* * *

They dragged him—gently, as promised—down stairs that had not existed on the way in, past galleries he did not recognize, into a cold that thickened around him like water. The last thing he saw of the hall was the Regent's face, turned toward him, moonstone eyes unreadable.

The last thing he heard was the dead, roaring back in all at once as the Regent's presence receded—a thousand voices, ten thousand, crashing over him like a wave—and beneath them, faint and clear and impossibly familiar, a voice he had paid away the memory of but not the sound of:

*"Hold on, Oscar. Hold on. Help is coming."*

His mother's voice.

Then the dark took him, and the door of the holding dark closed with a sound like a ledger shutting.
