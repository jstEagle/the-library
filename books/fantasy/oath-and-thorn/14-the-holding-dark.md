## 14. The Holding Dark

The dark did not have edges. That was the first thing Oscar learned about it, lying on stone that drank the warmth from his body the way sand drinks rain. He reached out and touched nothing—no wall, no bars, no seam where floor met ceiling. Only air, cold and still as well water, and somewhere in it, other people breathing.

He had been thrown here after the Regent's courtiers dragged him from the audience hall, after his refusal still rang in his own ears like a struck bell. *I will not sell you my hearing. I will not give you anything you ask for.* The words had felt brave in the moment. Now, in the black, they felt like a coin he had spent too quickly.

"Oscar." Wren's voice came from somewhere to his left, low and rough. "Are you whole?"

"I think so." He flexed his fingers. They ached from where the veined courtiers had gripped them, marble hands with blood moving sluggishly beneath the skin like sap in frozen wood. "Wren. Where are we?"

"The holding dark. Under the archive levels, I'd guess—the air tastes of old ink and older bone." A pause, the sound of her palms pressing flat against stone, testing. "No doors that I can find. No light. The pledged are kept here until the court has use for them."

"Use for them," Oscar repeated, and the words turned to ash on his tongue.

Around him, in the darkness, he could hear them. Not with his gift—at first not with his gift at all—but with his ordinary ears, the way one hears rain through a roof. Breathing. Shifting. And beneath it all, a sound he knew intimately from a lifetime among the dead: the thin, fraying whisper of souls worn down to almost nothing.

Then he opened the door inside himself, the one he had kept closed since the gate, since the descent, since the chorus had nearly split his skull—and the dark filled with voices.

*Cold,* said one. *So cold, and the mountain takes and takes.*

*I had a name. I know I had a name. It was blue, my name was blue—*

*They come at moonset. They come at moonset and they drink.*

*Please. Please, someone. Is anyone there who can hear?*

Oscar sat up so fast his head swam. "Wren," he whispered. "There are dozens of them. Hundreds. They're—they're still alive, some of them, barely, and they're all calling out."

"I know." Her voice was flat, but he heard the tremor under it, the sound of guilt grinding against itself. "I carried three of them down this corridor once, when I still wore the court's colors. I told myself it was mercy. Better the dark than the harvest."

"And was it?"

Silence. Then, quieter: "I don't know anymore."

* * *

He found the first dying one by crawling.

It was not a decision so much as an instinct, the same instinct that had guided his hands over memorial bones for twenty years in Thornvale. Someone was fraying. Someone was going out like a candle in a draft, and every part of him that had ever listened to the dead leaned toward that guttering flame.

His fingers found a shoulder first—thin, birdlike, wrapped in cloth gone soft with age. An old woman. She flinched at his touch, then went very still.

"You're warm," she said. Her voice was papery, astonished. "You're warm and you're *here.*"

"My name is Oscar. I'm going to stay with you, if you'll let me."

She laughed then, a cracked, disbelieving sound. "Stay. No one stays. The ones who can walk are taken up for the harvest, and the rest of us wait. But you're warm." Her hand rose and found his face, traced his cheekbone with fingers light as moth wings. "And your voice—it doesn't echo wrong. Everything down here echoes wrong. Yours sounds like a room with a window in it."

"What's your name?" he asked, because names were the first gift he always gave, and the last.

A long pause. "I've been trying to remember. I think—I think it started with an S. Or maybe I only remember the letter because they took the rest." She coughed, a dry rustle. "You're the one they brought before the Regent. The hearing carver. We heard it through the stone. You refused him."

"Yes."

"Foolish boy." But her hand tightened on his cheek, and her voice broke open. "Blessed foolish boy."

He settled beside her, her head against his chest, and he did what he had done a thousand times in Thornvale's bone chapel. He listened. Not just to her words—to the whole of her, the shape of the life still clinging to her soul like burrs to a shepherd's coat. And when she began to speak, it poured out of her: a village on the mountainside, goats, a sister named for a river, a wedding song sung off-key by a groom who wept while he sang. Small things. Enormous things. The things the dead always carry farthest.

"They didn't take all of me," she murmured, growing fainter. "They took pieces, year by year. First the color of things. Then music. Then my sister's face. Each time they said it would be the last piece." Her breath caught. "Listen to me. Listen, carver, because I won't be able to say it twice."

"I'm listening."

"The Regent is starving."

The words dropped into the dark like a stone into a well.

"He is not cruel the way the courtiers are cruel. The courtiers drink because they like the taste. He drinks because he is *dying*, has been dying for longer than this mountain has had a name, and every soul they feed him buys him another season, another year, another century of not-quiet-yet. I saw him once, long ago, before they hollowed me. Through a door. He was alone in his hall, and he was weeping, and the tears cut grooves in his face like water cuts limestone." Her grip weakened. "Your grandmother knew. Your grandmother *knew*, carver. Ask yourself why she wrote what she wrote. Ask yourself why her bargain bought your valley only time."

"Time," Oscar said. His mouth had gone dry. "Not permanence."

"She bought you a hundred years. Maybe less now. And when the hundred years run out—" The old woman's voice thinned to a thread. "When it runs out, the bargain breaks, and the court goes hungry, and hungry courts do not knock politely at valley walls. Find the unfinished thing. She left something unfinished. I heard her say it, at the end, when they brought her down here to die. *I meant to finish it. I meant to give it freely. I ran out of—*" 

She never finished. The breath went out of her, slow and long, and Oscar held her small body in the absolute dark and felt the exact moment her soul slipped its moorings—not torn away, not harvested, simply *released*, drifting upward through stone the way smoke finds a chimney.

And then, for the first time since he had crossed the valley line, a voice spoke to him clearly, wholly, without static or distance. Hers.

*Thank you, listener. Go up. Finish what the Ilsa-woman started. And tell them my name was Sable. Tell them it was Sable, and I loved the river-sister, and I am not afraid anymore.*

He repeated it aloud, to the dark, to Wren, to anyone: "Her name was Sable. She loved her sister, and she is not afraid anymore."

Somewhere in the blackness, someone began to weep—not in despair, but the way people weep at funerals done properly, with the name spoken right.

* * *

He lost count of the hours. There were no hours in the holding dark, only moonsets and the space between them, and he measured time the only way he could: by the dying.

There were nine of them in his stretch of the dark, in the end. Nine souls guttering toward their ends, and Oscar moved among them like a lamplighter, kneeling in the cold, holding their hands, taking down their words the way he had once taken down the whispers of the dead in Thornvale—except these were not whispers. These were testimonies. Confessions. Love letters dictated across the final threshold.

A man who had been pledged in the cradle, like the children of the border town, and who remembered nothing of the world except a lullaby in a language he couldn't name. Oscar hummed it back to him until the man smiled and let go.

A young woman whose last piece had been taken only days ago—her own laughter; the court had drunk her laughter and left her silent—and who pressed her hand over Oscar's heart as she died, as if to borrow its rhythm.

An elder who had once been a courtier himself, before he grew a conscience and was cast down into the dark with those he had helped harvest. He wept as he died and asked forgiveness of no one and everyone, and Oscar gave it, because that was the work.

Each time one of them passed, Oscar spoke their name into the dark—if they had one, or if they didn't, he spoke the truest fragment he could find—and each time, the listening dark grew quieter around the place where they had lain. Not emptier. Quieter. The difference mattered. He could feel the difference the way a musician feels a resolved chord.

Wren stayed near him through all of it. She could not hear what he heard, but she watched his face in the pitch blackness as if it were lit, and she learned to bring him water when his voice wore ragged, to press food into his hands—hard gray bread that tasted of mineral and patience.

"You're killing yourself," she said at one point, after the sixth. "Every one of them takes something out of you. I can see it. You're grayer than when they threw us down here."

"It's not taking," Oscar said. "It's spending. There's a difference." He wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist. "In Thornvale, I carved bones for the dead who were already gone. Here I get to do it while they can still hear me do it. Do you understand what a gift that is, Wren? Do you understand that in twenty years of this work, I have never once known if it mattered?"

"And does it? Matter?"

"Yes." His voice cracked on it. "It matters more than anything I have ever done."

She was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—softer, stripped of the exile's armor. "That's what I couldn't do, you know. All those years ago. I couldn't sit with the child they wanted me to deliver. I couldn't look at her and spend myself on her comfort, because I was too busy being afraid of what refusing would cost me. So I ran instead. I saved her and I damned myself and I never once held her hand." A breath. "You hold their hands."

"That's not courage," Oscar said. "It's just the work."

"No," Wren said. "It's both. It was always both. I just didn't know that yet."

* * *

It was during the ninth vigil—the elder, the fallen courtier—that the truth assembled itself fully in Oscar's mind, the way a picture assembles from scattered tiles.

Sable had told him the Regent starved. The elder confirmed it, and added the architecture around it: the court was not a kingdom but a *body*, a great organism grown around its founder's hunger, and every courtier, every tollman, every hollow-eyed servant on the toll road was a limb of that hunger, extended outward, gathering. The harvests were not greed. They were meals. Scheduled, rationed, desperate meals for a thing that should have died ten centuries ago and could not.

"And the valley?" Oscar asked the dying man. "Thornvale. My home. What was it bought with?"

"Time," the elder rasped, smiling with ruined teeth. "Time and time and more time. Every harvest feeds him, and every feeding renews the bargain a little—a season, a decade. Your grandmother understood the arithmetic better than any of us. She didn't buy your valley sanctuary forever, carver. She bought it a reprieve, and she paid with a soul not yet born, because a soul not yet born accrues *interest*. You've been ripening in that magicless valley for twenty years, growing sweeter and stranger, and the court has been patient, because the interest on you was worth more than a hundred common souls." He coughed wetly. "But interest runs out. Contracts mature. And when yours matures—"

"I know," Oscar said quietly. "Moonset."

"Moonset," the elder agreed, delighted and horrified. "And here is the joke, listener, the beautiful terrible joke: he doesn't even want it anymore. The Regent. Oh, he wants the *relief*—twenty years of hunger makes any soul look like bread. But what he wants, what he has wanted since before your valley had a name, is to be allowed to stop. To be permitted to die like a creature instead of enduring like a god. He built the court to save his people from a dying age, and the court ate him, and now he cannot stop eating because the court will not let him, and neither will the hunger." The elder's voice sank to a murmur. "You looked at him in the audience hall. Did you see it? Behind the glamour?"

"I saw something tired," Oscar said. "Something old behind the pale face. I thought it was a trick."

"It is the oldest trick there is. It's called the truth." The elder's breathing was failing now, long gaps opening between the words. "Pity him, carver. Pity him if you can manage it. Not because he deserves it—maybe he doesn't—but because pity is the only weapon that has ever worked against a starving god. Anger feeds him. Fear feeds him. Even defiance—he drank your refusal in the audience hall like wine. But pity..." A smile ghosted across the ruin of his face. "Pity costs the pitier everything, and gives the pitied nothing he can swallow. It is the one coin he cannot take."

Oscar sat with that in the dark, turning it over like a carved bone, feeling its weight and its edges.

Pity. For the monster beneath the mountain. For the thing that had sent tollmen to strip travelers, that had hollowed Sable piece by piece, that had held his soul in trust before he drew his first breath. His anger rose up hot and ready, the way it always did, and he let it rise—and then he thought of the weeping heard through a door, tears cutting grooves in stone. He thought of a guardian who had become the thing it guarded against. He thought of his grandmother, walking down into this dark with a journal full of horror and a plan she never got to finish, and he wondered—not for the first time—whether she had pitied the Regent too. Whether that pity was the unfinished thing.

"She left something unfinished," he murmured. "Sable said it. *I meant to finish it. I meant to give it freely.*"

"Freely given," the elder whispered. "Yes. That's the phrase. That's the old law underneath all their laws. What is taken can be stolen back. What is traded can be renegotiated. But what is given *freely*—" His hand found Oscar's sleeve and gripped it with sudden, startling strength. "What is given freely binds the giver and the receiver both, forever, past all contract, past all death. If she had finished it, the debt would have burned. But she died with the giving half-made, and a half-made gift is worse than none—it becomes transferable. Inheritable. It fell to you like a debt falls to an heir." His strength failed. "Finish it, listener. Whatever it was. Finish it before moonset, or finish it a hundred years from now, but finish it, and free us all—him most of all."

He died at what might have been dawn, in some upper world neither of them would see. Oscar spoke his name—the elder gave it willingly at the last, a courtier's name, silver and sharp: *Vessic*—and the dark grew quieter again.

* * *

Afterward, Oscar sat with his back against the invisible wall and let himself shake. Not from fear. From the sheer weight of knowing.

Wren found him there. She sat down beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"They're starving him," Oscar finally said. "The court. His own court. He made it to protect people from a dying age, and now it keeps him alive past all sense or mercy, feeding him souls so he can keep... functioning. Like a bellows on a fire that no one remembers how to put out." He stared into the blackness. "And my grandmother saw it. She came down here, she saw *him*, and she tried to build a way out—for the valley, for him, maybe for herself—and she died before she could finish it."

"Oscar." Wren's voice was careful. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking that he asked for my hearing tonight. In the audience hall. He offered my soul back for it, freely, and I refused because I thought he wanted to consume me like he consumes everything else." Oscar laughed, a small broken sound. "Maybe he does. But maybe—maybe it's more complicated than that. Maybe the hearing isn't a meal to him. Maybe it's the one thing in the world that could actually *feed* him without costing anyone else. Voices. Names. The sound of the dead being laid to rest properly. What if that's the meal a starving guardian actually needs?"

"Or it's exactly what he wants you to believe," Wren said sharply. "I served that court, Oscar. I know how it thinks. It will dress a cage in whatever clothes will make you walk into it."

"I know." He pressed his palms against his eyes. "I know. But Wren—Sable said he weeps. Vessic said he wants permission to stop. My grandmother tried to give him something *freely* and died halfway through the giving. Three separate threads, all pointing the same direction." He lowered his hands. "I don't think the way out of this is refusing harder. I think the way out is finishing what she started. On my terms. With my eyes open."

Above them—or below them, or through a hundred feet of living rock—something shifted. A vibration in the stone, deep and slow, like a heartbeat heard through a floor. Then another. Then a third, faster.

Wren went rigid. "Do you feel that?"

"Yes."

"The court's waking. Something's happened upstairs—in the archive, or the ledger hall. They know something." She was on her feet, hands finding the stone, reading it the way he read bones. "Oscar, if they've discovered what you are—if they're coming down here—"

The heartbeat in the stone quickened. And far above, muffled by fathoms of mountain, Oscar heard a sound that raised every hair on his body: a great door unsealing, and behind it, many footsteps, descending.

He rose slowly, his knees cracking, his body gray with spent grief and borrowed deaths. Somewhere in the dark around him, the remaining pledged stirred, sensing the change, and one of them—a child's voice, impossibly small—whispered:

*The listener. The listener is here. Ilsa's heir is here.*

Oscar turned toward the sound of the descending footsteps, and in the absolute dark, he smiled—not with hope, exactly, but with the terrible clarity of a man who has finally read the last page of a contract and knows, now, precisely which clause to break.

"Then let them come down," he said quietly. "I have something to finish."
