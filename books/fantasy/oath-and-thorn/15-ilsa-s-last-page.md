## 15. Ilsa's Last Page

Wren had stolen things before.

She had stolen a name—her own, once, from the mouth of an oath she no longer wished to keep. She had stolen a child from a cradle of woven bone and carried him through three galleries of sleeping courtiers with her heart hammering so loud she was certain the whole mountain could hear it. She had stolen her freedom afterward, in the ugliest way, by letting everything else be taken instead.

But she had never stolen from the archive.

The Ledger Hall lay two galleries below the holding dark, and between them wound the ossuary corridors where the court's dead were shelved like wine. She moved through them now in the gray hush between patrol rounds, barefoot because her boots sang against the marble, wearing the veil and sash of a lesser courtier whose shift she had watched for two nights until she knew its rhythm the way she had once known the rhythms of oaths. The disguise was imperfect. The sash itched. The veil smelled of cold ash and something sweeter beneath it, like lilies left too long in water.

But it would do. It had to do.

Behind her, three levels up, Oscar Sandoval sat in the dark among the pledged, doing what he did best—listening, comforting, easing the dying into their last words as though the holding dark were a sickroom and he its gentlest nurse. He had made her promise not to tell him what she intended. Not because he forbade it—he had no rank here, no standing, nothing but his gift and his stubbornness—but because if she failed, he didn't want to know how. He wanted to spend whatever hours remained believing they might be spent on something other than grief.

Wren understood that. She had spent thirty years understanding it.

*Two hours until the changing of the veined guard,* she told herself, counting her heartbeat the way she'd been taught in her first century of service, when she had still been someone whose heartbeats were worth counting. *One hour down, one hour back, and a margin for error that does not exist.*

The corridor opened onto the antechamber of the archive, and Wren stopped in the shadow of the doorframe and let herself look at it properly, because looking was free and touching was not.

The archive of the bone court was not a room of books. It was a room of *bodies*—or rather, of the shapes bodies leave behind when everything soft is taken away. Shelves rose from floor to vaulted ceiling, carved from vertebrae the size of doors, and upon those shelves rested scrolls of vellum stretched on ribs, ledgers bound in hide she refused to let herself identify, and tablets of pressed ash inscribed in scripts older than any kingdom above ground. Pale light came from nowhere and everywhere, the same sourceless glow that lit every hall beneath Mount Cindrel, and it fell across the shelves like moonlight falling across snow: beautiful, and utterly without warmth.

At the far end, behind a screen of fused finger-bones, sat the archivist.

It had once been a person. That was the thing about this court that no outsider ever understood—the horror was never the monsters. The horror was how much of the monstrous was only people who had stayed too long. The archivist had been a scholar once; you could see it in the careful hands, the ink-stained fingertips gone gray and hard as slate. Now it catalogued souls the way it had once catalogued poems, and it did not sleep, and it did not eat, and Wren suspected it did not remember why it had loved books in the first place.

She needed it to move.

Fortunately, courts ran on ritual the way mills ran on water, and Wren had served enough years beneath Cindrel to know the archive's small liturgies. At the turn of each watch, the archivist carried the day's new entries to the Regent's receiving alcove—a walk of some four hundred paces, there and back, during which the archive stood empty except for whatever wretches were being processed at the intake desk.

Wren waited in the doorway's shadow and listened to the mountain breathe.

That was the strangest part of coming back, she thought. She had expected the court to feel like a wound reopened. Instead it felt like a house she had lived in too long—every creak familiar, every echo assigned to its cause. She knew which stair groaned under weight. She knew the interval between the veined guards' footfalls. She knew, God help her, the exact pitch of the intake clerk's voice when it lied to the newly pledged about how painless the transfer would be.

You could exile a person from a place. You could strip her name and rank and magic and cast her out through the Gate of Teeth with nothing but the clothes she wore. But you could not exile her from her own memory, and memory was its own kind of citizenship.

The bell-tone sounded—soft, subterranean, felt in the teeth more than heard. The archivist rose. Its joints clicked like an abacus. It gathered the day's entries into a case of black lacquer, bowed to the empty room out of habit older than thought, and shuffled away toward the Regent's alcove, its footsteps fading down the long gallery like rain moving off.

Wren counted to two hundred. Then she crossed the archive floor.

* * *

Finding Ilsa Sandoval's file was almost insultingly easy.

Wren had expected locks, wards, the jealous sorcery of a court that guarded its debts the way dragons guarded gold. But the archive had no need of locks. Who would steal a soul-debt? What thief could carry it away? The contracts were kept in open shelving near the intake desk, sorted by valley and village, because the court considered them not treasures but facts—as unstealable as the color of the sky.

Thornvale's shelf sat low, at knee height, as though even the filing system found the valley insignificant. Wren knelt, lifted the rib-bound ledger marked with the valley's sigil—a thorn wreathing a spring—and turned to the most recent entry.

*Sandoval, Oscar—one soul, held in trust.*

Beneath it, in a hand she now recognized as well as her own reflection: Ilsa's signature, sharp-angled, impatient, the letters leaning forward as if the woman who wrote them had been in a hurry to be done with writing and on with doing. And beside the signature, the clause that had snapped shut around Oscar like a jaw:

*This debt is binding upon the signatory and shall descend, undiminished, to any heir of the signatory's blood who reads these pages and learns the sum owed.*

Wren read it twice. Her stomach did not clench—she was past such luxuries—but something behind her sternum went very quiet and very cold.

A trap laid forty years in advance. A grandmother who had known, somehow, that her bargain would fail to finish—that her death would come before her sacrifice could complete itself—and who had done the only merciful terrible thing left to her: she had written the failure into the contract itself, so that the debt would not simply dissolve and take Thornvale's sanctuary down with it. Someone would inherit it. Someone of her blood. And the court, patient as stone, had only needed to wait for that someone to find the prophecy book in a chapel archive and read today's date beside his own name.

*She meant it kindly.* Wren closed the ledger with exaggerated care, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child. *Every cruelty in this place is dressed as kindness. That is how it endures.*

But Ilsa had also written, somewhere, a way to void the debt. Oscar had said so—had recited the marginal notes from memory, his voice going strange and thin in the lamplight of their camp: *a debt may be voided by payment, by forfeiture, or by willing surrender of the thing itself.* Payment, the court would refuse—it always refused coin, wanting flesh instead. Forfeiture meant death without consent, which fed the contract nothing and freed no one. Which left surrender.

And Ilsa had died before she could surrender anything.

Or had she?

That was the question that had brought Wren crawling back into the belly of the mountain. Because contracts in this court were living documents—they grew appendages, they sprouted riders and codicils the way rot grows mold—and a half-made surrender did not simply evaporate. It lingered. It waited. Somewhere in the archive there should be a record of Ilsa's final days: her petition, her intent, perhaps even the beginning of the rite. And if the surrender had been begun but not finished—

Then it could be finished. By another hand. By the heir himself.

If the pages still existed.

Which was the problem, wasn't it. Because Wren had seen the journal. She had held Ilsa's travel journal in her own two hands in a ruined monastery while Oscar slept, and she had felt the ragged edge of the torn pages under her thumb, and she had known—with the certainty of a woman who had spent decades learning how courts destroyed inconvenient truths—that the missing pages had not been lost. They had been *removed*. Deliberately. By someone who understood exactly how dangerous an unfinished surrender could be.

The court had torn them out. And the court would have kept them—not discarded them, never that; the archive kept everything, that was its sickness and its function—but filed. Separately. In the place where dangerous documents went to be forgotten.

Wren turned and looked at the back wall of the archive, at the shelf of black bindings set apart from all the others, higher than the rest, close against the ceiling where the pale light barely reached.

The condemned shelf.

* * *

Getting up to it required the intake ladder, which required the intake desk, which required her to pass within arm's reach of the clerk.

The clerk was called Soft-Voice now; it had surrendered its original name centuries ago, as all long-serving courtiers eventually did, trading identity for tenure. It sat at its desk of stacked pelvises with its gray hands folded, and it did not look up as Wren approached, because a veiled lesser courtier carrying nothing and asking nothing was beneath notice—which was precisely why she had chosen the disguise.

She reached the ladder. She put her hand on the rail.

"Watch rotation changed," said Soft-Voice, without lifting its head.

Wren's blood went to ice water. "I was not informed."

"No one informs anyone. That is the point of changes." The clerk raised its head at last, and its eyes were the eyes of all the court's old servants: patient, hollowed, faintly amused. "You are wearing Marrow-Sister Vesh's sash. Vesh stands the west galleries until the deep bell."

For a moment—one long, ringing moment—Wren stood perfectly still and let the silence do her lying for her. This was the oldest trick in the court's repertoire and she had learned it from masters: when accused, become boring. Guilt flinched. Bureaucracy did not.

"I was reassigned," she said, in the flat tone of someone repeating information they found tedious. "The west galleries are overstaffed since the pledges came up from the third level. Check the roster if it pleases you."

Something flickered behind Soft-Voice's eyes—the flicker of a creature weighing whether an argument was worth the effort of having it. Centuries of service had burned away everything except the path of least resistance.

"It does not please me," the clerk said, and returned to its ledger.

Wren climbed.

The condemned shelf was exactly as she remembered it from her years of service: a graveyard of documents the court wished it had never created. Treaties with kingdoms that no longer existed. Apologies drafted by Regents and never sent. And, wedged between a peace accord written on skin and a box of ashes labeled in a script predating the mountain itself, a slim gathering of loose pages, torn at the spine, wrapped in oilcloth against damp that did not exist down here.

Her hands shook as she unwrapped it. She noticed the shaking distantly, the way one notices weather.

The first page was dated forty-one years past. The handwriting was Ilsa's—sharper now, hurried, the letters of a woman writing by bad light and worse fear.

*I understand now what they want. Not my death. My death is common currency here; the court is drowning in deaths. They want my death GIVEN—willed, offered, surrendered freely, the way a gift differs from a tax. A willing death completes the circuit. It would pay the debt entire and seal the sanctuary forever, because a gift freely given cannot be collected again. That is the law older than this court, older than the Regent, older than the mountain. Even they must obey it.*

*They are afraid of it. Did you know that? The great hungry court beneath the world, and it is AFRAID—because if word spread that a willing death pays all, no one would ever sell their soul again. They would wait. They would give. And the ledger would starve.*

Wren turned the page. The candle-glow of the sourceless light seemed to dim, or perhaps her vision did.

*I have begun the rite. Three nights of preparation, then the offering at moonset. If you are reading this, I did not finish. Do not grieve the unfinished thing—finish it. A surrender begun in love can be completed by love. The law does not care whose hand holds the knife so long as the heart behind it consents. Let my heir give what I could not, and let him give it not to the court but THROUGH it—to the starving thing at the center, the guardian that was, so that it may finally eat without stealing and rest without dying.*

*Tell him—I could not write his name, they would have read it—tell him his grandmother did not sell him. She bought him time. Time is the only thing I had to give, and I gave all of it, and I am sorry it was not more.*

The last page was different. Older ink, grayer hand—not Ilsa's. An archivist's notation, clipped and bureaucratic:

*Document seized prior to completion of rite. Subject deceased (natural causes, accelerated). Surrender incomplete. Contract remains open, transferable per rider. Pages retained pending Regent's review.*

*Review pending 41 years.*

Forty-one years of bureaucratic limbo. The court had caught the pages too late to stop Ilsa's intent but too early to destroy the possibility—and so it had done what courts do with everything unbearable. It had filed the problem and forgotten it.

Until a carver's son read a prophecy and walked through the Gate of Teeth.

Wren wrapped the pages back in their oilcloth with fingers that would not quite obey her, and pressed them flat against her chest beneath the borrowed sash, and stood for a moment on the ladder with her forehead nearly touching the ceiling of the archive, breathing the cold ash air in and out.

Hope was a dangerous thing down here. She had watched hope hollow people faster than hunger. But this was not the vague, praying kind of hope. This was hope with handwriting. Hope with a date and a method and a grandmother's love folded into it like a letter into an envelope.

*A surrender begun in love can be completed by love.*

Oscar could finish what Ilsa started. Not by dying—God, no, the rite allowed for a partial offering, she could see it now in Ilsa's cramped annotations, a covenant rather than a corpse, a giving that renewed instead of ended. The court would hate it. The Regent would resist it. The ledger would scream.

But the law was older than the Regent, and even the hungry must obey it.

Wren descended the ladder, passed Soft-Voice's desk with her face arranged into perfect tedium, and was three steps into the corridor when the deep bell began to ring.

Not the watch-change bell. The other one. The one she had heard only once before, thirty years ago, on the night she broke her oath—the alarm that meant something precious had been noticed missing.

Somewhere above them, in the holding dark, the veined guards had come for a prisoner and found the cell empty—or found the prisoner awake and listening, or found anything at all out of order. It didn't matter. The mountain was waking, gallery by gallery, and its waking rolled down through the bones of the world like thunder rolling through a struck bell.

Wren ran.

She ran through the ossuary corridors with Ilsa's last words beating against her heart like a second pulse, past shelves of the shelved dead, past veined courtiers turning their smooth faces toward the sound, and she thought—not prayed, she had lost the right to prayer, but thought, fiercely, with everything in her—

*Hold on, carver's son. Your grandmother's answer is coming. Run to meet it.*
