## 16. Moonset Approaches

*Wren*

The holding dark had a door, and the door had a keeper, and the keeper was Wren Ashgrave, who had once been someone whose name meant something in these halls.

She stood before the iron-banded gate with her hand pressed flat against the cold, listening the way she had been taught to listen as an oath-keeper—not with her ears but with the oath itself, that splinter of old magic still lodged in her chest like a fishhook she could never work free. It was broken. It was useless. But it remembered the shape of doors.

Beyond the gate, in the dark where they kept the pledged, Oscar Sandoval was doing something no one in this mountain had done in a hundred years. He was *listening back*.

She could feel it through the iron—a low hum, like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. The pledged souls pressed against the far side of the dark were not screaming. That alone should have brought every warden in the ossuary running. The court fed on despair the way its Regent fed on souls, and despair had gone quiet below, replaced by something Wren had no name for and dared not speak aloud.

Hope. It might have been hope.

"Open," she whispered to the lock, and gave it the only thing she had left: her true name, spoken backward, the way oaths are unmade. "Evargsa Hsa Nerw."

The lock considered her. Somewhere inside its mechanism, a soul-spring coiled and uncoiled, tasting the name, finding it hollow, finding it *hers*. Then, with a sound like a tooth being pulled, it turned.

The door swung inward on darkness so complete it seemed to have weight. And out of that darkness came a murmur—hundreds of voices, soft as ash falling on snow—and beneath them, one voice rising clear:

"—the third gallery, there's a gap behind the femur arch, I can hear air moving through it—"

Oscar Sandoval emerged from the black like a man surfacing from deep water. His face was streaked with grime and something else—the residue of other people's dying, which clings to those who sit with it. His eyes found hers in the gloom and held them without flinching.

"You came back." He said it simply, without surprise, and somehow that undid her more than anger would have.

"I said I would."

"People say things." He glanced back at the dark, at the shapes stirring within it, hands reaching toward the thin light spilling through the open door. "They said things to me all night. Dying people say all kinds of things. Promises mostly. *Tell my daughter. Come back for me.*" His jaw tightened. "I'm not leaving them here, Wren."

"We're not discussing this in a corridor."

"No," he agreed. "We're discussing it while we run."

* * *

*Oscar*

The ossuary galleries were built for silence, and Oscar broke them with every step.

He heard everything now—not the muffled fragments of Thornvale, where magic died at the valley line and the dead spoke through wool and water, but the full terrible clarity of the deep places. The walls themselves were vocal. Femurs stacked like cordwood hummed with the names of their owners. Skulls in their niches muttered half-finished prayers. The floor was paved with vertebrae worn smooth by centuries of bare feet, and each one whispered under his boots: *this way, this way, not that way, the warden comes, the warden comes.*

"The dead are chatty down here," he breathed.

"They're not supposed to be anything," Wren hissed over her shoulder. She moved through the galleries like water finding its course—left at the pillar of knucklebones, down the stair carved from a single giant's tibia, always three steps ahead of pursuit that hadn't yet realized it existed. "The court keeps them quiet. Bound. What did you *do* down there?"

"I listened." He ducked under an arch woven from ribs, each bone etched with a debt-mark, a ledger-line of some bargain struck generations ago. "That's all I've ever done. Apparently nobody's tried it on them before."

Behind them, far off, a bell began to toll. Not bronze—bone. A great suspended sternum somewhere above, swung by hands they could not see, and its voice went through Oscar's teeth like ice water.

Wren froze. "That's the waking bell."

"They know?"

"They'll know everything. The Regent dreams the whole mountain. When something changes in the dream—" She grabbed his wrist. "Run. Don't listen. *Run.*"

But he couldn't not listen. That was the wound and the gift of him. As they fled along the gallery of the small bones—phalanges by the ten-thousand, rattling faintly in their alcoves like insects—the whispers rose around him into something almost organized, almost eager:

*Left. Left at the weeping wall. The wardens take the high road when the bell rings—they always take the high road—*

*The marrow channel is dry this season, dry since the last harvest, go by the marrow channel—*

*Little carver. Little listener. We remember your grandmother. She ran these halls too.*

Oscar stumbled. "What?"

Wren hauled him upright without breaking stride. "Later. Everything is later. Move."

"She ran these halls," he repeated, letting himself be pulled. The thought lodged in him like a splinter, working deeper with every step. Ilsa Sandoval, who had walked out of Thornvale sixty years ago and never come home, who had signed her name beside his soul in a ledger of the damned—she had run this same flight, ducked under this same rib-arch, been guided by these same whispering dead.

And they had loved her for it. He could hear that too. In the chorus of the galleries there was one name that surfaced again and again, worn smooth by decades of repetition, passed from bone to bone like a relic: *Ilsa, Ilsa, the kind one, the one who stopped to listen.*

The bell tolled again, closer now, and beneath it Oscar heard something worse: footsteps. Many. Above, and closing.

"The high road," he gasped. "They're on the high road—but there's a fork ahead, the dead say there's a fork—"

"I know the fork."

"The dead say take the *low* road. Through the marrow channel."

"The marrow channel floods."

"Not this season." He caught her arm, made her stop, made her look at him. In the bone-light of the gallery—pale luminescence seeping from the walls themselves, the slow glow of old deaths—he saw her weigh it: her knowledge against his hearing. Her map against theirs.

"Borrowed courage," she muttered. "That's what you are. Every step of this has been borrowed from someone braver than me." Then she turned left, down, into the dark that smelled of dust and old rain. "Marrow channel. If we drown, I'm haunting you specifically."

"That can be arranged," said a skull they passed, and Oscar laughed—a wild, cracked sound that echoed down the dry channel like a dropped bell.

* * *

*Wren*

The marrow channel had been a river once, before the court drank the mountain's waters to feed its hungers. Now it was a canyon of pale stone, its bed cracked into hexagonal plates like dried mud, and Wren led Oscar down its throat with the waking bell tolling overhead and her broken oath shrieking in her chest like a gull.

She knew what was coming. She had known since the lock turned. There was only one way this night ended for a disgraced oath-keeper who broke into the mountain a second time, and it was not the way it ended for heroes.

But Oscar was climbing over a fallen slab of channel-wall, whispering thanks to the dead who had shown him the handholds, and his face in the bone-light was lit up with something she hadn't seen on anyone in forty years underground: purpose, clean and unashamed. He looked like Ilsa. The journal pages she'd stolen from the archive were folded against her heart, and Ilsa's handwriting crawled across them in furious, beautiful script, and the resemblance was unbearable.

*The court wanted a death freely given,* the torn pages said. *Not taken—given. That is the flaw in every contract they write. They cannot compel love; they can only purchase it. And purchased love spoils.*

Wren had failed an oath once because she could not purchase a child's love, could not even steal it, could not carry a screaming girl through the Gate of Teeth no matter what the order said or what it cost her. They had taken her name for it. Her rank. Her magic. They had left her exactly enough self to understand what she'd lost.

And now here was Ilsa's grandson, offering to walk back into the ledger hall and unmake the whole rotten architecture of the place.

"Halt," said a voice above them.

Wren's blood went to lead.

Three figures stood on the channel rim, silhouetted against the glow of the galleries: wardens in veined marble masks, their bodies moving with the wrong fluidity of things that had been servants too long. One of them carried a net of silver chain that sang softly to itself, a song about surrender.

"Down," Wren breathed, and pulled Oscar behind the slab.

The chain-net sang louder. The wardens descended the channel wall without hurrying, because nothing in this mountain ever needed to hurry; time belonged to them.

Except—it didn't. Not tonight.

From everywhere and nowhere, the dead began to speak.

Not whispers this time. Voices. Hundreds of them, thousands, pouring out of the channel walls, out of the cracked riverbed, out of the very stone: *here, here, they're here, in the shadow of the slab, throw wide, throw wide the noise—*

And the noise came. Every skull in the nearest gallery alcove began to scream at once—not in agony but in *chorus*, a wall of sound that hit the wardens like a wave. The marble masks cracked. The silver net's song drowned in the din. One warden staggered, another raised its hands to its head, and Wren understood, with a shock that nearly buckled her knees, that the court's power over the dead had always been the power of silence—that everything here was built on voices kept bound, and one boy from a magicless valley had walked in and *unbound them*.

"Now," Oscar shouted over the roar. "Now, Wren!"

They ran. Up the channel, past the staggering wardens, through a fissure in the rock that the dead opened for them—actual stone grinding aside, guided by voices that had waited a century for someone to ask—and out onto a ledge overlooking the great central shaft of the mountain, where the bone-light fell like snowfall and the Ledger Hall glowed gold and terrible in the depths below.

The waking bell stopped.

In the sudden silence, a new voice filled the mountain. It came from everywhere—from the walls, the air, the marrow of the stone itself—and it was gentle, and it was vast, and it was hungry the way winter is hungry.

*"Someone has taken a page from my book."*

Wren's hand found Oscar's arm. Below them, in the golden hall, shadows began to move. Courtiers waking. Wardens converging. And at the center of it all, a figure rising from a throne of fused vertebrae, pale as the memory of snow.

*"Bring me the hearing carver,"* said the Pale Regent, *"and bring me the oath-breaker. Gently, if you can manage it. I find I am... curious... to meet the grandson."*

Oscar looked down at the gathering court, then at Wren, and his voice, when it came, was steady as a carving knife.

"The Ledger Hall. That's where the contract is. That's where moonset finds us."

"Moonset is four hours away," Wren said.

"Then we'd better be quick." He stepped off the ledge onto the stair the dead had already begun to whisper into being, one bone at a time, descending into the gold. "They're clearing a path for us. Can you hear them?"

Wren could. For the first time in forty years, she could hear them—faintly, through the ruin of her broken oath, like music through a wall.

*Ilsa's kin. Ilsa's kin. Make way. Make way.*

She followed him down, borrowing his courage one more time, as the mountain woke around them like a beast remembering it had teeth.
