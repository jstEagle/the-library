## 17. The Bargain Reversed

The Ledger hall was burning.

Not with fire—nothing so merciful—but with light. The Regent's waking had kindled every vein of pale stone in the archive until the galleries glowed like the inside of a shell, and the shelves of soul-ledgers threw shadows that moved wrong, sliding against their owners, reaching for the two intruders who stood at the center of a hundred centuries of theft.

Oscar stood with his back to the great open book, his palms flat against its pages as though he could hold it shut by will alone. The ink beneath his fingers was still warm. *Sandoval, Oscar—one soul, held in trust.* Beside it, Ilsa's signature curled like a bird's footprint in snow, and below that, the clause that had snapped shut around him like a jaw: *the debt transfers to any heir who reads this page.*

He had read it. He had read it aloud, because Wren had asked him to check the wording, and the hall had heard him, and the hall did not forget.

"They're coming," Wren said. She had put herself between him and the archway, her broken oath-sword drawn—a weapon without edge now, more symbol than steel—and her breath came fast and shallow. "I can hear them on the stairs. Six. Maybe eight. Courtiers don't run; they *glide*, which is worse."

"I hear them too." Of course he did. Down here the dead were not whispers but weather, a pressure system of voices pressing against his skull from all directions at once, and among them he could feel the courtiers approaching—not dead, these, but something adjacent to death, something that had traded its way across the border long ago. Their souls made no sound at all. That was how he knew them. In a hall full of screaming, they were silence shaped like people.

"Oscar." Wren's voice dropped low, urgent, stripped of everything but honesty. "When they take you, I want you to know—what I did, what I failed to do thirty years ago—I'd do it again. Refusing them was the only clean thing I ever—"

"Wren." He almost smiled. His hands were shaking against the ledger, so smiling was an ambitious undertaking. "Save the confession for after. We have maybe forty heartbeats, and I need most of them to think."

But thinking was hard when the dead were this loud. They pressed in—the pledged souls stored in the walls, the harvested ones shelved here in their thousands of ledgers, each book humming with the voice of the person it held. He could pick out individuals now if he tried: a farmer lamenting a flooded field, a child counting to soothe herself, an old man repeating a name over and over like a prayer worn smooth by use. And beneath them all, deeper than the ledgers, vaster, there was the other sound. The hunger. It came up through the floor of the mountain itself, a low tectonic moan, the sound of the Pale Regent starving in the dark at the center of everything.

*Starving.* The word the dying woman in the holding dark had given him, or rather the word her last breath had shown him. The Regent was not fed by cruelty for cruelty's sake. The Regent was fed because the Regent would otherwise cease, and the court—the whole rotten architecture of bargains and tolls and teeth—was built around that single, endless appetite. Harvested souls went into the hunger the way grain goes into a millstone, and the millstone was never full.

"Oscar," one of the dead said, very close, and he flinched before he understood. It was his mother. She had found him again in the chorus, the way she always did, her voice threading through ten thousand others like a hand finding his in a crowd. *Listen to me. You are standing in the one room in this mountain where you can win.*

"How?" he breathed.

*You are a carver,* she said, in the tone she'd used when he was small and frustrated with a chisel that wouldn't obey. *What do you do when a piece of bone is cracked all the way through?*

"You don't mend it." His own voice sounded strange to him, answering air. "You work the crack. You make it part of the design."

*Yes.*

The footsteps—or the glide, the terrible frictionless approach of things that had forgotten how to walk honestly—reached the archway. Wren raised her dead sword. Oscar turned to face the door.

They came in like frost forming. Six courtiers, veined marble skin catching the ledger-light, faces beautiful in the way that empty houses are beautiful, all proportion and no presence. Behind them, filling the archway, came the Regent.

Up close, out of the audience hall's careful glamour, the Pale Regent was taller than any person had a right to be and thinner than anything living could survive being. Robes of grey silk hung from a frame like winter branches. The face above them was smooth and ageless and utterly exhausted—there was no other word for it. Oscar had seen hunger on the dying a hundred times in Thornvale, had carved memorial bones while it worked behind their eyes, and he recognized it instantly, horribly, here at the crown of the court beneath Mount Cindrel. This creature was dying. It had been dying for a thousand years, slowly, expensively, on a diet of stolen souls.

"The heir of Ilsa Sandoval," the Regent said. Its voice was quiet, and the quiet was worse than shouting; it made the whole hall lean in to listen. "You ran. You were caught. Now you stand upon your own page and call it defiance." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down between courses. "I could simply take what is owed. Your hearing, your soul, your years—all of it is already signed for. Why did you run, if not to delay the inevitable?"

"Because I needed to reach this room first," Oscar said.

Something flickered across the Regent's face. Not surprise exactly. Interest—the first honest expression he had seen on anyone in this mountain.

"The ledger is the contract," Oscar went on, and his voice steadied as he spoke, the way it always did when he explained his craft to the grieving families back home. "Wren told me. My grandmother told me, in the pages she left. A bargain freely offered can be freely remade, but only at the place where it was written, and only before moonset." He lifted his hands from the page. "So here I am. Before moonset. At the place where it was written."

"You cannot void it," the Regent said. "Your grandmother proved that. She meant to void it with her death, and her death was taken before the act completed. Half-made contracts do not dissolve, carver. They *transfer*. You are reading your inheritance."

"I'm not going to void it."

The silence that followed had texture. Even the dead in the walls went still, ten thousand voices holding their breath at once.

Wren stared at him. "Oscar—"

"I'm going to rewrite it." He turned back to the great book, and the ledger-light poured over the page, over Ilsa's signature, over his own entry waiting like an open grave. "That's what you actually want, isn't it? Not my soul. Souls are grain—you burn through us and you're hungry again by the next moonset. Always hungry. A thousand years of harvests and the hunger never closes." He looked up, straight into the Regent's ancient, hollowed eyes. "But my hearing isn't grain. It isn't consumed. When I listen to the dead, I don't use anything up. The voices pass through me and they're not diminished. If anything—" he thought of the pledged souls in the holding dark, how they had quieted under his attention, how the dying woman had gone to her end eased instead of screaming— "*they're* diminished less. I'm not food. I'm a door."

The Regent had gone very still. Stillness, on that marble frame, was absolute—a statue's stillness, a held avalanche.

"A door," it repeated.

"Every year, at moonset, I come to this hall. Freely. And I open myself to the dead of this mountain—all of them, every soul in your ledgers, every pledge in your walls—and I carry their voices. Not to feed on. To *hear*. Because that's what the hunger actually is." His heart was slamming against his ribs, but the words kept coming, and they felt less like invention than discovery, like carving along a seam that had been waiting in the bone all along. "You've been eating souls because you didn't know what else to do with them. But souls don't want to be eaten. They want to be *heard*. Every one of them. The farmer and the child and the old man calling his wife's name—they're all still here, Regent. You've been sitting on a thousand years of grief with no one to tell it to. I can be the one who tells it. I can be the ear this mountain never had."

"That is not sustenance," the Regent said, but its voice had changed. There was a tremor in it now, fine as a hairline crack in porcelain.

"Isn't it?" Oscar asked gently. "Then why does the hunger never close? You've tried everything else. A thousand years of harvesting, and look at you." He let the silence sit. "Maybe the appetite was never for souls. Maybe it was for what's *in* them. The last words. The unfinished sentences. Everything the dead carry that the living never got to receive." He spread his hands. "I receive it. That's my gift. That's the whole of it. And I'm offering it to you—freely, annually, forever—as a covenant instead of a debt. Not theft. Not harvest. *Gift.*"

"You understand what you offer," the Regent said slowly. "This is not the valley's muffled half-hearing. Below, at moonset, opened fully, the chorus will pour through you entire. It will hurt. It has nearly killed you twice on the descent, and those were only fragments."

"Yes."

"And you will come every year. For the rest of your life. Bound to this mountain by your own word, as surely as any soul in these ledgers is bound by mine."

"Yes."

"And why," the Regent said, and here the exhaustion in its voice broke open into something rawer, something almost young, "would you bind yourself to the thing that hunted you? To me?"

And Oscar, who had spent his life listening to last words, gave his answer in the shape of one.

"Because I heard you starving," he said. "All the way down. Through the floor of the world. And I couldn't sleep for pity."

The Regent closed its eyes.

For a long moment nothing in the hall moved—not the courtiers, not Wren, not the shadows sliding wrong against their shelves. Then the Regent extended one hand, grey silk falling back from a wrist like bleached driftwood, and touched the open ledger.

"Speak the terms," it said. "Exactly. The book will know if you lie."

Oscar wet his lips. His mouth was dry as bone dust. "The contract of Ilsa Sandoval, dated one hundred years past, is hereby superseded. The soul of Oscar Sandoval is returned to him, whole and unpledged, and the clause of transfer is struck from the page. In its place: a covenant, freely entered. Once each year, at moonset, Oscar Sandoval will come to this hall and open his hearing to the dead of Mount Cindrel, that they may be carried, witnessed, and remembered. In exchange, the court beneath the mountain takes no soul by force, by trick, or by debt, from this day forward. No harvests. No pledges. No tollmen. The hunger shall be answered with listening, for as long as Oscar Sandoval lives—and when he dies, the covenant passes to whoever inherits his gift, freely, as he gives it now."

He paused. "Do you accept?"

The Regent's eyes opened. And in them—for the first time—Oscar saw not hunger, not cunning, not the cold arithmetic of a thousand years of bargains, but something startled and blinking, like a creature emerging from a burrow into light it had forgotten existed.

"I accept," the Pale Regent said.

The ledger moved.

It was not like fire and it was not like water; it was like both at once, like the moment a fever breaks. Ink crawled across the page in lines no hand was writing—Ilsa's century-old signature dissolving thread by thread, the clause of transfer unraveling into blank vellum, and then new script rising, silver-pale, sealing itself letter by letter as the words left Oscar's mouth. Where the old contract had been, warmth spread through the paper like blood returning to a frozen limb. And then, from the spine of the great book upward, the change caught: shelf after shelf of soul-ledgers shivering, their covers softening, the trapped voices inside them sighing—one vast, collective exhalation, ten thousand souls feeling, for the first time in memory, a loosening of the hand around their throats.

The sound they made was not cheering. It was quieter than that and infinitely larger: the sound of a held breath released. It rolled through the archive, through the walls, down through the bones of the mountain, and Oscar felt it pass through his chest and out the top of his skull and he wept without deciding to, tears cutting hot tracks down his cold face, because he had spent his whole life hearing the dead alone, one whisper at a time, and he had never once heard them *together*.

Beside him, Wren had lowered her useless sword. She was staring at the Regent—at the space just above it, where something invisible had been coiled for a thousand years and was now, visibly, uncoiling and drifting apart like smoke off a doused candle. The glamour of the court was fraying. The veined marble of the courtiers' skin had gone dull, and they stood shifting their weight like men waking from a long operation, unsure whether the war was over.

"It's done," Wren whispered. "Oscar. It's actually done. The old contract—" She reached toward the page and stopped, because the original entry, Ilsa's signature and all, was lifting off the vellum in flakes of grey ash, rising, scattering, going out.

The debt of the Sandovals burned away to nothing, and where it had been, the covenant shone: *one hearing, freely given, yearly at moonset.*

The Regent withdrew its hand. It looked down at the new writing for a long time, the way a starving man looks at bread he does not yet believe is real.

"One year," it said at last. "Then you return. And carver—" the ancient head tilted, and though its face could barely move, something in it bent, some stiffness of a thousand years giving way, "—when you come, bring word of the valley. Tell me if the sanctuary holds. Tell me what becomes of Thornvale without its wall of silence. Ilsa bought that silence with everything she had, and I have wondered, every moonset since, whether she regretted it."

"She didn't," Oscar said quietly. "I found her notes. She called it the only bargain she never doubted."

Something moved across the Regent's face that on a human face would have been called grief, and on this one could only be called its ancestor.

"Go," it said. "Before the court remembers what it is. Some of my household will not surrender their appetites so easily as I have—and the mountain itself is old, and set in its ways, and may not welcome the change you have written into it." It turned away, robes settling like snowfall, and its voice came back thin and strange across the glowing hall. "Climb quickly, hearing carver. And when the dead sing for you on the road up—"

It stopped. The whole hall waited.

"—listen kindly," the Regent finished. "Some of them have waited a very long time to be heard."

Oscar took Wren's arm. Together they turned from the Ledger of Souls, from the covenant shining on its page like a star set in vellum, and started for the stairs—and that was when the mountain groaned.

It was not an earthquake's groan, nor a settling. It was older and more personal than that, a sound like a vast throat clearing after centuries of silence, and it came from everywhere at once—from the walls, the floor, the roots of the world. The ledger-light guttered. Somewhere far above, stone shifted against stone with a grinding that Oscar felt in his teeth.

"What was that?" Wren whispered.

Oscar stood very still, listening past the groan, past the freed souls murmuring in the walls, down into the deep architecture of the mountain. And what he heard there made the covenant-warmth in his chest go suddenly cold.

Beneath them, at the center of everything, something that had been sleeping inside the hunger for a thousand years was beginning to wake up—and it was not the Regent.

"Run," Oscar said. "We have to get out. *Now.*"
