## 18. The Regent's True Face

The burning of the old contract did not smell like paper.

It smelled like the inside of a bell—like bronze and cold air and something older than either, a scent that had been waiting in the dark of the world since before anyone thought to name it *dark*. The ledger page curled at its edges first, Ilsa's signature going soft as breath on glass, then the whole entry lifting off the vellum like a moth made of ash. It rose between us, turning slowly, and where it touched the Regent's outstretched hand it came apart into motes that drifted downward instead of up, sinking through the black floor of the audience hall as though the mountain itself were drinking them.

I stood with my palm still pressed to the new covenant's surface—the second page, the one I had written in my own blood because ink was too small a thing to mean what I meant—and felt the words settle into me like stones settling into a riverbed. *One hearing, freely given, each year at moonset. In exchange: no soul harvested, no pledge enforced, no child born owed.* The court around us had gone utterly still. Hundreds of veined faces, marble-pale, their eyes like chips of moonstone set in flesh, all fixed on the space where the old debt had died.

And the Regent began to change.

It started at the hands. I remember that clearly, because I was looking at its hands when it happened—they had been beautiful until then, long-fingered and luminous, the kind of beauty that makes your teeth ache—and the beauty simply *left* them, the way light leaves a window at dusk. Not gradually. All at once, like a held breath released. The luminous skin went grey, then translucent, and beneath it I saw not veins but roots, dark and branching, spreading down from the wrist into the body as if the Regent were a tree grown wrong-side-out.

"No," whispered one of the courtiers near the dais. It was the first time any of them had spoken in my presence, and the voice was dry as a beetle's wing. "No, no, it cannot—"

The glamour failed the way a dam fails. First a crack, then a flood.

The tall silhouette folded inward. The crown of pale antler-light guttered and went out. And what remained, kneeling on the steps of its own throne with its palms flat against the stone, was—

Small. That was the thing that undid me. I had expected vastness, horror scaled to the mountain itself, but what knelt there was barely taller than Maren, thin as a winter branch, wrapped in robes that had once been white and were now the color of old teeth. Its skin hung close to the bone beneath, and the bone was visible in places where the skin had worn through entirely—at the shoulders, along the ridge of the spine, across the cheekbones—as though centuries of hunger had rubbed it away like water rubbing away stone.

Its face turned up toward me, and I saw eyes the color of deep well-water, ancient and exhausted and, God help me, *kind*.

"Ah," said the Regent, and its voice without the glamour was nothing like the resonant bell-tone it had used for bargaining. It was cracked. It was quiet. It sounded like my grandmother's voice had sounded, near the end, when she spoke to people who weren't in the room. "There it is. There is always an *ah*."

The chorus of the dead, which had roared through me since the gate, went soft. Not silent—I don't think they were ever capable of silence down here—but hushed, the way a congregation hushes. And underneath their hush I heard something else, something I had been hearing all night without knowing I heard it, because it had been so constant it seemed like part of the mountain's own sound:

A heartbeat. Slow. Enormous. Failing.

The Regent's heart, beating somewhere in that starved chest like a drum at the bottom of a well, and every beat a little further apart than the last.

"You're dying," I said. My voice came out strange in my own ears, scraped raw from the descent, from the holding dark, from singing—or nearly singing—to three hundred pledged souls as Wren broke us out through the ossuary galleries. "You've been dying this whole time."

"I have been dying," the Regent agreed, "since before your valley had a name." It tried to rise and could not, and after a moment it stopped trying, folding its ruined hands in its lap with a terrible sort of patience, the patience of someone who has waited so long that waiting has become the shape of their soul. "Come closer, hearing carver. You have paid enough tonight that you may look upon me without flinching. Though I confess—" and here the ruined mouth bent, not quite a smile, "—I would understand flinching."

Wren's hand closed on my arm. She had fought her way back to my side through the chaos of the waking court, her broken oath-marks blazing silver along her forearms, and now she stood between me and the dais with her weight forward, ready to be a wall. "Oscar. Don't."

"It's all right," I said, and I was surprised to find it was true. Fear was there—I want to be honest about that; fear was there, sitting in my stomach like swallowed river-stone—but it was no longer the largest thing in me. What was largest was the heartbeat. I had spent my whole life listening to the dead, and I knew the sound a life makes when it is almost finished. I had carved it into bone a hundred times. *This* was only the largest instance of the oldest thing I knew.

I stepped past Wren. She let me, cursing under her breath in a language I suspected the mountain had taught her.

Up close, the Regent smelled like snowmelt and old incense and, faintly, unbearably, like the chapel in Thornvale. Like beeswax and cut bone and the particular dust that gathers where people have grieved for generations. I went very still.

"You," I said slowly, "smell like home."

"I should." The well-water eyes closed. Opened. "I built it."

* * *

What follows, I will tell as it was told to me—not because I trust every word of it, but because the dead confirmed it even as the Regent spoke, murmuring agreement in the spaces between sentences like a choir answering a cantor. When the Regent talked, the whole mountain listened. Perhaps it always had.

"Before the valley, there was only the mountain and the fear of it," the Regent said. Its voice gathered strength as it went, or perhaps I only grew better at hearing it. "Men feared the dark places. They feared what slept beneath the stone, and they were right to, for there were things below then that fed on terror the way your court feeds on souls now—worse things, older things, things I will not name because naming gives them a handle by which to climb back into the world. I was set as a warden. A guardian. The first of my kind to hold this mountain, and I held it alone for longer than your language has existed to describe it."

Its gaze drifted past me, up toward the vaulted dark where the galleries branched away into blackness.

"But a guardian alone grows strange. A guardian alone begins to mistake the guarding for the purpose, rather than the protected. So I built a court. Servants, at first. Then stewards. Then nobility. I gave them forms drawn from marble and marrow because I wanted them beautiful—do you understand? I wanted the mountain to be *lovely*, so that the people above would come to it without dread. I wanted to be loved instead of feared. It seemed such a small vanity. Such a forgivable one."

"And they learned to hunger," I said. I didn't know how I knew it. I did know. The dead had told me in the holding dark, whispering it against the bars of my cell while I eased a pledged girl through her last hours: *the court learned it from him, the wanting-to-be-loved, and twisted it into the needing-to-be-fed.*

"They learned to hunger," the Regent said, "and I taught them the taste without knowing it. Every bargain I struck to protect the valley above—every soul taken, every pledge enforced—I told myself it was medicine. Bitter, but necessary. The mountain must eat to keep its wards strong. The wards keep the worse things sleeping. Generations of guardians before me had done the same, but I did it *better*, I did it *cleaner*, I told myself, and meanwhile the court grew fat and clever and hungry in ways I had never been hungry, and by the time I understood what I had built—" the cracked voice caught, and for a moment the well-water eyes were simply an old creature's eyes, wet and lost, "—by then I was the throne and the throne was me, and neither of us could stand up and walk away."

"The valley," I said. "Thornvale. The sanctuary. My grandmother bought it with my soul—"

"With her own death, first." The Regent's head tilted. "You know that much, then. Good. She deserves to be known. Ilsa Sandoval walked into my hall with nothing but her fury and her faith and asked me for a miracle: a valley where magic could not enter, where the worse things below could never reach, where her people's children could grow up unafraid. I told her the price of such a warding. I told her honestly—I want you to know that; whatever else I am, I have always bargained honestly—it would cost a soul, freely given, bound in perpetuity. She signed. But she did not give me hers."

The heartbeat stumbled. I heard it miss, and resume, and miss again, and something in my chest lurched in sympathy, the way you flinch when someone else trips.

"She gave me yours. Unborn. Unformed. A soul not yet arrived, promised forward—a debt to be collected from her line, generation by generation, until one came due who could pay. She thought she was being clever. She *was* clever. She bought her valley a century of safety and pushed the cost down the road where she'd never have to watch it arrive." The Regent exhaled, a long rattle like wind through a bone flute. "But she also wrote the prophecy. She marked the date. She left you a map, hearing carver, because she could not bear to leave you only a bill. Half love, half wound. That is what inheritance is. I have watched it pass through human hands for a very long time, and it is always both."

I thought of the prophecy book in the chapel archive, the faded circle around today's date, the marginal notes in handwriting I had known without knowing—had traced with my finger a hundred times over the years on the flyleaves of hymnals, on the labels of Maren's preserves, on the cards tucked into funeral wreaths. Grandmother Ilsa, who vanished into the mountain before I could walk. Who had held me, maybe, once, in arms I couldn't remember, and signed my name in a ledger I couldn't read, and left me the means of my own rescue hidden in plain sight among the psalms.

"She died before she could finish it," I said. "Her plan. The torn pages."

"Yes." Something moved across the ruined face—grief, or its fossil. "She meant to void the contract by giving me her death willingly, freely, which is the one coin I may not take and the one gift I have never refused. A willing death freely offered feeds a guardian for an age. It would have freed her line entire. But the mountain is large and her heart was old, and she died on the stairs, two galleries below this hall, with the offer half-spoken. Unfinished acts are the most binding magic there is, hearing carver. Her death stayed locked inside her, ungiven. And the contract, half-made, became transferable. I am sorry. I have been sorry for fifty years. Sorry does not burn contracts."

"No," I said. "But covenants do."

The well-water eyes regarded me for a long moment. Around us, the court had begun to stir—not approaching, not yet, but shifting, veined faces turning toward their sovereign with expressions I could not parse, hunger and horror and something that might have been hope wearing hunger's clothes.

* * *

"Why?" I asked. It was the question that had been building in me since the pact sealed, since the old page burned, since the Regent accepted my terms with that weary nod—*weary*, I had noticed it even then, weary the way a starving man is weary of bread he cannot reach. "You could have refused. You could have taken my soul by force—you had me in your hall, you had three hundred courtiers and a mountain full of teeth. Instead you let a mortal write terms into your own ledger. Why?"

"Because I remember what I was for." The Regent lifted one translucent hand and pressed it flat against the stone floor, and I felt it then—felt it through my boots, through the soles of my feet, through the bones the dead say are the oldest instruments—the mountain answering its touch. A tremor. A leaning-in. The whole mass of Cindrel, miles of granite and grief, pressing up against its guardian's palm like a dog pressing its head into a failing master's hand. "The court forgot. They think the feeding is the purpose. They think the hunger is holy. But I was set here to *keep*, not to consume. To keep the worse things sleeping. To keep the small lights burning above. Your valley burns, hearing carver. It has burned for a hundred years because I kept the ledger balanced, however foully. And I am—" the heartbeat missed again, longer this time, and the gallery lights dimmed with it, every flame in the hall guttering in sympathy, "—I am nearly done keeping."

"What happens when you stop?"

"The wards fail. The court, mad with an empty larder, goes hunting in the world above. And the things beneath the court—the ones even the veined ones fear—wake up and find the door unlatched." The Regent smiled, and it was the most terrible thing I had seen all night, more terrible than the gate of teeth, more terrible than the holding dark, because it was gentle. "So you see why your covenant matters. One hearing a year. Freely given. Enough to feed a guardian honestly, for the first time in the history of this place. Enough to keep the door latched. Not enough to make the court fat. Hunger will teach them humility, or it will teach them nothing, and either way the world above gets to keep its children."

"That's why you accepted so easily."

"I accepted," the Regent said, "because in four thousand years, no one has ever offered to feed me *without being emptied*. Do you understand what that is worth to a creature who has forgotten the taste of a free gift? Your grandmother offered her death. You offered your ears. Both of you looked at the monster on the throne and saw something worth feeding." The well-water eyes shone. I would swear it, though no one down there weeps; I would swear the shine was real. "I thank you. I thank you with everything left in me, which is not much, but it is *true*, and true things are the rarest currency under this mountain."

It bowed its head. The whole court, moving as one, bowed with it—hundreds of veined figures folding down toward the floor, and the sound they made was like wind through a forest of hollow reeds, and the dead in the walls sang one low note beneath it, and I stood in the middle of all that bowing with my mother's face forgotten at the gate and my soul returned to my chest like a coal relit, and I did not feel victorious. I felt like a man standing at the bedside of something enormous and dying, holding its hand.

Because that was what it was.

I knelt. Wren made a strangled sound behind me. I put my hand over the Regent's where it lay on the stone, and the skin beneath my palm was cool and thin as parchment over kindling, and the heartbeat came up through the floor into my fingers, slow and enormous and failing.

"When do you go?" I asked quietly.

"Soon. Years, perhaps. A decade, if the covenant holds sweetly. Guardians do not die quickly; it would be irresponsible of us." The ruined mouth curved again. "But I will go, hearing carver, and when I go, this court will need a new keeper. One who listens. One who has already proven he can turn a harvest into a promise." The well-water eyes held mine. "I am not asking. I am telling you what the mountain will ask, someday, when it finds its voice again. Think on it. Mountains are patient."

I rose. My knees ached. Somewhere far above, dawn was coming, and Wren was already moving toward the ossuary stairs with her oath-marks blazing, saying something about the ascent, about the freed pledges, about the groaning in the stone that had begun like distant thunder and was growing louder.

The Regent watched me go. At the edge of the dais I paused, because the dead had gone suddenly, urgently loud, all of them at once, a tide of voices surging up through the walls—and beneath their surge I heard the mountain itself, deep down, speak for the first time in my life.

Not in words. In a single, vast, rising *note*—the same note the dead had sung, the same note I had nearly sung in the holding dark, the same note that lived in the covenant's cooling ink.

The mountain was singing. And it was singing *upward*.

"Wren," I said, turning toward the stairs as the first tremor shook dust from the galleries high above, "how many of the freed pledges can climb?"

Her face, when she looked back at me, was lit by flames that were guttering everywhere at once.

"Not enough," she said. "Run."
