## 19. Climbing Toward Morning

Wren had climbed out of the mountain once before, eleven years ago, with an empty cradle-shaped ache in her arms and no name left to carry. She remembered the ascent as a kind of drowning in reverse—light thickening above her like the surface of a lake she wasn't sure she deserved to reach.

This time was worse. This time she was not alone.

The mountain groaned around them as they ran, a sound like a millstone the size of the world turning over in its sleep. Dust sifted from the vaulted ceilings of the ossuary galleries. Somewhere behind them, deep in the dark, the court was waking—the Regent's will moving through its marble-skinned servants like blood through veins—and Wren did not look back. Looking back cost breath, and breath cost time, and time was the only coin they had left.

"Keep talking," she said to Oscar.

He was three steps ahead of her, one hand trailing along the wall of stacked femurs and skulls that lined the gallery, his face pale as the bone beside it. The freed pledges crowded behind him—a ragged river of them, forty or fifty souls in bodies gone soft from years below, stumbling, weeping, clutching each other's wrists so no one would be lost in the dark. They could not see. The galleries were lit only by the faint green phosphorescence of lichen on the older bones, and most of these people had spent their lives in the holding dark, where eyes forgot their purpose.

But Oscar's voice carried, and they followed it the way drowning sailors follow a lantern.

"I hear you," he was saying, over and over, his voice raw. "I hear all of you. Stay close to my voice. Left at the next arch—Wren, is it left?"

"Left," Wren confirmed, and raised her hand, and the small stubborn ember of magic she'd hoarded for eleven years flared against her palm—not much, not even enough to light a candle properly, but enough to throw shadows dancing down the gallery and show her the fork ahead. "Left, then up. The stairs are broken in the middle. Step where I step."

The dead were louder here than anywhere she had ever been. She felt them pressing against the edges of her awareness like cold air through a cracked window—she had never been able to hear them, not truly, not the way Oscar could—but even she caught fragments now, this deep, this close to the archive halls where so many contracts had been signed and sealed. Whispers like wind through teeth.

Oscar stopped so suddenly she nearly walked into him.

"What is it?" she asked.

"They're showing me a shortcut." His eyes were unfocused, turned inward toward voices she couldn't share. "The old pilgrim stair. It's collapsed partway but there's a way through—they used it before the court closed it. They say—" He swallowed. "They say Ilsa used it."

*Of course she did,* Wren thought, and something twisted in her chest that was not quite grief and not quite hope. "Then we use it too. Show me."

* * *

The old stair was a wound in the mountain's side, a spiral of steps hacked from living rock before the court grew grand enough to prefer marble. Halfway up, the collapse blocked the passage with a tumbled mass of stone and ancient timber, and beyond it the air moved differently—colder, sweeter, carrying the first ghost of pine and snowmelt from the world above.

Oscar pressed his palm flat against the rubble and stood very still.

"There are four of them," he murmured. "Dead in the collapse. Digging their way out when it came down on them." His voice cracked. "They've been here a hundred years, Wren. Nobody ever heard them finish their last words."

Wren looked at the pile of stone and understood what he was about to do, and hated how little time there was to do it. "Oscar—"

"I know. I know we don't have time." But he knelt anyway, in the dust, in the groaning dark, with the whole waking mountain hunting them, and he listened. She watched his lips move, answering the dead, giving them the thing no one else in all the world could give: an ending. A witness. She saw his shoulders shake once, twice, and then set themselves.

When he rose, his eyes were wet but steady. "They'll hold the stones," he said. "They want us through. They want someone to get out." He turned to the crowd of pledges huddled behind him. "Pass the word back—hands on the person ahead of you. We climb through in the dark. No one lets go."

It took everything. It took twenty minutes that felt like twenty years, Wren hauling herself and then others through a gap barely wide enough for a child, stone scraping her back, the mountain shuddering around them as somewhere far below the Regent's voice rose through the rock itself—not words anymore, just pressure, just fury made seismic. Three times the passage trembled hard enough to shake loose grit in stinging sheets. Three times Oscar stopped, laid his hands on the stone, and spoke to the dead inside it until it held.

The last one through was a girl of perhaps nine, born pledged, who had never seen the sky. Wren pulled her through the final gap and into open air, and the girl screamed—not fear, Wren realized. Wonder. Above them, through a fissure in the mountainside no wider than a coffin, stars were visible. Actual stars.

"Move," Wren rasped, because wonder was a luxury. "Move, move, move."

* * *

They lost the first ones on the switchbacks.

There was no other way to say it, though Wren rehearsed gentler versions in her head the whole climb. The path from the old stair to the Gate of Teeth was a scar of switchbacks cut into the mountain's throat, and the freed pledges were hollowed people—years of the holding dark in their muscles, years of harvested hope in their spirits. They climbed because Oscar's voice pulled them, but flesh has limits that love cannot legislate.

The first was an old man named Doran who had been pledged sixty years. He sat down on a ledge halfway up and would not rise. "I can hear my wife," he said, smiling, terrible. "She's been dead thirty years and I can finally hear her. She says the grass up there is still green."

"Doran—" Oscar crouched beside him, and Wren saw him listen, really listen, and saw what it cost him. "She says you should come with us. She says you promised her one more spring."

"I kept every promise I ever made except that one." The old man patted Oscar's hand. "You keep yours, carver. Go on. I'll follow when my legs remember how."

He didn't. Wren knew it the way you know weather coming. When they reached the next switchback and looked down, the ledge was empty, and Oscar stood very still for a long moment, and then he said, quietly, "His name was Doran Hale. He liked honey in his tea and he apologized to furniture when he bumped into it. Sixty years under the mountain and those are the things he wanted remembered." His jaw worked. "I'll remember them. I'll carve them if I have to carve every one of them myself."

"We're not stopping," Wren said, because someone had to be cruel enough to say true things. "We can't stop."

"I know." He wiped his face with the back of his hand and kept climbing. "I know."

The second loss was faster and worse—a young man who simply stepped off the path at a bend, walking with sudden calm straight toward the drop, and two women screaming and grabbing at his coat and catching nothing but air. Oscar cried out after him, and the echo came back changed, and Wren saw him freeze, head cocked, listening to something in the wind.

"He says thank you," Oscar whispered. "For getting him as far as the sky."

After that, Wren counted. She hated herself for counting, but she had been an oath-keeper once, and oath-keepers learn that hope without arithmetic is just a slower way of lying. Forty-one started the climb. Thirty-six reached the upper galleries. Thirty-one crossed beneath the Gate of Teeth, where the ancient jaws hung silent now, their toll paid, their hunger cheated. Twenty-six made the treeline.

Five names. Five last words. Oscar gathered them all like a man gathering spilled grain, kneeling by each one who fell, holding their hands, taking into himself whatever they needed to say before the dark took them. Wren watched him do it five times and understood, with a clarity that felt like a wound, that he was building himself into a reliquary. That every word he carried up was a weight he would carry for the rest of his life, and that he had chosen it, freely, the same way he'd chosen the covenant.

Survival, she thought, watching him haul a weeping grandmother over a fallen trunk. Survival doesn't feel like victory. It feels like arithmetic done in blood.

* * *

Dawn found them at the tree line, and the mountain began to die.

Not quickly—it was a mountain, and even dying, it took its time—but Wren felt it in her boots, in her teeth, in the marrow the court had never managed to harvest from her. The groaning had changed pitch. The tremors came closer together. High above, veils of dust poured from crevices, and somewhere deep in the rock something vast shifted its weight and did not shift back.

"The Regent," she said. "Or the court. The covenant burned the old contract, and the old contract was holding more than souls. It was holding the mountain together." She looked at Oscar, whose face was gray with exhaustion and ash. "How long do we have?"

He listened. He was always listening now; she suspected he would never stop. "Years," he said at last. "Maybe decades. It's not collapsing. It's... settling. Like an animal lying down." A pause. "It's sad, Wren. The whole mountain is sad. And relieved. Both at once."

"That's the truest thing anyone's said about grief in a hundred years." She turned to the survivors. Twenty-six people standing among black pines with dawn breaking gold through the branches, many of them seeing trees for the first time in their lives. One woman had her arms wrapped around a trunk, forehead pressed to the bark, sobbing. The nine-year-old girl was trying to catch falling needles in her palms.

Wren raised her voice. "Listen to me. The valley of Thornvale lies west, past the ridge—two days' walk for the strong, three for the rest. There's no magic there. None. It can't touch you, can't claim you, can't collect you. Whatever debts were written against your names died with the contract." She swallowed. "You'll be welcomed. Or if you're not, tell them Wren Ashgrave sent you, and tell them she's sorry it took so long."

An old woman squinted at her. "Ashgrave? The oath-keeper who broke her oath? They still sing about you down there. The ballad of the coward."

"Yes," Wren said simply. "That's me."

The old woman spat, thoughtfully, at the mountain. "Well. Cowards brought us out. Write that in your ballad."

* * *

They went down the western slope in loose columns, and Wren walked at the rear, because rear guards see everything and are seen by no one, which suited what remained of her pride fine.

From behind, she watched Oscar Sandoval become something new.

He did not lead anymore—he didn't need to; the path was plain and the terror was behind them—but the pledges clustered near him anyway, drawn like moths, and wherever he passed he paused. He took the hands of the dying—because some of them were dying, hearts and minds worn through by decades below, and the mountain's mercy was only that it let them see the sky first. He knelt in the pine duff beside a man whose breathing had gone wet and labored, and he listened, and he nodded, and he repeated the man's words back to him so the man could hear his own life spoken aloud one final time: *Tell Petra the loom is hers. Tell her I was proud every single day.* The man died smiling, and Oscar closed his eyes, and Wren saw the words go into him, another stone in the reliquary.

By midmorning he had collected eleven more. Eleven last words added to the five from the climb. Sixteen voices he would carry home.

"You can't save them all," Wren said quietly, when he swayed on his feet and she caught his elbow. "No one can. Not even you."

"I'm not saving them." His voice was hoarse as graveled road. "That's the thing nobody understands, Wren. It's not about saving. Saving is the court's language—souls harvested, souls redeemed, souls as currency. This is different. This is just—" He struggled. "My mother taught me this. In Thornvale, when someone dies, we carve their name so they're not forgotten. That's all this is. Carving. Just with sound instead of chisels."

"And it's killing you."

"It's keeping me alive," he said, "in the only way that counts," and she had no answer for that, so she gave him her water skin instead.

* * *

Near dusk, the last of them fell.

Her name was Sef, and she was ninety if she was a day, and she had been pledged as a child in the first years of Ilsa's bargain. She made it further than anyone had a right to expect—past the treeline, past the ridge's shoulder, to a granite shelf where the whole western world lay spread out below: darkening valleys, a ribbon of river catching the last copper light, and far off, impossibly small and impossibly dear, the green cup of Thornvale with its chapel roof glinting like a held breath.

Sef looked at it for a long time.

"Well," she said. "Would you look at that."

Then she sat down on the warm stone, folded her hands, and let go between one breath and the next, gentle as a lamp going out.

Oscar knelt beside her. Wren stood watch—someone had to, though the mountain behind them was quiet now, settling into its long sleep, and the road ahead showed nothing but evening and birdsong. She heard Oscar murmur, heard the wind answer, heard him repeat Sef's words back to the emptying body: *Tell them the valley was worth it. Tell Ilsa's boy the valley was worth it.*

He stayed kneeling a long moment after the wind went still.

"She knew," he said finally, without turning. "She knew what my grandmother traded. She was there when the bargain was struck—she was one of the witnesses. She spent her whole life under the mountain for us, Wren. For people she'd never meet." He rose, unsteady, and Wren took his arm without asking. "Seventeen last words. Seventeen. And every single one of them is about someone else. Not one is about themselves. Not one is afraid."

"That's what the court never understood," Wren said. "It measured souls like coin. It never learned what they're actually for."

They buried Sef under a cairn of stones at the trailside, because Oscar insisted and Wren agreed, and Oscar marked the stone with his knife—no chisel, no workshop, just a borrowed blade and seventeen hours without sleep—cutting *SEF* into the granite in letters shallow but true. Then he stood back, and the wind came up the valley, and for a moment Wren would have sworn the pines bent toward the cairn like mourners.

"Come on," she said softly. "Thornvale by tomorrow night, if we push."

"Then we push." He looked west, at the green darkness gathering below, at the valley that had been bought with his soul and saved by his covenant and would never, ever be the same. "Wren—when we get there. Maren's going to ask what it cost. Everyone's going to ask what it cost."

"And what will you tell them?"

Oscar was quiet. Below them, the first stars came out over the lowlands, and somewhere in the wind, faint as memory, Wren thought she heard singing—or maybe it was just the mountain, settling, sighing, letting go.

"I'll tell them the truth," he said. "That some of us made it home. And that the ones who didn't are coming too—in the only way that matters. And that every year, at moonset, I have to walk back up that road." He touched the cairn once, lightly, a benediction. "And that I'm not afraid of it. That's the part they won't believe. But it's true."

They walked down into the dark together, twenty-five living souls and seventeen dead ones, and behind them the mountain slept, and ahead of them the valley waited, holding its breath, not yet knowing that everything about it was about to change.
