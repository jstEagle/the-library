## 20. Crossing Back

The mountain let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long—slowly, grudgingly, with a tremor that ran through the stone like a sigh released after years of being swallowed.

Oscar felt every step of the ascent in his bones. Not the ache of climbing, though there was that too, deep in his thighs and the small of his back, but the mountain itself: a low groan rolling through the rock beneath their feet, as though Cindrel were turning over in its sleep and finding its bed emptier than expected. Behind them, far below, the freed pledges were still coming. He could hear them. He could not stop hearing them.

That was the thing no one had warned him about—not Wren, not Ilsa's journal, not even the dead themselves. His gift had never been this loud before. In Thornvale, the whispers came to him like moths to a lamp, one at a time, polite in their smallness. Here, on the pilgrim road with the dawn still an hour from breaking, they were a river. A flood. Every soul he had spoken to in the holding dark, every dying man whose last words he had carried like stones in his pockets, every pledge who had followed his voice up through the ossuary galleries—they were all still speaking, still clinging to him the way drowning things cling to driftwood.

*Tell her I tried,* said one. *Tell him the bread was for him,* said another. *The name was Aldous. My name was Aldous.*

"I know," Oscar murmured, not breaking stride. "I know. I'm carrying them."

Wren walked three paces ahead, her staff striking the road in a rhythm that had become, over these long days, as familiar to him as his own heartbeat. She did not look back often. When she did, her face was gray with exhaustion, her eyes rimmed red, and her mouth set in the particular line of someone who has decided that feeling guilty is a luxury she cannot currently afford.

"We should reach the valley line by midmorning," she said. "If we push."

"If we push," Oscar agreed.

He did not tell her that part of him wanted to stop walking. That part of him wanted to sit down on the cold road, among the frost-killed bracken, and simply listen until the chorus wore itself out or wore him down, whichever came first. There was a temptation in it—the same temptation he had felt in the holding dark, kneeling beside the pledged woman whose name had been Sera, holding her hollow hand while she remembered how to be a person for eleven minutes before the hunger took her back. It would be so easy to stay here, in the loudness, where his gift mattered desperately, where every word he spoke was a rope thrown to someone sinking.

But home was ahead. Home was always ahead now. And home was where the dead waited quietly, unlistened-to, for a carver who had left them to go bargain with a starving god.

So he pushed.

* * *

The valley line announced itself before they saw it.

It was nothing—a seam in the air, a place where the light changed quality the way water changes when a stream runs over different stone. Oscar had lived his whole life within sight of that seam without ever knowing it was there. From inside, Thornvale simply ended: fields giving way to forest, forest to the steep shoulders of the hills, and beyond them, nothing anyone spoke of. From outside, looking in, you could see the valley laid out like a painting—golden morning light pooling in the low places, smoke rising straight from the chimneys of Sandoval Hollow, the chapel bell tower thin and dark against the brightening sky.

And you could see, if you knew how to look, the way magic died at that invisible line. Wren had shown him on the journey out, standing at the boundary with her palm outstretched, watching the faint shimmer of her residual oath-magic gutter and snuff like a candle pinched between wet fingers. Nothing crossed. Nothing entered. For a hundred years, that silence had been Thornvale's shield, purchased with a soul sold before its owner drew breath.

Oscar stood ten paces from the line and could not make himself take the eleventh.

"Your aunt will have the kettle on," Wren said. "Or the axe. Possibly both."

"Maren doesn't own an axe."

"She owns a temper. Close enough."

He almost smiled. Almost. But the chorus was pressing against him again—*Aldous, my name was Aldous*—and the seam in the air was right there, shimmering faintly in the morning like heat off a summer road, and crossing it meant something he had not yet let himself think all the way through.

Because here was the truth he had been carrying since the Ledger hall, since the Regent's ruined face had looked up at him with something terrible and gentle in its starved eyes: the covenant was signed. Not in ink. In something older than ink, something that lived in the marrow of the world. Every year, at moonset, on the anniversary of the night he had stood in the court of small mercies and offered the Pale Regent a gift rarer than any soul, Oscar Sandoval would walk out of Thornvale, past this very line, up the toll road, through the gate of teeth, down into the bone-lit dark—and give the mountain his hearing for one night. So it could feed without harvesting. So no more children would be born pledged. So no more elders would be hollowed into servants with stolen voices.

One night a year. Forever. Or until the mountain died, and the Regent with it, and Oscar suspected—with the weary certainty of someone who had looked into that ancient face—that the mountain would outlast the valley, the language, the very idea of names.

Crossing the line meant the clock started. Crossing meant that next autumn, and every autumn after, he would stand exactly here, exactly this afraid, and walk away from home anyway.

And it wasn't only the journey. It was what he carried now. The gift had changed in the mountain—deepened, widened, cracked open like a geode struck with a hammer. Before, he had heard the dead of Thornvale in whispers. Now? Now he could feel the dead of the valley pressing against the boundary like faces against glass. He had been aware of them for the last mile: the old ones under the chapel hill, the plague dead in the barrow field, his mother, somewhere near the orchard where she used to sing. They were quiet. They were waiting. But he could feel them the way you feel weather coming in an old wound.

When he crossed, that would come home with him. All of it. Every whisper in every grave in Thornvale, suddenly audible, suddenly urgent, suddenly *his*. The valley had bought its sanctuary with his soul; now it would receive, along with its returning son, a hundred years of unquiet dead, all of them finally able to speak, all of them finally speaking to him.

Maren would want to know why he flinched at dinner. The children would ask why the bone-carver talked to empty air. The elders who had spent lifetimes pretending death was a door that closed cleanly would have to hear, from his mouth, what their mothers had actually said at the end—and some of those words were not kind, and some were not forgivable, and none of them could ever be taken back once spoken aloud.

His gift would change Thornvale forever. That wasn't a figure of speech. It was a debt of a different kind, and there was no Regent to bargain with about it, no covenant to sign, no moonset deadline. Just him, and the listening, and the long work of teaching a valley how to mourn honestly.

Ten paces from the line, Oscar Sandoval understood all of this completely.

And he could not move.

* * *

Wren noticed. Of course she noticed. She had spent nine days reading his silences the way he read the dead, and she stopped walking and turned, and leaned on her staff, and waited with the patience of someone who had once failed to wait for anything and had paid for it with her name.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stand very still and look at the horizon like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money," Oscar said. "Everything owes me something. That's rather the problem."

She laughed—short, rough, surprised out of her—and then sobered, because she knew what he was actually standing in front of, and she had stood in front of doors like it herself. Doors that only opened one way. Doors that, once passed, made you into someone your old life didn't have room for.

"Talk to me," she said. "Not the dead. Me."

So he talked.

He told her about the arithmetic he couldn't stop doing. One night a year, weighed against a lifetime of freedom—except it wasn't freedom, was it, because the hearing stayed, the chorus stayed, the responsibility stayed. He told her about Maren, who had spent twenty-three years building walls around him out of love, and who would open the door and see her sister's son standing there changed in ways she couldn't carve or mend, and who would grieve the boy she'd protected even while welcoming home the man he'd become. He told her about the valley itself—how it had never asked for this, how it had slept for a century behind its bought silence believing itself safe, and how he was about to wake it up.

"They'll hate it," he said. "Some of them. Some of them will hate *me*. I'm going to bring the mountain's shadow to their doorsteps. Every year. Forever. They'll watch me leave at moonset and wonder if I'll come back, and some years they'll be right to wonder."

"Yes," Wren said.

"That's it? Yes?"

"What do you want me to say, Oscar? That it isn't true?" She limped a step closer—her knee had never forgiven the descent, and probably never would. "You stood in front of a starving god and offered it the one thing it wanted more than souls, and you did it *freely*, knowing the price, knowing you'd pay it every year until you die. Do you think I don't know what that costs? I broke one oath. One. And it hollowed me out so thoroughly I spent six years forgetting my own name." Her voice caught, steadied. "You're choosing to keep yours. Every year. On purpose. That's not a burden, exactly. It's—" She searched for the word and didn't find it, and for once, the exiled oath-keeper, who could recite the legal architecture of a hundred covenants, had nothing.

"A promise," Oscar said quietly.

"A promise," she agreed. "The heavy kind. The kind that's actually worth making."

The chorus stirred against him—*Aldous, tell them my name was Aldous*—and Oscar closed his eyes and breathed, and for a moment the flood of voices parted, the way water parts around a stone, and underneath it he heard something else.

Something small. Something close.

Something that sounded like an orchard in late summer, and a woman humming off-key, and hands—small hands, his hands, decades younger—sticky with apple juice, held in larger ones.

"*Oscar.*"

His mother's voice. Not a whisper. Not a fragment. His mother, whole, the way the dead became whole only when they had something vital to say—as if love condensed them back into shape.

He opened his eyes. The valley lay golden before him, and the seam in the air shimmered, and somewhere beyond it, under the apple trees, his mother was waiting with words she had been keeping for twenty-three years.

"You can hear her," Wren said softly. It wasn't a question. She'd learned to read that look on his face—the faraway focus, the tilt of the head, the way his lips parted around a reply no living person could hear.

"Yeah."

"What is she saying?"

Oscar hesitated. Some things belonged to the dead and the bereaved alone. But Wren had given him her name back, piece by piece, over nine days of road and ruin and rescue, and some debts you paid in kind.

"She says—" He listened harder. The voice was patient, unhurried, warm as the light on the chapel tower. "She says the kettle's on. She says Maren burned the bread again, and that she's been watching her burn it for twenty-three years and it never gets any better." He smiled despite everything, despite the chorus, despite the fear sitting in his chest like a second heart. "She says—"

He stopped.

The smile fell off his face slowly, the way leaves fall—held, then released.

"She says she knows I'm afraid to cross. She says she's been watching me stand here for"—he checked the sun's angle against the ridge, did the grim arithmetic—"for nearly half an hour. She says she watched her own mother hesitate at this same line, sixty years ago, going the other direction, toward the mountain, and that Ilsa stood right about where I'm standing, and turned around twice, and went anyway."

The morning wind moved through the bracken. Wren's cloak snapped once and settled.

"And?" Wren asked.

"And she says the difference between a wall and a door is whether you're willing to open it." His throat worked. "She says—she says she doesn't need me to come home. She needs me to *choose* to. Because a son dragged home by guilt makes a poor listener, but a son who walks in with his eyes open—" His voice broke, and he let it, because there was no one left in either world who hadn't already seen him weep. "She says come home anyway. *Anyway.* Not because it's easy. Not because it's safe. Anyway."

The word hung in the air between them. *Anyway.* Such a small word for such a large door.

Oscar looked at the valley. He looked a long time—at the smoke, at the bell tower, at the orchard he couldn't quite see but could feel, the way you feel a hand resting on your shoulder. He thought about the boy who had grown up in that valley thinking the whole world was a place where magic couldn't follow, thinking safety was a birthright instead of a bargain, thinking his gift was a small strange talent for carving and listening and nothing more. That boy was gone. The mountain had eaten him, or the covenant had, or growing up had, which amounted to much the same thing.

But the man who stood here now, at the seam in the air, with a chorus of the dead murmuring their names against his skin and an oath-keeper leaning on her staff beside him and his mother's voice warm in his ear—that man knew exactly what he was carrying, and exactly what it would cost, and exactly who was waiting.

There was no escape. There never had been. The prophecy had seen to that before he was born. The only choice, the only real choice anyone ever got, was what to do with the weight once it was yours.

Oscar Sandoval took the eleventh step.

The seam parted around him like water around a wading child—no resistance, no fanfare, just a faint cool pressure across his skin, like passing through a curtain of still air. And then the valley took him back, and the dead of Thornvale rose up to meet him.

It was not deafening. That was the miracle of it, the mercy he hadn't dared hope for. It was like coming home to a house full of sleeping family—every whisper distinct, every voice known, a hundred years of the valley's dead stirring gently at the sound of his footsteps on the road, recognizing him, settling around him like a coat being put back on his shoulders. *The carver's come back,* they murmured, from the barrow field, from under the chapel hill, from the graves behind every farmhouse. *The hearing carver's come home.*

And beneath all of them, closest of all, from the orchard:

"There you are," his mother said. "Come on, then. Your aunt's worried sick, the bread's burnt, and you have a great deal to tell me. We have time, my love. We have nothing *but* time now."

Oscar laughed—a wet, cracked, unmistakably alive sound—and kept walking, and Wren fell into step beside him, and together they went down into the golden valley, two small figures on a long road, carrying the mountain's voices home.

Behind them, far to the north, Mount Cindrel groaned once in its sleep—and waited, patiently, for moonset.
