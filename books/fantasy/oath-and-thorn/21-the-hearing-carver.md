## 21. The Hearing Carver

The valley took him back the way a river takes back a stone—without ceremony, without resistance, closing over him as though he had never been gone.

Oscar felt the change at once. At the boundary line, where magic died in the air like a snuffed candle, his gift had roared; here it settled into something quieter, something domestic. The whispers of Thornvale's dead did not vanish inside the valley's protection—they had never vanished, he understood now, they had only been muffled, folded down to embers by whatever old bargain kept the valley clean of sorcery—but they softened, became companionable, became *home*. He could hear them the way one hears wind through wheat: constant, gentle, easy to lean into and easier still to ignore.

Except that he would never ignore them again. That was the difference. That was what the mountain had made of him.

Wren stopped three paces past the line, her boots planted on Thornvale soil as if she expected it to reject her. She looked around at the orchards going amber with early autumn, at the slate roofs of the town below, at the bone gate where Oscar had spent his whole life carving names into the femurs of the beloved dead—and her face did something complicated. Longing and dread braided together, the expression of a woman standing in a country she had heard described all her life and never believed existed.

"It's quiet," she said. "I'd forgotten places could be quiet."

"The dead are still here," Oscar said. "They're just polite about it."

He listened for a moment, head tilted, reading the valley's murmured weather. An old man near the mill complaining about the grain prices of forty years ago. A child under the alder tree counting to some enormous number and starting over. And beneath them, closest of all, from the orchard path ahead:

*He's brought a stranger,* his mother said. *Maren will have opinions.*

"Maren will have opinions," Oscar translated aloud, and Wren startled, then laughed—a short, rusty sound, as if laughter were a tool she'd had to pawn years ago and was only now learning to buy back.

"Does it ever stop?" she asked quietly. "The hearing?"

"No." He said it plainly, because she deserved plainness. "But you learn which voices are yours to carry and which are only passing through. That's the work. That's what I'm going to teach, if anyone will learn it."

"They'll learn," Wren said. "People always want to hear from their dead. They just don't know how much it will cost until they've paid." She touched the collar of her coat, where an oath-keeper's sigil had once sat and now there was only smooth, worn leather. "I'd know."

They walked down into the valley together, and behind them the mountain waited for moonset, patient as a debt.

* * *

Aunt Maren met them at the door of the carver's house with a bread paddle still in her hand and flour on her cheek, and for a long moment nobody moved.

Oscar had imagined this reunion a hundred times on the road home—in the monastery ruins, on the toll road, in the dark of the holding cells beneath Cindrel when sleep was the only escape left to him. In every imagining, Maren wept or raged or gathered him up. What she actually did was worse and better: she looked at him the way one looks at a house after a flood, checking the foundations, cataloguing what the water had taken.

"You're thinner," she said finally.

"I'm alive."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive." Her voice cracked on the last word, and the paddle trembled, and then she did gather him up, flour and all, crushing him against her apron with a strength that seemed impossible in so small a woman. "You stupid, brave, *impossible* boy. I told you not to go. I told you—" She stopped. Steadied herself against him. When she pulled back, her eyes were wet and furious and full of a love so heavy it had bent her whole life out of shape. "I told you because I knew. I knew what it would cost. I've known since before you could walk."

"I know," Oscar said gently. "I read her notes, Aunt Maren. All of them. You don't have to carry the secret anymore either."

Maren closed her eyes. For a moment she looked every year of her age, and then some—the age of a woman who had spent decades guarding a truth too heavy to set down and too terrible to share. When she opened them again, she looked past Oscar's shoulder to Wren, who stood at the gate with the awkwardness of someone uninvited to her own rescue.

"You're the Ashgrave woman," Maren said. "The disgraced one."

"Formerly disgraced," Wren said. "The position's been... restructured."

Something flickered across Maren's face—not quite a smile, but its distant cousin. "Then you'll stay for supper. Both of you. I burnt the first loaf worrying and I refuse to have burnt it for nothing." She turned back to Oscar, and her hand came up to his cheek, rough and warm, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Your mother's in the orchard. She hasn't stopped talking since you crossed the line. I can't hear her—I've never been able to hear her—but I know. There's a feeling in the air, like before rain." Her jaw tightened. "What did it take from you, Oscar? Tell me true. What did the mountain take?"

He thought of lying to spare her. Then he thought of Ilsa's journal, of half-made bargains and truths deferred until they curdled into traps, and he told her true.

"My memory of her face," he said. "I paid it at the Gate of Teeth. I remember her voice, her hands, the way she hummed when she baked. But when I try to see her—" His breath caught, even now, even telling it. "There's a shape where her face should be, and it won't resolve. It never will."

Maren made a sound like something tearing. She held his face in both her flour-dusted hands and pressed her forehead to his, and for a long time neither of them spoke, and the dead of Thornvale murmured softly around them like a congregation waiting for the service to begin.

"She knows," Maren whispered at last. "She must know. And she stayed anyway. Oh, my boy. My foolish, faithful boy."

"That's what I keep telling him," said his mother's voice, warm and dry, from the direction of the orchard. "He gets it from your side of the family."

* * *

Word moved through Thornvale the way water moves through chalk—slowly, invisibly, and then everywhere at once.

By the third day, people were finding reasons to pass the carver's house. By the fifth, they stopped pretending. They came to the door with their hats in their hands and their griefs in their arms like bundles they had carried so long they'd forgotten the bundles weren't part of their bodies.

An old widow whose husband had died mid-argument eleven years ago, wanting to know if he'd forgiven her. A farmer whose son had drowned in the millrace at nine years old, wanting—only wanting—to know if the boy had been afraid. The cooper, gruff and red-eyed, who asked nothing for himself but whether his mother, dead these twenty years, had heard him singing to her at the end.

And Oscar sat with them, in the workshop among the shavings and the sweet smell of bone-dust, and he listened, and he answered what he could, and he told them honestly what he could not.

Because that was the first lesson, and he learned it fast: the gift was not a window. It was a door, and doors open both ways, and some things on the other side should not be dragged through. Some of the dead did not want to speak. Some had said everything they meant to say before they went. And some—this was the hardest—some had nothing left to say at all, having worn themselves thin as tissue in the years of silence, whispering into a valley that could not hear them.

To those families he gave what truth he could: *they loved you; they knew you loved them; the argument was never the shape of the marriage.* And then he taught them the second lesson, the one that surprised him most, the one he hadn't known he knew until Wren asked him about it over Maren's supper table.

"You're not a medium," Wren said one evening, watching him carve a memorial bone for the widow's husband—his chisel finding the letters the way it always had, as if his hands remembered a craft older than his memory of learning it. "You're a carver who happens to hear. Why does the order matter?"

Oscar set down the chisel and considered. Through the window, the last light lay gold on the orchard where his mother's voice hummed an old baking song to no one.

"Because listening alone is just haunting," he said. "Anyone can be haunted. But a carver gives the dead somewhere to rest. Before, I carved their names blind—heard their last whispers and put them into bone without knowing why it mattered. Now I understand. The whispers fade, Wren. Even the loudest dead go quiet eventually, worn away like a path across grass. But a name cut into bone, cut *right*, with the true words spoken over it—that holds. That's an anchor. The hearing tells me what needs saying. The carving makes it last." He picked the chisel back up. "That's what I want to teach. Not fortune-telling. Not séances. Listening, and then laying down. Hearing, and then rest."

"And what will you call yourself?" Wren asked. "The court called you the hearing carver. It sounds like a title already."

"It sounds like a leash," Oscar said. But privately, in the dark, turning the words over like river stones, he found he didn't mind them. Let the court name him for its own hunger. Names could be reclaimed. He was living proof.

* * *

The first student was the cooper's daughter, Tilly, aged fourteen, all elbows and impatience, who announced at the workshop door that she'd heard her grandmother say "mind the step" three days after the funeral and wanted to know if she was cursed.

"You're not cursed," Oscar said. "You're receptive. It runs in some families like brown eyes. Sit down. First lesson: most of what you'll hear isn't meant for you. Learning what to answer and what to let pass is ninety percent of the work."

"What's the other ten percent?"

"Grief," Oscar said. "Yours and theirs. It's the language you'll both be speaking. Get fluent."

Tilly proved to have a natural ear and a terrible temper, which Oscar suspected were related. By the end of the month there were two more students—an orchard-keeper's widow who wanted to tend the graves properly at last, and a solemn boy of ten who heard the alder-tree child counting and had been frightened of the tree his whole short life. Oscar sat them in a circle among the bone shelves and taught them to breathe, to center, to hold a name in the mind like a candle flame, and above all to be kind, because kindness was the only instrument this work required and the only one that couldn't be improvised.

Wren watched from the doorway those first weeks, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman auditing her own redemption. Then one gray afternoon she came in, pulled up a stool, and said, "Show me. If I'm to guard a hearing carver, I should know what I'm guarding." And when Oscar raised an eyebrow: "And perhaps there are oaths worth keeping after all. Perhaps keeping them is a thing one learns twice."

He taught her too. She was slow at it—her ears had been closed by years of court silence, sealed like a wound heals over—but on the first frost morning, sitting among the bones with her eyes shut and her scarred hands flat on her knees, she went very still and said, in a voice gone young and wondering: "Someone's singing. Someone's singing in a language I don't—" and then she wept, quietly, without shame, and Oscar pretended to be very busy with his chisel until she was done.

* * *

On the last evening of autumn, Oscar walked out to the bone gate alone.

The memorial bones stood in their long rows, catching the low sun, each one glowing faintly ivory-gold, each one holding a name and, now, something more. Since his return he had been walking the rows in the evenings, laying his palm against the oldest carvings, listening to what the years had thinned nearly to nothing. Where a whisper remained, he renewed it—spoke the name aloud, re-cut the faded lines, gave the dead back their anchor. Where nothing remained, he let it go, and marked the bone with a small sun instead of a name, because the second lesson applied to carvers too: some things are yours to carry, and some are only passing through.

His mother walked beside him, unseen, unheard by anyone in the world but him.

"Tilly has a good ear," she said. "Better than yours at fourteen, though don't tell her I said so. And the Ashgrave woman hums when she thinks no one's listening. Court songs. Old ones. She doesn't know she remembers them."

"She's healing," Oscar said. "Slowly."

"You all are." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had gentled into the register she'd used when he was small and feverish. "You know what tomorrow is."

He did. One year since moonset. One year since the Ledger burned and the covenant was struck and the Pale Regent's glamour fell away to show the starved guardian beneath. Tomorrow night, at moonset, he would walk north out of the valley for the second time, and every year after, for as long as his body held, he would climb to the mountain's mouth and give the court its due: his hearing, freely offered, one night in twelve moons, so that the hungry dead below might feed without devouring.

He had made his peace with it the way one makes peace with weather—with respect, with preparation, and without pretending to control it. But tonight, standing among the bones of everyone Thornvale had ever loved, he felt the weight of it settle differently. Not lighter. Truer.

"I used to think the prophecy was a cage," he said aloud, to his mother, to the bones, to himself. "A date circled in ink before I was born. Every step I took toward it felt like walking into a trap someone else built."

"And now?"

"Now I think Ilsa wrote a map and called it a prophecy, because maps are only terrifying if you don't know where you're going." He laid his hand on the newest bone in the row—his grandmother's, carved with his own hands two months ago, the letters deep and true, the words spoken over it the ones she had torn from her own journal: *I did it for love, and love is a debt I pay gladly.* "She bought the valley time. I bought it permanence. And next year, and every year, I'll carry the mountain's voices home, and Tilly will learn, and whoever Tilly teaches will learn, and someday some child will stand at that boundary line and cross it without dread, because the bargain will be a road by then, not a cage." He smiled, thin and real. "That's the inheritance. Not the debt. The road."

His mother was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice was thick.

"Your grandmother would have liked you very much," she said. "I wish—" and then she stopped, because they both knew what she wished, and it was the same thing he had lost at the Gate of Teeth, and some wishes are just grief wearing its Sunday clothes.

"I know her," Oscar said. "Through her handwriting, her notes, her mistakes, her courage. It isn't her face. But it's her." He looked north, where Mount Cindrel stood black against the bruised evening sky, its summit catching the last light like a banked fire. "Tomorrow I'll tell her so myself. I hear she's been waiting."

Far off, so faint it might have been wind, the mountain groaned once in its sleep—and beneath the sound, unmistakable to a hearing carver, something vast and ancient and patient began, very softly, to sing.
