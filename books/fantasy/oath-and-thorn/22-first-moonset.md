## 22. First Moonset

A year had taught Oscar the weight of a promise.

It sat differently than grief did—grief was water, always finding the low places, pooling in the hollows of an ordinary afternoon when he caught himself reaching for his mother's voice before remembering he could no longer hear it as clearly as he once had. But the promise was stone. It sat in his chest where his soul had been held in trust for twenty-six years, and every night when the moon leaned toward its setting, he felt it turn over once, like a sleeper reminding him: *soon.*

Tonight was soon.

He rose before dark, as he had learned to do on these pilgrimages—not that there had been many yet. This was the first. The covenant he had struck in the Ledger of Souls required one journey each year at moonset, one night of his hearing given freely to the starving court beneath Mount Cindrel, and though Wren had argued through the whole of autumn that he might wait until the path was safer, until the tollmen were cleared from the roads, until the freed pledges who now lived in Thornvale's outskirts had settled enough to guard the valley themselves—Oscar had known he would go on the first moonset of the new year or not at all. A covenant kept grudgingly was no covenant at all. He had watched what half-kept bargains did to people. He carved their bones.

Maren met him at the door with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: bread still warm, dried plums, a flask of the bitter tea she swore by. She did not try to stop him anymore. That had taken most of the year—the nights she'd stood in the doorway of the bone-carving shed with her arms crossed like a gate, the shouting match that had cracked something loose between them, and then the slow thawing, the morning she had come to him holding Ilsa's prophecy book and said, quietly, "She would have gone too. I see that now. I just wish she hadn't taught you to."

"She didn't teach me," Oscar said, taking the bundle. "I think she hoped I never would."

"Hope's not the same as teaching." Maren touched his jaw, the way she had when he was small. "Come home."

"Always."

The valley at dusk was gold going to ash. He walked the familiar road past the chapel, past the archive where the prophecy book now lived behind glass because three families had asked to see it and one had tried to steal it, past the new graves—so many new graves, the ones from the mountain's groaning exodus, the ones whose last words Oscar carried in his memory like coins he could never spend. At each stone he paused. Not out of ritual, exactly. Out of courtesy. The dead of Thornvale had been silent for a century, sealed away by Ilsa's bargain, and now that the valley's magic-free sanctuary had ended—now that magic seeped back into the soil like spring thaw—they were waking. Faintly. Like sleepers murmuring before full consciousness.

*Cold,* said the grave of a farmer named Doss. *Was cold a long time.*

"I know," Oscar said softly. "Spring's coming. You'll feel it soon."

That was his work now, the work the valley had slowly, warily, come to accept: the hearing carver walked among the graves and listened, and carried messages between the living and the almost-gone, and helped the dying say what they needed to say while they could still shape the words themselves. Some people crossed the street when they saw him coming. More did not. Grief, he had learned, was hungrier than fear.

At the valley's edge, where the invisible line had once killed all enchantment and muffled the dead to nothing, he stopped and let it wash over him—the boundary now, a shimmer only he seemed able to see, like heat off summer stone. A year ago crossing it had felt like drowning. Now it felt like opening a door into wind.

Wren was waiting beyond it, as arranged, sitting on the milestone with her hood down and her ruined oath-marks visible on her wrists—faded brands that had begun, in recent months, to darken again. Regaining color, the way a bruise heals in reverse. Whatever the Pale Regent had done in that last audience, whatever terrible gentleness had passed between the starved guardian and the disgraced keeper, something of Wren's name had been returned. Not all of it. She still flinched when strangers asked. But she had started signing her letters *Wren Ashgrave* instead of leaving them unsigned, and that, she said, was more than she'd had in nine years.

"You're late," she said.

"The dead wanted gossip."

"They always do." She stood, rolling her shoulders, and Oscar saw her check the horizon out of habit—east, toward the mountain, where Cindrel stood black against the last violet light, its summit wearing clouds like a crown worn ironically. "Roads are quiet. Tollmen scattered after the exodus; most of them followed the pledges here hoping for scraps. We'll make the Gate of Teeth by midnight if we don't dawdle."

"We never dawdle."

"We dawdled in the monastery in autumn. Three days reading Ilsa's journal." Her voice went soft around the name. They had both grown tender toward the grandmother neither had truly known—Ilsa, who had written prophecies in margins and bargains in ink and died halfway through the act of saving everyone she loved. "You read her pages like scripture."

"They're the closest thing I have to her voice." Oscar adjusted the strap of his pack. Inside it, wrapped in linen: the carving tools his mother had given him at fourteen, a candle stub, and the ledger page he had cut free the night the old contract burned—a page that now read only *Sandoval, Oscar—one soul, returned,* in ink that glowed faintly whenever the moon was low. Proof of ownership. His own name, finally his.

They walked.

* * *

The Toll Road had changed. Where once hollow-eyed men had crouched behind barricades demanding memories and names, there were now waystones with lanterns, tended by some of the freed pledges who had chosen not to cross into Thornvale—people who remembered exactly what it was to be robbed and had decided no one else would be. One of them, a broad woman with pledge-scars silvering her throat, recognized Wren and waved them through without a word. Another pressed two apples into Oscar's hands and refused payment.

"For the listener," she said. "My mother's last words were for me. You brought them up the mountain yourself, last spring. I've said them to her grave every week since."

Oscar carried those words too. He carried hundreds. Sometimes, in the quiet stretches of road, he recited them to himself like a man counting beads—not to keep them, but to keep them *warm*. Last words cooled fast if left unspoken. He'd learned that in the Court of Small Mercies, watching the dying woman's family receive her message and weep as if she'd only just left the room.

By midnight the mountain filled half the sky. The Gate of Teeth still stood at its base—the ancient beast's jaw, fanged and vast—but the teeth were draped now with offerings: ribbons, bones carved with names, little clay figures of children and mothers. Pilgrims had begun coming, drawn by rumor of the court that no longer took. The gate stirred as they approached, and Oscar felt the old demand gather like breath before speech—

—and then release, unfinished.

"It remembers me," he said quietly.

"It remembers what you paid." Wren's jaw tightened. She had never fully forgiven the gate for taking his memory of his mother's face. Neither had Oscar, though he had made peace with the shape of the absence: he knew he had loved her, knew her voice still—voice was sound, and sound was his country now—but the face was a portrait painted over. Sometimes, in dreams, a woman turned toward him and he woke before he saw her.

"Thank you," he told the gate anyway, because manners cost nothing and the beast had guarded this door since before the valley had a name. Its breath washed over them, smelling of deep stone and old iron, and they descended.

The descent was easier than the first time. The chorus of the dead still rose to meet them—layer upon layer of voices, the pledged generations stacked in the galleries like sediment—but the screaming was gone. What remained was something closer to weather. Wind through canyons. And beneath it, unmistakable now, a thread of melody.

Oscar stopped on the stair. "Do you hear that?"

"Hear what?" Wren said, as she always did, patient, toneless—she couldn't hear it, none of the living could, and he sometimes forgot how alone he was inside his gift.

"Singing." He tilted his head. "Not the dead. Or—" he frowned, listening harder, feeling the sound move through his ribs like water moving through reeds, "—the dead *and* someone else. Something keeping time."

"Then we shouldn't keep it waiting." But Wren's hand found his shoulder briefly, a squeeze. She had learned, this year, that his silences meant listening and his pauses meant wonder, and she had learned to give him room for both.

They came down through the ossuary galleries, past walls of interlaced bone lit with pale vein-light, past chambers where the freed pledges' empty alcoves stood open and swept clean, marked with small tokens left by relatives who had dared the descent to reclaim their ancestors' remains. The court was thinner now. Hungrier, perhaps—or perhaps simply quieter, having learned over twelve months that its food would arrive on schedule, given freely, and that terror was a poor preservative compared to trust.

The doors of the audience hall stood open.

* * *

The Pale Regent waited in the center of the hall, standing—always standing; Oscar had never seen it sit—beneath the high vault where the vein-light gathered like captive aurora. And as they crossed the threshold, Oscar saw what he had seen only once before, at the sealing of the covenant, and had spent a year trying to unsee:

The true face.

No glamour now. No marble skin, no veined elegance, no beauty like frost on a window. What stood before him was gaunt as winter, ancient as the mountain's roots, its skin stretched over a skull that might once have belonged to any valley farmer. Its eyes were the worst part—not cruel, not cold, but *tired*, tired the way only a very long vigil can make a creature tired, the exhaustion of something that had guarded these people when they were alive and then could not stop guarding them after, feeding on them because there was nothing else, hating itself in the wordless way of animals caught in traps.

The valley's first guardian. Corrupted by the court it built to protect the dead. Starving for a hundred years on harvested souls, sustained against its own nature, waiting—for what, it had probably not known until a carver's grandson stood in this hall and offered it something kinder than a soul.

"Carver." The Regent's voice was dust and bellows. It inclined its head—not a ruler's nod. A penitent's. "You came."

"I said I would."

"One year ago tonight. Moonset." The Regent looked toward the high slit of the entrance where the last sliver of moon still hung, thin as a paring of bone. "I confess I counted the hours. I have not counted hours toward anything good in a very long time. I did not entirely believe you would come. Hope is a muscle I allowed to atrophy."

"Wise policy, historically," Wren said dryly. The Regent's gaze slid to her, and something moved across the ruined face that was almost humor.

"Keeper Ashgrave. Your marks continue to heal. Slowly. Names grow back like bone—it is why we use them as currency." It turned back to Oscar. "Are you prepared?"

Oscar set down his pack. Drew out the candle stub, the tools, the ledger page. He had rehearsed this a dozen times in his head, and every rehearsal had dissolved in the face of the actual moment, because the moment was not a transaction. It had never been a transaction. That was the whole point of what he'd done here a year ago—he had taken the cruelest arithmetic in the world, souls weighed and spent and harvested, and replaced it with a promise between two beings who could choose.

"Yes," he said. "But first—tell me how they are. The ones who stayed. The ones who couldn't leave."

The Regent was silent a moment. When it answered, its voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "Comfortable. As comfortable as the dead may be. They ask after you, Carver. The newly arrived especially—they remember your voice on the stairs. You sang to them, in the dark, when the mountain shook. Did you know that? Half the court followed the sound of you out of the deep galleries. They thought the sun itself was calling."

Oscar hadn't known. On that terrible climb, singing had been instinct—his mother's lullabies, pitched loud over the groaning stone, anything to keep the frightened dead moving upward. He had thought he was carrying their last words. He hadn't known he was also carrying a song.

"I'm glad," he said, which was insufficient, and he suspected the Regent knew it was insufficient, and accepted it anyway, because the Regent had spent a century learning the exact value of insufficient mercies.

"Then we begin." It raised one gaunt hand. "Moonset in a quarter hour. Sit, if you wish. The giving is easier seated, I am told."

There was no altar anymore. They had dismantled the Ledger's dais together, the three of them plus a dozen pledges, the day after the covenant—pried up the stones where souls had been weighed and left the floor bare, ordinary, honest. Instead the Regent gestured to a simple bench of mountain oak, and Oscar sat, and the Regent knelt before him.

A god of the underworld, kneeling to a bone-carver from a valley it had once owned outright. If anyone in Thornvale ever believed this story, Oscar thought, he would count himself lucky.

"Whenever you are ready," the Regent said.

Oscar closed his eyes.

The giving was hard to describe to anyone who hadn't felt it. His hearing was not his ears—it was deeper, a sense woven into whatever he was at the root, the thing Ilsa had seen in her unborn grandson and traded away to buy a sanctuary, the thing the court coveted above all souls because with it, the dead could be *fed* in the oldest sense: heard, witnessed, remembered into continued existence. When he opened it deliberately, fully, without the careful valves he'd built over the year, the whole mountain poured in.

Every voice. Every gallery. The pledged generations in their thousands, murmuring, singing, weeping, laughing at jokes only the dead found funny. The beast at the gate dreaming its long dream. The stone itself, groaning its slow tectonic patience. And threading through all of it, the hunger of the court—not the Regent's hunger alone but the court's collective need, vast and aching, drawing on him like roots drawing water.

He gave it. Freely, as promised. And as the hunger drank, he felt what he always felt in the first moments: not pain, exactly, but *exposure*, the vertigo of being utterly permeable, every secret of his inner weather open to the dark.

And then, as always, the dead noticed him.

*The carver comes,* rippled outward through the galleries, wave after wave. *The listener. The grandson of Ilsa.*

*Ilsa.* His grandmother's name moved through the dead like wind through wheat. She was here somewhere—he had felt her presence a year ago, a warmth at the edge of the chorus, and had not yet had the courage to seek her out. Tonight, in the openness of the giving, he felt her turn toward him the way a flower turns toward a window.

*Little one,* her voice said, and it was the voice from the journal margins, wry and tired and fierce. *You actually came. Your grandfather bet me you wouldn't. I owe him a kiss.*

"Grandmother," Oscar breathed, and the word went out of him into the dark, and the dark took it gently and passed it along, gallery to gallery, until ten thousand dead were murmuring *grandmother, grandmother* like a blessing returning to its source.

The hunger eased. It always did, after the first minutes—the court fed quickly when the meal was given in love rather than torn away in fear, and what remained of the quarter hour was something else entirely. Communion. The dead pressing close, not to take but to be near the living ear that heard them. Old men telling him about harvests from two centuries gone. Children asking if the sun was really warm. The dying of long ago asking him to carry messages to descendants who had never known their names.

And underneath everything, the melody he'd heard on the stairs grew clearer, and Oscar understood at last what it was: the court was singing. Not the Regent—the whole court, the dead harmonizing with the starving guardian's low drone, a sound older than Thornvale, a guardianship-song from the days when the mountain protected the living instead of consuming them. They were singing it *to him.* Welcome home. Welcome back. Keep your promise. We will keep ours.

Moonset came. The last light failed at the high slit. And the Regent, still kneeling, released him gently, folding the connection closed like hands closing a book.

Oscar opened his eyes. Tears had frozen on his cheeks in the hall's cold air.

"Thank you," the Regent said. "For the year. For the next one. For—" it hesitated, and the hesitation of an ancient creature was a terrible, humbling thing to witness, "—for teaching me that a debt can become a covenant, and a cage can become a home, if someone is willing to walk through the door freely. Ilsa began that work. You finished it."

"No," Oscar said. "She left me the map. I just walked it." He stood, legs unsteady, and looked up at the high slit where stars now showed. "Next year. Same moon. I'll bring news of the valley. And—" he smiled despite his exhaustion, "—I'll sing with them properly next time. I've had a year to practice."

The Regent bowed its ruined head. "We will listen."

* * *

They camped that night in the monastery ruins, under the broken reliquary dome where Ilsa's journal had been hidden, and Oscar lay awake listening to the far-off chorus fading as the mountain settled into its post-communion quiet.

"You were different coming out," Wren said from her bedroll. "Lighter."

"She knew my grandfather. She owes him a kiss. Apparently they bet on me." He laughed, and it came out wet. "A hundred years dead and they're still betting on me."

"Families." Wren said it like a prayer. Then, after a while: "Your marks are showing. On your wrists. Did you notice?"

Oscar lifted his arm to the starlight. There, faint as watermarks, curved lines had appeared along the bones of his forearm—intricate, deliberate, beautiful. Covenant-marks. The Regent had mentioned, at the sealing, that a freely given bond leaves a trace on both parties. He had assumed the Regent's trace would show on the Regent.

"What does it mean?" he asked.

Wren studied them a long moment, and when she spoke, there was wonder in her voice, and something underneath the wonder that sounded almost like fear. "It means the covenant isn't finished growing. It means the mountain has claimed you as more than a debtor, Oscar Sandoval. It means—" she stopped, started again. "In the old orders, before my time, there were stories of keepers who bound themselves to courts so deeply that the court began to live in them. Voices in the valley. Magic that traveled with the bound one wherever they went."

Oscar lay back and looked at the broken dome, through it, to the wheeling stars. In the distance, Mount Cindrel stood silent against the sky, and from somewhere in its depths—faint, unmistakable, patient—the dead were singing still.

And for the first time in his life, Oscar Sandoval sang back.
