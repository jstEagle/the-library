## 1. The Debt Ledger

The night market of Corvane began where the lamplighters gave up.

Marguerite Voss had learned that in her first week — three years ago now, though some nights it felt like thirty. The sanctioned stalls clustered under the temple's oil-glow along the Processional, all brass scales and blessed salt and clerks with ink-stained cuffs who counted your change like they were counting your sins. But past the tannery row, where the drainage ditch breathed its sour breath and the city's watchmen found urgent business elsewhere, the market went dark and honest at once. No lamps. No ledgers. Just a hundred small fires and a hundred small crimes, and everyone pretending not to see each other by the light of them.

She had a corner between a woman who sold eels out of a rain barrel and a man whose entire inventory was secondhand boots arranged by smell rather than size. Her own stall was a plank across two crates, a scrap of red cloth to draw the eye, and a lacquered box with a false bottom that had cost her two weeks' earnings and was worth every coin.

"Charms," she said, when customers drifted close enough. Only that. Never *lucky charms* or *love charms* or anything with warmth in it. The word itself was neutral as gravel. A woman selling charms might be selling buttons. A woman selling *blessings* would be dead before the bells rang matins.

Tonight her stock was modest: nine knotted cords dyed the gray-green of canal water, three iron nails filed smooth and wrapped in waxed thread, a pouch of grave-dirt from the paupers' field that she'd stretched with ordinary earth until it could serve four buyers instead of one. The good piece — the piece she was saving for someone desperate enough to pay for it — lay against her ribs beneath her coat: a small disc of green glass ground flat on both sides, with a spiral scratched into its face so fine you needed firelight to see it.

She hadn't made the spiral. She'd found the glass years ago in the mud of the riverbank, back when she still had a riverbank to stand on, and the spiral had been there already, worn nearly smooth, as if a thousand thumbs had worried at it before hers. She told herself it was nothing. A glazier's mistake. A child's game pressed into soft glass a century ago.

She told herself that every time she touched it, which was often, and always without deciding to.

"Eel-woman says you're honest," said a voice at her elbow, and Marguerite didn't jump, because jumping was the first thing they taught you not to do. Instead she turned her head slowly, the way a person turns who has nothing to hide, and found a fishmonger's boy looking at her cords with the naked hunger of someone whose mother coughed all night.

"Honest is expensive," Marguerite said. "Lucky is cheap. Which do you want?"

He wanted lucky. He had four coppers and a look that said he'd have traded his teeth. She sold him a cord for three and threw in the fourth copper's worth of advice: "Tie it left over right. Say nothing while you tie it. And if your mother asks where it came from, tell her you won it at dice."

"Dice," he repeated solemnly, as though memorizing scripture.

"Children win things," Marguerite said. "It's the only miracle anyone believes."

He went, clutching the cord, and she watched him go with the particular ache she never let reach her face. That was the trade, really. Not the charms. The face. Three years of practicing a face that said *I am nobody, I sell trinkets, I am not worth your attention*, and she could hold it now through almost anything — through the eel-woman's stories about the Inquisitor's clerks shaking down the tannery row, through the sound of temple bells that reminded her the debt clock never stopped, even through the spiral glass warm against her skin on cold nights for no reason she could name.

Almost anything.

* * *

The debt ledger lived in the temple of Saint Ossian, in a vault behind the counting-house, and Marguerite had seen it exactly once.

That once had been the day she signed. She remembered the room too well — the way memory insists on keeping what you least want — the smell of tallow and dust, the clerk's dry throat-clearing, the ledger itself lying open on a lectern like something fed. It was a book the size of a paving stone, bound in gray leather gone soft as skin, and every debtor in Corvane had a page in it. Her page held her name in the clerk's careful hand — *Marguerite Voss, scribe, indentured, term seven years or sum in full* — and beneath it, the figure. Four hundred and eleven crowns. The price of her mother's last winter: physicians, medicines, the good bed, the burial.

Her mother had died owing that much. Marguerite had inherited it the way other people inherited eyes or tempers.

Seven years of scribe work at the temple paid it down at a rate that was almost mathematical in its cruelty — enough progress each year to make the next year bearable, never enough to see an end. She copied psalters, indexed donation rolls, entered tithes into columns so long they made her eyes swim. Honest work, watched work, work that kept her small and visible and exactly where the temple wanted her.

And then, three nights a week, she came here, to the dark market, and sold knots and nails and lies to people the temple had no use for, and carried the coins home sewn into her hem, and every crown went into a clay pot beneath her floorboard, and every crown was a sentence crossed out of the ledger in advance.

Two hundred and ninety-six crowns remained. She did the arithmetic nightly, sometimes twice, the way other people prayed. One hundred and fifteen crowns saved. At her current pace — temple wages plus the market, minus rent, minus food, minus the bribes that kept the ditch-watch blind — she would be free in a little over two years.

Free. She turned the word over some nights like a coin she wasn't sure was real. Free meant a name no one else held. Free meant walking out of Corvane's gates without a chit. Free meant — she didn't know what. She'd been owned since twenty-two. She suspected freedom felt like falling.

"Voss." The eel-woman's voice, low, from the barrel. "Clerk coming up the row. Temple gray. Move your pretty things."

Marguerite's hands were already moving. This was the liturgy she knew best: cloth over the cords, crate-lid over the nails, the lacquered box slid into the gap between the rain barrel and the wall where the shadow was thickest. Ten seconds, start to finish. She'd timed it with her thumb against her pulse.

By the time the clerk reached their stretch of the row, her plank held nothing but a half-finished embroidery — a border pattern she kept for exactly this purpose, needles and gray thread, the portrait of a woman mending linens by firelight because she had nothing better to do.

There were two of them. That was wrong. Clerks worked alone; alone they were bullies, and bullies could be endured. Two moved differently — one carrying the lantern high, one carrying a slate, and the one with the slate was young, new, eager, which was the worst kind. The lantern-bearer was old Hask, whom the ditch-watch paid in silence, and Hask's light swung with a certain studied carelessness that Marguerite read like a letter from a friend.

But the young one stopped at the eel-woman's barrel first.

Marguerite bent over her embroidery and listened with her whole body, the way she'd learned to listen in her mother's sickroom — not to words but to weather.

"—reported, that's all I'm saying," the young clerk was saying. His voice had the wet enthusiasm of a man early in a career he intended to make on other people's backs. "Reports of unlicensed sales. Charms. Someone on this row is selling charms."

"Charms," Hask repeated, bored. He played his lantern across the stalls — across the boot-man, who was asleep; across the eel-woman, who was suddenly very interested in a bucket; across Marguerite, hunched and gray and stitching, a woman so thoroughly unremarkable that his light passed over her like weather over stone. "Half this city sells charms. Old wives tying knots against colic. You going to arrest grandmothers?"

"I'm going to arrest whoever's making money at it." The slate scratched. "There's a list. Names that've come up."

Marguerite's needle did not pause. Her heartbeat did. She kept both steady by force of will and three years of practice, and stared at her embroidery hoop, and became, with the completeness of long discipline, a woman named nothing, doing nothing, worth nothing.

Because there was a list. Of course there was a list. There had been rumors of a list since midwinter — the Inquisitor's office compiling names from informants, from tailors who noticed hems heavy with coins, from neighbors with grudges. She'd told herself her name wouldn't be on it. She was careful. She was forgettable. She was a temple scribe, for mercy's sake, a woman the clerks nodded to in corridors without seeing her face.

But lists didn't care about faces. Lists cared about patterns. And a scribe who bought waxed thread in quantities no seamstress needed, who visited the paupers' field more often than grief required, who had a standing arrangement with a dye-seller for gray-green pigment —

The young clerk's boots stopped in front of her plank.

"Mending?" he said.

"Linens," Marguerite said, and looked up with the exact degree of wariness a respectable woman shows a stranger at night — not fear, not guilt, just the tired displeasure of being interrupted. "If you're buying, I've none for sale. If you're preaching, I'm already saved. If you're collecting, the temple took my tithe Tuesday and I've the receipt somewhere."

Something flickered in his face — disappointment, maybe, or the frustration of a man who'd rehearsed a confrontation and been handed a housewife instead. His eyes went over her stall. Embroidery hoop. Thread. A basket with bread ends and a stoppered jug of small beer. A woman's things. A poor woman's things.

"Sell charms?" he asked, and it was almost perfunctory now.

"Do I look like I can afford to give things away?" Marguerite said. "Charms are for people with money to lose. I mend. I drink. I sleep. Ask Hask, he's watched this row longer than you've been shaving."

Hask, summoned, grunted confirmation without looking at her — and there it was, the return on three years of overpaying a corrupt watchman, delivered with the timing of a gift.

The clerk lingered a moment longer. Marguerite felt his gaze like a hand patting down her coat, and thanked every indifferent saint that the lacquered box was in the wall-shadow and the green glass disc was against her ribs and her face was doing what her face did best, which was nothing at all.

"Name?" he said finally, chalk ready.

"Voss. Marguerite Voss." She gave it flatly, wearily, the way you surrender a thing that's been taken so many times it's lost its weight. Let him write it down. A thousand Marguerite Vosses mended linens in this city. A name on a clerk's slate was a grain of sand on a beach of names.

He wrote it. He moved on. He shook down the boot-man awake now and frightened, and bought nothing, and learned nothing, and eventually the lantern-light drained away up the row toward the Processional, and the dark market exhaled, and the eel-woman spat into her barrel and said, "You've ice in you, girl, I'll say that."

"I've debt in me," Marguerite said. "It's colder."

* * *

She packed slowly after that, letting the row settle around her, letting her pulse come down from wherever it had climbed. The eel-woman pressed a smoked eel on her — payment, she claimed, for the charm that had kept her son's boat off the rocks, though the son swore it was the rope he'd doubled. Marguerite took it because refusing gifts in the dark market was its own kind of insult, and walked home the long way, past the tannery, past the shrine of Saint Ossian with its lamp burning over the poor-box, past the temple itself.

She always looked at the temple. She couldn't help it. It rose over the Processional like a gray mountain given architecture, and somewhere inside it was the vault, and the ledger, and her page — *Marguerite Voss, scribe, indentured* — waiting for her like a cell with her name already carved on the door.

One hundred and fifteen crowns in a clay pot. Two hundred and ninety-six to go. Two years, if nothing went wrong.

Nothing ever went wrong for long. That was the bargain she'd struck with the world: be small, be dull, be a woman the city's great machinery of records and rules passed over like Hask's lantern, and in exchange, survive. She had kept the bargain faithfully. She intended to keep it until the day the last crown dropped into the pot and she walked out of the counting-house with her page torn out and burned, and became, at twenty-five, the sole owner of her own name.

At her lodging — two rooms above a cooper's shop, reached by stairs that complained in six languages — she checked the door's latch, checked the floorboard, checked the pot. One hundred and sixteen crowns now; tonight had added one. She sat on the edge of her narrow bed with the green glass disc in her palm and let herself look at it, the way she allowed herself one small dishonesty per night.

Firelight caught the spiral. Faint. Fine. Turning, almost, if you looked too long.

She put it away. She banked her candle. She slept the thin sleep of the indebted, and dreamed, as she sometimes did, of a country she had never seen — green hills under a sky with too many stars, and a language being spoken somewhere below her window, soft and rhythmic, that she understood perfectly and could not, waking, remember a single word of.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the shutters, and with it a sound.

Not a knock. Knocking she knew — the cooper's apprentices, the rent-collector, the temple summons-bell. This was smaller. A scratching. A soft, patient scraping at the base of her door, like a cat, or like fingers.

Marguerite rose with the poker from the hearth and opened the door on a child.

A boy, eight years old perhaps, barefoot on the frost-dusted landing, in a smock too fine for a beggar and too filthy for anything else. He was crouched at the threshold, and he had been drawing in the dust of the boards — spirals, a dozen of them, overlapping, radiating out from her door like ripples from a dropped stone.

He looked up at her. His eyes were the gray-green of canal water, of dyed cord, of the glass against her ribs.

And then he spoke — a few words, quiet, unhurried, in a language that had no business existing, that had been outlawed and erased and struck from every record in the kingdom before Marguerite was born.

She understood him.

Every word.

The poker slipped in her hand and rang against the floorboards, and the boy watched her with those eyes, and waited, as if he had known — as if he had walked all night through a city that had forgotten a country, to the one door where someone would open it and understand.

"*You're the one who keeps the words,*" he had said. "*I looked for you by the spirals.*"

Marguerite stood in her doorway with the frost coming in and her heart going like a trapped bird, and heard herself answer him — fluently, impossibly, in the same tongue:

"What words? What are you?"

And the child smiled, faint and tired, like someone setting down a weight carried very far.

"You'll want to sit down," he said.
