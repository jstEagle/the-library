## 2. A Child at the Threshold

Dawn in Corvane came the color of old pewter, sliding down between the tenement roofs like something ashamed to be seen. Marguerite Voss had been awake for it — she was always awake for it, because dawn was when the night market's stragglers went home and the temple bells began their first quarrel with the sky, and between those two sounds there was a narrow hour that belonged to no one.

She had spent it counting.

The coins lived in a clay jar beneath the floorboard by her bed, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth gone soft with handling. She counted them the way other people prayed: copper first, then the two silver bits she'd earned off the widow Anselm's charm against joint-ache, then the grubby half-copper someone had bitten to test and left dented like a small moon. She knew their total the way she knew her own heartbeat. Fourteen marks, eleven coppers, three silvers. Against a debt of four hundred marks owed to the Temple of the Ledgered Word, it was a grain of sand against a mountain, but she counted anyway. Counting was a kind of defiance. As long as the number changed, however slowly, she was still moving.

Outside her window, Corvane woke in its usual order. The fishwives first, their voices like knives on stone. Then the temple acolytes in their grey robes, sweeping the steps of Saint Verane's with brooms that made a sound like slow forgiveness. Then the carts, the dogs, the children sent out for water who turned the errand into a game and were shouted back into line.

Marguerite watched none of it directly. She had learned, over three years of being owned, that watching things directly invited them to watch you back. Instead she kept her eyes on the ledger page propped against the water jug — her own hand, neat as stitching, recording last night's sales in the cipher only she could read. *One charm, hearth-luck. One charm, sweet-dreams. One charm, unlisted.* The unlisted ones paid best. They always did. People would haggle over luck all night, but they'd pay silver without blinking for something they were too frightened or too ashamed to name aloud.

She was grinding ink for the day's copying work — legitimate work, the kind that kept her hands clean enough for inspection — when she heard the knock.

Not a knock, precisely. A sound at the door. Soft, arrhythmic, like a bird testing glass.

Marguerite went still. Her lodgings sat above a dyer's shop in the Tanners' Reach, which meant anyone climbing her stairs had already passed through a corridor of chemical stink that discouraged casual visitors. The people who came up anyway fell into two categories: customers, and trouble. Customers knocked twice, quick and confident. Trouble announced itself louder.

This was neither. This was barely anything at all.

She set down the pestle. From the shelf above her cot she took the little knife she used for trimming quills — not a weapon, exactly, but sharp enough, and near enough to her hand that it counted as one if it needed to. Three years of selling forbidden charms taught you where every edge in your house lived.

The stairs creaked under her weight in the places she knew. At the door she paused, ear close to the wood, and heard breathing. Small. Shallow. The breathing of someone trying very hard to be quiet and not quite managing it.

"Who's there?" she said.

Silence. Then the breathing again, faster now.

She should have opened it. She told herself later that any sensible person would have waited, would have called down to the dyer, would have done any of the hundred careful things her survival depended on. But there was something in that breathing — something that snagged on a hook buried so deep in her she hadn't known it was there until this moment pulled the line taut.

She opened the door.

* * *

The boy stood in the grey light like something the fog had set down and forgotten to take back.

He was eight years old, perhaps, though hunger and sleeplessness made age hard to read on children. Barefoot. His feet were grey with street dust, the toenails chipped, one heel cracked in a way that must have hurt with every step. He wore a shirt that had belonged to someone larger once, sleeves rolled and unrolled and rolled again, and trousers gone thin at the knees. His hair was dark and cut badly, in the blunt way hair gets cut by someone impatient and afraid of being caught doing it.

He didn't look at her face. He looked at her hands — at the quill-knife in one of them — and then, deliberately, he lifted both his own hands, palms out, empty.

*I'm not armed,* the gesture said. *I know what you're holding. I'm not a threat.*

Children from the Reach learned gestures like that early. It wasn't the gesture that unsettled her. It was how precisely he made it, how calm he was, standing barefoot in the cold with dawn still wet on the rooftops. He wasn't trembling. He was waiting.

"Are you lost?" Marguerite asked.

He shook his head.

"Are you hurt?"

Another shake. Then, after a moment, a tilt of the head — *yes and no*, the gesture said, which was an answer that made no sense and which he offered anyway, honestly, as if nonsense were simply the shape of the truth.

"Where are your parents?"

He looked at her then. Not at her hands — at her face, straight on, and his eyes were dark and very old in a way that eyes shouldn't be at eight, the way wells are old, the way winter is old. And in that look there was something she couldn't name, something that raised along her arms the fine hairs, some animal recognition with nothing to attach itself to.

Then he stepped past her into the room.

It was so smoothly done, so entirely without hesitation, that she found herself stepping aside to let him, and only understood afterward that she had done it — that some part of her had opened the door wider before her mind had finished objecting. He crossed her single room the way you cross a room you have crossed a thousand times. He avoided the loose floorboard by the window without looking at it. He settled onto the stool by the cold hearth, folded his hands in his lap, and looked at her with the patience of someone who has traveled a long way and intends to be understood.

"You can't stay here," Marguerite said. Her voice came out harder than she intended, and she was glad of it. Hardness was armor; she'd worn it so long she sometimes forgot it was wearing her. "I don't know you. I can't feed you. I have work, and if anyone sees a child in my rooms I'll have the temple down on me before noon, and believe me, you do not want to learn what that means."

The boy regarded her steadily. Then he rose from the stool, crouched by the hearth, and dipped one finger in the fine ash that hadn't been swept from the last fire.

He drew a spiral.

Not a scribble. A spiral — precise, deliberate, wound outward from a center point like a shell cut open, each curve equidistant from the last, drawn in one unbroken motion that ended exactly where it began to end, as if he had drawn this shape many times and his hand remembered the way even if his mind had never needed to think about it. In the ash it gleamed faintly, darker than ash should be, as if the finger had left something behind besides disturbance.

Marguerite stared at it, and the staring hurt.

That was the only word for it. Looking at the spiral produced a sensation behind her sternum like a key turning in a lock that had rusted shut — pressure, resistance, and then a terrible smooth give, as if something inside her had begun, reluctantly, to open. She knew the shape. That was the absurd part. She had never seen it in her life; she was certain of that the way she was certain of her own name; and yet her body knew it, the way a body knows a staircase in the dark, and the knowing arrived not as thought but as ache.

"Stop," she said, and didn't know what she was telling him to stop.

He didn't stop. He drew a second spiral beside the first, smaller, and then a third, and arranged them — she saw it happen and could not afterwards describe the arrangement except that it was right, that the placing of them carried meaning the way words carry meaning, grammar without language. Then he sat back on his heels and looked up at her, and his expression was the expression of a messenger who has delivered the message and is now waiting to learn whether it will be believed.

"I don't understand," Marguerite whispered.

And the boy spoke.

* * *

Later — much later, in the hours when she would lie awake cataloguing everything that had happened to her since dawn and finding all of it impossible — she would try to reconstruct those first words, and fail, because the memory refused to sit still. What she could hold onto was this: the sound of it. The sound came first, before sense. A language with vowels that opened like doors and consonants that fell soft as ash, syllables that seemed to arrive from further away than the boy's mouth, as if the room itself were speaking through him. It was nothing like trade-tongue. It was nothing like High Corvanic, the temple's liturgical snarl. It was nothing like anything she had ever heard.

And she understood every word.

Not guessed. Not inferred from context and tone, the way you might follow a foreign prayer. Understood — instantly, completely, the way you understand your own thoughts, with no gap at all between hearing and meaning. The boy looked at the spiral in the ash and said a word, and the word meant *the turning*, and she knew it meant *the turning* the way she knew that water was wet, knowledge arriving from somewhere below thought, from somewhere older than her learning.

"The turning," she repeated, in his tongue, before she could stop herself.

Her own voice saying it nearly undid her. The language fit her mouth. That was the horror of it — the beauty of it — the thing she would carry for the rest of whatever her life was going to be. The syllables sat in her throat like they had been measured for it. There was no accent. There was no effort. Somewhere in her, in the marrow-deep place where a person keeps the languages of childhood, this language had been waiting, folded up small, for longer than she could account for.

The boy's whole face changed.

It was like watching frost reverse. The oldness went out of his eyes, or rather the oldness was joined by something young and startled and desperate, and he scrambled forward across the floor on his knees and took her hands — took them in both of his, his fingers cold as river stones — and spoke again, fast now, tumbling, the words pouring out of him the way water pours through a broken dam.

*You speak it. You speak it, you speak it, I walked so far, I walked until my feet stopped being feet, and everyone looks at me like the words are wind, everyone, the grey-robed ones, the kind ones, the ones who gave me bread and then looked at each other over my head — but you heard me. Say it again. Say my name.*

"I don't know your name," Marguerite said in trade-tongue, because her mind had caught up with her mouth at last and was screaming caution, and caution in Corvane spoke trade-tongue, always, only.

But the boy shook his head and said a word — a short word, two syllables, round at the front and closed at the back — and the word went into her like a hook going into water, silent and deep, and something on the far bottom of her shifted.

*Tam,* the word meant. Just that. A name. A boy's name, worn smooth as a river pebble from use.

"Tam," she said.

He wept. There was no warning in it, no build — one moment he was kneeling there gripping her hands, and the next his face simply broke and the tears came down in silence, because of course he didn't cry loudly; crying loudly was a luxury of children who had someone to come running. He cried the way the very poor cry, economically, terribly, his shoulders doing all the work while his face tried to hold its shape and failed.

Marguerite did not decide to comfort him. That was the thing she would remember most clearly afterward, the detail that made the rest of it real: there was no decision anywhere in it. Her arms were around him before her fear could file its objection, and he was pressed against her chest with his fists knotted in her shirt, and she was rocking him slightly, back and forth, humming — humming, in a language she did not know she knew, a fragment of melody with no beginning and no end, a lullaby-shaped thing that surfaced through her like a bubble through deep water and burst into the room.

She stopped humming the instant she heard herself.

Tam lifted his head. His tear-streaked face wore an expression she had no name for — hope and grief fused into a single unbearable alloy.

"That's hers," he said. "That's the song. You knew it all along."

"Whose?" Marguerite asked. "Whose song? Whose—"

But the question dissolved even as she formed it, because the answer was rising in her the way the melody had risen, and the answer had no edges yet, only weight. Something long-buried had stirred. She felt it the way you feel weather changing — not seeing the wind, only its effects: pressure in the ears, a taste of iron, the sense that the air had become full of something about to fall.

Outside, the temple bells rang the third hour. Grey-robed acolytes were sweeping the steps of Saint Verane's. Somewhere in the city, a clerk was opening a ledger in which her name was written in a hand that was not her own, and the debt it recorded was four hundred marks, and the debt was the least of what the ledger held.

Tam wiped his face with his sleeve and looked at her with those old, well-deep eyes, and said, in the language no one should know, in the language her own mouth had been keeping secret from her:

"We have to go soon. Before they remember where I am."

Marguerite Voss, who survived by being small, by being unmemorable, by never asking questions whose answers might have weight — Marguerite Voss heard herself say:

"Who remembers?"

And the boy pointed one ash-dusted finger at the floor. Down. Toward the temple. Toward the archive. Toward the ledgers.

Toward whatever it was, sleeping beneath her own name, that had just begun to wake.
