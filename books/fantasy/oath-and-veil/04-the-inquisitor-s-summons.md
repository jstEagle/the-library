## 4. The Inquisitor's Summons

The summons came at the hour when Corvane's bells rang wrong.

Marguerite had learned to read the temple's bells the way sailors read weather — the dawn bell bright and brassy, the noon bell flat with heat, the dusk bell so worn it hummed a half-tone below true. Three years of debt-service in the Scriptorium of Saint Vellum had taught her that the bells were never wrong; only her ears were. But this morning, as gray light seeped through the shutters of her rented room above the chandler's shop on Tallow Row, the bell that woke her was one she had never heard before: a single low stroke, struck eleven times, each toll spaced exactly as long as a held breath.

She lay still beneath her thin blanket and listened to it end.

Then she heard the footsteps on the stairs — not the chandler's widow, whose knees announced every landing like a town crier, but someone lighter, someone who climbed without touching the walls. Someone official.

Marguerite sat up and did what she always did first: she counted. The coins from last night's market were sewn into the hem of her spare shift, forty-one coppers and two silver marks wrapped in waxed cloth. Her charms — the ones she hadn't sold — were dissolved in vinegar in the wash basin, their inscriptions bleeding off the bone like old bruises. The boy was not here. She had sent him out the window before full dark with three coppers and instructions to stay away until the evening bell, and he had gone without argument, which frightened her more than any argument would have. Children who obeyed too easily had learned somewhere that obedience was survival.

A knock. Two raps, precise as a signature.

"Marguerite Voss." A voice through the door, smooth and unhurried. "You are expected."

She knew that voice. Everyone in Corvane who dealt in the temple's shadow economy knew that voice, though most knew it only by reputation, the way you know the sound of a wolf you've never seen. Inquisitor Halev did not raise his voice because he had never needed to.

"I'm expected where?" she called back, and made her voice come out rough with sleep, annoyed, small. "I have scriptorium duty at the seventh hour. If I'm late they dock my ledger."

"They will not dock your ledger today," the voice said. "Open the door, please."

There was no threat in it. That was the terrible thing about Halev, according to everyone who had survived an interview with him — there was never any threat. Threats implied uncertainty about the outcome.

Marguerite dressed quickly in her scribe's gray, bound her hair back plainly, and opened the door.

The man standing on the landing was narrower than his reputation. He wore the Inquisition's dove-gray coat with no ornament except the small brass seal at the collar — a closed book stamped with a keyhole. His face was long and mild, the face of a patient schoolmaster, and his eyes were the color of weak tea. He held a leather folio under one arm and nothing else. No guards. He didn't need them; the whole building was his guard, the whole city was.

"Inquisitor," Marguerite said, and bowed the exact depth a debtor bows to an officer of her creditor — respectful, incurious, already bored.

"Scribe Voss." He inclined his head in return. "You slept well, I hope."

"Well enough."

"The widow downstairs says you keep odd hours." He said it pleasantly, as if remarking on the weather. "In and out after dark. Visitors at strange times. And last night—" he paused, consulting nothing, remembering everything, "—last night, sounds. Singing, the widow thought. Or chanting. In a language she didn't recognize. She found it upsetting."

Marguerite felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath her, the way a deck tilts when a wave passes underneath. *The widow.* Of course. The walls of Tallow Row were paper and prayer; she had known that when she took the room, had counted on the widow's bad knees and earlier bedtimes, and last night — last night Tam had stood at her table tracing spirals in spilled flour while the candle flame bent toward his mouth like a flower toward sun, and Marguerite had answered him in a language she had no right to know, and they had both forgotten, in the wonder of it, to be quiet.

"It was me," she said. "I say prayers sometimes. Old ones. My grandmother taught me. It helps me sleep."

Halev smiled. It was a good smile, warm even, and it reached all the way to his eyes, which was how Marguerite knew it meant nothing at all.

"Your grandmother," he said. "What was her name?"

"She died when I was small. I don't remember much."

"What was *her* name?"

"Voss," Marguerite said. "We're all Voss. It's a common name in the river districts."

"It is," Halev agreed mildly. "Very common. So common that the census rolls list four hundred and six Vosses in Corvane alone, and yet the debt ledger of Saint Vellum lists yours with unusual care. Unusual detail." He tapped the folio under his arm once, lightly, the way a man might tap a locked door. "Would you like to know why?"

"No," Marguerite said honestly, and then caught herself. "That is — I mean, if it concerns my debt, I'd rather hear it at the counter, from the clerk, where corrections can be entered properly. I wouldn't want to misremember anything important."

Something flickered behind the tea-colored eyes. Not anger. Interest. It was worse.

"You are careful," Halev said. "Three years in the Scriptorium and not one late mark, not one disputed entry, not one complaint. You copy contracts for a living, so you know better than anyone what a sentence can do to a life. You are careful with words." He stepped back from her doorway, gesturing down the stairs with an open hand. "Come. Walk with me. Careful people make the best conversations."

* * *

They walked through morning Corvane side by side, and Marguerite hated every step of it, because walking beside an Inquisitor was its own kind of announcement. Fishwives fell quiet as they passed. A beggar outside the tannery looked at Halev and then at her, and his eyes asked a question she pretended not to see: *what did she do?* By the time they crossed the Tanner's Bridge, half the district would know that Marguerite Voss had been collected at dawn, and by dusk the story would have grown teeth. Smallness, she thought bitterly, was a garment that tore easily when someone pulled it.

The temple of Saint Vellum rose over the city's heart like a paper mountain — white stone, slate roofs, and the great Ledger Hall at its center where every debt in Corvane was recorded in triplicate. Marguerite had walked through its public courts a thousand times. But Halev led her past the public courts, past the Scriptorium's antechamber where her fellow scribes glanced up and quickly down, past doors she had dusted around for three years without ever learning what was behind them, down a stair that smelled of cold stone and lamp oil, into a room with no windows.

It was furnished almost gently. Two chairs. A table. A carafe of water and two cups, as if this were hospitality. On the wall hung a framed document, yellowed with age, its text too faded to read — some ancient decree, displayed the way other offices displayed hunting trophies.

"Sit, please," Halev said.

She sat. He sat across from her, set the folio on the table between them, and did not open it.

"Do you know what happens to a debt when the debtor cannot be found?" he asked.

"The debt is transferred," Marguerite said. "To kin, or to the estate. Standard practice, Article Nine."

"Mm. And what happens when the debtor has no kin? No estate? When the person who owes us is, in every record that matters, *alone*?"

Marguerite kept her hands folded in her lap and her breathing even. "Then the debt... waits."

"Yes." Halev poured water into both cups, an oddly courteous gesture, and pushed one toward her. "Debts wait. Debts are patient. Do you know why? Because a debt isn't money, Scribe Voss. Money is only the shape it wears. A debt is a claim on a *person*. As long as we hold the claim, the person is — in a sense — ours. Their labor, certainly. Their time. And their name." He drank. "The ledger holds your true name, Marguerite Voss. Your full name, written the day you signed, in ink mixed for permanence. Everything else about you can change — your lodging, your work, your face, God forbid — but that page does not change. That page is more real than you are. If you and the page ever disagree about who you are, the page wins. That is not cruelty. That is simply what a record *is*."

"I've copied that principle often enough," Marguerite said. Her voice sounded steadier than her hands felt. "Is there a correction pending on my account? An error?"

"No error." He set down his cup. "But there is a report. Filed yesterday, the ninth hour of night. A neighbor reports strange sounds from your lodging — voices, chanting, a language unknown to her. She used the word 'unholy,' though I suspect she simply means 'unfamiliar.' The report was routed to me because—" he paused, and for the first time something almost human crossed his face, something like reluctance, or its excellent imitation, "—because I have a particular interest in unfamiliar languages."

The room felt smaller. Marguerite became aware of the stone pressing in on all sides, of the weight of earth above the ceiling, of the door behind her, which she had not heard lock but suddenly could not be sure hadn't.

"There are many languages in Corvane," she said. "Dockside traders speak six apiece."

"There are many languages," Halev agreed. "And then there are languages that appear in no grammar, no trade roll, no parish register. Languages that arrive from nowhere, spoken by people who insist they learned them from grandmothers who also appear in no record." He leaned forward slightly. "Tell me about your grandmother, Marguerite."

"I told you. I barely remember her."

"That is what interests me." His voice stayed gentle, almost kind. "Everyone remembers their grandmother. Even orphans remember grandmothers — invented ones, borrowed ones, the neighbor woman who fed them. Memory abhors a vacancy. But you —" he opened the folio at last, and turned it toward her, and there was her own signature looking up at her, three years old, in ink the color of dried blood, and beneath it, in the clerk's careful hand, the words *origin: unrecorded*, "— your contract says you came to Corvane from nowhere at all. No birth parish. No family roll. No prior residence. You walked out of blank space at nineteen years of age and asked the temple for bread and a pen. Do you know how rare that is? People have histories the way rivers have beds. You arrived dry."

"I was orphaned young. War took my village."

"Which war?"

"I don't recall the name of it."

"No one recalls the name of it," Halev said softly. "Isn't that curious? There are gaps in the archives, Scribe Voss. Whole districts of the past with nothing written in them, and everyone agrees not to notice, the way a family agrees not to mention the empty chair. I have spent eleven years noticing." He closed the folio. "So when a report arrives concerning a woman with no history, chanting in a language with no name, I am naturally attentive. Naturally curious. Curiosity is my whole profession."

Marguerite's mouth was dry. She reached for the water and stopped herself halfway, refusing the small surrender of the offered cup. "What is it you want from me, Inquisitor?"

"The truth," he said, and smiled again, warm as lamplight. "Which is a thing you clearly know how to counterfeit, so let me make the exchange simple. Tell me who was in your room last night. Tell me what language you spoke. Do this, and the report dies here, in this room, unwritten. Your ledger stays clean. Your small, careful, unmemorable life continues exactly as you have built it." He spread his hands. "I am offering you the cheapest price you will ever be offered. Silence for silence."

For one vertiginous moment Marguerite was tempted — truly tempted — to give him Tam. To say *a beggar child, a stray, I found him and he ran off*, and let the machinery of Corvane grind after some ghost while she went back to copying contracts. It would work. She was good at this. Three years of being small had made her fluent in the dialect of almost-true.

But she saw the candle flame bending toward a child's voice. She heard her own mouth answering him in words she had never been taught. And she understood, with a cold clarity that felt like falling, that Halev already knew more than he was saying — that this whole courteous theater was not an interrogation but a *measurement*. He wanted to see what she would protect. Whatever she protected, he would take.

So she lied the way she had never dared lie before: completely.

"There was no one," she said. "I chant to myself. Old prayers, nonsense syllables, whatever settles the mind. If my neighbor's ear is offended, I'll pray in trade-tongue going forward, loudly and orthodoxy-approved. Write whatever correction you like against my account. I'll serve the extra hours."

The silence that followed lasted five full breaths. Halev studied her the way a jeweler studies a stone — not for beauty, but for flaws, for the line where it might split.

"You refuse," he said finally, wonderingly. "Over nothing. Over a report worth less than a day's fish. Fascinating." He rose, tucked the folio under his arm, and moved to the door. Then he paused, hand on the latch, and spoke without turning around.

"One more thing, Scribe Voss. A courtesy, since you've been so cooperative about nothing at all." His voice dropped into something quieter, and somehow vaster, like the space inside a bell. "Yesterday afternoon, a clerk reviewing market-toll records noted a woman selling charms on Wrackbone Stair. Bone inscriptions. The vendor was described as small, plain, forgettable — the description is nearly useless, vendors like that are everywhere, the city breeds them. But the same clerk noted that among the charms was one inscription he did not recognize, and he has an excellent memory for scripts. He wrote it down from recollection." Halev turned his head just enough that she could see the edge of his profile, the mild line of his jaw. "It matched nothing in our libraries. It matched nothing anywhere. Except that last night, according to my informant at the chandler's, the woman upstairs was speaking in what sounded very much like the same alphabet."

He opened the door. Gray light from the stairwell cut across the floor between them.

"Someone reported strange sounds at your lodging, Marguerite Voss. I am telling you, as a professional courtesy, that the reporting has only begun. The ledger holds your true name — and names, unlike debts, cannot hide forever. Go home. Copy your contracts. Pray in trade-tongue." He stepped through the doorway, and his parting words drifted back down the cold stairwell, soft as ash settling:

"And do give my regards to whoever taught you the old tongue. They must be very proud."

* * *

She did not go home. She walked out of the temple into the hard white morning and made herself move at a scribe's pace, unhurried, anonymous, while her mind screamed. At the Tanner's Bridge she stopped, gripped the rail, and watched the brown water carry its cargo of cabbage leaves and candle-stubs toward the sea, and she understood the shape of the trap closing around her: Halev had not needed her confession. He had needed only to confirm that she would lie for *someone* — and now he knew there was a someone, and now every eye in the temple district would be turned toward Tallow Row, and the boy would come back at the evening bell, barefoot and trusting, straight into the light of it.

Unless she reached him first.

Marguerite pushed off the rail and walked — faster now, still not running, never running — toward the warren of lanes behind the chandler's shop, where a child who spoke the language of a country that had never existed was waiting for her, counting coppers in the dust, believing she would come back.

The evening bell was seven hours away. She intended to be there in one.
