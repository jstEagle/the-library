## 6. What the Ledger Remembers

The contract arrived the way all temple documents arrived: without ceremony, without apology, folded twice and slid beneath her door by a hand she never saw. Marguerite found it at dawn, when she opened the door to shake out the dust of yesterday's candle smoke, and stepped on it before she saw it.

She stood very still for a moment, one bare foot on the paper, listening.

Corvane's morning noises came up the stairwell — a water-seller crying his rounds, shutters banging open, somewhere below a dog arguing with a cart. No footsteps retreating. No clerk lingering in the dark of the landing. Whoever had delivered it was long gone, which was its own kind of message. The temple did not sneak. The temple *arrived*, and let you know that it had always known exactly where you slept.

She picked up the paper. Her name was written across the top in the trade-tongue's clean, blunt characters: **MARGUERITE VOSS, DEBTOR OF THE THIRD RANK, LEDGER REFERENCE 4417-C**.

Below that, a single line: *Annual review of terms to be conducted at the debtor's place of residence. Present all records.*

They wanted to see her ledger. All of it — every charm sold, every coin tucked into the false bottom of her flour tin, every small crime of survival she had committed over three years of being owned.

Marguerite closed the door. She leaned her back against it. Tam watched her from the corner where he had made himself small again, knees drawn up, the way children learn to take up no space at all. He had been with her four days now, four days of spirals drawn in dust and words in a language that should not exist, and already he could read her silences better than most people read her speech.

"They're coming," she said.

He tilted his head.

"The temple." She held up the contract. "They want to look at my accounts."

Tam looked at the paper for a long moment. Then, slowly, he raised one finger and pointed not at her name but at the seal at the bottom — the black sunburst of the Office of Records — and then at himself, and made a sound low in his throat. Not a word. A warning note, like a plucked string.

"I know," she said, and was surprised to find she meant it. She did know. Some animal part of her had gone cold the instant she touched the paper. "I know."

* * *

She spent the day doing what she had done every day for three years: being meticulous.

If they were going to examine her ledger, then her ledger would be perfect. She sat at her worktable with the official copy spread before her — the debt contract itself, the founding document of her servitude, renewed each year with fresh ink and fresh signatures — and beside it her private record of sales, and she began the slow, grinding labor of reconciliation. Coin in. Coin owed. Interest calculated to the quarter-mark. Every discrepancy smoothed over or explained away in the flat, blameless prose of a woman who had learned to write herself invisible.

It was nearly dusk when she found it.

Not a discrepancy. Something stranger. She had been copying the contract's opening clause — the boilerplate every debtor memorized until it lost all meaning — and her pen had slowed, because for the first time in three years she was actually *reading* it, reading it as a scribe reads, line by line, letter by letter, the way you read an enemy's face.

*Let it be recorded in the sight of the Ninefold Office,* it began, *that the party herein named does acknowledge a debt of [amount redacted] incurred in consideration of services rendered, and does bind herself, her person, her labor, and her name to the satisfaction of said debt—*

Her name.

There it was, where it always was. **MARGUERITE VOSS.** Bold, centered, unambiguous.

But Marguerite Voss was also a scribe. And scribes, unlike other people, knew what ink did. Knew how a page remembered things its writers tried to bury. She tilted the contract toward the window, toward the last slanted light, and there — faint as breath on glass, visible only when the light raked across it at the proper angle — she saw them.

Characters beneath her name. Underneath it, in the literal sense: pressed into the parchment before the trade-tongue had been written over them, showing through like bones through skin.

They were not trade-tongue letters.

Her heart did something complicated — a stumble, a lurch, a strange falling sensation, as though the floor of the world had developed a seam. Because she recognized those characters. Not from study; Corvane burned books in older alphabets, and the Inquisition's lists of forbidden scripts were longer than its lists of permitted ones. She recognized them the way you recognize your own handwriting.

She recognized them from Tam.

He had drawn spirals in her doorway dust, and loops, and tall strokes crossed with short ones, and she had understood him without understanding why. Now the same shapes rose ghostlike through the officious black of her own contract, and she could *almost* read them — almost, the way you almost remember a dream, the meaning sitting just behind a door that would not quite open.

She fetched a candle. Held the flame close, low, so the light climbed the page instead of striking it flat.

And the hidden script came up out of the parchment like something surfacing through water.

Two lines. Perhaps three. Written in a careful, practiced hand — a scribe's hand, a professional's hand, someone who had set these letters down with confidence before some other hand had covered them. She bent until her nose nearly touched the page, and the shapes swam, and settled, and —

She read them.

That was the vertigo. Not the reading itself — the reading was easy, effortless, the letters pouring their meaning into her the way water pours downhill. The vertigo was in knowing she *shouldn't* be able to. It was like looking down and finding a second pair of hands resting in her lap, hands she had never seen before, holding a tool she had never been taught.

The first line said:

*Margerit Vel-Ossa.*

A name. Her name. But not her name. *Vel-Ossa.* It fit her mouth strangely and perfectly at once, the way a key fits a lock it was cut for — she said it under her breath and her tongue knew the stresses without being told, knew to fall soft on the *Vel* and hold the *Ossa* like something carried.

The second line said:

*Born of the terraces, daughter of the salt-road, citizen of Veilmark, in the year the bells sang green.*

Citizen of Veilmark.

Veilmark, the word that was barely a word in Corvane anymore. People used it the way they used curses they'd half-forgotten the meaning of — a shape of the mouth with nothing behind it. Erased from every map. Struck from every registry. A country that had, according to everyone who might once have known better, never existed at all. When Marguerite had asked her temple tutors, as a child, what lay beyond the western hills, they had shown her maps of empty grassland and told her the question itself was impertinent.

*In the year the bells sang green.*

She didn't know what that meant. That was the horror of it — she didn't know, and yet the phrase ached. It ached the way a missing tooth aches, the tongue returning again and again to the gap. Somewhere in her there was a year when bells sang green, and she could feel the shape of its absence, a wound shaped exactly like a memory.

Marguerite sat back. The candle guttered. Tam had come to stand beside her chair — she hadn't heard him move; he moved like dust settling — and he was looking down at the contract with an expression no eight-year-old face should have been able to make. It was grief. Old, patient, personal grief.

He reached out one finger and touched the hidden name.

"Margerit Vel-Ossa," he said, in the old tongue, and the candle flame stood up straight and tall, leaning toward his voice like a flower toward sun.

Then he looked at her, and waited.

"That's not my name," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "My name is Marguerite Voss. I was born in Corvane. In the Tanner's Quarter. My mother was a dyer. My father—" 

She stopped.

Her father.

She reached for her father the way you reach for a stair in the dark, and there was nothing there. Nothing. She had a mother, a dyer, the smell of mordant and wet wool, that memory intact, vivid, hers. But her father was a smooth blank wall in the architecture of her past, and she had never once in twenty-six years thought to wonder at it. Who wonders at a wall? You hang things on it. You live beside it.

Except now she could see the edges of the wall. See that it had been *built*. See the mortar-marks of deliberate erasure, neat as a mason's course.

"My father," she said again, testing, and got nothing, and felt the floor tilt.

Tam took her hand. His fingers were cool. He pressed them, once, against the parchment — against the ghost-script — and then against her own chest, over her heart, and back to the page. *This. Is in here too.*

"No," she whispered.

But even as she said it, other gaps were opening, quiet as doors swinging on oiled hinges. Her childhood home — she had a street, Tanner's Quarter, a lane of dyers' sheds — but the house itself? She could not picture its door. Her mother's face: present, clear, beloved. Her mother's *name*: — nothing. A woman she loved beyond speech, and she could not summon the two syllables that called her. How had she never noticed? How had she walked around inside her own skull for twenty-six years and never once put her weight on these missing boards?

Because the missing pieces had been replaced. Smoothed over. Filled with plausible plaster — Corvane-born, temple-raised, debtor by misfortune — a life story with no holes a casual eye could catch. Only now, with the old script glowing up through the forgery, could she see that the whole edifice was a renovation built over ruins.

Someone had edited her. The way a scribe edits a flawed manuscript. Scraped the vellum. Wrote over it.

*Identity as something that can be confiscated.* The thought arrived complete, fully armored, and she understood with total clarity that it was true, that it had been done to her, that the woman named Marguerite Voss was not a person but a *record* — an entry, a fiction maintained by ink and habit — and that underneath her, patient as seed under snow, someone else had been waiting this whole time.

Margerit Vel-Ossa. Daughter of the salt-road. Citizen of a country that officially did not exist.

Tam tugged her sleeve. He pointed at the third hidden line — the last one, the one she had not yet dared read — and then at the seal of the Office of Records, the black sunburst, and his mouth went thin.

She looked. The light caught it. Three words, in the old tongue, in a different hand than the first two lines — a colder hand, an administrative hand, the hand of whoever had processed her like cargo:

*Payment received in full.*

Her memories. Her father, her mother's name, her house, her country, whatever else had been scraped away — they hadn't been destroyed. They had been *taken as payment*. Traded. Entered into a ledger somewhere in the temple's depths, filed among thousands of others, the scattered contents of an entire nation converted into revenue.

Three years she had counted coins against her debt, hoarding coppers, selling charms in the dark, believing she was buying back her freedom coin by coin. And the whole time, the price of her actual self had already been paid — paid by *her*, extracted from her, spent by men whose faces she had never seen. She was not working off a debt. She was the debt. Walking, writing, breathing collateral.

The candle flame bent sharply sideways, though there was no draft, bending toward the page, toward the name, as if the fire itself recognized her.

Marguerite laughed — one dry, cracked syllable of it, with nothing behind it — and pressed both hands flat against the table to keep the world from tilting off its axis. "I need to sit down."

She was already sitting down.

Tam climbed onto the chair beside her and leaned his small shoulder against her arm, and they stayed that way while the light died and the hidden script sank back beneath the trade-tongue, bone sinking back under skin.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the shuttered window instead with the contract in her lap, watching moonlight do what candlelight had done, making the ghost-letters rise and fade, rise and fade, like breathing. *Margerit Vel-Ossa.* Each time the name surfaced she felt that same double sensation — recognition and wrongness braided together, a bell rung in a room she couldn't enter.

Toward the smallest hour of the night, she heard movement on the stair.

Not the temple's tread. This was softer, deliberate, a walker who had learned to distribute her weight — and Marguerite was on her feet with a knife off the table before the second step creaked, because four days ago she had been a woman with only debts to lose, and now there was a sleeping child in the next room, and everything had changed weight.

The footsteps stopped outside her door. A pause. Then, quietly, through the wood, a woman's voice — low, roughened, controlled:

"You've been reading old scripts, scribe. I could feel it from the street. The words wake when their people wake."

Marguerite's grip tightened on the knife. "Who's there?"

"A keeper," the voice said, "of an oath I broke. I've been walking the border of a country that isn't there for eleven years, looking for the door out of it. And tonight the words woke up in this building." A beat. "Open the door, Margerit Vel-Ossa. We need to talk about what was taken from you — and about the child."

Outside, far off across the rooftops of Corvane, a temple bell began to ring.
