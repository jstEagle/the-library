## 7. The Erasure

Sera had not spoken the country's name aloud in eleven years.

She said it now, quietly, in the back room of Marguerite's lodging while Tam slept curled in the corner like a cat that had learned to distrust warmth. She said it the way you'd say a dead man's name at a grave you weren't sure was still there — *Veilmark* — and she watched the word land on Marguerite like a stone dropped into still water. The scribe went very still. Her hands, ink-stained and clever, flattened against the table as though the word had weight enough to knock her off balance.

"Say it again," Marguerite whispered.

"No." Sera kept her voice low. "Once is a debt already. Twice is a habit, and habits are how they catch you." She turned her cup of barley tea without drinking it. The tea had gone cold an hour ago; she'd made it only to have something to do with her hands. "You asked me why I came here. Why I've been following the sound of that language across half of Corvane. So I'll tell you. But you're going to wish I hadn't, by the end."

Marguerite sat. She did it the way Sera imagined she did everything — deliberately, economically, like someone who had learned that every motion might be counted against her. "Tell me anyway."

So Sera told her.

* * *

"You know what they teach in the temples," she began. "That Veilmark rebelled. That it raised its banners against the crown and was destroyed for it — burned, salted, broken on the wheel of righteous war. You've copied the histories a hundred times, I'd wager. You have better handwriting than most of them deserve."

"I've copied them," Marguerite admitted. "The Fall of Veilmark takes four pages in the standard codex."

"Four pages." Sera laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the hollow ring of a thing that had been dropped down a well. "Four pages for a country with three hundred years of songs."

She leaned forward, forearms on the table, and let herself remember — which was its own kind of punishment, because remembering was the only crime left in Corvane that no one could prove.

"The war story is a lie, but it's a *useful* lie, so it survives. Here's the truth instead. Veilmark didn't fall. Veilmark was *edited*."

"Edited."

"You work in records. Think about what a record does." Sera tapped the table with one finger. "A record tells reality what happened. That's not poetry, scribe — that's the oldest law of the craft, older than any temple. Write a thing down truly enough, in enough hands, witnessed by enough oaths, and the world agrees to keep it so. Every charter, every deed, every ledger in this city is a small act of enforced memory. The temples don't just *record* Corvane. They hold it in place."

Marguerite's eyes flicked toward the sleeping boy, then back. "And someone wrote Veilmark out."

"Not out. Not all at once — that would have failed, the way a wall fails if you pull one stone. No. They were patient." Sera's voice dropped, and the room seemed to lean in around them, the candle guttering as if listening. "It started with the maps. Cartographers' guilds received new commissions — redrawn borders, and where Veilmark had been, nothing. Not enemy territory. Not wilderness. *Nothing*. A blank space with no label, because a label, even a wrong one, would be something. Then the trade agreements. Every contract mentioning the name was re-copied without it, and the originals were burned in temple fires — properly burned, with the ash sealed, so the words couldn't haunt the smoke. Then the genealogies. Then the hymnals. Then the gravestones."

"How long?"

"Nine years." The number came out of Sera like a splinter. "Nine years of clerks and copyists and quiet men with ledgers, working from the edges inward, each edit small enough that no single one seemed worth dying over. A merchant's contract loses a line about tariffs at a border crossing he barely remembers visiting. A widow's marriage record omits her husband's birthplace. Who protests? What does a missing line matter?" She shook her head. "By the fourth year, people outside Veilmark couldn't say where it was anymore. By the sixth, they couldn't say what it had traded in or who had ruled it. By the ninth—"

She stopped. The candle flame stood perfectly straight, unnaturally still, and she realized her own voice had been doing the thing she hated — the old border-craft, the words-that-hold, leaking out of her unbidden because some part of her was always still standing watch.

"By the ninth year," she finished flatly, "the blank space on the map began to fill itself in. Forests grew where roads had run. Rivers moved. Reality complies, scribe. That's the whole horror of it. The world doesn't argue with its records. It never has."

* * *

"And the people?" Marguerite asked. Her voice had gone thin. "Three hundred years of people. Where did they go?"

Sera looked at the boy.

Tam slept with his fists closed, the way children do when they've learned that open hands get emptied. Even asleep, faint spirals marked the dust near his feet — he drew them in his sleep sometimes, she'd seen it, his finger tracing shapes in the floorboards as if writing letters too large for any page. He had faded at the edges since dawn. She hadn't told Marguerite yet. She would, but not yet.

"They were the payment," she said.

"Payment for what?"

"For the erasure itself." Sera spread her hands. "You can't strike a country from the record without paying the record's price. Words don't vanish free — ask any debtor in this city. Every edit had to be *balanced*, and the only currency heavy enough was memory. Each citizen of Veilmark carried their country inside them — every street they'd walked, every song, every face. So the editors took it. All of it. They opened people up the way you'd open a ledger, and they transferred the contents."

Marguerite had gone white. "They scattered them."

"Scattered them like seed. Like ash. One family split across four provinces, none of them knowing why they woke weeping. A man who could no longer remember his own mother's face but kept, forever, the shape of grief that went with it. Children who forgot their names but flinched at certain songs." Sera's jaw tightened. "They walk past you in the market every day. They serve you bread. Some of them are probably debtors in your own temple ledger, owing money for memories that were stolen before they ever arrived in Corvane. And the worst part—" her voice caught, and she forced it level, "—the worst part is that most of them are *happy*. Content. Small and unmemorable and safe. Because the editors weren't cruel for cruelty's sake. They were thorough. They gave everyone a reason not to look too hard at what was missing."

The silence stretched between them. In the corner, Tam stirred and murmured something in the old tongue — two syllables, soft, that Sera translated before she could stop herself: *home*, or possibly *the place where home was*.

"That's why I broke my oath," Sera said, quieter now. "You'll want to know, eventually, so hear it now. I stood on the Veilmark border the night they finished it. Oath-keeper, third rank, sworn to hold the line against any threat to the realm's integrity." She smiled without warmth. "No one told us the threat was administrative. We got our orders through the normal channels: stand aside, admit no refugees, report no anomalies. And I stood aside. I watched the last of them come to my post carrying everything they owned and less than nothing of themselves, and I turned them away, because the order was countersigned and orders are orders until you understand what they cost. By morning there was no border to guard. There was forest, and a road that went nowhere, and me standing on it with a sword I'd sworn an oath on, feeling the oath go out of me like heat from a stone."

Marguerite was staring at her — not with pity, which Sera could have borne, but with recognition, which she couldn't.

"Why can you see it?" the scribe asked slowly. "The holes. If everyone else forgot—"

"Because I disobeyed once. Just once, just partly — I let one family through my line before the final seal came down. A woman and her daughter." Sera looked at her hands. Old sword calluses, new ink stains from forging travel papers these eleven years. "Breaking an oath leaves a scar in you that edits can't close. The erasure passed over me like water over an oiled cloth. So I remember. I'm one of perhaps a dozen people alive who can stand in a village square and *see* that there's a hole in it shaped like a country. It's not a gift. It means I spend my life walking past absences and being the only one who grieves them."

* * *

Marguerite said nothing for a long time. Then, in a voice carefully stripped of feeling — the voice of a woman reading terms aloud before signing — she asked: "Why me? Why bring this to me?"

"Because of him." Sera nodded at the boy. "He spoke to you and you understood. That language hasn't had a living listener outside the erased in eleven years. And because of this." She reached into her coat and set something on the table between them: a scrap of parchment, brittle, salvaged from a burning years ago. On it, half a line of text in the trade-tongue — and beneath it, pressed faintly into the fibers as if the paper remembered more than the ink showed, the same words in the old script.

Marguerite touched it and her breath went ragged.

"This is a fragment of the erasure decree," Sera said. "The master document. Somewhere in Corvane there's a complete copy — the original, signed and sealed, the instrument that killed a country. Find it, and we find the terms of the payment. Find the terms, and we find where the payment went. Where the memories went." She nodded again at Tam. "All of it. His included."

"And the Door," Marguerite said. "You think there's a Door."

"I *know* there's a Door. Every erasure leaves one — the record needs somewhere to file what it removed. A seam in the world, at the center of the blank space, where everything taken was carried through." Sera leaned forward. "I've spent eleven years looking for it, scribe. I'm tired, I'm hunted, and I'm one woman with a broken oath and forged papers. You're inside the temple. You read the ledgers. You are, right now, the only person in Corvane positioned to find a decree everyone else believes doesn't exist."

Marguerite looked down at the fragment, then at the boy, then at her own ink-stained fingers, as if seeing them for the first time.

"There's something else," she said at last, so quietly Sera almost missed it. "My contract. My debt. I found writing under the trade-tongue last night. Old script." Her laugh was small and terrible. "I thought maybe I'd been sold a forged document. That happens to debtors."

"It's not forged."

"I know." Marguerite lifted her head, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid, all at once. "I know it's not forged. Which means either you're lying to me, or I am — and I've been lying to myself so long I can't tell which."

Outside, the bells of the temple quarter rang the hour, and both women flinched at the sound. Somewhere in those towers sat ledgers that held Marguerite's true name, and reports of strange sounds at her lodging, and men whose courtesy was a blade wrapped in velvet.

"We should sleep," Sera said. "In shifts. From tonight, assume we're watched."

But as she banked the fire and settled against the door with her sword across her knees — an oath-keeper's posture, worn like a ghost wears clothes — she heard Marguerite whisper the country's name once more to herself in the dark. *Veilmark.* Testing it. Trying it on.

And beside the cold hearth, in his sleep, the boy smiled — and the spirals he drew in the dust began, very faintly, to glow.
