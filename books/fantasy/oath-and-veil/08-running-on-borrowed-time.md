## 8. Running on Borrowed Time

The first sign was his shadow.

Marguerite noticed it on the ninth morning after Sera came to stay — or perhaps it was the tenth; the days had begun to blur together like ink left in rain. Tam sat cross-legged on her floor, drawing spirals in spilled flour while she ground charcoal for charms, and his shadow lay wrong against the boards. It was thinner than he was. Not smaller — she would have understood smaller — but *thinner*, as though someone had pressed a book flat and only some of the pages remained.

She told herself it was the light. The window faced east, and dawn came through at an angle that made everything lean.

But by evening, when she lit the candle and watched his shadow stretch long across the wall, it was still thin. And where his fingers should have cast five distinct lines, there were four, and one of them wavered like a reed in water.

"Tam." She kept her voice level, the way she kept her voice level with Inquisitor's clerks and temple debtors and everyone else who might take a tremor and turn it into a weapon. "Come here, love."

He came. He always came when she used that tone — not because he obeyed, but because he seemed to know that obedience was a currency she could not afford to spend carelessly, and he spent none of hers for her. He stood in front of her and looked up with those eyes that were too old for an eight-year-old's face, eyes the color of river ice over deep water.

She took his hand. It was warm. That was something. It was warm and small and the bones of it were exactly the bones of a living boy, and she held it and counted his fingers twice to be sure.

Five. Five now. But when she looked at the shadow again, it still had four.

* * *

Sera saw it too, three days later, and did not pretend otherwise. That was the thing about Sera of the Broken Oath: she lied beautifully about large things and refused to lie at all about small ones. She could look an Inquisitor in the eye and swear she'd never crossed the Veilmark border, but she would not look at a fading child and say he was fine.

"He's losing edges," Sera said. They stood in the narrow yard behind Marguerite's lodging, watching Tam chase pigeons between the wash-lines. The late-afternoon sun threw his shadow ahead of him, and even from here Marguerite could see that the outline of it broke apart at the shoulders, like breath fogging glass. "Look at his heels when he runs. They don't quite touch."

"They touch."

"They almost touch." Sera's jaw tightened. She had a soldier's face, all planes and old weather, and right now every plane was set hard. "I've seen this before. Not this exactly — nothing exactly like this has ever happened — but I've seen what happens to things that come out of an erasure half-finished. A village well I knew, after. The water stayed sweet for a year, then one morning it was just a hole in the ground, and no one remembered there'd ever been water, because there never had been. The world closed over it like skin over a wound."

"And you think he's a well."

"I think he's worse than a well." Sera turned to face her fully. "I think he's the last unspent coin of an entire country, and coins get spent."

Marguerite's hands, hanging at her sides, curled into fists without her permission. She looked at the boy — *her* boy, though no one had said so aloud and no paper said so anywhere, which in Corvane meant it wasn't true, which in Corvane meant it could be taken away — chasing pigeons that scattered from him a half-second too early, as if they sensed something at his edges that their small bright minds found unbearable.

"How long?" she asked.

"That's the question, isn't it." Sera rubbed the scarred knuckles of her right hand, a habit Marguerite had catalogued the way she catalogued everything — the way debtors catalogue, hoarding details against future need. "I don't know. Weeks, I'd guess. Fewer than ten. More than two. He fades slowest when he's near things that remember — old words, old places, people who can hear him. He faded fastest the night we hid in the tannery district. Nothing remembers anything there. The whole quarter smells like forgetting."

Marguerite thought of the candle bending toward Tam's voice. Of the flour spirals that held their shape hours longer than they should have. Of the way her own dreams, since he arrived, had been full of a word she couldn't pronounce upon waking — a word with too many vowels, a word that felt like a place.

"Weeks," she repeated.

"Maybe less if we stay here. Corvane is the worst place for him. This city was built on the erasure. Every street name is a bandage over a wound." Sera glanced toward the house, lowering her voice though no one was near enough to hear. "We need the Last Door, Marguerite. Not eventually. Soon."

* * *

That night Marguerite sat up past midnight with her ledger open on her knees — her debt ledger, the temple's property, the book that owned her — and pretended to study the columns of interest accrued, penalties assessed, days remaining until her indenture matured. Three years left. One thousand and ninety-four days, each one priced and recorded in a clerk's neat hand.

Tam slept in her bed. She had given it up without discussion; he had simply appeared in it the second night, curled small as a comma, and she had taken the floor and found she didn't mind. Now she listened to his breathing and tried to hear whether it sounded thinner than it used to.

It did. Or she was imagining it. There was no way to know, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She got up and crossed to the bed and looked down at him. In sleep his face went younger, slack and soft, and for a moment he was just a child, barefoot and hungry and far from wherever children ought to be. Then moonlight shifted through the window, and she saw it again: the edge of him, where his shoulder met the blanket, wasn't quite an edge. It was a suggestion. Light passed through the outermost layer of him the way it passes through smoke.

"Tam," she whispered, uselessly, because he was asleep, because whispering to sleeping children is something mothers do and she had apparently become a mother somewhere between the night market and now, without signing anything, without paying anything, the one transaction in her life that had cost nothing and was worth everything.

His eyes opened. Not startled — he never startled. He looked at her the way he always did, patient and ancient, and then he lifted one hand and spoke.

"*Veth-ka-lumen,*" he said. Home-of-warmth. He patted the blanket beside him.

She should say no. She should keep the wall of herself intact, the scribe's discipline, the debtor's careful distance from everything she couldn't afford to lose. Instead she lay down beside him, stiff as a plank, and he tucked himself against her side with the unselfconscious certainty of a creature who has decided a thing and will not be argued out of it, and within minutes he was asleep again, and she lay awake feeling his warmth soak into her ribs and thinking: *this is what they'll take from me. Not my name. This.*

* * *

In the morning she began to plan the way she planned everything: obsessively, in silence, on scraps of paper she burned afterward.

Sera talked in terms of roads and borders and the borderlands where Veilmark used to be — six days' travel east, if the mountain passes were open, twelve if they weren't. But Sera thought like a soldier, and soldiers assume you can walk out of a city. Marguerite thought like a debtor, and debtors know better. Her name was written in a ledger in the temple archive. Her lodging was registered. Her charm-trade, her charcoal, her customers in the night market — all of it was thread, and every thread ran back to the spool, and the spool was Inquisitor Halev's hand.

Someone had already reported strange sounds at her lodging once. Someone would report them again. And now there were three of them: a woman bound by contract, a disgraced oath-keeper whose face was known in certain circles, and a child who spoke a language that carried a death sentence in eleven kingdoms.

They couldn't just leave. Leaving required money, papers, a story. Marguerite had money — forty-one coppers and a silver piece sewn into her hem — and no papers, and a story that would need to be very good indeed.

But first, before any of it, she needed to know how much time they actually had. Guesses weren't enough. Sera's *weeks* was a soldier's estimate, and Marguerite had learned never to bet her life on another person's arithmetic.

So she did what she did best. She measured.

For six days she measured Tam. She marked his height on the doorframe each dawn — a scribe's habit, precise marks in charcoal, dated. For four days the marks matched exactly. On the fifth day he was a hair shorter. On the sixth, the hair had become a finger's width, and when she pressed her palm flat against the crown of his head she felt something she had no words for: a faint resistance, like pressing on the surface of still water, as though the top inch of him had already stopped being entirely *here*.

He noticed her noticing. Of course he did.

"*Mama-veth,*" he said quietly — mother-of-the-house, a term from a language that officially did not exist, spoken in a voice too calm for what it meant. Then, in the trade-tongue, halting and rusty, the first full sentences she'd ever heard him manage outside the old words: "It goes faster now. I can feel where I end. Before, I couldn't feel it. Feeling it is bad."

"How fast?" Her voice cracked on the second word. She hated that. She steadied it. "How fast, Tam?"

He considered, head tilted, the way he considered everything — seriously, completely, as if questions were doors he intended to walk through properly. "Before, I was a jar with a crack. Now the crack is a hole. When we are near remembering things, the hole closes a little. When we are here—" he gestured at the room, the city beyond it, the whole gray weight of Corvane, "—it opens."

"The Door," Marguerite said. "If we reach the Door. What happens?"

"You put me back," he said simply. "Or you spend me. Those are the two things a last coin can do."

"Those aren't the only—"

"They're the only ones." He took her hand, and his grip was a child's grip, warm and absolute. "Don't be sad yet. Sad spends me faster. I heard Sera say it and I checked, and it's true. Be angry instead. Anger doesn't leak."

Marguerite laughed — a broken, wet sound that was mostly not laughter at all — and pulled him against her, and felt through her own body the terrifying lightness of him, the way a bird feels lighter than its feathers suggest, as if part of his weight had already gone ahead somewhere.

Anger, then. She could do anger. Anger was the oldest thing she owned.

* * *

She laid it out for Sera that evening, all of it, on the table between them: the doorframe measurements, the dates, the rate of loss extrapolated in a scribe's tight columns. Sera read the figures twice and then looked up, and whatever she saw in Marguerite's face made her stop being a soldier for a moment and be something rawer.

"You've turned him into a ledger entry," Sera said.

"Yes."

"That's the coldest, most useful thing anyone's done since I met you." Sera exhaled slowly. "All right. By your numbers, worst case, we have nineteen days. Best case, thirty-three. The Door is east, through the passes, and the passes close with the autumn storms, and the autumn storms are—" she counted silently, lips moving, "—eleven days away at the earliest. So we go through the passes in the gap, or we don't go at all."

"Then we leave in four days."

"Four? We need provisions, a route, a cover story, money—"

"We need four days," Marguerite said, "because in four days I can finish what I started tonight." She turned her ledger around and pushed it across the table. "Every charm I've ever sold, every customer, every coin — it's all threads back to me, and Halev holds the spool. If we run, he pulls the threads and finds us on the road with a fading child and no explanation. So before we run, I'm going to cut every thread myself. Burn my stock. Close my accounts. Make Marguerite Voss, seller of forbidden charms, vanish so cleanly that when Halev comes looking, there'll be nothing left to follow but cold ash."

Sera stared at her. "And your debt? Your name in the ledger? You walk out on the temple and you're not a debtor anymore, you're a fugitive. Fugitives get hunted. Debtors just get collected."

"I know what I am," Marguerite said. She was surprised by how steady her voice came out, how much it sounded like someone else's voice, someone braver, someone she might be becoming. "I've been small and unmemorable for three years because small things survive. But small things also get erased, Sera. I've seen it happen to a country. I'm done practicing."

Outside, the wind was rising off the harbor, carrying the smell of coming autumn. In the bedroom, Tam slept, and his breathing was a little thinner than yesterday, and tomorrow it would be thinner than today, and every hour they spent planning was an hour leaking out of him like sand through a torn seam.

Nineteen days, worst case. Nineteen days to cross a city that wanted him forgotten, mountains that wanted them buried, and a border that no longer existed — to find a Door that might be a legend, guarded by an Inquisitor who already knew her name.

Marguerite blew out the candle and stood in the dark, listening to the boy breathe, and let the fear move through her and out the other side into something harder.

Four days. Then they ran.
