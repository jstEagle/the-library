## 9. The Archive Beneath the Temple

The temple of Corvane kept its secrets the way a miser keeps coins: counted nightly, touched often, and never spent. Marguerite had copied ledgers in its undercrofts for three years, and in all that time she had never once looked at what she was copying. That was the first rule of a debtor scribe — eyes on the line, hands on the quill, mind nowhere at all. Curiosity was a luxury the ledger did not cover.

Tonight she intended to be extravagant.

She dressed for it carefully, the way she dressed for every sale in the night market: plain grey wool, scribe's collar buttoned to the throat, hair pinned back so severely her face became a kind of furniture. Unmemorable. She had built a whole life out of being unmemorable, and tonight that life would finally pay her something back.

* * *

The temple above was all incense and gold leaf, but beneath it the corridors went down like the rings of a felled tree. Marguerite knew them by their smells before their shapes: the tallow-and-parchment floor where the junior clerks worked; the wax-and-iron level where debts were sealed; and below that, past a stair that most scribes pretended did not exist, the cold mineral breath of the restricted archive.

She carried a legitimate reason strapped to her chest like a shield. Master Ondrel, third archivist of the second rank, had sent her down three nights ago to fetch a folio of tithe records from 411 — a real errand, a real folio, and more importantly a real chit of authorization bearing his seal. The errand was finished. The chit was not expired. In the temple's arithmetic, an authorization was a door, and doors did not ask why you came through them twice.

Her heart was not calm. She noted this with a kind of detached professional interest, the way she noted weather. Three years of small crimes — charms sold in alley mouths, hexes inked onto buttons, blessings whispered over cheap candles — and none of them had made her pulse climb like this. Petty theft was survival. This was something else. This was walking into the belly of the thing that owned her name.

At the bottom of the stair stood the archive door: black iron, unornamented, with a keyhole shaped like a closed eye. Two wardens flanked it, but they were not looking at her. They were looking at the ledger on their lectern between them, because that was how the temple worked — everyone watched the record, no one watched the person. Records were holy. People were interchangeable.

"Chit," said the left warden, without raising his head.

She passed it through. He pressed his thumb to Ondrel's seal, murmured the verification syllable, and the syllable came back true, because the seal was true, because Ondrel really had authorized her, three nights ago, for a folio she had genuinely returned. The temple's own precision would be her lockpick.

"Purpose."

"Retrieval. Archivist Ondrel requires cross-reference of the 411 tithes against the second-register holdings." She had invented the phrase that morning while washing, and it had the two qualities that mattered: it sounded tedious, and it sounded like it would take a while.

The right warden waved her through. The iron eye opened.

* * *

Cold hit her first, then silence — a silence so complete it had texture, like wool packed in her ears. The restricted archive was not shelves. It was a cavern cut into the city's bones, and the records hung from it: thousands of slender lead cylinders suspended on chains from the vaulted dark above, each one tagged with a tin plate, each one turning very slowly on its chain as if the room breathed. The lamplighters had left a single oil lamp burning near the entrance, and beyond its small gold circle the hanging cylinders went up into blackness like a forest of sleeping bats.

Marguerite had heard rumors about this place since her first week as a scribe. They said the restricted holdings were where the temple kept what it could not destroy — condemned genealogies, heretical liturgies, the confessions of nobles too useful to execute. They said some of the cylinders were older than Corvane itself.

They said nothing at all about Veilmark. That was the strange part, she realized now, standing in the cold with her breath ghosting silver. There were no rumors about Veilmark because there was no Veilmark. Everyone knew that. Everyone knew there had never been any such country, that the word itself was a drunkard's nonsense or a child's invention, and if you asked anyone what *Veilmark* meant they would frown slightly, as if the shape of the word were familiar the way a scar is familiar — present, painless, and utterly without a story.

Except Tam. Except the boy who drew spirals in dust and spoke a language that lived in her mouth like water in a well she'd never known she owned.

Sera had been explicit, in the safehouse smell of her rented room, in the low urgent voice of a woman who had learned to talk around walls: *The Door is real. I held the border once; I know what stands behind it. But to find where it stands now you need the decree — the original instrument of erasure. Every erasure leaves a seam, girl, and the decree is the needle that made it. The temple keeps the instruments.*

*And the temple keeps them where?*

*Underneath,* Sera had said. *It always keeps them underneath.*

Marguerite moved into the dark.

The chains were spaced just wide enough to walk between, and the tags turned overhead, catching lamplight when she raised her lamp: *HOLDING 2-114, GENEALOGY, SEALED.* *HOLDING 2-115, LITURGY, CONDEMNED.* *HOLDING 2-116, TREATY, NULLIFIED.* The numbering ran upward along rows that curved away out of sight, following the round of the cavern. Somewhere in this forest of lead and tin was a cylinder whose tag should have read *VEILMARK, KINGDOM OF, ERASED* — except that no one could read such a tag, because the name on it had been struck from every mind that might look.

That was the trap Sera had warned her about. You could not search for a thing whose name had been eaten. You could only search for the shape of its absence.

So Marguerite searched for gaps.

She walked the rows with her lamp held high, reading tags the way she read debt contracts — quickly, mechanically, letting her trained eye snag on irregularities. And the irregularities were there, once she knew to look for them. A run of holdings numbered in perfect sequence — 3-401, 3-402, 3-403 — and then a gap where 3-407 should be, the neighboring chains re-hung slightly closer together, the way teeth drift when one is pulled. Another gap in the seventh row. Another in the ninth. The archive was full of pulled teeth, and someone had been careful, but no one is careful enough for a scribe who has spent three years finding the half-copper hidden in a merchant's columns.

The largest gap sat deep in the fourth ring, where the air was coldest and the lamp flame burned thin and blue. Here the missing cylinder had left more than a space. The chains on either side bore tags, and the tags bore dates, and the dates told a story by themselves:

*HOLDING 4-088, CENSUS, ANNULLED, YEAR 398.*
*HOLDING 4-090, REGISTRY OF DEBTS, DISSOLVED, YEAR 398.*
*HOLDING 4-091, MAP FOLIO, WITHDRAWN, YEAR 398.*

Year 398. Nine years ago. The year, though she could not have said how she knew it, when everything had gone quiet.

And between 4-088 and 4-090, where 4-089 should hang, there was nothing — except that there was something. A single chain descended from the dark with no cylinder on it, ending in a hook, and the hook was polished bright with use. Someone came here. Someone took the holding down and put it back, took it down and put it back, again and again, year after year.

The temple did not destroy everything. The temple *used* things.

Marguerite stood beneath the empty hook with her heart going hard against her ribs, and she understood, with the vertigo of a woman finding her own footprints in a room she has never entered, that whatever had hung here was still in circulation. Still being consulted. Still feeding something.

She thought of Halev's courteous voice. She thought of the way the temple's interest rates never seemed to fall, the way debt in Corvane behaved less like money and more like weather — everywhere, inescapable, owned by no one and benefiting someone always. She thought of Tam, fading at the edges like smoke deciding which way to blow.

*They are drinking it,* she thought. *Whatever was erased, they are drinking it, a cup at a time.*

* * *

Finding the cylinder was one problem. Getting it out was another, and the other was shaped like a rule she had recited a thousand times without ever hearing: *No holding leaves the archive. No holding leaves the archive. Copies may be made only by archivists of the second rank or higher, within the archive, under seal.*

But she was a scribe. Copying was the one thing in the world she was unquestionably, provably, ledger-certified good at.

She found a clerk's station tucked into an alcove of the fourth ring — a slanted desk, a pot of ink gone skin-dry, a rack of pens, and, crucially, a stack of blank archive vellum stamped with the temple's blind watermark, paper that existed officially nowhere and therefore could not be missed from anywhere. She worked the ink back to life with a drop from her own flask, trimmed a pen, and began taking down holdings.

Not the decree itself — she didn't have it yet. She copied the tags. All of them. Row after row of annulled censuses and dissolved registries and withdrawn maps, the entire skeleton of Year 398, building in her neat commercial hand a map of everything that had been taken apart. If the decree was elsewhere in the cavern, its neighbors would point to it the way a wound points to the knife. It was slow work, and it was glorious. Her hand did not shake. Her hand, at last, was doing exactly what she wanted it to do, and the thrill of that — the pure transgressive joy of the obedient pen turned bandit — nearly made her laugh aloud in the blue-lit dark.

She was three pages deep when the lamp guttered.

She looked up. Down the long curve of the fourth ring, between the hanging cylinders, a light was moving. Not lamplight — lantern light, steady and official, swinging with the walk of someone who had every right to be here.

Voices. Two of them, low, indistinct, growing clearer.

Marguerite did not think. Thinking was for people with time. She snuffed her lamp, folded the three pages into the flat of her forearm where scribes carry loose sheets a hundred times a day, invisible precisely because it was ordinary, and stepped backward off the aisle into the narrow slot between two hanging cylinders, pressing herself against the cold stone wall as the lead records swung gently around her, ticking against their chains like slow clocks.

The lantern came around the curve.

"—Ondrel swears the fourth-ring inventory balanced in spring," one voice said. "If holdings are drifting now—"

"Holdings don't drift," said the second voice, and Marguerite's blood went to ice, because she knew that voice. Courteous. Patient. The voice of a man who had once told her, smiling, exactly how much of her life remained unpurchased. "Records are the one thing in this world that stay where they're put. Which means if something has moved, it was moved."

Inquisitor Halev. In the archive. At night. Beneath the temple, in the place where the temple kept what it drank from.

The lantern stopped. She could see its glow through the gap between cylinders, a warm orange slit against the blue dark, and she could see the shadow of a hand rise and touch one of the tags — the census, 4-088 — the way a man touches a fence post on land he owns.

"Year 398 again," Halev said softly. "Everything interesting happened in 398, and no one remembers why. Isn't that convenient? Isn't that *profitable?*" A pause. "Pull the access ledger. I want to know every soul who's come down here this month."

"Yes, Inquisitor."

"And check the clerk stations. Ink, paper, pens. See if anything's been disturbed."

Marguerite stood motionless in the dark with three stolen pages against her arm and her stolen ink drying on a stolen pen, and she listened to the lantern swing away around the curve of the fourth ring, toward the alcove, toward the desk, toward the evidence of her — and she made the calculation that obedient Marguerite Voss would never have made, that small unmemorable Marguerite Voss would never even have imagined making.

There was a service stair at the far end of the fourth ring. She'd seen it on the survey map she'd copied her first week, filed under infrastructure, forgotten until now. Thirty paces, past the empty hook, through the gap in the holdings. If she moved on the turn of their voices, in the pauses, soft-footed on stone—

Behind her, faint as breath, one of the lead cylinders ticked against its chain.

The lantern light stopped moving.

"Inquisitor," said the clerk, very quietly. "Did you hear—"

"I heard." And then Halev's voice, rising gently through the hanging dark, courteous as ever, terrible as ever, addressed to the whole breathing cavern and to her specifically, the way a fisherman addresses the river:

"You can come out, scribe. The archive tells me everything. It's already told me your name."

Marguerite's hand closed around the folded pages. Her name — her *true* name, the one written twice, the one she'd only just learned to fear — sat in his mouth like a coin he was deciding whether to spend.

She ran.
