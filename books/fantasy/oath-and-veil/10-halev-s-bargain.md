## 10. Halev's Bargain

Inquisitor Dorian Halev had a theory about thieves, and it was this: they always touched things twice.

The first touch was greed. The second was doubt — the moment when the hand returned to confirm the theft was real, that the object still sat where it had no right to sit, that the world had not yet noticed. In nineteen years of service to the Temple of the Ledger, he had never known the theory to fail. Thieves doubted. Honest men did not return.

So when the restricted archive's outer seal whispered open at the third hour past midnight — a seal only four living people could unlock, and two of them were dead — Halev did not hurry. He set down his pen mid-sentence, leaving a small ink bloom on the ledger of temple expenditures he had been reviewing, and he listened.

The archive beneath the Temple of the Ledger was the quietest place in Corvane, quieter even than the Quiet House, because the Quiet House at least held breathing. The archive held paper. Ten thousand years of paper, or what passed for ten thousand years in a city that had rewritten its own accounting more than once. Scrolls in lacquered cases. Ledgers bound in grey calfskin. The great Erasure Decrees, kept in iron cabinets that no one opened because no one remembered why they should.

Halev took the stairs down slowly, his boots making no sound on the stone. He had learned silence from his mother, who had been a debt-bound laundress in the temple's lower wards and who had taught him that quiet was the only inheritance worth having. He had learned everything else from the ledgers: that every soul in Corvane owed something, and that the ones who pretended otherwise were simply the ones who hadn't read their own contracts.

The lamp at the archive entrance still burned. Someone had lit it. That was either carelessness or preparation, and Marguerite Voss did not strike him as careless.

He stood in the dark beyond the light for a long moment, letting his eyes adjust, letting the cold of the deep stone settle into his bones. Somewhere among the shelves, paper shifted — the smallest sound, a breath of vellum against vellum, the sound a page makes when a hand hesitates over it.

There. The second touch.

He walked into the lamplight.

* * *

She was kneeling at the iron cabinet with her back to him, and she had done something clever with her scribe's robes — turned them inside out, or some trick of the sort, so that at a glance she read as one of the night-archivists rather than a third-tier copyist with no business below the second landing. Her hair was pinned up under a cap. Her hands, gloved in cheap cotton, hovered over an open scroll as though it were a sleeping animal she meant to steal without waking.

Halev let the silence stretch until it became its own kind of announcement.

"You're holding it wrong," he said.

She went very still. He watched the stillness travel through her — shoulders, spine, the small muscles of her neck — and then, remarkably, she did not bolt. She turned her head just enough to show him her profile, and her voice, when it came, was admirably level.

"The decree? I wasn't aware there was a correct way."

"Two hands. Always two hands. The erasure scrolls are written on memory-vellum." He stepped closer, and the lamplight found his face, and he arranged his features into the expression that had made three separate clerks weep in this very room: courteous, patient, faintly disappointed, like a tutor confronting a promising student's first real failure. "They remember being handled. Rough treatment leaves marks, and marks leave evidence, and evidence is my entire profession."

Now she rose, and turned, and faced him fully, and he gave her the courtesy of a long look. Three years he had held her contract. Three years of quarterly reviews, of watching her sign her name in the ledger — *Marguerite Voss, debt-bound, third-tier scribe, balance outstanding* — of noting the small economies of her life the way a farmer notes the weather. She was not beautiful. She was not remarkable. She was precisely the kind of woman Corvane was built to produce: small, careful, forgettable, surviving by the grace of being overlooked.

And yet here she was, at the third hour past midnight, kneeling before an iron cabinet that contained the single most dangerous document in the kingdom.

"I can explain," she said.

"No," Halev said gently, "you really can't. But please try. I find the attempts instructive."

* * *

Her explanation, as it happened, was better than most. She had been sent down to retrieve a copy of the Ward Tax Rolls for Auditor Ceyl — a plausible errand, verifiable, and Ceyl would confirm it if asked, because Ceyl confirmed whatever was asked of him after his third cup of wine. She had taken a wrong turn. The archive was labyrinthine. The lamp was lit; she had assumed an archivist was working.

Halev listened with his head tilted, the way he listened to all confessions, cataloguing the architecture of the lie. It was well-built. Load-bearing walls in the right places. But it had been built too fast, and fast work shows its seams.

"You assumed an archivist was working," he repeated. "At the third hour past midnight. In a sealed wing."

"The lamp—"

"Burns only when the inner seal is keyed open. It is tied to the lock, Voss. You know this; it is covered in your orientation materials, which you signed for." He let that sit. Then he extended one hand, palm up, in a gesture that was almost gentle. "The scroll, please."

For a moment — a long, interesting moment — he thought she might run. He had already mapped the room in his mind: the door behind her, the door behind him, the narrow aisle between the iron cabinets where a desperate person might imagine escape was possible. He hoped she wouldn't run. Running would require him to be rough, and roughness was inelegant, and besides, he had come down here tonight hoping for exactly this conversation, and it would be a pity to spoil it.

She placed the scroll in his palm. Two hands, as instructed. Her fingers trembled — genuinely, he noted, not performed — and he felt something almost like respect.

He unrolled it and read what he already knew was there: a fragment of the Second Erasure Decree, the one concerning Veilmark, the name of which he did not say aloud because saying it aloud, even down here, even alone, felt like pressing on a bruise the size of a country. The fragment listed the disposition of assets. Not gold. Not land. *Memories.* Eleven thousand recorded identities, seized at the moment of unmaking, transferred to the Temple's custody, entered into the general fund.

"Do you know what you're holding?" he asked.

"A decree."

"A revenue instrument." He rolled it closed with care, two hands, and watched her face as he said the next part slowly, giving each word its full weight. "You think the Erasures were about safety. About dangerous magic, outlawed tongues, threats to the crown. That is what the pulpit says, and the pulpit says it beautifully. But temples do not run on beauty, Voss. They run on income. And an erased country is the most profitable kind, because a country that no one remembers cannot object to how it was sold."

Her eyes had gone fixed on the scroll in his hands. "Sold."

"Eleven thousand lives, folded flat and filed. Every memory of Veilmark — every street, every song, every childhood — extracted from the people who carried them and stored in vellum exactly like this. And stored memories retain value. They appreciate, actually. Grief appreciates." He smiled, and knew the smile was terrible, and let it be terrible, because sometimes the truth needed teeth to be believed. "The temple has been spending Veilmark for nine years. A memory of a harvest festival buys a favor from a magistrate. A mother's face, sold whole, funds a cathedral wing. We are rich, Voss, in ways the faithful will never see, because the currency is invisible by design."

"And mine?" Her voice came out smaller than she wanted it to. He heard her hear it, and hated the question even as he admired the courage of asking it. "Is mine in there?"

"Yours," Halev said, "is the finest piece in the collection."

* * *

He set the scroll down on the reading table between them, weighting its corners, and gestured for her to sit. She did not sit. He sat instead, folding himself onto the bench with the unhurried ease of a man settling in beside a fire, and looked up at her.

"When your contract was drawn," he said, "three years ago, you came to the temple destitute. No family record. No birth registry entry. No history before the age of — what does your file say? Twenty-two. A woman who appeared out of nowhere and owed everyone." He tapped the table. "That is not how people happen, Voss. People happen in registries. People have mothers with names and parishes with records. You had nothing, because someone had already taken everything, and the taking left a shape. I have spent three years looking at that shape."

"What shape?"

"The shape of a citizen." He said it plainly, without drama, and watched it land harder than any accusation could have. "Your handwriting has habits that don't exist in Corvanese script schools. You hum songs the archives classify as 'unattributed folk material' — except the attribution field isn't empty, Voss. It's redacted. You flinch at the word *veil*. You dream, according to the sleep-ward notes from your first winter here, in a language our linguists flagged and then quietly buried, because flagging it properly would have required explaining it." He leaned forward. "You are not from nowhere. You are from somewhere that was made into nowhere. And the ledger holds the proof, because the ledger holds everything — including eleven thousand confiscated lives, one of which fits you the way a key fits the lock it was cut for."

The lamplight moved slightly — a draft from the stairwell — and her shadow swayed against the iron cabinets like something trying to get free.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "You should be calling the wardens."

"Yes," Halev agreed. "I should. Breaking into the restricted archive, handling an original decree, at night, while under investigation for the matter of a certain child—" He paused delicately, and saw her go white, and understood that she had not known he knew about the boy. Good. Fear works best in layers. "The punishment for that is not a fine. It is the Quiet House, and then it is the rendering, and then it is a line in a ledger where your name used to be. I have written such lines. They take less time than you'd think."

Silence. The good kind — the kind where the other person is doing arithmetic.

"So I am going to offer you a bargain instead," he said, "and I want you to understand that it is a genuine mercy, though I know mercy sounds like a trap coming from me. Deliver the child to the temple. The mute boy. The one who speaks the dead tongue, the one currently hidden — I would estimate — somewhere Sera Vane can reach. Bring him to me within six days, alive and undamaged, and your debt is cleared. Not reduced. *Cleared.* Every coin, every year of servitude, struck from the book. And more than that—" he lifted the decree fragment and held it where she could see it, "—this goes back in its cabinet, and your file goes into a drawer, and no one ever asks again where you came from. You walk out of the temple a free woman with a clean name. You could marry. Travel. Forget me entirely. Isn't that what you want? Isn't that all any of us want — to owe nothing?"

He watched her take it in. He was honest enough with himself, in the privacy of his own skull, to admit that the offer was real. The temple wanted the boy with a hunger he only half understood — something about the last unspent memory, something the Archon's circle whispered about — and they would pay enormously for his delivery. Clearing one scribe's debt was rounding error. It cost Halev nothing but the pleasure of watching her refuse, if she refused.

And he thought she would refuse. That was the strange thing. Everything in her file said *survivor*, said *small*, said *take the deal* — and yet she stood there with her jaw set in a way that reminded him, unpleasantly, of documents he had read about people who had once stood on borders and been ordered to stand aside.

"The child," she said carefully, "is eight years old."

"He is also a national asset with a depreciation schedule."

"He's *dying*." The word cracked out of her before she could smooth it, and then she pressed her lips together, furious at herself, and Halev felt the trap close around his own heart in a way he had not anticipated. Because that was not the answer of a criminal. That was the answer of a mother, and he had read enough ledgers to know that mothers were the one variable his profession had never learned to price.

"Six days," he said quietly. "Bring him in, and I give you back your name. Refuse, and I take it — and I promise you, Voss, I have read what's underneath your name in that ledger, and it is worth far more than you imagine, and far worse than you fear. You are not a debtor pretending to be a woman. You are a country pretending to be a debtor. Countries can be repossessed."

She looked at him for a long moment. The lamplight caught the wet in her eyes and did not let it fall.

Then she said, in a voice gone perfectly calm, perfectly courteous — his own voice, turned back on him like a mirror: "Thank you, Inquisitor. I'll consider it."

And Halev, who had a theory about thieves, realized only later — much later, when the archive lamp had burned out and the cabinet stood open and the decree fragment was gone along with the woman — that he had made the oldest error in his profession.

He had told her exactly what she was worth.

And she had used the six days to become priceless.
