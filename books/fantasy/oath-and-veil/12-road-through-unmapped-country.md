## 12. Road Through Unmapped Country

Sera had walked out of cities before — desertions, mostly, or retreats dressed up as strategy — and she knew the particular weight that lifted from the shoulders at the first milestone past the walls. This was not that. Corvane's weight did not lift; it followed them, patient as a creditor, in the shape of a folded decree fragment riding against Marguerite's ribs and a child who flickered at the edges when the light went through him wrong.

They took the pilgrim road north because it was the only road the temple did not watch closely. Pilgrims were poor and plentiful and lied about their destinations as a matter of piety. Sera bought three hooded cloaks from a drover with a squint and no curiosity, and by midmorning they were three more shapes among the dust-colored stream of the faithful, walking at the speed of the slowest ox.

Tam walked between them.

He had not spoken since the drainage arch. He didn't need to. He simply set off in one direction after another with the certainty of water finding a slope, and they learned quickly to follow the turn of his head rather than the signposts. When the road forked at a shrine to some harvest saint whose name had been repainted twice over older paint, Tam did not hesitate. He turned left, toward the hills, and Marguerite looked at Sera with an expression Sera was beginning to know well: the scribe's face trying very hard to be a soldier's face.

"He knows where he's going," she said.

"He knows where *it* is," Sera corrected. "That's not always the same thing."

* * *

The first village had no name.

Not in the way of villages that have lost their names to time — there were plenty of those, places where the oldest resident could only shrug and say it had been called something before her grandmother's day. This village had a hole where its name should be, and everyone in it stepped around the hole without looking down.

Sera noticed it first at the well. They had stopped to fill their skins, and an old man working a patch of onions beside the square had waved them toward it with the easy hospitality of country people who hadn't yet learned to fear strangers again. The well was old, stone-lipped, its rope grooved deep into the wood of the crossbeam by generations of hands. And above it, where every well in every village Sera had ever passed through bore some mark — a carved patron, a painted name, even just a crude initial scratched by a bored shepherd two hundred years dead — there was nothing. A smooth patch of stone, lighter than the rest, like skin that had never seen sun.

"Who cut that?" she asked the onion man, nodding at it.

"Cut what?" He looked at the well. He looked at the blank stone. His eyes slid across it the way a cart wheel slides on ice, catching nothing. "Well's been here longer than me," he said. "Longer than the church, I'd say. Good water." And he went back to his onions.

Marguerite stood beside her, and Sera heard the breath go out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes somewhere far away.

"It's not just records," Marguerite said quietly. "It's not just paper. It's *stone*. Someone chiseled a name off this well so thoroughly that the villagers can't see the scar."

"The decree didn't erase Veilmark," Sera said. She had explained this before, in the archive, in the dark, but explaining it here, standing inside the erasure, made it worse instead of better. "It edited it. And reality — reality is a lazy clerk. It takes the shortest path to consistency. If no record says this village ever traded with Veilmark, then the trading posts come down, the names come off the wells, the songs lose their second verses—" She stopped, because Tam had gone still at her side, his small face tipped up toward the blank stone, and his lips were moving.

No sound came out. But Sera had spent eleven years on the borders of a country whose language she was sworn to guard, and she knew the shape of those words even silent. *Water remembers,* he was saying, or something near it. *Water remembers what stone forgets.*

She crouched beside him. "What does it remember?"

Tam looked at her. For a moment his eyes were not a child's eyes; they were wide and layered, like looking down a well yourself, and Sera felt the hair rise on her arms. Then he blinked, and he was eight years old again, tired and dusty, and he pointed down into the well.

Marguerite leaned over the lip. So did Sera.

Far below, where the daylight gave out, the water lay black and still — and on its surface, faint as breath on glass, there was a reflection that did not match the sky. Not the grey noon overhead. A different sky. A sky with a color in it Sera had not seen since she was nineteen years old: a deep violet-blue that existed nowhere else in the world, the color of the Veilmark dusk, the color they had named a whole season after because no other country had it.

She straightened too fast and had to put a hand on the well's rim.

"You saw it," Marguerite whispered. It wasn't a question. Her knuckles were white on the crossbeam.

"I saw it."

"That's—"

"Don't say the name." Sera's voice came out flat and hard, and she hated herself for it, but the word felt dangerous here, in a village where the stones themselves had been taught to forget. "Not out loud. Not until we're further out. Names have weight, and right now we're carrying enough."

Tam tugged Marguerite's sleeve and held up his waterskin, solemn as a tax collector, and the moment broke around them like ice on a spring river.

* * *

They walked on. The pilgrim road thinned through the afternoon, the faithful peeling off toward shrines and reliaries while the three of them kept north, and by evening the road itself began to fail them.

It wasn't abandoned. That would have been simple — roads went derelict all the time when trade shifted. This was stranger. The road continued, graded and graveled, well-made, and then it would simply stop, ending in a farmer's field or a hillside, as if the surveyors had run out of conviction. And beyond each stopping place, a track would begin again a few hundred yards on, heading the same direction, as if the country had tried to draw a line to somewhere and kept flinching.

"Like a sentence with words missing," Marguerite murmured, picking her way along a hedgerow around one of the gaps. "You can read your way through it if you guess at what should be there."

"You'd know." Sera meant it as a joke and heard, too late, how it landed. Marguerite's jaw tightened. Three days ago the woman had found her own true name written in a dead script beneath her debt contract, and she had not yet finished falling into that discovery; Sera could see it in the way she touched her collarbone sometimes, absently, as if checking that she was still all there.

"I'm sorry," Sera said.

"No." Marguerite shook her head. "No, you're right. It's the only way I can think about it without screaming. Words missing. Guess at what should be there." She walked a few steps in silence. "Except I'm the one guessing about myself, and I keep getting it wrong."

"How do you know you're getting it wrong?"

Marguerite stopped. Ahead of them, Tam had climbed onto a stile and was sitting with his legs dangling, watching the sun go down behind hills that had no names either — Sera had checked the map she'd stolen alongside the decree, and the map showed the borderlands as empty parchment, a clean white space with a single printed note: *unproductive land, sparsely settled.* As if an entire country had been a bad crop year.

"When I try to remember my childhood," Marguerite said, "I remember a house. A narrow house, three stories, with a blue door. I remember it perfectly. The loose step, the smell of the stairwell, my mother's voice through the floor." She turned to look at Sera, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "But I remember it from *outside*. From street level. I have no memory of being inside it. Every memory I have of my own life, I'm watching myself from across the street."

Sera had no answer for that. There wasn't one. She put her hand briefly on Marguerite's shoulder — a touch that lasted a heartbeat longer than comfort required, and neither of them remarked on it — and they walked on to catch up with the boy on the stile, who watched them come with the patience of someone who had been many people and could afford to wait.

* * *

The second village had a name, but it was the wrong one.

Sera realized it over supper, in the common room of an inn that smelled of tallow and wet wool. The village was called Harrow's End, according to the sign and the innkeeper and the parish register nailed proudly to the wall, and everyone said it fluently, comfortably, the way people say anything they've never had cause to doubt. But the register was recent — forty years old at most — and beneath the fresh ink of *Harrow's End*, where the older entries had been scraped and rewritten, the parchment had a ghost. Sera saw it when the lamplight caught the page at an angle: the shadow of letters that had been washed off but not forgotten by the paper itself. The ghost-letters were shorter than the ones written over them. Denser. Written in a script that ran right to left.

Her hand closed around her cup hard enough to whiten her fingers.

"What?" Marguerite asked, low.

"Nothing." Sera drank. "Everything. The same thing." She nodded fractionally toward the register. "This village had another name. The decree replaced it, and the replacement doesn't fit — look how the innkeeper says it. He says it fine, but he pauses half a beat first, like a man stepping over a threshold in the dark. Everyone here does. They're all tripping over the shape of the absence and calling it normal."

Marguerite glanced at the innkeeper, a broad cheerful man hauling stew to a table of drovers. She watched him talk, and Sera watched her watch him, and saw the exact moment the scribe saw it — the tiny hitch, the half-beat stumble under every fluent sentence, invisible unless you knew to listen for it.

"Gods," Marguerite breathed. "It's everywhere. It's like a limp the whole village shares."

"Now you understand what was done." Sera kept her voice level and her eyes on her bowl. "Everyone told you Veilmark was conquered. Or cursed. Or that it never existed and the stories are smugglers' lies. The truth is quieter and much worse. Somebody sat in a room with a pen and unmade a nation the way you'd correct a manuscript. And the world — the lazy, obedient world — copied the correction down."

Across the room, Tam sat on the bench by the fire, eating bread with the concentration of the perpetually hungry. An innkeeper's daughter, maybe six years old, had appointed herself his friend and was showing him a wooden horse with one leg. He was smiling. It was a good smile, a real child's smile, and it hurt Sera to look at, because she knew what it cost. She had seen the fading begin — the way his outline went soft in direct sunlight, the way the drover who'd sold them cloaks had looked directly through him twice before noticing he existed. He was made of the last unspent memory of a murdered country, and the purse it came from was running empty.

The little girl said something to him, and Tam laughed, and then he said something back — and Sera's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

He'd answered in the trade-tongue. Mostly. But threaded through it, quick as a birdcall, had been three words of the old speech, and the little girl had *repeated them*, laughing, getting them almost right, and for one instant the firelight in the common room bent — just bent, the flames leaning toward the child like flowers toward sun — and then straightened, and nobody noticed except the two women at the corner table who had reason to notice everything.

Marguerite was already moving. She crossed the room casually, gathered Tam with a hand on his shoulder and a murmured apology about an early start, and brought him back. He came willingly, but he looked back once at the little girl, and the look on his face was the oldest thing Sera had ever seen on a child.

"What did you say to her?" Marguerite asked gently, when they were seated again.

Tam considered. Then, in the trade-tongue, careful and clear: "I told her the fire used to be warmer here. Before." He frowned at his bread. "I don't know how I know that. It's like — there's a coat with a lot of pockets, and I keep finding things in the pockets, and I don't remember putting them there."

Sera set down her spoon. "How many pockets?"

Tam looked at her with those layered eyes. "More than me," he said.

* * *

They slept in the hayloft rather than take a room, because Sera trusted rafters more than locks, and in the dark she kept the first watch out of habit while the other two breathed slow beneath rough blankets.

She couldn't sleep anyway. Through the gap in the boards she could see the village: the inn's lamp, the black bulk of the church, the well in the square with its blind stone mouth. A dog barked twice at nothing and stopped, embarrassed. Somewhere a shutter closed. An ordinary night in an ordinary place, except that the ordinary place was a wound wearing clothes, and four hundred people lived their lives inside it, pausing half a beat before saying their own village's name, loving a country-shaped hole without knowing it was there.

*I guarded that border,* she thought, the old thought, the thought that lived in her like a splinter worked too deep to dig out. *Eleven years. I knew every ford and every smuggler's path and every shrine on the Veilmark side. And on the last night, when the order came down — stand aside, let the clerks through, this is above your rank — I stood aside.*

She had told Marguerite part of it, on the road, in the flat voice she used for confessions. She had not yet told her the worst part: not that she had obeyed, but that obeying had been *easy*. That the order had arrived wrapped in such perfect bureaucratic calm that her body had accepted it before her mind could object, the way the villagers accepted the name that didn't fit. The erasure hadn't needed to fight her. It had simply handed her a sentence with words missing, and she had read it through, and stepped over the threshold in the dark.

Below, the well waited in the square with whatever it remembered sleeping in its black water.

Tam stirred in his sleep and murmured something, and Sera leaned over the edge of the loft to check on him, and froze.

He was talking in his sleep. Not crying, not thrashing — talking, calmly, in several voices. A man's voice, a woman's, an old voice, a young one, cycling through phrases of the old tongue like someone shuffling through a deck of cards, laying each down and taking the next. And with each voice, the hay around him dimmed slightly, as if the lantern of him were being carried through rooms and leaving each one darker.

Then, distinctly, in a voice that was not a child's at all — a woman's voice, warm and worn, the voice of someone who had sung children to sleep in a house with a blue door — he said a single name.

Sera did not know the name. But she saw Marguerite, asleep, flinch as if struck, and saw her hand come up to her collarbone, pressing against the place where a ledger entry lived, and heard her say, thick with sleep and grief: "*I'm coming. I'm coming, Mama.*"

Above the borderlands, clouds crossed the unnamed hills, and the moonlight fell evenly on everything, hiding the holes, the way moonlight does.

Sera sat back against the rafter and did not wake her. There would be time enough tomorrow for the road, and the road was long, and somewhere ahead of them a Door stood in a hollow waiting to be paid. But she marked it, the way she had once marked everything along a border she'd failed: the boy spoke in borrowed voices, the mother's voice had found its daughter, and the daughter had answered in her sleep.

Whatever waited for them in the white space on the map, it already knew they were coming.
