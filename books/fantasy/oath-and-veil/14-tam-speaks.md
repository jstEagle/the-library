## 14. Tam Speaks

The borderlands had a way of making silence feel deliberate.

Three days past the last village with a name — or rather, three days past the last village whose name Sera could find in any record, though people lived there all the same — they made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower. The stones were old enough that moss had eaten the mortar, and young enough that no one remembered who had built it. That was the pattern out here. Wells with no names cut into their lips. Fences that ended for no reason, as if the hand that raised them had been interrupted mid-thought. Fields that lay fallow not from blight but from a kind of collective forgetting, as though the soil itself had been told to wait.

Marguerite fed the fire stick by stick, rationing flame the way she'd once rationed coins. Tam sat cross-legged across from her, his bare feet tucked under the hem of the coat Sera had given him — too big, sleeves rolled four times. He was watching the fire with an attention that unsettled her. Not the idle stare of a child. The fixed regard of someone reading.

He had faded again that morning. She'd seen it at the stream: the edges of him gone soft, like ink touched by wet fingers. For a heartbeat his left hand had been translucent, the firelight passing through it, and then it had returned, and he had looked at her with those old, old eyes and said nothing, because Tam did not speak in the trade-tongue. Tam spoke only Veilmark's dead language, and only rarely, and each word cost him something she could see but not measure.

Sera was walking the perimeter, as she did every dusk, checking the dark the way other people checked their purses. Marguerite heard her boots crunching gravel somewhere beyond the firelight, unhurried, thorough. A woman who had guarded a border once and could not stop guarding things now that borders were all she had left to guard.

"Tama," Marguerite said softly, using the name they'd given him, the one that fit like borrowed clothing.

His eyes came up. And there was something different in them tonight. Something gathered.

She set down the stick she was holding. "What is it?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. His small hands pressed flat against his knees, and she saw them trembling — not with cold. With effort. Like a scribe facing a page too large for the desk.

Then he spoke, and the words were not spirals and candle-bending whispers in a dead tongue. They were trade-tongue. Plain, Corvane, market-day trade-tongue, roughened by disuse the way a hinge roughens.

"The fire is warm," he said. "I remember fires. I remember — " his brow folded — "I remember *many* fires."

Marguerite went very still. "Tam. You're speaking."

"I'm speaking." He turned the words over like foreign coins, testing their weight. "It's hard. There are so many mouths in me. They all want to talk at once." He looked down at his hands. "I'm trying to let just one talk now. Just one voice. It's like — like holding a door shut against wind."

* * *

Sera came back into the firelight at some point during what followed, and Marguerite never knew exactly when; she only registered, later, that the oath-keeper was sitting beside her, motionless, her scarred hands wrapped around one knee, listening the way people listen at deathbeds.

Tam told them about the fires first, and then about everything else, in a voice that kept changing without ever leaving his throat.

"I was a woman who baked bread in a town called Vessery," he said, and his posture shifted, shoulders squaring, chin lifting, a matron's authority in an eight-year-old frame. "No. Wait. I wasn't. I *remember being* her. Her name was Odile Marchand and she burned her loaves every second day and her husband teased her and — " the child's face crumpled slightly — "and I can't remember his face. Why can't I remember his face? I had it. I had it this morning."

"Slowly," Sera said. Her voice was gentle in a way Marguerite had not heard before, gentle the way you are gentle with a wound.

"There was a boy," Tam said. "A boy my own age. He had a dog. No — I am the boy? Or I have him?" He shook his head hard, frustrated. "It's all folded up. Like — like your ledgers. Pages written over pages. Everyone wrote themselves onto me when the country — when it — "

"When it was erased," Sera finished quietly.

"When it was edited." Tam's mouth twisted around the word, tasting its wrongness. "That's what it felt like. Not dying. Being *corrected*. Someone read us and decided we were a mistake."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, a nightbird called and got no answer.

"How many?" Marguerite asked. Her voice came out thin.

Tam looked at her, and for a moment he was not a child at all. The look he gave her was ancient and crowded, a window lit by a hundred lamps behind it.

"I stopped counting at nine hundred," he said. "There were more after that. There were so many after that." He hugged his knees. "They didn't all come willingly. Some of them fought it. One man — a ferryman, Ilan, he held on for three whole days, screaming his own name over and over so it wouldn't be taken, and when they finally pulled it out of him the last thing he did was — " Tam stopped. His eyes had gone glassy. "He pushed the memory of his daughter into me. So someone would keep it. So she wouldn't be nobody's daughter."

Marguerite's chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with the cold. "And now you carry her."

"I carry everyone." He said it simply, terribly, the way children say the truest things. "Every street. Every song. There's a lullaby in me that four mothers used to sing, and they harmonized without knowing it, and I can hear all four of them right now if I listen." He closed his eyes. Listened. Tears slid down his face without any change in his expression, as if crying were merely another bodily function happening alongside the others. "It's very loud inside me. That's why I don't talk much. Talking means choosing, and choosing means the others get quieter, and when they get quieter they start to go out. Like coals."

"Like the fading," Marguerite whispered. "Your edges. That's what it is. You're burning through them."

"I'm spending them," Tam corrected, with a bookkeeper's precision that made her flinch. "Every time I use the old words, I spend a little of somebody. The candle-bending was a cooper named Brannic. He loved doing that trick for his sons." He opened his eyes and looked at her with terrible apology. "I'm sorry. I know you need me to talk. But every word is a person."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted a long time. Sera fed the fire without being asked, feeding it more than rationing allowed, and neither woman stopped her.

Marguerite sat with her arms around her knees and tried to hold the shape of what he'd said: nine hundred, and more after. Nine hundred lives folded into one small body that was running out of itself. Every charm she'd ever sold for coppers, every whispered word of power she'd traded in the night market — she had thought magic was a current, a thing you drew from the world. But it wasn't. Out here, in what remained of Veilmark, magic was *people*. It had always been people. The country hadn't been destroyed. It had been *liquidated*, its citizens converted into raw material, and the temple had been drawing off the interest for twenty years.

Her debt ledger. Her contract. The old script beneath her name.

"You said names," she said at last. "You've been carrying names. Do you — " She stopped. Started again, hating how her voice shook. "Is there a name in you that isn't yours? A name you keep saying, that doesn't belong to any of the others?"

Tam tilted his head. The firelight moved on his face.

"There's one," he said slowly. "It sits differently than the rest. The others are memories. This one is more like... an instruction. Like something written on the outside of me instead of the inside." He frowned, digging. "It's yours, I think. Or it wants to be."

The night seemed to lean closer.

"Say it," Marguerite whispered.

He opened his mouth. And for a moment nothing came, and she saw him strain against himself, nine hundred voices all shoving toward the front, and then —

"*Maren.*"

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash. Waited for the memory to rise and break open — a mother's voice saying it, a teacher, a friend, anyone, anything. But there was no splash. Only the falling, and the falling, and the sense of a depth she could not see the bottom of.

"Maren," she repeated. Her tongue moved around it clumsily, and then, horribly, gracefully — her mouth knew the pronunciation before her mind did, the vowels placed with a native's ease, the way her tongue had known the dead language the day a mute child drew spirals in her dust. "*Maren.*"

"It's close to you," Tam said. "Not touching. Close. Like a coat hanging on a hook that someone took down and put back up a thousand times." He crawled forward, across the firelit ground, and took her hand in both of his small ones. His grip was warm and frighteningly light, as if he might come apart if he held on too hard. "Maren Voss. No — Maren *something else*. The something else got taken. But the shape of it is still here." He pressed her palm flat against his chest. "Feel? Right here. That's where they put the hole."

And she felt it. Not physically — or not only physically. She felt it the way you feel a missing tooth: the tongue returning again and again to the absence, mapping the gap. Twenty years of her life before the temple, before the debt, before Corvane — a smooth blank wall where a door should be. She had always assumed the blankness was ordinary. Everyone had a childhood that dimmed. Everyone lost years to time.

But time blurred. Time did not leave clean edges. And hers, she understood suddenly, had been *cut*.

"They took it from me," she said. "My name. My — whatever I was. They took it as payment." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I've spent three years counting coppers against a debt, terrified of losing my name to the ledger. And the temple already has it. They've had it all along. It's probably filed somewhere in that archive, in a drawer, in the old script, next to nine hundred others — "

"Maren." Tam said it again, gently, insistently, and this time she heard it: underneath the word, faint as a watermark, a second name trying to surface through it. A surname. Two syllables, maybe three. It rose almost to the light and sank again, and Tam's face fell with it. "I can't reach it. It's heavy. It's at the bottom with the ferryman's daughter and the baker's husband and all the faces I lost." His chin trembled. "I'm sorry. I'm supposed to give it back to you and I can't lift it."

Marguerite pulled him into her arms.

He came stiffly at first, the way children do when they have been carrying too much for too long, and then all at once, collapsing against her, his whole slight weight surrendering. She felt him shaking. She felt, absurdly, impossibly, that she was shaking with more than one tremor — as if the nine hundred in him had all leaned toward her at once, a congregation turning to face the altar.

"Hush," she said, into his hair. "You don't have to lift it. We'll dig it out together. That's what we're walking toward, isn't it? The Door. Whatever's at the bottom of me, it's behind that Door. Everything they took is behind it."

"Not everything," Tam murmured against her shoulder. His voice had gone strange again, layered, several speakers deep. "Some of it is in front of you. Some of it walks beside you every day and sleeps with a sword under her coat and pretends she doesn't remember why the border makes her sick."

Marguerite went still.

Across the fire, Sera had stopped moving entirely. The oath-keeper's face was half shadow, half flame, and her expression was the one Marguerite had learned to read on debtors' faces in the temple queues: the look of someone hearing their own secret spoken aloud in a public place.

"Sera," Marguerite said carefully.

"Don't," Sera said. Just that. One word, quiet as a drawn blade.

But Tam lifted his head from Marguerite's shoulder and looked at the disgraced oath-keeper across the flames, and his eyes were crowded and kind and merciless all at once.

"You stood at the border," he said. "The last night. All of you in grey coats, and the order came, and you — " he faltered, and Marguerite felt his small fists clench in her sleeve — "you *obeyed*. And that's why you can see us. That's why the forgetting slides off you. Because a broken oath leaves a crack, and we live in the crack." His voice dropped to a whisper. "One of the women in me was there that night too. On the other side of the line. She saw your face. She said you were crying while you did it."

For a long moment Sera did not breathe.

Then she rose, unhurried, controlled, every inch of her the soldier — but Marguerite saw her hands, and her hands were not controlled at all.

"We'll want to move before dawn," Sera said, in a voice sanded perfectly flat. "The ford at Ash Hollow is two days east. After the ford, the terrain opens up, and so does the temple's reach. If Halev's hunters pick up our trail, the crossing is where they'll take us." She turned away from the fire, toward the dark, toward the watchtower's broken silhouette against the stars. "Sleep. Both of you. I'll take first watch."

"Sera — "

"*Sleep*, Marguerite."

Marguerite did not sleep. She held the boy until his breathing slowed and evened, until the crowd inside him went quiet, and she watched the oath-keeper stand motionless at the edge of the firelight with her back to them both, straight as a blade and shaking, very slightly, like a wire.

And it was near dawn, in the grey hour when the fire had burned to a red eye and the birds had not yet begun, that Tam stirred against her side and spoke without waking, in a voice that was not one voice but many, murmuring in his sleep the way other children murmur of sweets and games:

"...the ford... don't let her take the ford road... they're already *there*..."

Marguerite sat up in the cold, her heart beginning to hammer, and looked east, toward the hollow and the water and the two days of open country between them and it.

The hunters were not following them.

They were waiting ahead.
