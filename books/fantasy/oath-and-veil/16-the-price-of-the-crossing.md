## 16. The Price of the Crossing

The river took Sera's scream and gave back nothing.

Marguerite heard it from the reed bank, half a mile downstream, where she had run with Tam pressed against her chest like a stolen thing. The ford was behind her now, hidden by a shoulder of black rock and the gray curtain of morning mist rising off the water. She could not see it. She could see everything anyway — the way her mind insisted on painting it: Sera turning to face the three temple hunters alone, her broken oath-sigil raised in one scarred hand, the old border-magic waking in her like a wound reopening. Light the color of drowned things. Then the mist closed, and there was only the river's voice, patient and indifferent, carrying away whatever it carried.

"Keep running," Marguerite whispered to Tam, though her own feet had stopped.

Tam tugged her sleeve. He looked up at her with those eyes that were too old for his face — eyes that had held a hundred lives folded into one small body — and shook his head slowly. Not at her words. At something else. His lips moved, shaping sounds in the dead language, and even without understanding every word she felt their weight, the way you feel a change in the air before a storm.

*Fading,* he was saying. Or something near it. *Less.*

She saw it then, in the flat gray light. Three days ago his edges had begun to blur — the way ink bleeds into wet paper, softening lines that used to be sharp. Now his left hand was translucent at the fingertips. When he held it up against the mist, she could see the pale suggestion of reeds through his smallest finger. He was made of the country's last unspent memory, and the memory was spending itself faster the closer they came to home. As if the land itself were drinking him back.

"No," Marguerite said. It came out as a denial aimed at nothing, at the river, at the erasure decree, at the whole machinery of forgetting that had ground a kingdom into silence and left this child holding what remained. "No. We're close. Two days, maybe less. You hold on. You *hold on*, do you hear me?"

Tam smiled. It was the worst thing she had ever seen — that patient, adult sadness on a child's face, the smile of someone comforting the living because he had already made peace with what he was. He touched her cheek with his fading hand, and where his fingers passed she felt nothing at all, only a coolness like breath on skin.

Behind them, upstream, a horn sounded. One long note, then two short ones. The hunters' signal. Not a pursuit signal.

A capture signal.

Marguerite's whole body went cold. She knew the temple's codes better than she knew her own handwriting; she had copied them ten thousand times in her years of debt-bound service. One long, two short meant *prisoner secured, escort to Quiet House.* They would take Sera to Corvane. To Halev. To the white rooms where names were unwritten from people the way ink was scraped from vellum, patiently, until the page could be reused.

They would ask Sera where the Last Door was. And Sera, who had already given up everything once for an order she should have refused — Sera would give them an answer, or they would hollow her out until there was no Sera left to give anything.

*Go,* Marguerite told herself. *You have two days. Maybe less. The Door is north, in the hollow where the unmapped country folds in on itself. Tam is dissolving in your arms. Every hour you spend going back is an hour he doesn't have.*

She started walking north. Her legs obeyed the way a scribe's hand obeys — trained, automatic, empty of anything but the next stroke.

She made it forty paces.

Then she stopped, because she could still hear the river, and under the river's voice she could hear something worse: silence. The particular quality of silence that followed when border-magic spent itself out. Sera had held the ford with half-forgotten words from a country neither of them could picture, and those words did not come cheap. Marguerite had watched her pay, at the ambush — watched the magic pull color out of Sera's face, pull years out of her stance, pull something essential out of her spine. If the hunters had taken her alive after that, they had taken someone barely standing.

And Halev knew exactly how to handle people who were barely standing. He had been handling Marguerite that way for three years, with courtesy and ledgers and the gentle arithmetic of debt. He would sit across from Sera in the Quiet House with his neat hands folded and his voice like warm oil, and he would say, *Tell me about the Door, oath-keeper, and I will tell you about mercy,* and Sera — proud, ruined, loyal Sera, who thought she deserved nothing and therefore protected nothing of herself —

Sera would trade herself for Tam. Marguerite knew it the way she knew her own contract's hidden script. Sera had been moving toward that bargain since the day she'd knocked on Marguerite's door: a disgraced keeper looking for one last act large enough to balance the ledger of her shame. Give me the child, she would tell Halev. Let the woman and the boy go. I'll draw you a map with my own blood if it pleases you.

And Halev would accept. Because Halev didn't want the Door destroyed. Halev wanted the Door *managed* — wanted its price paid by someone else, its power flowing into the temple's coffers, another erased memory bought cheap and sold dear.

Marguerite stood in the reeds with the fading boy in her arms and understood, with terrible clarity, that the choice in front of her was not a choice between two roads. It was a choice between two ways of being emptied.

If she went back, Tam died. Not died — *dissolved*, unwritten, scattered into the air of a country that had already forgotten him once. Everything the erased citizens had been, folded into one small body, gone forever. And with him the Door's purpose, the restoration, the returning of names — all of it dependent on a citizen standing at the threshold before the last memory ran dry.

If she went forward, Sera was unwritten instead. Slower. Colder. The temple's method rather than the country's hunger. But just as final. There would be no Sera to find afterward, no wry voice, no scarred hands, no one who had seen Marguerite clearly — seen past the smallness, past the charms and the lies — and chosen her anyway. No one whose capture would feel like this, like a hook set behind her sternum, pulling with every step she took away from the river.

"Choose," said the morning. Or perhaps it was Tam, whispering in the old tongue, translating the world into its simplest terms.

* * *

They walked while she thought, because stopping meant thinking harder. North along the river's eastern bank, through country that grew stranger with every mile — villages with wells that had no names, churchyards full of stones worn blank, farmers who paused mid-gesture as if they'd forgotten what their hands were for. The shape of absence. Sera had named it once, on the road, in her flat keeper's voice: *This is what edited land looks like. Like a sentence with the middle cut out, and everyone pretending the grammar still holds.*

Now Marguerite walked through the grammar alone, and every blank stone looked like Sera's future.

Tam faded as they walked. By noon his outline shimmered when the light hit him wrong. By midafternoon he could no longer hold her hand — her fingers closed on cool air and a pressure like the memory of fingers. He kept pace beside her anyway, stubborn as a candle flame in wind, and he spoke to her in the old tongue in a low continuous murmur, and she caught fragments now: *door, threshold, citizen, price.* He was telling her, over and over, in the language of a dead country, what she already knew.

At dusk they reached the ridge above the hollow. Below, in the fold of land where no map had ever bothered to lie, the Last Door waited — a seam of absolute silence in the hillside, visible only as an absence, a place where the evening birdsong stopped and the wind forgot itself. Marguerite's heart slammed against her ribs. So close. A single night's descent. A single dawn's payment.

And behind her, two days south, Sera sat in a cage on a cart rolling toward Corvane, and every mile of road was a mile of Marguerite's choosing.

She sat down on the ridge with Tam beside her — beside the space where Tam mostly was — and forced herself to think like a scribe. Not like a woman in love, not like a mother in everything but name, but like the person who had survived three years in Corvane by being precise.

*Option one:* go down to the Door tonight, pay the price, release the memories, let the world remember. Veilmark returns. The erasure decree unravels. And when reality complied — when every record rewrote itself, when the temple's power crumbled, when Halev's authority dissolved into the flood of returning names — the Quiet House doors would open too. Wouldn't they? The prisoners of an illegitimate institution, freed by the very restitution she was unleashing.

It was a beautiful argument. It was also a gamble dressed as logic. Because the restoration might take a day to ripple outward, or a week, or a season. And the Quiet House did not wait. The Quiet House *worked*. She had seen the ones who came out of it, when she delivered fresh ledgers: people with smooth faces and missing middles, who smiled politely and could not say why they had come. Sera, who remembered everything — who had built her whole broken self around the memory of one disobedient night — Sera would be the temple's first priority, its greatest prize. They would start with the border. With the ford. With the night of the erasure. They would take the exact memories that made her able to love, and leave the rest.

*Option two:* go back. Two days south, plus however long to reach Corvane, plus the impossible mathematics of getting into the Quiet House — which she could not do, which no debtor scribe could do, except that she had spent three years learning every corridor of the temple archive, every rotation of the guards, every ledger route in and out. She knew the Quiet House's delivery schedule the way she knew her own heartbeat. She could get in. Probably. And then get out with a prisoner, past hunters, past Halev, with Tam dissolving hour by hour behind her—

No. Not behind her. She could not split them. Tam could not be left alone on a ridge; he was barely holding shape in company. Where she went, he went. Which meant going back cost the Door everything. There was no version of rescue that left time enough to return.

One path saved the mission. One path saved the woman. The ledger offered no third column.

That was when Tam took her hand — or put his almost-hand where her hand was — and spoke clearly, deliberately, in the trade-tongue. His first true sentence in it, unbroken, no borrowed voices inside it:

"You can't carry both of us."

Marguerite looked at him. The mist was rising off the valley below, and through his shoulders she could see the first stars.

"I know," she said.

"She would tell you to let her go." Tam's voice was thin, layered faintly with others, the way it always was — a hundred citizens speaking through one throat. "I remember her kind. Oath-keepers spend themselves. It's what they're for. She'd stand at that ford again, and again, and count it fair."

"I know that too."

"Do you know what I remember most?" The boy tilted his head, and for a moment one of the other voices surfaced, older, a woman's, amused and tired. "From all of us. Before. We remember being traded. Our memories sold to pay a kingdom's debts. We remember that no one chose us. That we were *ledger entries*." He squeezed her almost-hand, and she felt it, faintly, finally. "You are the first person in the history of our country who gets to choose. Don't waste it being noble. Noble is just another word for obedient."

Marguerite laughed — a cracked, awful sound — because the erased dead of Veilmark were lecturing her on obedience, and they were right, and she hated them for it.

But she did not move toward the Door.

Because here was the truth underneath the truth, the line written beneath the trade-tongue in the old script, the thing she had known since the horn sounded: she could restore a country later. There might be no later for Sera. Love was not a duty she could schedule; it was the one debt she had incurred freely, and freely-chosen debts were the only ones worth paying first.

And yet — and yet — if she turned south, she abandoned every erased soul folded into the boy beside her. She became the temple with a kinder face. She chose one love over a nation of the forgotten, and called it freedom.

There was no clean answer. There was only the ridge, and the dark, and the seam of silence glowing faintly below like a held breath, and somewhere south a cart creaking toward Corvane with the only person who had ever truly seen her.

Marguerite stood up. Decision crystallized in her the way frost forms on glass — not gradually, but all at once, along invisible lines that had been waiting all along.

"We're going back," she said. "Both of us. For her."

Tam stared. "The Door—"

"The Door has waited twenty years. It can wait three more days." She was already pulling her scribe's knife from her boot, already cutting the hem from her cloak, already planning — routes, rotations, robes, the delivery schedule burned into her memory. "Halev thinks I'm a debtor. Debtors are predictable. He has never once considered what happens when I stop paying."

She looked south, toward the river, toward Corvane, toward the white rooms where Sera was learning what the temple did to keepers who broke twice.

"And when I have her," Marguerite said quietly, to the boy, to the country, to whatever was listening in the dark, "we come back here, and I pay whatever the Door asks. All of it. My name, my record, my self — whatever it costs to open that seam. But I will not choose between them. Do you hear me? I refuse the choice. I am done letting other people write my ledgers."

For a moment Tam said nothing. Then, softly, in the old tongue, he gave her a word she had never heard him use — one of the few the erasure had left intact, a word for what a citizen swore to another citizen across a threshold. A promise older than kingdoms.

Then he turned south with her, a fading child and a nameless woman walking together through unmapped country, toward the city that owned them both.

Behind them, on the ridge, the Last Door waited in its seam of silence — patient as a debt, certain as a door — and began, very quietly, to count the days.
