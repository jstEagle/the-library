## 17. What I Would Give

The hollow where the Door stood was not far now — Tam could feel it, the way a compass needle feels north, his whole small body leaning toward it like grass toward sun. But we had stopped walking an hour past dawn, because Tam had begun to come apart at the edges.

It was subtle, at first. The hem of his coat going translucent where the light caught it, so that I could see the gray stone of the road through the wool as though the wool were smoke. Then his left hand, when he reached for mine: my fingers closed on something like cold water, and for one terrible heartbeat there was nothing there at all before he gathered himself and became solid again, gasping, gripping me with both hands as if he were afraid to let go of his own shape.

"I'm sorry," he said in Veilmark's tongue, which no longer startled me to understand. "I'm trying to hold on."

"I know." I knelt in the road and took his face between my palms. It felt real enough. Warm enough. A boy's face, wind-chapped, freckled across the nose with a constellation I had never seen before this month and could no longer imagine living without. "You don't have to try so hard today. We're close."

He wasn't close to fine, though, and we both knew it. Every day he spent a little more of himself just being here. He slept longer. He forgot words — trade-tongue words, mostly, but yesterday he had looked at a hawk circling over the pines and called it by a name in the old language that made the air shiver, and then he couldn't remember what hawks were called anywhere, in any tongue, and he had cried without sound, the way he did everything, until I held him and named the bird for him in three languages until one of them stuck.

He was made of the last unspent memory of a country that had been edited out of the world. And the ink was running dry.

* * *

We sheltered that night in the ruin of a waystation — one of those buildings that dotted the borderlands with holes in their histories, walls standing, roof half-collapsed, a hearth blackened by fires no one remembered lighting. Sera would have known what this place was. Sera knew all of them; she had walked this border for eleven years before the erasure, and eleven more after it, guarding a line that guarded nothing, waiting for a country to exist again.

Sera, who was in a cell in Corvane's Quiet House because she had turned her back on temple hunters and held a river ford with half-remembered magic so that Tam and I could run.

I had not let myself think about it for two days. Thinking about it was a door I kept shut the way you keep shut the door of a room where someone has died. But the fire was small and Tam was asleep with his head in my lap, solid for the moment, breathing in the slow rhythm of a child who trusted me absolutely, and the door swung open on its own.

The ford. The sound Sera's oath-blade made when she drove it into the shallows and spoke words I didn't know and the river rose up around her like a wall of silver glass. The hunters' crossbow bolts shattering against it. Her face over her shoulder, fierce and calm and certain, mouthing two words at me: *Go. Now.*

I had gone. Of course I had gone. There had been no choice — Tam fading in my arms, Halev's hunters fanning out along the bank, the decree fragment sewn into my bodice and the Door three days' walk away. Any other choice was everyone dying for nothing.

And yet.

I sat in the dark with a sleeping miracle in my lap and understood that I had made a bargain with myself, these two days: reach the Door, save the boy, and then — *then* — think about Sera. As if rescue were a thing that could be scheduled. As if the Quiet House were gentle. I had been inside it once, delivering ledgers. I remembered the silence that lived in its halls, a silence with weight, a silence designed to press names out of people. They would be asking her questions. They would be very patient. Inquisitor Halev was nothing if not patient; he had spent three years demonstrating that to me, one courteous summons at a time.

Tam stirred. "You're sad," he murmured, not opening his eyes. Children always know. This child especially — he was made of feeling, of everything a whole nation had felt in its final hour, and grief was the grain of his wood.

"Go back to sleep."

"You're thinking about Sera." He said it in the old tongue, soft, and the fire bent toward him the way candleflames did when he spoke things that were true. "Me too. My chest feels wrong where she should be."

Mine too. I looked down at him, at the faint translucence creeping up his wrist, and felt the shape of the trap close around me with a click I could almost hear. Two people to save. One of me. The Door would not wait, and neither would the Quiet House, and I was one woman with a stolen decree fragment and no plan worth the name.

* * *

I did not sleep. Instead, by the light of the coals, I unrolled the decree fragment again — the original erasure decree, torn from the restricted archive beneath the temple, the document Halev had offered to forgive my theft of in exchange for a child. I had read it a dozen times since the ford. Tonight I read it differently, because tomorrow I would stand in front of the Last Door, and I wanted to know exactly what I would be asking it to do.

The decree was written in two hands. The outer text was trade-tongue, bureaucratic, obscene in its tidiness: *By order of the Crown and Temple Concordant, the territory formerly designated Veilmark is hereby struck from all registries, rolls, maps, and memories. Let no record bear its name. Let no memory hold its shape. So it is written; so it shall be.*

Beneath it, in the older script — the script I could read, the script that had been waiting in my bones all along — ran the operative clause. The part that made it work. I had translated it piecemeal over the weeks, but tonight, by coal-light, with Tam's breath warm against my knee, I finally read it whole, and the whole of it went through me like cold water.

*Let the Door remain as the seal of this decree,* the old script said. *It opens only to a citizen of the erased land, and only for a price entire: a recorded identity, surrendered whole. Name, debt, deed, and place — all that the world holds of the payer must pass through the threshold and be unmade, that the seal may loosen and the stored years return. No partial payment. No substitution. One citizen, one self, given freely or not at all.*

One citizen, one self, given freely or not at all.

I read it three times. The fire settled. Somewhere outside, an owl asked its question of the dark and got no answer.

Here is what I understood, sitting there with the words swimming in front of me: the Door did not want blood. It did not want magic, or sacrifice in any dramatic sense, or anything I could steal or forge or charm my way around. Three years of selling forbidden charms, of learning every loophole in Corvane's law, of being small and quick and unmemorable — none of my skills applied. The Door wanted the one thing I had spent my whole life protecting.

A recorded identity. Surrendered whole.

My name. Not just the sound of it — the *record* of it. The ledger in the temple archive where Marguerite Voss lived as ink: her debt, her contract, her scribe's mark, her lodging, her purchases, her crimes, her three years of small deceptions all catalogued in a clerk's neat hand. The place she rented. The reputation, thin as it was. The whole architecture of a person as the world had filed her. All of it would have to go through the threshold and be unmade. And on the other side, I would be — what? A woman with no name in any registry. No debt, yes. No leash. But also no proof she existed. In Corvane, an unrecorded person was less than a criminal. An unrecorded person was a rumor. Beggars had papers. Corpses had death-rolls. I would have nothing, and worse than nothing: I would have no way to ever be found again, by anyone, because there would be nothing left to find.

That was the price. That was what it cost to open the seal and let a country's stolen memories pour back into the world — including, presumably, whatever was left of *my* stolen memories, the ones traded away when Veilmark was erased, the childhood I couldn't picture, the mother whose face was a smudge behind smoked glass in my mind. The Door would give those back to the world. But not to me. To pay, I had to stop being anyone the world remembered paying.

And Tam — Tam would be saved. He was made of the country's last unspent memory; if the seal loosened and the stored years returned, he would finally be allowed to spend himself, to become a boy with a history instead of a boy who *was* one. He'd get his eight years back, and the thousands of others folded inside him would scatter home to the people they'd been taken from. Everyone in Corvane would wake remembering streets and songs and a green country east of the mountains. Everything Halev had built his power on would crumble in a single night.

All it required was Marguerite Voss ceasing to exist.

* * *

Here is the strange thing, the thing I keep turning over even now: I did not agonize.

I had expected — I think I had always expected — that if the moment came, I would bargain with it. That I would lie awake doing arithmetic, weighing Tam against Sera against myself, hunting for the version of events where I paid less. That is what I had done my whole life: counted coins against the debt, counted risks against rewards, survived by never giving more than I had to.

But sitting in the ruined waystation with the decree in my hands, I felt no arithmetic at all. I felt something I can only call clarity, quiet as the coals. The choice was not being made tonight. It had been made long ago — in a hundred small moments I hadn't recognized as choices at the time. It was made the morning I opened my door to a barefoot boy drawing spirals in the dust and did not send him away. It was made the night I read my own debt contract and found my true name written beneath the trade-tongue in the old script, and instead of collapsing under the vertigo of it, I had thought: *then I am still hers, whoever she was.* It was made at the ford, when Sera mouthed *go* and every fiber of me screamed to stay, and I went — not because I valued my life over hers, but because saving Tam was the only way any of her sacrifice would mean anything.

I had been choosing this all along. I had simply been calling it survival.

Because here is what I finally saw, plain as the firelight: my identity was already a fiction. Marguerite Voss, debt-bound scribe, seller of charms, small and unmemorable — she was a costume I had worn so long I'd mistaken it for skin. The temple owned her name, but the name had never really been mine; it was theirs, a handle they used to lift me and move me and set me down where I was useful. Even my memories of the last three years were mostly fear, and fear is not a self. It is a cage with a person-shaped dent in it.

What the Door asked for was the cage. It asked me to hand over the dent.

And what remained, afterward — the unnamed woman walking out of a borderland hollow with a living boy beside her and a country's worth of memory flooding back into the world behind her — that woman would be more *me* than Marguerite Voss had ever been. Because she would be chosen. Every hour of her would be chosen, freely, with no ledger and no leash, starting from nothing. I had spent three years dreaming of freedom and imagining it as the absence of debt. It was more than that. It was the absence of everything, and the terror and the gift of building from zero.

I folded the decree along its creases and sewed it back into my bodice, and my hands were steady.

* * *

Tam woke near dawn, the way he always did — all at once, like something surfacing from deep water. He looked at my face and went still.

"You've decided something," he said.

"Yes."

"About the Door?"

"About everything." I fed the fire a stick and watched it catch. "Tam, I need to tell you what it costs, so you can carry it with me. When we get there — if it works — I'm going to give the Door my name. All of it. The ledger, the debt, the whole of who I've been in Corvane. Afterward I won't be able to prove I exist. People won't be able to find me. Records won't remember me. Do you understand what that means?"

He considered it with the terrible seriousness of children, his fading fingers laced together in his lap. "It means you'll be like the country was," he said finally. "Erased. On purpose."

"On purpose," I agreed. "Freely. That's the difference. That's the only difference, maybe, but it's the whole one."

"And you'll forget me?" His voice cracked on the last word, high and thin, and the fire guttered.

"No." I took his hands — solid this morning, thank every god anyone had ever named in any language, solid and warm. "The price isn't my memories, little one. It's my *records*. What the world wrote down about me. What's in my head stays in my head — the Door doesn't take that. I'll remember your face when you're a hundred and I'm dust. That's the one thing no clerk can confiscate."

He exhaled, and some of the tension went out of his small frame, and for a moment he was just a boy relieved, and I loved him so hard it was a physical pain, a hand closing around my heart.

Then he said, "Good. Because Sera's coming back too. She has to. We're not finished without her."

And there it was. The trap I had built for myself out of love, sprung at last, and I found I wasn't even surprised.

"Tam—"

"The Door needs you," he said. "But Sera needs you *first*. She's in the dark place with the heavy quiet, and she thinks nobody's coming, because that's what she's believed her whole life — that her oath broke and so she doesn't get to be saved." He looked up at me, and his eyes were old, old, old, full of everyone he had ever been. "You told me once that you survive by being small and unmemorable. But you're not, Marguerite. You never were. You're the kind of person who walks *into* the Quiet House."

I laughed — a broken, wet sound, half sob. "I don't have a plan."

"You never do," he said serenely. "You make one while you're walking. That's what Sera says. She says it drives her mad and she loves you for it."

Outside, the sky was turning the color of pewter, and eastward, beyond the black line of the pines, the air over the borderlands had that faint wrongness I had learned to feel in my teeth — the seam in the world, the silence shaped like a doorway, three hours' walk away. Behind me lay Corvane, the temple, the Quiet House, a woman in a cell who had given everything for us and expected nothing back. Ahead lay the Door, and the price, and the end of Marguerite Voss.

I could not do both. I had one body, one day, and a boy dissolving at the edges.

So I did the only thing the last three years had taught me, the thing I was actually good at, better than scribing, better than charm-selling, better than being small: I began to plan a theft. The greatest of my life. From the most secure house in Corvane, I would steal one disgraced oath-keeper — and then I would walk to the Door and pay for everything with the only coin I had left.

"Get up, little one," I said, shouldering our bundle, already moving. "We're going back to the city. And Tam — we're going to need your spirals."
