## 1. The Courier's Last Breath

The rib bone had been whispering all evening, and Yara Montrose was tired of its voice.

It belonged to a fisherman named Aldous Crane, drowned three winters past when the Greymoss tide took his boat and his crew and left his widow nothing but debts. The widow wanted a keepsake that would remember him kindly — not the drowning, but the man before it. So Yara carved, and listened, and chose.

*The morning she said yes,* the bone murmured under her burin, *gulls on the roof like a blessing. Flour on her hands. He laughed with flour on his own nose and didn't know it.*

She cut the line of it into the rib's pale curve, small as a hair, and the whisper settled into something warm. Finished work always went quiet in the end — not silent, but content, the way a fire is quiet after the logs catch. She wrapped the rib in wool, pressed her thumb once to its surface out of habit rather than need, and set it in the box marked *CRANE*.

Outside her window, Greymoss did what Greymoss always did after dark: lied about itself. Lanterns swung over the harbor where the king's tax ships pretended not to see the smugglers' boats, and the tavern below her stairs roared with men who would sell their mothers for a cask of untaxed brandy and their mothers' graves for two. Yara had chosen the town for exactly that quality. In a place where everyone trafficked in things better left unmentioned, no one asked what a quiet woman kept in jars.

She banked the coals in her little hearth, hung up her leather apron, and was reaching for the latch to carry the Crane commission down to the widow when someone hammered on her door hard enough to split the wood.

"Open," a voice rasped. "In the name of — open, *please.*"

Not the widow. Not any voice from the tavern. This one came shredded, wet at the edges, the way voices sound when blood is filling up the lungs it should be feeding.

Yara did not move. Her mother had taught her one lesson above all others, though she'd taught it by dying rather than by words: *curiosity is how they find you.*

The hammering stopped. Then came a sound worse than knocking — a body sliding down the other side of her door, boots scraping wood, ending in a heavy, final thump.

She should have stayed still. She told herself she would stay still. But twelve years of carving other people's dead had given her a terrible tenderness for them, and her hand was on the latch before her fear could argue.

The man lay crumpled against her threshold like something the sea had rejected. Grey cloak, road dust, a courier's satchel still buckled across his chest. Three arrows stood in him — shoulder, hip, and one deep beneath the collarbone that must have opened something vital, because his lips were grey and each breath came with a soft clicking sound, like a door trying to close.

His eyes found hers. They were brown and frantic and, even now, absurdly apologetic.

"You're her," he wheezed. "The carver. Montrose."

"I'm closed," Yara said, which was foolish, and they both knew it.

He laughed — a horrible bubbling laugh — and fumbled at the satchel. "They'll come. Behind me. Priests. You have to — " A cough took him, and when he bent forward he spat dark onto her floorboards. "Take it. It knows you. I heard it say your name, gods help me, I heard the *skin* say it."

"The skin?"

But he had already dragged it free: a folded scrap of hide, mottled grey and silver, and Yara knew in one cold drop of recognition that it was wolf-pelt, cured by hands that had never been human. It was stitched along one edge with sinew gone amber with age, torn raggedly along the other, as if ripped from something larger. As if half of something larger.

He pressed it into her hands and gripped her wrists with the last of his strength, and the map — for map it was, mountains and rivers inked in a pigment that shifted like oil on water — grew warm against her palms.

And it spoke.

Not aloud. Not in any voice a sane person would report to another. But Yara had spent her life listening to the dead through their bones, and so she heard it plainly, the way you hear your own heartbeat: a dry, ancient whisper rising out of the pelt itself, threading through her fingers and up her arms.

*Yara.*

Her name. Not the name she used in Greymoss — she'd been careful, so careful, signing her commissions as *Mistress Vane*, keeping Montrose buried like everything else of her mother's. But the skin said *Yara Montrose* anyway, said it the way her mother used to say it across the kitchen, said it with the same cadence, the same warmth, as if the map had learned the name from the same mouth.

The courier sagged back against her doorframe. His grip loosened. "West," he breathed. "Past the passes. Find the other half before — solstice. Before the famine comes for both — " His eyes lost their focus mid-word, sliding past her shoulder to some horizon only the dying can see. "Tell them... tell the kindred... we tried..."

Then the clicking stopped, and he was only a man-shaped weight against her door, and the map lay in Yara's hands whispering her name like a lullaby.

* * *

She had perhaps forty heartbeats to decide, and she spent thirty of them standing frozen while the map murmured and the courier cooled and the sensible part of her mind screamed that this was how her mother had died — not from witchcraft, not really, but from being *found*. From letting the wrong person see the gift. From mattering.

On the fortieth heartbeat, hooves rang on the cobbles below. Many of them, moving slowly, in the deliberate formation of men who owned the night.

Yara looked down at the wolf-skin. It looked back, in whatever way a thing like that looks back, and the whisper shifted — not her name now, but something older, a word like a root: *keeper.*

"No," she whispered to it. "I'm not anyone's keeper. My mother wasn't either. That's why they burned her."

The hooves stopped below her window. Boots hit cobblestone. A voice, smooth as tallow, carried up through the floorboards: "Search the carver's shop. Gently, brothers. What we seek may be fragile."

Bone-priests. Here. Twelve years of careful anonymity collapsing in the space between one sentence and the next.

Yara moved without deciding to move, the way she'd once seen a hare bolt from a hawk's shadow. The courier's body she could do nothing for; she stepped over him with an apology she didn't speak aloud. Into the satchel went her burins — all seven, wrapped in oilcloth, the good ones her mother had left her, handles worn to the shape of her family's grip. The jar of memory-bones she hesitated over, then took, because leaving them felt like leaving graves unguarded. The Crane commission she shoved beneath the floorboard with a stab of guilt she promised herself she'd repay.

The map she bound against her ribs, beneath her shirt, where its whisper sat against her skin like a second pulse.

The stairs were the problem. There was only one set, down into the tavern's back hall, and she could already hear boots on it, climbing, unhurried, certain.

So she went out the window instead.

Greymoss rooftops were built by smugglers, for smugglers — uneven, overlapping, connected by gutters and laundry lines and the casual lawlessness of a town that had never trusted the ground it stood on. Yara had lived above them for six years and crossed them in the dark more times than she could count, chasing cats, avoiding rent collectors, once fleeing a drunk who'd decided her silence was an invitation. She went now with the map's whisper guiding her strangely, tugging at her awareness whenever her foot found the wrong slate, so that she moved through the dark less like a fugitive than like water finding its course.

Below, lantern light bloomed in her workshop windows. Voices rose — the tallow-smooth one giving orders, others answering. Then a sharp crack, and glass rained into the alley behind her as they smashed her jars.

She did not look back. Looking back was how people fell.

She dropped from the last roof into the salt-marsh path beyond the town, where the mud swallowed boot-prints and the reeds stood tall enough to hide a kneeling woman. Behind her, Greymoss glowed with borrowed firelight. Ahead, the Thornfell mountains rose black against the stars, their western passes lost in cloud — the direction the courier had died pointing, the direction the map pulled at her ribs like a hook set in flesh.

The whisper came again, patient now, almost gentle. *West, Yara. West, keeper.*

"My mother said no," she told it, teeth gritted, reeds scratching her face as she crawled deeper into the marsh. "Whatever this is — whatever she died refusing — I say no too."

The map said nothing to that. It only pulsed once against her ribs, warm as a hand, and waited — the way her mother had once waited, every night of Yara's childhood, for an answer Yara hadn't understood until it was far too late to give.

Behind her, a horn sounded from the town walls. Long, low, and unmistakable: the bone-priests' hunting call, the one from her nightmares, the one from the day they took her mother.

They had her scent now. And the passes west were three days' hard walking through country where, everyone knew, the wolves had never signed the king's peace.

Yara ran.
