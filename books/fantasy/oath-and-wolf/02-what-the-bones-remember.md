## 2. What the Bones Remember

The passes above Greymoss were carved into Yara's hands the way other people learned their prayers. She had climbed them as a child, before her mother's name became a warning, before she learned to keep her gift folded small and hidden like a knife against the ribs. Now she climbed them again in the dark, with bone-priests' torchlight crawling through the valley below like a wound that would not close.

She did not stop until the stars wheeled past midnight. Then, in the lee of a split boulder where the wind couldn't reach her, she finally allowed herself to look at what the dying courier had given her.

The map was wolf-skin, soft as smoke, and it was wrong in a way her fingers understood before her mind did. Bone whispered to her — always had, always would — but skin that still remembered being alive was something else. It didn't whisper. It *waited*.

And when she unrolled it across her knees, it said her name.

Not aloud. Not in words. It rose through her palms like cold water rising through a well: *Yara Montrose. Daughter of Maren. Keeper's blood.* The letters inked across its surface stayed stubbornly blank — mountain ranges and rivers drawn in faded umber, but no path, no destination, no mark of where the covenant's second half slept.

"Keeper of what?" she asked the dark.

The map said nothing more. It only pulsed once against her knees, patient as a held breath, and Yara thought of her mother on the pyre, and of all the years she had spent carving other people's dead into keepsakes so she would never have to carve her own grief into anything at all.

She wrapped the map away and slept in snatches, one hand on her mother's pendant — a sliver of carved antler on a leather cord, the only thing the priests had not burned.

* * *

Fen had tracked the thief for three days, and he was tired of it.

The humans called these mountains theirs, which was the first lie he'd ever learned about them. The second was that the Montrose woman would be easy prey — some trembling carver-girl who'd never been farther from her workshop than the smuggler docks. But she had taken the high passes, the ones that killed his kindred's young every winter, and she had taken them alone, at night, in wind that would strip the warmth from a hearth-fire.

He hated humans with the clean, simple hatred of someone who had watched them burn his sister's name out of the world. He had told himself this errand was duty, nothing more: retrieve the map-fragment, carry it home across the mountains, let the elders decide what to do with the last keeper's daughter. The covenant was breaking. The famine was already gnawing at the far herds. Sentiment was a luxury for creatures who had a future.

He found her camped below the Widow's Tooth just before dawn, and he came down on her the way his kind hunted — silent, fast, certain.

Except she wasn't there.

The bedroll was empty. The fire was ash. And the voice that spoke from the rocks above him was calm as a chisel striking true.

"I've known you were behind me since the ford," Yara Montrose said. "You walk like you own the ground. Humans don't."

Fen looked up. She stood on a ledge with a boning knife in one hand and her weight balanced like she'd been born on stone, and her eyes in the grey light were the eyes of someone cataloguing exactly where his arteries lived.

"You have something of mine," he said.

"The map." She turned the knife idly. "Then we want the same thing. Which means you're either my ally or my problem, and I've buried enough problems this week."

"My kindred sent me to retrieve it. Not to negotiate."

"And your kindred sent you alone?" She tilted her head. "For a girl the bone-priests want badly enough to burn a town's customs house over? Either they think very little of you, or very little of me. I'm guessing both."

Something in Fen's chest twisted — anger, or its cousin. "Give me the map, human, and I'll let you walk back down."

"Down is where the priests are." She crouched on the ledge, unhurried. "So here's my offer. The map goes where it's going, and I go with it, because apparently my blood is the lock and your people are the door. You can share the road or bleed on it. I've carved enough bones to know neither of us wants the second option."

He should have taken the map off her corpse and been done with it. That was the plan. That was three days of tracking distilled into a single clean act.

Instead he heard himself say, "The Widow's Tooth pass. We move at dusk. If you slow me down, I leave you."

"If I slow you down," she said, climbing down with infuriating ease, "it'll be because you're lost. Follow me."

* * *

They walked three days into an argument that neither of them would have admitted was becoming companionship.

Fen learned that the Montrose woman navigated by memory — not maps, not stars, but memory. She'd touch a cairn her father had raised, a blaze cut into pine bark twenty years ago, and read the mountain like text. He learned she talked to her tools. He learned she flinched from open flame and hid the flinch badly.

Yara learned that the exile ate almost nothing, moved without sound even when he wasn't trying, and went rigid whenever she mentioned the lowlands. She learned his name was Fen, and that he said it like a man handing over something he expected to lose.

On the fourth night, in a hollow beneath a frozen waterfall, she took out the map again because she could no longer stand not knowing.

It lay between them, blank of any path, humming its patient hum. Fen stared at it with naked longing.

"It won't show for me either," he said. "Three days I carried it before the priests took the courier. Blank as a liar's face."

"It showed me my name." Yara frowned, turning the skin toward the firelight. "My name, and my mother's. Keeper's blood." Her thumb drifted, without her deciding it, to the pendant at her throat — the antler sliver her mother had worn every day of her life, carved with marks Yara had never been able to read.

The map shivered.

Both of them saw it. Across the wolf-skin, faint as breath on glass, a line of light crawled out from the mountains' edge and began to wind westward — up the Thornfell spine, through the passes, toward a peak marked with a symbol like a tooth inside a circle.

Fen made a sound low in his throat. "What did you do?"

"I don't know." But her hand was still on the pendant, and understanding arrived the way her gift always arrived — through the hands first, the mind after. "Bone carries memory. That's what my gift is. That's what it's always been." She lifted the cord over her head, hesitated, then pressed the antler sliver flat against the map.

The path blazed.

Every pass, every fork, every place where the way narrowed to a knife-edge — lit in silver, drawn by a hand that had walked this route long ago. And beneath the path, in letters that flickered like something breathing, a line of script appeared in a language Yara didn't know and Fen clearly did, because he went pale under his weathering.

"What does it say?"

His jaw worked. When he answered, his voice had gone quiet and rough. "*The keeper walks the old road. The keeper pays the old price.*"

"Which means what?"

"It means," Fen said slowly, "that my elders didn't send me to retrieve a map. They sent me to retrieve a *keeper*. And they didn't tell me why the last one died."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the frozen falls with a sound almost like voices.

Yara looked down at the pendant in her palm — her mother's last gift, warm from her own skin, its carved marks suddenly legible in a way they had never been. Not decoration. Instructions. A route. A *warning*.

Her mother had known. All those years, wearing this at her throat, walking to the pyre rather than give it up — she had known exactly what her daughter would one day be asked to do.

"She refused," Yara said aloud, though she hadn't meant to. "Whatever they wanted from her. She said no."

Fen was watching her with an expression she couldn't name — pity, maybe, or its wolfish cousin. "Then we should hope," he said, "that you're braver than she was. Or luckier."

Yara closed her fist around the antler and felt, for the first time since Greymoss, something that wasn't fear.

"No," she said. "We should hope I'm smarter than both of them."

The map pulsed once, as if in answer, and the silver path stretched on toward the peak with the tooth-marked summit — three days' hard climb, solstice two days behind it, and somewhere on the road between, she could feel in her carved-bone sense a presence waiting that hungered worse than the famine itself.
