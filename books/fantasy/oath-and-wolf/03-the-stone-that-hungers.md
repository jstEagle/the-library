## 3. The Stone That Hungers

Fen smelled the priests before he saw them — old blood and incense, tallow smoke and the sweet rot of bone that had been worked too long. The wind came down from the covenant pass in slow, cold breaths, and every breath carried the same message: *they are here, they are waiting, they have always been waiting.*

He crouched at the lip of the ridge with snow gathering on his shoulders and let his eyes do what human eyes could not. Below, in the bowl of the mountain where the covenant stone stood like a broken tooth against the sky, torches burned in a ring. Figures moved between them, robed in grey, their hoods stitched with sigils that hurt to look at too long. Bone-priests. A dozen of them, maybe more, arranged around the stone with the patience of people who had set a trap seasons ago and only now watched it close.

And at their center, beside a brazier that burned without warmth, stood a woman taller than the rest, her hands bare despite the cold, her fingers stained black to the second knuckle.

High Carver Vess.

Fen's hackles rose. He had never seen her, but every exile in the kindred lands knew her name the way they knew the name of the plague. She was the one who had taught the king's order to *carve* — not the gentle shaping Yara did over her little workbench, but the flaying of memory from living bone, the hollowing-out of a person until nothing remained but an obedient shell. The kindred called her the Silence-Maker. They said she had once been kindred herself, before she cut that out of her too.

Below him, half-hidden in the rocks where he'd told her to wait, Yara Montrose was doing something to her mother's pendant. He could hear the faint click of her thumbnail against carved bone, a nervous habit she probably didn't know she had.

*She doesn't know,* Fen thought. *She still doesn't know what we're walking into.*

Or maybe she did. Maybe she'd known since Greymoss, since the courier died with her name in his mouth, and she'd simply decided that knowing was no reason to stop. Humans were like that. They walked into fire holding things they loved and called it courage.

He looked at the ring of torches again. Looked at the stone — vast, older than either realm, its surface seamed with channels that might have been cracks or might have been writing, all of it dark now, hungry now, two winters past its renewal. The famine had already begun in the lowlands; he'd seen the fields on their way north, black stalks standing in frozen mud like burnt matches. Another day without an oath and the hunger would cross the mountains into kindred lands as well. Another day, and both worlds would begin to eat themselves.

*Turn back.* The thought came easily. His exile was already sealed; the elders had cast him out for the crime of speaking plainly about the humans' crimes. If he turned back now, if he left the map-fragment and the Montrose woman to the priests, the kindred would take him home. There would be snow and pack and the long honest silence of the high country. No more human smell. No more of this ache in his chest that he refused to call anything.

The pendant clicked. Clicked. Clicked.

Fen closed his eyes, exhaled through his nose, and went down the slope toward the woman who clicked her dead mother's bones when she was afraid.

* * *

Yara saw the torches and felt the world tilt sideways, the way it had the day her mother was taken — that sense of the ground quietly removing itself from beneath your feet while everyone pretended nothing had happened.

"There," she whispered, though Fen was already crouched beside her, already pointing with one claw-tipped finger. "That's — how long have they known?"

"Longer than us." His voice was flat, but she had learned to read the flatness, to hear the fury banked underneath it like coals under ash. "This wasn't pursuit. This was an invitation."

"Then we go around." Her mind was already moving, the smuggler's maps unfolding behind her eyes — the passes of Thornfell, every goat-track and drainage tunnel she'd memorized in a childhood spent running contraband past the king's tollmen. "There's a chimney on the west face, a fissure. We could come down inside the ring itself—"

"And land in the middle of twelve priests."

"—or we turn around and let both realms starve." She said it harder than she meant to. Harder than she needed to. Because the truth under it was simpler and worse: she had come four hundred miles carrying a dead courier's map and her mother's pendant and a secret she'd kept for fifteen years, and she could not make herself stop now. Stopping would mean all of it had been for nothing. Stopping would mean her mother had died for nothing.

Fen looked at her for a long moment. In the blue dusk his eyes caught the light strangely, gold going to green, and she thought — not for the first time — that there was something almost human in the way he grieved without admitting it.

"The fissure," he said finally. "Show me."

They were halfway across the scree field when the voice came out of the darkness ahead of them, warm and reasonable and terrible.

"You won't need the west face, child. I've had men in the fissure since moonrise."

A lantern bloomed. Standing on the rock shelf above them, wrapped in a good wool coat that Yara recognized because she had sold it to him, was Doss Marlowe — the smuggler who had fed them, hidden them, guided them through three checkpoints with a wink and a lie. He wouldn't meet her eyes. He was looking at the coin-purse in his hand instead, turning it over, as if the weight of it explained everything.

"Doss." Her voice came out very small. "You sold us."

"I sold *passage*," he said, and laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "God forgive me, girl, I sold passage. Vess pays in grain. Real grain, from the southern granaries. My people eat through winter." He finally looked up. "You'd have done the same."

"No," Yara said. "I wouldn't."

Torches bloomed all around them then, closing out of the dark like a wound sealing shut, and the bone-priests came walking out of the snow with their hoods back and their faces calm, and Yara understood that the fissure, the scree, the whole mountain had been a shape drawn around her long before she ever started climbing it.

High Carver Vess descended the rock shelf last. Up close she was not what Yara expected — not cruel, not gaunt. She had a broad, ordinary face, a mother's face, and her black-stained hands were folded gently in front of her like a woman waiting at a well.

"Yara Montrose," she said. "Daughter of Serra. You have your mother's hands. I remember them well."

"You killed her."

"I *asked* her," Vess said, and there was something in her voice then, some hairline fracture under the calm, "and she refused. Do you know what refusal costs, child? The stone demands a life freely given — that is the oldest term of the oath, written before your kingdom had a name. A keeper of the covenant line gives herself at each renewal, and the realms eat in peace for another age. Your grandmother gave. Her mother gave. And your mother stood on that altar with the knife in her own hands—" Vess's fingers spread, black-stained, empty, "—and she looked at me and said, *I have a daughter.*"

The words went into Yara like cold water into a crack. She heard herself say, "So you cut her throat for it."

"We took the debt from her," Vess said. "Unwillingly given, unwillingly taken — that is why the famine has come twice as fast, why the stone hungers the way it does. Every inheritance is also a debt, Yara. Your mother knew that better than anyone. She simply chose to pay it in a different coin than the ones we offered." Her gaze dropped to the pendant at Yara's throat, and something moved across her face that was almost tenderness. "She carried you out of Greymoss in her arms the night before we came for her. Did you know that? She ran first. She only stopped running when they told her the running would be paid for in your life instead of hers."

The world went very quiet. Somewhere above, wind worried at the torch flames. Yara's hand found the pendant and closed around it, and the bone whispered against her palm — her mother's whisper, her mother's laugh, faint as breath on a window — and she thought: *she ran for me. She died for me. And I climbed the mountain she died trying to keep me from.*

"Take the fragment," Yara said hoarsely. "Take the map. Take me. Just let him go — he's kindred, he's exiled, he owes your king nothing—"

"Yara." Fen's voice, low, at her shoulder.

"—he's nothing to you, he hates humans, he only came for the map—"

"*Yara.*"

She turned. Fen was looking at her with those green-gold eyes, and his jaw was set in a line she had learned meant a decision already made, and behind him two priests had begun to move toward him with iron manacles etched full of silencing runes.

"Walk away," he said. "Go back down the mountain. The elders will take me home. It's what should happen anyway — I was sent to retrieve the fragment, not to die on a human altar."

"You were sent to retrieve the fragment," Yara said, "and you stayed."

Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice. "The stone is waking," he said instead, turning toward it, and she let him turn, because she had seen what he was hiding and knew he would deny it to his last breath.

The covenant stone was glowing. Faintly, fitfully, like embers breathing under ash — the seams in its surface pulsing with a dim red light, and with each pulse Yara felt a tug behind her ribs, gentle and insistent, the way a sleeping thing pulls at a blanket.

Vess raised her black-stained hands. "Solstice eve," she said softly. "Midnight comes in six hours, and the stone will open whether you walk to it willingly or are carried. Your mother chose defiance, and the world starved for it. Choose differently, Yara Montrose. Choose freely. That is all the oath has ever asked."

They bound Fen's wrists in rune-scribed iron and led him away from her, and he did not fight, and that frightened her more than any struggle could have — until, at the edge of the torchlight, he glanced back at her, and his lips moved around three silent words she couldn't hear.

But she didn't need to hear them. She had spent her whole life listening to what bones said instead of mouths.

*Don't give it freely.*

Behind her, the stone pulsed again, and deep beneath the mountain, something ancient turned over in its sleep — and opened its mouth.
