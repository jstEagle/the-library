## 4. Oath Rewritten

The altar was older than the kingdom that had tried to own it.

Yara saw that now, bound at the wrist and dragged up its steps by two bone-priests whose faces she would not give the dignity of remembering. The covenant stone rose from the summit of the mountain like a tooth from an old jaw — black basalt shot through with veins of silver that pulsed faintly, like something breathing under frost. Carved into its face in spirals so worn they were nearly smooth: oaths in a language no one living spoke, promises made when the world was younger and crueler about its debts.

Below her, the famine had already begun to show itself. The valley beyond the stone lay gray as ash, the pines stripped bare though it was only midwinter's eve, the river a ribbon of black ice where fish floated belly-up beneath the surface. On the far side of the pass, she could smell it too — the wolf-kindred lands going hungry, their herds thinning, their pups crying in dens dug deep against a starvation neither realm had chosen.

Both realms, starving for a promise no one had kept.

High Carver Vess stood before the altar in robes stitched with finger-bones, her face calm as a frozen lake. "Your mother stood where you stand," she said. "She was offered the same mercy. A life freely given renews the covenant; the giver is honored forever in both worlds. She refused."

"I know." Yara's voice came out steadier than she felt. "I've heard her refuse it every night for eleven years."

Vess tilted her head.

"She carved bone," Yara said. "Keepsakes. They whisper what their owners remembered. One of them was hers." Her mother's pendant hung against her sternum now, warm through her shirt, the little carved wolf on it worn smooth by a decade of her thumb. "Every night I hear the moment they came for her. Every night I hear her say *no.*"

Something moved behind Vess's eyes — not pity. Calculation. "Then you understand what refusal costs. Famine devours Thornfell first, then crosses the mountains. Tens of thousands dead because one woman loved her life more than her—"

"No." The word cracked out of Yara like splitting bone. "She didn't love her life more. She loved *mine* more. That was the trap, wasn't it? The covenant wants a keeper with a child. Someone who'd rather die than leave their daughter alone. And when she wouldn't play the part—" Yara's wrists ached where the bindings bit. "You killed her anyway. You just called it justice instead of sacrifice."

Vess's calm did not flicker. "Kneel her at the stone. If she will not give freely, we will take what the old words allow."

They forced her down. The basalt was cold through her knees, cold as a grave, and the silver veins pulsed faster now, quickening like a heart scenting blood. Fen was somewhere behind her — she could hear him, a low continuous growl that never quite became words, held back by four priests and iron chains that smoked where they touched his skin. He had come for her anyway. Exile be damned. Oath be damned. He had broken every law of his kindred to follow her up this mountain, and if she died here he would die here too, and the famine would take everything after.

So don't die, she told herself. Do what you do.

*Carve.*

"Wait." She lifted her head. "Before you kill me — I want to see the words. The original oath. My family kept it for nine generations; I should at least know what I'm dying over."

Vess considered. Vanity, Yara thought. Even bone-priests want witnesses. "Show her."

A priest took her chin and turned her face toward the stone, close enough that her breath fogged the ancient spirals. And there they were, revealed this near: not one oath but *two*, interlocked, human script and wolf-script wound around each other like rope. She read enough of the human half to feel her stomach drop.

*A life of the keeper's line, freely given, binds realm to realm until the next solstice of need.*

Blood. It had always been blood. Nine generations of Montrose women, each one waiting to be asked, each one hoping the asking would wait until after they were gone. Her mother hadn't been a coward or a saint. She'd been the first one brave enough to say *find another way* — and they had killed her for the audacity of it.

But there, at the bottom of the spiral, half-erased, a line even Vess had missed:

*The covenant is memory made flesh. What is given in truth shall bind what blood has bound.*

Memory made flesh.

Yara had spent her whole life making memory flesh. Bone into keepsake, grief into something you could hold. The covenant wasn't demanding her death. It was demanding a gift — and her mother's death had taught the stone, over eleven bitter years, that blood freely taken was not the same as a gift freely given. The famine wasn't hunger. It was the covenant *starving for honesty.*

"Untie my hands," Yara said.

Vess laughed once, sharp. "You'll carve your own death-wound? Fitting."

"I'll carve a new oath."

Silence on the mountaintop, except the wind and Fen's ragged breathing. Then Vess stepped closer, studying her the way she'd study a bone for cracks. "The old words cannot be altered. The covenant is fixed."

"The covenant is a carving." Yara met her eyes. "And carvings can be rewritten. That's all any oath ever was — someone's hand, someone's will, cut into something permanent. You people have been reading the stone for centuries and never once thought to *answer* it." She raised her bound wrists. "Unless you're afraid of what I might write."

Pride would refuse. Pride would drive the knife. But Vess was a High Carver, and High Carvers believed, above all things, that the stone could not be defied. Let her watch. Let her witness the failure. That was the calculation Yara watched bloom across her face — and it was the last mistake Vess would ever make.

The knife cut her bonds. The chisel was pressed into her hands — Vess's own ritual chisel, antler-handled, its edge honed on the same whetstones that had prepared the blades that killed her mother. Yara knelt at the stone with her mother's pendant pulled out over her heart, and pressed the tool to the basalt.

It was like carving into a sleeping animal. The stone resisted, then shivered, then *leaned into her* — and the silver veins flared white, and every priest on the mountain stumbled back as the old oath lit up along its whole length, both scripts burning, both realms suddenly watching through the stone's single eye.

*What is given in truth shall bind what blood has bound,* Yara carved, letter by letter, her breath smoking in the freezing air. *Not death but remembrance. Not one life spilled, but memory exchanged between realms, freely, forever. Each kindred keeps what the other surrenders. This is the new covenant, cut by the last keeper of the old.*

The stone drank the words. Then it demanded payment.

Yara had known it would. She had known since Greymoss, maybe since childhood — every gift takes, her mother used to say, smiling as she shaved curls of bone into the fire. She braced herself and opened the door she had kept locked for eleven years.

Her mother came all at once. The workshop smelling of tallow and juniper smoke. Hands guiding hers around a chisel, patient, laughing at her first crooked attempt. The pendant being fastened around her neck — *so you always know the way home.* The night the priests came. The door breaking. The last look, fierce and terrified and certain, thrown back over one shoulder. *Run, little wren. Run and remember.*

*I gave you the remembering,* her mother whispered, from somewhere inside the stone now, from everywhere. *Give it forward.*

Yara paid.

It did not hurt the way she expected. It hurt the way an amputation hurts — clean, total, the phantom limb already aching where nothing remained. The memories poured out of her through the chisel and into the stone, and the stone sang them out across the mountains in a voice like winter sunlight, giving them away, exchanging them — and something came *back*, she felt it arrive, memories not her own flooding in to fill the emptied rooms: wolf-kindred recollections, den-warmth and snow-hunts and griefs and joys, a thousand years of the other side of the mountain settling into her like snowfall.

Then it was done. The silver veins dimmed to a steady glow, contented, banked like a well-fed fire. Far below, color crept back into the valley — subtle, not miraculous, but real. Ice groaning as the river loosened. Wind carrying the green, impossible smell of thawing earth.

On the far side of the altar, Vess was screaming.

Not in rage — in recognition. Because the exchange ran both ways, and every bone-priest on that mountain had just received what the realms received: the truth of what their order had done. Yara watched them stagger, drop their weapons, fall to their knees as eleven years of murders surfaced in their minds wearing their own faces. Vess clawed at her own skull, mouthing her mother's name — a name she had never known until this moment — and then she turned and fled down the mountain, and her order broke apart behind her like rotted rope.

* * *

Fen found her at dawn, sitting against the stone with the chisel loose in her fingers and tears running down her face.

She didn't know why she was crying. That was the worst of it — the grief was enormous and sourceless, filling her chest like floodwater with no banks, and when she searched for its shape she found only smooth walls. She knew she had lost something. She couldn't say what. Touching her pendant, she felt only a pretty carving by an unknown hand.

"It's gone," she said to him. Her voice sounded strange. New. "Whatever it was. It's gone."

"Not gone." Fen sat beside her, his shoulder warm against hers, fur still matted with yesterday's blood. His eyes were wet too, and now she understood why — he was carrying it. All of it. The workshop, the laughter, the door breaking, the last fierce look. The covenant had given her mother's memories to the kindred, and Fen had taken them into his keeping the way he took everything: stubbornly, completely, as if it were the only task in either world worth doing.

"I remember her," he said quietly. "All of it. As long as I'm alive, she's not gone. When you touch carved bone and find yourself weeping without knowing why — come to me. I'll tell you a story. I'll tell you about a woman who carved wolves into pendants so her daughter would always know the way home."

Yara looked out over the waking valley, at the thaw crawling green up the mountainside, at a world remade by a bargain struck in grief and paid in love. She didn't remember her mother. But she remembered *that she had been loved* — the shape of it, the weight of it, the way you remember warmth after the fire goes out.

"Tell me the story now," she said.

And as the sun cleared the peaks and the famine died forever, Fen began — and Yara Montrose, keeper of a covenant rewritten in her own hand, listened to her mother's memory like a stranger hearing a beautiful tale, and wept, and did not know why, and was free.
