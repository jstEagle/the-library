## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The dead collector's library smelled of dust and vinegar and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath — like fruit left too long in a locked room. I noticed this before I noticed anything else, because noticing smells is a clerk's first instinct. We are trained to inventory. A man who can name what he perceives is a man who has not yet been perceived himself.

That was the theory, anyway. I had built my whole life on it.

"Crane." Senior Clerk Hollis stood in the doorway with his hands folded over his waistcoat like a man attending his own funeral and finding the eulogy adequate. "You'll take the Ambrose collection. Catalogue everything by week's end. The assessors want valuations before the estate is divided."

"Ambrose," I said. "The collector who died on Tuesday?"

"The collector who *stopped*," Hollis corrected, with the pedantic precision that made him beloved by no one. "There's a difference, and the difference matters to the Guild. Men die. Collections merely pause." He handed me a ring of keys heavy enough to anchor a fishing boat. "Sealed items go in the black crate. Anything that hums, whispers, or addresses you directly goes in the black crate as well."

"And if it addresses me by name?"

Hollis smiled the way a ledger balances — exactly, and without warmth. "Then you write down what it says, Crane, and you file it under Correspondence, Unsolicited. That's what we do with everything in this world that wants something from us."

He left me alone with three hundred books and the sweet rot smell, and I did what I always do when a room makes my skin prickle: I started at the far end and worked methodically toward the thing I was afraid of.

This is not cowardice. This is technique. Any hedge-witch worth her salt will tell you that fear is just information arriving ahead of schedule, and the correct response is to give it an appointment.

* * *

I spent the morning among Ambrose's lesser acquisitions. He had been the kind of collector whose taste ran to the melancholy: pressed angels' feathers gone grey as ash, a music box that played only funeral marches regardless of how cheerfully you wound it, forty-one jars of preserved weather arranged by mood rather than season. One jar held a storm that had been bottled mid-sulk; when I tilted it, the little thundercloud inside turned its back on me. I catalogued it as *Temperamental Cumulus, one (1), sulking*, because a clerk who cannot amuse himself quietly does not survive the quiet.

By noon I had filled two ledgers and eaten half a meat pie standing up, which is the traditional Guild lunch, and I had almost forgotten the smell — the sweetness under the dust — because rooms stop smelling at themselves after an hour, or perhaps we stop hearing them.

The prophecy section was at the heart of the library, naturally. Collectors always put their hearts where they think no one will look, which is why clerks always look there first. Ambrose had kept his behind a false shelf that opened when you pulled the third volume of an agricultural almanac, because even collectors believe in passwords, and even their passwords are boring.

Inside: eleven books. Ten of them were the usual market-grade prognostication — horoscopes for merchants, auguries priced per omen, a charming fraud titled *Your Fortune in the Flight of Pigeons* that had been annotated by a previous owner with increasingly furious marginalia. (*"The pigeon flew WEST, you charlatan. WEST."*)

The eleventh book sat apart from the others, on its own small stand, as though the other ten had agreed not to touch it.

It was bound in leather gone the colour of tea left overnight, and it had no title on its spine, which in my trade is either a sign of humility or a threat. When I lifted it, the weight was wrong — too heavy for its size, the way a purse full of coins is heavier than a purse full of promises — and the pages gave off that sweetness again, close up now, unmistakable.

Ash. Someone had written this book in ink mixed with ash, and ash remembers being fire.

I set it on Ambrose's reading desk, trimmed the lamp, and began, as I am paid to begin, at the beginning.

* * *

The opening pages were ordinary enough, if a book written a century ago in the handwriting of someone who clearly never learned to hurry can be called ordinary. Dates. Observations. The author recorded weather, prices, the comings and goings of notable persons in a city I did not immediately recognize — though the street names nagged at me, familiar the way a dream is familiar, shaped right but lit wrong.

Then, around the twentieth page, the entries changed character. They stopped recording what had happened and began recording what would.

I know the difference. I have spent six years learning to tell the difference between a record and a claim, between ink that describes and ink that insists. Most prophecy is the latter — wishful thinking with good posture. But this was neither. This was bookkeeping.

Whoever wrote this was not predicting the future. She was *entering* it. Each entry had the dry, unimpassioned cadence of a transaction: item received, item owed, date of settlement. And the dates marched forward, year upon year, decade upon decade, past the author's own plausible lifetime, past any human span, into years that had not yet arrived when the ink dried.

A century of forward-dated entries. Written in advance. Settled on schedule.

I told myself it was a hoax. I want that on the record, since I keep records: I told myself it was a hoax, and I believed myself approximately as much as I believe the jars of weather.

Because here is the thing about hoaxes — they end. A fraudster writes his impossible dates up to the edge of his own credibility and stops, satisfied. This book did not stop. It continued through years I knew to be historically accurate, describing events I knew to have happened — the fire at the Tannery Row, the flood year, the succession crisis of the old king's reign — each one entered *before* it occurred, each one settled precisely as written, with the same small notation beside every fulfilled entry:

*Paid in full.*

And then the handwriting changed.

Not gradually. Not the way handwriting changes across a life, loosening or tightening with age and circumstance. It changed between one entry and the next, mid-decade, as though the writer had set down her pen and someone else had picked it up — except the letters were still recognizably hers, same slant, same cramped ascenders, just... wearier. As if the hand had been writing for a very long time indeed, longer than hands are issued for.

The new entries were shorter. Colder. And they had begun to include names.

* * *

I worked until the lamp burned low, and I will confess to the methodology of a coward: I read the book out of order, skipping toward my own era, telling myself I was assessing valuation and not searching for myself. A clerk's rationalizations are the finest in the world. We practice them daily against our consciences, and we win on points.

The entries for the last twenty years concerned the Guild itself. I should have expected it — everything concerns the Guild eventually; water concerns the sea — but seeing us there, in a century-old hand, listed among the trades and settlements, made my stomach perform a slow, professional revolution.

*The Ledger Guild, established. Entry accepted. Interest accrues.*

*The vaults dug deeper than the foundations require. Purpose: below. Payment deferred.*

*A choir engaged for the settling of volatile goods. Their voices accepted as surety.*

I thought of Odile then — the choir-singer who sometimes passed me in the corridors with her guarded eyes and her throat wrapped in scarves even in summer — and I filed the thought away in the drawer where I keep things I am not ready to open. The drawer was getting full. Drawers like that have a way of bursting.

I turned another leaf. Another decade. The entries grew sparser, the hand wearier, until the pages thinned toward the present year like a river narrowing toward its source.

And there — on a page near the end, in that tired, tireless script — tonight's date.

Circled.

Circled in ash-brown ink, drawn twice around, the way a merchant circles a figure that must not be misread, the way a creditor circles a name.

I sat very still. In the counting-house we have a saying: *the ledger does not blink first.* I did not blink. The circled date did not blink. Between us, the lamp flame leaned in a draft that had no source, and somewhere above me, in the sealed dark of Ambrose's shelves, forty-one jars of weather went quiet all at once, as if listening.

Below the circled date, in smaller letters, the entry itself:

*Tonight: the reader arrives.*

*He comes to catalogue. He believes he is working.*

*Note the true name, so there is no mistake when settlement falls due:*

And beneath that, in letters I had not seen written down in seven years — letters I had buried so deep in my own past that I'd nearly convinced myself they belonged to a boy who had died on a road outside a hedge-witch's cottage —

*Rafael Amberglass.*

Not Crane. Not the surname I'd bought from a forger in Milltown for three silver pieces and a convincing limp. My true name. The one Mother Yew had taken from me along with my apprenticeship, my freedom, and most of my sleep — the one I had fled a hundred miles and six years to escape — sitting in a century-old book in a dead man's library, patient as accrued interest.

The past does not chase you. I understood that then, sitting in the sweet-smelling dark with my own name looking up at me like an open palm. Chasing implies effort, urgency, the possibility of escape. The past simply *waits*. It files you under Correspondence, Unsolicited, and it knows that sooner or later every debtor reads his mail.

* * *

Here is what a sensible man would have done: closed the book, logged it as *Prophetic ms., provenance uncertain, value pending assessment*, crated it, and reported the anomaly to Hollis, who would have reported it upward, and the Guild — efficient, thorough, hungry — would have taken the book into the vaults below the foundations, into whatever "below" meant, and I would never have seen it again, and perhaps that would have been a mercy.

But I had spent the whole day reading forward-dated entries, and one of them, three pages earlier, had said:

*If the reader follows instruction, the debt may yet be renegotiated. If he delivers the book to those who feed, it is finished.*

Deliver it to those who feed. The Guild. My shelter. My invisibility, purchased and maintained at rates I had never dared to calculate.

So I did the unsensible thing. I did the thing the book, written a hundred years ago by a hand that should have been dust, seemed to be begging me to do.

I stole it.

Me — Rafael Crane, model clerk, six years without a single irregularity in his columns — slipped a century-old prophecy into the false bottom of my document case, trimmed the lamp, locked Ambrose's library, and walked out past the night-porter with my face arranged into the expression we all learn: *nothing here, nothing to see, nothing owed.*

The porter nodded. The fog took the street. And the whole way home I felt the book against my ribs like a second heartbeat, slower than mine, older than mine, keeping time to a schedule I hadn't agreed to.

* * *

At my lodgings I did not sleep. I sat at my table with the book open to the final pages, because there was one more thing I had not let myself read in the library, and a man can only defer an appointment with himself for so long.

The last entry. Dated tomorrow.

I read it by candlelight, alone, with my true name still ringing in my ears like a struck bell that refuses to dampen:

*Tomorrow the reader will remember the bargain he ran from. He will tell himself he was a child, that the choice was made for him, that running was his own idea.*

*It was not his idea.*

*I am sorry. I have been sorry for a hundred years. The ink is nearly gone and the account is nearly due, and everything I did, I did to save him —*

The sentence broke off there. Not ended — *broke*, the way a voice breaks, the way a promise breaks, the nib tearing the paper as though the writer had pressed too hard against something she could not say.

*I*, the entry said. She had used formal entries for a century — *the reader, the subject, the debtor* — and at the end, with her ink failing, she had called me by no title at all. Just *him*. Just *I*.

I knew that voice. I knew that slant of letter, that cramped ascender, that habit of pressing too hard. I had watched it write cures and curses across seven years of my childhood, across a kitchen table that smelled of rosemary and iron, across the contract I signed the night I ran.

Mother Yew.

Dead these six years — I had heard it from a passing tinker, and I had not mourned, and I had told myself that not-mourning was the same as freedom — was the author of this book. Had been its author for a century. Had written tomorrow's date in ash-brown ink while I was still a boy who hadn't yet learned to lie about his name.

The candle guttered. Outside, the city settled into its nighttime arithmetic of debts and dreams, and somewhere below the streets — below the foundations, *below* — I felt, for the first time but not, I suspected, the last, something vast turn over in its sleep and notice the sound of a page turning.

Tomorrow, the book said, I would remember the bargain I ran from.

I looked at the torn sentence, at the word *sorry* pressed hard enough to nearly cut the page, and I understood that I already remembered everything — the cottage, the contract, the road, the running — and that remembering had never been the problem.

Understanding what I had run *from* was.

Tomorrow, then. But the night was long, and the ink was old, and some appointments a man keeps early.

I reached for the page where the memory lived.
