## 3. The Hedge-Witch's Mark

Memory is a ledger too. Every entry dated, every debt accruing interest, and no matter how carefully you keep the books, some clerk in the back room of your skull keeps making entries you never authorized.

Here is the entry I've spent nine years trying not to read.

---

The house at the edge of the Thornmere marshes leaned like a drunk against a lamppost, which is to say it had been leaning so long that leaning had become its character rather than its condition. Mother Yew bought it that way, she said, because honest houses stood up straight and lied about their foundations, while a crooked house told you everything about itself the moment you looked at it. I was eleven years old and thought this was wisdom. I was eleven years old and thought most things were wisdom if they were said slowly enough by someone who owned a cauldron.

She'd found me—or I'd found her; the direction of finding is one of those entries we disputed for years—in the market at Lower Wick, where I was running a small and technically fraudulent enterprise involving a dead crow, a length of red thread, and the gullibility of farmers who believed crows could carry messages to their sons at war. It's worth noting that the fraud worked because I could write. Not well. But I could make marks on paper that looked official, and in a village where three men out of ten could sign their names, looking official was ninety percent of magic.

Mother Yew watched me sell a farmer a message to his dead wife instead of his son at war—the crow was ambiguous about its destinations—and when he shuffled off clutching his thread, she said:

"You lie beautifully."

I braced to be beaten. That was generally what happened next.

"Thank you," I said, hedging my bets, because thank-you works for both compliments and sentencing.

"Lying is just writing on air," she said. "Most people can't do it because they're afraid of blank space. You aren't afraid of anything except being seen. Come along. I need an apprentice and you need to not be hanged."

And that was it. No parents consulted—there were none to consult, or none who would admit it. No contract signed, though as I would learn much, much later, there was very much a contract. An eleven-year-old boy walked out of Lower Wick carrying a dead crow and a future he hadn't read the fine print on, which is more than most of us get and less than any of us want.

* * *

Apprenticeship under Mother Yew was less wand-waving and more washing-up. Her craft, she explained over the first of ten thousand pots I scrubbed, was hedge-craft: the magic of boundaries. Doors and thresholds, fences and property lines, the seam between one thing and another. "Everything true happens at an edge," she told me. "The middle of a field teaches you nothing. Stand at the fence line and watch what crosses it."

What crossed our fence line, mostly, were people with problems and insufficient coin.

A woman whose husband had wandered off with a tinker came to have him *unwandered*. A man wanted his name taken off a debt-note that had been sold three times downriver until the debt itself seemed to have multiplied like mice. A girl brought a baby that wouldn't stop crying and left with a baby that slept, and paid in bread, and Mother Yew waved off my objection that the bread wasn't enough. "You're keeping accounts already," she said, pleased and not pleased at once. "Good. But learn which columns matter."

My job was the paperwork. She taught me letters properly, then numbers, then the strange grammar of binding-marks—sigils that weren't decoration but *terms*, each stroke a clause. She taught me that every working needed recording or it curdled into something else. Magic unrecorded, she said, was like a promise made in the dark: easy to deny later, dangerous to both parties.

So I kept her book. Great leather thing, swollen with decades of entries in a hand that started crisp and grew, over the years, looser and stranger, as though the writer were gradually being pulled off the page by something behind her.

I didn't notice that. I was twelve, and then thirteen, and then fourteen, and I noticed girls and whether my boots fit and how to fake illness convincingly enough to skip church. The book was just work. The way a fish doesn't notice water.

* * *

The bargain—I knew even then it was a bargain, though I didn't know whose side of it I was standing on—came the winter I turned fifteen.

A man came to the door at dusk, which is when all the worst customers arrive, dressed too finely for the marsh road and walking too carefully, as though the ground might report on him. He smelled of ink and cold cellars. Later I would learn to recognize that smell from across a city. That night I only remember thinking he looks like a ledger wearing a coat.

He and Mother Yew talked in the kitchen with the door shut, which she never did when I was home. I sat on the stairs and listened through the gap, which is the apprentice's oldest art and still the most reliable.

"—the arrangement stands," the ink-smell man was saying. "The guild holds the note. You hold the asset. When the term matures, delivery is expected."

"It will be delivered," Mother Yew said. "It has always been going to be delivered. That was the point of the whole wretched design."

"The guild prefers certainty."

"The guild can prefer whatever it likes. Certainty is for people who haven't read the prophecy." Something scraped—her chair, or her knife on a whetstone; with her it was often hard to say. "Tell Vess the account is current. And tell him if his clerks come sniffing at my house again, I'll bind their shadows to their boot-heels and let them walk in circles till spring."

He left without goodbye. I heard the front door, then nothing, then her voice, close: "Rafael. Stairs creak. You'll want to work on that before you spy on anyone important."

I came into the kitchen expecting a beating and got tea instead, which frightened me considerably more.

"Who was that?" I asked.

"A creditor," she said. "Drink your tea."

"Creditor of what? We don't owe anybody. You told the miller we don't owe anybody."

"I tell the miller many things." She wrapped her hands around her cup and looked at me over the rim, and I saw—for the first time, and only once—that she was tired in a way that went all the way down past her bones into whatever held her bones together. "Boy. Do you know why I took an apprentice?"

"To wash pots."

"To be *seen*," she said. "Everything true happens at an edge. I am an edge, Rafael. There are things on either side of me that can't touch each other except through a middleman, and a middleman must be visible or the whole trade fails." She drank. "You asked who he was. He's from the Ledger Guild, in the capital. They record trades. Impossible trades, mostly. Bottled weather, borrowed luck. Names."

"What kind of name?"

"The kind that belongs to a person," she said, and changed the subject so smoothly I only realized days later there'd been a knife hidden in the pivot.

* * *

I stayed two more years. Those are the years I audit most often, lying awake in my garret above the counting-house, trying to find the exact entry where love should have appeared and checking whether it ever actually did.

Because here's the trouble: I think it did. I think she loved me, in the way she loved everything—obliquely, usefully, with terms and conditions attached, but really. She taught me to read because reading was power. She fed me first at every meal, which in a hedge-witch's household is practically a coronation oath. When I took fever at sixteen she sat up three nights running with her hand flat on my chest, doing something slow and expensive that aged her visibly, and when I woke she said only, "Don't be ill again, it's ruinous bookkeeping."

And yet. And yet there was the locked chest under her bed that hummed faintly, like a struck string deciding whether to keep ringing. There were nights she stood at the eastern window facing away from me and spoke words in a rhythm that made my teeth ache. There was the fact that she never once, in seven years, answered a direct question about where I'd come from—not the market, not before the market—with anything truer than "you arrived."

And there was the night I found the page.

I was seventeen. I'd been copying entries into her master book, the fair copy, the one that went—to where? I never asked; asking was one of my failures as an apprentice—when my eye snagged on an entry from eleven years back. My own arrival year. The hand was hers, unmistakable, the letters tall and stern as fence posts:

*Received: one asset, male child, approx. 11 yrs. Provenance: arranged. Term: seven years' service, thereafter transfer of custody per original agreement. Note appended:* He writes well. Better than expected. This changes the arithmetic.

This changes the arithmetic.

I sat with that page for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the walls the house settled deeper into its lean. And I understood, in the wordless way you understand a thing before your mind assembles the sentences for it, that I had never been found. I had been *sourced*. Seven years of service, and this spring marked seven years exactly, and somewhere a guild held a note with my name on it, and the woman who taught me my letters had written me into existence as a line item before I'd ever sold my first fraudulent crow.

I want to tell you I confronted her. That I threw the book down and demanded truth and got it. What I actually did was what I have done my entire life: I copied the entry out in my own hand, folded the copy small, put it in my boot, and began to plan.

Running, after all, was the one skill I'd brought to the apprenticeship fully formed.

* * *

I left on the last night of my seventeenth year, which I chose deliberately, because if the term was seven years I wanted the theft of myself completed before the invoice arrived. I took the copy in my boot, the clothes I stood in, and—this shames me now, in the specific shame of a thief who later learns what the stolen thing cost—her good penknife, because it cut clean and I'd always liked it.

I meant to leave quietly. I got as far as the gate.

She was standing at it. At midnight, in the rain, at a gate half a mile from the house, which meant she'd known the hour and had walked out ahead of me, which meant she'd known the route, which meant—

"How long have you known?" I shouted over the rain. It was the bravest sentence of my life to that point.

"Since before you were born," she said, "which is the answer to nearly everything, and the one nobody wants."

"You sold me."

"I *held* you," she said, and her voice cracked like green wood, like something alive being bent too far. "There's a difference, boy, and the difference is the whole of everything, and I cannot explain it to you tonight because tonight the difference doesn't help you. Run. Run now, run south, run to the city, lose yourself in the ledgers—" and she laughed, horribly, at her own joke or the world's, "—hide among the clerks, they never look at their own kind. Go. Go and don't look back and hate me if it keeps you warm."

"Why?" Rain, tears, same thing at that distance. "*Why?*"

And Mother Yew, my mistress, my captor, the nearest thing to family the world had ever assigned me, pressed something into my hand—small, wax-sealed, warm as a bird—and said the sentence I have carried for nine years like a stone in the boot opposite the one with the page:

"Because everything true happens at an edge, and you, my dear, terrible boy, were always going to be the edge. I only wrote down what was already coming. Ask yourself someday—who taught you to run, and who made sure you'd run *here*?"

Then she turned and walked back toward the house, and the rain closed behind her like a door, and I ran south with a sealed thing in my fist that I never opened, because opening it felt like agreeing to something.

* * *

Nine years. Nine years of ink-stained fingers and anonymous excellence in the Ledger Guild, nine years of being a man nobody looked at twice, and I told myself every night that I had escaped. That the running had been mine. That the boy who fled the marshes had chosen the city, chosen the guild, chosen the comfortable gray invisibility of double-entry bookkeeping for impossible things.

But tonight I catalogued a dead collector's books, and in one of them—a prophecy older than my grandmother—someone circled today's date in ash-brown ink and wrote my true name beneath it. Not Crane. The name Mother Yew gave me, the one nobody in this city knows.

Her handwriting. Unmistakably. Tall and stern as fence posts.

Which means the question she left me with on a rainy midnight has stopped being rhetorical, and I am going to need considerably more than stolen wine to sit with the answer—which is unfortunate, since the only person in this building who seems to know anything is a choir-singer who just caught me elbow-deep in restricted stacks and is currently looking at me like I'm the interesting kind of problem.

She's humming something under her breath. The shelves are humming back.

I don't think that's supposed to happen.
