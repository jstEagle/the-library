## 4. Odile Sings the Staircase

The Ledger Guild keeps its volatile trades in the cellar, and the cellar keeps its own opinions about visitors.

I learned that my first week. A jar of bottled thunder had been shelved next to a crate of secondhand dreams, and by morning the two had quarreled so thoroughly that three clerks woke speaking in rhyme and one of the apprentices could only say the word *herring* for two days straight. The guildmaster's solution, before I arrived, had been to lock the door and post a sign reading DO NOT OPEN UNTIL FEELINGS SUBSIDE. Which is rather like posting a sign on the sea.

Then they hired me.

Not for singing, officially. Officially I am "an acoustic remediation specialist," which is guild-speak for *a woman who hums at problems until they stop being problems*. My contract says I settle trades. What that means in practice: when a widow's shadow arrives still grieving its former owner, someone has to sit with it in the dark and sing it calm. When a borrowed year comes back from its borrower worn thin and resentful, someone has to croon it back into shape before it's resold. When a jar of laughter goes sour—which happens more than you'd think; laughter ferments—someone has to open the lid and let the old joke out before the new one can breathe.

That someone is me. Odile Vane, twenty-six years old, voice like a key that fits every lock in the building, employed to soothe objects that should not have feelings and are furious about having them.

I want to be clear about something: I am very good at my job. Not because I love it—I don't, particularly, or I tell myself I don't—but because I understand those objects down to the marrow. A shadow that grieves. A year gone thin with use. A laugh that's soured in the dark. I know what it is to be kept somewhere you didn't choose, doing work you didn't pick, humming along to someone else's tune because the alternative is to find out what happens when you stop.

So when the cellar started moaning on the third Thursday of autumn—a low, structural sound, grief-shaped, coming from the stairwell that wasn't there anymore—I went down without being asked.

* * *

The stairwell had been sung into existence forty years ago by a choir-singer named Bethel Quill, who was, according to the oldest clerks, the finest acoustic remediator the guild ever employed. He'd built the staircase as a favor: eleven steps down into the deep archive where the truly dangerous trades were kept, each step a held note, each note a small act of obedience extracted from stone. Stone doesn't mind holding a note, if you ask nicely. Stone has nothing but time.

Except that Bethel Quill died nine years ago, and nobody thought to renew the song, and songs are like fires: they go out if nobody tends them. So the staircase had been fading for years—the eleventh step gone first, then the seventh, the fourth going soft around the edges—and now, tonight, the whole thing had finally given up the ghost, leaving a sheer drop into the deep archive and a hole in the air shaped exactly like the absence of stairs.

Which meant the deep archive was sealed. And the deep archive, per the ledger I wasn't supposed to read, contained items awaiting "final disposition," which is guild-speak for *things we bought and can't sell and can't destroy and would very much like everyone to forget*.

And per the same ledger, one of those items had been moaning all evening in a voice I recognized, professionally, as the voice of something abandoned.

I stood at the lip of the drop with my lantern and considered my options. Report it to the day-clerks and wait a week for permission. Or—

Well. I have never in my life waited a week for permission. Permission is a currency, and I've never had any, and people who've never had a currency develop strong opinions about how it's spent.

I did what any sensible woman does when faced with a missing staircase.

I sang a new one.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about singing architecture: it costs. Every step is a note pulled out of your chest and set down in the world, and the world takes it and keeps it, and you feel each one leave like a coin from a purse you can't refill. Bethel Quill gave this building eleven steps and it aged him ten years. The clerks remember his staircase. Nobody remembers what it took.

I gave it seven steps, because seven was what I had tonight, and I made them good ones—a descending scale in the old choir-mode, minor third on the landing, each tread a sustained tone that the stone accepted with the weary gratitude of an old dog asked to work past retirement. The stairs hummed faintly under my hand as I descended, still warm from being born. If you pressed your ear to them you could hear me in there, a little bit. That's the bargain nobody reads. Part of the singer stays in the song.

At the bottom, the deep archive stretched away in rows of iron shelving, and the moaning was louder here, and it was not coming from the shelves.

It was coming from a man.

He was crouched behind the last row with a candle stub and a book open across his knees, and he was so absorbed in whatever he was reading that he hadn't noticed seven newly-sung steps appearing above him, which told me either he was very focused or very foolish, and the way he was hunched over the book—shoulders up around his ears like a man expecting a blow—suggested both.

"Excuse me," I said.

He made a noise like a kettle being murdered and dropped the book, caught it, clutched it to his chest, and looked up at me with the expression of a man deciding between fainting and lying. He chose lying. I respected that. It's what I'd have done.

"I'm inventorying," he said.

"At midnight."

"The deep archive requires... nocturnal inventorying."

"Mm." I lifted my lantern. He was youngish, ink-stained to the elbows, with the particular pallor of a man who works indoors and sleeps badly. Clerk's grey coat, clerk's careful hands. But he held the book the way you hold a thing you've stolen—not guiltily, but *carefully*, the way you hold a bird with a broken wing, or a lit fuse. "You're Crane," I said. "Rafael Crane. Third desk from the window. You balance the ledgers."

His face did something complicated. "You know my name."

"You balance the ledgers," I repeated. "Everyone knows your name. There are eleven of us in this building after dark and we all know each other's names, it's a consequence of the arithmetic." I nodded at the book. "What is it?"

"Nothing. An old catalogue."

"It's moaning-adjacent, though, isn't it? Whatever's in it. Whatever's in the archive." I tilted my head toward the shelves. "Something down here has been crying for six hours. Things in the deep archive don't cry unless they've been touched recently, or read recently, or—" I looked at him, at the candle stub, at the ink on his fingers that was darker than clerk's ink, ash-brown almost, "—or unless someone found them interesting."

He stood up slowly, still holding the book. Up close he had the look of a man who had practiced being unremarkable so long that the practice itself had become remarkable—you couldn't help noticing how hard he was trying not to be noticed. I knew that look. I wear it myself, some days, under the uniform.

"Who are you?" he said.

"Odile Vane. Acoustic remediation." I smiled the smile I use on volatile trades. "I settle things."

* * *

We ended up on the seventh step, sharing a bottle of wine I stole from the guildmaster's office on our way up, because it seemed important, somehow, that our first joint crime be a good one. He didn't argue. That was the first thing I liked about him—that and the fact that when I'd asked him point-blank what was in the book, he'd said, after a long pause, "A date. Tonight's date. Written a hundred years ago," and then, quieter, "and my name underneath it."

Most men would have lied again. Most men would have kept lying until the lying became load-bearing. Rafael Crane had lasted four minutes, which for a guild clerk is practically a confession of passion.

Now he sat beside me on the humming step with his knees drawn up, turning the wine bottle label toward himself and away, toward and away, a nervous habit he probably thought nobody noticed. The stairs sang faintly beneath us. Somewhere below, the deep archive had gone quiet, its grief interrupted by company.

"So," I said. "Acoustic remediation specialist. That's the official title. Would you like to hear what it actually means?"

"If you'd like."

"I hum at things until they stop suffering." I took the bottle. "Shadows. Borrowed years. A jar of laughter that went off. Once, a mirror that had been sold so many times it no longer believed in anyone who looked into it—that one took all night." I passed the bottle back. "Your turn. 'Balancing the ledgers.' What does that actually mean?"

He was quiet long enough that the stairs nearly finished a full phrase.

"It means I write down impossible things in columns," he said finally, "so that they add up. Bottled storms. Names. Years of people's lives. Someone sells their childhood, there's a line for it, and I make sure the line balances against whatever they got in exchange." His thumb moved over the bottle's neck. "And lately—" he stopped.

"Lately?"

"Lately I keep finding entries in my own handwriting that I never wrote." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Whole entries. Trades I never recorded, in my hand, dated tomorrow. Last week." He laughed, one dry note with no floor under it. "I checked the ink against my pot. It matched."

I looked at him properly then. The hunched shoulders. The practiced invisibility. The man who makes impossible things add up, discovering that impossibility has started making entries in him.

"That's not a haunting," I said. "Hauntings repeat. This is *editing*. Something's using your hand as a pen."

"Yes," he said, with the relief of a man who has been told his house is haunted by something specific instead of something general. "Yes, exactly. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'm not sure it's better." I drew my knees up too. Below us, the archive breathed. "Why do you stay, Crane? In this building. With the editing handwriting and the crying objects and the guildmaster whose office wine I just robbed. Why not run?"

The question landed harder than I meant it to. I watched it land. He stared at the wine bottle for a while, and when he answered, his voice had gone somewhere flat and far away, the voice of a man reciting something he'd memorized to survive.

"Because I ran once already. From someone. Before the guild." A muscle worked in his jaw. "She was—my teacher. I owed her everything and I paid it by disappearing, and I told myself the guild was shelter, but it turns out shelter is just a smaller room with better locks." He glanced at me, quick and defensive, waiting for judgment.

He didn't get any. Because here is the truth I don't put in my contract: I understood completely. I have a mask too. Mine is the smile I use on volatile trades and the title on my employment papers and the story I tell that I'm here for the wages. Underneath it is a girl who was bound to a choir she didn't choose, singing other people's cages quiet, who tells herself every night that she can walk out whenever she wants and has never once tested the theory.

Two masks, one stairwell. I looked at Rafael Crane looking at his wine, and I thought: *there you are.*

"Why do you stay?" he asked. Fair. Turnabout.

"Because I signed something once," I said, "that I don't fully remember signing, and I suspect the remembering is part of the terms." I took the bottle and drank, because the sentence deserved it. "Because my voice fits locks, and someone went to the trouble of fitting me to this building. And because—" I gestured vaguely upward, at the floors of sleeping clerks, the counting-house, the vaults, "—I think this place is a mouth, Crane. I think it eats. I've settled enough of its trades to recognize the appetite. And I'd rather be inside the mouth, watching the teeth, than outside wondering."

He turned that over. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a real one, startled out of him, brief and rusty from disuse. "A mouth. Yes. God, yes, that's exactly—" He shook his head. "I've been writing its meals down in columns and calling it accounting."

"We're very employable, the two of us."

"Deeply."

We sat with that, passing the bottle, and the stairs hummed under us, and I became aware—gradually, the way you become aware of weather—that the humming had changed. My seven steps were still holding their notes, but they'd shifted, subtly, to accommodate two weights instead of one, the pitch settling lower and warmer, the way a chord changes when a second voice joins it. The stairs were holding us both. I hadn't asked them to. They'd heard us talking, maybe, or they'd felt the two of them—his loneliness and mine, sitting close enough to touch—and adjusted themselves around it like a harmony finding its root.

Singers aren't supposed to notice that kind of thing. We're supposed to believe our songs end when we stop singing.

I decided not to mention it.

"What's in the rest of the book?" I asked instead. "The prophecy. After tonight's date."

His face closed like a ledger. "Pages I haven't read yet."

"Will you?"

Another silence. Longer. Down in the archive, something sighed and settled, comforted by company even the company didn't know it was giving.

"I don't know," he said honestly. And then, because honesty is contagious and we'd clearly caught it from the wine: "Would you—if I read them. Would you—"

"Be there?" I handed the bottle back. "Yes. Consider yourself settled, Crane. Acoustically remediated. I'll hum at anything that tries to edit you."

He looked at me then—really looked, mask to mask, the way you can only look at someone when you've both just admitted the masks exist—and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, which from a man built like a drawn bow is practically an embrace.

Above us, faint through the floors, the guild's great clock began striking one.

And beneath the clock, beneath the floors, beneath even the deep archive with its sleeping, grieving trades—so far down I shouldn't have been able to hear it at all, except that hearing is what I *am*—something answered the bell.

One note. Deep. Patient. Held the way you hold a debt.

Rafael didn't hear it. He was saying something about the eleventh step, about whether Bethel Quill's original song could be recovered, and I nodded and made the right noises, because that's my job.

But I kept my hand flat against the stair, feeling my own notes vibrate, and I listened to the thing below listening to us.

It had heard my name, I thought.

Or it had heard my voice—and voices, in this building, are currency.
