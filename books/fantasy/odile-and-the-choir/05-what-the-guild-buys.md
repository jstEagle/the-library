## 5. What the Guild Buys

There is a particular hour in the Ledger Guild when the candles burn low and the arithmetic turns honest. Around two in the morning, the clerks go home to their narrow beds and their narrow dreams, the lamps in the counting-house are trimmed to a miser's glow, and the ledgers lie open like mouths that have finally stopped talking. That is when I do my best reading.

I had told myself I was only being thorough. A good clerk reconciles his columns; an excellent clerk reconciles everyone's. The prophecy book sat locked in my desk drawer—Master Vess had not yet asked for it back, which was its own kind of answer—and every night since I'd found my name in it, I had come down to the archive with a candle stub and a professional excuse, and I had followed the ink.

Ledgers are honest in the way that drunkards are honest: they tell you everything if you ask at the right hour. And the guild's ledgers, I was discovering, had been drunk for a very long time.

* * *

It started with the widow's shadow.

The entry from my first night—the one I'd recorded myself, the widow Hessel trading her shadow for three years of her husband's voice returned from wherever voices go—had a counterpart. Two lines below it, in handwriting so fine it might have been engraved, someone had noted the shadow's onward sale. Not to another buyer. *Upward.* The word appeared twice in the margin, underlined, in that same engraver's hand: **upward. upward.**

In the Ledger Guild we have buyers and sellers and brokers and the occasional thief, but everything moves sideways. Trade is horizontal. It's one of the first things they teach you: value flows between equals or it isn't trade, it's tribute. There is no *upward* on any chart of accounts I was ever shown.

Except there was. Once I started looking, it was everywhere.

A jar of laughter, sold by a dying comedian to a merchant who wanted to remember joy—onward, upward. A storm in a bottle, uncorked over the harbor by some collector with more money than sense—its residue, the wet dark heart of it, logged as received and then marked *upward*. Borrowed years. Sold names. Half the trades that passed through our hands left residue behind, and the residue all flowed the same direction, up through the ledger like water finding a drain, and at the top of every column, where a name should be, there was only a sigil.

I knew the sigil. Every clerk knows it. It's stamped on the inside cover of every master ledger, small and gold-leafed: a crown above an open hand. We're told it's the guild's mark of royal charter, the king's own seal granting us license to traffic in impossibilities. Clerks call it the Open Hand. Children in the city call it the King's Generosity. There are ballads about it. I may have hummed one once, in a previous life, before I learned that ballads are just lies set to a tune you can't argue with.

I sat back in my chair in the empty archive and looked at that little golden crown for a long time.

Then I did what any sensible man does when he finds a hole in the floor of his world. I got a lamp, and I went looking for how deep it went.

* * *

The residue accounts were kept in the Restricted Stacks, which sounds impressive until you learn that the Restricted Stacks are guarded by a lock older than most of the clerks and a superstition older than the lock. The lock yielded to a hairpin. The superstition—that the ghost of Founder Aldous Vane walks the stacks and strikes blind any who enter unworthy—yielded to the observation that half the senior clerks wore spectacles anyway, and none of them had gone blind, merely dull.

Inside, the residue ledgers went back further than I had ever been told the guild existed. The charter histories say two hundred years. The oldest volume here was dated four hundred and eleven years past, and its first entry was already a payment *upward*: nine hundred days of a fisherman's luck, rendered unto the Hand, per contract.

Nine hundred days of luck. I tried to imagine what a fisherman's family ate during those years and decided I didn't want to know. This is the occupational hazard of my profession: you learn to stop imagining the people behind the numbers, because otherwise the numbers scream at you. I had gotten very good at not hearing screaming. I was beginning to suspect that this, too, had been part of somebody's design.

I worked backward through the centuries, building a picture the way you build any picture from ledgers: slowly, line by line, until the shape of the thing emerges and you wish it hadn't. The payments grew. In the early volumes they were annual—a tithe, almost modest, the way a landlord's rent is modest compared to what he could take. Then quarterly. Then monthly. In the last fifty years, weekly. In the last ten—

In the last ten years, daily. Sometimes twice. The entries blurred together, a litany of small thefts: a child's imaginary friend, surrendered by a father who needed debt relief. A soldier's courage, traded for a discharge. A young woman's grief—she sold her grief, do you understand, she paid us to take it away, and we took it and sent it *upward*, and whatever sits at the top of that column has been dining on a grieving woman's sorrow while she walked around light as air and hollow as a bell.

And always, at the bottom of each entry, the counterweight. Because every trade balances; that's the first law. Something given, something received. The sellers got their money, their relief, their borrowed years. And the Hand—the crown above the open hand—received its feedings.

What did *we* receive? I flipped to the guild's own account, the master column, the one that justified two hundred years of charter and four hundred of practice. Our receipts were written in the same engraver's hand, and they were things no coin could buy: continuance. Protection. One entry, dated the year of the great fire, read simply: *the fire turned aside, per agreement.* Another, from a plague year: *the sickness passes over our doors, per agreement.*

We hadn't been sheltering under the crown. We'd been buying protection. Extortion, dressed in gold leaf, running four centuries deep, and every impossible thing that crossed our counters had been scraped for crumbs to keep the arrangement alive.

I closed the ledger. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Inside, something had gone very quiet and very cold, the way a house goes quiet when you realize the noise you've been sleeping through all these years was someone walking around downstairs.

* * *

The trail ended, as trails in the guild always end, at Master Vess.

I want to be fair to him here, because fairness is a kind of discipline and I need disciplines now more than ever. Corvus Vess has been guildmaster for thirty years, and in thirty years he has never once raised his voice, missed a payment, or shorted a clerk's wages. He remembers birthdays. He found me starving off the street—or let me believe he had—and gave me a desk, a candle allowance, and a profession in which invisibility is not a flaw but a qualification. Whatever else is true, and I now know how much else is true, he was kind to me in the specific, unglamorous way that matters to a man who has never trusted kindness.

He also kept the final ledger himself. Of course he did. I'd known that the way you know a house has a cellar: structurally, without thinking about it.

His office sat at the top of the counting-house, above the archive, above the Restricted Stacks, above everything—which, given what I'd learned about direction of flow, should have told me something sooner. Payments went up. Vess was up. The geometry of the building was the geometry of the crime.

I went up on the third night, after the lamps were trimmed, carrying a folio of misfiled entries as my excuse. The door to his office stood open a hand's width. From within came a sound I couldn't place—not voices, exactly. More like the memory of voices. Like standing outside a church after the congregation has gone home and the walls still hold a little of the singing.

I looked through the gap.

Vess stood at his window with his back to me, and he was not alone, though nothing in the room had a body. Before him, hovering at the height of a man's chest, hung a sheet of vellum—no, not vellum. Skin, I realized, with a lurch. Tanned and ruled and bound at one edge, a living page, and across it figures moved of their own accord, tallying themselves, sums crawling like insects toward a total. The singing-memory poured softly out of it.

"Receipt confirmed," Vess said. His voice was level, courteous, the voice he used for closing trades. "This week's remittance. The choir's shortfall has been covered as specified."

The page shivered. Figures rearranged themselves into a question.

"I'm aware of the shortfall," Vess said, and for the first time in thirty years of knowing him—or thinking I knew him—I heard something underneath the courtesy. Not fear. Weariness. The weariness of a man who has carried the same bucket up the same hill for three decades and been told the hill grows longer every year. "The singers die faster than we can find them. You know this. I've told you in every language you accept."

The page flared. The singing sharpened into something with edges.

"Yes," Vess said quietly. "Hunger makes demands. I understand hunger." He paused, and his reflection in the dark window looked, for a moment, like a much older man wearing a well-preserved face. "There is a new asset. A clerk. He has... aptitudes. And a history that touches the matter directly. If the Hand approves, I will bring him up."

My blood did something complicated. Part of it froze. Part of it understood, with horrible clarity, that Vess had known I was following the trail—he had probably watched me do it, the way you watch a mouse run a maze you built—and that *bring him up* meant bring me to the thing at the top, the thing behind the throne, the thing that wore King Aldric like a coat wears a hanger.

The page pulsed once. Assent, or appetite; from outside, they look the same.

Vess turned from the window, and I stepped back into the shadow of the stairwell with my heart going like a debtor fleeing a bailiff. He passed within arm's reach of me. He stopped.

"Crane," he said, without looking at me. "You misfiled nothing tonight. Your folio is blank. I checked."

Silence. The singing-memory faded up through the ceiling, toward the palace on the hill, toward the crown.

"You're a fine clerk," Vess said to the darkness, mildly, terribly. "The best I've had in thirty years. Come to my office tomorrow evening. There is a matter of advancement I'd like to discuss." A pause. "Bring the book you found. Yes—I know about the book. I've known since the day it arrived. It's why you were hired, Rafael."

He said my name. My true name. The one I'd buried under Crane a lifetime ago, the one Mother Yew had stitched into me like a label into a coat collar, the one that was circled in ash-brown ink in a dead collector's prophecy with tonight's date beside it.

He climbed the stairs. Above me, faint as breath, the singing went on rising—upward, upward—toward whatever waited behind the throne with its open hand, patient as interest, hungry as arithmetic.

Tomorrow evening. I had until tomorrow evening to decide whether I was the guild's clerk or its next payment.

I found, examining myself in the dark, that the answer had already been made somewhere below thought, the way a ledger balances itself before the clerk ever picks up his pen.

I was going up. But not the way he meant.
