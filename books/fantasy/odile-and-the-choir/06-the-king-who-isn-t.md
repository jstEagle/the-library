## 6. The King Who Isn't

They send a carriage for me, which is how I know it's serious.

The Ledger Guild does not own carriages. The Ledger Guild owns *access* to carriages, the way it owns access to everything—secondhand, at a discount, with the original owner's name carefully scraped off the door panel. This one still says *House Vellum* under a coat of gray paint, and I spend the whole ride to the palace picking at that name with my thumbnail like a scab, because it's easier than thinking about why they want me.

The summons came an hour after midnight, delivered by a clerk so young his guild badge was still shiny. He wouldn't come inside my room at the choir-house. He stood in the corridor and read from a card in a voice pitched for reciting inventory: *"The Guildmaster requests the presence of Odile of the Ninth Choir at His Majesty's residence, for the settling of a volatile emotional trade."* Then he waited for me to sign for the message, because everything in this city must be signed for, even fear.

I signed. Of course I signed. We all sign.

Here is what nobody tells you about being a choir-singer: you spend your whole life learning that your voice is not yours. It belongs to the cage-song. It belongs to the guild's contracts. It belongs, on nights like this, to whatever rich and frightened person has paid for a professional to *settle* them—and by settle, I mean smooth over, and by smooth over, I mean sing something into their chest that wasn't there before, or sing something out of it that needed to go.

I have settled griefs. I have settled rages. Once I settled a merchant's terror of dying by singing him a small, warm conviction that he would live forever, which was a lie, and which he knew was a lie, and which he paid triple rates for anyway, because a beautiful lie sung well is worth more than ugly truth for free. That's the trade. That's always been the trade.

But no one has ever sent me to the king before.

* * *

The palace at night smells of cold stone and older money. Guards walk me through corridors hung with portraits of Aldric's line—a long gallery of the same jaw repeated down the centuries, each face wearing the family expression, which is patience stretched over appetite. Somewhere around portrait number eleven I realize I'm humming under my breath, just a thread of the cage-song, the way you touch a talisman when you're scared. I stop myself. Bad habit. Worse omen.

The antechamber to the royal bedchamber holds three people: a physician who looks like he'd rather be anywhere, a steward wringing his hands as though trying to wring answers out of them, and Vess.

Vess in daylight is a ledger made flesh—gray, precise, every crease accounted for. Vess at one in the morning, alone in a palace corridor, is something else. He looks tired in a way that isn't sleeplessness. He looks like a man standing near a fire and pretending it isn't hot.

"Odile." He doesn't smile. He never smiles; I once watched him decline a joke the way you decline a dance. "You've been briefed?"

"I've been *summoned*. There's a difference."

"The king suffers from melancholy." He says it flatly, the way you'd say *the king suffers from gout*. "His physicians have exhausted their arts. The court has requested the guild's services. You are our finest settler."

"And if I can't settle him?"

Something moves behind Vess's eyes—not anger, not worry. *Accounting.* "Then we will know that too," he says, "and adjust accordingly."

I should have asked then. Adjust *how*, Vess? Adjust *what*? But the steward was already opening the doors, and there is a momentum to being needed by powerful people that carries you forward whether your feet agree or not.

* * *

King Aldric sits by a window with the shutters closed against the night, and my first thought—the shameful, human first thought—is that he looks smaller than his coinage.

You grow up with a king's face in your pocket. Every copper bears that heavy jaw, that patient brow. You expect the man to be built to the same scale as the money. Instead here is a middle-aged man in a nightshirt, hands folded in his lap like a schoolboy called before the headmaster, and the room around him is immaculate in the way rooms get when no one has been allowed to move anything for weeks. No dust. No disorder. No life.

"Your Majesty," the steward murmurs, "the singer has come."

Aldric looks up. And that's when I hear it.

Not with my ears. With the part of me that hears bindings—that hears the shape of a song inside a thing, the way a locksmith hears the shape of a lock inside a door. Every person has a sound. Most people are chords: messy, warm, several notes arguing pleasantly. Grief is a flattened fifth. Joy is an open octave. I have spent twenty years learning to read people the way Rafael Crane reads ledgers, though neither of us knows yet that we're reading the same book.

King Aldric is not a chord.

King Aldric is two songs played at once, slightly out of time, in the same throat.

The top layer is fine. The top layer is a man's voice saying, "Thank you for coming," in tones of weary courtesy, asking the physicians' questions, submitting to my examination with the patience of someone who has done this many times. The bottom layer—

The bottom layer is *pleading*.

It's very quiet. It's been quiet a long time; you can hear the wear in it, the way you hear wear in a rope that's been holding a weight for years. It doesn't say words, exactly. It says things like *please* and *it's me* and *can anyone hear* in the wordless language underneath language, the one singers use when words would break the spell. And it comes from somewhere deep in the king's chest, deeper than lungs, deeper than the heart, from some place where a self has been folded up small and stored away like winter clothes.

I keep my face pleasant. Twenty years of training. My hands do not shake. Inside, I am doing arithmetic I don't want to finish.

Because I know what a folded-up voice means. I have heard it before—in miniature, in the choir crypt, in the elder singers whose voices were spent binding things. A voice folded away is what happens when something *takes up residence*. When something larger moves into the house of you and puts the original owner in a box in the attic.

"Tell me about the melancholy," I say gently, and begin to hum.

I start low, a settling-melody, something warm and simple designed to ease a troubled mind. It's a lie, technically—a manufactured calm—but it's a kind lie, the kind I've told a hundred times, and I expect it to land the way it always lands: softening the edges of whatever's wrong.

Instead, the king flinches.

Not the top layer. The top layer smiles apologetically and says, "Forgive me, I'm poor company tonight." The *bottom* layer flinches—flinches away from my melody like a man in a cellar flinching from light through a grate. Because my settling-song is a binding-adjacent working, you see. Small, harmless, but it has the same *shape* as a cage. And the thing in the attic of King Aldric heard it and thought: *another wall going up.*

And the pleading voice heard it too, and thought: *help.*

The change is subtle. A tightening around the eyes. The folded voice swells beneath its covering layers, pushing upward, and for three seconds—one, two, three—I hear it almost clearly, almost in words, a drowning man's hand breaking the surface:

*"Don't let them sing me back to sleep."*

Then it's gone. Smoothed under. The king apologizes again for his mood. The physician notes something on a chart. The steward exhales.

And I understand, all at once, sitting in that immaculate room with my hands still raised in the shape of a song, exactly what I have been doing for twenty years.

Every volatile trade I settled. Every grief I smoothed, every rage I sang out of a rich man's chest, every beautiful lie I sold at triple rates. All of it—*all of it*—was practice. Was maintenance. Was the guild keeping its instruments tuned and its techniques sharp for the one performance that matters, the great ongoing settlement that keeps a certain someone in the throne room comfortable and quiet and *asleep*.

I have been a lullaby for a parasite.

The horror arrives slowly, which is worse than fast. Fast horror you can scream at. This seeps. It gets into the spaces between my ribs the way damp gets into stone. I look at the king's patient, courteous face and I see the lid of a box, and behind my eyes I hear Maren's oldest teaching, the one every choir-child learns before they learn any song: *a voice kept is a voice owed.*

Whose voice is being kept in there? Whose debt is this?

* * *

I do the only useful thing I can think of, which is to fail on purpose.

I tell the court my art cannot help His Majesty—that his melancholy is of a nature beyond settling, that perhaps time and prayer—and I watch Vess's face while I say it. He doesn't look disappointed. He looks like a man confirming a figure he'd already tallied. Which tells me something else, something that makes the damp spread further into my bones: Vess *knows*. Whatever wears the crown, Vess has been paying it for years, and tonight was not a request for healing. Tonight was an assessment. They wanted to know if I could hear it.

I could hear it. And now they know I could.

The carriage ride home is long and dark and the scraped-off name on the door panel says nothing to comfort me. I press my palm flat against my own sternum, feeling for my voice the way you check for a purse in a crowd, and I make myself a promise in the wordless language underneath language, the one that binds better than ink:

*I will not be a lullaby. Whatever sleeps in that crown, I will find the song that wakes it—or I will die hoarse trying.*

Somewhere across the sleeping city, in a counting-house full of paper, a clerk named Rafael Crane is balancing accounts he doesn't understand. I met him once, briefly, over stolen wine and a staircase that shouldn't exist. He had the look of a man reading a book that was reading him back.

I find, to my considerable alarm, that I'm praying he reads faster than they do.

Because tomorrow, or the day after, the guild will come for my voice—it's the only instrument left that can reach the thing in the king—and when they do, I intend to be somewhere they don't expect, holding something they can't price.

And the first entry in that something, I suspect, is written in ash-brown ink, in a dead woman's hand, under tonight's date.

Under *his* name.
