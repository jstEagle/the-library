## 7. Entries in a Dead Hand

I did not sleep. This will surprise no one who has ever found their own name in a dead man's book, but at the time I felt the sleeplessness was a personal failing, something a better clerk would have overcome through discipline and perhaps a stiff drink.

Instead I sat at my desk in the guildhall annex with the prophecy book open under a green lamp, and I did what I am best at. I read.

The Collector's name had been Ambrose Fell. He had died in the spring, alone, in a house so full of seized curiosities that the guild's assessors had needed three days to catalogue it. The book had come to me because it was classified as *financial instrument, probable*, and financial instruments were my department. A prophecy, in the Ledger Guild's cold taxonomy, is simply a debt owed by the future to the past. Someone had to record the interest.

The book itself was unremarkable to look at—calfskin gone soft and dark as old bacon, brass corners green with age. The paper inside was another matter. It had the particular creamy resilience of paper made a century ago, before the guild cornered the pulping trade and everything got cheap and grey. And the ink. The ink was ash-brown, the color of burnt hair, and it had a faint smell I couldn't place until nearly dawn, when I finally did and wished I hadn't: yew smoke. The bitter, resinous stink of the hedge-witch's hearth where I had spent four years of my life learning which herbs forgave and which ones kept accounts.

Coincidence, I told myself. Ink was ink. Smoke was smoke. A man who has run as far as I ran learns to explain away smells.

I started at the beginning, because that is where clerks start, and because I wanted very badly for this to be a book about someone else.

*The first debt was a wish*, the opening page read, in a hand that slanted hard to the right, as if the writer were always leaning into a wind only she could feel. *A king did not want to die. Kings rarely do. He went to a woman of the hedges and asked her for forever, and she—being younger then, and prouder, and poorer in wisdom than she would become—told him forever could be bought.*

*It can. I have since learned the price. I write this so that someone, someday, will audit it.*

I turned the page with a finger that was not entirely steady.

*The king paid with his line. Not their lives—the woman was not a murderer, whatever else she was. He paid with their futures, which is a subtler theft and a crueler one. Each heir born to him would arrive owing. The entity he purchased—do not call it a god, it has no divinity in it, only appetite—would wear his crown and eat his years and grow fat on the deferred deaths of his blood. The woman bound it as best she could. A cage-song, sung by singers sworn to the work. The song holds. The song is not forever. Nothing bought is forever; forever is only the installment plan.*

I sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below me, the night clerks were stamping receipts for bottled weather, and the ordinary world went on making its ordinary small trades, unaware that a hundred-year-old woman was explaining the kingdom to me in a dead hand.

*You will want to know who I am*, the next page said. *I am the woman of the hedges. I am the creditor and the debtor both. I bound the thing and I could not kill it, and I have spent every year since trying to find the arithmetic of its unbinding. I will not live to finish. But I have a binding of my own—ink that writes when I am dust, a ledger-entry that outlives the ledger-keeper. Pride again. It seemed useful at the time.*

So the book was writing itself. Or had written itself, a century ago, in advance, by a hand that refused to stop being a hand. I had seen stranger things in the guild's vaults. I had *stamped* stranger things. But I had never before been the subject matter.

I should say here that I had not, at that point, looked for my own name. Some part of me understood that the book was a trapdoor and that my name was the thing waiting underneath, and some deeper, stupider part of me believed that if I read carefully enough, quickly enough, I could get through the whole account without falling in.

I read until the candles drowned in their own wax.

* * *

The middle chapters were history, and I will spare you most of it, though not because it was dull. It was terrible in the way ledgers are terrible: patiently, line by line, with no melodrama at all.

The cage-song had required twelve singers. Mother Yew—she did not call herself that in the book; she called herself *the hedge-woman*, though the hand was hers, I knew it the way you know your own scar—had recruited them from villages that owed her favors. They sang in shifts. They aged. They died. They were replaced by their daughters, and then their daughters' daughters, until the choir became a thing inherited rather than chosen, a debt passed down the female line like a name nobody wanted.

*The guild*, she wrote, in an entry dated some eighty years back, *has grown around the wound like scar tissue. I confess this is partly my doing; I needed clerks to track the payments, and clerks beget clerks, and now there is a building and a seal and a guildmaster with a chain of office, and the clerks believe they are merchants. They are not merchants. They are almoners at a leper's gate, passing food to a thing that should have been starved. Every impossible trade they record—a bottled storm, a borrowed year, a name sold at auction—siphons a little more of the city's slack out of the world, and the slack was the choir's margin. The singers sing the cage shut. The trades pry it open a finger's width at a time. The guildmaster knows. Or half-knows, which is worse, because a man who half-knows can still sleep.*

I thought of Vess's chain of office. I thought of the payment I had witnessed going up, up, past the throne. I thought, with a nausea that was becoming familiar, of the ledger entry in my own handwriting that I had never written.

*And now*, the book said, *the arithmetic.*

Here the hand changed—not the slant, not the ink, but the *pressure*, as though the writer had leaned so hard into her invisible wind that the pen nearly tore the page.

*The entity can be unbound only when every debt it holds is forgiven or repaid at once. All of them. A single outstanding obligation and the cage holds, for a debt is a hook, and the thing is hung upon its hooks. This is why it cultivates debt. This is why it taught men to trade in years and names and laughter. It is not greedy. It is prudent. It is building itself a wall of hooks and calling it a kingdom.*

*Every debt, forgiven at once. You can see the difficulty.*

I could. I was a clerk. I could see it the way you see a cliff edge: precisely, completely, and with the immediate animal understanding that you are standing on it.

*But debts can be tracked. And I have tracked them. All of them. A hundred years of them, in this book, because a thing that lives on bookkeeping can be killed by better bookkeeping, and I am, whatever my other sins, the better bookkeeper.*

Then, on the next page, the tone shifted. The old fury drained out of it and something else came in, something that made the hair on my arms stand up, and I will tell you honestly that I almost closed the book there. Almost. A clerk reads to the end of the entry. It is the first thing you learn and the last thing you keep.

*And there is one debt I have not yet entered, because I have been a coward about it for ninety years, and this book deserves better than my cowardice.*

*The first payment the entity ever received from the guild was not a storm in a bottle. It was not a year or a shadow. It was an apprentice. A boy who was himself the collateral on a bargain struck in a hedge-witch's kitchen—the boy's service sold against his mistress's own outstanding debt, the one she took on the night she made the king's wish possible and could not unmake it. The guild did not know what it was accepting. It thought it was hiring a clerk.*

*The boy ran to the guild because he believed he was escaping her. He was not escaping. He was being delivered. The running was part of the design—hers, the entity's, the ledger's, I no longer know whose. I only know that I watched it happen from a century away, through this ink, and I could not stop it, and I have hated myself for it in every tense the language allows.*

*His name is Rafael Crane.*

I did not move for a long time. The lamp hissed. My heart did something arrhythmic and unprofessional.

There it was. Not my name in the abstract, not a name like mine—*my* name, in ash-brown ink, written by a dead woman who had been my mistress, who had been old and crooked and sharp-tongued and secretly a hundred years deep in the kingdom's oldest wound, and who had sold me.

Or saved me. The book was not clear on which, and I suspect she had not been either.

* * *

I turned the page. I want it noted, for the record, that my hands were shaking and I turned the page anyway. Whatever else the guild made of me, it made me thorough.

The entries after that one were different. They were dated—real dates, future dates, a century of them, marching down the margin like soldiers. And my name was on every one.

Not prominently. That was the worst of it. Not *RAFAEL CRANE SHALL* in letters of doom. Just my name, in the corner of each entry, the way a clerk signs a page he has verified. *R. Crane, examined.* *R. Crane, present.* *R. Crane, witness to the payment.* *R. Crane, burns the ledgers.*

I stopped on that one. Read it three times. *Burns the ledgers.* No date given, which in the guild's grammar meant *soon*, or meant *she didn't know*, or meant *she didn't dare write it down*, and I sat there in the green lamplight understanding that a dead woman I had fled ten years ago had already recorded the rest of my life, and that the rest of my life appeared to involve arson.

I laughed. I will admit it. A short, ugly laugh, the kind you hear from clerks at three in the morning, alone, in rooms full of paper. Because this was the punchline, wasn't it? I had run from her to the one place in the world where nothing was free, and the whole time my freedom had been an entry in her book, itemized, dated, and filed. I had traded a hedge-witch's kitchen for a counting-house, and both of them turned out to be the same room.

Fate, I thought, is just bookkeeping you haven't audited yet.

I turned more pages. My name, my name, my name. *R. Crane, forgives.* *R. Crane, repays.* *R. Crane, sings.* That one made no sense at all—I had a voice like a door hinge, and I said so out loud to the empty room, and the empty room declined to correct me.

Near the end of the book, the entries thinned. The dates grew closer to tonight. And on the final written page—the last one with ink on it, the rest of the book blank and waiting, patient as a tax season—there was a single line, and beneath it a space where an entry should have been, and the space was empty.

The line read: *Tonight, he reads this.*

I looked at tonight's date. I looked at the empty space beneath it. And I understood, with the particular vertigo of a man who has just realized the floor is a page, that the book was waiting for me to fill it in. That somewhere, a century ago, a woman had bound her hand to keep writing until the very moment her hand and my eyes arrived at the same place, and that from here forward, the ledger and the life were the same document.

There was a pen on my desk. There was ink in the well—ordinary black guild ink, not ash-brown, but ink is ink, I had told myself that already, hadn't I, and the book had heard me.

I did not pick up the pen. I am not proud of what I did instead, but I will report it accurately, because accurate reporting is the one virtue they beat into me that I intend to keep. I closed the book. I wrapped it in my own cloak, which is against regulation, and I carried it out of the annex past the sleeping night-clerks, which is theft, and I took it down through the counting-house, which is treason, or would be, if the guild ever admitted that a book could matter more than a balance.

I was taking it to the only person I knew who could hear a cage-song and tell me whether it was still holding. A choir-singer. A woman named Odile, who hummed staircases into existence and drank stolen wine like a person with nothing left to lose, and who had looked at me across a bottle two nights ago and said, *You've got the face of a man whose own handwriting is lying to him.*

I had laughed then. I was not laughing now.

The book was heavy under my cloak. It weighed no more than any book its size. It weighed exactly as much as the rest of my life, which I was, it seemed, already living—every step toward the crypt another line filling itself in, my boots writing the entry my hand refused to.

Behind me, in the empty annex, the green lamp hissed over the blank final page, waiting for ink.

Ahead, down the stairs, into the dark under the city, I went to find out whether a song could still hold a cage—and whether a dead woman's arithmetic had left me any line in it that was truly mine.
