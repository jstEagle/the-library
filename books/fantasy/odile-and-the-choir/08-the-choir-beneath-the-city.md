## 8. The Choir Beneath the City

*Odile*

There is a door in the wine cellar that isn't a door.

I don't mean it's hidden, exactly, though it is that too—tucked behind a rack of the guild's truly appalling cooking sherry, which I have always suspected was placed there deliberately, as a deterrent. No one steals bad wine. I mean the door itself is wrong. It has no hinges on this side. It opens inward onto nothing, and when you put your ear against it, you hear breathing. Not one person's breathing. Many. Layered, like a tide made of lungs.

Rafael had been following me for three corridors by the time I noticed, which was flattering and also annoying, because I had been trying to decide whether to show him at all.

"You walk like a clerk," I told him over my shoulder. "All elbows and apology."

"I walk like someone who doesn't want to be heard," he said.

"Then you're doing it badly."

He caught up to me at the sherry rack and looked at the door the way clerks look at things—like it might have an entry in a ledger somewhere, some line item reading *one (1) impossible door, amortized over forty years*. He reached out and touched the wood, and I felt him flinch through his fingertips before I saw it in his face.

"It's warm," he said.

"Singing does that," I said. "To walls. To doors. To people, if they stand too close for too long." I set my palm flat beside his. "Come meet them."

I should explain what I am, since Rafael clearly wasn't going to ask and would instead spend the whole descent constructing theories, which is his way of being brave. The guild employs me as a settler. When a trade goes volatile—a bottled storm that wants out of its glass, a borrowed year that remembers its owner—I sing it calm. That's the job description they gave me, written in their tidy hand: *settles volatile assets vocally.* As though I were a tuning fork with a salary.

What I actually do is hold things still with my voice. And the reason I can do that—the reason any of us in the choir can—is because we've been practicing our whole lives on something much larger and much worse.

The door opened inward, as it only ever does, as if the room beyond were inhaling it open. The stairs went down farther than stairs should. Forty steps, then sixty, then a landing where the candlelight did something strange—not dimming but *thinning*, as if the dark down there were drinking it slowly and politely.

"The choir," I said, "is beneath the city. Has been for longer than the guild has kept its ledgers. Longer than the guild, probably. The guild just inherited us, the way you inherit a roof leak."

Rafael said nothing. Clerks don't, on staircases. They count.

* * *

*Rafael*

She counted the steps wrong, I'm pretty sure. Or she counted them right and lied about it, which in Odile's case amounted to the same gesture of intimacy. She said sixty. I made it seventy-three. I know because I was counting the way I count everything now, compulsively, the way a man who found his own handwriting in a stranger's ledger counts everything afterward—looking for the entry that explains him.

At the bottom of the stairs, the corridor opened into a crypt.

Not the dramatic kind. No bones artfully arranged, no sarcophagi. Just a long low vault of old black stone, ribbed like the inside of a throat, and along its length—cots. Blankets. A stove. A shelf of candles burned down to puddles. And people. Eleven of them, maybe twelve; I lost track twice, because several of them barely seemed to be there. Old women wrapped in shawls. A man about my age with his eyes closed and his mouth slightly open, producing, without any apparent effort, a single sustained note so low I felt it in my teeth rather than heard it. Two children, asleep, and even asleep they were humming. Not a tune. A *function.*

"What is this place?" I asked, and immediately hated myself for asking, because the answer was already in the air. You could feel it. The whole crypt was under pressure, like the inside of a held breath.

Odile crouched by the stove and lit a fresh candle from an old one, and her face in that light was different than it was upstairs. Softer around the eyes and harder around the mouth, both at once, which I was learning was simply her face.

"This is the cage," she said.

I looked around for bars.

"There aren't any," she said, watching me look. "That's rather the point. Bars are for things you can see." She stood and walked to the center of the vault, where the floor was worn into a shallow depression, smooth as a river stone, as if generations of feet had stood in one place for a very long time. "Underneath us—directly underneath this spot, if you care about your geometry—is something the old books call the Tenant. The guild calls it an asset. The court calls it the king's blessing. Everyone calls it whatever lets them sleep."

"And you call it?"

"A tenant," she said, "because it pays no rent and will not leave." She laughed, briefly, and the laugh came out strange, bent at the edges, like a bell with a crack in it. "We sing it its cage-song. Continuously. For centuries. There used to be hundreds of us. Then dozens. Now—" she counted the cots with a sweep of her hand, and the number she arrived at visibly frightened her, "—now eleven who can still manage the full verse. Twelve, counting me, and I'm the newest and the least trained, so really eleven and a half."

"Why are there fewer?"

"Because it eats us," she said, plainly, the way you'd tell someone the stove burns wood. "Slowly. A voice at a time. We come here young and we leave here—" she gestured at the eldest singer, a tiny woman asleep in a chair with her mouth still moving, shaping notes like a dream of notes, "—empty. Maren sang lead for fifty years. She can't whisper above a breath now. But she won't stop coming down. None of us stop."

I stood in the middle of a crypt beneath the Ledger Guild, listening to twelve people hum a prison into existence around something I couldn't see, and I thought—and I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the one trade I never learned properly—I thought: *this is not my problem.*

That was my first thought. My second thought, arriving about four seconds later and considerably less comfortable, was: *whose problem is it, then? Who feeds it?*

And then I thought about the ledgers. About the entries. About the jar of laughter purchased Tuesday last, and the widow's shadow bought at a fair price, and the thirty years sold by a farmer who'd needed grain seed more than he'd needed his future. All of it flowing, entry by entry, upward. Everything the guild recorded flowed upward. I had assumed it flowed to Vess. Vess was a man. Men have ceilings above them.

Odile watched me arrive at it. She was patient about it, the way you're patient with someone walking into cold water.

"Say it," she said.

"The trades," I said. "They're not income. They're *provisions.*"

She nodded once.

"The guild buys up everything people can spare—their shadows, their laughter, their years, their names—and it carries all of it down here, past my door, past the sherry nobody drinks, and it *feeds* it to the thing under the floor. That's why the trades never balance. That's why the master ledger has pages even Vess doesn't read. It's not bookkeeping. It's a menu."

"Eleven singers," Odile said quietly. "When my mother joined, there were ninety. The guild's volume of trade has tripled in that time. You do the arithmetic—you're better at it than I am."

I did the arithmetic. The arithmetic was obscene. Every year the choir shrank, the guild traded more, and the entity below grew correspondingly harder to hold—which meant the choir had to spend more voice per verse, which meant it shrank faster. It wasn't a decline. It was a curve. And curves like that don't drift anywhere by accident. Someone was steering.

"Vess knows," I said. Not asking. I could hear myself becoming certain, and certainty in me had recently become a symptom.

"Vess *administers,*" Odile said. "Whether he knows what he administers is almost beside the point. The guild has been feeding it since before Vess was born. Since before his grandfather. The guild *is* the feeding mechanism. Everything else—the auctions, the appraisals, the beautiful leather spines, all that lovely copperplate—it's packaging." She picked up the candle and carried it along the row of cots, and I followed, because I had stopped trusting myself to stand still near the center of the room. "Look at them. Really look."

I did. An old man whose lips moved around a note I couldn't hear, holding it, apparently, forever. A girl of perhaps fifteen, awake, braiding another girl's hair while she sang, because after enough years down here you learn to do two things at once or you go mad from doing one. Their faces were calm. That was the worst part. They weren't suffering, most of them, not visibly. They were *employed.* They had blankets and soup and each other and a purpose, and the purpose was to be slowly spent, note by note, year by year, in a hole under a counting-house, for a kingdom that didn't know they existed.

"I was recruited at nine," Odile said. "A woman from the guild heard me singing in a fish market and told my mother I had a gift that shouldn't be wasted. Which was true. She just didn't say what the gift was *for.*" Her voice did something complicated on the word *for*—flattened, then sharpened, like a blade being tested. "My mother took the signing fee and cried about it for a decade, and I have never decided whether to forgive her, because she was poor and the fee fed four children through two winters, and mercy is easy when it's not your winter."

"That's how they got me too," I said, before I could stop myself.

She turned. In the candlelight her expression was not surprise. It was interest, the focused kind, the kind that takes notes.

"Mother Yew," I said. "My mistress. Hedge-witch, out past the fen villages. She took me as an apprentice when I was eleven. Fed me, taught me letters, taught me ink. And then one day I found the contract she'd signed over me—my name in it, my *true* name, the one I don't use—and the counterparty's signature wasn't a person's. It was a seal. The guild's seal. And I ran. I ran to the guild, which sounds mad until you understand that the guild was the only place in the world with a rule about names, and the rule was *we keep them safe.* I hid inside the thing I was running from. I've been hiding for six years, balancing books, telling myself I was invisible."

"And now you've found your own handwriting in entries you never wrote," she said, "and tonight's date circled in a dead man's prophecy, and your true name printed underneath like a lot number."

"When you say it all together like that," I said, "it sounds less like a coincidence and more like a delivery schedule."

"Yes," said Odile. "It does."

* * *

*Odile*

Here is what I knew about Rafael Crane before tonight: he was the night clerk with the careful hand who never spoke to anyone, who drank exactly one cup of the guild's terrible tea and winced at it every time as though hoping it would improve, who once spent forty minutes re-inking a single marginal correction because the original line offended him. I knew he was afraid of something. Everyone in the guild is afraid of something; fear is practically a benefit of employment. But his fear had architecture. It had load-bearing walls.

Now I knew what the walls were built around. A witch. A contract. A name. And a boy who had run straight into the jaws of the thing chasing him because the jaws had a sign on them reading *sanctuary.*

You could pity that. I mostly did. But I'll confess to something less generous too: I recognized it. I had done the same thing, in my way. Given a gift, been told it was a gift, and then sung in a crypt for years without once asking the question whose answer was standing right next to me holding a candle—*what happens to the singers when the song ends?* Because the song doesn't end. That's the design. We are not employees of the choir. We are consumables of it, and I had known, in the place where you know things, since I was perhaps thirteen.

So when he said *delivery schedule,* I didn't correct him. I let him sit in it. People need a moment to sit in the shape of their own situation before they can do anything about it, and Rafael struck me as a man who had spent six years refusing to sit in anything.

Maren woke while we talked. Of course she did; the entity below shifts when it's discussed, everyone's voice tightens half a tone without meaning to, and Maren slept the way lighthouse keepers sleep—lightly, and only between emergencies. She looked at Rafael across the crypt with eyes gone pale as skimmed milk and said, in a voice like paper:

"Who's this?"

"A clerk," I said.

"Ah," said Maren. "One of the *keepers of the accounts.*" There was no anger in it. That was somehow worse. There was the tired patience of a woman who had watched clerks come down these stairs before, usually once, usually never again. "Well. Come and look at us properly, then, boy. We're what your ledgers cost."

Rafael went. I'll give him that—he went, when a hundred sensible men would have invented a reason to be elsewhere. He stood at the foot of Maren's chair and listened to her breathe her broken note, and I watched his face do the thing faces do when arithmetic stops being numbers and becomes grandmothers. His jaw tightened. His hands, those precise, inky, careful hands, curled slightly, as if around a pen that wasn't there yet.

"How long," he asked her, "have you been singing?"

"Fifty-one years," Maren said. "I came down at nineteen with a voice that could crack a wineglass at forty paces, and I thought I'd been chosen. Chosen!" She laughed, and the laugh was mostly air. "Child, we're not chosen. We're *stocked.*"

"The guild's trades," Rafael said, and his voice had changed register entirely, dropped its clerkly polish, gone rough. "The years, the shadows, the names. Where do they physically go? After they're entered. After they're shelved."

"Down," Maren said.

"All of them?"

"All that matters." She closed her eyes. "A shadow is an appetite. A name is a handle. A year is strength. The Tenant eats appetite and grows hungrier, grips handles and reaches farther, drinks strength and stands taller inside the king. Your ledgers aren't records, boy. They're *rations.*"

I watched Rafael take that in, and I want to record precisely what happened in his face, because I think it was the moment the chapter of his life turned. First there was horror—that came fast, the ordinary human kind. But underneath the horror, and visible through it, the way a flame shows through paper, there was something else. Something almost like relief. Terrible relief. The relief of a man who has spent years suspecting the books are lying and finally, finally hears the books confess.

"That's why the entries don't reconcile," he said. "I thought I was bad at my job. I stayed late every night for six years trying to find my own error." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "There was no error. The imbalance *is* the transaction."

"Now you have it," Maren said kindly.

"And the choir shrinks because—" He stopped. Started again. "It's starving you to feed it. The guild starves its own cage-keepers to fatten the prisoner. That's not negligence. That's *management.*"

"That," I said, "is why I brought you down here."

He looked at me. I looked at him. Between us, in the candlelight, in the low thrumming dark, something passed that I don't have a guild-approved term for. An alliance, I suppose, if you wanted to enter it in a ledger. But ledgers record debts, and this wasn't a debt. Nobody owed anybody. We had simply both been eaten by the same thing, from opposite ends—one of us through his name, one of us through her voice—and had crawled, separately, into the same cellar, and discovered the cellar had a floor, and the floor had a Tenant, and the Tenant had a menu, and we were both on it.

"We're going to need more than anger," I said. "Anger sings badly. Believe me, I've tried."

"I don't sing at all," Rafael said.

"Not yet," said Maren, opening one milk-pale eye, and there was mischief in it, ancient and undimmed. "But you will, boy. Anyone who can hear a false entry in a ledger can hear a false note in a song. It's the same ear. The guild trained it in you by accident, thinking it was teaching you to copy figures."

Rafael looked from Maren to me and back again, and I saw him begin—slowly, reluctantly, the way he did everything—to consider the possibility that he had not been hiding in the guild at all. That he had been *apprenticing.* That six years of ink and imbalance had been training his ear for exactly this crypt, exactly this fight, exactly this night.

"I need to see the prophecy book again," he said abruptly. "Tonight. There's a cipher in the margins I skipped because it looked like decoration. Mother Yew decorated nothing in her life."

"Tomorrow," I started to say, and then stopped, because tomorrow was a word the guild owned. Tomorrow was when they scheduled the auctions and the payments and the slow meals served to the thing beneath us. Tomorrow belonged to them.

"Tonight, then," I said. "But Rafael—if the cipher says what I think it might say, if it gives us a way to end this—understand what ending it costs. The choir pays first. The choir always pays first. Ask me how I know."

"How do you know?" he asked, gently, because he was capable of gentleness in the gaps between his terrors.

"Because I can feel it leaning on my voice already," I said. "Every verse I sing downstairs, I get back a little less. One day I'll sing the last note I have, and I know exactly which note it will be, because Maren sang it before me, and her mother-figure sang it before her. It's the same note every time. The choir ends on the same note it began with. We're a circle being erased from the top."

The candles guttered, all of them at once, in air that hadn't moved. Below us—below the worn stone, below the roots of the city—something shifted its weight, the way a large animal shifts in a stall that has become too small for it. The sleeping singers stirred. The humming faltered for half a heartbeat, caught, held.

Rafael's hand found my sleeve. Not gripping. Resting. A clerk's touch, light as a marginal note.

"Show me the cipher," he said. "And then show me everything else they've hidden. I'm done reconciling their lies. I want the original entries."

Above us, faint through seventy-three steps of stone, the guild clock began to strike midnight, and I thought: *good. Let the day end. Whatever comes next, let it come tonight, while the candles are still lit and the choir is still singing and there are eleven and a half voices left to spend.*

"Bring the book," I said. "And bring your real name. Whichever one survives this, we'd best learn to use it honestly."
