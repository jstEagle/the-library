## 9. Vess Makes an Offer

The guildmaster's office smelled of cedar and old money, which is to say it smelled of cedar and lies that had been kept long enough to develop a patina.

I had been summoned—there was no other word for it—at half past midnight, an hour when honest clerks were asleep and dishonest ones were pretending to be. The summons came by way of a junior archivist named Pell, a boy whose entire personality was a nervous swallow, who appeared at my desk in the counting-house and said, "The master will see you now," in the tone of a man delivering a diagnosis.

I went. What choice did I have? I had spent the evening in the crypt beneath the city learning that my employer was a feeding trough for something wearing the king, and I had come back up into the lamplight of the Ledger Guild carrying a century-old prophecy book against my chest like a stolen loaf. Odile had wanted me to take it straight to Maren, down where the cage-song was still holding, but I'd argued—clerk's instinct—that the book was safest where no one would think to look for it: on my person, in the one place in Vess's kingdom where I was considered furniture.

Furniture gets summoned at half past midnight when it stops behaving like furniture.

* * *

Vess received me standing at his window, hands clasped behind his back, watching the city burn its small midwinter fires against the cold. He was not what strangers expected. People imagined a guildmaster of impossible trades as some kind of sorcerer-tyrant, all rings and menace. In truth Master Aldous Vess looked like what he had been before anything else: a senior clerk. Grey at the temples, ink-stained at the second knuckle of his right hand, spectacles pushed up into thinning hair. He had the posture of a man who had spent forty years proving that arithmetic could be a form of violence.

"Crane," he said, without turning. "Do you know how long you've been with us?"

"Six years, master. As of the spring reckoning."

"Six years." He nodded slowly, as though confirming a figure against a ledger only he could see. "And in six years you have never once asked for advancement. Never petitioned for a better desk. Never joined the others when they complain about the hours—which are, I grant, obscene." Now he turned, and the lamplight caught his spectacles and turned his eyes into two blank coins. "Do you know why that is?"

Because I came here to disappear, I did not say. Because a man running from a hedge-witch learns that ambition is a lantern held up to your own face. "I assumed contentment was not a disciplinary matter," I said instead.

Vess smiled. It was a good smile. It had been maintained the way one maintains a fine instrument—oiled, tuned, deployed only at moments of maximum effect. "Contentment," he repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. That is precisely the quality we prize in you, Crane. Contentment. Discretion. A man who balances the books and asks nothing about what the numbers mean."

He crossed to his desk—a vast thing of black oak, older than the guild charter—and poured two glasses of amber liquor from a decanter that I recognized, because I had catalogued it myself three years ago, as Lot 114: *Borrowed Courage, vintage unspecified, effects temporary.* He handed me one.

"I don't drink on duty," I said.

"You're off duty as of this conversation. One way or another." He drank his own without ceremony. "Sit down, Crane."

I sat. The prophecy book pressed against my ribs under my coat, and I became acutely aware of it, the way one becomes aware of a heartbeat only when something threatens to stop it.

"Do you know," Vess said, settling across from me, "what your file says? Your intake assessment, from six years ago. It's a document I wrote personally, which is unusual—I don't assess apprentices; that's what I pay assessors for. But you interested me. A young man arrives with no surname he'll speak, no references, and handwriting so precise it looks typeset. Do you remember what I wrote?"

"No, master."

"'Subject demonstrates exceptional aptitude for transcription and an equally exceptional desire not to be perceived. Recommend placement in deep cataloguing, where invisibility is an asset rather than a liability.'" He set down his glass. "I have never once regretted that recommendation. You have been the finest clerk this guild has produced in my tenure. Perhaps in its history. And tonight, Crane, I find myself needing to ask you a question I should have asked six years ago."

Here it comes, I thought. The word *prophecy* or the word *book*, and then either the cells or the window, whichever is more efficient.

"What do you want?" Vess asked.

Of all the questions in the world, that was the one I had armored against least. "Master?"

"It is not a rhetorical question, and it is not a trap—or rather, it is a trap, because every question in this building is a trap, but it is one I am asking with the safety catch on." He leaned forward. "You have spent six years wanting nothing, and I have found that men who want nothing are either saints or fugitives, and you, Crane, hum too much for a saint. So. What do you want? Name it. Tonight, for the first time in the history of this guild, the answer will be granted."

The candle between us guttered and steadied. Somewhere below us—seventy-three steps below, in a crypt where eleven and a half voices were spending themselves note by note to keep a monster caged—the choir sang on, and neither of us mentioned it, and the silence around that omission was the loudest thing in the room.

What did I want?

I wanted Mother Yew's voice out of my head. I wanted the entry in the prophecy book—*Rafael Crane, true name inscribed, this date*—to be someone else's handwriting. I wanted the last six years to have been refuge rather than arrangement. I wanted, with a suddenness that nearly showed on my face, to be the man Vess believed he had hired: content, discreet, invisible, safe.

That was the trap, of course. He wasn't offering me things. He was offering me the version of myself I'd been performing since the night I ran.

"I want to understand my own entries," I said carefully. "There are transactions in the ledgers bearing my hand that I never made. I would like them explained."

Vess studied me for a long moment. Then he laughed—not unkindly, which was worse. "There it is. The real question underneath the clerk's question. Yes. I know about the entries. I authorized them."

My stomach performed a maneuver I can only describe as archival. "You forged my hand."

"I *borrowed* it. There's a difference, and the difference is documented." He rose, went to a cabinet behind his desk, and returned with a slim ledger bound in grey calfskin—my grey calfskin, the binding style used only for clerks' personal working ledgers. He laid it open before me.

It was mine. Every page. Six years of my own hand, including entries I had no memory of making: disbursements to the palace kitchens, transfers marked only with a symbol I now recognized from the crypt—a closed circle bisected by a line, the mark of the thing behind the throne. Payments upward. Years of them. All written in my perfect, typeset hand.

"How?" My voice came out very level, which took work.

"A binding, Crane. A simple one, embarrassingly cheap at auction—you'd be astonished what a scribe's obedience fetches when the seller doesn't know what they're selling. Your mistress sold a great deal of you before you ever arrived here. Did you really think she let you go out of sentiment?" He tapped the page. "When the entity requires a payment recorded in a trusted hand, yours records it. You sleep through it. Most nights you don't even lose the hour. You've been our most reliable instrument, and you never knew, and that, Crane, is the highest compliment this guild can pay a clerk."

I thought of Odile's face in the candlelit crypt. *Two lonely people recognizing each other's masks.* Well. Mine had just been removed by the man who'd fitted it.

"And if I refuse to keep being an instrument?"

Vess sat back down. And here he changed—not dramatically, not into a monster, but into something quieter and far more frightening: sincere.

"Then I make you the offer I came here to make," he said, "and I'll ask you to hear all of it before you decide anything, because I suspect you've already decided, and decisions made in advance are how clever men die stupidly."

* * *

"The guild has stood for four hundred years," Vess began. "Longer than the crown's current shape, longer than most of the city. Do you know why? Not power—we have remarkably little, and we like it that way. Not wealth, though there's wealth. We endure because we are *necessary*. Every kingdom needs a place where the impossible can change hands somewhere out of sight, or else the impossible changes hands in the street. We are the gutter that keeps the flood off the cobblestones. Bottled storms, borrowed years, names at auction—all of it has to go *somewhere*, Crane, and the alternative to us is not nothing. The alternative is chaos with teeth."

He refilled his glass. His hand, I noticed, was not entirely steady. Interesting. Fear, in a man like Vess, was data.

"The thing behind the throne predates the guild. Predates King Aldric's grandfather's grandfather. We did not summon it. We did not bind it. We inherited it, the way one inherits a mortgage, and for four hundred years the guild's true function—the function beneath the function—has been *payment administration*. We keep it fed on schedule, in agreed quantities, through agreed channels, and in exchange it keeps to its agreement. No rampages. No famines of the soul. No kings waking up hollow. You have lived your whole life in the peace it sells, Crane, and you have never once paid retail."

"Someone pays," I said. "The choir pays."

Something flickered across Vess's face—there and gone, fast, but I had spent six years reading micro-expressions in trade negotiations, and I catalogued it: guilt, briefly, then dismissal of guilt, then irritation at having felt it. "The choir is an older arrangement," he said. "Not ours. We maintain the terms we inherited. If you want someone to blame for the choir, blame the king who made the original bargain—a hundred years dead and conveniently beyond complaint."

"Maren says the payments have grown."

"Maren says many things." He waved this off. "Yes, the appetite increases. Appetites do. Which brings me, at last, to the point."

He opened a drawer and set something on the desk between us. A contract. Heavy paper, wax seals I didn't recognize from any guild inventory—which meant they predated the current charter, which meant they were old, which meant they were serious.

"Partnership," Vess said. "Full partnership. Not senior clerk, not deputy—partner. A seat at the table where the terms are negotiated. Access to every ledger, every vault, every secret this institution holds, including the ones I've shown you tonight. And—" he paused, and I understood this pause had been rehearsed, "—the dissolution of every binding currently held against your name. The scribe's obedience. Whatever else your mistress sold. All of it, voided, by people whose entire profession is voiding things cleanly. You would walk out of this room owning yourself for the first time since you were a boy."

The room tilted, very slightly. I gripped the arm of the chair.

I want to tell you I saw through it instantly. I want to report that my heart said *no* with the clean clarity of a struck bell. But I'm a clerk, and clerks know that the devil's contracts are never the ones with obvious claws—they're the ones with genuinely excellent terms. Partnership. Self-ownership. An end to waking with hours missing and my hand full of someone else's arithmetic. Everything I had fled Mother Yew to find, offered freely, by the institution that had been quietly wearing me like a glove.

"And the price," I said. My voice sounded distant to me, as if transcribed by someone else. "Contracts this generous have a consideration clause. What am I paying?"

"The prophecy book," Vess said simply. "The one in your coat."

I did not move. I am proud of that, even now. "You knew I had it."

"Crane, I have known where that book was since the collector died. I let it sit in seizure storage for a month because I hoped it was a fake—it's happened before, prophets are a growth industry. Then your friend the singer started asking questions, and then you started asking questions, and then the crypt started losing singers faster than schedule, and I stopped hoping." He folded his hands. "The book is dangerous not because it predicts—it doesn't; prophecy is just bookkeeping done in reverse—but because of what it *itemizes*. Debts. Dates. Names. If certain parties read certain pages, agreements that have held for a century come up for renegotiation, and I cannot survive a renegotiation. Neither, frankly, can you. Or the city. Or the choir, such as it is."

"You'd give it to it. To the thing behind the throne."

"I'd give it to *nobody*," Vess said sharply, and for one moment the clerk-mask slipped and I saw the actual man underneath: tired, old, afraid, doing sums that kept coming out wrong. "I would put it in the deepest vault we have and let it rot, and the world would continue, imperfectly, the way it always has. That is the whole of my politics, Crane. Imperfect continuation. You've read the histories—every age that tried to settle all accounts at once ended in fire. Debts are how civilization breathes. Forgiveness is a fantasy for people who've never balanced a column."

He slid the contract toward me. The wax seals gleamed.

"Sign, and by morning you are free, partnered, and the most powerful clerk in the history of the guild. Refuse, and..." He shrugged, and the shrug was worse than any threat, because it contained no threat at all. Just arithmetic. "And you remain what you are. An instrument. With an instrument's lifespan."

* * *

I looked at the contract for a long time.

I will not pretend the temptation was small. There is a version of me—call him the man I might have become if Mother Yew had never found me starving in a hedgerow—who signed that page before Vess finished speaking. That version sleeps through the night. That version owns his own hand. That version has a title and a door with his name on it and colleagues who call him *master* and mean it.

But here is what six years of balancing impossible ledgers teaches you, the lesson underneath the lesson: **you can audit anything except the party offering you the discount.** When the terms are too good, the question is never *can I afford this?* The question is *why do they need me to say yes tonight?*

"Why tonight?" I asked aloud. "If the book is so containable, why summon me at half past midnight and offer me everything? What's changed?"

Vess's composure held, but barely. I watched the exact moment the answer arrived in his eyes ahead of his mouth.

"She's coming," he said quietly. "Your mistress. Mother Yew. She crossed the river at dusk. My watchers say she's walking up the Processional Road as we speak, and she is coming here, Crane, for her ledger—for the record of everything she sold of you—and God help us both, she is the only creditor in this city that thing behind the throne has ever feared."

Outside the window, far off along the dark spine of the Processional Road, a light was moving toward the guild. Small. Unhurried. The way a lamp moves when it is being carried by someone who has walked a very long way and intends to arrive exactly when she pleases.

I stood up, leaving the contract unsigned on the desk between us, and Vess watched me go without calling the guards, because we both understood that the negotiation hadn't failed.

It had merely been interrupted by the senior partner.
