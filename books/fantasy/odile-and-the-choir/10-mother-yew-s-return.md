## 10. Mother Yew's Return

I should have known she'd come the way weather comes—announced by nothing, felt in the bones first.

It was the third bell past midnight when the knock arrived at my door in the clerks' quarters: three raps, a pause, then one more. Nobody knocks like that. Nobody knocks at all, really, in the Ledger Guild; people are *summoned*, or they are *processed*. A knock is a courtesy, and courtesies died somewhere between the second and third floors of this building decades ago.

I opened the door with a candle stub in one hand and a letter-opener in the other, which tells you everything about how the last fortnight had gone.

She stood in the corridor wearing a traveling cloak the color of wet bark, hood back, silver hair cropped short as a boy's. She looked older than I remembered and younger than she had any right to be. That was the problem with Mother Yew. She had been old when I was nine years old, and she had been exactly that same old for the eleven years since I ran, and now here she stood looking perhaps five years further along instead of thirty.

"Hello, Rafe," she said. "You've let your handwriting get sloppy."

Every clever thing I had ever planned to say to her—the accusations, rehearsed on sleepless nights, the questions, the one speech where I finally told her what her bargain had cost me—all of it went out of my head like smoke through a cracked window. What came out instead was:

"You're supposed to be dead."

"Everyone's supposed to be something." She looked past me into the room, cataloguing it the way she used to catalogue my herb racks—approving nothing, forgiving less. "May I come in, or do you intend to make an old woman stand in a corridor owned by people who buy shadows?"

"They don't buy shadows," I said automatically. "They *lease* them. The widow gets them back at solstice."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile. "You always did correct the ledger before you corrected yourself. Let me in, Rafe."

I stepped aside. It was, I would think later, the second-worst decision of my life, and the first had been made eleven years ago on a night much like this one, when I climbed out her window with nothing but my name and my fear.

* * *

She took the chair. Of course she took the chair. In her cottage there had only ever been one chair worth sitting in, and I had spent my childhood perched on stools and windowsills and stacks of firewood while she held court from that single seat, dispensing wisdom and chores in equal measure. Some habits outlive their furniture.

"I brought tea," she said, producing a paper packet from her cloak and setting it on my desk beside the prophecy book, which I had covered with a spare shirt like a man hiding a corpse under laundry. Her eyes touched the lump of it, weighed it, moved on. She was letting me believe she hadn't noticed. That was its own kind of message.

"There's no kettle."

"There's no *time*, but we manage." She nodded at the little iron pot I kept for ink. "Clean it. Water's fine."

And because I was nine years old again in every way that mattered, I cleaned the pot and boiled water over my candle flame like a beggar and made tea that tasted faintly of oak gall and regret. She drank it without complaint, which from Mother Yew was a standing ovation.

"You look well," she said. "The city suits you. All these straight lines and locked doors. You were always happiest inside a rule."

"I was happiest *invisible*."

"No." She set down the cup. "You were safest invisible. Those aren't the same thing, and you've had eleven years to confuse them, so I'll forgive you the error once."

The fury arrived then, right on schedule, the way it always did when she started talking in that tone—patient, precise, each sentence a small surgical instrument. Eleven years of it came up my throat at once.

"*Forgive me?*" My voice cracked on the second word, which was humiliating, so I leaned into the crack and let it break all the way open. "You sold me. Do you understand that? You sat me down at your table with bread and honey—you gave me *honey*, you knew exactly what you were doing—and you told me my apprenticeship was a debt, and then you signed that debt over to the Guild as payment for—" I stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and I had spent two weeks trying very hard to keep it theoretical. "For whatever it is behind the throne. You fed me to it. First payment. That was me. I was the *first installment*."

"Yes," she said.

Not *no*. Not *you misunderstand*. Not any of the dozen defenses I had drafted for her in the dark. Just *yes*, flat as a ledger line, and it knocked the wind out of me worse than any lie would have.

"Why?" I asked, and hated how small it sounded.

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the city murmured its midnight murmur—carts, drunkards, the distant clatter of the auction hall being prepared below us. Somewhere in this building, Vess was awake. Vess was always awake. I tried not to think about that.

"Because the alternative was worse," she said finally. "And because you wouldn't have understood the worse, and I didn't have time to teach you. So I taught you bookkeeping instead, and sent you where the books were."

"That's not an answer. That's a *summary*."

"It's the only answer I have left, Rafe. The long version died with everyone who could have told it." She reached into her cloak again, and my hand went to the letter-opener before my brain caught up, and she saw it, and something in her face went tired and old in the honest way, the way she only ever looked when she thought no one was watching. She drew out not a weapon but a bundle of papers, tied with string gone soft as cloth. "Do you know how old I am?"

"Older than you should be. The prophecy book is a hundred years old and it's in your hand."

"Mm. And?"

"And I haven't worked out how yet. Binding, presumably. Something you never broke."

"Something I *couldn't* break." She untied the string with fingers that trembled slightly—I had never seen her hands tremble; hers were the steadiest hands in three counties, the hands that pulled thorns and set bones—and spread the papers on my desk. They were letters. Drafts, mostly, crossed out and rewritten, in her hand, in several different inks spanning what looked like decades. "A binding doesn't stop time, Rafe. It stops *payment*. I owed something enormous, and rather than pay it, I froze the account. But frozen accounts accrue interest. Every year I didn't die, the debt got larger, and every year the thing holding the note got closer to collecting in ways I couldn't control."

"The entity."

"The entity." She said the word the way you'd say a neighbor's name—familiar, distasteful. "It has had many names. Aldric's ancestors called it the Quiet Partner. The choir calls it the Listener. The Guild, in its more honest ledgers—which is to say none—calls it the Senior Creditor." She looked up at me. "I called it my landlord, once, in a moment of weakness, and it laughed. It has a terrible laugh, Rafe. Like a page turning."

I sat down on my bed because my legs had opinions of their own. "The prophecy book. You wrote it. A century ago."

"A hundred and six years ago, if we're being precise, and we're always being precise, you and I." She tapped the covered shape of the book through my shirt. "It isn't a prophecy. Prophecies are for people who can't do arithmetic. It's a *schedule*. A repayment plan, written far enough ahead that the thing on the other end would sign it, because it loves nothing more than a schedule. Dates. Amounts. Names."

"My name is in it. Under tonight's date."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because tonight is when the account comes due, and you are the last entry I control." She pushed the drafts toward me. "Read them. Read what I tried. I wrote that book to build you a road, Rafe—a narrow one, ugly, with walls on both sides—but a road that ends somewhere other than its mouth. Every date in it, every entry, every name, I placed so that when you finally read it, you'd be standing exactly where you needed to stand to burn the whole rotten ledger down."

I wanted to believe her. That was the terrible thing, the thing I turned over and over like a coin with two identical faces: I *wanted* to believe her. Eleven years of anger, and one hour of her sitting in my room drinking bad tea, and some starved part of me rolled over like a dog hearing its name.

But I was a clerk of the Ledger Guild. I had been trained by professionals to know a forged entry when I saw one.

"You wrote a road," I said slowly. "With walls on both sides. You wrote me running away. You wrote the bargain. You wrote the honey." My voice came out level, which surprised me. "Mother Yew, you wrote my whole life as a *repayment structure*, and you're presenting it as rescue."

"Yes."

"How is that different from what Vess does?"

The question landed. I watched it land—watched her flinch, actually flinch, this woman who had set my broken arm when I was ten without so much as changing expression. For a moment she looked every year of her impossible age, and then some.

"It isn't," she said quietly. "That's the truth of it, and I won't dress it up for you, because dressing things up is how I lost you the first time. I used you. I have been using you since before you could write your own name—I put the love of ledgers in you deliberately, did you know that? Gave you sums to do instead of songs. Built you into an instrument. And then I aimed you." She folded her hands. "The difference—the only difference—is that Vess feeds the thing to keep his position, and I built you to kill it. One of us is feeding the fire and one of us is throwing water, and we both used the same bucket."

"And I'm the bucket."

"You're my apprentice," she said. "You were always going to be the bucket. That's what apprentices are. I'm sorry. I have been sorry for a hundred and six years, which is longer than most people manage to be anything."

* * *

We sat with that for a while. The candle burned down. Neither of us trimmed it. There is a particular silence that exists only between people who have hurt each other competently and thoroughly, and we sat in it together like two patients sharing a waiting room.

"Odile," I said at last. "The singer. Was she in your book too?"

Something moved behind her eyes. "Yes."

"What date?"

"Does it matter?"

"*What date, Mother Yew.*"

"The nineteenth," she said. "Four days from now. The entry reads: *the voice is spent where the song must end.*" She met my eyes steadily. "I wrote it a century ago and I have spent a hundred years trying to find a way to strike it out, and I couldn't, because a schedule is a schedule, and the thing countersigned it. Some entries can be renegotiated. Some can't. Hers—" She stopped. Started again. "Hers is load-bearing, Rafe. The whole final verse depends on it."

My chest did something complicated and painful. Four days. I had known Odile for a matter of weeks—had shared stolen wine with her on a staircase that shouldn't exist, had watched her hum a cage-song in a crypt full of dying singers—and apparently a woman dead a century had already penciled in the cost.

"You said you wrote it to save me," I said. "Did you write it to save her?"

"I wrote it to save everyone I could," she said, "and I have spent a hundred years learning that 'everyone I could' is a smaller number than 'everyone,' and the difference is paid for by someone, always, and the arithmetic never forgives." She rose, stiffly, cloak whispering. "Which brings me to why I'm here, and why you should be afraid, because I only travel when the numbers have gone wrong."

"The numbers have gone wrong."

"Vess has the book."

I stood up so fast the stool went over. "He—he can't. It's been in my possession—"

"He has a copy. Or he believes he does. Either way, he has an entry in your hand authorizing its surrender, dated tomorrow, witnessed by a guild seal you've never seen." She smiled without warmth. "Your handwriting, Rafe. It really has gotten sloppy. Or someone has gotten very good at forging it, which amounts to the same thing in a court of signatures."

"That's not possible. My hand is—"

"Yours. I know. I taught it to you stroke by stroke, and I could forge it in my sleep, and so could anyone I ever taught, and Rafe—" she paused at the door, hood rising, and the candlelight caught her face one last time, old and young and unbearably fond, "—I taught someone else too. Years before you. Someone who didn't run. Someone who stayed, and learned everything, and is currently sitting three floors above you with a copy of my book and a warrant with your name on it."

The floor tilted. Or I did. "Who?"

"You already know who," she said gently. "You've just been refusing to say it, the way you refuse to correct yourself before you correct the ledger." She pulled the hood up. "Her name is Maren, Rafe. The eldest of the choir. And tomorrow at dawn, she signs the order that takes the book, takes Yew—" she tapped her own chest, "—and takes you. Which means we have until dawn to decide whose side the arithmetic lands on."

She knocked three times on the inside of the door, out of habit or ceremony or farewell, and was gone into the corridor, and I stood alone in my room with the candle guttering and the prophecy book under its shirt, thinking about buckets, and water, and fire, and how many hours remained until dawn.

Then I picked up the letter-opener, wrapped the book in the shirt properly this time, and went to find a staircase that shouldn't exist.
