## 11. The Auction of Names

The invitation arrived folded inside a hymnal, which was the guild's idea of a joke.

*You are cordially requested to attend the Midnight Auction of the Ledger Guild, as guest and — should occasion arise — as lot.*

"Should occasion arise." I'd read that phrase four times before the meaning settled into my stomach like a swallowed stone. They weren't even pretending anymore. The guild had employed me for six years to *settle* volatile trades — to sing a quarreling jar of storms calm, to hum a haunted mirror back to sleep — and in all that time I had believed myself furniture. Useful furniture, well-paid furniture, but furniture. Now they were inviting me to my own sale with the courtesy of a dinner party, and I understood that I had never been staff at all. I had been inventory on a very long leash.

I could have run. Let's be honest about that, since honesty is the one thing nobody bids on. I could have taken the hymnal, walked out of the choir-house before dusk, and been three counties away by morning, singing under some new name in some small church where the worst thing anyone would ask of my voice was a psalm.

But Rafael Crane had stood in the crypt beneath the city three nights ago, holding a candle against the dark where Maren and her dwindling singers kept their cage-song, and he had said, in the flat voice of a man reading his own death warrant: *"The trades have been starving them. Every year the ledgers grow and the choir shrinks. It's arithmetic."*

And then he had looked at me — at me, Odile, who has spent her whole life making sure no one looks too closely — and said nothing at all, which from Rafael is the loudest sentence there is.

So I stayed. And I dressed for the auction the way you dress for a funeral you intend to interrupt.

* * *

The counting-house by night is a different animal than by day. By day it smells of ink and tallow and the particular dust of paper that has been handled by too many anxious hands. By night, when the lamps are turned low and the great ledger-hall is arranged with its rows of chairs facing the auctioneer's dais, it smells of something older. Something like a cellar where wine has gone wrong. The singers call it *the hunger-smell*. We joke about it because joking is cheaper than screaming.

Maren had insisted on coming, wrapped in a shawl, leaning on my arm, though she can barely walk and the cage-song requires her presence below every third hour. "If they're going to sell your voice," she'd rasped, "someone in that room should know what a voice actually weighs."

Rafael met us at the door. He was wearing his clerk's grey, buttoned to the throat, and he had the look of a man who has spent the afternoon burning correspondence and regrets none of it. There was ash on his cuff. I chose not to mention it. Some things are better left as gifts between friends.

"You came," he said.

"You doubted it?"

"I hoped it. Doubted is what I do instead of praying."

"That's not praying, that's just bookkeeping with extra steps."

Almost, he smiled. In the hunger-smell of that hall, with the chairs filling up behind us — clerks in their greys, merchants in their silks, and at the far end, seated alone in a box draped in royal blue, the entity's agents wearing faces that didn't quite agree with their eyes — almost smiling felt like an act of rebellion worthy of the ledger itself.

We took seats near the back. Maren sat between us like a wedge driven into fate, which I suspect was her intention. She squeezed my hand once, hard, and whispered: "Whatever happens up there, don't sing unless you choose the song."

I have carried stranger advice into worse rooms.

* * *

The auctioneer was Vess himself.

I had seen the guildmaster perhaps a dozen times in six years, always at a distance, always surrounded by clerks moving around him like water around a stone. Up close he was smaller than rumor made him, a neat dry man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles he did not need — I've sung for enough liars to know when glasses are costume. His voice, when he opened the proceedings, was pleasant in the way of a well-kept ledger: balanced, legible, utterly without mercy.

"Ladies and gentlemen of the trade," he said, "tonight we offer eleven lots. As ever, provenance is guaranteed. Payment may be rendered in coin, in kind, or in consideration." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Our honored patron attends remotely, as is customary. Its bids will be entered through the blue box. Let us begin."

Eleven lots. A bottled storm from the Sundering Coast. Thirty years borrowed from a farmer who would die at forty and never know why his hair went white so early. A name — an actual name, spoken aloud only as *Lot Seven*, belonging to some debtor whose family would forget him by Friday. Each item was brought forward on a cushion of black velvet by a clerk whose face had the careful blankness of someone paid not to grieve.

And the blue box bid on everything. Every lot, without hesitation, without ceiling. Coin meant nothing to it. Years meant nothing. Names meant nothing except as weight, as ballast, as food. I watched Lot Seven's name vanish into that box and felt the hall get colder, the way a room does when something large inhales.

Beside me, Rafael had gone very still. Stillness is his native language; I've learned to read its dialects. This stillness said: *soon.*

Then Vess said, "Lot Twelve."

There was no Lot Twelve on the printed list. I checked. The clerk beside the dais checked. Vess smiled his balanced smile and produced a single card from his breast pocket, and read:

"One voice. Soprano. Trained in the settling arts, capable of unbinding song. Provenance: the Choir Beneath the City. Condition: excellent. Reserve: none." He looked directly at me over his unnecessary spectacles. "Miss Amberdale, if you would stand, so our patrons may appraise the goods."

The hall turned. Dozens of faces, and behind them the blue box, and behind the blue box — I felt it the way you feel weather change — something vast shifting its attention toward me like a whale turning in deep water.

Here is what nobody tells you about being appraised: your body wants to perform. Six years of training, of singing on command, of making myself useful and therefore safe — all of it rose in my throat at once, offering itself up, *look how well I work, look how valuable, please don't.* My hands actually began to rise toward the position of song.

Maren's grip on my wrist stopped them. Her whisper was barely breath: "*Choose the song.*"

So I stood. And I did not sing. I stood there in my funeral-best with my mouth shut and let them look, and I discovered that silence, held on purpose, is the loudest thing I own.

Vess opened the bidding at two hundred crowns.

The blue box answered before the echo died.

* * *

What followed took perhaps four minutes and lasted approximately a decade.

A silk merchant bid three hundred. The box took it. A gem-trader whose face I knew from court functions bid five hundred with the enthusiasm of a man spending money he owed elsewhere. The box took it. Vess's gavel ticked like a clock running down, and between each bid the hall grew colder and the hunger-smell thickened until I could taste it, old and sweet and patient, and underneath my fear another feeling was assembling itself, one I recognized only dimly at first because I had never been permitted to feel it before.

It was anger. Clean, specific anger. Not at the box, exactly — the box was only appetite, and you don't blame a mouth for eating. At the room. At the gem-trader raising his hand to buy my voice while checking his reflection in a silver case. At Vess reading my life off a card. At six years of furniture-hood. At every hymnal I'd ever been handed instead of a choice.

Somewhere in that rising heat I noticed Rafael was no longer beside me.

I noticed this the way you notice a missing stair: too late, and with your stomach already falling.

He was walking down the aisle. Clerk-grey, ash on his cuff, hands empty, spine straight, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man delivering a ledger entry he has already signed. The hall quieted around him the way water quiets around a sinking thing. Vess watched him come with his head tilted, curious, a collector observing an item walk off its own shelf.

"Master Crane," Vess said pleasantly. "The floor is for bidders. Are you bidding?"

"No," Rafael said, and stopped at the foot of the dais, and turned to face the whole hall, and faced the blue box last, longest, the way you face the largest dog in the yard. "I'm paying."

He reached into his coat and drew out a slip of paper. Small. Ordinary. The kind of scrap a clerk carries by the hundred. But when he held it up, the lamplight caught it strangely, and every singer in that room — Maren's hand crushing mine, myself, and somewhere far below us, the dwindling remnant at their candles — felt it in the teeth.

"I, Rafael Crane," he read, and his voice did not shake, which I will admire until I die, "being the true holder of my own name, free of debt, lien, or claim — my apprenticeship having been repaid in full by the forgiveness of Mother Yew, deceased — do hereby enter my name as payment. For Lot Twelve. Final bid."

Silence. The particular silence of a room doing arithmetic.

Then Vess laughed. It was a small sound, dry as turned pages. "Crane. Your name isn't worth two hundred crowns. It's worth nothing. You're a runaway apprentice with a forged history and ash on your sleeve. The house cannot accept—"

"The house," Rafael said, "accepts what clears its books. That's the whole religion, isn't it? Debts in, debts out. Check your master ledger, guildmaster. Check tonight's page. I think you'll find my name is the only currency in this room that isn't already owed to something else." He paused, and here his voice dropped into something quieter and infinitely more dangerous. "Or would you rather open the ledger and explain to everyone present why it isn't?"

I have sung in front of kings. I have sung storms to sleep. I have never heard anything in any hall as frightening as a clerk threatening to show his work.

Vess's smile stayed fixed but his eyes went to the blue box, and for one naked instant I saw the transaction behind them — the calculation of a man realizing his patron's appetite might be satisfied tonight by something other than me, and weighing whether he could afford the loss.

The blue box considered.

I felt it consider. A pressure across the whole hall, vast and slow, tasting the offered name the way a connoisseur tastes wine it intends to swallow the cellar of. And I understood, with a singer's ear for structure, what Rafael had done: he had made himself the better purchase. A clean name, freshly freed, unencumbered, *bound publicly to the fight* — to me, to the choir, to the whole doomed arithmetic of resistance. He had hung a sign around his neck saying *eat here first*, and he had done it in front of forty witnesses so that no back-room erasure could undo it.

No more hiding. He'd told me once, in the crypt, that hiding was the only prayer he knew. Apparently he'd learned a new one.

The blue box bid again — a number, entered in silver script that appeared on the card in Vess's hand, absurd, astronomical, a sum with more zeroes than the guild had ever moved in a single night.

Rafael didn't flinch. "Counter: everything I am. Which, unlike your figure, is not borrowed."

The gavel fell.

I swear the sound it made was different from every other gavel-strike of the night — lower, final, like a door closing in a house where all the other doors have already closed. Vess announced the sale in his balanced voice: *Lot Twelve, sold, together with the counter-lot, one name, to be held in escrow until settlement.* Settlement, I understood, meant the rite. Meant the throne room. Meant whatever came next, now arriving whether we were ready or not.

The hall exhaled. The gem-trader ordered wine. The world resumed its appetite for less interesting things.

Rafael walked back up the aisle and sat down beside me, and his hands, I saw, were shaking now that no one was watching — fine tremors, like a plucked string settling.

"You absolute idiot," I whispered.

"Yes."

"They could have just taken both. They could have—"

"They could have. But now it's public. Forty witnesses, a signed entry, a gavel-fall. Vess lives by the ledger; let him try to forge his way past his own congregation." He looked straight ahead, at the dais, at the blue box already darkening as its attention withdrew. "Besides. If I'm going to be eaten by an immortal parasite, I'd rather it happened on purpose."

And there it was — the thing I'd been circling for weeks, the thing I'd refused to name because naming things gives them power and I had spent my whole life giving my power away. He hadn't bought my voice. He'd married his fate to mine, in front of God and the Ledger Guild, with a gavel for a ring.

"Maren," I said, because my voice needed somewhere to go that wasn't tears, "your elder is asleep on my shoulder and the entity just spent a king's ransom on a clerk. Is this what winning feels like?"

From beneath the shawl, muffled and ancient and amused: "No, child. Winning feels quieter. This is what *choosing* feels like. Learn the difference; you'll need it soon."

Above us, Vess struck the lamp out on the dais, and the blue box emptied, and somewhere in the dark of the hall a clerk's pen scratched the night's entries into the master ledger — including, I would learn within the hour, one line written in a hand that was not Rafael's, authorizing seizure of a certain prophecy book, *on the authority of Master Crane's own signature.*

But that discovery belonged to tomorrow. Tonight we walked out of the counting-house into the cold, three conspirators and a stolen future, and above the city the stars were doing their ancient indifferent bookkeeping, and Rafael said quietly, to no one, to me:

"Now we move. Before they read the fine print."
