## 12. Interlude: The Cage-Song

I was born in the year the plum trees failed, which my mother always said explained my disposition. She sang in the choir then—second voice from the left, the one that carries grief—and she named me Maren because it was her grandmother's name and because names, in our family, are not given but handed down like debts.

She was right about the debt part. She was wrong about nearly everything else.

You will want me to begin with the entity, I suppose. Everyone does. The young singers come to me in the crypt with their candles guttering and their questions sharp as awls: *What is it? Where did it come from? Why us?* And I tell them what I was told when I sat where they sit, ninety years ago now, with my knees knocking against cold stone and my mother's name still warm in my mouth.

But the truth is you cannot understand the thing behind the throne until you understand the man who invited it. And you cannot understand him until you understand what it is to want something so badly that you would let your children's children pay for it.

So we will begin with a king. They usually do.

* * *

His name was Aldric too. That is the first cruelty of this story—the kingdom has been recycling its kings the way a poor housewife recycles her candle stubs, melting one into the next until you can no longer say where one ends and the other begins. Aldric the Third, they called him in the histories. The Beloved. The Winter King. He reigned two hundred years before any of us were born, and he reigned well, by all accounts, which makes what came after neither better nor worse. Virtue is no armor against wanting. Sometimes I think it makes the wanting worse, because a good man believes his desires must be good too.

He had everything a king could count. Land, gold, peace, a queen who loved him, children who thrived. And he lay awake at night counting it all anyway, because that is what kings do—they audit their own happiness and find the ledger short.

What he feared was the last page. The entry marked *end*. He had watched his father die badly, gasping and afraid, a whole kingdom's worth of power unable to purchase one more hour. And Aldric the Third looked at that ending and thought, as powerful men have always thought: *not for me. Not if I am clever enough. Not if I pay enough.*

There was a guild even then. Smaller, humbler—a back room where clever people traded things that ought not be traded. Bottled weather. Borrowed luck. A widow's second sorrow, sold to a merchant who wanted to know grief in advance. The guild has always told itself it merely keeps accounts, that it does not make the trades, only records them. This is the lie every clerk tells himself at night, and I confess it is a comfortable lie. I have envied it.

The king came to them with a wish. And the guild, being clever, found something willing to grant it.

* * *

Here is where I must be careful, because what happened next is not a story with a villain in it, or not only. It is a story about arithmetic.

Something answered the guild's inquiry. What it was before—whether it was ever a god, or a demon, or simply a hunger that learned to speak—I do not know, and neither does anyone living. The oldest singers called it the Creditor, which I have always thought was honest of them. It did not demand souls, despite what the fire-side versions say. Souls are crude currency. It wanted futures. Time owed rather than time lived. Promises made against days not yet come.

And oh, a king has such collateral.

Aldric signed. Of course he signed. The contract was beautiful—I have seen a copy of it, preserved in wax beneath the crypt, and even now, knowing everything, I felt my fingers itch toward it. That is the thing no one warns you about evil done properly: it is gorgeous. Every clause balanced, every term fair-seeming, the whole document singing with symmetry. The king would live. He would live a long time, long past the natural span, long enough to see his grandchildren's grandchildren, and the price was—

Well. You have guessed the price. His descendants' futures, paid out in installments, generation upon generation, each child born owing a portion of their years to the Creditor's account. The king read that clause. I am quite sure he read it. But there is a trick the mind plays when the alternative is dying, a sleight of hand performed on oneself: *it will be different for mine. I will find a way around it. I am the exception.*

No one is the exception. That is the first lesson of the ledger and the last lesson of life.

His children began to fade—not die, nothing so clean. They dimmed. They grew forgetful, then translucent, then gone, like ink left in rain. And the king, who could not die, sat in his brightening palace and buried his line one washed-out ghost at a time, and discovered that immortality purchased this way is just dying very slowly while everyone else finishes first.

By the third generation, someone understood what the contract truly was. Not a gift. A feeding arrangement. The Creditor had not granted a wish; it had installed itself. So long as the debt ran, it had a hold on the royal blood, and through the royal blood, the throne, and through the throne, the kingdom. It wore the crown the way a hermit crab wears a shell. Perhaps it wears the king too—I will come to that, and to what Odile heard inside him, though that is her story to tell and I only borrow it here.

The guild kept the books. The guild always keeps the books. And somewhere in those centuries, the keeping became the feeding, and the clerks stopped asking what the entries were *for*.

* * *

Now. The choir.

You cannot unmake a contract; this is doctrine older than the guild. What is signed is signed, and the Creditor holds the original in whatever place it keeps its things. But a binding—any binding—is also a kind of song. Everything that holds is humming, if you listen closely. Chains hum. Walls hum. Promises hum most of all.

A woman discovered this. Her name has been worn away, which I used to think was tragedy and now think may have been mercy. She was a singer at court in the fourth generation, when the royal children were fading like lamps running dry, and she noticed that when she sang—certain notes, certain intervals—the fading slowed. Held off. Like frost retreating an inch from a window where your breath has warmed the glass.

She could not break the contract. But she could *hold* it. Wrap the binding in sound the way you wrap a cracked jar in wool, and carry it, and carry it, and carry it.

That is the choir. That is all the choir has ever been: women and men carrying a cracked jar through the dark, singing so it does not shatter, passing it hand to hand down two hundred years. We call it the cage-song. It is not one melody but a structure—voices laid over voices, each holding a clause of the contract in suspension, the whole edifice sustained by breath and attention and the willingness to give your life's music to a task that will never finish.

We dwindle. I should say that plainly, since everyone else says it in whispers. When I joined, we were forty-one voices. My mother's generation numbered sixty. Now we are eleven, and after the rite that failed—after what took Sefa and Bram and little Ilse, whose voice hadn't even settled yet—we are eight. The guild's trades siphon what feeds us. Every borrowed year sold in that marble counting-house above our heads is a year skimmed from the pool the choir draws on. We sing the cage shut, and the guild quietly unbuckles it a notch at a time and sells the slack.

They do not know they do it. That is the terrible grace note of it. Vess signs his tidy columns and thinks himself a steward of impossible commerce, and underneath his feet, eight old and tired people starve musically so that his ledgers stay balanced. I do not hate him. Hatred requires believing someone chose, and Vess chose ignorance the way most men choose it—gradually, comfortably, one willful blindness at a time.

* * *

Let me tell you what the cage-song costs, since no one else will say it aloud.

It is not that we cannot stop singing. Anyone can stop. It is that the song must never *gap*. There must always be a voice on every note of the structure, day and night, sleeping and waking, and so we learn to sing in our sleep, to sing through fever, to sing while we bury each other. My mother held her line through her own final illness, three weeks of pneumonia, humming the interval in her hospital bed while the nurses thought her delirious. Her last words to me were not goodbye. They were *you're flat, love, lift it a quarter-tone.* Then she died mid-phrase, and I slid into her place before the echo faded, because that is how it works. That is how it has always worked.

Two hundred years of mothers dying into daughters' mouths. If that sounds like devotion to you, you have not heard it. It sounds like drowning in relays.

And yet—and here is the thing I have never managed to make bitter, though I have tried—I do not regret it. There is a moment, deep in the structure, when all the voices lock and you feel the contract strain against its own weight, and you understand that you are the only reason a kingdom sleeps safely tonight. No one thanks you. No one knows your name. The plum trees fail and the winters come and the guild grows fat overhead, and you sing anyway, because the alternative is a thing wearing a crown forever, and some debts you pay not because they are fair but because stopping would be worse.

My mother believed that. I believe that. I am trying, these last few years, to believe something larger—that the song could *end*, properly end, not collapse but conclude, resolved like a chord finding its root. But hope is expensive at my age, and I hoard it like a miser.

* * *

Which brings me to the two of them. The clerk and the singer. Because I promised myself I would not gossip, and I am about to gossip.

Odile brought Rafael down into the crypt three nights ago, past the wax-sealed contracts and the bone-niches of dead singers, and stood him in front of the cage-song while we sang the midnight watch. I watched him listen. I have watched a hundred people listen to the cage-song—guildmasters come to inspect their investment, priests come to argue theology, once, memorably, a tax assessor—but Rafael Crane listened the way a man listens to his own handwriting read aloud. Like something he'd written without knowing it.

And Odile stood beside him with her hands folded and her chin up, the way she does when she is terrified and refuses to spend the fear, and I thought: *ah. There it is.* Two instruments, tuned to the same broken key. I am old enough to recognize it when I see it, and old enough to know better than to say so, and I said it anyway, afterward, to her alone: "Be careful, girl. People who recognize each other's cages tend to try opening them."

She said, "Isn't that the point?"

"The point," I told her, "is that some doors open onto freedom and some open onto the thing that was waiting patiently on the other side the whole time. And you don't get to know which until the hinge swings."

She didn't like that answer. The young never do. But she is thinking about it—I can tell, because she has started asking questions with edges on them. What exactly does the contract require? Who holds the original? Can a debt be forgiven rather than merely carried?

Forgiven. She used that word, and I confess it went through me like a needle through cloth. Because there is a clause, an old one, half-forgotten even among us elders, that Mother Yew knew about—I recognized the hedge-witch's cipher-work in the margins of the prophecy book the moment Odile described it, because Yew studied under my grandmother's generation, back when the choir still trained outsiders. A clause about simultaneous settlement. About every debt coming due at once. About what happens if, in a single breath, everything owed is either repaid or released.

If that clause is true—if it can be triggered—then the cage-song is not a life sentence. It is a bridge, and bridges are meant to be crossed, not inhabited.

Eight of us left. Eight voices to hold the structure while whatever comes next is attempted. When I joined there were forty-one, and we said *we cannot possibly manage with fewer*, and then there were thirty, and we said it again, and again, and here we are, still saying it, still singing.

Perhaps eight is enough. Perhaps eight plus a clerk whose name appears in a century-old prophecy, plus a singer whose voice can unmake bindings, plus a king buried alive inside his own crown—perhaps that arithmetic works. I have spent my whole life learning that the ledger balances in ways no clerk expects.

Last night, after the midnight watch, Odile stayed behind in the crypt. She stood where the light from the grate falls at that hour, a thin blade of moon on the stone, and she hummed—not the cage-song, something of her own, small and private—and the walls of the crypt *leaned in*. I heard it. Two hundred years I have sung in this room, and I heard the stone attend.

And far above us, through six floors of marble and ledger-paper, something shifted in its sleep. The candles guttered in sequence, north to south, like a held breath moving through the building.

It heard her too.

Whatever comes, it is coming soon now. The song is thinning, the hunters are circling, and the guild's auction is in three nights, and I am an old woman with a hoarded scrap of hope and eight voices and a cracked jar I have carried since I was nine years old.

But when Odile finished humming, she turned to me in the dark and asked the question I have waited ninety years for someone to ask:

"Maren. Teach me the whole song. All of it. Before it's too late to matter."

So I lit the candles, all of them, and I opened my mouth, and I began—at the beginning, with the king, with the wish, with the first note of the first debt—

Because if this is to be the last verse, then by all the plum trees that ever failed, we will sing it *in tune*.
