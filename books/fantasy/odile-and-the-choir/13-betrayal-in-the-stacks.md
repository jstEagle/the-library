## 13. Betrayal in the Stacks

I should have known the guild would come for the book. I had spent six years learning how institutions think, and the first lesson of institutional thinking is this: nothing is ever destroyed, only transferred to a more convenient owner.

But knowing a thing and feeling it are different trades entirely.

I was in the counting-house at dusk, doing what I did best—being furniture. Vess liked me visible in the evenings now. He'd been liking me visible ever since the auction, since I stood up in front of three hundred clerks and bid my own name against a thing that wears a king like a coat. He hadn't said a word about it. That was the worst part. Vess's silence had weight, and I could feel it accumulating on my shoulders like wet snow, entry by entry, waiting for the day it would be called in.

The prophecy book sat in my satchel under the desk, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a heartbeat. Mother Yew's cipher filled its margins—her century-old hand marching alongside her modern one, the arithmetic of salvation half-solved. Every debt forgiven or repaid, all at once, all together. Including mine. I'd copied out the cipher twice, hidden one copy in the choir crypt beneath Odile's sleeping pallet, and still I kept the original close, the way you keep a wound close. Because it was one. Because it was hers.

Because she was here. In the city. In my life again after eleven years of running, sitting somewhere above me in the guild cells with salt on the floor and paper over the windows, arrested on charges I still couldn't make sense of.

That was the other thing I should have known. You don't arrest a hedge-witch for owning a book. You arrest her for what the book proves.

"Crane."

Vess stood at the end of my row of desks. He does that—arrives without arriving, the way fog arrives. He's a narrow man, Vess, narrow the way a blade is narrow, all edge and no waste. His cuffs were immaculate. His eyes were the color of ledger ink, which is to say they reflected whatever he looked at without holding any of it.

"Guildmaster." I rose. Six years of practice made the motion smooth. Six years of practice also taught me that smoothness is a kind of confession; only guilty men move that carefully.

"You've been diligent lately," he said. "Working late. Working early. I admire diligence in a junior clerk. It suggests ambition." He let the word sit there. Ambition, in the Ledger Guild, is both a compliment and an accusation, depending on which way the wind of the next audit blows.

"I have a great deal to catalog," I said. "The seized collections alone—"

"Yes. The seized collections." He smiled. It was a small smile, precise as a decimal point. "Speaking of which. There's been an irregularity discovered in your intake records. A volume entered into evidence from the Harrowgate estate—the collector who died in the spring. A book of prophecies. Do you know it?"

My heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as a clerk's heart trying to become a bird. "It was catalogued," I said. "Condition: poor. Binding: water-damaged. Content: apocryphal nonsense. I flagged it for the incinerator."

"Did you." Vess walked the length of my desk, unhurried, reading the room the way he read everything—as a document with errors in it. "Then perhaps you can explain why the incineration log shows no such entry. Why the book appears nowhere in the destruction schedule. And why—" he produced a folio from inside his coat, and my stomach dropped through the floor and into the crypt below, where Odile was probably singing softly to herself, unaware that the world was ending two floors up— "why we have *this*."

He opened the folio on my desk.

It was a page. A single page, guild stock, the good rag paper with the watermark of crossed quills. And on it, in handwriting I knew better than my own face—in handwriting that *was* my own face, every downstroke, every impatient little hook at the end of a numeral—was an entry.

*Received into custody, this date: one (1) volume, prophetic, provenance Harrowgate estate. Disposition withheld by clerk R. Crane pending personal study. Clerk acknowledges the volume contains material relevant to the security of the Guild and has elected to conceal said material for reasons of private advantage.*

Signed: Rafael Crane.

Dated three days ago.

Three days ago I had been in the crypt. Three days ago I had been copying cipher by candlelight while Odile hummed the cage-song low, keeping the walls of the world stitched together, and I had not touched a pen except to copy her a shopping list. I remembered the shopping list. Bread, tallow, the good honey from the stall by the fountain because she liked it and never bought it for herself. My hand had written *honey* three days ago. My hand had not written treason.

"This isn't mine," I said.

"No?" Vess tilted his head. "Look closely, Crane. Look very closely. Whose hand is that?"

And that was the trap within the trap, wasn't it. Because it was mine. Utterly, perfectly mine. The slant of the capitals. The way my sevens crossed too high when I was writing fast. The little ink-blot where a left-handed man drags his sleeve. Whoever had forged it knew my hand the way a lover knows a face in the dark—and there are only two ways someone learns a man's handwriting that well. Years of watching him work. Or years of teaching him.

"It's a forgery," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and careful, the voice I used for difficult accounts. "A good one. But the ink is wrong. Guild iron-gall darkens over days—it hasn't darkened enough. And the paper's been pressed to age it, but the watermark position is off by—"

"Stop." Vess held up one finger, and something flickered across his face that I had never seen there before, and it took me a moment to identify it because it was so out of place among all that precision.

It was regret. Or something wearing regret's clothes.

"Do you know," he said quietly, "how many times I have watched a clerk stand exactly where you're standing, saying exactly what you're saying? *The ink is wrong. The hand is not mine. Someone has done this to me.* They always say it. And they are always lying, until the rare occasion when they aren't, and by then it makes no difference, because the record says what the record says." He closed the folio. "The Ledger Guild does not deal in truth, Crane. Truth is volatile. We deal in *record*. And the record shows that you concealed a proscribed text for private advantage, in your own hand, dated, signed, witnessed by the intake seal."

"The intake seal can be applied by anyone with a stamp—"

"Anyone with a stamp," Vess agreed. "Which is everyone. Which is the point. Which has always been the point." He looked at me then, really looked, and for one strange moment the blade-narrow man seemed tired, seemed almost kind. "You were good, Crane. You were the best hand in the building. I told them that when I brought you in off the street with no references and no history. I vouched for you. Do you understand what that cost me?"

No. No, I didn't, and I was beginning to understand that I never had understood anything about Vess at all, that the sheltering institution I'd resented and relied upon had been a feeding trough all along, and that the man who ran it might be neither its author nor its master but simply its most senior hostage.

"Where is the book, Rafael?" he asked, and used my name, my whole name, gently, the way you speak to a man at a funeral.

"Incinerated," I said. "As logged."

"As *not* logged." He sighed. "Very well. Then we proceed as the record requires." He turned to the two men waiting by the door—guild wardens, big as doors themselves—and nodded once. "Search his lodgings. Search the crypts if he's been near the crypts. And send word to the cells: the witch's hearing is moved forward. Tonight."

"Tonight?" My voice cracked on it. "Her hearing was set for week's end—"

"The record has been amended," Vess said, "to reflect new evidence." And there it was, laid bare, the whole machinery of the place I'd hidden in for six years: reality was whatever the last entry said, and the last entry was whatever the guildmaster's stamp said, and the stamp was currently being whispered to by something behind a throne.

They took my satchel off the desk before I could move. Of course they did; I'd led them straight to it with my eyes, one fatal glance downward, six years of invisibility undone by a single human flinch. One of the wardens unwrapped the oilcloth with surprising delicacy, and there it was—Mother Yew's book, her cipher, her century of patient terrible arithmetic—and Vess took it in both hands the way a priest takes a relic or a thief takes a purse.

"Mother Yew," I said. "She's innocent. Whatever the charges are, they're yours—you wrote them, you amended the record, you—"

"She confessed," Vess said.

The word went through me like a cold nail. "*What?*"

"This afternoon. Before witnesses. She confessed to forging entries in guild records going back decades, to the falsification of trade documents, to the concealment of proscribed texts." He paused at the door. "In fact, she confessed in remarkable detail. Dates. Amounts. Pages. She described forgeries in your hand, Crane, with extraordinary precision. Almost as though she'd studied them."

*Almost as though she'd studied them.*

Eleven years ago I ran from that woman's cottage with nothing but the clothes I wore and a debt I didn't understand, and I told myself for eleven years that I had escaped her. And now she sat in a cell confessing to crimes committed in my handwriting, describing my own letters stroke by stroke to armed men, and I could not tell—if I am honest, I still cannot tell—whether she was protecting me, or framing me, or whether those had ever been different things in her long, long life.

Or whether, God help me, some of those forgeries were real. Whether the hand I thought of as mine had been hers first, trained into me like a graft, so that anything written in it would always belong to her too.

*

They gave me a cell instead of the salt room, which I chose to read as a message. Salt rooms are for things the guild wants bound. Cells are for things the guild wants *kept*—kept available, kept useful, kept frightened. Vess had not yet decided what I was. Neither, I think, had Vess.

The cell was papered, floor to ceiling, with old ledger pages. This is guild custom; paper remembers, and a wall of records is supposed to remind a prisoner that he is himself a record now, an entry awaiting disposition. I lay on the cot and read my neighbors in the wall by candlelight. A widow's shadow, sold 1841. A jar of laughter, three installments remaining. A boy's apprenticeship, term indefinite, consideration received.

*I know that entry,* I thought. *I balanced that entry.* I had run my finger down that line a hundred nights ago, back when the strangest thing in my week was a widow's shadow, and felt only a clerk's mild pity for whoever the boy had been.

Consideration received. Four words. I had read them a hundred times and never once asked: received by whom?

The candle burned low. Somewhere above me, muffled through floors of stone and paper, I heard the guild begin to gather for the hearing—doors, footsteps, the particular hush of many clerks assembling to watch a record be finalized. They would bring Mother Yew up to the reading hall. They would read the confession aloud in her voice or in mine or in no one's, and the record would close over her like water, and the entity behind the throne would take its payment, and the arithmetic would go on accruing.

Unless the debts were all forgiven at once. Unless the king consented. Unless Odile sang. Unless—

Unless I got out of a locked cell in the basement of the most secure building in the kingdom, using nothing but a candle, a wall of paper, and the one skill I had left.

I got up. I put my palm flat against the wall, against the widow's shadow and the jar of laughter and the boy sold into indefinite terms, and I felt the pages hum faintly under my hand, the way all guild paper hums when it holds something true.

Paper remembers. Everyone in this building believes that. It's the first thing they teach you, and the last thing they forget:

Paper can be *amended*.

I took down the candle and began, very carefully, to read the wall for a door.

Above me, the bells began to ring the hour, and beneath the bells, thin and rising through the stone like smoke through a floorboard, I heard singing—Odile's voice, unmistakable even muffled, even afraid, coming not from the crypt below but from the reading hall above, where she had no business being, where she had gone, clearly, deliberately, straight into the wolf's mouth to buy time for a dead woman and a clerk in a cell.

The hearing had begun. And Odile was singing to it.
