## 14. The Unbinding Rite

There is a particular silence that lives in the crypt beneath the Ledger Guild, and I have spent eleven years learning to hate it.

It isn't the silence of an empty room. Empty rooms are honest. This silence has weight to it, a pressure behind the ears, like the moment before a thunderclap stretched out over decades. It is the sound of a cage holding something that does not wish to be held, and every singer in the choir can feel it pressing against our songs like a tongue against a rotten tooth.

We were supposed to have another month.

Maren said so herself, three nights ago, her voice thin as winter light: "The rite wants nine voices. We are seven. Give me until the new moon and I will teach Rafael's part to someone, or find an eighth singer in the gutters if I must." She had said it with the calm of a woman who has been dying slowly for sixty years and finds it rude when the world hurries her.

The world hurried us anyway.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes do, with good news.

Tam came up from the western stair at dusk, breathless, his choir-robe still smelling of the tallow candles he'd been hoarding. "The guild's emptied out," he said. "Vess has called every clerk, every guard, everyone with a ledger-stamp to the counting-house. Something about an auction. The corridors above are bare as a plucked chicken."

I remember thinking, *an auction*, and feeling my stomach drop through the floor, because Rafael had told me about the auctions—midnight affairs where names changed hands like coins—and because if Vess was gathering the whole guild in one room, then either something wonderful was happening or something terrible was being sold.

"We could go now," Tam said. "While they're all upstairs patting themselves on the back for whatever they've bought."

"Go where?" I asked, though I knew.

"The rite. Tonight."

Maren was already shaking her head before he finished. "Seven voices. The song needs nine. You might as well try to hold a door shut with two fingers."

"With respect, elder," Tam said, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before—not fear exactly, but its ambitious cousin, desperation—"we might not have seven voices by the new moon. Have you looked at Sera lately?"

I had. That was the trouble. Sera had taken to sleeping fourteen hours a day, and when she sang her part of the cage-song, the notes came out gray. Not wrong—nothing so useful as wrong—but *thin*, like thread worn nearly through. And Pell had begun humming in his sleep, which none of us did, ever, because the melody we carry is not meant for dreaming. And little Wren, who was nineteen and had never known a life outside the crypt, had asked me last week, very quietly, what color her voice used to be.

"It's fading faster," Maren admitted. Her hands were folded on her cane the way she folded them when she was losing an argument with herself. "The thing below is pulling harder. Every trade the guild makes feeds it, and the guild has been making trades like a drunkard makes promises."

"So we starve waiting for perfection," Tam said, "or we sing early and imperfect and hope imperfection is enough."

"And if it isn't?"

Nobody answered that. In the candlelight, seven faces turned toward the deep stair—the one that went down past the archive levels, past the salt-lined cells, down to the place where the choir's true work lived. The place we sang toward every day without ever approaching, the way you can love someone your whole life through letters and never dare knock on their door.

I should say, since this account may be all that survives of us, that the choir knew what the rite would cost. We had always known. The cage-song holds the entity bound; the unbinding rite is meant to do the opposite—to sing the cage open around nothing, once the debts that feed it are erased, so that the thing starves loose and dissolves. But a rite that unmakes bindings can unmake singers. The song passes through us. If the binding resists—if anything is left holding—it takes the nearest voices apart like a child taking apart a clock to see why it doesn't tick.

Maren had watched it happen once, eighty years ago. She didn't talk about it. She only had a habit, when she thought no one was looking, of touching her throat with two fingers, the way you'd touch a scar.

"Seven voices," she said again, but softer now, and I understood she had already lost the argument with herself too.

"Elder," I said, "let me lead the descant. If the melody goes wrong, it should be mine that goes wrong with it."

That was vanity dressed as courage, and I knew it even as I said it. But I'll confess it here: I wanted my voice to matter. I had spent eleven years singing into a silence that never answered, and some starving animal in me wanted, just once, to be heard by the thing itself. To make it flinch.

Maren looked at me a long moment. Then she said, "Very well. But Odile—if you feel the song begin to take you, you stop. You bite the note off clean and you let the others carry it. Promise me."

"I promise," I said, and the animal in me crossed its fingers, and I let it.

* * *

We went down at midnight, which felt right. Catastrophes should keep proper hours.

The deep stair is cut from stone that predates the city, and it is cold in a way that isn't temperature. Our candles guttered blue as we descended, all seven flames leaning the same direction, like grass under wind. Behind us, faint and far above, I could hear the guild's auction going on—the murmur of clerks, the ring of a bell—and it struck me as obscene, that commerce should continue overhead while we walked down to die or not die.

At the bottom is the chamber. I won't describe it fully; descriptions give it power, and I have given it quite enough already. Suffice to say it is round, and old, and the walls are carved with the names of every singer who ever held the cage-song, thousands of them, spiraling down and down like the rings inside a tree. My own name is near the top of the spiral. When I was newly bound, I used to read the names below mine and wonder how many of those people had stood exactly where I stood, promising exactly what I'd promised.

We took our places in the circle. Seven points of a nine-pointed star, the gaps where the eighth and ninth singers should have been left open like missing teeth. Maren stood at the crown of the star, oldest voice, anchor-note. Sera to her left, gray-threaded Sera, who smiled at me across the circle and mouthed something I chose to interpret as encouragement.

Rafael wasn't there. He was off decoding Mother Yew's cipher, hunting the arithmetic of salvation, and I had argued with myself for an hour about whether to send for him. I hadn't. Some part of me—the wry part, the part that narrates my disasters even as they happen—had decided that if the rite failed, I did not want the man I was beginning to love to watch it fail. Cowardice, probably. Or courtesy. With me the two wear the same coat.

Maren raised her hand. Dropped it.

And we sang.

* * *

I cannot describe the cage-song in words any more than you can describe salt to someone who has never tasted the sea. But I can tell you what it does. It is a song with no beginning and no end, a circle drawn in sound, and each voice is a length of the circumference. For eleven years I have sung my arc of that circle, and tonight, for the first time, I sang it downward—turned the cage inside out, made the walls into a door.

For perhaps thirty seconds, it worked.

I felt the entity notice. That is the only word. A vast attention swung toward us like a lighthouse beam, and the pressure in the chamber changed, and the candle flames stood up straight for the first time since we'd entered. Somewhere in the dark below the floor—or not below, the directions don't mean anything down there—something enormous shifted its weight, the way a prisoner shifts when the cell door first rattles.

Sera's voice cracked.

It was a small thing. A hairline fracture in her arc of the circle, right where her worn thread finally gave. Any other night, the rest of us would have covered it, bent our own notes around the gap the way water bends around a stone. But tonight the song was turned outward, straining against a binding, and there was no slack anywhere in the circle for mercy.

The gap became a wound. The wound became a door.

I heard it happen before I understood it—a sound like a page tearing, very long and very slow—and Sera's voice came apart mid-note. Not stopped. *Came apart.* One moment she was singing; the next her voice was everywhere in the chamber at once, thinned to fog, and then it was nowhere, and Sera herself folded down onto the stones like a coat slipping off a hook.

Pell screamed her name and broke his own arc reaching for her, and the song took him too—I heard his tenor unravel, that same terrible page-tearing, and he was gone between one heartbeat and the next, leaving nothing behind but his robe and a smell like rain on hot stone.

Wren lasted longer. Wren, nineteen years old, who had asked me what color her voice used to be—Wren held her note with both hands, so to speak, sang it white-knuckled while the chamber shook and the entity pulled at the tear in our circle like wind through a broken window. She held it for six full seconds. I counted. I will count those seconds for the rest of my life.

Then she was gone too, and the circle was four, and the song collapsed.

* * *

Here is what I did not tell Maren I would do, because I did not know it myself until the moment came: I did not stop singing.

The song had fallen apart around me, but my arc of it was still alive in my throat, still burning, and the tear in the binding was still open, and everything in me that had spent eleven years singing into silence refused—absolutely, animal-refused—to let the silence win. So I kept singing. Alone. Four gaps in the circle and me pouring my voice into the wound like a girl sticking her finger in a bursting dam.

And the entity *listened.*

That is the thing I need to set down while my hands still shake enough to prove it happened. It listened the way I have dreamed of being listened to my whole life—with total, devouring attention. And then, worse, it began to answer. Not in words. In melody. It took my descant—the one I had begged Maren to let me lead, my descant, the one piece of music I have ever made my own—and it repeated it back to me, note for note, a half-beat behind, the way you'd echo someone whose language you were learning.

Then it changed a note. Improved it. My melody, corrected.

I bit the note off clean, just as I'd promised Maren, and the silence slammed back in like a door, and I was on my knees on the stones with three empty robes around me and Maren's arms locked around my shoulders, dragging me back from the center of the chamber, saying my name over and over in a voice gone rusty with horror.

"They're not dead," she was saying, or maybe I was saying it, I honestly don't know. "Unmade, not dead. The song can be rewoven, they can be—"

But we both knew the odds of rewaving anything, and neither of us finished the sentence.

* * *

We climbed the stair slowly, carrying what could be carried, which was almost nothing. Sera's robe. Pell's candles. Wren's shoes, because she had always sung barefoot and no one remembered why.

Above us, the auction was ending. We could hear the bell, and applause—actual applause, polite and dry as paper—and I found I was laughing, a small ugly laugh with no humor in it whatsoever, because three of my people had just been unwritten from the world and upstairs the merchants of impossible things were congratulating one another on their purchases.

"What now?" Tam asked at the top of the stair. His voice was wrecked. He had loved Sera; everyone had loved Sera; Sera had been the only one who could make Maren laugh.

"Now," Maren said, and her spine straightened by sheer fury, "now we learn from it. The rite failed because the circle was broken, and the circle was broken because the choir is starved, and the choir is starved because the guild keeps feeding *it* instead of *us*. Which means the arithmetic hasn't changed. Rafael finds the cipher. Every debt forgiven or repaid, all at once, or nothing. We simply have less time and fewer voices and—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, elder to the last— "and a great deal more reason."

I said nothing. I was listening to the inside of my own skull.

Because the melody was still there. My descant, sitting in my mind where I keep my own thoughts, except now it had a correction in it—one improved note, one note that was not mine, placed there as neatly as a forged entry in a ledger. The entity had learned my melody, and it had left its edit behind like a signature, like a calling card, like a cat leaving a dead bird on a doorstep: *see what I can do with what you gave me.*

Every time I sing now, I will have to sing around that note. Around my own melody, wearing someone else's improvement.

I climbed the last steps and stepped out into the corridor, and that was when I saw him—Rafael, coming up from the archives at a dead run, ash on his sleeves and terror on his face, and behind him, at the far end of the hall, a figure in traveling grays leaning on a blackthorn staff, watching us with eyes like a woman deciding which of her children to eat first.

Mother Yew had come to the city.

And Rafael was running *toward* her.
