## 15. Ledger of Debts Owed

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a counting-house at three in the morning, and I have come to think of it as the sound of arithmetic holding its breath.

I sat at my old desk with the prophecy book open before me, a candle guttering at my elbow, and I did what I have done every working night for eleven years: I read numbers. But these were not the guild's tidy columns, not the elegant double-entry of bottled storms and borrowed years. These were Mother Yew's figures, scrawled in that ash-brown ink across the margins of a book she had written a century before my birth, and they made no sense at all.

Or rather—they made no sense *as* numbers. That was the trick of it. I had been staring at them for two days like a man trying to price a poem.

The cipher was simple once you saw it, which is the cruelest thing about ciphers. Every one of them is. You spend weeks convinced you are wrestling an intelligence greater than your own, and then you discover you were only wrestling your own assumptions, and the intelligence was laughing at you from inside your skull.

Here is how it worked. Each entry in the prophecy's margins carried three elements: a figure, a word, and a small mark—a dash, a circle, or a cross. I had catalogued forty-one such entries over two nights, copying them onto scrap paper by candlelight because I did not dare write anything in the book itself. Not anymore. My handwriting had already betrayed me once; Vess had forged an entry in my own hand and used it to put Mother Yew in a cell beneath the guild. I trusted nothing that came off my pen now except this scratch-paper, and even that I burned each dawn.

The figures were dates. The words were names—some I recognized from the ledger trails I'd followed to Vess's door, some so old they predated the guild itself. And the marks—

I laid out my forty-one scraps on the desk like a card sharp laying down a losing hand, and I looked at them until my eyes watered, and then I did the thing I should have done first, the thing any apprentice learns in his first month: I sorted by column instead of by row.

Dates ascending. Names alphabetical. Marks grouped.

And there it was.

Every entry marked with a cross was paired with a name that also appeared in the guild's ledgers as a *debtor*. Every circle marked a *creditor*. And the dashes—the dashes marked debts that appeared in both columns, owed and owing at once, closed loops, paid in full.

Mother Yew hadn't written a prophecy. She had written a *balance sheet*.

I laughed. It came out wrong, a dry bark in the empty room, and the candle flame shivered. Of course. Of course she had. She was a hedge-witch; hedge-witches don't deal in fate, they deal in bargains. What else would a century of foresight look like, written by a woman who thought in terms of what was owed?

But then why encode it? Why hide the plain truth of it in cipher, if the truth was simply that the thing behind the throne kept accounts?

Because—and here my hands went cold on the scraps of paper, cold enough that I had to cup them around the candle—because the cipher wasn't hiding the debts. The cipher was hiding the *arithmetic*. Anyone could read the entries and learn that the entity held claims on forty-one souls across a hundred years. Only someone who did the sums would learn what the sum *meant*.

I did the sums.

Forty-one entries. Crosses, circles, dashes. I counted the crosses: thirty-three open debts, still held, still accruing. Circles: six creditors, parties to whom the entity itself owed something—which seemed impossible until I remembered that even parasites have hosts, and hosts can make demands. Dashes: two. Two debts closed. Two loops completed.

Two.

In a hundred years, out of forty-one bargains struck with the thing that wore King Aldric like a crown, exactly two had ever been fully settled.

And I knew both of them. Because I had spent the last week reading nothing but guild ledgers, and a clerk never forgets an entry, not even one he wishes he could forget.

The first closed debt belonged to a woman named Sarelle Vane, who in the year 847 had sold her grief to the guild—genuine, bottled, grief—and whose account showed, decades later, a repayment in kind: the entity had returned her grief to her, whole, when her husband died. A cruel mercy. But a payment. The loop closed. Sarelle Vane had died two years later, free, according to the parish records I'd pulled from the civic archive under a borrowed seal.

Free.

The second closed debt—

I didn't want to look at it. I looked anyway. That is either courage or compulsion, and after eleven years in the Ledger Guild I have stopped pretending to know the difference.

The second closed debt was dated sixty-one years ago. The debtor's name was written in Mother Yew's hand, in the margin of a page describing a plague winter: *Yew, of the Hedgerow*. My mistress. And beside her name, in the same ash-brown ink, a single line that I must have read four times before it let go of me:

*Paid in full: one apprentice, given before he was asked.*

My apprenticeship. The bargain she struck—the one I had run from, the one I had spent eleven years telling myself I had escaped—was not an open debt. It was a *closed* one. She had paid it. Sixty years ago, before I was born, she had handed something over to the entity, something labeled "one apprentice," and the transaction had cleared, and the loop had shut, and somewhere in the great dark ledger of the thing behind the throne, my name sat in a column marked *received*, not *owed*.

Which meant I wasn't its property.

Which meant—I set down the scraps carefully, the way you set down something fragile when your hands are shaking—that I was the only person in this entire miserable arithmetic who stood outside the equation.

No. Wait. I picked them up again. Because there were thirty-three crosses, and I had counted myself among the uncounted, but the cipher said otherwise. The cipher said my apprenticeship was a *dash*. Closed. Settled. Except—

Except Mother Yew had told me, the night she arrived in the city demanding her ledger back, that she wrote the prophecy to save me. And a woman who writes a hundred-year rescue plan does not leave the rescue half-finished. If my debt were truly closed, why would the book need me at all? Why would my name appear on every page after tonight?

Unless the closure was conditional. Unless "paid in full" meant something more like "paid pending." Unless the entity's books, like all books kept by creatures that feed on futures, contained entries that could be *reopened*.

I got up and walked the length of the counting-house, past the sleeping shelves, past the iron cabinets where the volatile trades were stored—the jar of laughter from my first strange night, still faintly chuckling to itself in the dark—and I tried to think like her. Like Mother Yew. Which meant thinking like a hedge-witch, which meant thinking like a lawyer, which meant thinking like the worst kind of accountant: the kind who loves loopholes the way other people love their children.

A debt closes when it is forgiven or repaid. That is the first law of the ledger, older than the guild, older probably than writing. Repayment transfers value. Forgiveness destroys the claim entirely—it doesn't move the debt, it *unmakes* it. And the entity fed on claims. On holding. On the weight of things owed. A repaid debt gave it a meal. A forgiven debt starved it.

So what happened if *every* claim it held was destroyed at once?

Thirty-three open debts. If thirty-two were forgiven while one remained, the entity simply tightened its grip on the last debtor and went on feeding. Any creditor knows this; it's why they never forgive piecemeal. But if all thirty-three collapsed in the same instant—if every claim was simultaneously forgiven or repaid, every loop shut at once, the whole balance sheet brought to zero in a single breath—

Then there would be nothing left to hold. Nothing left to feed on. Nothing left to *be*. A parasite is a relationship, not a creature. Take away the relationship and you take away the creature, and what remains is whatever was underneath the crown all along: one tired mortal king, and a hole in the world shaped like forever.

That was the rite the choir had attempted early and failed. That was what Odile's voice was for—not to fight the thing, but to *forgive* it, note by note, binding by binding, all at once. The singers weren't warriors. They were auditors. They sang the debts to zero.

And they had failed because the arithmetic didn't balance. Three singers unmade, and the entity had learned Odile's melody besides. I pressed my palms against the cold iron of a cabinet and understood, with the terrible clarity that only comes at three in the morning when you have finally done the sums, why they had failed.

The choir could forgive the debts the entity held on *others*. But the entity's greatest asset—its anchor, its collateral, the loan that secured everything else—was a debt it held on itself. Or rather, one it held through its host. King Aldric's wish for immortality had been paid for with his descendants' futures; Maren had told us that much in the crypt, in her voice like dry leaves. But a wish granted creates a counter-claim. The king had bought eternity. Eternity, therefore, owed the king. And the entity had collected that debt in advance—it had taken Aldric's body, his voice, his name, his reign—as payment.

You cannot forgive a debt the debtor refuses to acknowledge. You cannot close a loop when one party insists the transaction is still open. The choir had sung forgiveness to thirty-two debts and the thirty-third had stood fast, because the thirty-third required the consent of the only soul in the kingdom who didn't know he was a debtor.

The king himself. The final creditor. The last entry in the book.

Mother Yew's cipher didn't just describe the unbinding. It *priced* it. And the price was this: every debt forgiven or repaid, simultaneously, including the crown's—including the wish that started it all, which could only be released by the man who wished it, choosing, freely, to let it go. To die mortal rather than reign hollow.

One yes. That was all the arithmetic lacked. One yes from a man who had been a prisoner inside his own triumph for longer than most kingdoms last.

I sat back down. The candle had burned down to a stub; wax had pooled across my scratch-paper, sealing three of the scraps into a little translucent tomb, and I found I didn't mind. Let them be buried. They were only copies. The real book lay open in front of me, and I looked at it now not as a prophecy but as what it had always been: instructions. A hedge-witch's instructions, written a century in advance, addressed to whoever was stubborn enough to do the reading.

And on the final page—the page after tonight, the page where my name appeared on every line—there was one last marginal entry I had skipped, because it wasn't a date or a name or a mark. It was a sentence, plain, unciphered, in handwriting gone thin and spidery, as if the pen had been held by someone very old or very tired:

*The last debt is always the hardest to forgive, because it is the one we owe ourselves.*

Below it, smaller:

*Rafael. When you understand this, come and get me out. I will show you where the king keeps his yes.*

*

I read it five times. Then I did what any sensible clerk would do with a document that changes everything: I checked it for forgery.

Vess had taught me that lesson without meaning to. A forged entry in my own hand had arrested Mother Yew; I would not be caught twice by ink. I examined the sentence under the candle, tilted it, held it near the flame's heat to see if sympathetic ink bloomed. Nothing. I compared the hand against the rest of the book—the plague-winter page, the auction notes, the hundred years of margins. Same hand. Same pressure. Same slight rightward drift that gets worse toward the bottom of a page, the drift of a writer leaning into exhaustion. This was hers. Written whenever she wrote it—last year, last century, time being a suggestion rather than a rule where Mother Yew was concerned—but hers.

*Come and get me out.*

She had known she'd be arrested. Of course she had. She'd written a hundred-year balance sheet; a few weeks of foresight was pocket change to her. She had walked into the guildhall knowing Vess would take the book and take her too, because the book needed to be *here*, in my hands, decoded at three in the morning by the one person whose name appeared on every remaining page. She had arranged her own imprisonment the way she had once arranged my apprenticeship: as a line item in someone else's salvation.

I should have been furious. Part of me was—the part that remembered a girl's worth of nights crying behind a hedgerow, running from a bargain I hadn't chosen. But another part, the clerk part, the part that respects a well-kept book no matter whose blood the ink came from, felt something worse than fury.

It felt *understood*. Somewhere in the past, a woman I had hated and loved and fled had looked down a century of entries and seen me—seen exactly this moment, this candle, this cold—and had trusted me with the ending. Not commanded me. Trusted me. There was no order in the sentence, no binding, no geas. Just an invitation, and a promise: *I will show you where the king keeps his yes.*

She knew how to reach him. The true Aldric, buried alive inside his own immortality. She knew, and the knowledge was locked in a cell beneath the guild, in a woman who was dying—I had seen it in her face the night she came back, the too-long-alive look of someone spending her last coin—and every hour I sat here doing arithmetic was an hour subtracted from whatever she had left.

I looked at the sums one more time. Thirty-three debts. One song. One yes. And between me and the yes: locked doors, a guildmaster who wanted me erased, an entity that had already learned to mimic the voice of the woman I— 

I stopped. Filed that thought. Unopened. There would be time for it later, if there was a later, and if there wasn't, then it could stay in the drawer forever, unexamined, like all my best feelings.

The candle died. In the dark, the counting-house breathed around me—the soft tick of the trade-cabinets, the jar of laughter murmuring in its sleep, the vast patient weight of ten thousand ledgers recording ten thousand ways a person can be owned.

I lit a fresh candle from the ember, took up my scratch-paper, and burned it, all of it, every copied figure, watching the arithmetic of salvation curl into ash. It didn't matter. It was in my head now, permanently, the way numbers get when you've done them honestly. Thirty-three. One song. One yes.

Then I opened the drawer where the guild keeps its master keys—the ones Vess thought no junior clerk knew about, because Vess had never been invisible the way I had been invisible—and I lifted out the ring for the lower cells.

Salt and paper, Maren had said the cells were made of. Salt to hold the body, paper to hold the name. Well. Paper was my trade. Paper had been turned against me once already.

Time to see what it could do for me.

I put out the candle, tucked the prophecy book inside my coat against my heart, where it beat like a second pulse, and went down into the dark to steal back the woman who had stolen me first.
