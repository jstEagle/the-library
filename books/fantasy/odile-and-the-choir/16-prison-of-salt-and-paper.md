## 16. Prison of Salt and Paper

The guild cells are not cells, properly speaking. They are ledgers made solid.

I learned this from Vess's own filing manuals, back when I still believed the guild's cruelties were merely bureaucratic. A prisoner here is not locked behind iron; he is *entered*. His name is written into a binding-contract, his debts itemized, and the paper does the rest. The walls of the holding rooms are salt-pressed vellum, thick as brick, and every sheet is inscribed with the occupant's obligations in a hand so small you'd need a jeweler's loupe to read it. The debt holds the door shut. You cannot walk out of your own arithmetic.

Which means that to break someone out, you don't need a key. You need a forgery.

And forgery, as it happens, is my native tongue.

* * *

I spent three days preparing, and I would be lying if I said I didn't enjoy a single minute of it, because I did, and that enjoyment is its own confession. There is a pleasure in ink that I have never found anywhere else—not in Odile's voice, though God knows it comes close, not in wine, not in the rare and dangerous warmth of being known. Ink obeys. Ink does exactly what you tell it. For three days I told it beautiful lies.

First I needed her cell number. That was simple theft: a night in the records hall with a candle shielded behind my coat, tracing the intake ledger for the week of the auction. Mother Yew—entered under her legal name, which I will not repeat here because she hated it and once threatened to hex my tongue for saying it aloud—was filed under *Assets, Contested*, shelf nine, alcove three. The guild had seized her as evidence. She was, technically, property pending adjudication.

Then I needed to know what she owed.

This was harder. Her debts were old, tangled, written in at least four hands across two centuries of paper, and some of them were not hers at all but had been *assigned* to her by creditors who preferred a living debtor to a dead account. The guild loves an assigned debt the way a leech loves a thigh. I sat on the cold floor of the archive with her file open across my knees and read until dawn came gray through the high slit windows, and what I learned made me sick in a way I hadn't anticipated.

She owed the entity. Of course she did. Everyone who had ever touched the guild's business owed the entity something—it was the hidden creditor behind half the entries in the master ledgers, the silent partner in every impossible trade, collecting interest on bottled storms and borrowed years and names sold at auction. But Mother Yew's debt was different. Hers was *original*. Not inherited, not assigned, not traded. It was one of the first entries ever made in the guild's founding books, and the consideration—the thing she'd received in exchange—was dated a hundred years ago.

A hundred years. She had been alive far too long, and now I knew why. She was still paying.

Her debt was her lifespan itself. Borrowed against, renewed, rolled over century after century like a farmer rolling over a loan he can never clear, each renewal costing more than the last, until what remained of her original term was measured not in years but in weeks. The file said so plainly, in the dispassionate clerk-hand I once emulated: *Remaining term: approximately nineteen days.*

Nineteen days. And she had spent them in a salt-paper cell waiting for a tribunal that would rule, inevitably, in favor of the creditor.

I put the file down and pressed my palms flat against the cold stone floor and breathed, because my hands were shaking, and a forger whose hands shake is worse than useless. Nineteen days. She had run from me a hundred years before I was born—or rather, she had arranged to run, had scripted her own disappearance into a prophecy book so that a boy born decades later would find it and follow—and all of it, all of it, had been servicing this debt. My apprenticeship. My name in her books. The bargain she struck the night I fled her hedge-cottage with nothing but the clothes I stood up in and a hatred I had nursed for fifteen years like a coal in my fist.

I had hated her for running. Now I learned she had never been permitted to stop.

* * *

The forgery took me one night.

Here is how the cells work, mechanically, for those who have never had occasion to break into one: at the head of each corridor stands a warden-clerk with a release-book, and the release-book is checked against the prisoner's entry-contract, and if the arithmetic balances—if every listed debt is marked paid, forgiven, or transferred—then the salt walls lose their grip and the door opens, because the paper has no further reason to hold anyone. The system is elegant. The system is also, like all systems built on paper, vulnerable to a man with steady hands and no conscience.

I have a conscience. I've just learned to set it aside for specific transactions, the way you take off a ring before kneading dough.

My forgery was a transfer instrument. I could not mark her debts *paid*—the amounts were too large, too well-known, and any auditor worth his salt (and these auditors are literally preserved in salt, their corpses propped in the archive corners as warnings, which is a detail I wish I could forget) would catch the discrepancy within the hour. But a *transfer* only requires a solvent party willing to assume the obligation. And there was one party in the guild whose credit was beyond question, whose signature appeared on more instruments than any other single hand, whose authority no warden-clerk would dare to second-guess.

Vess.

I forged the guildmaster's seal and signature transferring Mother Yew's entire debt portfolio—principal, interest, penalties, the whole rotten edifice—to himself, effective immediately, consideration: one prophecy book, to be delivered. It was almost true. He wanted the book. He'd told me so in his office with that soft, patient voice of his, the voice of a man who has never once raised it because he has never once needed to. Let him have the paperwork. By the time he discovered the book was not attached, I intended to be several districts away and the debt would be his problem, and there is a justice in that which appeals to the part of me that used to copy out fair-trade contracts and dream of being honest.

The warden-clerk on duty that night was a young man named Pell, who had been hired six months ago and still believed the guild was a respectable institution. I know because I was him, once. I showed him the transfer instrument, and he checked it against the entry-contract, and his lips moved slightly as he read the figures, the way mine used to move, and then he said, "Everything balances," in the tone of a man relieved to find the world orderly, and the salt wall of alcove three sighed and softened like a fist unclenching, and the door swung open on darkness and the smell of old paper and a very old woman sitting very still.

* * *

She was smaller than I remembered.

That's a stupid thing to notice first, I know, but memory is stupid. In my recollection Mother Yew had been enormous—a figure who filled a cottage doorway, whose shadow reached across a garden, whose voice could strip bark off an oak. The woman huddled on the cell floor was a sparrow wrapped in a confiscated cloak, white-haired, wrists like knotted twine, eyes that took a moment to find me in the candlelight and then, when they did, went wide and wet and terribly, terribly calm.

"Rafael," she said. Not a question. As if she'd been expecting me for a hundred years, which, I suppose, in a manner of speaking, she had.

"You look terrible," I said, because I am a coward, and wit is where cowards hide.

"You look tired," she returned, without heat. "Have you been sleeping?"

"I've been committing crimes."

"Good." She got up slowly, one joint at a time, like a gate opening on a rusted hinge. "Help me walk. These floors are filthy and I refuse to die with dirt on my knees."

"Die—" I started to say, and stopped, because I had read the file, because I knew about the nineteen days, except that the nineteen days had apparently become considerably fewer, because whatever the entity's agents had done to her in questioning had accelerated the collection. Debts come due faster when the debtor becomes inconvenient. I didn't ask. Some things you learn by reading and some things you learn by looking, and her face told me everything the file had left out.

We walked. Slowly, through corridors I had mapped in my head a dozen times, past the salt-cellars where the auditors stood propped in their corners with their fingers still curled around pens that had crumbled to dust, past the records hall where I had stolen her cell number, out through the delivery entrance where the night-porter was mercifully drunk on the guild's own confiscated wine. The city outside was raining. It was nearly midnight and the streets were empty and the rain came down fine and silver under the lamps, and Mother Yew stopped at the top of the guild steps and looked at it for a long moment with her face turned up.

"I haven't felt rain in forty years," she said. "They keep us indoors. Debtors indoors. Rain washes ink, you see. Bad for the collateral."

I had a safehouse. Odile had found it—a singer's lodging above a defunct chapel in the Marshend quarter, rented under a false name, paid in coin that couldn't be traced. We made it there by the small hours, and I put her in the bed by the window, and she lay down with the particular economy of motion that belongs to people who are conserving what little strength remains, and she looked at me across the little room with those wet, calm eyes and said:

"Now. Tell me what you've learned."

So I told her. All of it. The prophecy book and the date circled in ash-brown ink. The choir beneath the city and the cage-song dwindling year by year. Vess and the payments delivered upward, behind the throne. The cipher I'd cracked in her margins—the requirement that every debt the entity held be simultaneously forgiven or repaid, all of them, at once, or the binding would simply reassert itself around whichever single thread remained. The auction, and how I had bid my own name against the entity for Odile's voice, and won, and what that meant now: that my name was collateral in a fight I could not withdraw from, that I was bound into the arithmetic whether I liked it or not.

She listened without interrupting, which she had never once done when I was her apprentice. When I was fourteen she interrupted everything. It was one of the ways I knew she was listening at all.

When I finished, she was quiet for a long time. The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, in the defunct chapel, a loose shutter knocked in the wind like a patient knocking at a door no one answers anymore.

"The cipher is correct," she said finally. "Every debt, simultaneously. That is the lock and that is the key. The entity is a creature of contract, Rafael. It exists only in the space between a promise made and a promise kept. Close every gap at once and there is nowhere for it to stand."

"But there's a problem," I said. "There's always a problem. The debts are too many. Most of the creditors are dead. Some of the debtors are dead. How do you forgive a debt when both parties are dust?"

"You don't," she said. "You *assume* it. A debt doesn't die with its holders, boy. It passes. Down through estates, through guilds, through crowns. Every obligation in that ledger is still live somewhere, held by someone, even if the someone doesn't know it. Your job—your and Odile's job—is to find the final holder of each thread and get their consent. Forgiveness requires a living will. That's the cruelty of it. The entity made sure most of its creditors died intestate."

"How many threads?"

"Seven," she said. "Six you can reach. The seventh—" and here her voice did something I had never heard it do in all my years as her apprentice and all my weeks as her enemy and all the strange hours since: it faltered. "The seventh is the reason I wrote the prophecy. The reason I let you go. The reason for everything."

"Tell me."

"Not yet." She closed her eyes. "First, the forgiveness. Mine. Sit down, Rafael. This is a transaction, and I want you to witness it properly, because you're the only notary I trust and I raised you to distrust everyone including me."

I sat on the edge of the bed. She took my hand. Her grip was astonishingly strong for a woman made of twine and paper, and her palm was dry and warm and smelled faintly of the hedge-cottage, of rue and woodsmoke, of a kitchen I had fled at fourteen and never stopped smelling in my dreams.

"I, Yew, called Mother Yew, being of sound mind and failing body, do hereby forgive, remit, and release the debt of Rafael Crane: namely, his apprenticeship, entered into under duress of circumstance and sealed in the old fashion, the terms of which were never fully disclosed to either party." She paused. Opened her eyes. Looked at me. "Do you understand what I'm saying? The apprenticeship was a payment. *You* were the payment. I sold your service, your future labor, your name, to the guild's founders, a hundred years before you were born, because the alternative was worse and I was a coward and I loved you and I thought—I thought if I stayed close to the debt I could soften it. Manage it. Pay it down myself, quietly, over a lifetime. I ran the numbers for a hundred years, Rafael. I was so sure I could find a way to make the arithmetic kind."

"And you couldn't."

"No." The word came out flat and final, a door closing. "Arithmetic isn't kind. That's the first lesson I should have taught you and the last one you needed. Kindness isn't in the numbers. It's in what you do despite them." Her thumb moved across my knuckles, once, an old gesture I had forgotten I remembered. "So. Witnessed and sealed: your debt is forgiven. You are free of me. Whatever remains of my estate—which is a cottage that burned, a garden gone to weed, and a great deal of guilt—passes to you, along with the seventh thread. Listen carefully."

I listened carefully.

"The final creditor," she said, "the last holder of the last debt the entity carries—the debt that anchors all the others, the root-thread, the one that if it stands, everything stands—is Aldric. The king himself."

The room tilted. I gripped the bedframe.

"That's not possible. Aldric is the entity's—"

"—its vessel, its crown, its prison, yes, all of those. But before any of it, he was a debtor. Go back to Maren's history, boy, the elegy the choir elder sings. A king wished not to die. The entity granted the wish. But wishes are trades, and trades are debts, and the price of that wish was the futures of his descendants, paid forward generation by generation, until the descendants ran out and the only future left to pledge was his own death. That is the debt. Aldric owes the entity his natural end—the death he refused a hundred years ago. It holds his mortality in escrow. And as long as that debt stands unpaid, the entity stands inside the law of the trade, inside the contract, untouchable. Unbindable. You can sing at it until your throat bleeds and it will simply wait, because the king's consent is the keystone, and the king has never given it."

"He won't give it," I said. "He can't. He's barely—he's not even *there*, Odile says. There's a man inside pleading, but—"

"There is a man inside," Mother Yew said, "and men can say yes. Even buried men. Especially buried men." She squeezed my hand, hard, with the last of her strength, and her eyes were bright and fierce and utterly lucid. "Get his consent, Rafael. That is the seventh thread. That is why I wrote the prophecy—that is why I wrote *your name* into it, a hundred years before you existed, because I saw the shape of the solution and I needed someone stubborn enough and damaged enough and *free enough* to carry it, and I could not make such a person, so I had to wait for one to be born, and then I had to bind him to me so that he would hate me enough to leave and love me enough to finish. I am not asking your forgiveness. I am telling you the truth, which is better."

"It really isn't," I said, and my voice cracked, and I didn't care.

"No," she agreed, gently. "It really isn't. Truth is heavier. But it spends better."

And then, because she was dying, and because dying people are permitted a candor the living must earn, she said the thing I think she had carried longest of all:

"I loved you, Rafael. Not as a scheme. Not as a mechanism. Before the prophecy, before the design, there was a boy in my garden who talked to the bees and stole plums and wrote his letters backward, and I loved him first, and the design came after, and I have regretted the order of it every day of a hundred years. Love expressed as release. That's the only kind I was ever capable of. I'm giving you yours now. Take it. Don't waste it on grief. Grief is a debt too, and I won't have you paying it to me forever."

I don't remember answering. I remember the rain, and her hand going slack by degrees, not all at once, the way a fire dies down rather than goes out, and I remember that near dawn she said, "The shutter downstairs. Someone should fix that," in a perfectly ordinary voice, and then she said nothing ordinary again.

She died an hour after sunrise, with her face toward the window and the rain-washed light on it, and her last breath went out of her the way a signature leaves a page—completely, irreversibly, and with a strange sense of something finally *filed*.

I sat with her for a long time. Then I did the clerk's work, because she had raised me to do the clerk's work: I wrote the record. Date, parties, consideration, discharge. *Debt of Rafael Crane: forgiven by the creditor's own hand. Witnessed.* I signed it, and my hand did not shake, and I hated it for not shaking.

One thread closed. Six remained, and one of them ran upward through marble and gold leaf to a throne where a buried man waited for someone brave enough to ask him the only question that mattered:

*Will you consent to die?*

I folded the record into my coat, next to my heart, where I keep the documents that have cost me the most, and I went to find Odile, because the hardest yes in the kingdom was waiting, and neither of us could ask it alone.
