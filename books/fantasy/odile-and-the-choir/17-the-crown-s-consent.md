## 17. The Crown's Consent

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside a king's bedchamber at midnight, and I have decided it is the worst kind. Not the silence of empty rooms—empty rooms are honest about their emptiness—but the silence of a room that is pretending very hard to be full. Four guards who do not breathe loudly enough. A fire that burns without crackling, because even the logs in this palace have been taught manners. And in the great bed under the canopy of stitched gold thread, a man lying so still that for three full minutes I genuinely wondered whether I had been summoned to sing at a funeral rather than a melancholy.

Then his chest rose. Once. Slowly. Like a bellows operated by someone who had forgotten why.

"Your Majesty," said the chamberlain, whose name I had already forgotten on purpose, because names are currency and I am currently poor. "The singer will perform the soothing now."

*The soothing.* As if my voice were a poultice. As if I were going to press a song against the king's brow like a warm cloth and everything underneath would stop festering. I almost laughed, which would have been fatal, or at least career-ending, and possibly both.

I had come to court expecting a performance. That was the arrangement: the Ledger Guild lent out its choir-singers to settle volatile trades, and somewhere along the last hundred years someone had decided that a king's sadness was a trade like any other—a debt of joy, payable in melody. I had sung for merchants who'd sold their mothers' memories and wanted them back for an evening. I had sung for a widow whose shadow had been repossessed, humming her a lullaby she could feel but not see. I thought I knew what soothing sounded like.

I did not know what it sounded like inside a hollow man.

Because that was the thing, the thing I understood within four bars of the first hymn: King Aldric was not sad. Sadness has texture. It has weight and grain and a certain damp warmth, like earth turned over in spring. What was in that bed had no texture at all. It was smooth all the way down, like a stone worn by a river that no longer existed, and when I sang into him my voice went in and did not echo. It simply stopped, the way a coin stops when dropped into a well too deep to hear the bottom.

Except—and this is the part I will be explaining to myself for the rest of my life, however long Maren lets that be—it didn't quite stop.

At the very end of the fall, there was a sound. Faint. A syllable's worth of sound. Something that might have been *no*, or might have been *don't*, or might have been the last consonant of a name being swallowed.

I stopped singing. The chamberlain cleared his throat in the manner of men who believe throat-clearing is a form of governance.

"Continue," he said.

"No," I said, and discovered, pleasantly, that saying no to a chamberlain is much easier than saying no to a guildmaster. Vess had trained me out of it for years; apparently the training hadn't taken. "Your Majesty isn't melancholy. Your Majesty is occupied." I looked at the shape in the bed—the beautiful, waxen, royal shape of him—and I said, quietly, so only the room's true listener could hear: "Someone else is using this mouth. I'd like to speak with the tenant."

The guards shifted. The fire flinched. And the king—

The king opened his eyes.

I want to be precise about what I saw, because precision is the only weapon I have ever owned. His eyes were brown and kind and wrong, wrong the way a portrait is wrong: all the features correct, arranged by a hand that understood faces but had never been loved by one. They fixed on me with interest. Appetite, maybe. The look of something that has eaten a great many singers and remembers liking it.

"You are the one who hums staircases," it said, through Aldric's mouth, in Aldric's voice, pitched slightly too evenly, like a musician playing from notation instead of memory. "Maren's newest. The one they call Odile."

"I go by several names," I said. "Most of them recently acquired."

"It bid for you." The mouth smiled, and the smile fit the face the way a glove fits a hand: perfectly, and with nothing inside. "At auction. Three nights hence. I intend to win."

"Everyone intends to win," I said. "It's the bidding that sorts us out."

I should have been terrified. I was, a little—terror is just respect wearing its work clothes. But mostly I was doing what I always do when something enormous looks at me: I listened past it. And beneath that smooth, appetite-warm surface, beneath the crown-shaped thing that wore this man like a coat it had grown fond of, there it was again. That syllable. That drowned voice. *No. Don't.*

Two voices in one king. I had heard two voices in trades before—sellers who regretted, buyers who lied—but never like this. Never one voice pleading from inside the other's chest.

"Your Majesty requires rest," announced the chamberlain, and began herding me toward the door, and I went, because I am not stupid, and because I had learned what I needed: the entity could not fully silence him. It could wear him, feed through him, reign through him—but somewhere down in that bottomless well, a coin still rang when it hit water.

I came back the next night. And the next. I told the chamberlain the soothing required repetition, which was even true, though not in the way she believed. Each night I sang the cage-song—not the whole of it, never the whole of it, Maren would have flayed me—but fragments, phrases, the musical equivalent of leaving a window open. And each night, at the bottom of the king, that voice grew a little louder. A word here. A breath there. A whole sentence, once, spoken into my singing the way a drowning man speaks into a storm: *it dreams my dreams for me.*

Yes, I thought. Yes. I know exactly how that feels. There is a version of every one of us that the world dreams on our behalf—the obedient daughter, the reliable singer, the girl who soothes—and some mornings it takes real work to remember which thoughts were ours. I sang to him about that. About the long labor of taking your own mind back, one stolen inch at a time. I did not know if he could understand me. I sang anyway. Singing to someone who may or may not hear is the entire job description of a choir-singer; we are professionals at hope conducted blind.

On the fifth night, the entity was waiting for me.

Not angry. Worse: courteous. It sat the king up against his pillows like a doll arranged for company, and it spoke at length, in a voice gone silky with patience, about how tired Aldric was, how the human part of him had eroded past usefulness, how it would be a mercy to let the remainder go. How it would even permit it—if I stopped coming. If I stopped leaving windows open in a house it owned.

"It suffers," it said, using his lips, borrowing his tenderness the way a thief borrows a coat. "You prolong the suffering, little bird. Is that compassion?"

And here is what I have learned about parasites, having now met the founding member of the species: they always describe their host's pain as evidence for their own necessity. Vess did it. Mother Yew's bargains did it. Half the trades that crossed the guild's counters did it. *You were suffering; I relieved you of the burden; the burden is mine now; be grateful.* The oldest arithmetic in the world, and it never once balanced.

"He doesn't suffer," I said. "He's buried. There's a difference. Suffering ends. Buried things wait."

The eyes flickered—annoyance, quick as a fish turning—and for one half of one second the face above me was not smooth. It was lined. Tired. Human. Fifty-odd years of a man looking out through his own eyes from the bottom of a well, and then the surface closed over him again, and the thing wearing him said, gently, in his voice:

"You cannot reach him. No one has reached him in thirty years."

Thirty years. I wrote the number on the inside of my skull where I keep everything that matters, which is a crowded room these days.

* * *

Maren was not pleased with me. Maren is never pleased with me; it is one of the stable pleasures of my life, like bad wine and cold crypts. But when I told her the king was reachable—thirty years buried, not thirty years gone—she went quiet in a way that made the whole crypt go quiet with her, eleven surviving singers and their collective held breath.

"The rite needs consent," she said finally. "That has always been the flaw in it. The binding runs through the crown, and the crown runs through the king's own wish—the first wish, the one his grandfather's grandfather made. Immortality, bought with his line's futures. We can unmake any binding in the world except one freely entered. To unbind it, the wisher must unsay it. Or his heir. Or—" she looked at me, and her old eyes did something complicated— "or whoever can win the wisher's living consent. The consent of the man himself. Not the vessel. The man."

"And no one's managed that in thirty years."

"People have tried. Singers better than me. They came back hollow-eyed and some of them didn't come back at all." She touched my wrist, briefly, the way she touches everything—as if checking whether it's still real. "The entity learns, Odile. After the failed rite, after what it took from us—it listens now. It hears melody the way a spider hears vibration. If you go down into that well singing anything it recognizes, it will simply eat the song before it reaches him."

"So I don't sing."

She stared at me.

"I talk," I said. "I sit on the edge of his bed like a friend at a deathbed and I talk. Plain words. No melody, no structure, nothing with a spine for it to grip. Songs are cages and it knows all the cages. Words are just—words. Messy. Unbound. Terrible rhythm. It can't eat what has no shape."

Maren was silent for a long moment. Then she laughed—one dry note, like a stick breaking—and said, "You are either going to save us or get unmade, and I genuinely cannot tell which, and I find I don't care, because both options are more interesting than sitting here."

High praise, from Maren. I have decided to treasure it.

* * *

So that is how I spent the next six nights: talking to a king while a parasite listened through his ears and pretended not to.

I talked about nothing, at first. The weather, which in the capital is a rich topic because the weather is frequently illegal. The taste of the palace bread, which I had sampled from a tray and found guilty of ambition. The chamberlain's extraordinary collection of coughs, each deployed with tactical precision. I filled the room with small, shapeless, harmless words, and the entity grew bored—I could feel it grow bored, a sort of atmospheric pressure change—and drifted up out of attention the way a cat leaves a room. And then, in the space boredom left behind, I changed the subject.

"Aldric," I said, quietly. Just the name. Just the name, into the dark above the pillows.

Nothing. Then—so faint I might have invented it—a catch in the breathing.

"My name is Odile. I'm not here to soothe you. I'm here because I think you're in there, and I think you've been in there for thirty years, and I think that is the cruelest thing I have ever heard of, and I have read the guild's ledgers, so the bar is high."

The breathing caught again. Held. Released.

I kept talking. Night after night. I told him about the crypt, about Maren's terrible tea, about Rafael Crane—a clerk with ink-stained fingers and a face like a man perpetually apologizing to furniture—who had walked into our lives carrying a prophecy book and a guilt the size of a cathedral. I told him about the choir, dwindling year by year, singers spending their voices like the last coins of a failing house. I told him things I have never told anyone: about my mother selling my voice's future before I was born, about the first time I hummed a staircase and felt the world obey, about how lonely it is to be useful. I talked the way people talk to the dying, which is to say honestly, because what was left to lose?

And slowly, at the bottom of the well, the coin-ringing became a voice, and the voice became a man.

He couldn't speak, not at first. He answered in the only currency he had: breath. One breath for yes. Stillness for no. It was the slowest conversation of my life, and I have negotiated with Maren over ration portions.

*Are you in pain?* — a long, ragged breath. Yes. Thirty years of yes.

*Does it hurt all the time?* — a breath, then stillness, then a breath. Sometimes. Sometimes it lets him sleep, and sleeping is the only place it doesn't follow, and even then it waits at the edge of the dream like a creditor at a door.

*Do you know what you are?* — stillness. Long stillness. Then one shuddering breath, and I understood it meant: *I know what I let myself become.*

That was the night I cried, sitting on the edge of a king's bed at three in the morning with guards outside the door and a monster inside the man, crying for someone I'd known for six nights and who had known himself for none of them. Because that answer—that one breath—was confession. Thirty years of complicity, acknowledged from the bottom of a well, to a stranger. Kings don't confess. Whatever else was down there, it wasn't a king anymore. It was just a man who had said yes once, terribly, and had been paying installments ever since.

* * *

The entity noticed, of course. You cannot spend six nights watering a buried garden without the thing that paved over the garden noticing green shoots. On the ninth night it sat up—literally sat up, arranged its borrowed body upright, regal and awful—and spoke to me directly, and this time it did not offer mercy. It offered history.

"Do you know what he wished for?" it asked, in Aldric's voice, wearing Aldric's nostalgia like a mask at a masque. "He was twenty-two. His father was dead, his kingdom was bleeding, his enemies were at three borders, and he was going to die before spring and everyone he loved would burn for it. So he wished. He wished to live long enough to protect them. That is all. That is the whole crime. A boy who wanted to save his people." The mouth curved. "And I said yes. I have never broken a promise, little bird. Ask your elder Maren. Every singer in your crypt exists because I keep my promises. Can your precious choir say the same?"

And the horrible thing—the thing I turned over all night afterward, the way you turn over a stone knowing what's under it—is that it wasn't lying. That's the trap of it, the genius of it. The wish had been good. The wish had been *kind*. A young man staring down annihilation had reached for the only rope available, and the rope had been alive, and it had held, and everyone he loved had lived, and the price had simply been deferred, deferred, deferred, until the bill arrived addressed to his descendants and his own interior, and by then the kindness of the original act had become the lock on the cage. No villainy required. Just a yes, given in good faith, to something that does not have faith in any direction.

I have spent my life around bargains. I know how they rot. But I had never before stood close enough to smell the exact moment of it—the instant a rescue becomes a possession—and it smelled like this room. Like clean linen and banked fires and a man's own voice used fluently against him.

"You're wondering," the entity said, settling back against the pillows, languid, victorious, "what you can possibly offer him that I haven't. He has life. He has peace, of a kind. He has significance. And you have—" it considered me with those portrait-wrong eyes— "a cracked voice and a clerk and a book of ashes. Why would he choose your arithmetic over mine?"

"Because yours doesn't end," I said. "Mine does."

* * *

The tenth night, I brought no arguments. Arguments are for entities. I brought a blanket.

The palace blankets, I had noticed, were ceremonial—weightless, ornamental, useless, like everything else in that room. So I smuggled in one of the crypt's rough grey wools, smelling of candle smoke and stone, and I laid it over the king's feet, and I said, "This is what real things feel like. In case you'd forgotten. I forget too, sometimes."

And then I sat down on the floor beside the bed—on the floor, below him, deliberately, because kings spend their whole lives being looked up at and I suspected he was tired of it—and I said:

"Aldric. I need to ask you for the hardest yes there is. Harder than the one you gave at twenty-two. I need you to say no—to the wish. To unsay it. To choose an ending."

The breathing stopped entirely.

"I know what I'm asking. I know you said yes to save them, and it worked, and they lived, and their children lived, and this kingdom stands because a frightened boy made a bargain with a thing in the dark. I know that letting go of the wish feels like letting go of everyone you saved. It isn't. They're grown. They're gone. They built lives on top of your sacrifice without ever knowing it was there, and they would be sick with grief if they did, and not one of them would ask you to keep paying. Only the parasite asks that. Only the creditor insists the debt is eternal."

Stillness. The terrible, listening stillness of a man weighing thirty years against forever.

"Maren thinks the rite can work now. Rafael found the cipher—all debts forgiven or repaid, simultaneously, and the king's own consent is the final entry. Your consent. The last signature on the last page." My voice did something unsteady, and I let it. "You would die. Truly die. Mortal, finite, done. No more watching centuries through someone else's eyes. No more being a coat. Just—an ending. A real one. The thing you were promised at birth and never got."

A breath. Shallow. Then another. I leaned closer, put my hand lightly on the wool over his feet, and waited, the way you wait at a deathbed, the way you wait at a birth, the way you wait for anything that matters, which is to say badly and completely.

And the voice from the bottom of the well—rusty, cracked, thirty years out of practice, smaller than any king's voice has ever been—said:

"What... happens... to them? The singers. The ones... still holding... the cage-song."

I nearly broke. Of all the questions. Of all the things a buried man might ask, freed for one moment to speak—he asked after the choir. After the strangers who had spent their lives paying for his mistake.

"They'll be free," I said. "Voiceless, most of us, for a while. Maybe forever, some of us. It will cost us everything we have. But we chose it. That's the difference between us and you, Aldric—we get to choose it. And we're choosing you. A man none of us ever met. Isn't that absurd?" I laughed, wetly. "We're going to bankrupt ourselves rescuing a stranger, because Maren says that's what the song is *for*, and Maren is terrifying, and we believe her."

Silence. Long silence. The fire burned without crackling. Somewhere beyond the door, a guard shifted his weight, oblivious, guarding a doorway between a monster and a miracle and aware of neither.

Then: "If I say yes..."

"—we finish it. All of it. The same hour. Rafael is seeing to the debts tonight." I did not mention the ledgers, or the fire being planned, or the fact that a nervous clerk with beautiful handwriting intended to commit the largest arson in guild history before dawn. Some consolations are kinder withheld. "You will not be alone when it happens. I will be singing. Well—" I touched my own throat, which ached in advance of what it would soon give up— "I will be making noise. Someone else will be finishing the song. But I'll be there. I promise you that. And unlike your creditor, I keep my promises in the ordinary human way: imperfectly, briefly, and with my whole heart."

The breath came again. Deeper now. Steadier. And with it, word by word, dragged up from thirty years underwater, the true King Aldric of this kingdom—which is to say, the only part of him that had ever really ruled—spoke his second great decision, and his last:

"Yes."

One word. The hardest yes. I felt it land in the room like a stone dropped into still water, and I felt the thing that wore him recoil through every wall, every guard, every gilded log in the fire—the whole palace flinching at once, a building-sized shudder, because somewhere in its ancient ledger an entry had just been marked *disputed*, and it had not been disputed in three hundred years.

The eyes opened. Brown, and kind, and—for one unguarded moment before the surface closed—entirely, unmistakably, human.

"Thank you," the man at the bottom of the well whispered. "Tell the clerk... tell him... hurry."

I was already moving. Down the servants' stair, through the cold, past the sleeping city, toward the crypt where Maren waited with ten singers and a rite we were not ready to perform and would perform anyway. Toward Rafael, wherever he was, with whatever fire he was carrying.

The king had said yes. Now everything else had to happen at once.
