## 19. The Last Note

*Odile*

The throne room of Aldric's palace had been built to make visitors feel small, and it succeeded even on the dead. I knew because there were dead in it now — three of my sisters from the choir, unmade two nights past, and their absence sat in the vaulted dark like a fourth presence, colder than any of the living.

Maren had wanted to wait. Maren had wanted a fortnight, a full moon, better odds, all the things you want when you have spent sixty years singing a cage-song and cannot bear to imagine the silence after. But the entity had learned my melody. Every hour we waited, it sang it back to itself behind the king's teeth, perfecting it, wearing it smooth like a river stone. Waiting was just dying slowly with extra steps.

So we went at dawn, which is when all the worst ideas in history have been executed, and dawn found us standing on the long approach to the throne dais with our breath steaming and our hands shaking, and Rafael Crane — clerk, coward, arsonist, and the only man alive whose name no longer belonged to anyone but himself — holding a scrap of burnt ledger paper like it was a shield.

"It's going to try to bargain," he said for the fifth time.

"Then it can take a number," I said, "and wait in line."

He almost smiled. That was the thing about Rafael; terror lived in him the way weather lives in a valley, always passing through, never quite settling, and somehow he kept walking anyway. I had spent years reading voices the way other people read faces, and his was the strangest instrument I'd ever heard: a voice that had been sold once and bought back, and now rang true the way an empty room rings true.

"The choir takes the outer verse," Maren murmured beside me. There were nine of us left. Nine voices against a thing that had eaten kings. The arithmetic was obscene. "You carry the unbinding. If your voice goes—"

"If my voice goes," I said, "then someone finishes the song."

"Who?"

I looked at Rafael. He looked at me. His face did something complicated, the way a ledger does when the columns finally balance and the total is worse than you feared.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, that's what she meant."

Mother Yew's last gift, spent in a cell that smelled of salt and mildew: *the debt is repaid, boy, and a repaid name is a clean instrument.* I hadn't understood it either, not fully, until this moment. But understanding arrived the way most terrible things do — punctually.

The great doors at the far end of the throne room were open. No one had ordered them open. Beyond them, the city lay grey under ash-fall from the burning guild, and the light came in long and low across the marble, and at the end of that corridor of light sat the king.

Aldric sat very still on his throne, hands flat on the arms, the way a man sits when he is holding himself down. He was not old — fifty, perhaps — but the thing inside him had been wearing him for thirty years, and wear shows. His eyes were wrong in the way that a house is wrong when every curtain is drawn at noon.

And beneath the stillness, I heard them. Both of them.

The entity spoke first, and its voice was beautiful, which is the foulest trick it had. It sounded like every choir I had ever loved, blended into one impossible note.

*Little singer,* it said, without moving its stolen mouth. *You came all this way. Sit. Rest. I remember your grandmother's voice. She had a laugh like bells. Would you like to hear it? I kept it.*

"You keep nothing," I said. "You borrow everything, and you never give it back."

*I pay,* it said, wounded, reasonable, monstrous. *I have paid this kingdom a century of stability. A century of no wars, no famines, no hard winters. Ask the ledgers. Oh — forgive me. You burned the ledgers.* Something moved behind the king's face, a suggestion of amusement, like a fish turning under ice. *Yes. We heard the counting-house. Loud, your clerk. Sloppy, too. Fire forgives nothing, you know. It only erases the evidence.*

Behind me, one of the younger singers made a small frightened sound. Maren's hand found her shoulder.

The second voice came then, faint as a knock through a wall. The human voice. Aldric's voice, buried so deep I could barely find it, and it said only:

*Please.*

One word. Thirty years of one word. I felt it go through me like a splinter, and I thought of every trade I had ever settled for the guild, every volatile bargain smoothed over with a hummed chord, and how none of it had ever been music, really. Music asks. This thing only took.

I stepped forward onto the dais steps, and the nine of us arranged ourselves around me in the crescent Maren had drilled into us since midnight, and I did the last thing I would ever do with the voice my mother gave me.

I began to sing.

* * *

*Rafael*

Here is what nobody tells you about watching the woman you love sing a god apart: it is exactly like accounting.

Every verse was a column. Every held note was a figure being carried forward. And the entity fought her the way bad debt fights collection — first with charm, then with confusion, then with naked force. I stood at the foot of the dais with ash still in my hair from the counting-house fire and watched Odile's voice peel the king open layer by layer, and I understood, with the clarity that only comes to clerks at the end of a very long night, that everything I had ever done with ink had been rehearsal for this.

The entity screamed first through the courtiers' mouths. There were courtiers — of course there were; the palace guards had fled or been dismissed, but a handful of the king's household had come at dawn out of loyalty or curiosity, and now they turned as one, and their throats produced a sound like a hundred ledgers tearing.

*STOP,* they said, in chorus, in the entity's borrowed beauty. *SINGER. I WILL GIVE YOU BACK YOUR SISTERS. ALL THREE. WHOLE. THEY ARE ONLY FOLDED AWAY—*

"Liar," Maren said, steady as a metronome. "We know what unmade means."

*THEN THE CLERK,* the chorus said, and a hundred dead eyes found me, and I confess my knees had opinions. *THE CLERK WHO BURNED MY BOOKS. I WILL GIVE HIM WHAT HE ALWAYS WANTED. A NAME THAT NO ONE REMEMBERS. PERFECT INVISIBILITY. HE CAN VANISH SO COMPLETELY EVEN HE WILL FORGET—*

"That," I said, and my voice cracked, and I made myself say it again, louder, because some things you have to say twice to believe, "is the first honest offer anyone in this kingdom has ever made me. And no."

Odile didn't stop singing. Later I would learn that she had heard every word, that hearing while singing is part of what choir-singers train for, but in the moment she simply rose through the verses like a swimmer rising through dark water toward light, and the outer verse caught her and carried her, nine voices braided around the cage-song's final inversion, and the king began to come apart.

Not his body. His *wearing*. I saw it happen and I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe it. The entity rose out of him the way smoke rises off a snuffed candle — reluctantly, in threads, in shapes that kept almost becoming things: a crown, a hand, a mouth, a ledger page covered in entries in handwriting I recognized. It had been writing itself into him for thirty years, entry by entry, and Odile's song was calling those entries due.

Aldric slumped forward on the throne. And then — this is the part I will tell my grandchildren, if I am ever foolish enough to have any — the king lifted his head, and his own eyes looked out of his own face for the first time in thirty years, and he was crying.

"Is it—" he rasped. "Am I—"

"You're coming back," Odile sang, between notes, weaving words into the verse the way Mother Yew must have woven hers a century ago. "Your consent holds the door. Say it, majesty. Say you let it go."

"I let it go," Aldric whispered. "God forgive me. I let it go. I was so tired. I only wanted—"

"—to live," Odile finished, and the word became a note, and the note became the hinge of the whole song, and the entity tore free.

It did not leave gently. The windows blew inward. The banners ripped down. Three of our nine singers dropped mid-verse — not dead, *unmade*, folded away like the entity had promised, gone so completely that the air where they'd stood didn't even remember them — and the outer verse staggered, and Maren's voice bent double holding the line with six where there should have been nine, and the entity stood in the ruined light of the throne room in a shape it had stopped bothering to make beautiful, and it turned on Odile.

And it sang her own melody back at her.

That was the trap. That was what it had been doing all those hours behind the king's teeth — learning her, the way I had once learned Vess's cipher, the way any good clerk learns a system in order to own it. It hit her unbinding back at her, inverted, a mirror-note, and I saw it strike her like a physical blow, saw her voice *shatter* — not fade, shatter, the way glass shatters, all at once and along every possible line — and the song stopped.

The silence after was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

The entity laughed. It laughed with the whole room, with the walls, with the stones, and it reached for her, and I did the only arithmetic left.

*A repaid name is a clean instrument.*

Mother Yew had died buying that sentence, and I finally understood what she'd purchased. My name had been the first payment the guild ever made to this thing — my apprenticeship, my selfhood, signed over before I could write my own signature. And it had been forgiven. Repaid in full, in a salt-smelling cell, by a dying woman's last breath. Which meant that of everyone in this room, I was the only soul present with no outstanding claims against me. No debts. No bindings. Nothing for the entity to grip.

An unclaimed name is just a sound. And a sound, in the right throat, at the right moment, is a note.

I walked up the dais steps. My legs worked, which seemed rude of them given how much I wanted to faint. The entity's attention swung to me and I felt it *read* me — felt it flip through me like a clerk flipping through a ledger looking for the page with the numbers, and finding blank paper, blank paper, blank paper all the way through.

"What are you," it said, and for the first time its voice wasn't beautiful. It was confused. Confusion, in a thing that had worn a king for thirty years, is very close to fear.

"A clerk," I said. "Balance owed: nothing. Balance held: nothing." I knelt beside Odile. Blood had run from one ear; her lips were moving around a verse with no voice left to carry it, and her eyes found mine and were full of horror — at herself, at the cost, at whatever she thought I was about to do.

"Don't," she mouthed. "Rafael. You don't know the verse."

"No," I agreed. "But I've read it. Fourteen times. In her hand." I put my hand on the back of her neck, the way you steady a trembling bird, and I leaned toward the entity, and I opened my mouth, and I sang.

I will not pretend it was good. It was, by every technical measure, the worst singing ever performed in that throne room, including by the entity, including possibly by the architecture. My pitch wandered. My breath failed. But the verse doesn't need beauty — Mother Yew's cipher had told me that much, the fine print beneath the fine print: *the unbinding requires only a voice that owes nothing and means it.* And I meant it. Every syllable. I meant the forgiveness of every debt the thing had ever collected, the storm in the bottle, the widow's shadow, the borrowed years, the names sold at auction, the futures of kings — all of it called due and cancelled, written off, *burned*, the way I had burned the master ledgers, and my voice — my plain, unpaid, unremarkable human voice — carried the cancellation into the thing like fire carries into dry paper.

The entity screamed. Not with the room this time. With itself, alone, shrinking, the borrowed centuries peeling off it like pages in flame, and the last thing it did — I will grant it this — was reach for the one debt it still believed it held.

*HIS NAME,* it shrieked, clawing at me. *THE FIRST PAYMENT. I HOLD HIS NAME. IT WAS SIGNED—*

"Forgiven," I sang, and hit the note clean for the first time in my life. "Repaid in full. Check your books."

And the books, of course, were ash.

It came apart. All at once, the way a held breath comes apart, and the throne room filled with a rush of returning — I cannot call it anything else. Somewhere in the city, I knew, three singers were unfolding back into existence. Somewhere a jar of laughter was just a jar, a widow's shadow was back on her shoulders, a hundred stolen names were flying home to their owners like birds released at dusk. The debt of a century, settled in a single verse, by the two least likely instruments in the kingdom: a singer with no voice and a clerk with no claim.

Then it was quiet, and the quiet was real, and I sat down on the dais steps next to Odile and discovered I was shaking too hard to stand.

She was looking at me. Her mouth was working. What came out was a whisper, thread-thin, half a syllable at a time — the wreck of the finest voice in the kingdom, spending itself on one word:

"You... finished... it."

"We finished it," I said. "You wrote the verse. I just did the bookkeeping."

Her whisper came again, and this time it was almost a laugh, which is the closest thing to harmony two people can make with almost nothing left:

"Clerks."

Above us, King Aldric wept on his throne like a man stepping out of a thirty-year winter, and Maren was laughing and sobbing and counting heads, and through the broken windows the spring light was coming up over the ashes of the Ledger Guild, and somewhere in the crypt below the city the old cage-song was unwinding from the stones at last, leaving behind it a silence that was not empty but *open* — a rest, in the musical sense, waiting for whatever we chose to write into it next.

Odile tugged my sleeve. Her whisper was fading; each word cost her more than the last. But she got out four more, and they were the best four words I had ever heard anyone spend:

"Teach me... to sing... again."

I took her hand. Outside, the first day of the rest of the kingdom was beginning.

"All right," I said. "But we start with the scales. I'm told I'm a terrible teacher."

She smiled — a whole smile, costing her nothing, because smiles are free — and leaned her head against my shoulder, and together, in the ruins of the throne room, the last note of the old world faded out, and the first rest of the new one began.
