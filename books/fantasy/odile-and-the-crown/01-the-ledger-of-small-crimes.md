## 1. The Ledger of Small Crimes

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in a room with no windows, on the theory that sunlight was bad for the ink. Beatrix Delacroix had worked there for six years and had come to believe the theory was nonsense, but she kept her opinions the way she kept everything else—neatly filed and out of sight.

"Delacroix," said Mistress Ivo from the doorway of the ledger room, "the Vellum Street accounts are a month behind."

"They are not a month behind, mistress. They are a month *creative*."

Ivo's eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch, which for her was the equivalent of a standing ovation. She was a small, dry woman built like a filing cabinet, with spectacles that magnified her eyes to the size of a disappointed owl's. "Explain."

Beatrix turned the ledger around and tapped the offending column with her pen. "Junior Clerk Fenwick has been recording the acquisition of 'miscellaneous curiosities' at a flat rate of two crowns each. Mistress, no curiosity in this city costs two crowns. A curiosity costs either nothing, because it is a rock, or it costs four hundred crowns and a favor owed to a man with a knife collection. There is no middle ground. Curiosities are like cats that way."

"Fix it."

"Already fixing it. I've written to Fenwick requesting itemized descriptions. He'll either produce them or resign, and either way the Guild wins." She smiled her ledger-keeper's smile—warm, competent, and entirely without depth. "The Guild always wins, if the arithmetic is honest."

"The arithmetic is never honest," Ivo said. "That's why we have you."

She left. Beatrix watched her go, then turned back to the columns and let her smile fade into something more private: the small, fierce satisfaction of a woman who was very good at a job nobody knew was difficult. Keeping honest books for a Guild of impossible things was, in her private opinion, the most impossible thing of all. The Guild bought memories and sold forgetting; it brokered in last words and first loves; it held, in vaults beneath the city, the recollections of people who had paid to be rid of them. Every transaction had to be recorded. Every record had to balance. And every record, if you knew how to read it, was a confession.

Beatrix knew how to read them. She also knew how to write a few of her own.

She finished the Vellum Street corrections, blotted the page, locked the ledger in its drawer, and went home to have supper and a nap, because she had a job that night, and she was a great believer in being rested for one's crimes.

* * *

The dying man lived above a chandler's shop in Tallow Row, in a room that smelled of tallow and camphor and the particular staleness of a place where someone had been ill for a long time. His name, according to the Guild's contract, was Warden Oren Sable, retired, and he was dying of a slow failure that the physicians had declined to name, on the theory that naming it would make it official.

Beatrix arrived at the ninth bell, let herself in with the key the Guild had provided, and found him awake, propped against pillows, watching her with the flat calm of a man who had made his arrangements.

"You're the thief," he said.

"I'm the *acquisition specialist*," Beatrix said. "The Guild is very particular about the terminology. It's in my contract."

"Thief," he said again, but without heat. "Sit down, girl. I've been dying for three weeks. I'd like to get on with the interesting part."

She sat. Up close he was a ruin of a man, all tendons and weathered skin, but his eyes were steady, and there was something about the set of his shoulders that suggested a man who had spent a lifetime standing in a specific place on purpose. Wardens did that. Beatrix had read about them in the ledgers—the conscripted ones, the ones who stood the border and watched the dark beyond it. The ledgers recorded their pensions. The ledgers did not record what they watched.

"You know what I'm here for," she said, unpacking her kit: the silver needle, the vial of grey light, the little brass instrument like a tuning fork crossed with a key.

"The Guild bought my memories. All of them. Everything from my service." He said it the way a man reads a bill of sale. "Forty years at the border, and they're paying my widow eleven crowns a year and taking the only part of me that was worth anything."

"That's the contract," Beatrix said, and hated how smoothly it came out. "You signed it."

"I signed it because the alternative was my family starves." He turned his head and looked at her, and there was something in the look that made her hands go still. "You've done this before."

"Many times."

"No. I mean—" he lifted a trembling finger— "you've done this *to yourself*. I can see it. There are holes in you, girl. Big ones, clean-cut, like a hedge that's been trimmed. Nobody's memory has edges like that unless they cut it themselves."

Beatrix kept her face pleasant and her hands steady, and did not think about the sleeve, or about the morning three months ago when she had woken with a bruise shaped like a handprint on her own wrist and no memory of how it got there. "You're dying," she said gently. "Dying men see holes in everything."

He laughed, which turned into coughing, which he rode out with the grim patience of long practice. "Do it, then. Take the lot. But do an old man one courtesy first. Let me tell you what's in here, so somebody knows, even if it's only for a minute."

"Sir, the contract—"

"The contract sells the memory. It doesn't sell the telling." His eyes glinted. "You can bill me for the conversation."

She should have refused. It was in her professional interest to refuse; a client's deathbed confession was not in the ledger, and things not in the ledger had a way of growing roots. But she was tired, and he was dying, and some small honest thing in her—the thing she kept filed away even from herself—wanted to hear it.

"Talk, then," she said.

He talked. He told her about the border wards, the great woven lights that ran the length of the kingdom's edge, and how they were not fed by the king's magicians, as the ballads said, but by the wardens themselves—by their memories, poured out year after year into the weave. He told her that the wardens who came back from the border came back *lighter*, and that the Guild called it a pension bonus and paid it in silence. He told her that the stars over the border were not stars at all, but knots in the weave, and that the wardens had a saying: *when the sky goes out, the ledger's due.*

"The sky goes out?" Beatrix asked, threading the needle. "What does that mean?"

"Means the bill comes due," he said. "Means the thing on the other side collects." He closed his eyes. "I watched it collect once. Forty years ago. One night, no stars, and the dark came right up to the wards like a dog to a fence, and it *smelled* us. And then the stars came back and everyone said it was a cloud, and the Guild paid us all a bonus to say it was a cloud." He opened his eyes again. "I never spent the bonus. Seemed like blood money. Now they're taking the memory of it anyway, so I suppose the joke's on me."

Beatrix's hands had gone cold. She was, by trade and temperament, a skeptic; she kept books, and books did not deal in dogs at fences or stars that were knots. But she had also, in six years of ledgers, seen the pension bonuses. Hundreds of them. Paid to retired wardens, every one, in the same month of the year, with a code in the margin she had always assumed was clerical.

The code was *SKY-1*.

"Ready?" she said, and her voice was almost steady.

"Ready." He lay back. "Girl. One more thing. When you cut it out of yourself tonight—the memory of me, of this—leave yourself a note. Write down that Oren Sable told the truth. Somewhere you'll find it. Promise me."

"That's not how the procedure—"

"Promise me," he said, "and I'll die easier, and you can bill my ghost for the favor."

She promised. She did not know yet whether she meant it.

The theft itself was quick, when she finally did it. The needle found the seam; the brass instrument turned; the grey light came up out of him in a long, slow ribbon, forty years of border and dark and dog-at-the-fence, and she coiled it into the vial and sealed it with wax. He watched her do it with the calm of a man watching a debt get paid, and at the end he smiled.

"Better," he murmured. "Lighter. They were right about that, at least." His eyes were already going soft. "What was your name, girl?"

"Beatrix," she said, and then, because it seemed suddenly important: "Beatrix Delacroix."

"Good name," said Warden Oren Sable. "Keep it."

She was three streets away before she let herself cry, and she was angry about it, because crying was a leak, and leaks were unprofessional.

* * *

The erasure she performed at her own kitchen table, as she did every time, by lamplight, with the ritual precision of a woman saying her prayers. The needle turned inward. The grey light came up out of her own head—the job, the room, the chandler's smell, the dying man and his dog-at-the-fence and his *keep it*—and she coiled it away into the second vial, the private one, the safety net she told herself she would never need.

This was the method, and the method was the whole of her safety. A thief who could not remember stealing could not confess, could not be tortured into testimony, could not lie awake at three in the morning composing the story she would tell the enforcers. She had built the method herself, carefully, over years, and it had never once failed her.

The needle came out. The light went into the vial. She sealed it, labeled it with a date and a cipher, and put it in the floorboard with its sisters—eleven of them now, a little grey constellation of her own crimes.

Then she washed her face, went to bed, and slept the dreamless sleep of the successfully emptied.

She woke at dawn with the light in her eyes and a strange taste in her mouth, and she lay there for a moment doing her usual inventory: name, trade, address, the sense of a nap well spent. All present. All correct.

Then she sat up, because her sleeve was stiff.

Blood. A dark crescent of it, dried to brown, on the cuff of her left sleeve—the good sleeve, the one she'd worn to work yesterday, the one she distinctly remembered being clean. There wasn't much. A smear, a press of fingers. As if she had held something—or someone—and let go.

Beatrix sat very still and ran through the evening as she remembered it: supper, the walk, the lamplight, the needle, the vial, bed. It all balanced. It all *arithmetic'd*. And yet here was blood on her sleeve, and here, when she finally looked, was a scrap of paper on the floor beside her shoe that had not been there when she went to sleep.

She picked it up. Her own handwriting, hurried, the letters leaning like drunks:

*Oren Sable told the truth. Don't forget again.*

She had no idea who Oren Sable was.

Outside her window, the morning bell rang, and somewhere far to the north—though she could not have said how she knew, and would spend the next day refusing to wonder—something in the sky was very quietly going out.
