## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

Beatrix Delacroix had a theory about panic, which was that it behaved exactly like spilled ink: everyone assumed it would spread everywhere at once, but in truth it pooled in corners, soaked into the cheapest cloth first, and left the important documents untouched until someone had the presence of mind to move them.

She moved them.

By the time the third bell of the night rang out over Corvane — not the usual measured toll but the frantic, arrhythmic clanging that meant *something is wrong and nobody knows what* — Beatrix was already downstairs in the Guild's records hall with her sleeves pinned back and her ledger open to the contingency pages. The contingency pages were her favorite part of any ledger. They were where the Guild admitted, in its own dry way, that the impossible things it collected were not always contained.

"Delacroix!" bellowed Master Fenwick from the doorway, his nightcap still on and his dignity already lost somewhere on the stairs. "Why are you calm? Everyone else is screaming."

"Screaming doesn't balance columns," Beatrix said. "If we're all going to die horribly, I'd prefer the accounts be current when they find us."

This was, she reflected, only half a joke, which made it the most honest thing she'd said all week.

Fenwick gaped at her, decided this was normal, and fled toward the panic he understood better.

The truth was that Beatrix's calm had a seam in it. She could feel it, the way one feels a loose thread in a coat sleeve — a small wrongness tugging at the edge of every composed thought. She knew why, of course. She always knew why, in the general sense: last night there had been a job, and this morning there had been blood on her sleeve, and between those two facts lay a hole shaped like several hours of her life. She had woken, washed the sleeve, eaten breakfast, and walked to work, and she had done all of it without once asking herself the question she could feel circling like a moth around a lamp: *what did I take?*

She never asked. Asking was how you ended up chasing your own tail through other people's memories. She had rules. Rule one: finish the job. Rule two: forget the job. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, go looking for what you'd forgotten, because the person who went looking was never quite the person who came back.

The bells kept ringing. Somewhere overhead — she felt it rather than heard it, a pressure behind the eyes like a held breath — something enormous was happening to the sky.

* * *

By midnight, the city of Corvane had done what cities do, which is to say it had split neatly into three camps: those praying, those looting, and those selling things to the first two groups at extortionate prices.

Beatrix stood at the Guild's tall eastern window with Mistress Ivo's deputy, a nervous young clerk named Pell who kept adjusting his collar as though the sky's condition were somehow his fault.

"You can see it from here," Pell whispered. "Or — you can't see it. That's the whole problem."

He was right. The stars were gone. Not clouded over, not dimmed — gone, every one, as though the night had been rolled up like a rug and carried off. Where the constellation of the Wardens' Chain should have hung above the northern border, there was nothing but a black so complete it looked less like absence than like intention. The moon, mercifully, remained, hanging pale and embarrassed, the last coin in an empty purse.

Below them, torches bloomed across the city like fever spots. People were pouring into the streets, some toward the temples and some away from them. From the docks came the sound of a ship's captain very loudly refusing to sail anywhere ever again.

"The border wards," Pell said. "That's what everyone's saying. That the wards failed."

"Wards don't fail," Beatrix said.

It came out of her mouth before she had decided to say it — flat, certain, with the particular weight of a thing known rather than believed. She heard herself say it and went cold.

Pell blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"I mean—" She rearranged her face into the pleasant, unremarkable expression she wore like a uniform. "I mean, presumably that's what the engineers will determine. I keep ledgers, Pell. Stars are above my pay grade. Literally."

But the sentence sat in her chest afterward like a swallowed stone. *Wards don't fail.* And beneath it, faint as writing under water, something else stirred — not a memory, because she did not have memories of last night, but the *shape* of one, the way a missing tooth leaves a ridge in the gum. A voice. An old man's voice, or a man made old by duty, saying something urgent against the dark, and the urgency of it pressing up through the hole she'd cut in herself like grass through pavement.

She pressed her knuckles briefly to her temple, a gesture she told herself was just tiredness.

*Wards don't fail,* the shape insisted. *They're paid for.*

Paid for. What an odd way to think about wards.

"Delacroix?" Pell said. "You've stopped writing."

She looked down. Her pen had been moving. On the margin of the contingency page, in her own neat hand, she had written a single line:

*Ask what the wards were bought with.*

She did not remember deciding to write it. She did not know what it meant. With great care, and a feeling like stepping over something asleep, she drew a single clean stroke through it and turned the page.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things pretended business as usual the way a duck pretends to be serene — furiously, and entirely beneath the surface.

At dawn, the senior staff assembled in the long room with the bad lighting, and the acting Master of Ledgers read a prepared statement announcing that the disappearance of the stars was "a matter under review" and that "all guild operations would continue without interruption." This was received with the solemn nodding of people who had all seen the sky and none of them wished to discuss it.

Beatrix took minutes. It was good cover, taking minutes; it gave her a reason to watch everyone's face while pretending to watch her paper.

Master Fenwick's fear was genuine and loud. Deputy Quillon's fear was genuine and quiet. But old Mistress Ambrose, who handled acquisitions, wore the specific gray look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like — and when the statement mentioned the border wards, Ambrose's pen stopped moving for exactly three seconds before resuming.

Three seconds. Beatrix wrote down the time stamp and hated herself a little for it.

Because here was the trouble with being invisible: it worked beautifully right up until the moment you noticed something, and then you were no longer invisible to yourself. Every observation was a debt. Every detail she filed away was a small act of remembering, and remembering was the one currency she had sworn never to hoard.

Her whole architecture depended on emptiness. She stole from others and erased herself in payment, over and over, a woman made mostly of fresh pages. It was clean. It was safe. It was, she had always told herself, almost painless.

So why, standing in a room full of frightened bureaucrats pretending the sky hadn't been stolen, did the empty place inside her feel less like safety and more like a wound that had learned manners?

* * *

She stayed late. Of course she stayed late; staying late was what Beatrix Delacroix *did*, the reliable dullness of her was the best armor she owned. She reconciled the acquisition ledgers, she cross-indexed the contingency files, she drank tea that went cold beside her out of pure habit.

And when the office finally emptied, when the lamps were trimmed and the building settled into its creaks, she allowed herself the thing she never allowed.

She sat still, closed her eyes, and listened to the hole in her head.

Not *looking* — looking was forbidden, looking was rule three. Just... listening. The way you might stand outside a door you had no key for, hearing muffled voices on the other side.

The shape surfaced slowly, reluctantly, like something rising through deep water. Not images — those had been taken, cleanly, thoroughly; her thief's craft was too good for residue. What remained was worse. Impressions. The *feelings* the memory had worn, too tightly bonded to strip away without taking the vessel with them.

Cold. Extreme cold, the kind that gets into the bones of a word. Stone underfoot. The smell of ozone and old incense — ward-stone smell, though she couldn't have said how she knew that. And a voice, ragged, repeating itself like a prayer that had gone past faith into stubbornness:

*They don't fall. Wards don't fall. We paid. We all paid, year after year, and they don't fall unless—*

Unless what?

The impression broke apart like smoke poked with a finger. Beatrix opened her eyes, heart hammering, hands flat and steady on the desk through sheer professional discipline.

A dying man. Last night. Oren Sable, said the note in her own handwriting — a name she'd resolved to forget and had, except the note itself had survived, which meant some part of her had known she'd need it. A dying warden, up on the border, whose final hours she had stolen out of his head like coins from a sleeping man's pocket.

*We all paid.*

She got out a fresh sheet of paper. Then she crossed the room and locked the door, which she never did, because locking doors attracted attention, and attention was death.

She wrote, quickly, before the sensible part of her could object:

*1. The stars went out the same night the wards supposedly failed.*
*2. Wards are maintained. Wards are paid for. By whom?*
*3. The Guild has no contingency file for the sky.*
*4. Why does the Guild have no contingency file for the sky?*

She stared at item four for a long time. It was the sort of question that got ledger-keepers quietly reassigned to distant offices, or quietly reassigned out of existence.

Then she heard the floorboard.

One board, near the archive stacks, creaking under a weight that had clearly been standing there long enough to be careful. Beatrix did not turn around. She slid item four beneath her blotter with two fingers, and said, mildly, to the empty-seeming room:

"If you've come about the stars, the Guild's official position is that it's under review."

"It isn't," said a man's voice, low and rough at the edges, like rope that had been used hard. "And you know it isn't. Because yesterday evening you were at the Lantern Row hospice, and this morning you had blood on your sleeve, and last night the sky went dark — and you're the only person in this building who isn't surprised."

Beatrix turned, unhurried, her mind running cold and quick through exits and lies and finding, to her considerable annoyance, that both were suddenly in short supply.

In the shadow of the stacks stood a man she didn't know — broad-shouldered, travel-worn, with the particular stillness of someone who had spent years watching horizons for things crossing them. He watched her the way she imagined one watched a lock one intended to pick: with respect, and patience, and absolutely no intention of going home empty-handed.

"You have me mistaken for someone interesting," she said.

"No," he said. "I have you exactly right. My name is Cassian Vey. I was a border warden, once." His jaw tightened around the word *was*. "And eleven days ago, someone stole my brother's dying memory out of his head — including the reason the wards are failing. I intend to get it back."

The stone in her chest turned over.

*Oren Sable told the truth,* her own handwriting had said. *Don't forget again.*

She smiled the smile that had gotten her through six years of double life, and heard how thin it came out.

"Master Vey," she said, "I'm afraid you'll have to speak to our acquisitions department."
