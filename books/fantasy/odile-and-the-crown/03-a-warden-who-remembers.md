## 3. A Warden Who Remembers

The trouble with stealing your own memories, Beatrix had learned over six years of practice, was that you could never quite trust the person who woke up in your bed.

She stood at her washbasin that morning, three days after the stars went out, and studied her reflection with the polite suspicion of a landlady inspecting a new tenant. The face was familiar in the way a borrowed coat is familiar—correct in all its features, a little worn at the elbows, but belonging to someone she was taking on faith. There was a smudge of ink on her left ear that she had no memory of earning. Her knuckles were faintly sore.

*You did something last night,* said the note in her own handwriting, pinned to the mirror where she always left one. *Don't dig. Eat breakfast.*

She ate breakfast. She did not dig. Mostly.

At the Guild of Impossible Things, business had resumed with the grim determination of a shopkeeper sweeping glass after a riot. The sky outside the tall windows was still a flat, starless black—even at noon, which was its own kind of impossible—and yet Mistress Ivo had opened the ledgers at nine sharp, as though the heavens were a clerical error that would resolve itself by quarter day.

"Delacroix," Ivo said, passing her desk without slowing. "Column four is crooked."

"The world ended on Tuesday, Mistress."

"And my column four survived it. Straighten it."

Beatrix straightened it. That was the arrangement: the Guild kept impossible things, and Beatrix kept the Guild's books, and between those two occupations there was no room for questions about why the founder's private accounts showed monthly payments to an account holder listed only as *THE BORDER*, or why the entries stopped forty years ago and started again last week, in a hand that shook.

She had noticed that yesterday. She was almost certain she'd noticed it yesterday. There was a hollow in her attention shaped exactly like the answer, and she had learned not to press her tongue into hollows, because sometimes they were doors.

It was on her walk home—past the shuttered observatory, past the temple where priests were burning candles to a sky that wouldn't take the hint—that the man stepped out of the alley.

He moved wrong for a mugger and right for a soldier: weight low, hands open, positioning himself between her and the street rather than against a wall. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the wind-scoured look of someone who had spent years outdoors being rained on professionally. His coat bore no insignia, but the shoulders were cut for a warden's harness, and the way he stood—facing outward, always outward, toward a border that wasn't there anymore—gave him away more surely than any badge.

"You're the ledger-keeper," he said. "Delacroix. Guild of Impossible Things."

"I'm nobody," Beatrix said. "I file things."

"You were at the Lantern Row hospice eleven nights ago." His voice was quiet, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "Third floor. The dying warden's room. You came in through the window, and when the nurse's lamp swung around, you looked at it. Most thieves freeze. You *looked*. Like you were checking whether the light would reach the door."

Beatrix's mind performed the swift, cold arithmetic it always did. Eleven nights ago was before the stars went out. Before the fragment of memory had begun surfacing in her head like a cork refusing to stay sunk. Whatever she'd taken from that dying man, she'd taken it cleanly and paid herself for it in forgetting—which meant this stranger knew something about her that she did not know about herself, and that imbalance made her feel undressed in a public street.

"If you're here to rob me," she said, "you've chosen a poor victim. I keep other people's valuables, not my own."

"I'm not here to rob you." He held out his hand, palm up. On it lay a small tin badge, scratched nearly blank—the kind wardens wore before the wards fell, back when the border was a thing with a name. "Cassian Vey. Formerly of the Ninth Watch. Formerly, formerly, formerly. They took the rank, then the pension, then the memory of why they took either. I'm what's left."

"I didn't ask."

"No. But you flinched when I said 'memory,' and people who don't care about memory don't flinch." He closed his fist around the badge. "So we're going to talk, Ms. Delacroix, whether you asked or not. Preferably somewhere with a door."

Every sensible instinct told her to walk away. Every sensible instinct also told her that a disgraced warden who had tracked her across a city of panicked strangers was not a man she could simply out-walk. And beneath both instincts, quieter than either, was the hollow—the shape of the thing she'd stolen and couldn't remember, pressing at her from the inside like a tooth that wanted to ache.

"There's a tea house on Cordwainer Street," she heard herself say. "They don't ask questions. They don't even ask if you want tea; they just bring it, which I respect enormously."

* * *

The tea house on Cordwainer Street was called the Second Cup, on the theory that anyone who came for a first cup was merely thirsty, but anyone who stayed for a second was staying for reasons. Its proprietor, a vast and serene woman named Ottoline, brought two cups without being asked and withdrew with the discretion of a confessor.

"So," Beatrix said. "Talk."

"You first." Cassian wrapped his hands around his cup without drinking. "Tell me what you took from that warden."

"I don't know."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest answer I own." And because lying to him suddenly felt like lying to a mirror, she heard herself explain—carefully at first, then, appallingly, completely. The thefts. The method. The rule she'd never broken: every job paid for in advance, her own recollection of it surrendered the moment the work was done, so that no thief could ever be caught holding the goods. Six years of clean books. Six years of waking up with unexplained ink smudges and sore knuckles and notes in her own hand saying *don't dig.*

Cassian listened the way wardens listen, she supposed—to everything, including the pauses.

"You erase yourself," he said at last. "On purpose. Every time."

"It's the only way to be safe."

"Is it?" He turned his cup slowly. "Because here's what I know about safety, Ms. Delacroix. I served the border for twenty-two years. The wards run on memory—that's not poetry, that's mechanics. A warden stands the watch, and the watch remembers, and the remembering is what holds the line. The old enemy can't cross a mind that knows exactly what it's defending. So imagine my professional interest, eleven nights ago, when I found out the Guild has been buying memories off dying wardens for decades. Wholesale. And imagine my further interest when the stars went out three days ago, and the wards collapsed the same night, and the Guild's response was to *straighten column four.*"

Beatrix set down her cup so her hand would have something honest to do.

"How do you know about the buying?"

"Because I tried to sell them mine." His mouth twisted—not quite a smile, more like a scar learning manners. "Eight years ago, after they cashiered me. I was broke and bitter and I figured my worst memories were worth more than my best ones. The Guild's man met me in a room like this one. He asked me questions. And then he stopped writing things down and started looking *worried*, and he said, 'We can't buy this one.' I asked why. He said, 'Because yours isn't for sale. Yours was already sold. Forty years ago. By the founder. In bulk.'"

The tea house noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Beatrix thought of the founder's ledgers—the shaking hand, the resumed payments, the account called THE BORDER—and felt the hollow in her attention shift, deepen, become less like a missing tooth and more like a missing wall.

"In bulk," she repeated.

"Three hundred wardens, give or take. Conscripted, most of them—farm boys and dock girls handed a badge and told it was an honor. They served their term at the edge of the world, and when they came home, they came home *empty*. Their families got a stranger with a veteran's posture. The Guild called it compensation." He finally drank, grimacing at the tea as though it had personally disappointed him. "I spent eight years trying to find out where the memories went. Not metaphorically. Physically. Memories don't evaporate, Ms. Delacroix. Somebody is keeping them. And somebody is paying for the keeping."

"And you think I have them."

"I think you walked out of a dying man's window with something in your head that made the Guild's collector go pale eight years ago. I think you're the only person in this city who steals from the Guild and gets away with it, because the Guild can't prosecute a crime its own ledger-keeper doesn't remember committing." He leaned forward. "I think you are, quite possibly, the most dangerous woman in Vhalen's Reach, and you don't know it, because you sold yourself the knowledge."

Beatrix laughed. It came out wrong—too high, too quick—but it came out, and it kept her from doing something worse, like crying or agreeing.

"This is a charming story," she said. "But I file things. I don't hold the fate of the border in my head. If I did, I'd—" 

She stopped.

Because the hollow had shifted again. Because something had risen in her, unbidden, the way the warden's fragment had risen three days ago—a sensation without context, a certainty without a source. She was sitting in a tea house and she suddenly, absolutely knew what a ward-stone felt like under a bare palm: warm as a sleeping animal, humming at the frequency of a held breath. She knew the smell of the border salt-marshes. She knew—

She knew that the dying warden hadn't been afraid of death. He'd been afraid of *arriving*—of reaching whatever waited beyond the wards and finding it already wearing the faces of everyone he'd ever served beside.

"What," Cassian said quietly. "What just happened?"

"I don't know." Her voice sounded far away to her. "Something surfaced. Something I stole and paid myself not to remember. It's happening more often. The erasure isn't holding." She pressed her palms flat against the table, grounding herself in the grain of the wood. "Which means either I'm broken, or—"

"Or the memory matters too much to stay forgotten." Cassian nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis. "That's how it works with ward-memories. They're load-bearing. You can bury them, but the ground pushes back."

They sat in silence while Ottoline refilled their cups, having apparently decided that whatever was happening at the corner table required a second pot.

"We're not allies," Beatrix said finally. "I want that understood. You're using me to find your comrades' memories, and I'm using you to find out what's in my head, and the moment our interests diverge, we part ways with no hard feelings and no forwarding addresses."

"Naturally."

"And we don't lie to each other. Not because we'll be friends—we won't—but because two liars negotiating honesty is the only kind of partnership that survives, since each of us already knows every trick."

For the first time, Cassian Vey smiled properly, and it transformed his weathered face into something almost boyish. "Ms. Delacroix, that is the most romantic thing anyone has said to me in eight years."

"It wasn't romantic. It was contract law."

"In my experience those are frequently the same thing." He pushed his cup aside and produced, from inside his coat, a folded scrap of paper gone soft with handling. "Now, honesty, as contracted. Here's what I found this morning, before I found you. The Guild's collector—the man who refused to buy my memory—he didn't retire. He died. Three days ago. The same night the stars went out. And the death record lists his occupation as *custodian*, and his place of death as—"

He flattened the paper on the table between them.

"—the river-vaults under Old Weir Bridge," Beatrix read aloud. Then, because the hollow in her had grown teeth: "And the cause of death?"

Cassian's jaw tightened.

"'Cause withheld,'" he said. "'By order of the founder.' Which means the founder is alive, Ms. Delacroix. Forty years past any natural span, signing orders in a shaking hand, the same week his custodian dies and the sky goes dark." He looked up at her, and behind the warden's calm she saw the thing driving him—twenty-two years of service, three hundred empty comrades, one badge scratched blank. "So the question isn't whether you'll help me. The question is whether we get to the river-vaults before whoever killed the custodian decides a ledger-keeper and a disgraced warden are loose ends."

Beatrix looked at the paper. At the tea going cold. At the black, starless square of the window, where the night was waiting with the patience of something very old.

"I'll need to leave a note for myself," she said. "In case one of us doesn't come back."

"Leave two," said Cassian Vey. "I've heard about your handwriting."
