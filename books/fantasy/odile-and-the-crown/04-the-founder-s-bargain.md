## 4. The Founder's Bargain

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers the way a miser kept his coins: counted twice, kissed once, and never shown to anyone who might ask where they came from.

Beatrix Delacroix had balanced those ledgers for six years. She knew their habits the way she knew her own handwriting—which was to say, better than most people knew anything about her. She knew that Column Nine always ran two fingers short of Column Ten because Master Aldous Quill refused to admit that ink dried unevenly in winter. She knew that every entry involving the Warden's Pension Fund was written in a slightly different hand than everything around it, older, more careful, as though someone had traced it from a template they were afraid of.

And she knew that no ledger in the Guild's archive had ever been opened past page four hundred and twelve.

She knew this because she had tried. Three times over the years, idly, the way one tugs at a loose thread while waiting for tea. The pages beyond 412 were sewn shut—not locked, not sealed with wax, but *sewn*, with thread so fine it might have been spun from spider ambition. The Guild's official explanation, delivered by Mistress Ivo with the serene patience of a woman explaining weather to a child, was that the early records were "fragile."

Everything was fragile, if you were afraid of it.

Tonight Beatrix sat alone in the archive with a lamp turned low, a letter-opener borrowed from a dead man's desk, and the particular calm of a woman about to do something unforgivable to municipal property.

"Forgive me," she murmured to the ledger, because she was polite even to books she was vandalizing. "You've been very well kept. This isn't personal."

The thread parted on the third try. It did not snap; it *sighed*, which was somehow worse, and for one absurd moment Beatrix half-expected an alarm bell or an indignant ghost. Neither came. The Guild's founder had guarded his secrets with needlework, and needlework, whatever else could be said of it, did not scream.

Page four hundred and thirteen began the Founder's own hand.

* * *

Aurelian Voss had founded the Guild two hundred years ago, back when it was not yet a Guild but merely a man with a warehouse, a talent for paperwork, and—according to the portrait in the entry hall—a smile like a knife being introduced politely to your throat. Every apprentice learned the same three facts about him: he built the first border wards' administrative system, he standardized the tithe that paid the wardens, and he died young, tragically, of a fever.

Beatrix had always found the third fact suspicious. Nobody died of a fever anymore. Fevers had been conquered the way bad bookkeeping was conquered: through sufficient funding.

Now, reading his hand, she understood why the fever story had survived. It was the only version of events that let you sleep at night.

The early entries were mundane. Grain shipments to the warden garrisons. Candle allowances. A dispute over whether the night watch at Thornfell deserved wool blankets or merely *wool-adjacent* blankets (Voss had won, stingily). She read with the comfortable half-attention of a professional admiring another professional's work, and it was good work—the man had understood double-entry before double-entry was fashionable.

Then, on page four hundred and thirty-one, the grain entries stopped mid-column, and Voss wrote:

*I have made contact.*

No explanation of what "contact" meant. No salutation, no date in the usual format—just the date rendered strangely, in a calendar Beatrix didn't recognize, one that counted backward.

She turned the page slowly, the way you'd approach a dog that had gone very still.

*The thing beyond the wards is not what the wardens think it is. The wardens think it is hunger. It is worse. It is appetite with patience. It does not want to cross the border. It wants to be remembered across it, and memory is the only door we have left open.*

Below that, in a hand grown cramped and hurried:

*It has offered terms. I confess I listened. I confess further that the terms were generous.*

Beatrix set down the letter-opener. Her hands were steady—her hands were always steady, that was the whole point of her—but there was a coldness moving up her arms that had nothing to do with the archive's drafty windows.

She read on.

*The bargain, plainly stated, so that no successor may claim ignorance: it will hold the border closed forever, seal the old enemy out, in exchange for a tithe of its own. Not grain. Not gold. Memory. The wardens stand closest to the seam; their recollections are richest in the old enemy's name and nature. Give it those, year upon year, and it will forget us—and being forgotten, lose its grip on the door.*

*The stars are collateral. It holds them. When the final payment comes due, it returns them. Or it doesn't. Contracts, as I tell my clerks daily, are promises wearing armor, and armor can fail.*

Beatrix laughed. It came out wrong, thin and high, the laugh of a woman alone in a room with a dead man's confession. *Contracts are promises wearing armor.* He'd put it in a *clerical maxim*. He'd taught the joke to apprentices. Somewhere in her first year, Mistress Ivo had probably said it to her, smiling, and Beatrix had smiled back, not knowing she was memorizing the epitaph of the sky.

She kept reading, because stopping was no longer among her options.

*But there is a complication, and I record it here because honesty is the last luxury of a damned man: the wardens will not consent. Of course they will not consent. Who consents to be hollowed? So the payments must be taken, not given. I have devised a method—I will not describe it here; some knowledge should die with its author—that draws memory out quietly, in sleep, in service, in the ordinary course of duty. The wardens will believe they are merely forgetful. Forgetfulness is easy to explain away. Everyone misplaces things.*

*They will not know they are the payment. Their families will not know. The Guild will keep the ledgers, and the ledgers will keep the secret, and I—*

Here the handwriting changed. Grew slower. Almost tender.

*I will not age. This was the other term, the one I have not written above, the one I am writing now because a confession with omissions is merely a lie with good posture. It offered me time in exchange for my silence and my stewardship. Two hundred years, it said, perhaps more, to finish the work, to perfect the system, to become the thing this city needs. I told myself I accepted for the city's sake. I record, for whoever finds this, that I do not entirely believe myself. Ambition wears the mask of duty so well that sometimes the wearer cannot tell them apart. I certainly cannot.*

*The stars will go dark when the account nears settlement. Do not mistake the darkness for the enemy's victory. It is an invoice.*

Beatrix put the ledger down.

She stood up. She walked to the window, opened it, and breathed the cold night air for a full minute, because the alternative was being sick into Aurelian Voss's two-hundred-year-old filing system, and she had standards.

* * *

The stars had been gone for nine days.

Nine days of black sky, of panic in the lower quarters, of the Guild issuing reassuring bulletins about "atmospheric irregularities" with the untroubled confidence of an institution that had known for two centuries that this day would come and had done absolutely nothing to prepare anyone for it. Nine days of Cassian Vey appearing at inconvenient moments with his ruined warden's coat and his questions, and Beatrix deflecting him with the smoothness of long practice, and hating herself a little more each time, though she couldn't have said precisely why.

Well. She could say precisely why now. That was the trouble with comprehension: it arrived all at once, fully itemized, with interest.

She returned to the desk and worked until the lamp burned low, cross-referencing, because horror without documentation was just fear, and fear was useless, but horror *with* documentation was evidence.

The Warden's Pension Fund. She'd balanced it a hundred times. Generous, everyone said—suspiciously generous, wardens joked, for an institution that skimped on blankets. Now she saw the shape of it: the "pension" disbursements matched, month for month, against withdrawal entries in a second column she had always assumed was clerical noise. Withdrawals of nothing measurable. Withdrawals marked only with a symbol she'd never bothered to learn: a small circle with a point missing, like an eye that had forgotten what it looked at.

Memory went out. Gold came in. The wardens were paid for what was stolen from them, and the payment was called a pension, and everyone congratulated the Guild on its generosity.

And the fragment—the dying warden's memory she'd stolen eight nights ago, the one she'd erased from her own mind and then, impossibly, begun remembering anyway—rose in her like water finding its level. She couldn't recall it whole. But she recalled its *edges*: snow, a seam of light underfoot, and a certainty spoken in a voice going gray at the edges—*the wards don't fall. They're spent. Someone has been spending them.*

Someone had. For two hundred years. In installments.

Beatrix pressed her palms flat against the ledger, as if she could hold the truth down by main force.

Because here was the thing she could not stop seeing, the thing that made the cold in her arms reach her heart at last: she knew this system. She *kept* this system. Every forged line, every laundered disbursement, every entry designed to look boring enough that no clerk would ever look twice—she had maintained them with pride. With *craftsmanship*. The paperwork wasn't the weapon's shadow. The paperwork **was** the weapon. Voss had built a machine that ate people's minds and printed receipts, and Beatrix Delacroix had oil its gears every morning and gone home feeling useful.

"I stole memories," she said aloud to the empty archive, testing the sentence. "That was my crime. That was the terrible thing."

It sounded small now. A pickpocket discovering she'd been working for the mint.

There was one more page. She almost didn't turn it. Then she remembered the dying warden's face—she didn't remember the face, exactly, but she remembered that there had been a face, and that it had mattered—and she turned it.

Voss's final entry, dated in the backward calendar, recent. Recent as in *this season*.

*The account approaches settlement. The stars darken. My benefactor grows impatient, and I grow tired of pretending I am still the man who wrote page four hundred and thirteen. To whoever reads this: the debt can be discharged one of two ways. Let it collect the final tithe—all remaining warden-memory, every conscript, every garrison, emptied to the last recollection—or return what was taken. Restore the memories, and the contract voids itself, for a debt repaid cannot be collected again. But restoration requires hands that hold what was taken. And everything taken flows, eventually, through the Guild's ledgers.*

*Find the river-vault. What was confiscated was not destroyed. It was banked.*

Beatrix sat very still.

She thought about the vault she'd never been allowed to inventory. She thought about the symbol—the circle with the missing point—stamped on crates she'd signed for and never questioned. She thought about the fact that she, personally, held stolen memories in her head right now, including the last recollections of a dying warden who had known the truth, and that the Guild's enforcers would come for that memory the moment anyone guessed she had it.

Then she heard, distinctly, the front doors of the archive open below her. Unhurried footsteps on marble. More than one pair.

"—check the upper stacks," said a voice she knew, pleasant as a well-kept ledger. "Mistress Ivo believes our missing clerk keeps late hours."

Beatrix closed the ledger, snuffed the lamp, and reached for the window.
