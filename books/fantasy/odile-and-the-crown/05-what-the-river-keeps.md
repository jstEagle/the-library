## 5. What the River Keeps

Cassian Vey had spent eleven years learning the city's habit of pretending, and he had come to respect it the way one respects a well-built wall: not because it was beautiful, but because it held.

The morning after the stars went out, the city pretended magnificently. Bakers sold bread. Fishwives argued about the price of eels. A man in a fine coat stood on the Cornerstone Bridge and told anyone who would listen that the darkness was "a weather," and that weather, as everyone knew, passed. Nobody asked him what kind of weather lasted three days. Asking questions was how you got noticed, and getting noticed was how you got taken to the Guild of Impossible Things for a friendly conversation from which friendly conversations rarely returned.

Cassian had been noticed once, years ago. He still had the limp and the discharge papers to prove it.

So when he said to Beatrix Delacroix — ledger-keeper, memory-thief, owner of exactly one honest expression which she deployed like a coin she couldn't afford to spend — *"I know where they keep them,"* he expected her to argue.

She didn't. She just looked at him across the table in the back room of the Drowned Bell, where the ale was bad enough that nobody lingered, and said, "Keep what?"

"Memories." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the wood, a nervous habit he'd never managed to kill. "The ones the Guild confiscates. There's a vault."

Her eyes went very still. It was like watching a pond decide whether to freeze. "And how does a disgraced warden know about a Guild vault?"

"Because I used to guard it." He let that sit. "Before they disgraced me. Which, incidentally, was because I asked what was *in* it."

"You could have just stolen a look."

"I did steal a look. That's how I got caught." He smiled without much humor in it. "The Guild forgives theft, Delacroix. It's practically their business model. What they don't forgive is curiosity about their own books."

* * *

The river-vault lay beneath the old tide-mill on the Sarrow, two miles downstream of the city, where the water ran slow and green and nobody fished because the fish tasted of iron. Cassian had walked this route a hundred times in his guarding days, and now he walked it once more as a trespasser, with the woman whose face had haunted him since the night the warden Ondrel died.

He hadn't told her that part yet. That he'd seen her. That he'd been twenty feet away in the dark of the hospice corridor, watching her lean over a dying man and do something to him that made his eyes go smooth and empty as river stones. That Cassian had run instead of raising the alarm, because the alarm would have meant the Guild, and the Guild arriving at Ondrel's deathbed would have meant Ondrel's memories going into *their* vault instead of wherever she put them.

He told himself he'd run out of cowardice. He was fairly sure that was a lie. But it was a useful lie, so he kept it.

"The mill looks abandoned," Beatrix said, picking her way along the towpath. Her boots were wrong for mud. She'd dressed for a library and gotten a swamp, and she was pretending not to mind, which he was beginning to understand was her native language.

"It is abandoned. That's the point. The Guild doesn't hide things behind locked doors. Locked doors invite locksmiths." He gestured at the sagging structure. "They hide things behind boredom."

Inside, the mill wheel had long since rotted to its axle, but the wheel-pit still ran wet and dark beneath the floor, and the water that turned nothing anymore still turned *something* — a mechanism Cassian had only ever seen from the outside, a great slow clockwork of bronze and glass that drank the current and hummed a note too low to hear properly, more felt than sound, like a word spoken under a bed.

"The vault isn't a room," he explained, crouching at the pit's edge. "It's the water. Every confiscated memory gets pressed into a shard — glass, more or less — and the shards go into the current. The machine keeps them circling. Nothing decays down there. Nothing escapes either. The Guild calls it 'the long storage.' I called it a drowning, but nobody asked me."

Beatrix knelt beside him. In the green light rising off the water her face looked younger and older at once, the way faces do when they're about to stop performing.

"How many?" she asked quietly.

"When I guarded it? Forty thousand. Give or take." He watched her absorb that. "Mostly wardens', Delacroix. Conscripted ones. The border service stopped asking for volunteers around the time your Guild's founder got rich, and when men are taken rather than hired, their families start asking inconvenient questions. So the Guild solved the questions."

"How?"

"By taking the memories of the *asking*. The wardens served their term at the wall, came home, and remembered nothing — or remembered a pleasant fiction, a trade apprenticeship, a voyage, whatever the paperwork said they'd been doing for ten years. And the true memories—" he nodded at the black water, turning its endless slow circles, "—went in here. Paid in full, the ledgers say. *Compensated and discharged.*"

"And they weren't."

"They were erased, not paid. There's a difference between a wage and a wound, and the ledgers have always confused the two on purpose." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "My father was a warden. He came home when I was six and didn't know my name. My mother told everyone he'd been at sea. He believed it himself by the end. He died thinking he'd never had a son, and I stood at his funeral next to a coffin and a lie, and the Guild sent flowers."

The water hummed its low note. Beatrix said nothing for a long moment, and Cassian found he preferred her silence to most people's speeches.

Then she said, "Show me the proof."

"There's a shard. Warden's intake record, pressed into the memory itself — the last thing they took was the moment of taking. You can see the clerk's face. You can hear the number being assigned. If we pull it up—"

"We?" She glanced at him. "You're offering to help me rob the Guild of forty thousand memories. On day two of knowing each other. What happened to *disgraced and done with all that*?"

"I'm disgraced, not dead," Cassian said. "There's a difference, and the ledgers have always confused the two on purpose."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth that on another face might have been called a smile.

* * *

Getting a shard up required a net of woven silverwire that Cassian had bribed, borrowed, and half-stolen over the course of one very stressful week. Lowering it into the wheel-pit was like fishing in a dream: the wire sang faintly, the water resisted, and then — a tug, a weight, a glint — and a shard came up dripping, no bigger than a fingerbone, holding a sliver of someone's life inside it like a fly in amber.

Beatrix held it to the light.

Even without touching it, even before she pressed it to her temple the way memory-shards must be pressed, Cassian saw her go pale. Shards leaked, near the surface. That was the machine's one mercy or one cruelty, depending.

"What do you see?" he asked, though he already knew.

"A warden," she said. Her voice had gone careful, the voice of someone walking on ice they've personally cracked before. "Young. They're telling him his contract is complete. He's asking about pay. He's asking about his daughter — there's a wooden bird, he carved it, he's brought it to give her—" She stopped. Swallowed. "And then the clerk says the fee schedule has changed, and the man's face goes..."

"Smooth," Cassian said. "Like river stones."

"Like river stones." She looked at him sharply. "How do you know that phrase?"

Because I watched you make a dying man's face do exactly that, he almost said. Instead: "Lucky guess."

She didn't believe him. He could see her not believing him, filing it away, deciding — visibly, like a scale tipping — that today was not the day. He respected that. It was, he reflected, the most honest transaction they'd conducted so far.

"This is one shard," she said. "One warden. There are thousands."

"Tens of thousands, counting the older strata. The machine circles everything. Some of those memories have been swimming down there for sixty years."

"And to restore them—" She was doing arithmetic; he could see it moving behind her eyes like fish under ice. "To give a memory back, you have to hold it first. All of them. At once. In a head that wasn't built for any of it."

"That's the theory."

"No, that's the *problem*, Vey." She stood, wrapped the shard in cloth with hands that were almost steady. "Unless you've got forty thousand volunteers standing by with empty skulls, the only vessel here that can carry them is a thief. Me. I have... stores. Space I've cleared. Space I *made*, over years, by taking other people's—"

She stopped herself. But the sentence had already gone where sentences go.

Cassian said gently, "You mean you'd have to pour out what you've stolen to make room for what they lost."

"I mean I'd have to pour out *myself*." For the first time since he'd known her, her voice cracked along a seam. "Every job I've ever done, every memory I took to hide my tracks — that's not luggage, that's the walls of the house. Take them out and there's no floor. There's no *me*. I don't even know what's under there. I've never looked. That's the entire point of never looking."

The mill creaked. The water turned its cargo. Somewhere above, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"And the alternative," Cassian said, "is silence."

"Yes."

"The stars stay dark. The thing beyond the wards keeps gathering, whatever it is. And forty thousand wardens go on living pleasant fictional lives while the real ones drown in a mill-race."

"Yes." She hugged the wrapped shard to her chest like something warm in cold weather. "You're supposed to be talking me out of the silence, aren't you? Isn't that the scene where you do that?"

"I don't know what scene this is," Cassian said honestly. "I only know I've stood at one funeral built on a convenient story, and I won't build another. But I'm not going to pretend the truth is cheap, Delacroix. It's the most expensive thing there is, and the bill comes to you."

She was quiet a long time. The green light moved on her face. And Cassian watched — really watched — the temptation of silence move through her, the way weather moves through a valley: the whole sky of her darkening with it, the easy road, the safe road, the road where Beatrix Delacroix kept her ledgers and her secrets and her skin, and the world beyond the border could sort itself out.

Then she looked down at the shard in her arms, at the small drowned weight of one warden's wooden bird and one daughter's name, and he saw the weather break.

"All right," she said. "All right. But if I do this — if I empty myself into strangers — I need to know it's worth it. I need to see the whole shape of it first. The founder's bargain, the ledgers, all of it. Before I hand over the only self I have, I want to know exactly who I'm paying it to."

"That," Cassian said, "means going back into the Guild archive."

"Yes."

"Which means going back toward the people hunting us."

"Yes." She was already moving toward the door, tucking the shard away, and there was something almost frightening in how quickly resolve became motion in her, as though courage were simply another kind of bookkeeping and she had balanced the column. "Come on, Vey. You said you wanted to ask the Guild what's in its books."

"I said that eleven years ago."

"Well," said Beatrix Delacroix, stepping out into the grey light where the stars should have been and weren't, "it's never too late to be curious."

Behind them, the river kept turning. And far off through the starless morning, from the direction of the city, came the thin sound of bells — not the ordinary bells of markets and matins, but the Guild's own deep bronze, ringing an hour that had no name on any clock.

Cassian had heard it only once before, eleven years ago, on the day they took his badge.

They were sounding the vault alarm.

Someone knew they'd been fishing.
