## 6. The Guild's Long Hand

The fire started at the top floor and worked its way down with the patience of a tax collector.

Beatrix knew it was the Guild before she saw a single livery. Enforcers burned buildings the way she kept ledgers: methodically, from the first entry to the last, leaving nothing out of order. Common arsonists were sloppy. These flames had been *filed*.

She watched from the mouth of Tanner's Row, a half-eaten plum bun forgotten in her hand, as her lodgings of six years folded inward like a book being closed. The baker's boy beside her was weeping over the cobbler's shop next door. Beatrix found she could not summon tears for her own rooms. Perhaps because she had never allowed herself to keep anything in them worth mourning — no letters, no keepsakes, no photographs of people she might one day have to forget she'd known. A memory-thief's home is less a nest than a waystation. She had furnished it accordingly: one chair, one bed, one kettle, and four hundred and twelve books she would now grieve properly, later, when there was time.

The plum bun, at least, she finished. Grief is no excuse for waste.

"You'll want to stop standing there," said a voice at her elbow, dry as old vellum.

Mistress Ivo stood in the shadow of the tannery wall, wrapped in a grey traveling cloak that had never once been gray in its life but dyed that color by decades of secrets. She was small, sharp-faced, and seventy if she was a day, with hands that smelled permanently of ink and iron gall. She had taught Beatrix double-entry bookkeeping, the proper tempering of quills, and how to spot a falsified column at forty paces. She had never, in six years, asked Beatrix a single personal question — which, Beatrix understood now, was itself a kind of question, patiently awaited.

"Mistress Ivo." Beatrix did not turn her head. "Lovely morning for a conflagration."

"Walk with me. Grieving women who stand still get arrested. Grieving women who walk get pity and free soup."

They walked. Ivo set a pace that suggested her hips had opinions but her will outranked them, threading them away from the smoke and toward the river district, where the morning fog still clung to the cobbles like a cat that hadn't decided whether to leave.

"They came at midnight," Ivo said. "Six enforcers and a writ signed by the Second Ledger himself. Arson under Guild charter. Your landlady has been compensated and told you died in the fire, which is tidier for everyone. There's a body. It isn't yours, which I'm sure comes as a relief, though I confess I didn't check whose it was. One tries not to be curious about certain things."

"You're very well informed," Beatrix said carefully, "for a woman whose official duties end at auditing tallow imports."

Ivo smiled without warmth or apology. "Girl, I have audited tallow imports for thirty years precisely so that no one wonders what else I audit. Do you imagine the Guild of Impossible Things keeps its impossible things in plain view? Someone must count what goes into the dark. That has always been me. And someone must steal memories so cleanly that even the thief doesn't know she's stolen. That, apparently, has always been you."

The fog was cold on Beatrix's face. She kept walking, because stopping would mean admitting her knees had gone soft.

"How long have you known?"

"Since your third year, when you erased a debt of eleven crowns from Aldous Fenwick's ledger and then, three days later, tipped me eleven crowns 'found in the street' with an expression like a woman trying to remember why she was carrying money." Ivo's voice was almost fond. "You have always been generous with other people's missing things. It's your most dangerous quality."

"I don't know what you—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a struck bell. For one moment the dry old auditor was gone and something older looked out of her face — something that had counted too many columns that didn't add up. "You cannot lie to me about this. Not today. Today they burned your home and put a corpse in your bed, and tomorrow they will do worse, and the only currency you have left is the truth. So spend it."

* * *

They stopped beneath the aqueduct, where the fog pooled thickest and even the river seemed to be minding its own business elsewhere. Ivo drew a small tin from her cloak — peppermints, of all things — and offered one. Beatrix took it mostly out of shock.

"I can protect you," Ivo said. "There are records vaults under the Guildhall that even the Second Ledger has never seen, because he does not know I keep their keys. I can make you a ghost with paperwork. New name, new history, new debts in new towns. You could vanish so thoroughly that even you wouldn't find you. I know that appeals to you. Vanishing is your native language."

It did appeal. Beatrix hated how much it appealed — the way a drowning woman might appreciate a very well-drafted contract for air.

"But," she said.

"But," Ivo agreed, "protection is not charity. I want the warden's memory. The dying man's recollections — the ones you took from the border hospital three nights ago. The ones you've been dreaming about without knowing why."

The peppermint turned to lead on Beatrix's tongue.

She thought of the fragment that had been surfacing all week, uninvited, like a cork refusing to stay sunk: cold starlight, a ward-line humming under her palms, and the terrible certainty that wards do not simply fall. She thought of Cassian Vey waiting for her at the river-vault with his ruined honor and his inconvenient honesty. She thought of the stars, all of them, gone in a single night while the Guild filed its papers upstairs.

"You knew about him," Beatrix said slowly. "The dying warden. You knew what he carried."

"I suspected. I hoped. Hoping is cheaper than knowing." Ivo looked out at the fog. "Thirty years ago, girl, I trained a young founder's clerk. Bright, ambitious, frightened of death the way some men are frightened of water. I watched him grow into the man who now signs the Guild's charters and does not age. I have spent those same thirty years not asking myself what he paid, because I liked my life, and my pension, and my peppermints, and questions like that burn houses down." Her jaw tightened. "Well. Houses are burning down anyway. So I am done not asking."

"And the answer?"

"The wardens. All of them. Conscripted, sworn, and then — emptied. I believe he sold their memories to whatever waits beyond the border, and I believe the stars went into the bargain as collateral, and I believe that the proof of it walked out of a border hospital three nights ago inside the head of my best student." Ivo turned to face her fully. "Give it to me. I have channels, allies, thirty years of quiet favors. I can use it better than you can. And I can keep you alive long enough for it to matter."

The fog drifted between them. Somewhere across the river, a church bell rang the hour with the smugness of institutions that had never been on fire.

Beatrix heard herself say, gently, "No."

Ivo's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a door closing, or perhaps opening onto a darker room. "Think carefully. This is not an offer I—"

"I know exactly what kind of offer it is. It's a good one. That's what makes it terrible." Beatrix swallowed the ruined peppermint whole. "You're asking me to hand the only proof in the world to another person who wants to control it. That's how we got here, Mistress. That's the whole disease. Everyone who learns the truth wants to *keep* it — file it, lock it, trade it, spend it. Nobody wants to give it back."

"To whom?" Ivo said sharply. "The wardens don't remember they're wardens. They're plowmen and fishwives and drunkards in the Salt Quarter. They can't receive a gift they don't know they're owed."

"They can," Beatrix said quietly. "That's rather the point of me."

And there it was — the thing she had been circling since the night the sky went black, said aloud for the first time. Every stolen memory she carried was not a hoard. It was a *debt ledger*. Four hundred entries of other people's lives, held in trust, accruing interest in the form of her own unraveling. And somewhere in that ledger, tangled among them, was a dying warden's last certainty: that the wards could be relit, but only by hands that remembered lighting them.

Ivo stared at her. "You'd restore them. All of them. You'd pour yourself out like a jug and hand the dregs to a border that may already be open." Her voice, for the first time in six years, shook. "You'd die of it, girl. Or worse — you'd survive and not know your own name."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "Though I'm hoping to schedule it badly enough that it inconveniences the Guild."

Something flickered across the old woman's face — fury, grief, and underneath both, horribly, pride. "Stubborn, dishonest, self-destructive child. You're going to get everyone who loves you killed."

"Probably," Beatrix admitted. "But you haven't answered my real question."

"Which is?"

"Why tell me all this? Why show me your hand — the keys, the vaults, the thirty years? If you wanted the memory, you could have taken me to the enforcers and let them carve it out." Beatrix stepped closer. "Unless you wanted me to refuse. Unless some part of you, the part that audits tallow and counts what goes into the dark, needed to hear one person say *no* to the Guild of Impossible Things — just once, out loud, before you helped me anyway."

For a long moment Mistress Ivo said nothing. Then she pressed something into Beatrix's palm — small, cold, iron: a key, worn smooth by decades of a hidden pocket.

"River-vault, third arch, under the fishmongers' stairs," she said brusquely. "Your disgraced warden already knows the way; I gave him the second key yesterday, because unlike you, I hedge my investments. Go. Take what you need. And Beatrix—" the old woman's voice dropped, and the fog seemed to lean in to listen, "—the Second Ledger knows about the vault. He's known since Tuesday. Whatever you're going to do, do it tonight, because at dawn he sends his enforcers to empty it, and everything in it — every stolen memory, every conscripted warden's life — goes into the fire with the rest of the evidence."

Above the aqueduct, far off in the direction of the Guildhall, a bell began to ring — not the hour, but the alarm, quick and urgent, rolling across the city like a stone dropped down a well.

Ivo pulled her hood up. "That'll be them finding out I'm missing," she said. "Run, girl. And when you give it all back — leave yourself one memory. Just one. Trust me. A person needs at least one thing to be sorry about, or she'll go and do it all again."

Then she turned into the fog, walking unhurried, toward the sound of the bells, and Beatrix ran the other way with a key in her fist and dawn — and the end of everything — coming fast behind her.
