## 7. Ashes of the Archive

The ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things were, Cassian had to admit, beautiful objects. Vellum covers dyed the color of dusk, brass corners, spines stamped with a sigil of a closed eye. He had spent three years of his life guarding a border built on lies, and these books had recorded every lie in a hand so elegant it looked like forgiveness.

He was going to burn them.

"Remind me," Beatrix said, hauling a crate across the archive floor, "why we're doing this the loud way? I had a plan. It involved not committing arson."

"Your plan involved picking a lock with a hairpin and walking out with the founder's private correspondence under your coat."

"Yes. Subtle."

"And it failed."

"It *paused*," she said with dignity. "Plans pause."

Cassian lifted the crate onto the pile. They'd been at it since the last bell of evening, stacking ledgers in the old counting room beneath the archive—three tiers of shelving now buried under a mountain of false accounts, the tidy fiction of a guild that had never conscripted anyone, never erased anything, never sold a sky. Mistress Ivo had given them until midnight before the night clerks came down to reconcile the quarter's entries. After midnight, this room would be full of witnesses. Before midnight, it would be full of fire.

"You understand," Beatrix said, striking a match with a gesture so practiced it was clearly habitual, "that I'm the one who has to live with this. I keep records for a living. There's a version of me somewhere weeping into a blotter."

"There's a version of you," Cassian said, "who watched the stars go out and kept filing."

She looked at him over the match flame, and for a moment he thought she'd argue. She was very good at arguing; he'd learned that in the week since the river-vault, learned it the way you learn the shape of a knife pressed into your hand—uncomfortably, and with growing respect. But she only exhaled.

"Right," she said. "For the wardens."

"For the wardens."

She dropped the match.

* * *

Fire, Cassian had learned at the border, has a personality. Wardfire was patient and cruel. Hearthfire was honest and dull. This fire—vellum and ink and a hundred years of meticulous lying—was *gleeful*. It went up the shelving like a rumor through a small town, and within minutes the counting room was a furnace, and the two of them were on the stairs with their collars over their mouths, listening to the archive's beautiful lies crackle into honesty.

It should have worked. It had been a good plan, in the way that plans are good right up until they meet the world.

The flaw was smoke.

The Guild's archive sat in the heart of the Chancery district, wedged between the Hall of Weights and the Assay Office, and the smoke went up the shared chimney like a signal flare. Cassian saw it before Beatrix did—saw the lanterns kindling in the Assay Office windows, saw the door across the yard open and men come out into the snow in their nightclothes, pointing.

"That's not clerks," he said.

Beatrix followed his gaze. Her face did something complicated and then settled into stillness, which he had learned was her version of screaming.

"How long to the river?"

"Six minutes if we run. Ten if we're clever."

"Let's be fast instead."

They came out of the archive's side door into an alley ankle-deep in snow, and the cold hit like a slap of clarity. Behind them the windows of the counting room glowed orange. Ahead, the street was still empty—but empty the way a held breath is empty. Somewhere a bell began to ring, not the fire bell, the *other* one, the one Cassian had only heard once before, the night the wards failed: the Guild's summons, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause.

"They'll seal the district," Beatrix said, already moving. "There's a way through the cloth market—the awnings, the—"

"I know. I've been hunting you through this city for a week. I know your routes better than you do."

She shot him a look that, under other circumstances, he'd have called flirtatious. Under these circumstances he filed it away as evidence of her character: even running for her life, she was offended on principle by being known.

They made two streets before the first enforcer found them.

* * *

Cassian would remember the fight as a series of bad decisions executed well. The enforcer was big and quick and wore the Guild's gray, and Cassian put him down with an elbow and a knee and an apology he didn't mean, and by then there were lanterns at both ends of the street, and Beatrix had grabbed his sleeve and hauled him into the cloth market, and for a hundred glorious seconds they were ghosts among the hanging silks, snow sifting down through the awnings, her hand in his, breath ragged, alive.

Then the second bell changed its rhythm, and Beatrix stopped so hard he walked into her.

"That's the vault call," she whispered.

"What vault?"

"Under the archive. The river-vault. The confiscated memories." She turned, and in the lantern light her eyes were wide and terrible. "Cassian. The fire. The fire's *above* them. The wardens' memories—the shards we found—the Guild keeps them in lead and salt, but lead and salt don't—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. He did the arithmetic himself: the vault below, the fire above, the shelves of stolen lives that were the entire point of everything—every burned ledger, every mile, every lie they'd told each other and themselves since the night the sky went dark. They could run for the river and live. Or they could go back.

"We can't," he said. "There are too many—"

"Ivo's down there."

That stopped him colder than the snow. "She's supposed to meet us at the bridge."

"She was supposed to. She's Ivo. She'll have gone down to move the shards herself, because she doesn't trust us, because she's never trusted anyone, and now she's—" Beatrix's voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in all that practiced calm. "She taught me my letters. She taught me which lies in a ledger matter."

Cassian looked back the way they'd come. The archive was fully alight now, a second false dawn over the Chancery district, and between them and it, lanterns, gray coats, the Guild's long hand closing finger by finger.

Every instinct he had left—the instincts of a warden, the ones that had survived disgrace and drink and three years of guarding a lie—said *run, live, fight another night*.

He took her hand instead.

"Fast," he said. "Not clever."

* * *

They went in through the coal chute, because Beatrix knew it existed and because the universe, having spent the evening being cruel, apparently decided to be ironic instead. They dropped into the lower corridor in a cascade of black dust, and the heat came through the walls like a living thing, and somewhere above them a beam groaned its last argument.

Mistress Ivo was in the vault.

She was kneeling among the lead caskets with her sleeves rolled, methodically loading shards into a courier's satchel, and she did not look up when they came in. "You're meant to be at the bridge," she said.

"You're meant to be *dead* by now," Beatrix said. "Or gone, or—give me the bag, we'll carry it together—"

"The bag stays with me." Ivo stood. She was a small woman, gray as her ledgers, and she moved like a door closing. "The east stair is blocked. The chute you came down is your way out, and it will hold for perhaps four more minutes, and someone has to make the Guild believe the vault is already empty and the two of you went the other way."

"Ivo—" Beatrix's voice had gone young. Cassian had never heard it go young. "Ivo, no."

"Beatrix." The old woman smiled, and it was the smile from a hundred mornings at the ledger desk, dry and warm and slightly wicked. "I have spent forty years keeping accounts for monsters and telling myself it was arithmetic. Let me balance one column honestly before the book closes."

The ceiling above them cracked. Through the smoke, at the top of the main stair, gray coats began to descend, and Ivo turned toward them with the satchel held high like a lantern, like a torch, like a woman who had finally, after forty years, found the entry she'd been waiting to make.

"Go," she said. "And girl—the thing beyond the wards. It isn't waiting anymore. I've seen the requisitions. Boots, rope, cold-weather pay." Her voice dropped, and the last thing Cassian heard from her, clear through the roar of the burning archive, was: "*It's already crossing.*"

Then the ceiling came down between them, and Beatrix screamed, and Cassian did the cruelest kind thing he had ever done, which was to put his arms around her and pull her into the coal chute while the world she'd grown up in collapsed behind them.

* * *

The river took them in the way rivers take everything—without judgment, without mercy, moving regardless. They came out of the chute's mouth into the culvert below the water-stairs, soaked to the bone, soot-black, and Beatrix was shaking, and it was not the cold.

They sat on the wet stone with the current talking its low endless talk, and for a long time neither of them said anything at all.

"She knew," Beatrix said finally. Her voice was scraped hollow. "She knew what I was. She knew what the Guild was. Forty years, and she stayed, and she filed, and she—" A breath. "She died buying us six minutes."

"She died buying the shards," Cassian said. "Six minutes, a satchel of stolen lives, and a message. That's not nothing. That's not nothing, Beatrix."

"No," she said. "It's a debt." She looked up at him, and her face in the culvert's dark was the face of a woman deciding something enormous. "And I'm done letting other people pay it."

Above them, far to the north, past the rooftops and the smoke, the sky where the stars should have been was not empty tonight. There was a smudge there, faint as breath on glass, a darkness that moved against the darkness.

Cassian saw it. He knew she did too. Neither of them said its name.

The enemy was already crossing—and somewhere in the satchel of lead and salt that had burned with everything else, or in the shards they still carried, or in the memory locked in Beatrix's own skull, was the only key that could call the wardens home.

They had until dawn. Perhaps.
