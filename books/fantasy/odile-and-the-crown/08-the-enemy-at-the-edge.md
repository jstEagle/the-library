## 8. The Enemy at the Edge

They rode north through the dead hours, and the world got quieter the closer they came to the border.

Not peaceful-quiet. Beatrix had spent enough nights in other people's heads to know the difference. This was the quiet of a room where someone has just stopped breathing—the held-breath quiet of a country waiting to find out if it still had an edge.

Cassian drove. He'd insisted, on the grounds that she hadn't slept properly in four days and he'd once driven a supply wagon through a hailstorm "purely out of spite." She'd let him, mostly because arguing required energy she was saving for more important things, like not thinking about what waited past the tree line.

The road was empty in the wrong way. Empty roads usually meant something: a festival elsewhere, a flood, a tax collector. This emptiness meant nothing at all. No carts, no pilgrims, no one fleeing south, which was somehow worse than refugees would have been. It was as if everyone within fifty miles of the border had simply agreed, without discussion, that there was nothing left up here worth visiting.

"Village back there," Cassian said, nodding at a cluster of dark roofs they'd passed a mile ago. "Doors all open."

"Maybe they're airing out."

"Beatrix."

"Fine. Maybe they walked south and left their doors open because they were in a hurry, or maybe—" She stopped. There was no version of that sentence that ended well. "I know what it means. I'm choosing not to say it out loud because saying things out loud makes them real, and I have a professional relationship with unreality that I'd like to maintain for another few hours."

"That's not how any of this works."

"You'd be amazed what I've made work through sheer refusal."

He almost smiled. She counted it as a victory and looked away before he could take it back.

* * *

The wards announced themselves by their absence.

She'd expected—she didn't know what. A wall of light, a hum, some grand architectural statement. What she got instead was a feeling, like walking into a room where a clock has stopped. You don't notice the ticking until it's gone, and then you can't stop noticing the silence where it used to be.

The border itself was a line of standing stones running along a ridge, each one taller than a man, carved with names.

That was the thing that undid her. Not the stones—stones were just stones—but the names. Hundreds of them, thousands, cut deep and kept bright, and every single one of them worn smooth now, unreadable, as though the border itself had forgotten who it belonged to.

"Wardens," Cassian said quietly. He'd dismounted and was walking the line with his hand out, not quite touching. "Every stone is a warden. The name's what anchored them. You give your name to the ward, and the ward holds your memory of why you stand here, so you never forget, even when you want to." His jaw worked. "Except the founder took the memories and left the stones. So now they're just rocks with dead men's names on them."

"Not dead," Beatrix said. "Erased. There's a difference, and I'm the only person alive qualified to make it."

It should have been a joke. It came out sounding like a confession, which, she supposed, it was.

* * *

They saw the first of them an hour before dawn, coming down the far slope through the frost-grass.

A figure, then three, then a dozen, walking in loose formation toward the ridge. And Beatrix's mind did the merciful thing first—it tried to file them as travelers, as survivors, as anything—and then the light of Cassian's lantern caught the nearest one's face, and the mercy ran out.

She knew that face.

Not from life. From a ledger. Third shelf, row nine, the conscription rolls she'd catalogued two years ago without ever reading closely, because reading closely was how you started asking questions and asking questions was how you stopped being safe. A young man with a crooked nose and a scar through his eyebrow, listed under a name she could no longer read off the smooth stone behind him.

He was wearing that face the way a thief wears a coat.

"It's wearing them," she whispered. "The enemy. It's wearing the wardens."

"I know."

"No, you don't—I mean—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Cassian, I've seen his face before. In paperwork. That's Halric Voss, conscripted 14th of Thawmonth, age nineteen, occupation cooper's son. He didn't die at the border. He was *filed*. And that thing down there is walking around inside his face like it owns the deed."

Below them, the thing with Halric Voss's face reached the first standing stone and put its hand against it, gently, almost tenderly. Where its palm touched, the old carvings began to smooth over. Erasing. It was erasing even the memory of the memory.

More figures came over the ridge behind it. An old woman with a warden's posture. A boy too young for the uniform he wore. Faces Beatrix realized, with a lurch, she recognized—not from ledgers now but from somewhere deeper, from the stolen stores in her own head, fragments of dying men's last thoughts that she'd carried for years without knowing whose they were.

"They're not attacking," she said. "Why aren't they attacking?"

"Because they don't have to." Cassian's voice was flat, the way voices go when the man using them is holding himself together with both hands. "The wards are memory, Beatrix. The founder sold the memories. The enemy isn't crossing the border. It's *becoming* it. Every face it wears, it takes another post. By winter there won't be a border. There'll be a shape wearing our whole country's history, and we'll thank it for holding the door."

* * *

They crouched behind the ridge with the frost soaking through their coats, and Beatrix did the thing she was best at in all the world, better than theft, better than lies: she thought.

Think, she told herself. You are a ledger-keeper. Ledgers are just truth with a system. Find the system.

The enemy wore the wardens' faces. Fine. But faces weren't the anchor—names were. Names cut in stone, given freely, remembered. The enemy could copy a face the way she could copy handwriting, flawlessly, and be just as hollow underneath. It couldn't wear what it didn't have. It had taken their bodies' shapes and their names' sounds, but the *memories*—the reason a nineteen-year-old cooper's son stood on a frozen ridge for thirty years without deserting—those had been sold. Locked away. Scattered into lead and salt and river-vaults and one very tired woman's skull.

Which meant the enemy was wearing uniforms it had never earned. Standing posts it didn't understand. Holding a door it didn't know the weight of.

And memories—restored, returned, given back to the living men and women they'd been torn from—would call those same men and women back to their stones. Not ghosts. Not the enemy's puppets. Wardens who remembered why.

"The vault shards," she said aloud. "The ones we pulled from the river. And the rest—" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, against the satchel-weight of stolen years she'd been carrying since she was nineteen herself. "The rest is in me. All of it. Every warden the founder erased, everything he sold, I've been walking around as the backup copy for a decade and treating it like a liability."

Cassian turned to look at her. In the lantern light his face was doing something complicated.

"You said the exchange nearly killed you last time. One shard. One warden."

"One shard, yes." She laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "There are four hundred and eleven names on these stones, Cassian. Give or take. I counted while you were checking the horses, because counting is what I do instead of screaming."

"So we find another way."

"There is no other way. That's the whole point of the way there isn't." She made herself look at him. "You want honesty? Here's my entire honest inventory. If I pour them back, I lose most of what I am. My method only worked because I stole from myself after every job—every memory of every crime, gone, traded away piece by piece to hold all of *this*. I'm not mostly Beatrix Delacroix anymore. I'm mostly a warehouse with excellent penmanship. And if I empty the warehouse—"

"What's left?"

"I don't know. That's the terrifying part. Maybe nothing. Maybe just a woman who knows how to hold a pen and doesn't remember why." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "But here's the other half of the inventory. Down that hill is something wearing a dead boy's face, smoothing his name off his own memorial stone. And every hour I spend protecting myself is an hour it spends learning how to be us."

The frost-grass whispered below. The thing with Halric Voss's face had moved to the second stone, and the third, patient as arithmetic.

Cassian was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "You don't have to decide alone."

"Yes," Beatrix said, "I rather do. That's what carrying a secret *means*, Cassian. Nobody warns you about that part. Everyone tells you secrets are heavy, but nobody tells you the weight is that you can't set them down. You can't share them without breaking them open, and broken open they either save everyone or destroy you, and you don't get to know which until after."

"But?"

"But." She exhaled, long and shaking. "But I watched Mistress Ivo die for this. I burned ten years of my own work to buy us one night. And I am so *tired* of being the only person who knows the sky was stolen, and I think—I think a secret that matters this much stops being a possession and starts being a duty. So."

She reached into her coat and drew out the last vault shard, dull lead wrapped in salt-stained cloth, warm from her body. Then she took his hand—his scarred, steady, disgrace-of-the-wardens hand—and pressed it into his palm.

"Cassian Vey. Warden, third ring, dishonorably discharged for asking questions. I'm going to tell you everything. Everything I stole, everything I am, every crime in every ledger, starting tonight and ending at that river-vault. Because if I'm going to give away everything else I have, I refuse to do it alone, and I refuse to do it as a stranger."

His fingers closed around the shard. He didn't say anything for a moment, and then, gruffly, in the tone of a man negotiating terms he'd already lost:

"For the record, I always thought you were a criminal."

"Flatterer."

"And I'm going to need you to start from the beginning. The actual beginning."

"The beginning," Beatrix said, looking down the hill at the enemy wearing the faces of the forgotten, "is four hundred and eleven people who deserve to remember their own names. Let's go wake them up."

Below them, the thing with Halric Voss's face paused at the fifth stone—and turned, slowly, up the ridge, toward the lantern light. Toward them.

It smiled with a borrowed mouth.

It knew what she was carrying. And dawn was coming fast.
