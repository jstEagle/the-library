## 9. The Giving Back

The river-vault smelled of cold stone and old rain, the way all important places do when they have been keeping secrets longer than people.

Beatrix stood at its threshold with a lantern in one hand and four hundred and eleven memories in her head, and she thought—absurdly, at the end of everything—that it looked like her day job. Shelves. Ledgers. Little labeled drawers. The Guild of Impossible Things had always understood that the impossible was mostly a filing problem.

"You're smiling," Cassian said. "We're about to be murdered by an immortal accountant and you're smiling."

"I'm organizing," she said. "It's how I cope. There's a difference, and I'll thank you to respect it."

He grunted, which from Cassian Vey was practically a love sonnet, and set his lantern on the central table. The vault stretched away beneath the river in long galleries, each drawer etched with a number and a name. Some of the names had been scratched out by careful, bureaucratic hands. Those were the conscripted ones. The ones who had never been paid for their service because they had never been permitted to remember they'd served at all.

Beatrix pressed her palm flat against the first drawer. Halric Voss. Warden, Third Watch. She could feel him inside her like a coal banked under ash—the night sky over the border, the taste of iron, a daughter's laugh, the exact weight of a spear he'd carried for thirty years.

"Once I start," she said quietly, "I can't stop. Every memory I give back is one I lose. Not just his—I'll unravel my own stores to make room, thread by thread. By the end there may not be enough of me left to walk out."

"I know." Cassian's jaw worked. "I did the arithmetic on the hill. I hate the answer."

"Then let me say the other part, so we don't have to say it twice. If I falter halfway, the wardens wake confused, the enemy crosses, and everyone dies anyway. So whatever happens—whatever I forget, whoever I stop being—you hold the line and you *don't* stop me."

"Beatrix—"

"That's not a request. That's a ledger entry. Debit: one thief. Credit: four hundred and eleven wardens and possibly the entire sky." She managed a crooked smile. "I've balanced worse books."

He caught her hand before she could turn away. His grip was rough and warm and entirely too honest for either of their comfort.

"For the record," he said, "I remember your face. All of it. The night at the border, every argument since, the way you take your tea like it insulted you. If you lose those things, I'll keep them for both of us. Somebody has to be the archive."

She wanted to say something clever. What came out instead was small and true: "Don't let me become someone who wouldn't have done this."

"Never," he said. "You'd haunt me. You're far too organized to skip that step."

She laughed once—a cracked, bright sound—and turned to the drawer, and began.

* * *

The first memory went home like water finding its level.

Halric Voss gasped against the far wall where Cassian had laid him—one of the few wardens whose bodies the Guild had kept, sleeping in stasis rather than dead, waiting for orders that would never come. His eyes opened. For a moment they were empty as winter windows.

Then he said, "Mara. My daughter's name is Mara," and began to weep.

Beatrix felt the absence where the memory had been—not pain, exactly, but a smoothness, a place in her mind worn blank. In its place rose something of her own, unpinned and drifting: her mother's kitchen, gone. The smell of ledger ink, gone. She watched them go with the strange detachment of a clerk watching stock leave the warehouse.

The second warden woke. The third. She worked down the gallery drawer by drawer, and with each restoration she reached deeper into herself, pulling up her own past to feed the exchange—the Guild's rules, her first theft, a rainy street, a name she'd been called at school. Each loss made the next easier, which frightened her more than any of it. She was becoming very good at letting go.

Cassian moved among the waking wardens, murmuring names back to them like a man returning lost luggage. "Sera Ondine. Tam Broadfoot. Yes—that's yours. Take it slowly."

By the hundredth warden, Beatrix had forgotten her childhood home. By the two-hundredth, she had forgotten Mistress Ivo's face, and grieved it without knowing what she was grieving, which was somehow worse.

"You're swaying," Cassian said, steadying her elbow.

"I'm fine. I simply can't recall why I trust you, which is inconvenient, because I distinctly still do."

"That's the whole marriage right there," he said, and she laughed again, thinner now, and kept pouring.

* * *

The founder came at the three-hundredth name.

There was no door. He was simply there, between one lantern-flame and the next, standing in the aisle in a coat cut from a fashion four centuries dead. He looked like a well-preserved portrait of a man—handsome the way ledgers are handsome, all balance and no warmth. Aldous Crane. Founder. Immortal. The thing the stars had been spent to buy.

"Do you know," Crane said mildly, "I have unmade kingdoms with less effort than this. Sit down, child. You're spending yourself like a drunkard spends a fortune."

"I know exactly what I'm spending," Beatrix said. Her voice sounded strange to her already—thinner, less certain of its own jokes. "I keep the books."

"The books were *mine*." Something moved behind his composure, quick and hungry. "Every entry. Every erased warden. Do you imagine I wanted this? The wards demanded memory as fuel—memory freely given or memory taken, the border never asked which. I merely found the cheaper supplier."

"You sold the sky," Cassian said, stepping between them, sword drawn.

"I *bought time*. Four hundred years of it. And in four hundred more, when the bargain comes due again, someone sensible will renew it, and no one will miss a few stars." Crane raised one hand, and the lanterns guttered. "Now return what you've taken from me, thief. The remaining wardens are collateral. Collateral stays *quiet*."

"They were never yours," Beatrix said. "That's the whole finding of my audit."

Crane's power came down the aisle like floodwater. Cassian met it with his blade and his body and four hundred years of warden stubbornness, and held—barely, screaming, boots skidding on wet stone—while behind him Beatrix opened the three-hundred-and-eleventh drawer and gave a woman named Pell her wedding day back.

And the wardens, waking, began to remember together.

That was the thing Crane had never accounted for, because he had never been one of anything. Memory shared is not the same as memory kept. Four hundred voices remembering the same border, the same watch-fires, the same oath spoken in the same words—they braided. They became a single vast recollection, and recollection, at the border, was the oldest magic there was.

The river-vault filled with light that had nothing to do with lanterns.

Crane staggered. "Stop. *Stop.*"

"It's not stopping," Beatrix said wonderingly. "It's reconciling. You wrote debts into them for four centuries, sir, and debts—" she smiled, and it was Halric Voss's smile now, borrowed and fierce, "—debts come due."

The collective memory closed over Aldous Crane like a ledger closing. He aged between one breath and the next—all four hundred years arriving at once, honestly counted—and what remained when the light faded was a small heap of dust and a coat, and, absurdly, a fountain pen, still inked.

Cassian sat down hard on the floor. "Remind me never to owe you money."

"I couldn't tell you what money is," Beatrix said. "But I'd figure it out. I appear to be good at figures."

* * *

The last drawer was hers to open, and by then she barely knew why.

She had given away nearly everything. Her name was a fog. Her reasons were a fog. But her hands remembered the work, and something below memory—deeper than memory, some bedrock of choice—kept her moving down the row, drawer after drawer, while Cassian stayed close and spoke to her steadily, feeding her the words she needed like a man feeding a fire.

"Your name is Beatrix Delacroix. You keep meticulous records. You take your tea like it insulted you. You are doing the bravest stupid thing I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a man duel a goose."

"A *goose*," she repeated, delighted, having no idea why.

"Long story. It won. Keep going."

Warden four hundred and ten woke and knew his own mother's face. Warden four hundred and eleven—Halric Voss's last gift, the memory she had stolen from a dying man on the night this all began—she pressed home gently, like setting down something breakable, and felt the final rooms of herself go dark.

She swayed. Cassian caught her.

Above them, through the vault's high grating, the black sky shivered. And then—faintly, impossibly, like embers remembering they were fire—the stars began to come back.

* * *

She woke to gray-gold light and the smell of the river, and she did not know where she was, or who the man sitting beside her was, though he had kind eyes and a tired mouth and he was holding her hand as if it were a document of state.

"There you are," he said.

"Where is here?" Her voice came out rusty. "Who am I?"

He didn't flinch. He'd promised, she thought—he seemed like a man who kept promises, though she couldn't have said how she knew. He reached into his coat and produced a folded page, soft at the creases, and put it in her hands.

In her own handwriting, in the neat hand of someone who kept meticulous records, were two lines:

*My name is Beatrix Delacroix.*

*If you are reading this, I gave everything away on purpose. Check the work.*

Below it, newer, in a different hand:

*One more line, added by the archive. — C.V.: You liked your tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. You laughed at geese. You chose truth when silence was safer. Welcome back whenever you're ready; I'll keep the rest until then.*

Beatrix read it twice. Three times. Beyond the grating overhead, the returned dawn was doing something extravagant with the clouds, and somewhere above, four hundred and eleven wardens were watching the stars fade gratefully into morning.

"Check the work," she murmured. "Yes. That sounds like me."

"It is," Cassian said. "It absolutely is." He hesitated, then, almost shyly: "Would you like to see the sky properly? There's a hill. Good view. Terrible company, but the view makes up for it."

She looked at this stranger with kind eyes who had kept her name safe in his pocket like a treasure, and she found—to her surprise, with no history whatsoever to base it on—that she trusted him completely.

"Yes," said Beatrix Delacroix, keeper of one page and one new memory, standing up under a returned dawn. "Let's start there."
