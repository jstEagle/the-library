## 10. Her Own Name

I woke to the smell of bread.

That was the first thing. Not the ache behind my eyes, though there was an ache, deep and wide, like a room in my head that had been emptied of all its furniture and swept besides. Not the cold, though the morning was cold enough to see my breath. The bread. Someone was baking bread somewhere below me, and the smell climbed the stairs and found me before anything else did.

I lay still for a long while and took inventory.

My name: unknown.

Where I was: a narrow bed, a narrow room, a window with frost ferning across the glass. Through the frost, faintly, light — real light, gold-edged, moving. Sunlight. I knew that much. I knew what sunlight was called.

What I knew otherwise: how to read. How to write, apparently, because there was a note on the table beside the bed in handwriting I did not recognize and yet somehow did. How to count — I caught myself counting the cracks in the ceiling plaster without deciding to. How to be afraid, which seemed less like knowledge and more like weather.

Everything else was a hallway with all the doors shut.

I sat up. My body worked, which felt like a courtesy from a stranger. There were calluses on my right hand, middle finger, the kind you get from holding a pen too hard for too many years. A thin scar along my left forearm I had no story for. My sleeves were clean. I checked twice. Whatever blood had once been on them, someone had washed it out, or it had never been mine to begin with, or both of those things were the same thing now.

On the table: the note, a stub of pencil, and a cup of water gone lukewarm with someone's care in it.

The note said:

*Your name is Beatrix Delacroix. You gave away almost everything you were so that other people could have back what was taken from them. It was the right thing to do. Don't let anyone tell you otherwise — especially not yourself.*

Below that, one more line, pressed harder into the paper:

*If a man named Cassian Vey offers you tea, drink it. He makes it badly, but he stays.*

I read it four times. On the fourth reading, something in my chest turned over — not memory exactly, but the shape where memory might have stood, the way a dent stays in a cushion after the person gets up. Grief without content. Gratitude without an object. I folded the note very carefully and put it in the pocket over my heart, which felt correct even though I couldn't have told you why.

Then I went downstairs toward the bread.

* * *

The woman baking was round and gray-haired and introduced herself as Marta, landlady, and asked no questions at all, which I understood — in some wordless way, the way you understand a door is locked — was itself an answer to questions other people had already asked her.

"You slept three days," she said, sliding a loaf onto the board. "He sat with you the first two. Didn't sleep, barely ate, glared at the wall like it owed him money. Third day he went out and came back with a new teapot, and I thought, well. That's a man who's decided to stay."

"Is he here?"

"Garden." She nodded toward the back. "Been out there since dawn, staring at the sky. You'd think he'd get tired of it."

I looked out the kitchen window. The sky was enormous and blue and full of stars' daytime ghosts — no, not ghosts. Stars. Actual stars, pale as pinpricks through the morning blue, hanging on in broad daylight as if they had things to make up for. As if they intended to stay visible until every last person who had missed them had seen them back.

I went out into the garden.

* * *

Cassian Vey was sitting on an upturned barrel with his elbows on his knees, looking up. He was thinner than — than nothing, I had nothing to compare him to, but he had the look of a man who had been eating badly for a long time and recently stopped pretending otherwise. When he heard the door he didn't turn around all at once. He turned around slowly, the way people turn when they've been afraid to hope.

"You're up," he said.

"I'm up." I held up the note. "You wrote this?"

"The warning part. She wrote the rest herself, before—" His voice snagged. He cleared it. "Before she gave everything back. We found it in your coat. I copied it out fair in case the original got lost. Yours is the original."

I sat down on the grass beside the barrel because standing suddenly seemed ambitious. "Tell me," I said. "All of it. I keep feeling shapes where things should be. I'd rather have the facts."

So he told me. He told it plainly and without flattery, which I suspect was a kindness — he had clearly decided that the kindest thing he could do for whoever I now was, was to refuse to make her into a legend. He told me about ledgers and stolen memories, about a founder who had bought eternity with a sky, about wardens conscripted and emptied and forgotten by their own families. He told me about Mistress Ivo, and he was quiet for a moment there, and I found myself quiet too, grieving someone I would never remember meeting.

"And me?" I said finally. "What was I like?"

He considered this with the seriousness of a man defusing something. "Meticulous," he said. "Stubborn. Funnier than she had any right to be, given how tired she always was. Terrible liar, actually, for a professional. Blushed every time."

"That sounds made up."

"It is entirely made up," he agreed. "But it's the version I'm keeping, and I'm the only witness left, so it stands."

I laughed. It surprised us both. And underneath the laugh, in the swept-out rooms of my head, something shifted — not a memory returning, but the sense that the rooms were not empty, exactly. More like they had been given away. Furnished by strangers now, warm with other people's lives, and I was the keeper of the house rather than its history.

"I don't regret it," I said. "I can tell that much. Whoever I was, she doesn't regret it. It's the one thing that came through whole."

"No," Cassian said. "She wouldn't."

* * *

They came to see me over the following weeks, the ones whose memories I had carried. I don't know how they found me; perhaps the restored wardens simply know things now, the way their stars know where to hang. They arrived at Marta's door with bread, with flowers, with small awkward gifts, and they stood in the parlor looking at me with an expression I learned to recognize: gratitude tangled with guilt, because they had everything back and I had nothing, and none of them — not one — could give it back again. Memory only flows one way. That was the whole architecture of the bargain, and its whole cruelty, and its whole mercy.

A woman named Odile came most often. She had been a warden on the eastern reach; she remembered the night the wards fell from the inside of it, and she remembered — this she told me gently, watching my face — that a stranger had once held her memories like something breakable, and had chosen, at cost of everything, to hand them home.

"Do you want them described?" she offered once. "Your life. I held it for a little while, on its way back to you. I could tell you pieces."

I thought about it for a long time.

"No," I said at last. "Thank you. But no." Because a life described by someone else is just a story, and I had apparently spent my whole first self trading in stories that weren't true, and I wanted — whatever I was now — to earn my own honestly, door by shut door, down the long hallway.

Odile nodded like she'd expected it. "She was like that too," she said, and smiled, and I let it stand.

* * *

Winter loosened. I got work keeping accounts for Marta and two of her neighbors, because the callus on my finger turned out to mean something after all, and numbers, unlike people, had kept all their history and were happy to share it. I was good at it. I was good at it in a way that felt less like remembering and more like nature, like water finding low ground, and I chose not to examine that too closely. Some inheritances you take on faith.

In the evenings Cassian and I sat in the kitchen, and he made tea — badly, exactly as promised, scalded leaves and all — and we talked about nothing, or about the stars, which had settled into their old courses and now behaved with the smugness of things that know they were missed. Some nights he told me a piece of the old Beatrix, one honest fact at a time, always asking first: *Do you want it?* And sometimes I said yes, and kept it like a stone from a beach I'd never visited. And sometimes I said no, and he put it away without complaint, because he had learned — I could see it in him, worn smooth like the note's creases — that a person is not their past. A person is what they do next.

One evening in early spring I found a fresh sheet of paper and the pencil stub on the table, set out neatly, waiting.

"What's this?" I asked.

Cassian didn't look up from his terrible tea. "Thought you might want to write something."

I sat. I thought. Then I picked up the pencil, and my hand moved with a confidence my mind couldn't account for, and I wrote:

*Beatrix Delacroix.*

Just that. My own name, in my own hand, on a clean page. It looked small and enormous at once, the way a seed does.

Underneath it, after a moment, I added one line — not the old warning, which belonged to the woman who had written it, but a new one, for whoever I was becoming:

*Keep what matters. Give the rest away freely.*

Cassian read it upside down, which was apparently a warden habit, and something in his face eased that I hadn't realized was tight.

"Well," he said. "First page of the new ledger."

"First entry," I corrected, and passed him the pencil, because if he was staying — and he was staying; Marta had already started charging him rent — then he could put his name on it too.

Outside the window, the first star of the evening came out early, found us through the glass, and stayed.
