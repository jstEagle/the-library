## 1. The Price of Small Wonders

The night market of Vell woke the way a cat does—one eye at a time, then all at once, and always hungry.

Noor Sandoval had been part of that waking for twenty years. She knew the exact hour the lantern-sellers began their rounds, the precise moment when the smell of frying onions overtook the smell of the canal, the way the cobbles of Lantern Row held the day's heat long after sunset like a beast too stupid to know it was being slowly cooked. She had a stall between a woman who sold secondhand grief—mourning veils, mostly, and the occasional widower's coat—and a boy who carved birds out of candle-wax and swore they flew if you believed hard enough. She sold charms.

Small ones. Harmless ones. A knotted red thread to keep a fever down. A copper bell with a hole drilled through its tongue so it rang silent and fooled the bad spirits into thinking they'd already been heard. Salt in a twist of blue paper, for doors that creaked with more than age. She priced them low and smiled often and never, ever promised anything.

That was the whole art of surviving with a gift in Vell: promise nothing. The moment you promised, you owed. And the moment you owed, the mountain began to take an interest in you.

So when old Marta from the fish stall pressed a sprained wrist under Noor's nose and begged, "Go on, girl, give it a real blessing, none of your thread-and-salt nonsense," Noor only laughed and wrapped the wrist in knotted thread and said, "This keeps the swelling honest. The rest is between you and whoever's listening."

"Nothing's listening," Marta said. "Gods are dead. Everyone knows it."

"Everyone knows it," Noor agreed, and sold her a bell.

But that was the trouble with everyone knowing things. Everyone in Vell knew the gods were dead, the way everyone knew the mountain was just a mountain, the way everyone knew the court beneath it was a polite fiction, a place where magistrates in grey silk weighed grievances no one could name. Everyone knew it, and everyone still left offerings at the old shrine on Tanner's Steps—just in case, just a little, just a crust of bread and a thimble of wine, because it cost nothing and the gods were dead and dead things couldn't hold a grudge.

Noor knew better. She'd known since she was six years old, since the day a gutter-rat of a dog had looked at her with its mud-brown eyes and said, in a voice like gravel rolling downhill, *You smell like the fire that sleeps.* She had never spoken to an animal again where anyone could see. She had built her whole life out of small, deniable wonders—charms that worked through coincidence and confidence and, when she was very careful and very tired at the end of a market night, through something older that she fed like a banked fire and never let flare.

Twenty years of hiding. She'd gotten good at it. Good enough that even Tam didn't know.

* * *

Tam found her at the stall just past midnight, when the market had gone soft and blue and the wax-bird boy had gone home. He was twelve, all elbows and sharp collarbones, with a gap in his smile where a tooth had been knocked out by a fishmonger's crate and never replaced because there was never quite money enough for anything that wasn't food. He dropped onto the stool beside her like he owned it, which in every way that mattered, he did.

"You're doing the face," Tam said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're smiling but you're counting everything." He plucked a dried fig from her supper basket. "Bad night?"

"Bad enough. Marta wants miracles at thread prices." Noor watched the last lantern-seller gutter down the Row. "How's your mother?"

The pause before he answered was half a breath too long. "Same. Coughing. The physicker wants three silver just to look at her, and that's before the medicines." He turned the fig over in his fingers. "She keeps saying she's fine. She keeps saying it in a way that means she isn't."

Noor's chest did the thing it always did around Tam—a tightening that was half love and half fear, because loving people was the one extravagance she'd never managed to give up, and extravagance was dangerous for women like her. "I'll bring something by tomorrow. The good thread. And there's a physicker on Weavers' Lane who owes me—"

"You can't keep owing people for me."

"I can and I do. It's the only debt I've ever chosen." She said it lightly, but Tam looked at her with those quick, clever eyes that missed nothing except, mercifully, the one thing she needed him to miss.

He grinned, gap-toothed, and stole another fig. "The shrine's been weird," he said, mouth full. "Down on Tanner's Steps. My mother sent me with bread last week and the bread was gone by morning, but the plate was warm. Not sun-warm. *Warm* warm. And old Hesper says her prayer got answered."

"Which prayer?"

"The one about her roof leaking." Tam's grin widened. "It stopped raining on her street. Just her street. Just her roof. It's still bucketing down everywhere else in Vell, and there's this circle of dry cobblestones around her door like somebody drew a line and the rain respected it."

Noor's hands went still on the counter. "That's not answering a prayer. That's showing off."

"That's what Hesper said! She was thrilled. She's telling everyone the gods woke up hungry and she's first in line." Tam shrugged. "People have been leaving whole loaves now. Chickens. Yesterday someone left a live goat, which seems like a lot for a roof."

It seemed like a lot for a roof. It seemed like exactly the kind of thing that happened when something that had slept for centuries opened one eye and remembered it was owed. Noor had spent her life around the small, half-asleep divinity that pooled in old places—the shrine-spirits, the hearth-wights, the little gods of doorways and drains. They answered prayers the way a dog answered a whistle: lazily, if at all, and never with more than was asked. What Tam was describing had *style*. Style meant appetite. Appetite meant a god that was awake enough to want something back.

"Stay away from the shrine," she said.

"It's a shrine, Noor, not a—"

"Stay away from it. Take me seriously for one minute of your life." She heard her own voice come out wrong, too sharp, and softened it with effort. "Please. For me."

Tam studied her, and for a moment she thought he'd argue. Instead he leaned over and bumped his shoulder against hers. "For you," he said. "But you owe me a whole bag of figs for that tone."

* * *

The neighbor's name was Dovet, and he lived two doors down from Noor's room over the chandlery, a widower with a fine baritone voice he used for singing to his bees. On the third night of the strange dry circle around Hesper's door, Noor heard him on the stairs, and then heard nothing at all, which was wrong, because Dovet never came home quietly. He hummed. He announced himself to the whole building like a man arriving at his own coronation.

She found him at his door at dawn, kneeling, his hands pressed flat to the floorboards, his mouth open and working. No sound came out of it. His eyes were fixed on something in the middle distance, something Noor could not see and did not want to, and when she touched his shoulder he flinched as if the touch had come from very far away.

"Dovet. Dovet, what happened?"

He turned to her and mouthed the words, over and over, shaping them with a precision that made her skin crawl: *I asked. I asked. I only asked.*

She got him water. She got him sitting down. And slowly, with gestures and scratched letters in spilled flour, he made her understand: he had gone to the shrine on Tanner's Steps. His bees were failing; the hives went silent in June, which was wrong, and he'd been frightened, and he'd knelt at the old stone and said, *Please let my bees live.* That was all. That was everything. Please let my bees live.

The bees were thriving. He'd checked at first light. Every hive humming like a struck bell, fuller than they'd been in years.

And his voice was gone. Taken, neat as a coin from a purse. Payment, Noor thought, for a prayer that had been answered *too well*—because the god beneath the shrine hadn't just spared his bees, it had made them magnificent, and magnificence had a price, and the god had reached into Dovet and taken the finest thing it found there.

*I only asked.* His mouth shaped it and shaped it, and Noor held his flour-dusted hands and felt twenty years of careful hiding crack down the middle like river ice in thaw.

The gods weren't dead. The gods had woken. And they had woken *hungry*, and Vell was full of people who had forgotten, in two generations of comfortable silence, that prayer was ever a transaction.

She walked home through the grey beginning of morning with her basket of unsold charms hanging from her hand like a dead thing. The mountain stood over the city as it always did, dark against a bruised sky, and for the first time in twenty years Noor let herself look at it properly, let herself feel the attention that poured down off it like cold air off a glacier. It was awake. Something in it was awake, and it had started *keeping accounts*.

* * *

She meant to sleep. She meant to spend the day doing nothing more dangerous than restringing bells and lying to herself about the mountain. But Tam came pounding up the chandlery stairs before noon, two hours after he should have been at the docks hauling rope, and the look on his face stopped her heart before he said a word.

"It's my mother," he said. "She's worse. The physicker came and went and wouldn't take our money because he says it's past taking, and she's—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, twelve years old and hating every soft thing in himself. "She's talking to people who aren't there. She's cold. Noor, she's *cold*, in June."

"Tam—"

"So I went to the shrine." The words came out in a rush, guilty and defiant all at once, his eyes daring her to say *I told you*. "I know what you said. I know. But everyone's going, and Hesper's roof, and the goat lady got a husband back from the dead or something close enough, and I thought—I thought if the gods are awake, then maybe one of them would—" He was crying now, furious at his own tears. "I didn't have anything to offer. I didn't have *anything*. So I just knelt there and I prayed. I said, please. Please, I'll pay anything. Please let my mother live."

The market noise below them seemed to recede, the whole blue morning drawing a breath.

"Anything," Noor repeated. Her mouth had gone dry as ash. "You said *anything*."

"I said anything." Tam wiped his face with his sleeve, and then he frowned, tilting his head, listening to something. "Noor. Did you hear—"

She had heard. Beneath the creak of the chandlery, beneath the cries of the fish-sellers and the rattle of the first market carts, there was a sound like a great door unbolting somewhere far below the street, far below the city, down in the roots of the world. And with it came a warmth against her skin, faint and familiar and utterly wrong, like standing too near a hearth in a freezing room—the same warmth she had carried hidden inside herself for twenty years, the warmth the gutter-dog had smelled on her all those lifetimes ago.

*You smell like the fire that sleeps.*

The fire wasn't sleeping anymore. And it had just heard a twelve-year-old boy offer it *anything*.

"Tam," Noor said, and her voice came out steady, which was the hardest thing she had ever done, "tell me exactly what the shrine felt like when you prayed."
