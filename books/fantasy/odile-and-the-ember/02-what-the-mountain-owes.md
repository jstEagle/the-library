## 2. What the Mountain Owes

Tam told her everything, in the way of boys who have done something enormous and do not yet know it. The shrine had gone warm under his hands. The dark behind the altar had leaned close, like a listener. And when he said *anything*, the word had not fallen into silence the way words should—it had been *caught*. Lifted. Carried away up through the mountain's roots like a coin dropped down a well that was deeper than wells should be.

Noor walked him home through the night market with her hand on his shoulder and her gift straining against its leash the whole way. The rats along the gutter-walls were restless. A cat on a fishmonger's awning stared at the mountain with its pupils blown wide as eclipses. Even the moths had stopped their drunken orbiting of the lanterns and hung motionless in the air, all facing the same direction, like iron filings around a lodestone.

*They're listening,* Noor thought. *All of them. Everything small and quick and alive is listening to something beneath us.*

She left Tam at his mother's door, where the coughing came thin through the shutters like wind through a keyhole, and she did not sleep. She sat in her room above the charm-stall with every warding trinket she owned hung around her neck like a madwoman's jewelry, and she listened too.

Beneath Vell, deep beneath, something turned over in its sleep. Not waking. *Counting.*

* * *

The receipt came three days later.

It arrived the way debts arrive—without ceremony, without mercy. Noor found it folded into Tam's cap, which lay where he always left it, on the nail by his mother's door. He was not on the stair. He was not in the sickroom, where his mother slept in a fever-sweat that had, Noor realized with a lurch, broken sometime in the night. Her breathing was easy. Her color was good. She would wake hungry and healed and asking for her son.

The paper was black—not inked black, but black all the way through, dense as slate, and cold to the touch in a way that made Noor's teeth ache. Across it, in script fine as hair:

**RECEIVED: ONE (1) SOUL, AGED TWELVE YEARS, SOUND AND UNMARKED. IN FULL SETTLEMENT OF THE DEBT OF MIRIAM SANDOVAL'S BREATH.**

And beneath it, pressed into the paper itself rather than printed upon it, a seal: a mountain with an eye at its peak, and around the eye, a ring of tiny flames.

Noor read it eleven times. On the twelfth reading her hands had stopped shaking, which frightened her more than the shaking had, because it meant the grief had finished its work and hardened into something else. Something with edges.

She went first to the magistrate's office, because grief wants the law, at first. Grief believes someone must be accountable.

The clerk looked at the black receipt for a long moment and then set it face-down on his desk, as if it might see him.

"There's no procedure for this," he said.

"There's a boy," Noor said. "There's a stolen boy."

"And there is no procedure." He slid the receipt back across the desk without touching it again. "I'm sorry. Take it up with the Court Beneath, if you can find its door."

* * *

Magistrate Ilsa Vane kept her office above the Hall of Records, and she kept her real work beneath the floorboards of it.

For six years she had been pulling threads. Soul-ledgers, the old clerks called them, half-laughing—the ledgers no one admitted keeping, in which the court beneath the mountain recorded what it was owed. A harvest promised here. A firstborn pledged there. Debts of miracle and debt of breath, compounding interest written in a hand that predated the city's founding. Vane had copied fragments by lamplight for six years, telling herself she was only documenting an eccentricity of the archives, a superstition of dead clerks.

Then, eight months ago, she had matched a ledger entry against a death record. A cooper named Anselm Roke, who had prayed at the old shrine for his wife's recovery and woken with no voice and no memory of ever having had one—and whose ledger line, dated the same night, read: **RECEIVED: ONE (1) VOICE, MALE, FORTY-ONE YEARS, IN PART PAYMENT.**

The superstition had arithmetic in it. That was the thing about the ledgers that kept Vane awake at night. They balanced. To the copper, to the breath, they balanced.

So when a woman came to her private door at dusk—a charm-seller from the night market, ordinary as bread, holding a black receipt in a hand that did not tremble—Vane did what she had trained herself never to do.

She believed her instantly.

"Tam Sandoval," the woman said. "Twelve years old. His mother was dying; he prayed at the shrine. This came instead of him." She laid the receipt on Vane's desk. "I want to know where the court keeps what it collects."

Vane looked at the seal for a long time. The eye at the mountain's peak seemed to look back, and she had learned, over six years, not to flinch from it.

"Sit down," she said. "This will take longer than you want it to, and end somewhere worse than you expect."

"I don't care where it ends," Noor said. "I care where it starts."

"Then we start with the ledgers." Vane pulled up a floorboard and drew out a bundle of papers tied in lawyer's string. "And then, I think, we start with the priest."

* * *

Brother Cassian lived in the ruin of the Shrine of the Sundered Flame, in the one cell still roofed against the rain, and he was sweeping ash when they found him—an old man in a robe gone grey as his beard, sweeping ash that only fell again, morning after morning, year after year, because that was what a last priest did. He tended a god who had been shattered before Vell had walls, and he swept the floor of a church with no congregation, and he had done it so long that the doing had become a kind of prayer.

He heard Noor out without interrupting. Then he set down his broom.

"Show me your hands," he said.

Noor frowned. "What?"

"Not the receipt. Your hands." When she hesitated, he said, gently, "Child, I have waited forty years for someone to come to this door asking about the Ember. I expected a scholar, or a fool, or an agent of the court. I did not expect—" he nodded at her hands, at the faint warmth that rose from them like heat off summer stone, visible now, in this place, to eyes that knew how to see— "*that.*"

Noor looked down. Her hands were glowing. Faintly, fitfully, the way coals breathe light between the ash—but glowing, here, in the ruin of the shattered god's house, and she understood with a falling sensation that they had always glowed, and that every charm she had ever sold, every harmless little trick she had used to bury her true gift, had been ash heaped over a coal.

"What am I?" she asked.

"A seed," Cassian said. "Or the ground a seed was planted in—I've never been sure which. The Ember was caged beneath the mountain before this city drew its first breath. A god, or the heart of one, cut loose from its body and locked away. And a caged god cannot feed on worship. So it learned to feed on souls instead. And to keep its feeding hidden, it seeded gifts into the bloodlines of the city above—small powers, beast-speakers, wish-whisperers, hearth-touched—so that prayers would rise like smoke, and it could choose among them. Choose the desperate ones. The ones who'd say *anything*." His voice cracked on the old word. "Your gift isn't yours alone, child. It's a fishing line, and the hook is beneath the mountain, and until three days ago you never once pulled on it."

"The boy," Noor said. "Tam. Can he be gotten back?"

Cassian was quiet a long moment. Ash sifted down through the broken roof, settling on his shoulders like a second robe.

"Souls paid to the Ember are kept in the court beneath," he said at last. "There are bone-roads through the mountain's roots—the old pilgrim ways, from before the sealing. I know them. I swore forty years ago I would walk them if ever there was a reason." He looked at her glowing hands, and then at Magistrate Vane standing grim in the doorway with her bundle of stolen ledgers, and something moved across his ruined old face that might have been hope, arriving like a stranger after long absence. "A priest with no god, a magistrate with no law that reaches down there, and a seed of the fire itself. If the three of us cannot open that court, nothing born in Vell can."

"No," Vane said. "Nothing born in Vell can. That's rather the problem, isn't it?"

But she was already untying the string on her ledgers, already spreading six years of stolen arithmetic across the ash-covered floor, and Noor stood among the falling grey with her hands burning and her grief gone hard and bright as slag, and said:

"Then we go down, and we take him back, and we make the mountain pay *us*."

Above the ruined shrine, far above, the mountain's eye opened in the dark—and read, in the ledger of the world, three new names beginning to move toward its door.
