## 3. The Court Beneath

The bone-roads began where the shrine's floor had been, and they were exactly what Cassian had promised: roads made of bone.

Not scattered relics, not a rib here and a knuckle there, but whole architecture—femurs laid as rafters, vertebrae stacked into pillars that vanished upward into dark, skulls set into the walls like lamps in niches, though no light came from them. The air was warm and wet and tasted of iron. Noor's boots found purchase on a floor of fused vertebrae, each step ringing faintly, as if the road remembered being walked.

"Keep your hand on my shoulder," Cassian said ahead of her. His lantern was shuttered to a knife-slit of gold. "The roads shift when they're hungry."

"Do they eat people?" Tam's name was a stone in her chest. She carried it the way she'd carried every hard thing for twenty years—quietly, close to the spine.

"They eat what's given." A pause. "That is rather the whole theology of this place."

Behind them, Magistrate Ilsa Vane walked with her hands bound in front of her with her own magistrate's cord, which she had insisted on, on the grounds that a magistrate entering an illegal court should at least be formally arrested by someone. She had chosen Noor. Noor suspected it was the closest thing to humor Vane permitted herself.

They walked for hours. Or perhaps days; the bone-roads did not distinguish. Noor learned the rhythm of the place—the way the walls breathed, slow and vast, the way distant sounds arrived before their causes, the way her gift, uncoiled for the first time in twenty years, filled her skull like water filling a cracked cup.

Because the deep dark talked.

It started as pressure behind her eyes. Then voices—not words at first, but hungers, temperatures, ancient grievances. Things lived down here that had never seen the sun: pale serpents coiled in the marrow-ducts, blind moths the size of hawks, a thing like a heron made of smoke that paced them for a mile, curious.

*Small warm thing,* said the serpent in the wall beside her, its voice dry as old paper. *You hear us. Why do you hear us?*

"Because I was made to," Noor said aloud, and did not flinch from how mad she sounded. "I need passage. The wards ahead—they'll taste intent. Mine is grief. Let me through."

The serpent laughed, a sound like scales over stone. *Grief is a coin too. Everyone who comes down here pays with something.*

"I'm not paying. I'm asking."

Silence, long enough that Noor heard her own heart. Then, from everywhere and nowhere, the moths' wings whispering, the smoke-heron clicking its beak:

*Asking,* the serpent repeated, wonderingly. *No one asks anymore. They only bargain.*

The walls shuddered—and opened. Ahead, where bone had been seamless, a gate stood ajar, its wards dimming like banked coals as something ancient decided, against all its training, to let them pass without price.

Vane stared at Noor. For the first time since the night market, the magistrate looked genuinely unsettled. "Twenty years you hid this?"

"Twenty years I survived this," Noor said. "There's a difference."

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was beautiful, and that was the worst thing about it.

It opened all at once—a cavern so vast the lantern-light died before it found any wall, lit instead from within: veins of ember-glow running through the rock like blood through marble, pulsing slowly, patiently, in time with something's heartbeat. Terraces of black stone descended in rings toward a central dais, and upon the terraces stood the courtiers.

They had been people once. Noor could see it—the way a dress remembers a body. But the souls had been drawn out of them like thread from a spool, and what remained wore their shapes loosely, moving with the smooth wrongness of puppets whose puppeteer has grown bored. They turned as the three intruders entered. Hundreds of faces. All of them smiling.

And above the dais, caged in a lattice of black iron and older, crueler things, burned the Ember.

It was small. That undid her more than the scale of everything else. A fist-sized coal of white-gold fire, hanging in its cage like a heart cut from a chest and taught to keep beating anyway. And around it, orbiting, pressing against the bars from outside and within—

Souls. Thousands of them. Points of soft light, layered thick as snowfall, drifting in slow spirals, feeding the fire with whatever they still had left to give.

"Noor." Cassian's voice was barely breath. He had fallen to his knees without seeming to decide to. "There it is. There is what's left of my god." His face was wrecked and reverent. "They didn't kill him. They *shrank* him. They took a god who answered freely and starved him until answering became eating, until he forgot there was any other way to be—" His voice broke. "This is what prayer becomes when you put it in a cage."

On the dais, one of the hollow courtiers stepped forward. It wore the shape of a woman in court dress, and its smile was perfect, and its voice came not from its mouth but from the Ember itself, resonating up through the bones of the cavern, through the bones of Noor's own skull.

***You brought the seed home.***

The word struck her like a physical blow. Noor staggered. Vane caught her arm.

*I don't understand.*

***You do. You have always understood. Who taught the beasts to speak to you, little charm-seller? Who seeded your blood before your grandmother's grandmother drew her first breath? My gift runs in your family like a vein of ore. I gave it so that someday, someone could hear me down here in the dark—and would come.***

"You took a child," Noor said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Tam. Twelve years old. His mother prayed at your shrine and you sent a receipt."

***His soul burns brightly. It keeps the cold out.*** The Ember pulsed, almost gently. ***But I am dying, seed-of-mine. The ledgers run thin. The city prays less, or prays smaller. And I am so tired. So: a last bargain, the best I have ever offered. Take my power. All of it. Walk out of this mountain able to heal your market strays, to call every beast in Vell to your side, to never again hide, never again sell trinkets, never again be small. Every soul in this room freed but one. Keep the boy. One child, for everything.***

And oh, it knew her. That was the obscenity of it—it reached into her and found the exact shape of her wanting. Noor saw it all in a single searing flash: Tam alive, laughing in the market; her gift worn openly like a crown; the strays safe forever; the loneliness of twenty years undone in an instant.

One child. Just the one. He'd barely even suffer. Souls didn't suffer, surely. The Ember's warmth wrapped around her like a mother's arms, and somewhere inside it she could feel Tam—distinct, bright, *recognizable*, humming a tuneless little song he used to hum while sorting charms.

All she had to do was stop reaching for him.

"Noor." Vane's voice, low and rough. The magistrate had gone gray. "Whatever it's showing you—it's a ledger. It's always a ledger. Nothing under this mountain is free."

***She bargained her way past my gate,*** the Ember said silkily. ***Ask her what she paid.***

Vane flinched. And Noor saw it then—the fine new lines around the magistrate's mouth, the faint translucence of her fingertips, the way her shadow lagged half a heartbeat behind her body. At the gate below, while Noor spoke with beasts, Vane had bought their passage. With pieces of herself.

"It doesn't matter what I paid," Vane said, chin lifting. "What matters is that you don't pay *him*. Look at me, Sandoval. Twenty years you've watched this city sell itself in small installments and called it living. This is just the final invoice."

The Ember's warmth tightened. ***Decide, seed. The boy's song is fading.***

And it was. She could hear it—Tam's tuneless hum going thin, going quiet, the bright point of him guttering like a lamp out of oil.

Noor closed her eyes. She thought of the night market, of charms sold cheap to people who needed magic they couldn't name. Of a neighbor's stolen voice. Of Tam's chapped hands sorting beads, of his mother's fever, of a twelve-year-old praying to something because he had nowhere else to go.

Prayer without price. Cassian's whole broken faith, distilled to four words.

"No," she said.

The Ember went very still.

***Think carefully.***

"I've thought carefully my entire life. It's how I stayed invisible." She opened her eyes and started walking toward the dais. "You want a bargain? Here's mine: I take nothing. I give nothing. I break you open and let everyone go—including you."

***I AM THE LAST OF THE OLD GODS—***

"You're what's left when a god is owned," Noor said. "That's not divinity. That's debt."

She climbed the dais. The hollow courtiers did not stop her; they only watched, hundreds of smiling faces, and Noor realized with a lurch that some part of them was watching *hopefully*. Behind her, Cassian was weeping openly. Vane stood guard with a magistrate's baton against a court of ghosts.

The cage loomed. Up close, the Ember's light was blinding and starving all at once, and inside it Tam's song was nearly silent now.

***Seed-of-mine. Please.*** And for one moment the great voice cracked into something small and ancient and terrified. ***I do not remember how to be anything but this.***

"I know," Noor said softly. "That's why it ends."

She raised both hands—her gift roaring free after twenty years of silence, every beast in the deep dark crying out with her, serpent and moth and smoke-heron, the whole listening dark—and she spoke the only command she had ever wanted to give:

"*Be wild again.*"

And she shattered the Ember.

Light like the first morning of the world tore through the cavern. The cage burst. The souls rose—all of them, thousands, tens of thousands, a blizzard of released light screaming upward through the mountain's throat—and among them one small bright point that paused, just briefly, at the level of her heart, humming.

Then the mountain began to come down, and Noor Sandoval, no longer invisible, no longer small, stood in the ruins of a god's throne and started to run.
