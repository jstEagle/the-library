## 4. Ashes That Answer

The Ember did not shatter so much as it *exhaled*.

Noor had expected violence—glass screaming apart, a sound like the mountain's spine breaking. Instead there was a moment of terrible stillness, the caged god's light gathering itself as if to speak one last word, and then the cage of gold and bone around it split along a seam no eye could find, and everything that had been held inside came out at once.

Light first. Then voices.

They poured through the vault beneath the court like water through a broken dam—centuries of souls, sold and stacked and fed upon, all of them moving at once toward the dark mouths of the bone-roads. Noor felt them pass through her like wind through wheat, each one carrying a name she would never know: a fisherman who'd traded his luck for his daughter's fever breaking; a bride who'd sold her grief; a child whose mother had prayed too literally at a shrine that should have stayed asleep. They brushed her gift as they went, and her gift—*their* gift, seeded in her blood by this dying thing so long ago—rang like a struck bell.

Somewhere in that flood was Tam.

She ran with them. Cassian shouted something behind her—the priest's voice cracked down the middle—and the mountain groaned around them, its wards unraveling thread by thread now that the thing they were woven to protect had come apart. Noor followed the river of light up through passages that had never known feet, speaking to the deep-dark things as she went: *move, little brothers, move, the house is falling*, and the pale blind creatures of the bone-roads scattered aside like startled moths.

She found him at the mouth of the shrine-cave where the mountain met the city's roots, sitting in the ash-fall with his knees drawn up, blinking at the dawn-gray light as though he'd never seen weather before.

"Tam."

He looked at her. He looked *through* her. His eyes were the same brown they had always been, but behind them was a long corridor of empty rooms, doors standing open on nothing.

"I know you," he said slowly, the way a man reads a language he learned as an infant and has not spoken since. "I think I know you."

The ash settled on both of them like snow. Noor sat down beside him in it and did not touch him, because some returning things startle if you reach too fast.

"That's enough," she said. "For now, that's enough."

* * *

The city learned what had happened before it understood it.

On the third morning after, prayers stopped being answered. A woman at the old shrine asked the god of thresholds for safe passage across the sea and received, instead, silence—and wept with relief, because silence meant the bargain was dead. The old gods had not died with the Ember. That was the strange mercy of it, the thing Cassian explained to anyone who would listen, standing in the ruins of his shattered temple with ash still under his fingernails: the gods had been *scattered*, flung back into the world like seed from a burst pod. They were in the rain now, and the roots of trees, and the crooked geometry of crows. Wild again. Unowned. Answerable to no ledger, purchasable by no court.

"They will hear you," Cassian told the crowds that gathered, growing larger each week, "but they cannot *keep* you anymore. Prayer is a door, not a contract. Knock when you need to. Walk away when you're done. Nothing is for sale."

Some people hated this. Noor saw it in the market—the ones who had grown comfortable trading pieces of themselves, who had built lives on the arithmetic of the soul-ledgers, who now stood before shrines offering coins that clattered unanswered into empty bowls. The court beneath the mountain was broken, but the court's remnants walked above ground still: gray-robed clerks with ink-stained fingers, hunting the ones who had done the breaking. Noor's face was on their lists. She felt their eyes in the night market, in the fish-gutters' alley, in the pauses between conversations that lasted a half-beat too long.

Her invisibility was gone. Twenty years of harmless charms, of being nobody, of selling luck-knots and fever-wards while keeping the true gift folded small and hidden—and now every beast in Vell turned its head when she passed, and every remnant clerk knew her name.

Magistrate Ilsa Vane solved the problem of the ledgers in the way Vane solved most problems: publicly, and with fire.

She did it from the steps of the Hall of Records on a morning of hard blue sky, with the confiscated soul-ledgers stacked in a pyramid taller than she was—eleven years of her quiet, disgraced, obsessive investigation made suddenly, catastrophically official. She read three names aloud. Just three. A cooper who had sold his steadiness. A girl who had sold her singing voice to buy her brother out of debt. Tam Sandoval, aged twelve, taken as payment for the Ember. Then she put the torch to the pyramid and stood in the heat of it with her magistrate's chain torn from her neck, no longer any kind of law, watching eleven years of bought and sold humanity curl into black butterflies.

"The court kept accounts," she told the crowd, her voice carrying flat and clear over the crackle. "I have burned the accounts. What remains of the court may come collect the debt from me personally."

Three remnants tried, over the following weeks. None of them managed it twice.

* * *

Tam remembered in pieces, the way ice remembers being water.

He came back to the market strays in the second week, drawn—Cassian said—by habit older than memory, and stood at the edge of Noor's stall turning a luck-knot over in his hands as though he'd never seen string before.

"You make these," he said.

"Yes."

"They don't do anything." Not an accusation. A question.

"No," Noor said. "They don't do anything. They're just knots."

He frowned at the knot, then at her, and something moved far back in those emptied rooms—a door swinging on rusted hinges. "You used to talk to the gulls," he said. "Behind the smokehouse. When you thought nobody watched. You talked to them and they *answered*."

Noor's hands went still on the string.

"I remember that," Tam said, wonderingly, like a man finding one lamp still lit in a burned house. "I don't remember much. But I remember that."

He stayed. He did not leave. And slowly, in the months that followed, the rooms behind his eyes filled again—not in the old order, not all the way, but filling. He forgot Tuesdays. He forgot his mother's face until Cassian sat with him and prayed—not *for* anything, just *toward* her, the new prayer without price—and afterward Tam wept for an hour and said her name right. He forgot how to whittle and relearned it worse. But he remembered the market. He remembered warmth. He remembered, always, always, the gulls.

* * *

They came for Noor on a night of thin rain, four remnants in gray robes with iron rods that hummed with borrowed warding, and they found her at her stall in the night market surrounded by everyone she loved.

Vane was there, ostensibly buying eels, with a magistrate's memory of exactly which laws still applied and a knife for the ones that didn't. Cassian was there, ostensibly drunk, with a priest's certainty underneath. The strays were there—Marta with her ladle, old Fen with his dogs, a dozen others who had eaten Noor's soup and worn Noor's charms through twenty hard winters—and Tam stood directly in front of her, twelve years old and small and utterly unafraid, holding a luck-knot that didn't do anything.

"She's ours," Tam said. "Whatever you think she owes you, she doesn't owe you. She's *ours*."

The remnants looked at the crowd. The crowd looked back. Somewhere overhead, uninvited and unbought, a gull cried once in the rain—and Noor felt her gift rise in her like a tide answering the moon, wild now too, unowned, hers and not for sale—and spoke to the birds of Vell, all of them at once, in a voice like weather:

*Come.*

They came. Hundreds of them, off rooftops and riggings and the smoking eaves of the fish-market, wheeling down through the thin rain in a white screaming spiral that sent the remnants running with their humming rods and their lists and their ink-stained fingers, and did not stop chasing them until they were well past the city gates.

Afterward the market laughed, and shook itself dry, and lit the lamps, and Noor stood in the middle of it with ash still somewhere in her hair that no amount of washing ever quite removed, and thought: *this*. Not safety. Safety was gone, sold at last for something better. This—belonging chosen over hiding, family standing openly in the rain, a boy who remembered her gulls and nothing else and loved her anyway.

Tam tugged her sleeve. He was looking past the market lights, toward the mountain, where the old peak stood dark and open-mouthed and empty for the first time in a thousand years.

"Noor," he said. "Something's moving up there. In the deep roads. Something *new*."

And far off, faint as a heartbeat, the mountain answered.
