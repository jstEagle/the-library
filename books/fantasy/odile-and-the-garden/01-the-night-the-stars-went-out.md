## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with stealing from a witch, Odile reflected as she sprinted down the moonlit path with a grimoire the size of a paving stone hugged to her chest, was that witches knew things.

Mistress Ysolde had known, for instance, exactly which floorboard creaked. She had known that Odile took her tea too sweet and her lies too thin. And she had known — this was the part that made Odile's stomach twist even now, even running, even free — she had known she was dying, because she had spent her last week of life arranging her own affairs with the brisk efficiency of a woman packing for a long journey, and had looked at Odile more than once with an expression that said *you'll do something foolish, child, and I intend to make sure you survive it.*

Well. Here was the foolish thing.

Odile's boots hammered the frozen ruts of the goat track. Behind her, the cottage sat dark on its knoll above the valley, windows shuttered, chimney cold for the first time in forty years. No one raised an alarm. No one ever would. The only witness to the theft was the wind, and the wind had never once carried tales out of Thornfell — it was too busy carrying them in.

She allowed herself one glance back, just one, and felt the old ache rise in her throat. Three years she'd lived in that crooked little house. Three years of grinding ink and memorizing herb-lore and being scolded for tracking mud across sigils freshly chalked on the kitchen floor. Three years of being called "child" by a woman who had never once called her anything warmer, and who had nevertheless taught her to read the sky like scripture and to hold a hedge-spell the way you'd hold a bird: gently, and ready to let it go.

And now the grimoire was hers. Stolen, if you wanted to be precise about it, and Odile did try to be precise, because precision was the difference between a thief and an heir, and she had decided — somewhere between the deathbed and the graveside, somewhere in the terrible quiet after they'd put the mistress in the ground — that she was going to be an heir.

Ysolde had left her nothing. Not a coin, not a shawl, not so much as the good iron kettle. The village had divided up the cottage contents before the grave-mound was even properly settled, the way villages do, quick and shameless and half-kind about it. But the grimoire had been under the floorboard beneath the bed, wrapped in oilcloth, with a note pinned to it in Ysolde's spidery hand:

*If you're reading this, you've already decided. Don't lose it. Don't read past page 300 until you must. And whatever happens to the border — and it will happen, child, sooner than either of us would like — remember what I told you about the garden.*

That was all. No explanation. No farewell. Just instructions, like always, and Odile had stood there in the emptying cottage holding a book heavier than grief and thought, *well then, I suppose I'm running away.*

It had seemed very clear at the time. It still seemed mostly clear, which for Odile was practically a religious experience.

The smuggler road dropped through the pines toward the pass, and she took it at a dead run, because the Crown's wardens kept their garrison in the valley town and it would take them perhaps two days to notice that a dead witch's most dangerous possession had grown legs. Two days' head start over the Thornfell roads — the winding, unmarked, deliberately-confusing paths that smugglers had used since before anyone could remember — and then down into the low country where a girl with a stolen book could disappear among ten thousand other girls with ordinary problems.

She was going to be rich. Or at least comfortable. Bramble Hask in Cindershade paid scandalously well for pages of genuine high craft, and the grimoire had three hundred pages she wasn't supposed to read yet, which meant — she did the arithmetic as she ran, because fear always sharpened her sums — which meant she could sell maybe fifty and keep the rest safe, and eat for a year, and find some warm southern town where nobody knew what a witch's apprentice smelled like (woodsmoke, tansy, and bad decisions) and start over.

Exhilaration hit her halfway down the switchbacks, sudden and fizzy as cider gone hard. She laughed out loud, breath steaming, and the laugh echoed off the cliffs and came back sounding younger than she felt. She was free. She was *fast*. She was twenty years old and the whole world lay below the pass like an unwritten page, and Mistress Ysolde could scold her no more, and —

The first star went out directly overhead, with a sound like a wet finger pinching a candle flame.

Odile stopped so abruptly she nearly went over the edge of the switchback.

She knew the sky. That was not a boast; it was simply true, the way it was true that a fisherman knows water. Ysolde had drilled the heavens into her nightly, standing in the frozen garden pointing with a stick: *the Shepherd's Yoke, the Seven Sisters, the Wandering King, and there, child, there along the peaks — the Border Stars, the old lights, the ones the covenant hung so the Fae would always know where the door was.* Forty-one stars in the Border Chain, bright enough to cast shadows on snow, steady as oath-stones. Odile had fallen asleep under them a thousand nights. They were as fixed in her mind as her own hands.

And one of them had just — blinked. Like an eye. Like a lamp in a window when someone inside decides it's late.

"No," Odile said, to the star, to herself, to the general unfairness of the universe. "No, no, you don't."

A second star guttered. A third. Then a whole cluster of them, south along the chain, winking out in a ragged line like a row of houses going dark street by street, and the darkness behind each one wasn't the ordinary soft darkness of night but something *thicker*, something with texture, something that made her eyes slide off it the way they slid off the place where a tooth used to be.

The exhilaration curdled. She felt it turn over in her chest, warm wine to cold water, and she stood on the smuggler road with her arms full of stolen magic and watched the sky die.

Because she knew what the Border Stars were. Of course she knew. Every child in the mountain villages knew the story, though most of them got it wrong, sweetened and simplified, the way children's stories are. *Long ago the Fae came over the mountains and the wise ones of both peoples made a garden between the worlds, and hung stars above it so neither side would forget where the fence stood.* Odile had gotten the unsweetened version, straight from Ysolde, who had gotten it from her own mistress, back and back and back: the covenant was not a wall. It was a *promise*, sworn in a garden that existed in both worlds at once, and promises, unlike walls, needed tending. The stars were the tending made visible. Each one anchored a thread of the old oath. As long as they burned, the door between the realms stayed shut — not locked, exactly, but *latched*, politely, mutually, the way civilized neighbors latch a gate.

And they were going out. All of them. North to south along the whole spine of the peaks, the Border Chain dimming like embers under ash, and Odile realized with a lurch that she was watching something that had not happened in six hundred years, something Ysolde had told her was coming *sooner than either of us would like*, and oh, the old woman had been right, the old woman had been right the way she was always right, and Odile wanted very badly to tell her so and could not, and that hurt worse than the cold.

The last star in the chain held on alone for a moment — the Anchor Star, highest over the First Garden, or where the First Garden had supposedly been, though every map Odile had ever seen marked that region with a polite blank and the words *here be weather*. It flickered. It steadied. It flickered again, stubborn, furious, a candle in a draft, and Odile caught herself whispering *hold on, hold on, hold on* as if encouragement had ever once worked on anything.

Then it went out, and the world made a sound.

Not loud, exactly. That was the horror of it. She had expected — her body braced for — thunder, a crack, some great dramatic report worthy of the end of an age. Instead it was a *tinkling*, delicate and enormous at once, like the sound of a chandelier falling in a house so far away you can't see it, like frost fracturing on a pond under your feet when you've walked one step too many. It came from everywhere along the peaks at once. It came from the air itself. The wards weren't shattering like glass, Odile thought wildly; glass was too honest a comparison, glass broke *outward*, glass made shards you could see and sweep up. This was more like the sound of a promise breaking — no edges, no pieces, just a wholeness that stopped being whole, everywhere at once, irretrievable.

Then the light changed.

There was no other word for it. The darkness beyond the dead stars leaned down over the mountains like a curtain drawn, and the moonlight itself went strange, silver shading toward grey shading toward a color Odile had no name for and suspected had no name in any human tongue. The pines along the road threw shadows in the wrong directions. Her breath hung in the air and did not dissipate but drifted, lazily, sideways, against the wind, as if the night had become a room and someone had opened a door at the far end of it.

The familiar mountains became alien territory in the space of a heartbeat. These were slopes she had climbed since she was seven, trees she had carved her initials into, a pass she could have walked blindfolded — and now every stone looked like it was waiting for her to look away first. The wind came down off the heights carrying smells that did not belong to it: crushed violets, hot metal, and underneath everything, faint as a memory, the smell of a garden in summer. In December. At nine thousand feet.

Odile discovered she was gripping the grimoire so hard her fingers ached, and made herself loosen them one at a time, the way Ysolde had taught her to loosen a grip on a spell before it burned her. *Panic is just power pointed the wrong way, child. Point it somewhere useful.*

"Right," she said aloud, because saying things aloud helped, and also because the silence had begun to feel inhabited. "Right. Okay. Options."

Option one: stand on a haunted road discussing options with herself until something in the new darkness noticed her. Poor option. Discarded.

Option two: back down the way she'd come, to the valley, to the wardens, to people whose entire profession was about to become catastrophically relevant. Also poor. Wardens solved problems with ledgers and rope, and Odile was carrying the single most incriminating object on either side of a border that had just ceased to exist. Besides, she had a note to deliver and a covenant to tend, or at least the memory of a note that said to tend it, and none of that happened in the valley.

Which left option three: over the pass, as planned, faster than planned, with considerably less sightseeing. The grimoire's marginalia — Ysolde's marginalia, decades of it, tiny annotations crowding the margins like ivy — mentioned places. A chapel that drowned. A shrine on the shoulder of the mountain. A garden beneath ice. Odile hadn't let herself think too hard about why those notes existed, or why the old woman had circled certain of them twice, or why page 300 was forbidden until she *must*. One catastrophe at a time. Tonight's catastrophe was getting over the pass before the dark finished settling in.

She ran.

The smuggler road climbed through the pines, and the pines were wrong now, their shadows lying toward the moon instead of away from it, and twice she passed spots where frost glittered on the ground in patterns too regular to be natural — spirals, she noticed, spirals within spirals, the kind of pattern that appears when something invisible has pressed its palm against the world. She did not stop to examine them. Examine them tomorrow. Examine them from a tavern, ideally, with a drink in hand and several strong walls nearby.

At the top of the last climb, where the road crested the shoulder below the high pass, she finally paused — bent double, lungs full of knives, sweat freezing in her collar — and looked back the way she had come, because some part of her needed to see the valley one more time, needed proof that home still existed even if the sky didn't.

The valley was fine. The valley was dark and sleeping and utterly unchanged, lamplit Cindershade a scatter of orange sparks far below, smoke rising straight from the chimneys, everything ordinary and oblivious. The catastrophe had stayed on the heights, hers alone, a private apocalypse with excellent views.

Then she turned forward, toward the pass, and saw the star fall.

It came down ahead of her — not one of the dead Border Stars, or maybe exactly one of them, she couldn't have said, physics having apparently resigned for the evening — a burning shard trailing green-white fire, carving a slow arc across the black gap where the constellations used to be. It fell without sound for a long, impossible moment, beautiful the way avalanches are beautiful, and then it went over the crest of the pass and the night lit up like noon and the sound arrived all at once, a *crack* followed by a rolling boom that set the pines shedding snow in white curtains.

The shockwave hit her like a friendly giant's shove. Odile sat down hard in the snow, grimoire and all, and watched a pillar of pale fire stand up over the pass and slowly, slowly collapse into sparks.

For a while she did nothing at all. Her heart performed arrangements it had no business attempting. Snow melted under her and soaked through, and she barely noticed.

Because here was the thing, the thing she would carry with her through everything that came after: the shard had fallen *on the pass*. On her pass. On the exact route between her and the rest of her life, with a precision that suggested the night sky was not merely dying but *aiming*.

And — smaller thing, worse thing — in the last second before it vanished behind the ridge, Odile could have sworn the falling star had changed course. A degree, no more. A single deliberate lean, as of a swimmer sighting a shore.

Toward her.

Somewhere up there, in the crater that was surely glowing at this very moment, something was climbing out. Odile did not know how she knew this. She knew it the way she knew the sky, the way she knew the creak of a certain floorboard, the way she had known — and ignored, and regretted — that her mistress was arranging a deathbed instead of recovering from one. Some knowledge doesn't arrive through the eyes. It arrives through whatever it is that witches spend their lives sharpening, and Odile's had just been honed to a point by terror.

She got up. She brushed the snow off with shaking hands. She addressed the grimoire, because addressing oneself was no longer sufficient company.

"We're going around," she told it. "Through the scree fields. Off the road. Quietly. Like sensible criminals."

The grimoire, heavy and silent against her ribs, offered no objection. But as she turned off the smuggler road into the deep drifts, she felt it — faintly, unmistakably — grow warm against her chest. Warm the way a sleeping animal grows warm. Warm the way a held breath feels just before the news, good or ill.

Above the pass, where the stars had been, the dark leaned closer.

And Odile Villalobos, heir to nothing, thief of one very large book, began to run again — downhill this time, toward the fire, because the only other direction was the dark, and she already knew, in the place where witches know things, that the dark knew her name.
