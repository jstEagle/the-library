## 2. What Fell in the High Pass

The stars went out behind her like a row of candles pinched by a giant's wet fingers, and Odile Villalobos did the only sensible thing a runaway apprentice could do under such circumstances.

She kept running.

*Very sensible,* said the sensible voice in her head, which sounded suspiciously like Mistress Yew. *Fleeing an apocalypse at night, alone, with no provisions, wearing shoes two sizes too large because you grabbed the wrong pair off the peg. Textbook.*

"Shut up," Odile panted, to nobody, to the voice, to the mountain.

The Thornfell smuggler roads were not really roads at all — they were suggestions, goat-paths and game trails stitched together by generations of people who had something to hide and a strong preference for not being found while hiding it. In daylight they were treacherous. At night, with every star above the peaks gone dark as a snuffed wick, they were a death sentence written in switchbacks.

Odile ran anyway, because behind her was the cottage where she had spent six years kneading poultices and copying sigils and pretending she wasn't planning to leave, and ahead of her was — well, ahead of her was also terrible, but it was terrible in a direction that didn't include Mistress Yew waking up and finding the grimoire gone from its iron box beneath the hearthstone.

The grimoire thumped against her spine inside its satchel with every stride, heavy as a sleeping cat, warm as one too. It had been warm ever since the wards shattered. She tried not to think about what that meant.

The pass ahead of her — the Kestrel Notch, the only gap through the Thornfells wide enough for a determined mule or a more determined fugitive — was a black seam between blacker rock. She knew it by heart. She'd memorized it from Bramble Hask's maps, back when she'd still been telling herself she was only *planning*, only *considering*, only idly sketching escape routes the way other apprentices idly sketched flowers.

She was perhaps half a mile from the mouth of the notch when the sky tore open.

There was no better word for it. One moment the dark above the pass was simply dark — starless now, but an ordinary, honest darkness, the kind you could trust. The next, something burned through it: a streak of white fire so bright it printed the whole mountainside onto the backs of her eyes, ridge and scree and the skeletal arms of a lightning-struck pine all rendered in searing silver.

Then came the sound. Not a boom — a *peel*, like the world was an orange and something enormous had decided it wanted the skin off.

Odile hit the ground without deciding to. Pebbles rained down around her, hissing where they landed on frost. The burning thing — the shard, she understood distantly, a shard of one of those dead stars, still falling, still falling long after the light of it had died — struck the pass directly ahead of her, precisely where she had intended to walk, precisely where the safe road through the Kestrel Notch began.

The impact rolled through the mountain like a bell being rung underground. Snow sloughed off the high shoulders of the peaks in long white sighs. Heat washed over her face, and with it came a smell she had no name for — ozone and myrrh and something underneath both, something old and cold, like air from a cellar that had been sealed for centuries.

*Mistress Yew would know what that smell is,* thought some small traitorous part of her, and then hated herself for it, and then kept running anyway, because the alternative was standing still and thinking, and thinking was how you ended up staying somewhere forever.

She made it perhaps two hundred yards before her feet betrayed her.

Not literally — though given the week she was having, she wouldn't have ruled it out. No: her feet stopped because her eyes stopped, and her eyes stopped because the crater at the mouth of the pass had begun to glow.

It glowed the way coals glow, deep and patient, a red-gold heart beating in the torn earth. And out of that glow, something climbed.

Her first thought, absurdly, was: *a man.* Her second thought was: *no.* Her third thought was the truest one, the one that lived in the marrow-deep place where hedge-witches keep their oldest instincts, the place Mistress Yew had called "the second sight and the first good sense": *that is not a man, whatever it is wearing.*

It crawled up the crater's rim with the unhurried grace of something that had never once needed to hurry. It was tall — taller than any man she'd seen, and it moved wrong for its height, joints flexing a half-beat after they should, like a puppeteer learning a new marionette. Frost steamed off its shoulders. Where its hands touched stone, the stone bloomed briefly with tiny white flowers before remembering itself and dying again.

And it wore a face.

That was the horror of it. Not horns, not too many eyes, none of the shapes from the cautionary tales. A face. A human face, weathered and kind around the eyes, with a short grey beard and a scar across one eyebrow — the face of a warden, one of the Crown's border wardens, the sort of face you'd trust to tell you the road ahead was flooded or the toll had doubled. Odile had seen a hundred faces like it in the market towns, checking papers, stamping manifests, being boringly, reassuringly official.

But the face sat wrong. It sat like a coat buttoned by someone who had only ever read about buttons. The eyes were the worst part: they were brown and warm and human, and behind them something else looked out, something vast and patient and *hungry in a way that has nothing to do with food*, the way winter is hungry for green things.

The figure reached the rim of the crater, stood, and brushed ash from its borrowed knees with two precise flicks of its fingers.

Then it smiled at her, and the smile fit the face perfectly, which somehow made everything worse.

"Young woman," it said, and the voice was the warden's voice too, gravelly and tired and kind, "you look like someone in need of safe passage."

Odile stood very still on the frozen trail, forty yards of broken ground between them, and did the arithmetic of terror. Behind her: six miles of switchbacks she'd already covered, and beyond them, eventually, the cottage, and the wards' broken glass still glittering in the dark like a spilled chandelier. Ahead of her: this. Above her: nothing, no stars, no moon, no witnesses but the dead ones.

"I'm fine," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Thank you."

"It is a hard road through the Thornfells," the creature said, tilting the stolen head with a gesture of genuine, almost tender concern. "Cold. Dark, now, especially dark. Bandits in the low passes, and worse things higher up." It spread its hands — the warden's hands, calloused in the right places, nails trimmed square. "I offer warmth. Company. Safe passage south, past Cindershade, wherever you're going."

"And what does safe passage cost?" Odile asked, because Mistress Yew had drilled one law into her above all others, above herb-lore, above sigil-work, above even *don't touch anything glowing*: **Nothing from the Otherfell is free, and the cheaper it sounds, the more it costs.**

The creature's smile widened by a degree. Somewhere in the dark, the tiny white flowers bloomed and died along the edge of the crater, a silent round of applause.

"Wise," it said. "Good. I prefer customers who ask the price; it saves such tedious surprise later." It pressed one hand to its borrowed chest. "I ask only the book you carry. The grimoire. Give it to me, and I will walk you to the far side of these mountains myself, and nothing on the road will touch you. You have my word."

"Whose word?" Odile asked. "Yours? Or the face's?"

Something flickered behind the brown eyes — not anger, exactly. Interest. The interest of a chess player who has offered a pawn sacrifice and finds the opponent studying the board instead of grabbing.

"The face," it admitted, and there was a strange music under the words now, the warden's gravel thinning like ice over a stream. "The face belonged to a decent man. Warden Emmerich Voss, twenty-two years of service, fond of his supper and his dog. He was on the wall when the wards fell. He screamed quite beautifully." It flexed the fingers, admiring them. "I am called Vesperin. Though I suppose names are premature between strangers. Call me... a fellow traveler."

"A fellow traveler who crawls out of holes in the sky."

"All travel is crawling out of one hole or another," Vesperin said pleasantly. "You, for instance, crawled out of a cottage. I watched you leave it. Six years you served the old woman, and you repaid her with theft. We are not so different, you and I."

The words landed harder than they should have, because they were true, and Vesperin saw her flinch — saw it, catalogued it, filed it away behind those warm brown eyes.

"She gave me nothing but chores and criticism," Odile said.

"Mm. And yet you took only the book. Not the silver. Not the good cloak. The book." He took a step forward, unhurried, frost blooming and dying under his boots. "Which tells me either that you are sentimental, or that you understand what the book is worth. Sentiment I can work around. Understanding interests me."

"Then here's my understanding," Odile said, and her heart was hammering so hard she could feel it in her teeth, and her voice shook, and she let it shake, because a shaking voice was honest and honest things were harder to twist. "No. The answer is no. Whatever you're offering, whatever this is — the answer is no, and it will stay no, and if you take one more step toward me I will do something regrettable with the contents of this satchel."

Vesperin stopped. He regarded her for a long moment, head tilted, and in the crater behind him the glow pulsed once, twice, like a slow heartbeat.

"Do you know," he said softly, "in three hundred years of exile, I have been offered many bargains and refused almost none of them, because I am the one who makes them. But I cannot remember the last time someone told me *no* to my face." The smile returned, smaller now, and stranger. "It suits you badly. You are much better at running."

"I've had practice."

"So I see." He stepped aside — actually stepped aside, clearing the path into the Kestrel Notch, gesturing at it with the warden's courteous hand. "Then go. Run south, little thief. The road is open. I give you that freely, with no price attached, purely for the pleasure of watching you take it."

"That's the most frightening offer yet," Odile said.

"I know." He sounded delighted about it.

Every instinct she owned was screaming at her to turn and bolt, and she nearly obeyed. But Mistress Yew's voice — the real one, the memory of it, dry as autumn paper — surfaced one last instruction: *When a Fae gives you a gift, look at it very carefully before you carry it anywhere.*

So instead of running, Odile looked. At the path. At the notch. At the snow lining its walls.

And there it was. Along both sides of the pass, delicate as lace, fine as spider silk: threads of pale light strung from rock to rock, invisible until you knew to look, humming faintly, like harp strings waiting for a finger.

A web. He'd strung the entire pass while they talked. Walk through, and those threads would sing her name and location and probably her shoe size to whatever waited at the other end.

She looked back up at Vesperin, and this time she smiled, and it cost her everything she had.

"Clever," she said. "But I grew up on a smuggler's maps, and my mistress taught me to see ward-threads before I could write my own name. Your web is lovely. I won't be walking through it."

For the first time, the thing behind the warden's face went very still.

"You can *see* them," it said. Not a question. Almost an accusation.

"Six years of copying sigils leaves marks on a person." She was already backing away, sideways, off the trail, toward the scree slope where no threads gleamed — the ugly way, the slow way, the way that would tear her remaining boots to ribbons and probably her legs besides. "Goodbye, Vesperin. Thank you for the flowers."

"*Wait—*" he said, and the warden's voice cracked down the middle, and underneath it something enormous spoke, something that made the dead stars feel close, "*girl, wait, you don't understand what's coming—*"

"No," Odile said, agreeably. "Probably not. Goodbye!"

And then she ran — properly ran this time, scrambling up the scree on all fours when the slope demanded it, the grimoire slamming warm against her back, pebbles singing away downhill behind her. Once, glancing back, she saw him standing at the crater's rim, the borrowed face tilted up to watch her go, and she saw him raise one hand — not in threat, not in pursuit.

In salute. Slow. Courteous. Certain.

Like a man waving goodbye to something he already owned.

* * *

She ran until the pass was a red glow far below and behind, until her lungs burned and her legs shook and the scree had chewed her palms bloody. Then she climbed into the hollow beneath a leaning boulder, wrapped her arms around her knees, and allowed herself, at last, to shake.

"He knows my face," she whispered to the dark. "He watched the cottage. He knows about the book. He knows I can see ward-threads."

Each sentence was worse than the last. She said them aloud because saying them aloud made them real, and real things could be planned around, and planning was the only weapon she'd brought with her besides a stolen grimoire and a pair of wrong-sized boots.

The grimoire was warm against her back. Warmer than before. When she pulled it out — carefully, the way you'd handle a sleeping animal with opinions — the leather cover was faintly flushed, like skin, and beneath her fingertips she felt it: a pulse. Slow. Patient. Contented, almost.

"Oh no," she said. "Oh, don't you dare be pleased with yourself."

Because here was the thought she'd been outrunning all night, the one that had finally caught her in the hollow beneath the boulder: Vesperin hadn't chased her. A creature that could crawl out of a fallen star and string a pass full of singing webs in the space of a conversation — that creature had let her go. Had *saluted* her going.

Which meant he didn't need to catch her.

Which meant he only needed to follow.

The grimoire pulsed against her palms, warm as a held hand, and far to the north, above the black peaks, the unlit stars hung in their rows like seeds waiting to fall — and Odile Villalobos, runaway, thief, apprentice to a dead woman whose death she hadn't yet heard about, pulled her cloak tight and began, in the smallest voice she owned, to laugh.

Because the alternative was crying, and she'd promised herself she'd save that for somewhere warmer.

"South," she told the dark. "Cindershade. Bramble Hask owes me favors, and I intend to collect every single one."

And down in the pass behind her, faint on the wind, she could have sworn she heard something laughing too — kindly, patiently, the way a gardener laughs at a seedling that thinks it has chosen where to grow.
