## 3. The Warden's Ledger

The wards had been dead for six hours by the time Captain Ilsa Reyes reached Thornfell Pass, and she knew it before she saw it, the way a sailor knows a storm by the smell of the air. The mountains had gone quiet in the wrong way. No birdsong from the pines. No wind moving through the high crags. Just a silence so complete it pressed against the ears like deep water, and above it all, a sky with no stars where stars had always been.

"Gods' teeth," muttered Corporal Denny Farrow beside her, reining his mule to a stop. "It's like the whole sky's been scrubbed."

"Mind your language, Corporal. There may be civilians."

"There are no civilians, Captain. We've passed nothing but rocks and one extremely judgmental goat for two days."

Reyes did not smile. She was not, by reputation or preference, a smiling officer. Twenty-two years in the Wardens of the Marches had worn her face into something the recruits privately called "the Ledger" — because looking at it, they said, was like being entered into a book of accounts, and no entry ever came out in your favor.

But privately — privately, in the locked drawer of herself where she kept everything that would have gotten her reassigned to a desk — she agreed with Farrow about the goat.

"Ride on," she said.

* * *

The ward-station at Thornfell was carved into the living rock of the pass itself, a squat granite fist of a building that had stood watch over the border for three hundred years. Its garrison numbered eleven men. When Reyes rode into the yard at dawn, nine of them were standing in formation, pale as whey, and two were missing.

Sergeant Aldous Vane met her at the steps. He was a broad, capable man with a soldier's weathered face and a manner so correct it could have been used to calibrate instruments. He saluted crisply.

"Captain Reyes. We weren't told to expect an inspection."

"I'm not here for an inspection, Sergeant." She dismounted slowly, letting him feel every second of it. "I'm here because last night, at the ninth bell, every ward-stone between Thornfell and the Salt Crags went dark at once. Three hundred years of covenant-work, extinguished in the time it takes to sneeze. The Crown wants to know how. I want to know who. And I suspect those are the same question."

Vane's face did something very small and very fast — a flicker, there and gone, like a fish turning under ice. Most officers would have missed it. Reyes had spent two decades learning to read men the way her mother read tea leaves, except her method actually worked.

"Where are the other two members of your garrison?" she asked.

"Private Ollis is on latrine duty. Private Corren—" a pause, measured to the half-second, "—Corren deserted, Captain. Night before last. Took his kit and went over the wall."

"Deserted. The same night the wards fell."

"The timing is unfortunate, Captain, but—"

"Yes," Reyes said. "Unfortunate things do seem to cluster around you, Sergeant. Show me the ward-chamber."

He led her down through the station's throat, past the barracks, past the mess hall where nine men sat pretending to eat, down the spiral stair cut into the mountain's bones. The ward-chamber was a round room at the heart of the rock, its walls set with the great anchor-stones of the Thornfell line — river-smooth boulders older than the kingdom, each one threaded with veins of silver oath-metal that should have glowed faintly blue until the end of the world.

They were dark. And they were not merely dark. As Reyes came closer, holding up her lantern, she saw that each stone bore a fresh wound: a shallow channel cut into the silver vein, precise as surgery, and packed with something black and waxy.

"Candlesap," she said. "Tallow mixed with grave-ash. It eats oath-metal. Slowly. Quietly. You'd need to know exactly what you were doing, and you'd need access to this room over many nights." She turned. "This room is sealed to garrison command only, Sergeant. That means you, and whoever holds your keys when you sleep."

Vane said nothing. His silence had a texture now. It had weight, like a held breath.

"And here's the part that interests me," Reyes continued, walking the circle of stones, her boots ringing on the old floor. "Grave-ash isn't army issue. It comes from churchyards, or worse places. It has to be bought, carried, smuggled up a mountain in quantity — a full sack of it, at least, to treat eleven stones. Which means carts. Which means the gate log. Which means—" she drew the gate ledger from her satchel, "—this."

She opened the ledger on the flat top of the nearest anchor-stone. Three months of entries, in Vane's own tidy hand. Supply wagons, pay deliveries, a visiting priest, a dog with mange. All correct. All boring. All signed.

Except.

"You've excellent penmanship, Sergeant," Reyes said. "Which is why I noticed that the ink changes color four times in these pages. Different batches. Entries written days apart, finished in a single sitting. Someone sat down with this ledger and *invented* a history." She looked up at him. "You didn't desert anyone, Vane. You buried them. Where's Private Corren?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above them, water dripped in the dark of the mountain, patient as a clock.

"He had family in the lowlands," Vane said at last. His voice was still level. That was the horror of it — how level it stayed. "A sick daughter. The kind of sick that costs more than a private's wage. Someone came to me in the spring. Offered gold enough to buy physicians, houses, a life. All I had to do was open the stones and look away for one night." His jaw tightened. "I told myself it would be harmless. A ritual, they said. An old rite, nothing broken, just... loosened. For one night."

"And you believed them."

"I wanted to believe them." For the first time, something human cracked through the correctness. "Captain, I've stood this pass for nine years. Nine years staring at a border nobody crosses, guarding against a threat nobody's seen since my grandfather's day. Do you know what that does to a man? It makes him *hungry* to matter. And then someone arrives and tells him he matters — tells him he's the hinge of history — and hands him a purse besides." He laughed, short and bitter. "They didn't even lie well. I just stopped checking."

Reyes closed the ledger. Her hands were steady. She made sure of it.

"Where is Corren now?"

"Gone south with them. With *him*. The one who paid us." Vane's eyes flicked up, and in them was the thing he'd been carrying alone for weeks. "Captain, you should know what we let through. It wasn't smugglers. It wasn't refugees. It was something wearing a stolen face, and it thanked me. It *thanked me*, and its voice was like a cellar full of bells, and I have not slept properly since."

* * *

They brought Private Ollis up from the latrines, mostly because he was crying before they even asked him anything. Nineteen years old, freckled, terrified. He'd carried the candlesap up the stairs on Vane's orders and hated himself for every trip, and he told Reyes all of it in a single breathless flood, including the detail that broke the case wide open: he'd seen Vane burning letters in the brazier two nights ago, and one page hadn't caught — a page bearing a seal like a moth eaten by shadow.

Reyes read the Crown's standing protocol aloud in the ward-chamber, because protocol demanded it be read aloud, because protocol was the spine that kept soldiers from becoming animals. Sabotage of the border wards in wartime conditions. Complicity resulting in breach of covenant. The words fell into the round stone room and lay there like dropped knives.

"Sergeant Aldous Vane," she finished. "Do you confess?"

"I confessed the moment you opened the ledger, Captain." He straightened his back. Even now, especially now, he was a soldier. "I'll ask one mercy. Not for me. Send my pay to the daughter. All of it. Let her never know what bought it."

"That can be arranged."

"And Captain—" he hesitated, and for a moment he was not a traitor but a tired man on a cold mountain, "—the one who paid us. He spoke of the wards coming down as though it were a homecoming. He said the border was a wound, and he'd been bleeding on the far side of it for three hundred years. Whatever you do next, don't think of him as a smuggler. Smugglers want *through*. This one wants the door itself torn out of the wall."

Reyes nodded once. Then she did her duty.

Afterward she stood alone in the ward-chamber for a long time, listening to the mountain drip. She had executed men before — deserters, murderers, once a boy of seventeen who'd sold a supply map during the famine winter — and it had never once been easy, and she had long ago decided that the day it became easy was the day she would hand in her commission and go grow turnips somewhere. But this was different. This was not a man who had failed the law. This was a man the law had failed first — hungry, forgotten, offered meaning by the first devil clever enough to notice his hunger.

*I could have been him,* she thought, and then put the thought away in the locked drawer, because thoughts like that did not keep borders intact.

* * *

She found the answer to the *how do we fix it* question where she'd known she would: in the station's archive, in a cedar chest behind the coal store, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of two centuries of neglect. The Founding Records. Every Warden captain was supposed to study them; almost none did; Reyes had, out of boredom during her first winter posting, and had remembered more of it than she'd ever expected to need.

She read by lamplight while Farrow pretended not to hover.

"So it can be fixed?" he asked. "The wards? We just... relight them? Say some words?"

"No." Reyes turned the brittle page. The old script was dense, formal, written by founders who had believed their work would outlast empires and had therefore wasted no ink on brevity. "The wards aren't machinery, Corporal. They're a promise. A covenant sworn between the human realm and the Otherfell, bound into five great oath-stones scattered across the marchlands, with the First Garden as their root. The anchor-stones in stations like this one are only echoes — mirrors of the true stones. When the true stones fail, the mirrors go dark, and no amount of relighting brings back a mirror whose original is broken."

Farrow frowned. "Then how do we make new true stones?"

"We don't." She traced a passage with her finger, reading it twice to be certain, because the third reading would not change it either. "The covenant was forged with a price built into its foundation. Blood freely given, willingly offered, life poured into the oath. That's what lit the stones the first time. The founders write that the covenant can be reforged the same way — but the price doesn't shrink with repetition. If anything..." She paused.

"If anything what, Captain?"

"It grows. Each reforging demands more, because each breaking teaches the border that promises are cheap." She closed the record. "There's a phrase here. *One life, freely given, seals the breach; the land accepts no substitute, no conscript, no coin.*"

Farrow was quiet for a moment. "That sounds like a sacrifice."

"It is a sacrifice."

"Whose?"

"Whoever offers. That's the cruelty of it — it has to be willing. You can't throw a prisoner at it. The land would spit them back." Reyes rubbed her eyes. She was forty-nine years old and tired in a way sleep didn't touch. "Which means somewhere in the capital, right now, someone is reading this same record and arriving at the same conclusion, and starting to wonder who in the realm can be spared."

"Surely they wouldn't—"

"They absolutely would, Corporal. I've met the people who run this kingdom. They balance books. And a book always balances easier when the entry is someone else's name."

She repacked the records and gave the order she'd been composing since dawn: seal the pass, double the watches, send word to the capital by the fastest rider. Then, because the mountain was silent and the sky was wrong and she did not want to sit alone with her ledger of accounts, she accepted Farrow's offer of terrible coffee from the mess urn, and drank it, and stared out the arrow-slit at the starless peaks.

"One more thing, Captain," Farrow said. "The dispatch that brought us here. There was a second sheet attached. Orders from the Marshal's office." He handed it over apologetically, as if the paper might bite.

Reyes read it. Read it again. The coffee went cold in her cup.

*Further directive: intelligence indicates a runaway apprentice, formerly resident at the cottage of the hedge-witch Maren Villalobos (deceased), fled the area on the night of the breach in possession of materials of critical covenant significance. The apprentice is designated a person of interest. She is to be located and returned alive and unharmed to Crown custody. Alive and unharmed, Captain. Repeat: alive.*

Alive. Unharmed. Returned intact — like an ingredient kept fresh.

Reyes thought of Sergeant Vane's tidy handwriting, of a sick girl in the lowlands who would get her gold, of a border held together for three hundred years by a promise that cost exactly one life each time it was made. She thought about how the Marshal's office had drafted these orders within hours of the breach — hours — which meant someone had known what the breach meant before the smoke cleared, had already begun choosing.

And she thought, with a weariness that felt almost like humor, that the judgmental goat had probably made better decisions than everyone involved.

"Farrow," she said quietly, "saddle the mules. We're going after an apprentice."

"A runaway, Captain? In *that*?" He gestured at the window, at the whole alien, starless dark pressing against the glass.

"She's the only person in this entire affair," Reyes said, folding the order into her satchel next to the ledger, "who seems to be running *toward* something instead of away from it. That makes her either our best lead or our next sacrifice. Possibly both." She pulled on her gloves. "And I intend to find out which before anyone in the capital decides for me."
