## 4. The Smuggler's Toll

Cindershade announced itself the way an old dog announces itself: by smell first, then a great deal of noise.

Odile smelled it before she saw it — coal smoke and wet slag and the particular sourness of a town where too many people lived on top of the same hole in the ground. Then came the sound: the endless coughing of the stamp-mills, the groan of the head-frame hoists, men shouting numbers at each other as though numbers had ever once listened.

She came down off the smuggler roads at dusk, when the shift bells changed and every gate in Cindershade stood open for the changing of watches. That was the trick her mistress had taught her — *towns are doors, Odile, and doors are only locked by people who've stopped expecting anyone to knock* — though she suspected Mistress Ysolde would have disapproved of the company this particular door kept.

The Thornfell road had brought her down through country that still didn't look right. Two days past the pass where the star had fallen, and the mountains wore their snow wrong, drifted against the wind instead of with it. Once she'd seen a hawk hanging motionless in the air for so long she'd begun to wonder if it was nailed there. And always, above everything, the sky's new emptiness, like a mouth missing teeth.

She pulled her hood lower and joined the trickle of miners shuffling toward the lamplit sprawl of Cindershade's low town. Nobody looked at anybody here. That was the other thing about towns built around holes: everyone was busy thinking about what they'd left at the bottom of one.

Bramble Hask kept his shop where he had always kept it, wedged between a chandler's and a defunct chapel that now sold pies, at the crooked end of Soot Row where the street gave up on being straight. The sign over his door read *HASK & SONS, PURVEYORS OF FINE GOODS*, which was funny twice over: once because Bramble had no sons, and again because Bramble had never in his life purveyed anything fine.

The bell above the door was muffled with rag, because Bramble did not believe in announcing customers.

Inside, the shop smelled of tallow, dried nettle, and old paper — the last of these being the only honest smell in the building. Shelves climbed the walls in precarious terraces, laden with goods of flexible provenance: boots that had walked away from their previous owners, rope of suspiciously good quality, lantern oil in bottles that had once held something you were not supposed to drink.

Bramble himself sat behind the counter on a stool worn into the shape of him, a small round man with a beard like a storm cloud and spectacles thick enough to start fires if he ever got bored. He was reading. He did not look up.

"We're closed," he said.

"It's four o'clock in the afternoon."

"Closed since noon." He turned a page. "Terrible thing, this economy."

"Bramble."

He went very still, the way small animals do when they hope the large animal has lost interest. Then he set down his book, removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with tremendous care, put them back on, and looked at her properly.

"Well," he said. "The prodigal apprentice. Back from the dead, or at least from *her*."

"I need supplies."

"You need a bath, is what you need, but I suppose that's dearer." He was already moving, though, hauling a ledger out from under the counter, and Odile felt something in her chest unknot slightly. Whatever else Bramble Hask was — fence, smuggler, liar, occasional coward — he had never yet let her stand in his shop hungry. "Rations? Rope? Boots? I've got boots that would fit you, barely used, previous owner no longer requiring them through no fault of mine—"

"Rations. Lamp oil. Cold-weather gear. And paper and ink, if you have any that isn't cursed."

"I have nothing cursed. Everything in this shop is blessedly ordinary, which is why it sells so well." He scribbled figures, muttering. "That's a fair list. That'll run you... well. Let's talk about payment, shall we?"

And there it was. Odile reached into her satchel and drew out the grimoire, and Bramble's eyes went wide and hungry as a cat presented with an unattended fish.

"No," she said.

"You haven't said what I was about to ask."

"You were going to ask for pages."

"I was going to ask *politely* for pages," Bramble said, wounded. "There's a difference. There are collectors in this world, Odile Villalobos, who would pay more for one leaf of Ysolde Vane's working notes than either of us will see in ten years of honest thieving. One page. A dull one. You won't even miss it."

Odile looked down at the grimoire in her hands. It was heavy the way an animal is heavy — not just weight but *presence*, a warmth against her palms, the faint tick of it like a sleeping heart. Mistress Ysolde's handwriting marched across its pages in violet-black ink, each spell annotated in the margins with the old woman's tart commentary: *works, but smells appalling*. *Do NOT attempt while drunk, Tamsin.* *This one cost me a fingernail.*

It was stolen goods. She knew that. She had known it every hour since she'd slipped out the cottage door with it under her cloak while her mistress lay cooling in her bed, and she expected to know it every hour for the rest of her life. But it was also the only thing in the world that told her what to do next, and so she carried it, and hated carrying it, and could not put it down.

"One page," she said. "A dull one."

"*Dull* pages are the ones collectors want most, actually, they say they show the author's true hand—" Bramble caught her expression and raised both palms. "Fine! Fine. A dull one. Your choice, I won't peek."

She opened the grimoire carefully, letting it fall open where it wished, and found herself at a section of weather-charms — rain-calling, frost-turning, the little domestic magics Ysolde had used to keep the garden alive through hard winters. In the margin beside a charm for coaxing early blossoms, the old woman had written: *For Odile, who asked why flowers can't just hurry up. Patience, child. Even magic keeps its own seasons.*

Odile stared at it for a long moment.

"Not that one," she said quietly, and turned the page.

The next leaf was a treatise on the proper preparation of crow-feather ink. Utterly dull. Perfect. She worked the stitching loose with a thumbnail, eased the page free, and laid it on the counter between them like a card played face-down.

Bramble picked it up with both hands, reverent as a priest. His lips moved as he read. His eyebrows performed a small dance.

"Ohh," he breathed. "*Ohh,* look at the ink work. Look at the — yes. Yes, this will do nicely." He tucked it into a lead-lined drawer beneath the counter, locked it, and became briskly commercial again. "Right. Rations, oil, cold gear, paper, ink. I'll throw in a flask, because you look like a woman who needs a drink and I don't trust you not to buy one somewhere worse."

"Bramble, you don't drink."

"Which is how I know the value of things." He was loading a basket as he talked, quick-fingered, wrapping cheese in cloth, stoppering bottles. "Now. While I pack. Tell me what happened up there, because the roads have been *chattering*, Odile, and when the roads chatter, I listen. Stars going dark. Wards coming down like a dropped chandelier. Something fell in the high pass and burned a hole in the winter. Is that your doing?"

"No."

"Is it *her* doing?"

"She's dead, Bramble."

He paused, a bottle halfway into the basket. For a moment the merchant fell away and something older and kinder looked out of his round face. "I'm sorry," he said. "Genuinely. She was..." He seemed to search for a word adequate to Mistress Ysolde Vane and, finding none, settled for honesty. "She was a lot."

"She was."

"And now you're running down my mountain with her book under your arm, and half the Crown's wardens in the province are asking questions about it." He tied off the basket and pushed it across the counter, then did not let go of it when she reached for it. His grip was surprisingly strong. "Which brings us to the unpleasant part of the evening."

"There's an unpleasant part?"

"There's always an unpleasant part. That's commerce." He released the basket and jerked his chin toward the back wall, where a door hung half-open on a storeroom. "Come and look. And keep your hood up."

The storeroom was dim and crammed with crates, and pinned to the inside of the door, lit by a single guttering candle, was a sheet of cheap paper covered in official script and a charcoal sketch.

The sketch was good. That was the worst part. Some warden with actual talent had drawn a young woman with dark hair escaping from beneath a hood, sharp-chinned, wary-eyed, a smudge of soot along one cheekbone. Beneath it, in letters sized for reading across a market square:

*BY ORDER OF THE CROWN: ODILE VILLALOBOS, APPRENTICE TO THE LATE YSOLDE VANE OF THORNFELL, SOUGHT FOR QUESTIONING IN MATTERS TOUCHING THE REALM'S SAFETY. LAST SEEN FLEEING NORTH. REWARD OFFERED. SHE IS TO BE TAKEN ALIVE.*

*Taken alive.* The words sat oddly in the notice, like a stone in a shoe. Wardens did not usually trouble themselves with taking hedge-witches' apprentices alive. Wardens usually posted notices that ended in *dead or alive*, with the *alive* printed smaller, as a formality.

"Why alive?" she asked.

Bramble shrugged elaborately. "When does the Crown want anyone alive? Either they think you know something, or they think you *are* something. Both options are bad. Option two is worse." He tapped the sketch. "They've got your chin right, curse them. Chins are hard."

"How many copies?"

"Every toll-house, every waystation, every tavern from here to the capital, I'd wager. They came through three days ago with a warden escort, nailing them up and buying rounds for the taproom so everyone would look closely." He sighed, and the sigh had weight to it, the sigh of a man about to do arithmetic he'd been avoiding. "Odile. Listen to me. I'm giving you the supplies. I'm giving you the flask. But I can't give you a bed tonight, and I can't give you the back room, and I can't have you seen leaving my shop in daylight tomorrow. Do you understand what I'm saying?"

"That I should leave."

"That you should leave *now*, by the cellar door, and not come back until—" He stopped. Rubbed his face with both hands. "Until whatever's happening up on that border stops happening. Or until you're someone else entirely. Whichever comes first."

Odile stood in the flickering storeroom light and looked at the sketch of her own face, and understood, with a slow cold settling, exactly what Bramble was telling her. Not unkindly. That was the terrible part. He wasn't betraying her; he was being honest, which among fences amounted to the same wound delivered with better manners.

Even friends have prices now. Even Bramble, who had taught her to pick pockets when she was eleven and fed her stolen plums afterward, could not afford to be seen knowing her. The Crown hadn't caught her yet. It didn't need to. It had only to make her expensive.

"Bramble," she said. "Who else knows I'm here?"

"Nobody. You came in the shift-change crowd, you're wearing a hood, and I'm the soul of discretion." He hesitated. "Mostly. There's a boy who sweeps my step. He sees who comes and goes. He's simple, poor lad, but he remembers faces the way I remember debts — perfectly and forever. If he saw you..."

"He saw me."

"He saw you," Bramble confirmed miserably. "He was whistling at you when you came in. Did you hear the whistling?"

She had. She'd thought nothing of it. A boy whistling on a doorstep, some tuneless scrap of a mining song.

"What tune?" she asked, though her stomach already knew.

Bramble hummed it. Four notes, rising, falling. An old border song — a warding-song, the kind children sang without knowing what it meant, the kind Mistress Ysolde had hummed over her seedlings. The kind that traveled. The kind that stuck in a head and stayed there, humming itself all the way to a guard post, all the way to a warden's ear, all the way to a charcoal sketch pinned up in a lamplit window.

Odile closed her eyes.

"The cellar door," she said.

"The cellar door," Bramble agreed. He pressed the basket into her arms, and then, abruptly, caught her shoulder with one small strong hand. Up close, behind the smudged spectacles, his eyes were frightened and fond. "Odile Villalobos. Whatever you stole that book for — whatever you think you're going to do with it — be smarter than her, at least in one respect. Ysolde Vane died alone on purpose. Don't you die alone on purpose too. Pride is a poor blanket."

"It's the only one I've got."

"I know," he said softly. "I know, child. Go out through the cooper's yard, turn left at the slag-heap, and don't stop walking until the mills can't hear you anymore. And Odile—" He fumbled in his apron and pressed something small into her palm, closing her fingers over it. A coin. Not a reward coin, not Crown-stamped. An old trade-token, worn nearly blank, the kind smugglers passed among themselves as a promise. "Debt of Hask. Any door on the Thornfell roads, show that, and they'll feed you first and rob you second. It's not much. It's what I've got."

"It's a great deal," Odile said, and meant it, and discovered her throat was tight, which was inconvenient, because she had a great deal of walking ahead of her and no time for crying in a storeroom.

She went down the cellar stairs into the cold dark, through the cooper's yard where barrels hunched under snow like patient cattle, out onto the left-hand path past the slag-heap, where the town's refuse steamed faintly against the night. Behind her, Cindershade coughed and clanged and glowed, full of people who would soon be looking at sketches of her face. Ahead, the mountains rose black against a sky with holes in it.

She walked. The token sat in her fist like a small warm heart.

She made it perhaps half a mile, to the place where the mill-noise faded and the road bent north toward the frozen waterfall her mistress's marginalia had promised, when she heard the whistling again.

Four notes. Rising, falling.

But this time it was not a sweeping boy. This time it came from the darkness ahead of her, unhurried, almost gentle, and it was followed by a voice she had heard only once before, in a burning crater in the high pass, wearing a dead man's face:

"Good evening, apprentice. We really must finish our conversation."
