## 5. The Garden Beneath the Ice

The marginalia said: *Follow the water that forgets to fall.*

Odile had read that line eleven times by lantern-light before she understood it, and even then she'd laughed out loud in her sleeping roll — a short, disbelieving bark that startled a marmot off its rock half a mile away. Her mistress had written like that. Every instruction in the grimoire's margins was a riddle wearing a smile, as though the old woman couldn't bear to simply say *go left at the birches* when she could make you earn it.

*Follow the water that forgets to fall.*

It took her two days to find it.

The stream came down from the high snowfields, chattering over stones like any other mountain water, until it reached a lip of black granite — and stopped. Not pooled. Stopped. It poured over the edge and then, halfway down, simply forgot itself. The water hung there, a curtain of silver beads suspended in air, trembling but never landing. Below the hanging fall, the ground was bone-dry. Moss grew in a perfect circle where the water should have made a marsh, green as a coin, absurd against all the grey.

"Show-off," Odile muttered at it, the way she used to mutter at her mistress's more theatrical enchantments. Then her throat tightened, because she had said it in exactly the old woman's tone, and for a moment she could hear the answer she would have gotten: *Magic without vanity is like soup without salt, girl. Edible. Pointless.*

She wiped her eyes with her sleeve, businesslike, and stepped through the hanging water.

It parted around her like a beaded doorway, cold needles against her cheeks, and on the other side—

On the other side was a door.

Not a grand one. That was the first thing that undid her. She had expected something worthy of a grimoire: iron bands, sigils, a lock shaped like a screaming mouth. Instead there was a low arch of living stone, moss-cushioned, barely taller than she was, set into the cliff behind the waterfall as if the mountain had grown around it the way an oyster grows around a pearl. Set into the stone at eye height was a single hollow, cupped like two hands held out to receive something.

Odile stood before it for a long moment, breath fogging, heart doing something complicated in her chest.

"You knew I'd come," she said to the door. To her mistress, really. "You absolute— you knew, and you still made me cross the Thornfell roads with wardens on my heels and my supper stolen twice by ravens."

The door, being a door, said nothing. But the hollow in the stone filled gently with meltwater from somewhere above, drop by drop, patient as an old woman waiting for a girl to stop complaining and start thinking.

Odile thought. The grimoire's margin, beneath the riddle, bore one more line, small enough that she'd nearly missed it: *Bring what you cannot spare.*

She went through her pockets. A bent hairpin. Three copper bits and a button pretending to be a fourth. Half a wheel of cheese she had been saving against despair. And — wrapped in flannel at the bottom of her satchel, because she could not bring herself to leave it behind and could not bear to look at it either — her mistress's thimble. Dented brass, worn smooth on one side where forty years of fingers had pushed it.

"That," she whispered, "I cannot spare."

Her hands shook so badly she dropped it twice getting it into the hollow. The second time it landed true, brass singing softly against stone, and the water in the hollow went still as glass, and the door opened without a sound.

* * *

Warmth came out first. That was the thing that made no sense and made her trust everything else. Beyond the arch, in the heart of a frozen mountain, the air was soft as late June. It smelled of loam and green things and, faintly, of beeswax, the way her mistress's cottage had smelled on winter evenings.

Odile stepped through, and the dark opened into light.

The cavern was vast — a cathedral hollowed by some patient ancient water — and it was full of a glow that came from nowhere and everywhere, pale gold, like the last ten minutes of a summer evening stretched out forever. Terraces of garden climbed the walls. Real soil. Real green. Apple trees heavy with fruit stood in impossible defiance of the ice overhead, which arched across the cavern roof in great translucent ribs, blue-white, glittering, close enough that she could see her own reflection warped in it like a fly in amber.

And everywhere, everywhere, roses.

Not tame roses. These grew wild and enormous along every terrace wall, blooms the size of dinner plates, in colors Odile had no names for — a red that was almost a sound, a white that seemed to remember being snow, a yellow like butter melting on warm bread. Their scent was thick enough to lean on.

"Oh," Odile said. Just that. Just *oh*, in a very small voice, to a very large silence.

At the center of the garden, where a fountain might have stood in a mortal court, rose a circle of standing stones — five of them, tall as two men, arranged in a ring worn smooth as river pebbles. Four were grey and silent as sleepers. But the fifth —

The fifth was *humming*.

She felt it before she heard it, a note in her back teeth, in the bones of her feet, in the fillings of her thoughts. The stone stood slightly apart from the others, and its surface moved faintly under its skin of lichen, like a chest breathing. Light pulsed within it, slow, deep, the rhythm of something alive and dreaming.

An oath-stone. She knew it the way you know your own name — not from study, but from somewhere older. This was what the covenant was *made of*. This was what the stars had been nailed to the sky with, before somebody pulled out the nails.

Her mistress's final letter — the one tucked in the grimoire's binding, the one Odile had read so many times the creases were going soft — had spoken of these. *Five stones, five oaths, five prices.* The old woman had never said what the prices were. She had only written, in handwriting that wobbled worse than usual: *Be certain, Odile. Be certain before you touch them. Certainty is the only coin they accept.*

"Well," Odile said aloud, to the humming stone, to the garden, to nobody. "I'm certain about one thing. Everything else is up for negotiation."

The stone hummed on, indifferent and inviting.

She crossed the garden slowly, because awe deserved to be walked through properly. Apples had fallen and rotted sweetly among the grass; she picked one from a low branch and bit it, mostly out of nervousness, and it was so good she nearly cried. Mountain apples were small and sour as spite. These tasted like the idea of apples, the apple all apples were only rehearsing.

At the ring of stones she stopped. Up close, the humming one was warm. She could feel it on her face like standing near a hearth. Within its slow pulse of light she thought she saw shapes — shadows moving, like figures seen through frosted glass. Dancers? Speakers? Hands joined?

The covenant, sworn here, whatever centuries ago. People who had stood exactly where she stood and given something up to hold two worlds apart.

"I don't even know what I'm supposed to give yet," she told it honestly. "My mistress didn't write that part. Or she did, and I haven't found the page, or—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Or she wanted me to find out for myself, because she was like that."

The stone's hum shifted. Warmed. Almost — almost — like recognition.

Odile took a breath. Wiped her palms on her trousers. Reached out and laid her hand flat against the stone.

* * *

The world stopped.

Not dramatically. No thunderclap, no shattering light. Simply: the hum ceased, the pulse froze mid-beat, the golden evening-glow dimmed as if a cloud had crossed a sun that wasn't there. Even the roses seemed to hold their petals still.

And the stone looked at her.

There was no eye. There didn't need to be. Odile felt attention settle on her the way a cat settles onto a lap — completely, weightily, with a judgment entirely its own. It went through her like a hand going through a drawer, lifting things, examining them. Her fear of Vesperin's borrowed face. Her guilt over the stolen grimoire, which sat in her satchel feeling suddenly very heavy and very much *not hers*. Her anger at her mistress for dying and leaving riddles instead of goodbyes. Her deeper anger, the shameful one, the one she never let herself think past the first word of: that she had been *chosen*, and choosing felt like being used, and loving someone who uses you is the most exhausting work there is.

The stone turned all of this over, gently, terribly.

Then it showed her what it wanted.

It wasn't words. It was a feeling, pressed into her like a seal into wax: *open*. Open the drawer. Empty it. Hold nothing back. Come to me with your whole self or do not come at all.

And Odile — clever, quick, cornered Odile, who had survived the past weeks precisely by keeping parts of herself hidden, by lying lightly and hoarding her truths like a dragon hoards gold — Odile flinched.

It was a small flinch. Inward. Barely a motion at all. But the stone felt it the way a spider feels a thread tremble, and the warmth withdrew from under her palm like a tide going out.

The hum resumed — but different now. Cooler. Patient. The hum of a locked door, not an open one.

"No," Odile said. "No, wait. Wait, I *am* open, I want to help, I came all this way—"

The stone breathed its slow light and gave her nothing. Somewhere in the terraces, an apple dropped from a tree and rolled a little way and lay still, and the sound of it was the loudest thing in the world.

She kept her hand on the stone anyway, pressing, begging, until her arm ached and her eyes blurred and the gold evening-light of the garden began, very gradually, to return — ordinary again, indifferent again, a beautiful room with the host gone upstairs.

Finally she took her hand back. It was printed faintly with the stone's grain, and the print faded as she watched.

"Not yet," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "That's what you're saying, isn't it? *Not yet.*"

The garden didn't answer. Gardens never do. But as she sank down onto the warm grass at the ring's edge, hugging her knees, she caught the scent of those impossible roses again, and underneath it, so faint she might have invented it, something like pity.

She laughed, wetly, at herself. "Rejected by a rock. Bramble would pay good money to see this." Then, quieter: "What am I supposed to give up that I'm not already giving? I've got nothing left. I've got a book that isn't mine, a thimble I already paid with, and—" 

She stopped.

Because that wasn't true, and she knew it wasn't true, and the stone had known it wasn't true, and that was the whole trouble, wasn't it. She had plenty left. She had memories stacked to the rafters — her mistress's voice saying *soup without salt, girl*; the smell of ink and lavender in the cottage kitchen; the exact weight of the grimoire the night she'd taken it, how her hands had shaken, how the old woman had been asleep three feet away and Odile had looked at her and hadn't said goodbye because goodbye would have meant admitting it was theft.

A whole life, hoarded. Guarded. *Too guarded.*

"Certainty," she murmured. "You want certainty, and certainty means deciding what I can live without. And I don't know yet. I don't know what I can live without, because I've never tried living without anything."

The stone hummed its cool, patient hum. *Come back when you know.*

Odile stayed until the ache in her chest settled into something she could carry. She ate another apple, out of spite this time. She refilled her waterskin from a spring that tasted faintly of starlight, or possibly just of minerals — it was hard to tell, in a place like this. At the door, she retrieved her mistress's thimble from the hollow, polished it on her sleeve, and put it away in flannel again.

"I'll be back," she told the door. "When I'm worth opening for."

Behind her, as the arch closed, she thought she heard the four silent stones shift — a settling, a creaking, like sleepers turning over. Like something waking up after a very long time, and noticing the fifth voice had spoken to a visitor, and wondering why.

She was halfway back down the pass, boots crunching on frost, when she saw it: far below in the valley, a thread of woodsmoke rising from Cindershade, and beside it, moving along the warden's road, a knot of riders in Crown grey, their banner snapping in the wind.

Riding toward the mountain.

Riding toward *her* mountain.

Odile stood very still on the ridge, the grimoire heavy against her spine, and watched them come.
