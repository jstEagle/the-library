## 6. Reyes Takes the Road

The order came at dawn, written in the Commander's own hand, which was how Ilsa Reyes knew it was going to be a bad day.

The Commander did not write things down when they were ordinary. Ordinary orders arrived by courier, stamped and countersigned and forgettable. The Commander wrote things down only when he wanted a witness to know that whatever happened next had been *his idea*, so that if it went wrong, the failure would belong to whoever carried out the order. Reyes had learned to read his handwriting the way other people read weather.

*Find the runaway apprentice Villalobos. Take her alive. Alive is not negotiable.*

Alive was not negotiable. That was the part that sat wrong in her stomach like a swallowed stone, because warden protocol had exactly three dispositions for a fugitive of interest: capture, kill, or lose the paperwork. Nobody in the history of the Thornfell wardens had ever specified *alive is not negotiable* unless they wanted the person delivered somewhere intact for a reason that could not survive being spoken aloud.

She read the order twice, folded it into her breast pocket over her heart, where she kept all the documents she intended to eventually burn, and went to saddle her horse.

Her sergeant found her in the stable, holding a currycomb and staring at nothing.

"Captain," said Sergeant Doss. Doss was a broad, cheerful man built like a grain sack, with a mustache he groomed more attentively than his kit. "We've got the road detail. Six riders, resupply at Cindershade, then up the pass after the girl."

"I know."

"You've got your *face* on," Doss observed.

"What face?"

"The one you get when you're about to ruin somebody's week. Usually yours." He leaned against the stall door. "It's just an apprentice, Captain. One runaway hedge-witch with a stolen book. We've chased worse with less breakfast."

Reyes hung the currycomb on its peg with slightly more force than necessary. "Have you read the addendum?"

Doss's cheer dimmed by degrees, like a lamp running low. "Take her alive," he recited. "Alive is not—"

"Not negotiable. Yes." She swung the saddle onto her mare, Marigold, who complained about it on principle, as she did about everything. "You know what we do with prisoners we're ordered to take alive and unmarked, Doss?"

"Deliver them to the capital."

"And what happens in the capital, lately, to people the wards need? Now that the wards are *down*?" She cinched the girth. Marigold bit at her sleeve, gently, the way a friend bites. "There's a rumor going through the officer corps. I've heard it twice from men who should have known better than to say it in front of me. About resealing the border."

Doss was quiet for a moment. Out in the yard, the morning muster was forming up, six riders in Crown grey, breath steaming, banner snapping. "Blood," he said finally, very quietly. "That's just campfire talk, Captain. Old stories. The covenant didn't run on blood, it ran on oath-stones and gardeners and—" He stopped himself, because everyone in the service stopped themselves there, at the same place, the way you don't step on a grave.

"On gardeners," Reyes finished for him. "Yes. And the last gardener died two years ago, and her apprentice ran off with her book the same night the sky went dark, and now the Commander wants that apprentice delivered *alive and unmarked* to the capital, and you tell me what arithmetic that is."

Doss looked at the horses. At the banner. At anything but her.

"Mount up, Sergeant," Reyes said, and led them out onto the warden's road.

* * *

The warden's road climbed out of the river valley in long switchbacks, and Reyes rode the first hour in silence, letting the country speak instead.

This was the discipline her mother had taught her, before the Crown took her: *if you want to catch someone, stop thinking like a hunter. Think like the ground.* The ground knew everything. The ground knew which way the frost lay thin, where a fire had been banked carelessly, which fences had been lifted and which cut. A fugitive left tracks in the world the way a fever leaves tracks in the blood, and if you were quiet enough, the world would tell you where it hurt.

The world told her this: the girl was good.

Not flawless — there was a coin paid to a toll-keeper at the second switchback, minted this year, and the toll-keeper remembered a young woman with ink-stained fingers who'd bought bread and asked too many questions about the high pass. But no fires lit after dark. No begging at farmhouses. No selling of the grimoire's pages, though any fence in Cindershade would have paid a fortune. The girl was moving fast, sleeping cold, spending almost nothing, and asking questions only about the old covenant roads.

Which meant she wasn't running *from* something. She was running *toward* something. And she knew exactly what.

"Captain," said Corporal Vane, riding up beside her. Vane was young, ambitious, and had the particular shine of a man who reported to more than one superior. Reyes kept him close for the same reason you keep a splinter where you can see it. "Toll-keeper says she headed for Cindershade. Mining town, rough quarter. Want us to sweep it?"

"No sweeps." Reyes didn't look at him. "Six riders clattering into a mining town announces us to every roof between here and the ridge. Two of us go in plainclothes. The rest hold at the tree line."

"The Commander's timetable—"

"The Commander's timetable will keep." She let that sit a moment. "Unless Corporal Vane would like to explain to the Commander why we lost the element of surprise to a *banner*."

Vane shut up. It was one of his few reliable qualities.

They came down into Cindershade near dusk, and Reyes felt the town before she saw it: that particular hush of a place where the mines had gone deep and the people had gone careful. Slate roofs, coal smoke, a tavern called the Broken Pick whose sign creaked in a wind that smelled faintly, wrongly, of ozone — the smell the fallen stars left behind, the smell that had been drifting down off the peaks for three days now and that made her back teeth ache.

She left Vane watching the horses and walked into the town alone, wearing a drover's coat over her uniform, which fooled nobody but satisfied the letter of the regulations, and regulations mattered to Reyes the way scripture matters to a man who has stopped believing but still prays.

She found the first answer within the hour, in the person of a fence named Bramble Hask, who was drinking alone in the Broken Pick and radiating guilt the way a wet dog radiates smell.

Hask was an old colleague — not a friend; Reyes did not have friends, she had assets and colleagues and one dead sister — and he had the look of a man who had recently done business with a runaway and was now doing arithmetic about reward posters. There was a poster tacked by the tavern door. Reyes had read it without stopping: *APPRENTICE OF INTEREST. ODILE VILLALOBOS. DELIVER ALIVE TO THE CAPITAL. REWARD: FIVE HUNDRED CROWNS.*

Five hundred crowns. For a girl with ink-stained fingers who bought bread and asked about the high pass.

Reyes sat down across from Hask without asking, and Hask flinched so thoroughly he nearly left his chair entirely.

"Captain Reyes," he managed. "What a — what an unexpected — I'm just having a quiet drink—"

"Hask." She set two coppers on the table, payment for the interruption, because Hask was a businessman and respected the forms. "I'm going to ask you one question, and then I'm going to leave you to your drink, and neither of us will remember this conversation. Did the girl come through here?"

Hask's eyes darted toward the poster. Toward the door. Toward the five hundred crowns hanging on it like ripe fruit.

And Reyes watched him — watched the greed rise and meet something else in him and *lose* — and understood, with a strange lurch under her ribs, that the fence was going to lie to a warden captain to protect a thief he'd known for perhaps a day.

"She came through," Hask said quietly. "Bought supplies. Traded some pages — fair weight, fair price, I'm not a monster. Headed north past the falls." He turned his cup in his hands. "Captain, can I say a thing? Off the record, as it were."

"You can say a thing."

"That girl was scared out of her wits, but she paid for an old woman's medicine while she was buying her rope. Didn't haggle. Left extra." He shrugged, embarrassed. "I've sold to wardens and smugglers and once to a fae lord wearing a bishop's face, and I'll tell you free of charge: I've never once been sorry to see any of you go. Her, I was sorry to see go." He met her eyes. "Whatever the Crown wants her for, Captain — five hundred crowns seems cheap."

Reyes put another copper on the table, stood, and left him to his drink.

Outside, the wind off the peaks carried that ozone ache again, and above the mountains, where the stars should have been, the sky was a smooth black nothing, like a page torn out of a book.

* * *

They made camp at the tree line below the frozen falls, and Reyes took first watch, because she always took first watch, because sleep and she had been estranged for eleven years.

Eleven years, four months. She could mark it to the night.

Her sister Sera had been the loud one, the laughing one, the one their mother swore would either become a bard or die young, and for a long time it had been genuinely unclear which. When the border wards still stood — tall and green-gold and humming like struck glass along the whole spine of the Thornfells — Sera had been obsessed with them. *What's behind them, Ilsa? What are they keeping out? Or keeping in? Why won't anyone say?*

Sera had gone past the border on a dare, at nineteen, with two boys from the valley and a bottle of plum brandy, on a night in autumn when the wards flickered — just flickered, a stutter, a heartbeat's gap, the kind of thing the wardens logged as *atmospheric interference* and never investigated.

Two of the boys came back. They were never right again. One of them spent the rest of his life drawing the same spiral on every surface he could find, over and over, walls and tables and the backs of his own hands, until his family wrapped his arms in cloth.

Sera hadn't come back at all.

The search had lasted nine days. Reyes had been fourteen. She had walked the whole length of the border herself, calling her sister's name until her voice broke, and the wards had hummed their glassy, indifferent hum, and the wardens had told her mother, with great professional kindness, that beyond the wards there was nothing to find because beyond the wards there was *nothing*, officially, and girls who wandered into official nothings were recorded as lost to weather.

Lost to weather. As if the Otherfell were a storm front. As if her sister had been struck by lightning and scattered.

Reyes had joined the wardens at sixteen for one reason, and it was not patriotism. It was because the wardens held the keys to the border archives, and somewhere in those archives, buried under atmospheric interference reports, there had to be a true accounting of what lived on the other side of that humming glass wall, and what it did with loud laughing girls who dared each other through.

She had found the archives. She had found the truth, or pieces of it: that the covenant was real, that the wards were not a wall but a *promise*, sworn in a garden by human gardeners and fae lords both, and that the promise held only while both sides kept faith. She had found records of exiles — fae lords cast out when the covenant was forged, denied the starlight, seething in the dark places. She had even, once, found a fragment of a report suggesting that things sometimes crossed during ward-flickers, and that what they took, they sometimes kept.

She had never found Sera. But she had stopped believing in *lost to weather.*

And that was the poison in her, the thing she carried into Cindershade and up the frozen passes: she knew better than anyone alive what the wards were worth, and therefore she knew better than anyone what their fall meant. Every flicker was a door. Every gap in the humming glass was a spiral drawn on a wall by a boy who had seen something no mind should hold.

So when the stars had gone out — all at once, every star above the peaks guttering dark like candles pinched between wet fingers — Reyes had stood on the barracks roof and felt something in her chest tear along an old seam.

Because she knew what came through gaps.

And now the Commander wanted a runaway apprentice brought to the capital *alive and unmarked*, and the campfire talk said *blood*, and the arithmetic was simple, and Reyes had spent her whole life learning to do that arithmetic honestly:

The Crown meant to seal the breach with a person. And the person they'd chosen was a girl who paid extra for old women's medicine.

* * *

Near midnight, Doss brought her tea and stayed, sitting on a root beside her, which was against about four regulations and which she permitted.

"You're doing the face again," he said.

"It's dark. You can't see my face."

"I can always see your face. It's a gift." He sipped his own tea. "Vane sent a pigeon tonight. Didn't use the field cipher."

Reyes went very still. "Where to?"

"Capital. Command, I'd guess. Report on our progress." Doss blew on his tea. "Progress being, as far as I can tell, that we found a toll-keeper and a fence and a cold campsite, and that our captain keeps ordering us to move slow."

"Does he think I'm moving slow?"

"He thinks," Doss said carefully, "that either you're incompetent, which we both know is false, or you're reluctant, which we also both know is false in the way that matters, or—" he paused, choosing his footing on ice, "—or you already know what 'alive and unmarked' means, and you're trying to figure out how to obey an order without committing a sin."

The fire crackled. Above the peaks, the starless black pressed down like a lid.

"Doss," Reyes said. "My sister went through a flicker in the wards when we were girls. You know that story."

"Everyone knows that story. It's why half the corps calls you Ghost."

"They call me Ghost because I don't sleep and I sign nothing I can't defend. Fine." She wrapped her hands around the tea. "Here's what I know that the corps doesn't. The wards weren't built to keep the fae *out*. They were built to keep a *promise* in. A covenant. Sworn in a garden, sealed in stones, tended by gardeners on both sides. And the reason the Crown's ritual books don't mention any of that is that a covenant can't be resealed with blood. Blood doesn't renew a promise. Blood *breaks* one. If they sacrifice that girl on the breach, they won't seal the border, Doss. They'll teach everything on the other side that our word means nothing, and they'll do it in the one language the fae understand best."

Doss stared at her. "Then what does seal it?"

"I don't know yet. But a girl with ink-stained fingers stole her dead mistress's grimoire the same night the sky went dark, and she isn't running away from the breach. She's running *along* it. North past the falls, following the old covenant roads." Reyes looked into the fire. "Either she knows something, or she's looking for something. And either way, she's the only person in these mountains doing anything except panicking or scheming."

"So what's the plan, Captain? Because Vane's pigeon lands by tomorrow noon, and after that we'll have company. Real company. Sacrifice-detail company."

Reyes was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the pines, and beneath it — so faint she might have imagined it, might have wanted it — she thought she heard a hum. Glassy. Green-gold. Like struck glass remembering itself.

One stone, waking. Maybe more.

"The plan," she said slowly, "is that tomorrow we follow the girl. And when we find her, I'm going to have a conversation with her instead of an arrest. And Vane is going to be very disappointed."

"And if the Commander asks why his fugitive of interest isn't in chains?"

Reyes smiled, and it was not a warm smile, but it was an honest one, which was rarer.

"Then the Commander can write *that* down in his own hand too."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn and climbed toward the frozen falls, and the world got stranger with every mile.

Frost lay on the southern faces of the rocks — wrong season, wrong sun. Birds flew in circles, patient and purposeless, like sentries. And twice, in the grey light, Reyes saw marks on the trail that stopped her heart: small boot prints, quick and light, the stride of someone hurrying but not stumbling — and beside them, overlapping, prints that were not boot prints at all. Long. Narrow. Too many joints in the toes. Heading the same direction, at the same pace.

Something was tracking the girl. Something patient.

"Captain," Vane said, crouched over the prints, and for once his voice held no ambition at all, only a young man's naked unease. "What makes tracks like this?"

"Nothing from this side of the border," Reyes said.

She straightened, hand on her sword, and looked north past the frozen falls, to where the old covenant road wound down toward a valley she'd only ever seen on maps — a valley marked, in the archive's oldest folios, with a single faded notation: *drowned chapel, wayside shrine, road forks.*

The girl was heading there. So was the thing with the long-toed prints.

And Reyes, hunter, doubting her hunt, drew her sword anyway and started down the mountain after them both — no longer certain whether she was chasing a fugitive, or racing to reach one before something else did.
