## 7. Bargain at the Drowned Chapel

The chapel had drowned a hundred years ago, and it had never quite learned to stop being a chapel.

That was Odile's first thought as she waded through its nave, water to her thighs, black and still as poured ink. Broken arches rose around her like the ribs of some enormous patient beast. The bell tower leaned at an angle that suggested it had given up on standing long ago but hadn't yet committed to falling. And everywhere — everywhere — the water reflected light that had no source, a pale silver glow seeping up from below, as though the lake itself had been taught to pray.

Her mistress's grimoire, strapped to her chest beneath her coat, hummed faintly. It had been humming since the pass. Odile had decided to interpret this as encouragement rather than alarm, mostly because the alternative was too exhausting to consider.

"Right," she said aloud, because talking to herself had become a habit somewhere on the third day of running, and because the silence in this place was the thick kind, the kind that pressed on your ears. "Drowned chapel. Obviously the next clue. Obviously. Why would it be anywhere sensible?"

The marginalia in the grimoire had led her here — a scrawled note in her mistress's crabbed hand: *If the first stone refuses you, girl, go where the bells learned to swim.* Odile had assumed this was metaphor. Her mistress had loved metaphor the way other people loved wine — excessively, and with poor judgment about when to stop.

It had not been metaphor. There were, in fact, bells down there. She could see them through the water, three of them, resting in the flooded crypt below, their bronze mouths furred with pale moss. And around the largest of them, coiled in the deep like a sleeping serpent of light, was a warmth she recognized now. Oath-stone warmth. The same humming she'd felt beneath the ice of the First Garden, before that stone had looked at her — actually looked at her, she'd have sworn it — and turned cold.

*Not yet worthy.* That had stung more than she liked to admit. She'd stolen a grimoire, crossed the Thornfell roads, slept in a hollow tree, and been told by a rock that she wasn't ready. Rocks, in Odile's experience, were supposed to be nonjudgmental.

She was halfway to the crypt stairs — a submerged archway descending into the silver glow — when the water went quiet.

Not calm. *Quiet.* There was a difference, and the difference was that calm water still moved. This water stopped. Every ripple she'd made died at once, as though the lake had held its breath, and the reflections in it sharpened until the broken arches above her appeared twice: once in the air, ragged and ruined, and once below, whole and perfect, a chapel restored in mirror.

Odile stopped wading.

"You know," said a voice behind her, warm as mulled wine and about as trustworthy, "most pilgrims kneel."

She turned slowly. She already knew who it was. She'd known since the water stopped, because her body had learned something on the smuggler roads in the last week that her mind was still catching up to: fear had a texture, and this was it.

Vesperin stood on the submerged rim of a shattered pew, dry-footed where the water lapped an inch below his boots, which was the sort of thing that should have been impossible and was therefore exactly the sort of thing he would do. He still wore the warden's face he'd crawled out of the crater wearing — a young man's face, open and honest, brown-haired and brown-eyed, the kind of face you'd ask for directions. But the light didn't sit on it right. It pooled in the hollows under the cheekbones and refused to leave, the way light behaves around deep water.

"Most pilgrims," Odile said, "are dead a hundred years."

"Time is softer here than you'd think." He stepped down from the pew, and the water bore him — actually bore him, a ripple of silver holding him up like a serving tray. "You've been difficult to find, apprentice."

"Have you tried not looking? Works wonders."

The smile he gave her was genuinely charming, which was the worst part. It was the smile of someone who found you delightful and was deciding, at his leisure, how much of you to keep. "There she is. The wit. Your mistress wrote about that. *The girl talks when she's frightened, and the talking is how you know the thinking has begun.*" He tilted the stolen face, considering her. "She was fond of you, you know. In her way. Which makes what you did rather remarkable."

Odile's hand drifted to the strap of the grimoire. "I don't know what you mean."

"You stole from a dying woman." He said it gently. That was the trick of him, she was beginning to understand — he never raised his voice, never bared a tooth. He simply laid things out, one by one, like a merchant displaying knives. "She was dying, and you took her life's work and ran. And now every power in two realms is hunting a frightened girl with a stolen book. The wardens want to burn it. I want to read it. And you —" he spread his hands, the picture of reasonableness, "— you want very badly to stop being hunted. I can arrange that."

"You can arrange a lot of things," Odile said. "I watched you climb out of a star. I'm aware of your range."

"Then let's not waste each other's time." He drifted closer across the water, and the reflections sharpened further, until she could see the drowned chapel below in perfect detail — and something else, too. Shapes in the deep. Tall shapes, standing very still among the pews, with the patience of furniture. "Give me the grimoire. In exchange: safe passage to the edge of the Otherfell, gold enough to buy a new name, and my personal guarantee that neither my creatures nor the Crown's wardens will touch so much as a hair of your head."

"That's a very generous offer."

"It is."

"Which is how I know it's a lie."

The smile flickered — not gone, but *adjusted*, the way a shopkeeper adjusts a price when the customer proves harder than expected. "Clever girl. Yes. There would be conditions. There are always conditions. But consider your alternatives." He gestured upward, through the broken roof, at the starless sky. "The stars are going out. Have you noticed? Not fallen — *going out*, one by one, like candles pinched by a careful hand. Each one that dies drops a shard. Each shard carries the forgetting. The plague is in Cindershade already, or will be within the week. The Crown's answer is a blood sacrifice. Do you know whose blood, apprentice? They haven't chosen yet. But they're looking for someone who knows both sides of the old covenant, someone who can be *spared*." His brown eyes — the warden's brown eyes, but wearing Vesperin's gaze behind them like a man behind a window — settled on her. "You are the only person alive who fits. Run to the wardens and they will thank you, kindly, and then they will open a vein. Run to me, and live."

It was a good speech. Odile gave it full marks. Her knees were shaking, and she made herself notice that and file it away, because her mistress had taught her that too — *fear is information, girl, not instruction.*

"You keep saying *covenant*," Odile said. "You want the covenant. That's what this is really about. Not the grimoire. The grimoire's just the key to it."

Something moved behind the warden's face. Not quite a flinch. A *stilling*, like a pond when a fish passes under.

"The covenant broke," Vesperin said. "I intend to break it further, or remake it, or — " he paused, and for one unguarded moment the charm dropped and what looked out of the young man's face was old, and cold, and *hungry* in the way winter is hungry — "— it hardly matters what I intend. What matters is what you intend, and what you intend is to survive. So. One last time, and then I stop asking."

The tall shapes in the deep began to move. Slowly. Unhurried. Rising through the silver light with the terrible patience of things that had never once needed to hurry.

Odile's mind was doing the thing it did — the talking-when-frightened thing, except inside, where it was safe. *He wants the covenant. He assumes the covenant is a thing you can hand over, a ritual, a text, a bargain. He assumes the grimoire contains it. And here's the beautiful, terrible truth: it doesn't. Not whole. Not in any form anyone could hold. The covenant is five stones and an oath and a price, and the price is memory, and nobody — not the Crown, not Vesperin, not the rocks themselves — knows that but me and a dead woman.*

*Which means I can promise him the covenant.*

*And I can promise him the truth.*

Her mistress's voice, in memory: *An oath is a door, girl. It only opens as wide as the words you say through it. Say less. Mean it fully. And never — never — swear on something you don't own.*

The grimoire was hers. Stolen, yes, but hers — her mistress had pressed it into her hands with the last of her strength, had said *run, and don't look back, and don't let them take it*, and whatever the law said, whatever Vesperin's clever knife-display said, that book had been *given*. She owned it. She could swear on it.

And an oath sworn on a covenant-book, by the last keeper of the covenant, was not a small thing. She felt it in the water around her — the chapel itself listening, the drowned bells gathering their moss-furred silence like a held breath.

"Vesperin," she said.

He inclined the stolen head, gracious as a host.

"I'll give you the covenant."

The shapes in the deep stopped rising. The water seemed to lean in.

"Sworn," Odile said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "on this book, which is mine by gift and by grief: I, Odile Villalobos, will deliver to Vesperin, exiled lord of the Otherfell, *the covenant*."

The words left her and she felt them *land* — felt them sink into the water and the stone and the silver light, felt the drowned chapel take hold of them the way a lock takes hold of a key. An oath was a door. She had said very little through it. She had not said *which* covenant — the old one, the broken one, the one Vesperin wanted, or some other thing entirely that a clever girl might, in the fullness of time, decide to call by that name. She had not said *when*. She had not said *where*. She had not said *whole*.

She had meant every word. That was the trick of it, the part that made her stomach turn even as relief flooded her: she hadn't lied. She had simply built a door so small that only the truth could fit through it, and left herself holding the only key.

Vesperin's smile bloomed, wide and warm, and for a moment the warden's face looked almost beautiful with triumph. "There," he said softly. "There it is. Sworn on the book itself. Do you feel it, apprentice? The chapel feels it. The water feels it. Oaths are the oldest magic there is — older than your Crown, older than your stones. You've just tied yourself to me with the one rope that can't be cut."

"Mm," said Odile. "About that."

His smile held. His eyes didn't.

"An oath is a door," she said, "that opens as wide as the words you say through it. I promised you *the covenant*. I didn't promise it whole. I didn't promise it now. I didn't promise it here, or unbroken, or in any form you'd recognize. I promised you the covenant, and I will keep that promise — the moment I have a covenant to give. Which, as of tonight, I don't. Because the old one is shattered, my lord, and the new one doesn't exist yet, and I am the only person in either realm who knows how to build it." She lifted her chin. "So here is what you've actually bought: not a book. Not a ritual. *Me*. My two feet, my clever tongue, and my continued, unharmed, extremely motivated existence — because the moment I die, your oath dies with me, and you get nothing but a dead girl's ghost to haunt."

The silence that followed had weight. The water pressed against her like a hand.

Then Vesperin began, very quietly, to laugh. It was not a kind sound. It was not entirely unkind, either, which was somehow worse. "Oh," he said. "Oh, she would have *loved* you. Your mistress. This is exactly her work — I can see her fingerprints all over you, little thief. Say less. Mean it fully." He shook the stolen head, admiring. "You realize you've just made yourself the most valuable creature in two realms. To me. To the wardens. To the stones themselves. Everyone will want you, and no one can take what you've sworn to me without breaking an oath older than their kingdoms." He drifted backward across the silver water, and the tall shapes sank with him, folding down into the deep like courtiers bowing. "So run, Odile Villalobos. Run and gather whatever it is you're gathering. I'll be watching. And when you have your covenant — *any* covenant — you'll bring it to me. Won't you."

It wasn't a question. But it wasn't, quite, a threat either. It was something more patient than either.

"The bells learned to swim," Odile said, because her mouth was still running and she couldn't stop it. "That's how I found this place. Your face is very good, by the way. You should return it."

The last thing she saw of him was the smile, hanging on the dark water a moment after the rest had gone, like a reflection outliving its source.

* * *

She didn't find the second stone that night.

She tried. She waded down the crypt stairs until the water closed over her head, and the silver glow wrapped around her like a held note, and the largest bell hummed against her outstretched fingers with a warmth that was almost — *almost* — an invitation. But when she reached for the warmth the way the marginalia instructed, the way she'd reached beneath the ice, the bell's hum changed pitch. It dropped low and slow, a sound she felt in her teeth and her ribs and somewhere behind her sternum, and it said, as clearly as a bell can say anything:

*Not yet. Not like this. You are hiding something even now.*

"I'm hiding *everything*," Odile said into the water, and a stream of bubbles carried her indignation up toward the distant, broken sky. "I'm hiding a stolen book and a false oath and a name that's worth blood now, and you want me to be *worthy*? Worthy of what? Being eaten politely?"

The bell hummed once more — she'd have sworn, in a less exhausted moment, that it sounded amused — and went still.

She surfaced, sputtering, and hauled herself onto the chapel's altar stone, which was the only dry surface in the entire ruin, because apparently even drowned chapels kept some standards. She wrung out her coat. She checked the grimoire — dry, always dry, her mistress's final enchantment holding — and found, to her complete lack of surprise, fresh marginalia in the crabbed hand that had been appearing since the pass, as though the book were keeping a diary of her failures:

*Well sworn, badly aimed. He'll forgive you that, eventually, or he won't, and either way you've bought time. Time is the only coin that spends everywhere. Rest. Then Cindershade — the fence with the bramble name. And girl: when the sky starts dropping its teeth, be somewhere with children in it. They're easier to save than the worthy.*

Odile read that last line three times.

"The sky starts dropping its — " she said, and looked up through the broken roof.

Above the drowned chapel, where the stars had gone out one by one like pinched candles, the darkness over the eastern peaks — over the mining country, over Cindershade, over Bramble Hask and his crooked little shop full of other people's lost things — was beginning, very faintly, to glow.

Not starlight.

*Falllight.*

"Oh," Odile said, to the bell, to the chapel, to the honest dark. "Oh, no."

She was running before the thought finished, splashing through the nave with the grimoire humming against her chest, out through the broken western door, into the night — toward the mountains, toward the town, toward whatever was falling out of the sky with her friend's name written on it in fire.
