## 8. Cindershade Burns

The plague came to Cindershade the way everything came to Cindershade: down the mountain, on the wind, smelling faintly of iron and old pennies.

Odile felt it before she saw it. She was sitting in Bramble Hask's back room, haggling over a coil of rope — "That's robbery," she'd said, and Bramble had said, "Yes, but it's *friendly* robbery" — when her teeth began to ache. Not a normal ache. A deep, resonant hum, like the grimoire had grown opinions about the weather.

She pulled it from her satchel. The pages were shivering.

"Bramble," she said slowly. "What's that light outside?"

Bramble leaned past her to the window. Down in the town bowl, where the miners' cottages huddled together against the cold like old men at a funeral, something green-white was drifting through the streets. Not fog. Fog didn't glow. Fog didn't move *toward* people, curling into doorways like a cat that knew which hearths were warmest.

"That," Bramble said, in the flat voice of a man revising his entire week, "is new."

* * *

Odile hit the street at a run and saw the first of them within thirty paces: a woman standing in her own doorway, holding a child's mitten, saying, "I made this for — I made this for —" Her face worked like a lock refusing a key. "Who did I make this for?"

The child was right there. Standing in front of her. Waving.

The woman looked at the child the way one looks at a polite stranger who has knocked at an inconvenient hour.

Odile's stomach dropped through the frozen ground and kept going.

She'd read about this. Three nights ago, by candlelight, in her mistress's cramped marginalia, in handwriting that slanted downhill like it was tired: *The unlit stars do not burn flesh. They burn the ties between. Touch the shard-light and you forget what holds you up.* And beneath that, smaller, almost apologetic: *There is a hedge for it. Crude, but it holds. See page 214.*

Page 214. She'd skipped it, because page 214 was titled *On Boils, Warts, and Other Small Miseries*, and she'd assumed her mistress had been listing her enemies.

Now Odile sprinted back toward Bramble's, dodging the glowing drifts, and found the relevant passage. It called for salt, ash, a copper nail, and — she squinted — "a true name spoken backward, or failing that, something you love enough to be embarrassed about."

"Well," Odile muttered, "I'm full of embarrassing things."

* * *

By full dark, the schoolhouse on Tanner's Row had become something it had never been before: full.

Cindershade's children had come running when the lights came, because children run *toward* strange glowing things, which is why the species survives despite itself. Their parents had not all come after them. Some were standing in the streets mid-sentence, mouths open around words that no longer had anyone attached.

Twenty-three children. Odile counted twice, hoping the number would shrink out of mercy. It did not.

They were frightened, and being children, they expressed this by asking questions.

"Are we going to die?"

"No."

"Are *you* going to die?"

"Also no."

"My da says magic is for cheats and foreigners."

"Your da sounds like a man who's about to learn something," Odile said, grinding ash in a mortar with more force than the recipe strictly required.

The hedge-magic was simple, which was its whole genius. Her mistress had written: *You cannot fight the forgetting. You can only give it something else to trip over.* A circle of salt and ash at each threshold. A copper nail driven into the lintel. And then the hard part: a spoken anchor. Something loved, named aloud, tied to the door like a knot in a rope so that when the shard-light came licking at the family inside, it would find the knot and slide off.

For the orphaned ones — there were four — Odile used herself. She stood each child in the doorway, put her hand on their head, and said their names backward, then forward, then said, "And if anyone asks, you're my terrible little cousins and I'm very sorry about it." The children, sensing a promotion, accepted instantly.

It took until nearly midnight. Her fingers were black with ash. Her voice was going. The smallest one, a boy named Pip with a gap in his teeth you could post letters through, appointed himself her assistant and handed her nails with tremendous ceremony, occasionally handing her things that were not nails, such as a beetle and half a biscuit.

"The beetle's for luck," he explained.

"I'll take the biscuit," Odile said, "and pray to the beetle separately."

At some point she realized the humming in her teeth had stopped inside the schoolhouse. Outside, the green light pressed against the windows like faces at glass. Inside, twenty-three children slept in piles, breathing salt-and-ash air, still knowing their own mothers' names.

Odile sat down on the floor among them, back against the wall, and let herself shake for exactly one minute. She timed it. Then she got up and checked the circles again.

* * *

Warden Captain Ilsa Reyes arrived in Cindershade two hours behind the plague and one hour behind her own temper.

Her orders were simple, as bad orders always are: secure the town, contain the outbreak, and apprehend "the runaway apprentice Villalobos, last seen trading in Cindershade, description attached." Attached it was. Odile Villalobos: nineteen, dark-haired, scarred left palm, carries a grimoire, looks guilty even when buying bread. Reyes had read it three times and thought, each time, *someone wrote this who has never once looked guilty buying bread.*

She found the apprentice at the schoolhouse. Or rather, she found the schoolhouse glowing softly at every threshold like a shrine, salt lines crisp as fresh snowfall, copper nails winking in the lamplight — and inside, a girl asleep sitting up, surrounded by sleeping children, with a beetle crawling on her shoulder and half a biscuit clutched in one ash-blackened hand.

Reyes stood in the doorway a long moment.

Protocol said arrest. Protocol had a whole section on unauthorized hedge-craft, subsection four, *practiced upon the public without license*, penalty attached. Reyes had helped write subsection four. She remembered being proud of it.

But protocol also assumed the practitioner was doing harm, and Reyes was looking at a roomful of children whose mothers would wake tomorrow still knowing their names — while across town, in the unsalted houses, a man named Aldous Ferrow had just asked his wife of thirty years why a stranger was crying in his kitchen.

The stranger was his wife.

Reyes backed away from the doorway. Quietly. Like a burglar, except in reverse.

* * *

She found the girl an hour later at the well, hauling water for the salt lines, moving like someone whose body had filed a formal complaint.

"You're Odile Villalobos," Reyes said.

The girl didn't drop the bucket, which Reyes noted with professional respect. She just went very still, the way small animals do when they've calculated that stillness is their only asset.

"And you're the warden who's been following me since Thornfell," Odile said. "I'd say good morning, but we both know what you're here for."

"Water first," Reyes said. "Arrests keep."

Something in the absurdity of it loosened the girl's shoulders half an inch. She finished hauling the bucket. Reyes watched the salt line at the wellhead — someone had circled the well too, which meant the girl was thinking ahead, protecting the water itself, which meant she understood the plague better than any of Reyes's own ward-scholars, who were currently three hundred miles away writing memos.

"You know what this is," Reyes said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know what my mistress wrote about it." Odile set the bucket down. "Unlit star-shards. They don't kill you. They take the *ties*. Family, love, the person you'd crawl through fire for — gone, mid-word, like a snuffed candle. The Crown's scholars call it resonance sickness. My mistress called it homesickness for a home you can't remember." She paused. "Why? What does the Crown call it?"

"A containment problem," Reyes said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

Odile laughed — short, sharp, no humor in it. "Of course it does."

They stood there in the pre-dawn cold, two women on opposite sides of a law, both looking at the same glowing streets. Somewhere a mother was rocking a child who no longer knew her, singing a lullaby to a stranger, and getting the words wrong.

"Why didn't you arrest me?" Odile asked finally. "In the schoolhouse. You had me asleep. That's the easiest arrest of your career."

Reyes considered several answers. *Duty requires judgment* sounded too much like a recruitment poster. *The children needed you more than the law needs me* was true but too soft to say aloud to a fugitive.

"Because I watched you work," she said instead. "And because whatever's coming for this border, salt and copper bought these people a night that my protocols couldn't. I don't have to like you to know which of us was useful tonight."

Odile studied her the way one studies a locked door, checking for hinges. "You're not what I expected."

"Nobody ever is. It's the great disappointment of being met." Reyes turned to go, then stopped. "Villalobos. The shards fell in the high pass first, then drifted here on the wind. Which means the next town downwind is Lowmarsh. Four hundred souls. No salt lines. No copper. No one who knows page 214 of anything."

Odile went pale in the lantern light. "How do you know what page—"

"I read your file thoroughly," Reyes said. "It's remarkably detailed for a girl who 'looks guilty buying bread.'" She pulled her cloak tight. "Lowmarsh is two days' ride. The plague will be there in one."

"Then I'll need a faster horse than the one I don't have."

Reyes looked at her for a long moment, feeling the shape of the decision settle over her like snowfall — heavy, silent, irreversible.

"Get your grimoire," she said. "And leave a note for Bramble Hask telling him where his rope went. He strikes me as the type to invoice the Crown."

Behind them, the eastern sky was turning grey, and above the peaks — for the first time in eight days — one single star flickered, guttered, and held.

Odile saw it and felt the grimoire hum against her hip, once, like a heartbeat remembering itself.

"It knows," she whispered. "The stones. One of them's close."

"Then ride," said Reyes, "and tell me everything on the way. Starting with why a book of boils and warts is humming like a kettle."

They rode out of Cindershade as the sun came up on a town of salted thresholds and broken sentences — and neither of them noticed the figure on the ridge above, wearing a warden's stolen face, watching them go with eyes the color of extinguished stars.
