## 9. Two Thieves, One Fire

They rode north through the morning with the mountains leaning over them like old men eavesdropping.

Odile had expected many things from Warden Captain Ilsa Reyes. Arrests. Iron. The particular politeness of a woman who intended to see you hanged and had already picked the tree. What she had not expected was conversation — and yet Reyes talked nearly the whole way, in a flat warden's cadence that made even questions sound like inventory.

"Where did you learn the threshold-salting?"

"My mistress."

"Your mistress being?"

"Dead," Odile said, and was surprised at how little it cost her to say it. "Three weeks now. Maybe four. I stopped counting."

Reyes absorbed this without comment. Her mare picked its way down a scree slope with the delicacy of a much larger animal, and Odile's borrowed pony — a shaggy, opinionated creature named Turnip, on loan from Bramble Hask at an interest rate Odile chose not to think about — followed because following was easier than thinking.

"You salted forty-one thresholds in Cindershade," Reyes said. "In one night. Alone."

"The children were forgetting their mothers' names mid-sentence. It seemed rude to wait for help."

Something happened at the corner of Reyes's mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile, or it might have been indigestion; with wardens it was difficult to tell.

"Hedge-magic is against the Crown Statutes," she said. "Article Twelve."

"So is letting a whole town forget itself, and nobody arrested the stars."

They rode on. Somewhere behind them, Cindershade's smoke thinned into the grey of the sky, and somewhere ahead — Odile felt it through the grimoire like a second pulse under her own — something waited that wanted to be found.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a granite shelf, where the wind went to complain about other people. Reyes built the fire with the efficiency of someone who had done it ten thousand times and resented every one, then produced from her saddlebag: hard bread, harder cheese, and a strip of dried meat that Odile suspected predated the border wards themselves.

"Dinner," Reyes said.

"This is a punishment."

"This is rations."

"This is what happens when bread gives up." But Odile ate it anyway, because running for your life does wonders for the appetite, and because refusing food from a warden felt like a bad idea in a week full of them.

Reyes sat across the fire with her sword across her knees — not drawn, not sheathed, just present, the way some men kept dogs. She watched Odile eat for a long moment, and then she said, quietly, in a voice entirely different from her inventory-voice:

"I could take you tonight. You know that."

Odile's hand drifted toward the grimoire. "You could try."

"No theatrics. I mean it plainly. You're exhausted, your pony is slower than my grandmother, and I have six years of training in putting people on the ground." She turned the bread heel in her fingers. "I've had three chances since Cindershade. You slept through two of them."

The fire popped. Odile looked up sharply. "You — what?"

"You talk in your sleep," Reyes said. "Mostly about a woman named Maren. And once, very passionately, about cheese."

"That last part is private."

"Everything you do is private until I make it a report." Reyes set the bread down. She rubbed her eyes, and for a moment — just a moment — she looked less like a warden captain and more like a tired woman in her forties who had been riding too long and sleeping too little and carrying something heavy that wasn't in any saddlebag.

"Here's my problem, Villalobos."

"You know my name?"

"I know everything the Crown knows about you, which is a page and a half and mostly wrong. Here's my problem." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the grey in her close-cropped hair. "My orders say bring you back alive. Not capture you alive — *bring you back* alive. There's a difference, and the difference is that somebody in the capital wants you breathing specifically, and there are only a few reasons a Crown wants a specific person breathing."

Odile's mouth went dry. "Blood sacrifice."

"You've read the old ward-rituals."

"I've read *everything*. My mistress didn't believe in unread books." Odile hugged her knees. "A covenant broken can be mended two ways. Reforged by oath-stone, or sealed by blood given willingly at the breach. That's what the grimoire says. My mistress wrote in the margin —" she stopped.

"In the margin what?"

"'Willingly,'" Odile said slowly. "'That word is doing all the work.'" She looked at Reyes. "You can't force it. If they drag me to the breach and cut my throat, it won't seal. It'll just be murder with paperwork."

"I know," Reyes said. "I've known since the pass. Which means either the council doesn't know, or the council knows and thinks willingness can be manufactured." Her jaw tightened. "Neither option reflects well on the council."

* * *

The wind changed, and with it came a smell Odile had learned to dread: burnt sugar and cold iron, the smell of star-shard. Reyes smelled it too; her hand moved to her sword before her head finished turning.

But nothing came out of the dark. The smell passed over their little fire like a rumor and moved on down the valley, toward some other town, some other family, and Odile watched Reyes track it with an expression Odile recognized because she'd seen it in her own face in Cindershade: the look of someone counting doors they couldn't close.

"How many?" Odile asked. "Shards. Since the wards fell."

"Four confirmed. Probably more in the high country where nobody counts." Reyes fed a stick to the fire. "Each one leaves a crater and a sickness. The sickness takes memories first. Then names. Then—" she waved vaguely, "—the rest."

"I know. I watched it happen to the cobbler's wife. She forgot her husband between 'good morning' and 'and you?'"

"And you salted forty-one thresholds."

"It's not a cure. It's a bandage. Hedge-magic slows the forgetting, doesn't stop it." Odile pulled the grimoire onto her lap, feeling its familiar weight, its faint warmth, like a cat that had decided to tolerate her. "Stopping it requires the covenant. The real one. Five oath-stones, scattered when the garden fell, each one holding a knot of the original binding. Reforge all five and the border seals itself — properly, permanently, no blood required."

Reyes stared at her. "And the Crown's plan is to skip all that and open a vein instead."

"The Crown's plan has fewer steps."

"The Crown's plan," Reyes said, "is what happens when clever men want credit for solving something quickly more than they want it solved." She was quiet a moment. Then: "Tell me about the stones."

So Odile told her. The First Garden beneath the frozen waterfall, and the stone that had hummed at her touch but refused her — *not yet worthy*, it had said, or rather not said, in the way stones say things. The drowned chapel where Vesperin had cornered her, and the half-truth oath she'd sworn him — "I will give you the covenant" — and how the words had sunk into her like hooks, still there, still waiting.

Reyes listened the way she did everything: completely, without interruption, missing nothing.

"When you swore that oath," she said, "you promised him 'the covenant.' Any covenant. Including, say—" her eyes narrowed, "—a new one. Sealed by someone else. At a time of your choosing."

Odile blinked. "I... hadn't gotten that far."

"You hadn't gotten that far. You swore an open-ended magical contract with an exiled Fae lord and you hadn't gotten that far." Reyes pinched the bridge of her nose. "Villalobos, remind me never to play cards with you. I'd lose the shirt and then find out you'd bet my horse too."

"It seemed like a good idea at the time! He was wearing a man's face and the water was rising!"

"It usually seems like a good idea at the time. That's what 'at the time' is *for*." But there was no real bite in it, and something in Odile unclenched slightly — the thing that had been clenched since the night the stars went out, the thing that braced for every voice to turn into an accusation.

Because that was the thing. Every voice, for weeks, had wanted something from her. Vesperin wanted the grimoire. The Crown wanted her blood. Bramble, dear mercenary Bramble, wanted payment plus interest. Even the stones wanted — well. She was beginning to learn what the stones wanted.

Nobody, so far, had simply wanted the plague stopped.

* * *

It was past midnight when Reyes finally said it, staring into the fire as if the words needed to be warmed before use.

"I had a sister."

Odile said nothing. Some sentences you let stand alone.

"Maren. Younger than me by two years and smarter by ten, though she'd have argued the order of those." The firelight moved on Reyes's face. "She used to slip across the old border. Before the wards, when there were still crossings the mountain folk kept. She'd come back with stories. Fae markets where they sold bottled weather. A lord who paid in songs." A pause. "She didn't come back the last time."

"I'm sorry."

"So was I. For about a decade. Then I got promoted, and grief is inconvenient to a career, so I filed it." She said it flatly, but her thumb had gone white pressing against the sword's scabbard. "When the wards fell, I stood in that crater and I thought — maybe. Maybe she's on the other side of whatever this is. Maybe if the border opens all the way instead of breaking ugly, I could go find her."

"And then you saw what actually comes through."

"A shard-sickness victim who'd forgotten his own children. And a Fae lord wearing Corporal Dennet's face, asking after a stolen book." Reyes looked up. "Whatever's on the other side of that border now, it isn't Maren's markets. So I need the border sealed. Properly. Permanently. No blood, no shortcuts, no clever men taking credit." She reached into her breastplate and drew out a folded paper — worn soft at the creases, handled often. She held it up so the firelight showed the sketch: a young woman, sharp-eyed, hair escaping its braid, a description beneath in a clerk's tidy hand.

"Your description," Reyes said. "Posted in every garrison from here to the capital. Reward, custody orders, the works." She turned it in her fingers. "Paper burns."

Odile's heart did something complicated. "Captain—"

"I'm not offering friendship. I'm offering arithmetic. One runaway apprentice with a grimoire and a plan is worth more to me free than hanged. Help me find another way to seal the wards — your stones, your mistress's rituals, all of it — and I lose this paper. Officially it was destroyed in the Cindershade fire, which conveniently enough actually happened." She extended the description toward the flames.

"Wait," Odile said.

Reyes paused, paper at the fire's edge.

"If you burn it, you're a traitor. To the Crown. To your commander. Everything you are." Odile leaned forward. "Why? Truly. Not the arithmetic. The arithmetic is good, I'll grant it, I'm excellent value. But why?"

Reyes considered the question longer than most people would have. When she answered, her voice was low and rough, like a door opening on a room long shut.

"Because I've spent twenty years enforcing laws, and in all that time I've met perhaps five people who broke them for a reason I could respect. You salted forty-one thresholds in one night so children would remember their mothers' names." She almost smiled; this time there was no mistaking it. "The law says arrest you. The law also says Article Twelve, and Article Twelve has never once stopped a star from falling. I'm tired of serving the part of the law that doesn't work."

She fed the paper to the fire.

It caught, curled, went up in a breath of orange light — a young woman's face becoming smoke, a price becoming ash. Odile watched it go and felt lighter than she had in weeks, and immediately suspicious of feeling lighter, because in her experience lightness was usually the moment before the floor opened.

"There," Reyes said. "Two thieves, one fire."

"I'm not a thief."

"You stole a grimoire from your dead mistress's cottage and fled the province."

"I *inherited* it. Prematurely. Through a window."

"Through a window."

"She was dead! It's not like she needed it! And besides—" Odile stopped. Something in her chest snagged, the way it always snagged on this particular truth, and she heard herself continue before she could decide whether to: "Besides, I don't actually know she left it to me. There's no letter. No note. I took it and ran and I've been telling myself it was inheritance ever since, because the alternative is that I robbed a corpse."

The fire crackled. Reyes studied her across it with those warden's eyes that missed nothing, and Odile braced for judgment.

Instead Reyes said: "Every thief I've ever caught had a story that made them the hero. Most of them believed it. The interesting ones knew it was a story." She tore the bread heel in half and threw the larger piece across the fire. "Eat. Tomorrow we ride for the drowned chapel — you said the second stone's in its crypt, yes?"

"Yes. Assuming Vesperin hasn't set a welcome for us. He knows I owe him a covenant. He'll be watching anything that smells of oath-stones."

"Then we arrive smelling of nothing. We split up, take the chapel from two sides, and meet in the crypt." Reyes settled back against the granite, sword across her knees, eyes already half-lidded in the way of soldiers who sleep in slices. "Wake me for second watch. And Villalobos—"

"Yes?"

"Don't swear any oaths tonight. To anyone. About anything. Including breakfast."

"No promises," Odile said, and Reyes made a sound that was probably a laugh and definitely a snort, and for the first time since the stars went out, Odile slept without dreaming of running.

* * *

She woke before dawn to the grimoire humming against her hip — louder than before, insistent, a kettle at full boil — and to Reyes already standing, already armed, staring north with an expression Odile had hoped never to see on her.

"What?" Odile scrambled up. "What is it?"

"The fog," Reyes said quietly.

Odile followed her gaze. The valley ahead lay under a low grey blanket, and it was moving — not drifting, *moving*, with purpose, rolling uphill against the wind, and where it touched the pines the pines stood bare in moments, and where it touched a herd of mountain goats the goats stood still, heads hanging, already forgetting what grass was for.

Star-shard fog. Miles of it. Between them and the drowned chapel.

And at the fog's leading edge, briefly visible as the greylight strengthened, something tall walked ahead of it — something wearing a warden's uniform and a stolen face, arms spread wide like a shepherd, guiding the forgetting gently, patiently, toward the only road through the mountains.

Toward the chapel. Toward the stone.

"He knows," Odile whispered, clutching the grimoire as it pulsed like a second heart. "He knows exactly where we're going. He's herding us."

"Then he's made one mistake," Reyes said, checking her blade with a cold bright click. "He thinks we're sheep."

The fog rolled closer, and the grimoire hummed, and somewhere beneath the ice of the drowned chapel, the second oath-stone waited — patient as memory, hungry as winter — for what Odile would have to give it.
