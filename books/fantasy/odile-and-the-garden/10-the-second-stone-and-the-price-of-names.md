## 10. The Second Stone and the Price of Names

The drowned chapel had been drowning for a very long time and seemed in no hurry to finish.

Odile stood at its threshold in the grey light before dawn, water lapping at her boots where the flood had swallowed the churchyard whole. The building rose out of a shallow lake like the ribcage of some patient whale — black stone walls, a roof half-collapsed into a crown of moss, and a bell tower leaning at an angle that suggested it had given up on standing straight sometime around her grandmother's grandmother's birth.

"It's under there," she said. "The second stone. Under all of that."

"Under the lake," Reyes said flatly.

"Under most of the lake."

Reyes crossed her arms. She was a tall woman built like a drawn bowstring, and even soaked to the knee in freezing marshwater she managed to look like she was conducting an inspection. "In Cindershade you told me these stones were scattered across the mountains. You did not mention they'd be *submerged*."

"The mistress's marginalia says the chapel flooded in the Year of the Long Rains." Odile held up the grimoire, open to a page crowded with her mistress's cramped, slanted handwriting — the old woman had written in the margins the way other people breathed, constantly and without much care for tidiness. "There's a crypt beneath the altar floor. Sealed against water. She drew a little diagram." The diagram was, typically, terrible: a box, a squiggle, and what might have been a fish or might have been an insult directed at the local clergy.

"And how do we get to this crypt?"

"We walk in," Odile said. "And hold our breath."

"I'm going to write a formal complaint about your mistress's planning," Reyes said, "if either of us survives to file it."

* * *

The water inside the nave was waist-deep and cold enough to make Odile's thoughts go small and simple. *Cold. Wet. Regret.* Pews floated past them at drunken angles, furred with weed, and pale fish scattered from their legs like startled coins. Light came down through the broken roof in long green shafts, and dust motes drifted through them so slowly they seemed suspended rather than falling — as if the whole chapel were holding its breath along with them.

Reyes waded ahead, one hand resting on her sword hilt out of what Odile suspected was pure habit rather than any expectation of enemies. Wardens, Odile had learned over two weeks of uneasy travel, touched their swords the way other people touched amulets.

"You know," Reyes said, "when I imagined hunting down a runaway apprentice, I imagined it would be harder than this."

"Oh?"

"I imagined running. Fighting. At minimum, some screaming." Reyes kicked a floating hymnal out of her path. "Instead I've spent fourteen days following a young woman who stops to apologize to donkeys."

"He looked tired."

"He's a *donkey*, Villalobos."

"That's no reason to be rude to him."

Reyes made a sound that in a less disciplined woman would have been a laugh. It was the fourth such sound Odile had counted, and she was keeping score privately, the way she'd once kept score of her mistress's rare smiles. Some things you collected because they were scarce.

The altar still stood at the head of the nave, though the lake had claimed everything below its third step. Carved into its face, half-eroded, Odile could just make out the old covenant words — the same words that circled the First Garden's door, worn now almost to suggestion: *We give what is ours to keep what is everyone's.*

Her chest tightened. In the First Garden, the stone had hummed under her palm and then gone cold, rejecting her. *Not yet worthy,* she'd thought. *Not yet honest.* She'd been carrying that coldness around ever since, like a pebble in her boot.

"There," she said, pointing. Beneath the altar, where the water ran clearest, a seam in the floor described a square — the crypt lid. And set into the seam, glowing faintly through six feet of green water, was a line of light the color of dawn on snow.

The oath-stone. Waiting.

* * *

Getting the lid open was the work of an hour and cost Reyes a bootlace, most of Odile's dignity, and one incident involving a leech that Odile refused to discuss afterward under any circumstances. But at last the square of stone groaned aside, releasing a breath of air from below — dry, ancient, and smelling faintly of roses, which was so profoundly wrong for a flooded crypt that both of them stopped and stared at each other across it.

"Covenant magic," Odile said quietly. "The garden doesn't really care about water. Water is just... weather, to it."

"Wonderful. And you're going down there alone, I assume, because you've decided that's how it works."

"You could wait up here."

"I could," Reyes agreed, and began unhooking her sword belt. "But if something in that hole eats you, I'll have to explain to my commander why I lost the sacrifice the Crown wants alive, and frankly the paperwork alone would kill me. Hand me that rope."

They went down together.

The crypt was small — ten paces by eight — and impossibly dry. Candle niches lined the walls, and when Odile brushed her fingers across one, an old stub of wax flared alight without being asked, flame steady and honey-colored. More lights woke as she passed, until the little room glowed like the inside of a lantern. Someone had tended these candles once, long ago. Someone had believed this room mattered.

And there, on a plinth at the center, sat the second oath-stone.

It was smaller than she'd expected — no bigger than a loaf of bread, a rough-hewn block of grey granite shot through with veins of light. It didn't glow so much as *breathe*, the light rising and falling like a sleeping animal's flank. The air around it felt the way the moment before sunrise feels: full, held, about to become something.

Odile knelt in front of it. Her heart was hammering, but not from fear exactly. From recognition. This was what the First Garden had promised her and then withheld. This was the thing she'd been walking toward since the night the stars went out.

"Well?" Reyes stood behind her, arms crossed, candlelight making her shadow enormous on the wall. "What does your book say?"

"It says—" Odile turned pages with damp fingers. "It says the stone will accept an offering. A memory. Freely given. And then..." She frowned. "Then it says something in Old Covenant script I can't quite read. The ink's faded."

"Convenient."

"My mistress wrote half her notes in code and the other half in complaints about her knees. Nothing about her was convenient."

She set the grimoire down. She put both hands on the stone.

It was warm. That was the first thing — warm as a hand, warm as bread, warm as the kitchen of the cottage she'd fled, and the warmth went up her arms and into her chest and she heard — not with her ears, somewhere behind them — a voice like wind through a hedge asking, gently, in the way of all old growing things:

*What will you give?*

And here was the thing Odile had not understood until that exact moment, kneeling in a dead woman's crypt with her palms on living stone: she had assumed the choice would be hard. She had lain awake at campfires rehearsing sacrifices. Her memory of the cottage. Her memory of Bramble. Her memory of the road, the escape, the wild bright terror of the night the wards fell.

She had never once considered that the stone would choose for her.

The warmth gathered. It moved through her the way a searchlight moves through a dark house — unhurried, thorough, touching everything. She felt it pass over her memories of her mistress and pause, and pause, and move on. She felt it find her mother — her mother in the orchard, her mother laughing, her mother singing —

*singing*

— and stop.

"No," Odile said aloud. "No, wait—"

Too late. Giving back was not among the options. The stone drank.

* * *

Grief, Odile discovered, has a shape. It arrived in her like weather, sudden and total, and it had a very specific geography: a hole exactly the size of a voice.

She could remember her mother. That was the cruel trick of it. She remembered her face — brown eyes, a scar through one eyebrow from a pruning knife, laugh lines deep as furrows. She remembered her hands, quick and dirt-stained. She remembered the orchard, the smell of crushed grass, the summer afternoon her mother taught her to whistle through a blade of grass and Odile had produced something between a note and a dying goose and her mother had laughed until she cried.

She remembered all of it perfectly.

Except the voice. She reached for it and found nothing. Not silence exactly — silence implies a space where sound could be. This was the absence of the possibility of sound. She tried to hear her mother say her name and got nothing, the way you get nothing when you try to remember a dream from childhood. She tried to hear her humming while she worked, that low tuneless thing she'd done over the stew pot, and there was simply — nothing. A portrait with the mouth sewn shut.

She was crying before she knew it, great ugly sobs that echoed off the crypt ceiling, and Reyes — stiff, formal, terrifying Warden Captain Reyes — crouched down beside her and did the last thing Odile expected, which was nothing at all. She just stayed. Put one hand flat on Odile's back between the shoulder blades, heavy and steady, like a weight keeping a tent peg in the ground.

They stayed that way for a long time. The candles burned. Somewhere above them, water dripped its slow count.

"What did it take?" Reyes asked finally. Quietly. Not a warden's question. Just a person's.

"My mother's voice." Odile wiped her face with her sleeve. "I can remember everything else about her. Everything. But I can't hear her anymore. She sang to me every night until I was nine. I can't—" Her breath hitched. "It's like she died twice. Once for real, and once just now, a little more."

Reyes was silent for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had a strange grain to it. "My sister used to sing too. Badly. Off-key, always, and loud." She stared at the candle flame. "She went beyond the border when we were girls. Before the wards fell, when people still slipped through sometimes. They never found her." A pause. "Some nights I can't remember her voice either. And I could never tell if that was grief or just... time. Time doing what time does." She looked at the stone with new understanding, and something that wasn't quite hatred. "This thing doesn't steal. It just makes time honest."

"That's worse."

"Yes," Reyes said. "That's considerably worse."

* * *

Later — Odile couldn't have said how much later — she opened the grimoire again, because there had been that unreadable passage, the faded ink, and grief makes some people lie down and makes other people *research*. She tilted the page toward the candlelight, angling it, letting the flame's glow rake across the paper the way her mistress had taught her for reading palimpsests.

And the hidden writing rose up out of the page like a fish surfacing.

*The stones do not take what you offer,* her mistress had written, in ink invisible to ordinary light. *They take what you withhold least. The covenant reads the heart, child, not the hand. You cannot choose your price. You can only choose to pay it.*

Below that, smaller, pressed hard into the paper as if the pen had been shaking:

*I am sorry. I should have told you sooner. I was a coward about it, and cowards make poor teachers. Forgive me. Or don't, and hate me a while first — hatred keeps a body walking when forgiveness would let her sit down.*

Odile read it three times. Then she laughed — a wet, cracked, astonished laugh — because of course. Of course the old woman had known. Had known the wards would fall, had known Odile would run, had known she'd kneel in this crypt someday with her hands on the stone. Had known, and said nothing, and left an apology in invisible ink because she hadn't been brave enough to say it aloud, and had trusted Odile to be angry enough to keep moving anyway.

"Hatred keeps a body walking," Odile murmured. "You absolute menace."

"Your mistress?" Reyes asked.

"She hid a letter in the margins. In secret ink." Odile traced the words with one finger. "She knew what the stones cost. She sent me anyway."

"Do you forgive her?"

"I don't know yet." Odile closed the book and held it against her chest, over her heart, where the ache was. "I'll tell you when we find the third one. Ask me again after I've lost something else."

Above them, through the broken roof, the sky was turning the color of iron filings. And as they climbed the rope back up into the flooded nave, wading out through the green light and the drifting hymnals, Odile caught Reyes glancing at her sideways — twice, three times — wearing the expression of a woman recalculating something.

"What?" Odile asked.

"Nothing." Reyes hesitated. "It's only — the sacrifice the Crown wants. To reseal the wards. Blood, they said. A life." She was quiet a moment, water swirling around her knees. "I'm starting to wonder if the Crown's ledger and this stone's ledger are kept by the same accountant. And whether anyone upstairs has actually read the terms."

Odile stopped wading.

Because Reyes was right, and the realization landed in her stomach like a dropped stone: the covenant's currency was memory. Willing memory, freely paid. And the Crown meant to seal the breach with blood — with a life taken instead of a life given — and every instinct she had, every marginal note in her mistress's crabbed hand, screamed that a covenant paid in stolen coin would not hold. Would not hold at all.

Or worse. Would hold, and become something monstrous.

"We need to move," she said. "The third stone is at the mountain shrine, four days east. And I think—" she glanced up at the leaning bell tower, at the grey sky beyond it, where the stars should have been and weren't, "—I think we're not the only ones who've figured out what the stones are worth."

Far off across the drowned marshland, thin as a needle, something howled. It was not a wolf. Wolves, at least, had the decency to want ordinary things.

Reyes's hand went to her sword. "Tell me that's a donkey."

"It's not a donkey," Odile said, and started to run.
