## 11. Vesperin's Court of Ash

The court had once been a garden.

Vesperin remembered this the way one remembers a scar — not as an image but as an ache, a shape of wrongness pressed into the flesh of him. Where now the ash drifted down in soft grey snowfall, there had been hedges of night-blooming silver. Where his courtiers sat in their rings of cold fire, silent as votive candles, there had been fountains that sang in three-part harmony. And where the fallen stars lay half-buried in the cinders, still smoldering after all these years, there had been *light* — the honest kind, the kind that asked nothing of you.

He walked among them now, his courtiers, and let them feel him coming before they saw him. That was courtesy, in the Otherfell. To arrive announced was a kindness; to arrive unseen was either an insult or an assassination, and Vesperin had never cared for ambiguity.

They were beautiful, his people. He had gathered them over the long centuries the way a miser gathers coins: the exiled, the oath-broken, the ones whose names had been struck from the rolls of the bright courts for crimes real or imagined or merely inconvenient. A lady with moths where her eyes should have been. A knight whose armor grew thorns inward. Twin musicians who could play a grief so pure it made stone weep, which is why no stone structure in the Otherfell would admit them through its doors.

They rose when he passed. They always rose. He had made sure of that early on, in the hungry first century, when a court is only as loyal as its fear.

"Lord Vesperin." The moth-eyed lady inclined her head, and one of her moths escaped and circled his wrist like a pale ring. "The stars fell well last night. Three more across the pass. The human wards are bleeding light at every seam."

"Three," he repeated, tasting the number. "And the girl?"

"Still moving. She sleeps by day in hollow trees like an animal. She traded two pages of the grimoire to a fence in Cindershade for boots and dried plums." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "She haggled."

Vesperin smiled despite himself. "Of course she did. Her mistress taught her that a thing freely given has no value, and a thing hoarded too long rots. Haggling is the middle road." He turned the moth gently from his wrist and set it on the air, where it hung, undecided. "I begin to think Marisol chose her heir for her flaws as much as her virtues."

He said the name aloud only rarely. Marisol. His sister-in-exile, though exile had made them strangers rather than kin. When the covenant was forged — when the garden was sealed and the border drawn like a knife-line between the realms — Marisol had stood on the winning side of it, and Vesperin had stood nowhere at all, because the winning side had voted him out of existence.

* * *

He went alone to the heart of the court, where the largest fallen star lay in its crater of glassed earth. It was the size of a cathedral bell and it hummed, low and constant, the way a struck note hums long after the ear gives up on it. The unlit stars. The ones the covenant had snuffed when it divided the sky between the realms — *yours above your mountains, ours above our forests, and never the twain* — and cast down into the dark between.

His people thought he kept this one as a trophy. Let them think it. It was not a trophy. It was a witness.

"Tell me again," he said to it, because some nights the silence required an answer even a dishonest one.

The star did not answer. Stars never did. But memory obliged instead, the way it always does at the worst hour:

---

There had been a garden, and in the garden there had been a table of living root, and around the table the nine who would make the covenant had sat to decide the shape of the world.

He had been the tenth. Not invited. Present anyway, because the garden had been his before it was anyone's — he had planted its first hedge when the mountains were young and petty, and a gardener does not need an invitation to his own soil.

"We will divide the light," Marisol had said, in that voice she used for pronouncements, the one that brooked no weather. "The humans fear what they cannot name, and we are weary of being named wrongly. A border. Clean and bright. Their stars and ours."

"And the stars that fall between?" Vesperin had asked from the hedge-shadow. "The ones that belong to neither sky? What becomes of them?"

Silence, of the particular quality that means *we had hoped you wouldn't ask*.

"They go dark," said the eldest of the nine, a creature like a winter tree given opinions. "It is the price of a clean line. Some light must be unlit so that the rest may be portioned fairly."

"Some," Vesperin had repeated. "Meaning you will choose which."

"Meaning the covenant will choose," the winter-tree said, "and the covenant must be sworn by those with something to lose. You have nothing to lose, Vesperin. You never took a realm, never kept a court, never bound yourself to anything. You cannot swear what you do not hold."

And there it was. The wound, delivered politely, in formal language, before witnesses. *You have nothing to lose because you are nothing.* Not condemned for a crime. Condemned for a condition — for having lived free, for having loved the whole of the world instead of a parcel of it, for refusing, all those long years, to be owned.

He had laughed. He remembered that with shame now; he had laughed, because laughter was the only weapon left to someone being erased, and he had said, "Then I hold the garden itself, and I vote against," and the winter-tree had smiled its splintery smile and said, "The garden is the table, not a guest. Your objection is noted in the wind."

Nine hands raised. The covenant blazed up around the borders of the world like a wall of dawn. And the tenth chair — the root-stool he had carved himself, three hundred years before any of them learned to walk upright — crumbled to ash beneath him, because the covenant had decided he was not part of the world it was dividing.

He had not been banished. Banishment requires a sentence, a door, a direction. He had simply been *left out*, the way a word is left out of a treaty, and the omission had become his country.

---

The star hummed. Vesperin laid his palm against its cooling skin.

"You understand," he told it, "why I cannot forgive them."

Not absolution — he had heard, in the long dark, the temptation to make peace, to accept some minor seat at some minor table, to be *given back* a small piece of himself by the same hands that took the whole. He had watched other exiles take such bargains and dim, year by year, into ornaments. No. If the nine had taught him anything, it was that a thing given back is never the same as a thing never taken.

But if he could not have his light back, then the realms could learn what it was to live without theirs. That was not cruelty. That was *curriculum*. He meant to teach them the lesson written into his own bones: that a border is a wound dressed as a line, and that everyone on the wrong side of it is someone's omitted word.

"The apprentice," he said to the star, and now his voice gentled, because here was the complication he did not speak of to his court. "Marisol's little thief. She carries the grimoire, and the grimoire remembers the covenant's first draft — my draft, the one they rewrote while I stood in the hedge-shadow. Do you know what that means?"

The star hummed.

"It means the girl is carrying a letter I wrote and never sent." He laughed softly. "Fate has a sense of humor. It is a cruel one, but it is a sense of humor."

* * *

In the morning-that-was-not-morning — the Otherfell kept its own hours, grey and sourceless — his court assembled in the ashfall, and he gave them their orders.

"The warden captain," he said. "Reyes. Tell me what you know of her."

The moth-eyed lady spread her hands, and the moths arranged themselves into shapes: a woman, tall, square-shouldered; a ledger; a sword worn at the hip like a promise. "Dutiful. Grieving. A sister lost beyond the old border, back when the crossings still happened by accident. She follows the law the way the devout follow scripture — literally, and looking for loopholes only when the scripture breaks her heart."

"And her commander?"

A different shape in the moths: a fat man, a purse, a handshake in shadow. The court murmured. Even here, even among the discarded, treachery-for-coin drew contempt. Treason with a *discount*, apparently, was vulgar.

"He sold the ward-rituals to our agents eight years ago," the lady said. "For less than we offered. He seemed almost relieved to be caught at it."

"Of course he was. Guilt wants a buyer." Vesperin walked the length of his ash-strewn hall, and his courtiers parted like water, and he felt — as he always felt — the old vertigo of it: that all of this, the court, the loyalty, the beautiful ruined people, was built on the foundation of a chair that had crumbled under him. He was lord of everything the covenant had thrown away. It was a kingdom. It was also, precisely, a rubbish heap, and he had never decided which description he hated more.

"Here is what we do," he said. "We do not touch the warden captain. Let her find her traitor. Let her carry it to the capital like a torch into a powder store. Crowns break best from inside."

"And the girl?"

"The girl," Vesperin said, "is to be found. Not harmed. Not frightened — frightened things clutch their treasures, and I want her *thinking*. Send the twins. Their music opens doors that locks don't know they have."

The moth-eyed lady hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a courtier's hesitation, but Vesperin had spent four hundred years reading omissions, and he caught it the way one catches a wrong note.

"Speak."

"She refused you once already, lord. At the high pass. You wore a stolen face and offered safe passage, and she ran." The moths resettled nervously. "Why does a mortal refuse a Fae lord? They are supposed to want things from us. It is the natural order."

Vesperin considered the question honestly, which was more than most lords would have done.

"Because I lied to her," he said. "Small lie, polite lie — the face, the voice, the whole costume of trustworthiness. And her mistress trained her nose for it. Marisol always said a bargain begins with the truth or it begins with a trap, and the girl can smell which." He paused. "When I meet her properly, I will tell her the truth. All of it. The garden, the table, the ninth hand and the tenth chair. I will show her the wound."

"And she will pity you," the lady said, "and pity is not alliance."

"No," Vesperin agreed. "Pity is not alliance. But pity *listens*. And a listening enemy is a door half-open." He smiled, and the court, seeing the smile, relaxed, because they did not know — how could they know? — that the smile was the sound of a man hearing his own argument and finding it thin.

Because here was the truth he kept below even the truth: he did not actually want the girl's pity. He wanted — and the wanting shamed him more than any crime of his long exile — he wanted her to *understand*. To stand in his ash-choked court and look at the fallen stars and say, *yes, this was done to you, and it was wrong, and I see it.*

Four hundred years of darkness, and what he missed was not the light.

It was being seen by it.

* * *

That evening-that-was-not-evening, he went out past the bounds of his court, alone as always, to the place where the ash thinned and the border's broken edge flickered like a torn hem. Beyond it, the human mountains stood black against a sky with holes in it, and through the holes the Otherfell's stolen stars leaked a little light onto the snow, and somewhere down in those valleys a girl with a stolen book was sleeping in a hollow tree, dreaming mortal dreams, haggling in her sleep perhaps, bargaining with God knew what.

"You have her grimoire," he said to the mountains, conversationally. "Well. Half of it. She sold pages to buy boots, which I confess I admire. But the binding holds the rest, and the binding holds the letter, and the letter holds my name in the margins where Marisol tried to scratch it out and failed."

He crouched and pressed his hand flat against the earth on his side of the torn hem. Through the broken wards he could feel them — the five oath-stones, scattered like knuckles of a severed hand, humming their stubborn, patient hum. Four still unfound. One already refusing her, the reports said. Good. Refusal was the beginning of every real education.

"I was cast out for holding nothing," he told the stones, quietly, in the old tongue of the garden, the tongue only he and one dead woman still spoke. "So I have spent four hundred years acquiring everything, and I find I am still empty, and I have concluded the arithmetic was rigged from the start. So now, little stones, now I will make the realms share my darkness — every realm, both skies, all the hoarded light poured out equally into the ash — and we shall see whether equality tastes as sweet as they promised me it would."

The stones hummed. They did not answer. Nothing answered; that was the whole of his kingdom.

But far off, down in the human valleys, a bell began to ring — Cindershade's alarm, thin and frantic, carried on a wind that smelled of smoke and forgetting. The plague-shards had reached a town. Mortals were losing their loved ones mid-sentence, mothers turning to children with polite blank faces, and Vesperin stood at the torn edge of the world and listened to the bells with his head tilted like a man hearing music from a house he'd been locked out of.

He should have felt triumph. The curriculum had begun; the first lesson was being taught.

Instead he felt the old ache, the scar-shaped one, and he understood — with the clarity that comes only at the worst hours, in the coldest courts, among the ashes of a garden that had once been his — that the girl down there in the burning town, saving children with hedge-magic and dried-plum stubbornness, was going to be either his undoing or his heir.

And he could not yet say which terrified him more.

"Find her," he said to the darkness behind him, where his messengers waited. "Before the Crown does. Before the wardens do. Before anyone else writes her story for her."

The darkness moved to obey.

And far away, in a garrison office lit by one guttering candle, a warden captain opened a ledger she was not authorized to open — though of course, Vesperin could not know that yet, and would not, until the consequences came marching up his mountain like a debt collected.
