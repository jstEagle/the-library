## 12. The Traitor Among the Wardens

The archive beneath Fort Meridian smelled of dust, tallow, and forty years of Captain Ilsa Reyes's patience. She had spent three of those years down here as a junior warden, copying ledgers by candlelight while the older wardens drank upstairs, and she had sworn then that she'd never be sent back. Yet here she was, holding a lantern in one hand and a requisition slip in the other, because Commander Aldric Vane had personally ordered her to "review the historical ward-rituals for discrepancies" before the council arrived.

"Discrepancies," Reyes said to the empty room. "In a fortress full of them."

The archive did not answer. It never did. That was one of the things she liked about it.

She had been back at the fort six days since Cindershade — six days of not arresting Odile Villalobos, which was fast becoming the strangest professional decision of her life — and every day the questions had grown heavier. The wards hadn't failed. Wards didn't fail; they were woven into the bones of the mountains themselves, older than the Crown, older than the fort. They had been *unmade*, from the inside, by someone who knew the rituals.

And someone had to have taught them.

Reyes set her lantern on the reading table and pulled the ritual registry — a ledger so old its leather cover had gone soft as chamois — and began where any sensible investigator begins: with signatures.

* * *

The ward-rituals were maintained through an unbroken chain of custody. Every copy, every annotation, every re-binding of the border spells was logged, signed, countersigned, and sealed. A thousand years of wardens' names marched down the margins like a funeral procession.

She found what she was looking for eleven years back. Or rather, she found what she was *not* looking for: a gap. Three months in which the master copies of the sealing rites had been removed from the archive — officially "for restoration" — and returned with no restoration record at all. The signature authorizing the removal belonged to Aldric Vane.

At the time, he had been a captain. Ambitious, charming, generous with his wine and stingy with his praise. He'd signed her first commendation. He'd stood beside her at her sister's memorial service and said, "The border takes, Ilsa. We hold anyway." She had believed him completely, the way you believe a man who has memorized all the right sentences.

Reyes sat very still for a long moment, listening to the candle gutter.

Then she did what any sensible investigator does next: she checked whether it happened again.

It had. Twice more over the following decade. Each time the master rites left the archive under Vane's authority and returned thinner — pages quietly excised, substitutions made so subtle that only someone who had spent three years copying these very ledgers would ever notice. Someone had been feeding pieces of the covenant's architecture out of the fortress a spoonful at a time, the way you'd steal flour from a mill, until finally whoever held the recipe had known enough to unmake the whole loaf.

She thought about the smuggler roads. About how the breach had opened precisely where the wards were weakest, precisely where the old texts had been hollowed out. Coincidence was a luxury she stopped affording herself around midnight.

* * *

By the second candle, she found the letter.

It was tucked inside a false spine in the restoration cabinet — Vane's own cabinet, she realized, with a small cold shock — wrapped in oilcloth against damp. Not a confession. Confessions were for amateurs and dying men. This was correspondence: neat, courteous letters between *Captain A. Vane* and a correspondent identified only by a sigil she recognized from the grimoire's marginalia, from Odile's frantic sketches drawn in firelight — a crescent cradling a falling star.

Vesperin's mark.

The letters discussed payment. Not gold — Vane had wanted advancement, appointments, a seat on the council eventually, and the letters promised all of it, in language so smooth it practically poured itself. In exchange: fragments of the sealing rites, delivered across eleven years, "for scholarly purposes, naturally." One letter, dated four years ago, thanked Vane for "the verse concerning the anchor-stone's naming" and remarked, almost playfully:

*"You may tell your superiors whatever fiction comforts them. When the border opens, your usefulness will be remembered. Exiles keep excellent books."*

Exiles.

Reyes read the line four times. Then she read the whole packet again, slower, and this time she noticed the thing she should have noticed first: the most recent letter was dated eight months ago. Eight months. The sabotage wasn't recent. It was a decade in the making, patient as rot, and the man who had signed off on the plan to reseal the border — the man now demanding a blood sacrifice to do it — was the man who had sold the door's hinges in the first place.

He wasn't trying to fix the breach. He was managing it. Keeping the crisis alive long enough to climb the bodies it made.

Reyes closed her eyes. Her sister's face surfaced, unbidden — Marta, laughing on the last morning, insisting the Otherfell couldn't be all bad if the stars over there were brighter. Marta, lost beyond the border when the old patrols ended. If the wards fell because of Vane, then everything after — the lost patrols, the grieving families, the whole slow bleeding wound of the frontier — traced back to a man who had shaken her hand at a funeral and said *we hold anyway.*

She laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, and echoed strangely in the archive, so she stopped.

"All right," she told the room. "Now what?"

* * *

Here was the arithmetic, and it was brutal.

If she reported the letters through channels, they went to Vane's allies on the council — men he'd promoted, men who owed him, men whose careers were braided into his like rope. The letters would vanish. She would vanish shortly afterward, quietly, some posting far north where disgraced captains froze productively. And the sacrifice decree would proceed exactly on schedule, because a scapegoat was worth more to that machine than the truth.

If she went around channels, she was no longer a warden. She was a mutineer with stolen documents, and mutineers got the same treatment as runaways — except nobody would write ballads about her.

And if she destroyed the letters?

No one would ever know. Vane would rise. The institution would survive, gleaming and rotten, and Reyes would go on serving it, and somewhere inside her something would die quietly, the way Marta had died, without witnesses.

She laid the letters out on the reading table in order, oldest to newest, and looked at them the way you look at a wound you're deciding whether to cauterize.

"You gave your life to this place," she said aloud. "That was the phrase. You *gave* it. Well, Captain, it seems the house took payment and never wrote you a receipt."

The candle flame leaned, as if listening.

There was a third option, of course. There usually was, if you were willing to be neither honest nor obedient but simply careful. She thought about Odile — about a girl who had escaped a flooded chapel by swearing an oath made entirely of loopholes, and who had looked genuinely apologetic about it. *Half-truths,* the girl had said once, over that campfire that felt like a hundred years ago. *My mistress always said a half-truth is a knife you can carry openly.* Reyes had pretended to disapprove. She had been lying, mostly to herself, and the girl had probably known that too.

Fine. She could learn.

* * *

What remained of the night, Reyes spent making a copy.

Not a scholar's copy — she had no time for fair hands and careful ink. She copied the damning lines onto the flyleaves of her own field journal, in her own cramped shorthand, interleaved with mundane patrol notes where a casual reader would see nothing but weather reports and complaints about the mules. The sigil she rendered as a doodle of a star falling into a cup. The dates hid among grain requisitions. Anyone who knew her shorthand — exactly two people alive, one of them retired to a goat farm — could reconstruct the whole conspiracy from those pages. Everyone else would see a bored captain's scribbling.

Then she built a small fire in the archive brazier, using the regulations that required the burning of expired requisitions as cover, and fed the original letters into it one at a time.

Vesperin's elegant script curled and blackened. *Your usefulness will be remembered.* The paper flared green for half a heartbeat — fae-touched paper, of course, even the treason was imported — and then it was ash, and the ash was swept, and the ash went into the stove that heated the officers' bathwater, which Reyes felt was a fitting final posting.

She watched the last of it go and felt — nothing? No. Not nothing. She felt the specific lightness of a woman who has amputated something infected and knows the fever will break, eventually, if she lives long enough. Loyalty, she decided, was not obedience. Loyalty was wanting the thing you loved to actually *be* what you loved. The Wardens were supposed to hold the line. Vane had handed the line over piece by piece for a chair closer to the fire. You did not stay loyal to the rot. You cut it out and kept the body alive.

"Burned," she reported to the empty archive, feeling obscurely that it deserved a report. It was, after all, the only colleague who had never lied to her.

But the copy sat warm against her ribs, sewn into the lining of her coat, and it weighed exactly as much as a career, a friendship, and possibly a war.

* * *

She was crossing the courtyard at dawn, gray-eyed and grim and hungry enough to consider the fort's porridge a viable foodstuff, when the horns sounded from the gatehouse.

A courier, Crown-liveried, mud to the thighs, waving a sealed scroll. Reyes watched the duty officers gather, watched the seal break, watched the duty officer's face do the thing faces do when orders arrive that are worse than anyone guessed.

She drifted close enough to hear, the way senior captains can drift close to anything without appearing to move.

The decree was read out for the garrison: the council had confirmed the rite of resealing. A blood sacrifice would be conducted at the First Garden upon the convergence of the stones. The victim's identity, per the divinations performed on the recovered grimoire's residue — residue Odile had left behind, Reyes realized, residue from *her own hands* — had been determined beyond appeal.

"The designated vessel," the duty officer read, in the flat voice regulation required, "is the runaway apprentice Odile Villalobos, currently sought throughout the Marches. All wardens are commanded—"

Reyes stopped listening. She was doing arithmetic again, faster now, and the sum came out the same every time.

Vane had written the decree. Vane had chosen the victim. Because a dead apprentice could testify about nothing, and a dead apprentice's blood on the new seal would bind the breach just tightly enough to buy him the credit of salvation — while Vesperin, holding the true rites Vane had sold him, kept the key to opening it again whenever it suited them both.

They weren't rivals. They were partners running a long con on two worlds, and the price of the con was a girl who saved children from plague-fog and apologized for clever oaths.

Reyes turned on her heel and walked toward the stables, already composing in her head the most insubordinate patrol route of her career. The archive had taught her the lesson thoroughly: report nothing, destroy nothing you might need, trust the goat farmer above the council.

Behind her, the horns kept sounding. Ahead, the road ran south toward the mountains, toward a frozen garden, toward a girl who had no idea she was now the most valuable corpse in the realm.

"Well," Reyes said to her horse, saddling it with brisk, furious efficiency. "You always said I'd end up a criminal. Congratulations to us both."

The horse, wise creature, said nothing — but it didn't resist the saddle either, which in Reyes's experience was as close to an endorsement as the world offered.
