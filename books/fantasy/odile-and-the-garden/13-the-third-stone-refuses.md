## 13. The Third Stone Refuses

The mountain shrine looked, at first glance, like a pile of very old rocks having an argument.

Odile stood in the wind at the top of the Pilgrim's Stair — three hundred steps cut into the shoulder of Mount Vellum, each one carved with a word of the old covenant worn nearly smooth by centuries of boots and weather — and tried not to feel disappointed. She had imagined something grander for the third oath-stone. Columns, maybe. Or at least a roof.

What she got was a ring of leaning monoliths on a bald summit, a stone basin full of last week's snowmelt, and a wind that seemed personally offended by travelers.

"Well," said Reyes, hauling herself up the final step with the expression of a woman who had been promised a desk job and had instead been given mountains, "I've been to worse shrines."

"You have not."

"I served two years in Marsh Hollow. There was a shrine there made out of a shipwreck and somebody's optimism." She dropped her pack against a monolith and rolled her shoulders until something cracked. "This one has better views, at least."

It did. The world fell away below them in folds of pine and grey stone, the road they'd climbed snaking back down toward the valley where Cindershade's smoke still smudged the horizon. Far to the north, if you squinted past the ache in your eyes, you could see where the border wards should have been — a seam of pale light that wasn't a seam anymore, just sky.

Odile didn't squint. She'd been avoiding looking north for three days, and she was getting quite good at it.

"Third one," she said instead, digging the grimoire out of her pack. "Two down." The book felt heavier than it used to. Not in the hand — in some other way she couldn't name, as though it were slowly filling up with everything it took from her.

Her mother's voice lived in those pages now, somewhere between the index of binding-sigils and the treatise on frost-wights. She'd stopped opening it to check. Checking hurt, and finding nothing hurt worse.

Reyes watched her handle it with that careful look she'd been wearing since the drowned chapel crypt — the look of a woman doing arithmetic about someone else's soul and not liking the totals. "You don't have to do this one today," she said. "We could make camp. Rest. Climb up tomorrow when your hands aren't shaking."

"My hands aren't shaking."

"Odile."

"They're *vibrating*. It's different. It's from the cold." She knelt at the basin and set the grimoire on its rim, where her mistress's marginalia said it belonged: *third stone, high place, offer what gladdens you.* The handwriting had been brisk and certain, the way all of Magistra Corvane's writing was, as though the old woman had never once doubted anything, including, apparently, how easy this would be.

Odile pressed her palm to the stone.

For a long moment, nothing. The wind moved through the standing rocks and made a low sound, almost conversational, like old men disagreeing politely. Then the stone beneath her hand warmed — once, faintly, hopefully — and went cold again.

It pushed her away.

Not violently. It was more like knocking on a door and hearing footsteps approach, pause, and quietly walk off again. The rejection was so polite it took Odile a moment to understand she'd been refused at all.

"That's new," she said.

"What happened?"

"It didn't take it. It just—" she pressed her hand flat again, harder, as if the first attempt had been a misunderstanding. "Come on. You know why I'm here. I'm the heir. It says so in the—"

The stone went cold as riverbed ice. Somewhere behind her left ribs, she felt a sensation like a disapproving tut.

"Oh, don't you *tut* at me," Odile told it. "You're a rock."

"A rock that's rejecting you," Reyes observed, settling onto a fallen monolith with the air of someone preparing to enjoy theater. "Interesting choice of venue for a tantrum."

"I am not having a tantrum. I am conducting a negotiation." Odile pulled the grimoire into her lap and started flipping pages with cold-stiff fingers. "There has to be something about refusal. Magistra wrote about everything else. She wrote four pages on the correct way to apologize to a beehive. Four pages, Reyes. There is no way she skipped 'what to do when the magic doesn't want you.'"

She found it eleven pages deep, tucked between a recipe for moon-tincture and an increasingly unhinged argument with a previous owner of the book about whether starlight counted as a liquid.

*On refusal,* the margin read, in ink gone brown with age:

*The stones are not locks. They are judges. A guarded heart offers a guarded gift, and the stone knows the difference the way a dog knows a stranger's smile. If you are refused, ask yourself what you are still holding back. Then put it down. All of it. The covenant was not sworn with clenched fists.*

Below that, smaller, squeezed into the corner as though written late at night:

*It will know. It always knows. Stop pretending otherwise, Odile.*

Odile stared at her own name in her dead mistress's handwriting for longer than she meant to.

"She wrote that before any of this," Reyes said quietly. Not a question.

"Years before. Maybe she always knew I'd end up here, hoarding things." Odile closed the book. "I'm not hoarding."

"No," Reyes agreed mildly. "You're vibrating."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the largest monolith, which leaned like a drunk uncle and sheltered them from the worst of the wind. Reyes built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to boil tea, because Captain Ilsa Reyes held exactly three truths self-evident, and one of them was that no catastrophe was so great it could not be faced marginally better with tea.

Odile sat with her knees drawn up and turned the problem over while the light died.

What was she holding back?

It wasn't a fair question. She'd already given up her mother's voice — given it up gladly, or mostly gladly, or at least without hesitating so long that anyone could call it reluctance. She'd confessed her theft to the second stone, hadn't she? Stood in that crypt and admitted aloud, to Reyes, of all people, that she'd stolen the grimoire from a dying woman's bedside—

No. That wasn't true, and she knew it wasn't true, and the stone apparently knew it too.

At the mountain shrine, the third stone had asked for honesty, and she had given it a performance of honesty. She had confessed the fact of the theft. She had not confessed the theft itself — the whole of it, the shape of it, the part that still sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

Because here was the truth under the truth: she hadn't taken the grimoire after Magistra Corvane died. She'd taken it *before*. Three nights before. While the old woman slept. And the old woman had woken — Odile had heard the bed creak, had frozen in the doorway with the book clutched to her chest like a child caught with bread — and Magistra had looked at her across the dark room, and had said nothing.

Nothing. She had simply turned her face back to the wall and let her apprentice walk out of the cottage and into the night with thirty years of her life's work.

And Odile had spent every day since telling herself that silence was permission. That the old woman had been too tired to fight. That surely, *surely*, she'd understood.

But she didn't know. She would never know. Because Odile had run rather than ask, and now asking was impossible, and the not-knowing had grown teeth.

That was what the stone wanted. Not the confession of a crime — the confession of a cowardice.

"Tea," Reyes said, pressing a tin cup into her hands.

"Thank you."

"You've got your thinking face on. The one where you look like you're losing an argument with yourself."

"I'm always losing an argument with myself. I've just learned to do it quietly."

"Mm." Reyes drank her own tea and watched the fire. In the firelight her face lost its captain's edges and became something younger, wearier, more honest. Since the gorge — since the bargain Odile had sworn with the whispering dark under the rope bridge, the debt unnamed and therefore unbounded — Reyes had stopped pretending she wasn't afraid for her. She did it in her own way: extra rations slipped into Odile's pack, watch rotations arranged so Odile always slept first, a hand hovering near her elbow at cliff edges without ever quite touching.

It should have made it easier, being cared for. Instead it made the coal in her chest burn hotter, because Reyes trusted her with a straightness that Odile had never managed to earn honestly. Reyes thought she knew who Odile Villalobos was. She knew a story Odile had edited.

"The stone wants something," Odile said at last.

"They generally do."

"Not a memory. Not yet. It wants me to say something first, and I don't—" Her throat closed. She took a sip of tea to disguise it, badly. "I don't want to say it."

Reyes set down her cup. "To me?"

"To anyone. But the stone's watching, so. Yes. To you, probably." Odile laughed, short and unhappy. "Do you know what the worst part is? The first stone wouldn't even touch me. The second one took my mother's voice, and that was terrible but it was — clean. It cost something real. This one won't take anything until I give it something that isn't mine to keep anyway. Isn't that obscene? It's charging me for my own honesty."

"Honesty usually costs," Reyes said. "That's rather the point of it. The free kind isn't worth the breath."

"When did you get wise?"

"I'm not wise. I'm forty-one and tired. It looks similar from a distance."

Odile hugged the cup to her chest and watched the stars come out over the valley — the real stars, the lit ones, though every night she counted them out of habit, checking for gaps, for falling. Two months ago the whole sky had gone dark at once, and she still flinched sometimes at open horizons.

"Magistra Corvane taught me everything," she said. It came out steadier than expected. Encouraged, she kept going. "Not just craft. How to read a contract before signing. How to salt a slug. How to lie to tax collectors — although she called it 'fiscal narrative.' She was the only person who ever chose me. My parents sold me to her as an indentured apprentice when I was nine, and everyone else in my life has treated me as either property or a problem, and she treated me as a *mind*. Do you know what that's worth to a child?"

"No," Reyes said simply. "I had the opposite. Too many people believed in me. It's its own prison." She paused. "Tell me the rest. The part you're circling."

And there it was. No mercy in it, and no cruelty either — just the captain's voice, the one that got confessions out of smugglers and truths out of subordinates. Odile could have hated her for it. She found she was grateful instead, because left to her own devices she would have circled until dawn.

"I stole the grimoire three days before she died," Odile said. "Not after. Before. She was sick — we both knew it was her heart, and we both pretended it wasn't, the way you do. And I told myself I was saving the book. Protecting her legacy from the wardens who were coming to confiscate it. And maybe some of that was even true. But I waited until she was asleep, and I took it, and she woke up, and she *saw me*, Reyes. She saw me standing there with it in my arms, and she didn't say a word. She just turned over. And I ran." The words were coming faster now, twenty years of them queued up behind the first. "I have spent every day since deciding that her silence meant forgiveness, because the alternative was deciding it meant disappointment, and I couldn't carry that and the book and the road. So I decided for her. I took her death too. I took the one thing she had left to give me — a chance to explain, to ask, to be forgiven or not forgiven like a person instead of a thief — and I stole that along with everything else. That's what the stone wants. That's the hoard. I've been sitting on her last words to me for a year because they might be nothing, and nothing is unbearable, so I made them into permission instead. I lied to myself with her mouth."

The wind moved through the monoliths. For once it sounded less like argument and more like listening.

Reyes said nothing for a while. When she spoke, her voice was rougher than usual. "My sister ran away beyond the border when we were girls," she said. "Everyone said she was stolen. Taken by the Fae. And I built my whole life on finding her — joined the wardens, learned the old rites, all of it. And the truth I've never said out loud is that she wasn't taken. She *chose* to go, and I helped her pack, and then I told everyone including myself a kinder story, because the kinder story let me be the rescuer instead of the accomplice." She looked into the fire. "So no. I don't think you're a thief. I think you're a person who couldn't bear the weight of a true thing, which makes you approximately human."

Odile discovered she was crying, which was undignified, and that Reyes had passed her a handkerchief without comment, which somehow made it worse and better at once.

"Thank you," she managed.

"Don't thank me yet. You still have to convince a rock."

* * *

Dawn came up thin and gold, and Odile climbed to the basin with frost in her hair and the grimoire under her arm, and she knelt, and she said it again.

All of it. Not the edited version, not the confession-shaped object, but the whole shabby, frightened truth: the waiting, the sleeping cottage, the bed creaking, the doorway, the silence, the running, the year of lies dressed as decisions. She said it aloud to the morning and to the stone and to Reyes, who stood a few paces off with her arms folded against the cold and her eyes bright, and when she finished, the wind had stopped entirely, which on a mountaintop is its own kind of miracle.

Then the stone warmed beneath her hands.

It was not gentle. The second stone had drawn its price like a splinter; this one reached in openly, the way a friend takes something from your fist when you've finally unclenched. And Odile felt her happiest day lift out of her — felt it go — and understood, in the last instant before it left, exactly which day it was choosing.

Not the day she'd passed her apprenticeship trials. Not the night of the harvest festival when Magistra had bought her honey-cakes and called her, in front of the whole village square, *my heir.*

It was an ordinary afternoon, years ago. Rain on the cottage roof. Magistra dozing in her chair with a catalogue of rare reagents open on her lap, spectacles slipping down her nose. And Odile, fifteen and bored and fond past all sense, practicing transfiguration on teacups just to see the old woman wake, sputter, and accuse the cat.

That was all. Rain, and a sleeping teacher, and teacups, and love so ordinary it had never seemed worth marking.

She felt it go the way you feel a river take a boat: smoothly, inevitably, with a kind of tenderness. And then it was somewhere in the pages of the grimoire, between the sigils and the frost-wights, and she knew it had existed only because Reyes had told her so a moment later — *"You were smiling. What did it take?"* — and she had answered, honestly, *"I don't remember."*

The stone cooled. Above the shrine, for one heartbeat, every star still visible in the morning sky flickered — a wink, a pulse, a ledger entry acknowledged. On the highest monolith, a word carved ten centuries ago blazed briefly into legibility:

*HONESTY IS THE FIRST KEY.*

Then it faded, and the wind returned, and Odile sat down hard on the frozen ground and laughed until it turned into something else, and Reyes sat beside her and let her, and neither of them said anything foolish or comforting, because there wasn't anything to say that the morning hadn't already heard.

After a while, Odile wiped her face and opened the grimoire to find the next page of marginalia.

She found it. She read it twice.

"Reyes," she said slowly. "How do you feel about bridges?"

Reyes followed her finger to the map inked in the margin, and to the route it traced eastward, down out of the mountains, along the spine of the Gorge of Whispers — and across it, by the only crossing for forty miles in either direction. A bridge. A rope bridge. Drawn in her mistress's hand, with a note beside it that Odile read aloud before she could stop herself:

*"The fourth stone waits past the gorge. Cross quickly. Cross quietly. And whatever you hear it whisper to you — do not answer."*

Far below them, at the bottom of a valley they couldn't yet see, something whispered.
