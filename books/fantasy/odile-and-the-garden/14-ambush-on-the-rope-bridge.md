## 14. Ambush on the Rope Bridge

The Gorge of Whispers announced itself long before they saw it — a low, sighing sound rising out of the earth, like the mountain itself muttering complaints about the weather.

"It's not wind," Odile said, for the third time in an hour. "The whispers are actual voices. The spirits that live down there. My mistress wrote about them. They collect words the way magpies collect buttons."

"Charming," Reyes said. She had been in a foul mood since breakfast, which was to say since Odile had eaten the last of the dried plums without asking. "And do these button-collecting spirits have any interest in eating wardens?"

"They mostly want interesting things *said* near them. Words they haven't heard before."

"Then you're safe. You've said the same thing about the plums four times now."

"That's slander. I've said it five times at most."

Reyes almost smiled. It was a small victory, and Odile hoarded it. Three days of walking side by side had done something she didn't have a name for yet: it had made the space between them comfortable. Reyes no longer walked with her hand on her sword hilt when Odile hummed. Odile no longer flinched when Reyes barked an order at her. They had developed, without discussing it, a rhythm — Reyes took first watch because she slept like a cat anyway; Odile cooked because Reyes's cooking was, by her own admission, "a war crime I refuse to be prosecuted for."

The gorge opened before them late in the afternoon: a black cleft in the mountainside, three hundred feet deep, its walls streaked with ice and older shadows. Spanning it was a rope bridge — planks gone silver with age, cables furred with frost, swaying gently as though it were breathing.

"I hate bridges," Reyes announced.

"You hate everything."

"Bridges especially. A bridge is a place where the ground has given up on you." She tested the nearest cable, tugged it twice, then stepped onto the first plank with the grim deliberation of a woman walking to her own execution. "Stay close. Weight in the middle of each board. Don't look down."

"Why?"

"Because if you look down, the gorge looks back, and I've heard enough whispers for one lifetime."

Odile looked down immediately. Far below, something pale moved between the rocks — not water, not mist, exactly, but a slow silver stirring, like milk poured into dark tea. The whispering was louder here. Individual words surfaced now and then, half-heard: *—remember when—* and *—she never came back—* and once, disconcertingly, *—plums—*.

"The gorge is mocking me," Odile said.

"The gorge has good taste." Reyes was ten planks ahead, moving steadily. "Come on. Before dark. The light's already going."

That was when Odile felt it — a wrongness in her teeth, the same pressure she'd felt the night the stars went out, the night the wards shattered. She stopped.

"Reyes."

"I felt it too." The warden had gone still, one hand flat against the cable. Her voice had dropped into the register Odile privately thought of as *trouble voice*. "Behind us. In the trees. More than two. Moving wrong — like they don't care about being quiet."

Odile turned. Along the rim of the gorge, among the wind-bent pines, shapes were detaching themselves from the shadows. They were man-shaped the way driftwood is boat-shaped — suggestive rather than actual. Their limbs were too long, their heads tilted at angles that made Odile's neck ache in sympathy, and where faces should have been there was only smooth grey skin, featureless as river stones. Ash-things. Vesperin's creatures. She'd seen one from a distance outside Cindershade and had spent two nights dreaming about it.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Six visible. Probably more behind the ridge line." Reyes drew her sword. Steel sang in the cold air, and every ash-thing on the ridgeline turned its blank head toward the sound at once, like flowers following a terrible sun. "They want us on the bridge, don't they."

"What makes you say that?"

"Because they're not charging. Because they're *herding*." Reyes backed another step along the planks. Behind them — ahead of them now, in the direction they needed to go — more grey shapes rose silently from the far side of the gorge. "Cut off both ends. Force us into the middle. This is a trap with excellent manners."

An ash-thing on the near rim took one lurching step forward, and the pine it brushed simply died — needles going grey and crumbling, wood bleaching white as bone. Plague-touch. Odile's mouth went dry. These weren't scavengers. These were the things that carried the forgetting.

"Run," she said.

"On my mark," Reyes said, "run."

*

They ran.

The bridge did not appreciate it. Every footfall sent waves rolling down its length, the planks bucking like a living thing, the cables groaning in a language of complaint that grew louder with each stride. Frost flew up around their boots. Somewhere below, the whispers rose to meet them, delighted — *oh, oh, listen, new words, new words* — and Odile had the mad thought that the gorge-spirits were enjoying this immensely.

Halfway across, the first arrow came.

It wasn't an arrow, quite — it was a sliver of grey wood trailing ash-smoke, and it hissed past Odile's ear close enough to sting. She looked back over her shoulder and wished she hadn't. The ash-things were on the bridge behind them, moving with a horrible flowing gait, their long fingers hooked into the cables, and the planks beneath their feet were turning white and brittle as they passed.

"They're rotting the bridge!" Odile shouted.

"I noticed!" Reyes had reached the far quarter of the span. Ahead of her, three ash-things had gathered at the anchor point, silent as furniture, waiting. "Change of plan! We're not running anymore!"

"What's the new plan?"

"Working on it!"

A second volley of ash-slivers arced from the near rim. One struck the cable above Reyes's head. Where it touched, the rope *died* — fibers greying, stiffening, and then, with a sound like an old man clearing his throat, snapping. The whole bridge lurched sideways. Odile screamed and grabbed the other cable with both arms. The world swung — sky, gorge, sky again — and when it steadied, Reyes was hanging.

Not fallen. Not yet. But the section of bridge under her had collapsed where the plague-sliver had bitten through three planks at once, and she hung by her hands from the severed edge, boots kicking over three hundred feet of hungry dark. Her sword was gone, clattering away into the depths. The whispers swallowed it with what sounded distinctly like applause.

"*Reyes!*"

"I'm fine!" Her voice was tight, controlled, a lie told very well. "Don't come closer — the planks near the break are rotten — Odile, *don't*—"

But Odile was already crawling forward, flat on her stomach, distributing her weight the way her mistress had taught her to cross thin ice. Ten feet from the gap, she stopped and peered over the edge. Below Reyes's dangling boots, the silver mist was rising. Gathering. Shaping itself, slowly, into something with too many eyes.

"Odile." Reyes's knuckles were white. Her face, for the first time since Odile had known her, showed fear without decoration. "Listen to me. If I fall, take the grimoire and go. That's an order. I'm still technically a warden, and you're still technically a criminal, so legally you have to obey me."

"Legally I'm supposed to arrest myself, so I'll take my chances."

"Odile—"

"There are things down there." Odile's voice cracked. "In the mist. They're looking at you."

"I know. I can feel them *shopping*."

Below, the mist-shapes pressed closer, pale faces forming and dissolving, and the whispers sharpened into something almost intelligible: *a falling thing, a falling thing, ours if it falls, ours if it falls.* The gorge-spirits wanted her. And they were patient. Patience was easy, three hundred feet down, where nothing ever changed except the words.

Reyes's grip slipped an inch. She caught it. Slipped again.

There was no time to think, which was fortunate, because thinking would have told Odile this was a terrible idea.

She pulled the grimoire from her satchel and held it against the frozen cable, and she spoke the way her mistress had taught her to speak to old things — clearly, formally, and with the absolute certainty that she would regret it later.

"Spirits of the Gorge of Whispers! Hear me! I am Odile Villalobos, apprentice of Marisol the Grey, heir of the garden-covenant, and I ask a boon!"

The whispers stopped. All of them. The silence was enormous, total, the silence of several thousand years' worth of collected words all holding their breath at once.

"*Speak,*" said the gorge, in a hundred voices layered over each other, some ancient, some childlike, one of them unmistakably repeating the word *plums.*

"A life hangs in your keeping. Catch her. Hold her. Deliver her to the stone shore below the eastern anchorage, unharmed, unhurt, alive." Odile swallowed. "And I will owe you a debt. Unnamed. Unbounded. To be claimed when you choose, paid in full."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in it, Odile felt the weight of attention — vast, curious, amused — turning over her words like a jeweler examining a stone for flaws.

"*A debt unnamed,*" the gorge murmured. "*From the keeper's heir. From the girl who smells of growing things and stolen books.*"

"Yes."

"*We could simply wait,*" the spirits said, with terrible gentleness, "*and she would be ours anyway.*"

"And you'd only get her body." Odile leaned closer to the cable, her breath fogging the frost. "But catch her, and you get a *debt*. From someone who owes the garden. Someone who will walk your length again someday, carrying things you cannot imagine, saying words you have never heard. Think of the collection. Think of the *buttons*."

A pause. Then, rippling up through the dark, unmistakable delight:

"*Ohhh.*"

The mist exploded upward.

It rose around Reyes like a hand made of winter — coiling under her arms, her knees, catching her as her fingers finally tore free of the broken planks. For one heart-stopping moment she was suspended above the abyss, held in a column of silver, her face a portrait of a woman rapidly renegotiating her relationship with reality. Then the column lowered her, smoothly, gently, down through the dark to the stony shelf beside the eastern anchor, and set her down as carefully as a mother laying down a sleeping child.

The whispers roared — joyous, greedy, triumphant. *Ours! Ours! The debt is ours! Marked and kept and ours!* And somewhere in the roar, Odile heard her own name spoken back to her in her own voice, filed away forever, a button for the collection.

Above her, the ash-things shrieked — a dry, furious sound like paper burning — and recoiled from the gorge's edge as though scalded. Whatever bargain the gorge-spirits had just sealed, the creatures of the Court of Ash wanted no part of it. They flowed backward into the treeline and were gone, leaving dead pines in their wake.

*

Reyes climbed back up the eastern anchorage on shaking legs, refused help twice, accepted it the third time, and stood at last on solid rock with her hands on her knees, breathing like a bellows.

"Okay," she managed. "Okay. New policy. No more bridges."

"You're welcome."

"I ordered you to let me fall."

"You ordered me to give Vesperin the grimoire, effectively. I ignore bad orders. It's a habit I picked up from watching you."

Reyes straightened. She looked at Odile for a long moment, and something passed across her face — gratitude, yes, but underneath it something rawer, the expression of a woman who had spent her whole career believing that no one would catch her if she fell, and had just been proven catastrophically wrong.

"You swore them a debt," she said quietly. "Unnamed. Unbounded. Do you understand what you just did? Spirits like that — debts like that — they don't expire. They don't forgive. They *wait*. You've hanged a lantern over your own future, Odile. One day, out of a clear sky, they're going to call it in, and you won't get to pick the hour or the price."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I watched you smile while you did it."

"I know what I did." Odile hugged the grimoire to her chest and looked out over the gorge, where the silver mist was settling back down, contented, murmuring to itself in its sleep. "My mistress used to say that magic is just love with consequences. Everything worth doing costs something. At least this time—" her voice wobbled, steadied, "—at least this time I got to choose what it cost, and it wasn't *you*."

Reyes said nothing for a while. Then, gruffly, in the tone of a woman constructing a wall out of professionalism: "Your mistress sounds like she talked a great deal of nonsense."

"She talked *exactly* the right amount of nonsense."

"Mm." Reyes shouldered her pack — minus one sword, which the gorge now owned, and which Odile suspected the spirits would display prominently, like a trophy. "Camp on the far side. Double watch. And Odile—"

"Yes?"

"When we get to the fourth stone, whatever it asks for — you tell me first. Before you pay anything. That's not an order. That's a request." Her jaw tightened. "I've lost enough people to places like this. I'm not losing you to a *bargain*."

Something in Odile's chest went warm and frightened at once. She nodded.

They walked on, up out of the gorge's whispering shadow, into the last red light of evening. Behind them, faint and satisfied, the spirits of the Gorge of Whispers began reciting their newest acquisition, over and over, in a voice that was Odile's own:

*A debt unnamed. A debt unnamed. Kept and marked and ours.*

And far to the north, where the fallen stars burned in their ash-choked court, something ancient lifted its head — because bargains, like screams, carry.
