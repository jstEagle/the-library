## 15. The Mistress's Last Letter

The rope bridge was behind them, the gorge-spirits were fed, and Odile had not slept in two days.

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang of gray stone, where the wind couldn't reach them and the fire could be kept small. Reyes took first watch without being asked, which had become her way — she did things without being asked, and then pretended she hadn't done them at all, so that no one would have to thank her and make it strange.

Odile sat with the grimoire in her lap.

It was the first time in weeks she had simply *held* it, rather than clutched it, or hidden it, or flung it open in desperation to some page of hedge-magic while something with too many teeth came over a hill. She turned it slowly under her fingers, feeling the leather binding, cracked and soft as old bread. Her mistress — Magistra Ellery Voss, terror of three generations of apprentices, woman who had once made Odile scrub the cottage floor with rosemary water because "magic lives in clean corners" — had carried this book for forty years. The spine was worn smooth exactly where a thumb would rest.

*She held it like this,* Odile thought. *Every evening. At the table by the window.*

And then, because grief is a strange animal that bites hardest when you are finally still enough for it to find you, she began to cry.

Not elegantly. Not the way heroines cried in ballads, one luminous tear sliding down a composed cheek. She cried the way she had cried at fourteen, when the magistra had made her bury her first failed charm in the garden and stand over the little mound until she understood what waste meant. She cried with her whole body, hunched over the book, hiccupping, her nose running shamelessly onto the leather.

"Odile." Reyes's voice, low, from the edge of the firelight. Not alarmed. Just present, the way she'd been on the bridge, falling, trusting a stranger's bargain to catch her.

"I'm fine," Odile said, which fooled neither of them.

"You're crying into a priceless artifact of pre-covenant magic."

"It's waterproof," Odile sniffled. "Mistress Voss charmed it against spills after I knocked an entire teapot onto it in my second year. She said if I was going to be a disaster, the library should at least be prepared."

Something that might have been a smile crossed Reyes's face and was immediately executed for treason. She returned to her watch.

Odile wiped her face on her sleeve and went back to the book. And that was when she felt it.

A thickness in the binding. A wrongness, or rather a *rightness* — a small hard shape sewn into the leather along the spine, where the thumb-worn place was. She had handled this book a thousand times. She had *repaired* this binding once, two winters ago, when a hinge cracked, and she had never noticed anything.

Which meant it hadn't been there two winters ago. Which meant her mistress had put it there *after*, deliberately, warded so thoroughly that even an apprentice with her hands inside the stitching had sensed nothing.

"Mistress," Odile whispered. "You secretive, impossible old woman."

She worked her thumbnail along the seam. The leather parted like a held breath released. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle with age that wasn't age — the letter inside was fresh paper, unyellowed, as though it had been written yesterday, because in every way that mattered it had been — was a single folded page sealed with plain wax and pressed with a thumbprint instead of a sigil.

Her mistress's thumbprint. She'd know it anywhere. It had inked a hundred punishment ledgers.

Odile broke the seal with hands that would not quite stay steady.

---

*My Odile,*

*If you are reading this, then you found the binding, which means you finally stopped treating books as things to be read and started treating them as things to be listened to. Good. Late, but good. You always were late; you'll be late to your own legend, mark me.*

*I am going to say difficult things now, and I will only say them once, because I am dead and death excuses a person from repetition.*

*You believe you stole this grimoire. You have been carrying that theft like a stone in your chest, and I know it, because you carry guilt the way other people carry umbrellas — everywhere, uselessly, in perfect weather. So hear me: you stole nothing. I left the cottage door unlocked. I left the ward-phrase written where you would find it. I left my strongbox open and my will unsigned precisely so that the Crown's lawyers would have grounds to call you a thief, because a thief runs, and an heir stays, and what waits for an heir in that cottage is a warden's cell and a sacrifice altar.*

*I needed you to run. Forgive me the trick. It was the last lesson, and like all my best lessons, it was cruel and necessary and you hated every minute of it.*

*The wards are failing. Do not ask how I knew; I knew the way a bone knows rain. The covenant weaves five oath-stones into one garden, and the garden has been dying since the day it was planted, because a covenant is not a wall, child, it is a promise, and promises must be tended by someone who remembers both sides of them. There are fewer of us every year. Soon there will be only you.*

*That is why I chose you. Not because you were the cleverest apprentice — sweet stars, no, that was Maren, and look how Maren turned out, selling ward-rituals to men with ash in their pockets. Not because you were obedient. You were never obedient a day in your life; it was your finest quality. I chose you because when you were nine years old and the star-shard fever came through the valley, you sat up all night with the miller's boy, whom you did not like, holding his hand and telling him stories about a fox who collected names, and in the morning he remembered his mother's face because of you.*

*Memory is the covenant's coin. You already knew how to spend it before you ever knew the price. That is the whole of the craft, Odile. Everything else is handwriting.*

*Now the hard part, and I will not soften it, because softening things is how this mess began.*

*The stones will take from you. First small things, then large ones. Your mother's voice. Your happiest day. Perhaps, at the end, everything — your memories of me, your own name, the girl who ran down the mountain with a stolen book and a heart full of fire. If it comes to that, do not fight it. The covenant does not want your suffering; it wants your truth, freely given. Hoard nothing. Clutch nothing. A closed hand can hold a stone but cannot set it.*

*And forgive me — oh, forgive me — for knowing this when I took you in. For choosing you anyway. For loving you anyway, which is the same crime committed twice.*

*You will be angry with me. Be angry. Anger is just love with its sleeves rolled up; I learned that from you, the week you found out I'd been feeding your letters to the stove to keep the chimney drawing.*

*(I still have them. They are behind the loose brick by the hearth. Every one. Even the terrible poems. Especially the terrible poems.)*

*One more thing. When the last stone takes the last of you, someone must carry your story out of the garden, or the price will have bought a silence instead of a spring. I do not know who that will be. But I know the kind of person it must be: stubborn, lawful to a fault, and capable of standing watch over a sleeping stranger without waking her.*

*If such a person is sitting across your campfire right now, tell her I said thank you. Tell her I chose you both, though only one of us signed the papers.*

*Go well, my Odile. Tend the corners. Magic lives in clean ones.*

*— E.V.*

---

The letter ended. Odile discovered she had read the last line four times without absorbing any of it, because her eyes had stopped working somewhere around *forgive me for loving you anyway.*

She read it again. Then a fifth time. Then she folded it along its creases with exaggerated care, tucked it back into its oilcloth, and burst into tears for the second time in one evening, which she believed might be some kind of personal record.

"Reyes," she said, when she could speak.

The warden came over at once. She didn't ask; she crouched down across the fire, and her eyes went to the letter, and Odile watched her read the last paragraph — the paragraph about a stubborn, lawful person — and saw the exact moment Reyes understood, because the woman went very still, the way she did when something struck too close to armor.

"She knew about me?" Reyes said quietly.

"She knew about *everything*." Odile laughed, wet and disbelieving. "She knew the wards would fall. She knew I'd run. She knew the stones would take my memories — she *warned* me, years of warnings dressed up as lessons, 'a closed hand,' 'the cost of tending' — and I thought she was being cryptic because she enjoyed it. She *enjoyed* it, but that wasn't why. She was—" Odile's voice cracked. "She was leaving me a map. The whole time. Every scolding was a map."

"And the theft?"

"The door was unlocked, Reyes. The ward-phrase was on the mantel. She *made* me a thief so I'd run instead of staying to be arrested." Odile pressed the letter to her forehead. "I spent six weeks hating myself for betraying her, and it turns out the betrayal was the inheritance. She forged my name on the deed with my own guilty feet."

For a long moment Reyes said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, past the gray ceiling of cloud, the dead stars hung in their wrongness.

Then Reyes reached out — awkwardly, the gesture of a woman who had been trained to restrain people and had very little practice comforting them — and rested her hand briefly on top of Odile's head, like one might pat a dog, or crown a very small queen.

"She sounds like she was a formidable woman," Reyes said.

"She once fined herself two silver pieces for tracking mud into her own kitchen. She paid it to a jar labeled JUSTICE and used it to buy sweets for children she disliked." Odile wiped her eyes. "I'm going to miss her so much it doesn't fit inside me."

"You won't," Reyes said, and Odile looked up sharply — but Reyes's face was gentle, gentler than Odile had ever seen it. "If the stones take her from you. You won't miss her, because missing requires remembering. But—" she nodded at the letter, at the oilcloth, at the binding. "Paper remembers. People remember. I remember. Whatever they take, it exists outside you now. I heard her name tonight. Bramble's told me stories. That's more than silence."

Odile stared at her.

"That," she said finally, "is the kindest thing anyone has said to me since this started, and you delivered it like a tax notice."

"I'm a warden. We comfort through documentation." Reyes stood, brushing off her knees. "Sleep. We reach the shrine pass tomorrow, and whatever the fourth stone wants, you'll want your wits about you."

She returned to her watch. Odile lay back against her pack, the letter over her heart, and did not sleep — not from fear, this time, but from the strange fullness of being *known*. Her mistress had known. Had planned. Had loved her in the most infuriating, elaborate, roundabout way imaginable, and had asked forgiveness in advance for a wound she herself would never live to see given.

*Be angry,* the letter said. *Anger is just love with its sleeves rolled up.*

"Well," Odile whispered to the dark, to the dead stars, to the old woman behind the loose brick with her hoard of terrible poems. "Consider my sleeves rolled, old woman. And I forgive you. Mostly. The teapot thing I'm still deciding about."

Above the peaks, for half a heartbeat — so briefly that Odile would spend the rest of her life unsure whether she had imagined it — one dead star flickered.

As if something far away had heard her.

And had begun, very carefully, to answer.
