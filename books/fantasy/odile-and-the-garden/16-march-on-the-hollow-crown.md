## 16. March on the Hollow Crown

The road to Hollow Crown was paved with good intentions and horse droppings, and Ilsa Reyes had stepped in both.

She rode at the head of six wardens she had chosen herself — not for their loyalty to the Crown, which was negotiable, but for their loyalty to her, which she had spent eleven years earning one saved life at a time. Corporal Fenwick, who owed her his left arm's continued attachment. Sergeant Okafor, who had once watched Reyes talk a mob out of burning a tax collector and had never quite recovered his faith in easy answers. The others were younger, hungrier, and easier to fool, which was why she'd told them nothing yet.

In the saddlebag behind her knee, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a false layer of ration biscuits, sat a copy of everything.

Ledgers of payments from an account that did not officially exist. Correspondence signed with Commander Aldous Vane's private cipher. A manifest of ward-rituals sold north across the border in the years before it fell — sold, if her reading of the dates was right, *before* Vane could have known the wards would fail, which meant either he had known, or someone had told him, or the whole rotten architecture of the border's collapse had been less an accident than an appointment kept.

Reyes had burned the originals in the garrison stove at Thornfell, page by page, watching Vane's elegant handwriting curl and blacken. It had felt like justice. It had also felt like cowardice, so she had copied every word first, in her own blunt hand, twice over. One copy rode with her. The other she had sewn into the lining of her sister's old coat, which hung in her quarters where no one would ever steal it because no one wanted a coat that smelled of woodsmoke and grief.

"Captain," Okafor said, drawing alongside. "You've been smiling at your horse for a mile. Either you know something we don't, or the horse said something."

"The horse is a better conversationalist than most of the council," Reyes said. "And no, I don't know something. I'm hoping something. There's a difference."

"What are you hoping?"

"That the decree hasn't been issued yet." She kept her eyes on the road. "Vane needs the council's full sanction before he can name a sacrifice. Councils are slow. Councils argue about seating arrangements. If we ride hard, we arrive while they're still arguing."

"And if they're not arguing?"

"Then we arrive anyway," Reyes said, "and I make them argue."

Okafor studied her the way he studied weather coming over the mountains — professionally, without hope of changing it. "You know what happens to wardens who march on the capital without orders."

"They get promoted," Reyes said. "Or hanged. Sometimes both, in sequence. I've read the histories."

Fenwick snorted from behind them. "I'd follow you to the hanging, Captain. I'd just want it noted in the record that I objected first."

"Noted," Reyes said, and meant it as thanks, and knew he took it as such, because Fenwick had been reading her long enough to translate.

* * *

They made Hollow Crown on the fourth evening, when the city lit itself against the dark like a nervous animal showing its teeth. From the ridge above the river valley, the capital looked almost beautiful — ten thousand candles in ten thousand windows, the palace crowning the hill with its pale towers, the great lanterns of the Council Ring burning green-gold above the rooftops.

Reyes remembered being nineteen and seeing it for the first time, riding up this same road with her commission folded in her pocket and her heart full of the belief that the Crown was a thing you could serve cleanly, the way you could love a person cleanly. Her sister had been alive then. The border had still held. The stars over the Otherfell had still been stars.

"Green-gold," Okafor said beside her, squinting at the Council Ring. "That's the working color. They're in session."

"At this hour?"

"When there's a decree pending, they stay in session until it passes. Vane will want it sealed tonight, before anyone can sleep on it." Reyes turned her horse down toward the river gate. "We go in through the warden post, quiet, and I go to the Ring alone."

"You said we'd all object together."

"I said Fenwick would object. You'll be busy." She looked back at the five of them, wind-burned and saddle-sore and trusting her. "If I'm arrested — and there's a real chance I'm arrested — you take my copy of the ledger to Warden-Adjutant Serrano in the Eastreach garrison. Not to another captain. Not to the council. To Serrano, who has no ambition and therefore cannot be bought. Understood?"

A grim little silence went around the group.

"Understood," Okafor said at last. "Though for the record, Captain, none of us thinks you'll be arrested."

"For the record," Reyes said, nudging her horse forward, "that's because none of you has met Commander Vane."

* * *

The warden post inside the river gate smelled of tallow and wet wool, and the duty officer was a boy Reyes had once taught to swim in the Thornfell millpond, back when the world was simpler and so was she.

"Warden-Captain Reyes!" He came around the desk half-rising, delight and alarm fighting openly on his face. "Nobody told us you were coming. Is it official business? Should I log it? Because if it's official business I have to log it, and if I log it, Commander Vane's office sees the log within the hour—"

"Don't log it," Reyes said.

"But—"

"Tomas." She put both hands flat on his desk and leaned in, and watched the swimming lesson come back to him — the specific terror of a woman who had fished him out of deep water by his collar. "I need three things. A stable for my people. A message carried to the Council Ring under warden seal, addressed to me, marked urgent. And one honest answer: has the council passed any decree regarding the border breach? Anything sealed today?"

Tomas swallowed. "There's a decree pending. Everyone knows something's pending; the Ring's been lit for two days. But—" he dropped his voice, glancing at the empty corridor, "—there's a rumor going through the post. About the sacrifice. About who it's meant to be."

Reyes felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it did on the rope bridges over the Gorge of Whispers. "Say it."

"They say the council voted last night. Provisionally. That the blood has to be willing blood, freely given, of covenant descent — some legal language nobody understands. And they say Commander Vane already has the person. Already has her identified, already has her—" Tomas's voice cracked. "Captain, they say it's the runaway apprentice. The one from the border. Villalobos. The girl with the grimoire."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above them, the green-gold light of the Council Ring burned on, patient as a held breath.

"Covenant descent," Reyes repeated. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, flat and strange, like words spoken underwater. "Why would a hedge-witch's apprentice be of covenant descent?"

"That's the part nobody understands," Tomas whispered. "But Vane's office sent the description to every garrison two days ago. Not 'apprentice of interest' anymore, Captain. Just... her name. Her face. And instructions that she's to be taken unharmed. *Unharmed*, sir. Alive and untouched and delivered to the Ring." He hesitated. "You know her, don't you? The description — it says she travels with a warden. It says the warden is suspected of harboring her."

Reyes straightened slowly. So Vane had already seen the shape of what she was doing. Had already written her into the decree as an accessory, a complication, a loose thread to be cut after the girl was spent. Of course he had. Vane had always been three moves ahead; it was what made him excellent, and it was what had made him sellable.

"Log nothing," she said again. "Stable my people. And Tomas — when the message comes back from the Ring, bring it to me yourself. Don't let it touch another pair of hands."

"Yes, Captain."

She walked out into the capital streets with the decree ringing in her skull like a struck bell. *Odile.* Not some nameless criminal. Not some stranger whose death could be filed under regrettable necessity. A girl who fed plague-struck children honey and nettle tea with dirt under her fingernails and an insult for anyone who tried to help too slowly. A girl who had stood in Reyes's campfire light and offered a truce with the gravity of a queen and the manners of a pickpocket. A girl who was currently somewhere on the frozen slopes of the Thornfells, chasing stones that ate memories, believing — because Reyes had promised it — that the law was a thing that could be reasoned with.

The law, it turned out, had already reasoned. The law had reached its conclusion two nights ago, in a candlelit chamber, and its conclusion was Odile Villalobos's throat.

Reyes stopped in the mouth of an alley, pressed her palm against cold brick, and breathed until the shaking stopped. Then she thought, with terrible clarity:

*The First Garden.*

It was in the marginalia — she'd seen Odile tracing it, seen the way the girl's finger lingered on the old map like a pilgrim touching a shrine. Every stone led home. Every road, every riddle, every memory the covenant devoured pointed back to the garden beneath the ice where it had all begun. If Odile was following the mistress's path — and she was, stubbornly, faithfully, heartbreakingly — then she was walking toward the one place in the world where the decree would find her waiting.

And Vane knew it too. That was the worst of it. Vane hadn't chosen Odile despite the garden. He had chosen her *because* of it. Let the girl do the dangerous work of gathering the stones; let her carry them, bleeding memories, to the exact spot where a resealing was possible; and then let the Crown arrive at the finish line and collect its price. Vane wasn't hunting her. He was *herding* her. The whole decree was a shepherd's crook dressed in law.

"Captain?" Okafor had appeared at the alley mouth, silent as ever. "Your face is doing something I haven't seen since Cindershade."

"We're leaving," Reyes said. "Tonight. All of us."

"Leaving? We just got here. Isn't the plan to confront the council? Expose Vane? March dramatically into the Ring and ruin your career in front of witnesses?"

"All of that still happens." Reyes pushed off the wall. "But not before I do something unforgivably insubordinate first."

Okafor waited. He was very good at waiting.

"Send a rider ahead to the Thornfell high pass," Reyes said. "Fastest horse in the post. Find Warden-Captain Reyes's unauthorized associate — the thief, the apprentice, the girl the whole Crown is whispering about — and tell her one thing." She heard her own voice harden into something between an order and a prayer. "Tell her the law is coming to the garden. Tell her the Crown doesn't want her captured anymore."

"Then what does it want?"

Reyes looked up, past the rooftops, to where the sky over the northern peaks was still starless — a black seam in the world where the Otherfell pressed close, patient, hungry, waiting for the covenant to fail one final time.

"It wants her blood," she said. "And we have four days to get there first."

* * *

They rode out through the river gate under a rising moon, seven wardens who had formally mutinied somewhere between the stables and the gatehouse, though no one had bothered to declare it. Reyes carried her copy of the ledger against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and in her head she composed and recomposed the speech she would deliver to the Council Ring when she finally stood before them — the evidence first, then Vane's name, then the truth about the decree, delivered loudly enough that no one in that candlelit chamber could ever again pretend the law was clean.

Let them hang her afterward. Hangings could be survived by other people carrying your story forward. She'd learned that lesson recently, from a girl who traded memories like coins and still smiled.

Behind them, Hollow Crown burned green-gold against the night, still arguing about seating arrangements.

Ahead, the mountains waited, and somewhere on their frozen shoulders, a girl was walking toward a garden with no idea that her own government had already decided how her story ended.

Reyes put her heels to her horse.

Four days.
