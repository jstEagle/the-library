## 17. The Fourth Stone and the Debt Collected

The fourth oath-stone sat in a hollow above the Gorge of Whispers, on a shelf of rock so narrow that two people could not stand side by side upon it. It had been there, according to the grimoire's marginalia, since before the mountains had finished arguing about their own heights, and it looked the part: a squat grey thing no bigger than a loaf of bread, furred with lichen, utterly unremarkable until you got close enough to hear it.

It hummed. Low and patient, like a kettle deciding whether to bother.

Odile crouched at the edge of the shelf and listened to it with her whole body, the way her mistress had taught her — not with ears, which were liars, but with the bones behind them. The hum climbed into her jaw and settled there, familiar now. Three stones had hummed at her like this. Three stones had reached into her and taken their price.

Her mother's voice. Her happiest day. And other things she could no longer name, which was the cruelest arithmetic of all: you could count the holes in yourself even when you couldn't see them.

"You're doing the face," Reyes said behind her.

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to do something brave and stupid and you want me to pretend I don't notice."

"That's slander," Odile said. "I have never once been brave."

Reyes snorted. She was sitting with her back against a wind-carved pillar of stone, her warden's coat spread over both their packs, her broken-and-badly-reset left arm cradled against her chest out of habit rather than pain. The gorge-spirits had caught her. That was the thing Odile kept turning over in her mind like a coin she couldn't stop biting: the spirits had *caught* her, four hundred feet above black water, because Odile had sworn them an unnamed debt in a voice that cracked down the middle. And every hour since, the gorge had been humming below them, patient as a creditor.

"Debts to spirits don't expire," the grimoire said, in her mistress's cramped marginal hand. "They *ripen*."

Odile had been trying very hard not to think about what that meant.

"The stone's ready," she said instead. "I can feel it. It wants me to come up."

"Then I'm coming too."

"It only fits one."

"Then I'll stand at the edge and hold your belt, and if it only fits one, one of us will be standing slightly behind the other, which is called tactics." Reyes hauled herself up with her good arm, grimacing, and Odile noticed — the way she'd been noticing for days now, in small unwanted increments — how easily the word *us* sat between them. How neither of them flinched from it anymore.

Two weeks ago this woman had been hunting her across three provinces with a warrant and a crossbow. Now she carried Odile's pack on her bad-arm side without being asked, and argued with her about rations like a sister, and had once — Odile treasured this — thrown a pinecone at Vesperin's retreating shadow-riders and shouted something so profane that even the gorge-spirits had paused to listen.

The climb to the shelf took ten minutes and cost Odile most of her dignity. Reyes followed, muttering curses at the mountain in the particular tone of someone who had grown up below sea level and considered all elevation a personal insult.

At the shelf's edge, the hum swelled. The lichen on the stone had begun to glow faintly, a green-gold light like sun through bottle glass, and beneath the glow Odile could see the old carvings waking: vines, and hands, and a circle of small figures joining fingers around something bright.

"Fourth of five," she murmured. "One more after this. One more, and then the First Garden."

"And then it's over," Reyes said.

"And then it's over." She said it lightly. Lightly was important. Because there was a question living under that sentence — the question of what *over* meant for the person who paid five times — and she had made a private, cowardly decision not to look at it directly. The mistress's letter had asked forgiveness for the memories the stones would take. Forgiveness implied a scale of loss Odile preferred not to weigh while standing on a ledge.

She knelt. Pressed both palms to the stone.

The hum became a chord. The chord became a voice — or many voices braided into one, the way the gorge-spirits spoke, water over water over water.

***Apprentice-of-the-sworn-hand.*** The shelf itself seemed to vibrate with it. ***You owe.***

"I know," Odile said. "I know I owe. I swore it. I'm here."

***We caught her falling,*** the voices said, and Odile felt the memory arrive uninvited: Reyes dropping past her into the dark, the rope burning through her palms, her own voice tearing open with a word that wasn't a word yet, just need. ***Four hundred feet. We held her soft as a leaf. You promised payment unnamed.***

"I did." Her mouth had gone dry. "Name it."

A pause. The gorge below them went quiet — truly quiet, the whispering stilled, as though the whole depth were leaning in to listen.

***We have watched you carry your dead,*** the spirits said. ***We are collectors of what falls. We know the weight of things. So we will take the heaviest thing you carry.***

Cold walked up Odile's spine. "That's not an answer."

***It is the truest kind,*** they said, almost gently. ***The heaviest thing you carry is her.***

And Odile understood, all at once, with the terrible speed of understanding that arrives too late to be useful, exactly what they meant.

"No," she said.

The stone glowed brighter under her hands, warm now, insistent.

"No. No, wait. Take something else. Take — take my memories of the cottage, take the smell of the smuggler roads, take every meal I've ever eaten, I don't care, I'll eat blind for the rest of my life—"

***The debt was unnamed,*** the voices said. ***Unnamed debts are paid in what the debtor values most, not what she offers cheapest. This is the oldest law we keep.***

"There has to be another way." Her voice was climbing, cracking. Behind her she felt Reyes go very still. "There's always another way, that's — that's the whole point of me, that's what I *do*, I find the other way—"

***Little thief,*** the spirits said, and there was something almost fond in it, ***you found every other way except this one.***

"Odile." Reyes's voice, low and level, the warden voice, the one she used for hard duty. "What are they asking?"

"Don't listen."

"Odile. What are they asking?"

The wind moved through the gorge and carried the whispering back up, and Reyes heard it — she must have, she was sharp as a drawn blade — because when Odile turned, the woman's face had gone carefully, deliberately blank. Warden-blank. The face she wore when the law demanded something terrible and she intended to do it anyway.

"They want my memory of you," Odile said. It came out flat. Factual. A report. "Of — us. Of everything since Cindershade. Since the fire. All of it."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Odile counted them the way her mistress had taught her, thumb against forefinger, and hated herself for counting.

"Well," Reyes said finally. "That's manageable."

"*Manageable?*"

"You'll still remember Bramble. You'll remember the mission. The stones, the garden, Vesperin, all of it — the work survives. You'll just..." She shrugged with her good shoulder, a small economical motion that fooled absolutely no one. "You'll lose one friend. Out of, what, several? You have Bramble. You have the goat we met outside Hollowmere, and you clearly loved that goat."

"I am not losing you," Odile said, "to pay a bar tab."

"Debts aren't bar tabs."

"This one is! I swore it in a panic to save your life, which means it's *your* debt as much as mine, and I don't recall you offering to—"

"Because you wouldn't let me offer," Reyes said quietly. "I tried. On the bridge. You talked over me. You talk over everyone when you're scared; it's one of your four hundred flaws, and I've been keeping a list."

"Give me the list, then. Give me the list and I'll trade it. Spirits love lists—"

"Odile."

"—there's a market for everything in Cindershade, someone will buy a list of my flaws, Bramble would give at least two silver—"

"*Odile.*"

And then Reyes did the last thing Odile expected. She smiled. Not the warden face, not the careful blankness — the real one, crooked and tired and warm, the one that had started appearing somewhere around the second province and that Odile had privately begun to think of as hers.

"I fell four hundred feet," Reyes said, "and a stranger I'd been chasing for weeks caught me by selling a piece of herself I didn't even know she was selling. Do you understand what that makes me? I'm the most expensive friend anyone's ever bought. It would be rude to refuse delivery."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's not—" Odile's voice broke, and she pressed the back of her hand against her mouth, furious at it. "I just got you back. From the fall. I just — I spent one night thinking you were dead, one night, and it was the worst night of my life, and I don't *have* those nights to spare anymore, don't you understand? The stones keep taking and taking and I keep saying yes because someone has to, because the stars are falling and the plague-fog is eating towns and if I don't say yes then who—"

"Someone else," Reyes said. "And they'd pay worse. They'd pay blood. The Crown already priced you at a sacrifice, remember?" She stepped closer, boots careful on the narrow shelf. "You're not choosing between paying and not paying. You're choosing what currency. And I am telling you, as a warden, as an accountant of the Crown's cruelties: this is the cheapest bill you will ever be handed."

"I hate that you're right."

"I know. It's one of my flaws. It's on *my* list."

Odile laughed — a wet, ruined sound, half sob — and Reyes's smile flickered at the edges, and for a moment the two of them just stood there on the shelf above the whispering dark, close enough that Odile could see the new scar at her hairline from the bridge, the one Odile had helped bandage, the one she would forget helping bandage.

"If I forget you," Odile said, "promise me something."

"Anything."

"When I look at you like a stranger — and I will, apparently any minute now — don't tell me. Don't explain. Let me meet you fresh. Let me get to decide again whether I like you." She swallowed. "I'd like the chance to choose you twice."

Reyes was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had gone rough. "That's the bravest stupid thing you've ever said, and I've watched you fight a plague-wraith with a soup ladle."

"It was a very good ladle."

"It was." Reyes extended her good hand, formal as an oath. "Warden Captain Ilsa Reyes. And you are?"

"Don't," Odile said, laughing and crying at once. "*Don't.*"

But she shook the hand. She held on maybe a heartbeat longer than a handshake required, memorizing the calluses, the grip, the way Reyes's thumb pressed once against her knuckles — a habit, she realized, that had become theirs somewhere on the road, a whole silent language she was about to lose every word of.

Then she turned back to the stone.

"Do it," she told the spirits. "Before I'm clever enough to find a sixth other way."

***Brave-and-stupid,*** the voices said, approvingly, and Odile thought wildly: *even the gorge knows my face.*

The stone flared green-gold. Warmth poured up through her palms, through her wrists, into the place behind her eyes where memories lived — and she felt it move through her like a careful hand through a drawer of letters, searching, sorting, finding the bundle tied with the brightest ribbon.

*No,* she thought, grasping at it. *Wait, I want — the campfire, I want to keep the campfire, the night she burned the sausages and blamed the altitude—*

The drawer opened.

***

The world came back slowly, like dawn through fog.

Odile was kneeling on a stone shelf above a gorge. Her hands rested on a humming grey stone, which was going quiet now, its light sinking back into lichen and sleep. Fourth of five. One more, and then the First Garden. She knew this. She knew all of it: the grimoire in her pack, the three prices already paid, the hollow places inside her where her mother's voice used to live.

Behind her, someone shifted their weight. Boots on rock. Armored coat rustling.

A stranger, then. Tall, dark-haired, one arm bound and splinted, a warden's coat worn like it had been lived in rather than issued. A scar at the hairline, recently bandaged — and oddly, Odile's fingers twitched with the sudden unexplained certainty that she knew how that bandage had been tied, though she couldn't have said why.

The woman was watching her with an expression Odile couldn't read. Something guarded. Something braced.

"Hello," Odile said, getting to her feet. She dusted off her knees. "You must be the escort the spirits mentioned. Sorry about the — whatever that was. The glowing. It's a whole process." She stuck out her hand. "Odile Villalobos. Apprentice hedge-witch, currently the most wanted runaway in three provinces, and you appear to be a Crown warden, which means we should probably establish immediately whether you're arresting me or not, because I've had a long day and I'd like to skip to the relevant part."

The stranger stared at her outstretched hand for a moment too long.

Something crossed her face — quick as a fish under ice, there and gone, and Odile would spend a long time afterward wondering what it had been.

"Not arresting you," the woman said at last. Her voice was low and dry, like gravel warmed by sun. "Ilsa Reyes. Captain. Currently... between postings." She took Odile's hand and shook it, firm and brief, and her thumb pressed once against Odile's knuckles — a strange little gesture, almost a habit, meaning nothing, surely meaning nothing.

"Reyes," Odile repeated. "Right. The commander's wardens — you're the one who didn't arrest me in Cindershade. Bramble mentioned you. Well, Bramble mostly complained about you, but he complains about everyone he secretly respects, it's his whole personality."

"Is it." Reyes's mouth did something complicated.

"He also says you throw pinecones at Fae lords, which frankly sounds like slander, but I've learned not to bet against Bramble's gossip." Odile shouldered her pack and nodded down the trail, brisk, businesslike, a woman with a mission and no time to linger. "Anyway. Fifth stone's in the First Garden, three days' hard walk, and Vesperin's host is moving faster than we are, so — shall we?"

She set off along the shelf path without waiting for an answer.

Behind her, for just a moment, Reyes did not move. Odile glanced back and saw her standing very still against the sky, one hand pressed flat over her sternum as though holding a door shut, looking at Odile with the particular grief of someone reading a letter addressed to someone else.

"You coming?" Odile called.

"Yes," Reyes said. Her voice was steady. It cost her something to make it steady; Odile noticed that much, without knowing why she noticed. "Coming."

They walked down together out of the high country, toward the pass, toward the frozen garden waiting at the end of everything — and Odile talked the whole way, brightly, endlessly, about stones and stars and the goat outside Hollowmere, while beside her a stranger listened to every word like a woman memorizing a language she was about to need.

And far below them both, the gorge began, very softly, to whisper again.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, and Odile dreamed — though she could not have said of what — of a campfire, and burnt sausages, and laughter, and woke with wet cheeks and no idea why, and decided it was the wind.

Across the ashes, Reyes lay awake a long time, watching the girl sleep, keeping a list in her head of everything worth telling her twice.
