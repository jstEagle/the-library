## 18. Trial by Candlelight

The Hall of the Hollow Crown had seen coronations, executions, and one memorable wedding between a duke and a bear-trainer, but it had never — in Captain Ilsa Reyes's long acquaintance with the place — seen anything quite so small as a single woman standing in the middle of it holding a leather satchel.

She felt very small. She also felt, for the first time in eleven years of service, entirely awake.

Candles burned everywhere. That was the fashion of the council these days: no mirrors, no polished silver, nothing that might show a reflection where Vesperin's agents could hide their glamour-work. Just candles, hundreds of them, guttering in their iron sconces like a congregation of nervous moths. The light made everyone look older and slightly guilty, which Reyes supposed was honest of it.

Above her, on the raised dais, sat the Council of Nine Wards beneath the empty throne — the Hollow Crown itself, displayed on a velvet cushion because the young king was "indisposed," which everyone understood to mean *in hiding*, which everyone further understood to mean *sensible*.

And at the center of the council's curve, in his chair of black oak, sat Commander Aldous Vane.

He looked exactly as he always looked: silver at the temples, medals arranged with geometric precision, an expression of patient disappointment worn like a well-tailored coat. He had been Reyes's commander for nine years. He had signed her first commendation. He had once carried her, half-frozen, down from the Thornfell passes after her first winter patrol.

He had also, according to the documents sweating against her ribs, sold the ward-rituals to agents of an exiled Fae lord for a sum she still couldn't make herself read twice.

"Captain Reyes," Vane said, and his voice was warm as ever, warm as a hearth in a house that was burning down. "You were not summoned to this session."

"No, sir." Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected. Somewhere behind her, she heard Odile Villalobos shift her weight among the petitioners' benches, disguised in a warden's cloak two sizes too big, and that helped. It was easier to be brave when you could feel someone behind you trying very hard not to fidget. "I'm here under the old rite. The Right of Witness."

A murmur went through the hall. The Right of Witness was older than the council, older than the crown, possibly older than the building. Any warden who had witnessed treason could demand audience before the assembled wards, and no power on either side of the border could refuse them.

It had been invoked four times in three hundred years.

Three of those witnesses had died within the year.

"Ah," said Vane softly. "I did wonder when we'd get to this part."

* * *

Three days earlier, in a candlelit room above a chandler's shop, Odile had spread the copied evidence across a table and said, "All right. Explain it to me again slowly, because I keep falling asleep at the boring parts."

"It's ledger fraud," Reyes had said. "That's what makes it damning. Anyone can forge a letter. But this is nine years of requisition records. Ward-salts ordered and never delivered. Ritual components drawn from the armory and marked 'expended in training.' He didn't just sell the rituals — he spent years making sure the wards were maintained by rote instead of truth. Half our border ceremonies have been theater since before I made captain."

Odile had propped her chin on her hand. "So the great impenetrable wall between us and the Otherfell was mostly paperwork?"

"Mostly paperwork and wishful thinking."

"And nobody noticed because—"

"Because the paperwork said everything was fine. The paperwork is how wardens think. I know because I am one." Reyes had rubbed her eyes. "I checked every figure three times. I even found the entry from six years ago — a shipment of oath-ash marked 'lost to weather' on the exact week my patrol nearly died in a storm that came out of a clear sky."

"You think he caused that storm?"

"I think I stopped believing in coincidence somewhere around the third forged signature."

Odile had been quiet for a moment. Then: "You know what happens to you if you're wrong."

"I know what happens to me if I'm right, too."

"Yes," Odile had said, with a gentleness that Reyes hadn't known the thief possessed, "but you've only been planning for one of those. Which tells me something about you, Warden Reyes."

"What does it tell you?"

"That you'd rather hang for the truth than live comfortably inside a lie. It's a terrible quality in a fugitive's ally." She'd smiled. "It's a wonderful one in a friend."

Reyes had looked away first. "We should sleep. Tomorrow I burn my career."

"Tomorrow," Odile had corrected, blowing out the candle, "you plant it somewhere better."

* * *

Now, in the Hall of the Hollow Crown, Reyes opened the satchel.

"The Right of Witness requires evidence," she said, and her voice carried in the hush the way a stone carries across still water. "I bring ledgers. Requisition records spanning nine years. Signatures — genuine ones, verified against the Commander's own seal-matrix, which I note has never left his office." She laid the pages on the council table one by one, each with a soft slap that sounded, in the silence, like a door closing. "I bring testimony. Warden Second-Class Ferro, currently held in the cells below, will confirm under truth-candle that he was paid, on three occasions, by an agent he now identifies as belonging to the court of Lord Vesperin of the Otherfell."

At the name *Vesperin*, several councilors flinched. One knocked over his inkwell and pretended he hadn't.

"And I bring this." The last page. The oldest one. Dated six years back, in Vane's own careful hand: *Disposition of oath-ash, 40 lbs., lost to weather.* "Commander. Would you care to tell the council what the weather was like, six years ago, on the fourteenth of Frostmonth?"

Vane regarded her for a long moment. Around them, the candles leaned in their sconces as though straining to listen.

"I remember that shipment," he said at last, mildly. "Terrible season. You were nearly killed in it yourself, weren't you? I signed your convalescence order personally." A ripple of sympathy moved through the younger councilors. He was good. He was so very good. "Captain Reyes is a fine officer. Diligent. Brave. But grief does strange things to the diligent and brave. Her sister was lost beyond the border, as I recall. Perhaps the captain has been looking for someone to blame."

There it was. The knife, slid in gently, angled toward the oldest wound she had.

Reyes had rehearsed for this. She had rehearsed in the chandler's room while Odile threw bread crusts at her whenever her voice went flat. (*"Again. You sound like you're reading a grocery list. Again."*)

"My sister is dead," Reyes said clearly. "She died because the wards failed. And the wards failed because—" she tapped the ledger, "—this man emptied them for profit. Grief doesn't enter into it, Commander. Arithmetic does. Forty pounds of oath-ash. Three payments through the Cindershade mint. One agent with ash-grey eyes who signed his receipts with a starburst. Shall I read the amounts aloud? I have them memorized. I've had rather a lot of time to memorize things lately, since learning my life's institution was a costume shop."

Silence. Then Councilor Merrin of the Eastern Reach — old, sharp, famously unbuyable — leaned forward and picked up the oldest page.

"This seal-matrix impression," she said. "Who verified it?"

"Warden-Lieutenant Corvane, keeper of the royal archives. Under truth-candle."

Merrin read. And read again. And then she did something no councilor had done in Reyes's living memory: she laughed, short and terrible, and turned the page to face Vane.

"Commander. You embezzled the *weather*."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way rot gives way all at once.

Councilor after councilor demanded the ledgers. Pages were passed hand to hand like contraband. A junior clerk was sent sprinting for the archive copies. Vane sat perfectly still through all of it, and Reyes watched him with the particular attention of a woman who had spent nine years reading his moods, and saw what almost no one else saw: he wasn't looking at the councilors.

He was looking at the doors.

"Captain," Odile murmured, materializing at her elbow out of the petitioner crowd with the smoothness of long practice. She nodded, very slightly, toward the eastern entrance. "The two guards there. They haven't blinked in about four minutes. Also the fellow selling chestnuts outside hasn't sold any chestnuts. I watched him turn away actual money."

"Ash-men," Reyes breathed.

"At least three. Maybe more glamoured." Odile's hand drifted to the grimoire-shaped lump under her stolen cloak. "Your honor, Warden. How attached are you to finishing this speech?"

"Very."

"Good. Me too. I just wanted you to know the odds first. It helps me enjoy things."

On the dais, Vane finally rose. "Enough." His voice filled the hall without effort, the voice of a man accustomed to being obeyed at volume. "These papers are forgeries. Elaborate, malicious—"

"They're *your* handwriting, Aldous," said Merrin, and used his Christian name like a slap.

"—and even if they were not," he continued, unbothered, "the council lacks authority in matters of the border. The sacrifice decree stands. The breach will be sealed. By blood, if necessary, and the blood has already been chosen—"

"*Whose blood?*" Merrin demanded.

Vane smiled. It was the worst thing Reyes had ever seen him do, worse than the ledgers, worse than the storm — because it was the smile of a man setting down a heavy load at last.

"Why," he said, "the runaway apprentice. The last soul who knows both sides of the covenant. The Crown has been looking for her for months. And here she stands—" his finger rose, and every ash-man in the hall turned with it, "—wearing a warden's cloak two sizes too large, in the fourth row of the petitioners' benches, holding a stolen grimoire against her ribs."

The hall erupted.

* * *

Later, Odile would maintain that her escape had been elegant. Reyes would maintain, with equal conviction, that it had involved knocking over a brazier, shouting "FOR THE KING'S DIGESTION" at the top of her lungs, and dragging a councilor out of the way by his robes. History would probably split the difference.

What mattered was the sequence:

One: Vane's finger pointing, and the ash-men moving, unhurried, through a crowd that was suddenly all elbows and screaming.

Two: Reyes drawing her blade — not at Vane, protocol be damned, but at the nearest ash-man, whose glamour peeled away under steel-light like wet paper, revealing a tall grey shape with eyes like burnt-out stars.

Three: Odile tearing a page from the grimoire — *her mistress's marginalia, she'd be furious, she'd forgive me* — and speaking three words of hedge-magic that turned every candle in the hall into a small furious sun. The shadows screamed. The ash-men recoiled, smoking.

Four: Merrin, magnificent old woman, slamming her staff on the dais and roaring over the chaos: "THE RIGHT OF WITNESS STANDS! SEIZE THE COMMANDER!"

Five: Vane walking backward through the eastern doors, unhurried even now, flanked by his grey-eyed guards, pausing only to say — to Reyes, across forty feet of smoke and panic — "You were the best of them, Captain. That's why I gave you the easy assignments."

And six: the doors closing, and in the sudden ringing quiet, Odile standing amid the wreckage of the council chamber with a scrap of grimoire page in her hand, going very pale.

"Reyes," she said. "The marginalia. On this page. My mistress wrote it years ago — she wrote *where the last stone sleeps*."

Reyes caught her arm. "Where?"

"The First Garden." Odile looked up, and her eyes were wide and bright and terrified. "Under the frozen waterfall. Where the covenant was sworn. It's been under our feet the whole time — the beginning and the end are the same place."

From the doorway, breathless, a runner in council livery: "The Commander's gone — horses waiting at the river gate — and there's word from the pass road, ma'am. Armed column moving north. Sacrifice detail. They have the decree. They have her description."

And from the other window, faint but unmistakable, rolling up from the southern hills where the border lay broken: a sound like stars falling, one after another, getting closer.

Reyes looked at Odile. Odile looked at Reyes.

"Well," Odile said, with the gallows cheer of a woman who had already run out of sensible options twice before breakfast, "it seems everyone's invited to the garden. We should hurry. I hate arriving late to my own apocalypse."

They ran.
