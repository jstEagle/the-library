## 19. The Race to the First Garden

The mountain did not want them there.

Odile felt it in her teeth before she heard it — a low grinding note beneath the snow, like a millstone turning somewhere under the world. She paused on the switchback trail, breath steaming, and pressed her mitten against the rock face as though she could listen through it.

"Odile." Reyes's voice came flat and low from behind, the way it always did when the warden was frightened and refusing to admit it. "Keep moving."

"It's the mountain talking," Odile said. "My mistress wrote about this. The old roads near the First Garden are *woken* roads. They don't like being walked by people who intend to change things."

"Then we'll apologize on the way down." Reyes shouldered past her, boots crunching, the copied evidence still wrapped in oilcloth against her chest even now that the trial was done and her career with it. She had traded a captain's insignia for a fugitive's company and had not once complained aloud, which Odile found both admirable and deeply suspicious. Wardens complained. It was half their diet.

They climbed. Behind them, far below at the tree line, torchlight crawled — the Crown's sacrifice detail, two dozen strong, moving with the patient terrible steadiness of people who had been told exactly what they were carrying and where.

Ahead, above the frozen waterfall that hid the garden door, the sky was wrong. The unlit stars hung there like holes punched in black cloth, and around each hole the darkness seemed to lean, hungry, toward the world.

"They'll reach the pass before us if we keep this pace," Odile said.

"No," said Reyes. "*She* will."

And indeed, threading down the glacier face like ink poured through water, came Vesperin's host — a hundred shapes that moved without disturbing the snow, wearing stolen faces, singing in voices borrowed from the dead.

* * *

Captain Dain Ferrow of the Sacrifice Detail had been told three things, and he repeated them to himself like a rosary as his column ground upward through the blue dark:

One: the prisoner Odile Villalobos was to be taken alive.

Two: her blood would reseal the border wards.

Three: he was not to think too hard about either of the first two.

He was forty-one years old, he had served the Crown for twenty-two years, and until this week he had believed the Crown was, on balance, a thing worth serving. Then the decree had come down naming a specific girl — a name, a face, a description sketched by someone who had clearly never met her, all sharp angles where Bramble Hask's tavern gossip insisted she was all elbows and apology — and Dain had understood that the law had stopped being a wall and started being a mouth.

"Sergeant," he called. "How many?"

Halvorsen spat over the edge of the trail. "Lights in the trees, sir. Smuggler's road's awake. And up top—" He squinted. "Something's coming off the glacier. Not soldiers."

Dain looked. The shapes descending were wrong in a way his body understood before his mind did; his hackles rose like a dog's. "Form square," he said. "Bayonets. And if any man breaks rank, I will personally—"

The fog got them first.

It came up out of the valley floor without wind, without direction, gray-white and warm as breath, and where it touched men they forgot. Dain watched Corporle Vetch turn to the soldier beside him mid-order and say, "Remind me, why are we climbing?" — watched the soldier answer, honestly, "I don't remember you" — watched twenty years of drill dissolve into a crowd of strangers standing confused on a mountainside, holding weapons they no longer knew why they carried.

Only Dain kept himself, because he kept saying the three things over and over, and one of them was a girl's name, and names were anchors. He'd learned that from the plague reports. Names were anchors.

"*Villalobos,*" he said aloud, into the fog. "*Villalobos.*"

Somewhere ahead, a woman's voice answered through the white: "That depends entirely on who's asking."

* * *

Reyes knew the voice before the fog thinned enough to show the face, and her sword was out and leveled before Odile could grab her sleeve.

"Stand down," Reyes said. "Ferrow. Captain Ferrow. Third garrison. You drilled me as a recruit."

Captain Dain Ferrow stood alone at the edge of the fog with his bayonet fixed and his eyes red-rimmed, and when he spoke, his voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "Warden Reyes. They burned your name off the rolls yesterday. I checked." A terrible pause. "I checked because I wanted to know whether arresting you would be treason or merely paperwork."

"And which is it?"

"I don't know anymore." His gaze went to Odile. Held. She saw him take in the grimoire strap across her chest, the frost in her hair, the fact of her — small, tired, twenty-something, and nothing at all like a sacrifice. Something in his jaw worked. "You're her."

"I'm her," Odile agreed carefully.

"The decree says your blood seals the border." He said it like a man reciting scripture and daring God to contradict him. "The council signed it. My orders—"

"Your orders," Reyes said quietly, "came from Commander Aldous Vey, who sold the ward-rituals to an exiled Fae lord six years ago. I have copies. So does every tavern-keeper between here and the capital, thanks to a fence named Bramble Hask, who I am beginning to suspect could sell sand in a desert." She stepped forward, slow, hands visible. "The decree isn't law, Captain. It's a purchase. Someone bought your orders, and the price is that girl."

The fog swirled between them. Below, the forgotten soldiers wandered the switchbacks calling one another by the wrong names. Above, the star-holes leaned closer.

Ferrow looked at his bayonet for a long moment. Then he reversed it and drove it point-down into the snow, like a grave marker for something.

"My men won't remember their orders till the fog passes," he said hoarsely. "When it does, some of them will remember them *correctly.* I can buy you maybe an hour. Go."

"Come with us," Odile said, before she could stop herself. "There might be another way. There has to be another way, and if there isn't — well. I'd rather not be sacrificed by someone who doesn't even like me."

For the first time in perhaps a year, Captain Dain Ferrow almost smiled. "I like you fine," he said. "That's rather the problem. Run."

They ran.

* * *

Vesperin watched the little drama on the switchbacks from the shoulder of the glacier, and found it — annoyingly — affecting.

He had worn a hundred faces in his long exile and had grown skilled at reading them, but he had never learned to read the human talent for ruining their own plans out of pity. The captain had a host of forty forgotten soldiers and a clear road to the garden door, and he had thrown it away for a girl's sake. Sentiment. It was the great flaw of the short-lived, and Vesperin had built his entire strategy upon it, and still it managed to surprise him.

"You're smiling," said Sallow, his herald, whose current face had belonged to a drowned fisherman and still smelled faintly of brine.

"I am *calculating.*"

"Your mouth is smiling, lord."

"My mouth does what it likes. It's stolen." He turned from the ledge. Below, the humans were running again — the warden, the apprentice, and now, absurdly, the captain trailing after them like a duckling imprinted on the wrong mother. Three of them, racing his hundred. "Take the ridge line. Cut them off above the icefall. Kill the soldiers if they interfere, leave the girl unmarked — her memories must be whole when the stones drink, or the covenant will be thin. And Sallow."

"Yes, lord?"

"If the mountain objects to us," Vesperin said mildly, "let it object to them first."

Sallow bowed and melted into the dark, and Vesperin began to walk down the glacier alone, unhurried, his feet leaving no prints at all.

He had waited ninety years in the ash country beyond the border, breathing cinders, watching the lit stars of the human realm through a veil like dirty glass. He had been young once, and bright, and beloved of courts that later swore he had never existed. The covenant had not merely sealed the border. It had sealed *him* — written him out of both worlds, made him a rumor in his own homeland. When the last stone drank Odile Villalobos dry and the wards blazed back to life, he would be behind them again. Forever. Ash, glass, darkness, forever.

So no. He would not be hurried. But he would arrive.

He always arrived.

* * *

The icefall nearly killed them twice before the mountain tried properly.

The first time was the crevasse — a seam in the glacier hidden by a skin of fresh snow, which Reyes broke through to the hip before Odile's grabbed hand and Ferrow's rope hauled her back into the daylight, gasping, laughing in that hollow way people laugh when the alternative is screaming. The second time was Sallow's creatures, three of them wearing the faces of miners from Cindershade, which Odile recognized and which made her furious in a way fear hadn't managed. She drove them off with a hedge-charm her mistress had taught her for clearing wasps' nests — adapted, on the spot, to clear faces. The creatures fled downslope wearing expressions of profound offended dignity.

And then the mountain spoke.

Not a sound. A *decision.* Odile felt it go through the glacier like a shudder through a struck bell, and she had time to shout — "Off the ridge! DOWN!" — and then the slope itself stood up.

Snow moves faster than anything has a right to move. One moment Odile was running; the next the world was white roar and tumbling dark, and she was inside the avalanche, rolled like a sock in a laundry press, unable to tell sky from ground, the grimoire strapped to her chest the single fixed point in a universe gone liquid. She thought, absurdly, of her mistress's voice: *when everything spins, hold the book, child. Books know which way is up.*

Then impact. Silence. Burial.

She dug. It took forever and no time at all. Her hand broke surface into blue evening air, and she clawed herself out onto the settled snow and lay there wheezing like a landed fish.

"Reyes?" Her voice came out cracked. "REYES!"

Nothing. Just the ringing quiet after the roar, and the star-holes overhead, closer now, leaning.

Then, thirty yards away, a gauntleted fist punched up through the snow, followed by its attached warden, coughing, helmetless, bleeding from the eyebrow and swearing with a fluency that would have gotten her flogged in the academy. "I'm FINE," Reyes announced to the mountain, daring it to disagree. "Where's Ferrow?"

They dug together. It took minutes that cost them their hour. They found the captain buried to the neck, conscious, and — Odile realized with a lurch — *wrong*. His eyes tracked them, but slowly, like a man listening to a distant conversation.

"Ferrow," Reyes said, gripping his shoulders. "Captain. Stay with me. What are the three things?"

He frowned. Fog was seeping out of the broken snowpack around him, thin and warm and gray. "Three... things..."

"Your orders. Say your orders."

"There's..." His brow cleared, clouded, cleared. Then, faintly, wondering: "There's a girl. Villalobos. She's the anchor. *Say the name, and you stay yourself.*" His eyes focused on Odile, and he managed something like his old drill-ground bark. "Well. Hello, anchor."

"We're getting you out."

"No." He said it simply. "Look at my legs."

She was pinned — no, *he* was pinned, packed to the waist in avalanche concrete that had set like mortar, and the fog was already curling up around his chin, and they had no shovel, no time, and a hundred Fae shapes descending the ridge line above.

"Captain," Reyes said, and her voice did something Odile had never heard it do — it *broke*, just slightly, just at the edges. "Twenty-two years. You don't get to spend them here."

"Somebody spends them everywhere, Warden." He smiled, and it was a good smile, steady as a parade ground. "Buy me one courtesy. When this is done — whatever 'this' ends up being — somebody come back and dig me out. Even if all I remember is my own name."

"Your name is Dain Ferrow," Reyes said, gripping his hand hard enough to whiten both their knuckles. "Third garrison. You taught a recruit named Ilsa Reyes how to hold a shield wall, and she's going to come back and tell you about it."

"That," said Captain Dain Ferrow, as the fog took him gently by the collar, "sounds like something worth remembering."

They ran. Behind them, the first of Sallow's creatures reached the burial site and paused, puzzled, over a man who sat in the snow smiling at nothing, having forgotten his orders, his mission, his king — but not, because the last thing he'd chosen to hold was the name, the girl named Odile Villalobos, and names were anchors, and the fog slid off him like rain off oiled canvas.

The creatures looked at him for a long time. Then, uncertainly, they moved on.

* * *

They reached the frozen waterfall as full night fell, and found the way already contested.

Below the falls, in the blue amphitheater of the basin, Vesperin's host had assembled — ranks of stolen faces catching the star-hole light, silent, waiting. Above, the curtain of ice glowed faintly from within, and behind the ice, Odile could feel it: the hum of the First Garden, the oldest stone, the door her mistress's marginalia had led her to across a whole wounded kingdom.

Between her and the falls stood Vesperin himself, unhurried, unprinted, his stolen face arranged into something almost kind.

"Apprentice," he said. "Warden. You've lost your soldier, your army, and most of your hour. I've lost nothing, because unlike you, I have not spent myself on sentiment." He spread his hands, and the host stirred like a field of black wheat. "Walk away, and I will let you. Refuse, and I will take the grimoire from your cooling hands. Those are the only two endings available on this mountain tonight."

Odile's legs were shaking. Her lungs burned. Somewhere behind her, Reyes drew her sword with a sound like a promise.

But Odile was looking past him — at the falls, at the glow, at the humming stone — and she was thinking about a letter hidden in a book binding, about a mistress who had known, about a price already paid in advance, and about the strange stubborn arithmetic of memory: *they can't take what I give away.*

"Those aren't the only two endings," she said. "You keep forgetting. I'm very good at doors."

And she ran straight at the icefall, and the ice — which remembered the first covenant, which remembered *her mistress*, which had been waiting ninety cold years for someone to knock — began, slowly, gloriously, to open.
