## 20. The Reforging

The First Garden did not look like a garden anymore.

Odile had imagined it a hundred times on the road — from her mistress's marginalia, from the half-remembered lullabies the old woman used to hum while grinding moonwort — and in every imagining it had been green. Terraces of living stone hung with ivy, fountains that sang instead of splashed, hedges trimmed into shapes that moved when you weren't looking. A garden where oaths grew like fruit.

What lay before her now was a graveyard wearing a garden's bones.

The terraces were still there, but the ivy was black and brittle as burnt hair. The fountain at the center had gone dry, its basin cracked down the middle like a dropped plate, and around its rim the five sockets for the oath-stones gaped empty — four dark circles and one, at the crown of the fountain's spire, larger than the rest, waiting. Snow drifted through the broken pergola and settled on statues whose faces had been worn smooth by centuries of weather, so that every figure looked like a person trying to remember their own name.

Which, Odile thought with a laugh that came out more like a sob, was about to be her problem too.

"You feel it, don't you," said Reyes beside her. The warden's voice was rough as gravel. She was bleeding from a cut above her eyebrow, her cloak torn where the avalanche had taken its toll, and she was standing between Odile and the eastern path with her sword drawn, because even now — *especially* now — she was standing guard. "The garden knows you're here."

"It knows I'm late," Odile said. "There's a difference."

Behind them, the sounds of pursuit had fallen away into an uneasy silence. The Crown's sacrifice detail had lost three men to the plague-fog on the switchbacks; what remained of them had turned back rather than walk into a place where the air itself seemed to disapprove of them. And Vesperin's host — his ash-creatures, his hollow-eyed courtiers — had simply stopped at the treeline, as if the garden were a threshold even they dared not cross without invitation.

All except one.

Vesperin walked through the ruined gate alone.

He was not wearing a stolen face anymore. Whatever glamour he had worn since the night he crawled out of the star-crater had burned away somewhere on the mountain, and what remained was beautiful the way a winter sky is beautiful: vast, cold, and utterly indifferent to whether you froze. His hair was the color of unlit stars. His eyes held no light at all, and yet they reflected everything — Odile saw herself in them, small and snow-dusted and clutching a grimoire to her chest like a shield.

"Apprentice," he said. His voice filled the garden without echoing, the way frost fills a windowpane. "You brought them all home. How diligent."

"I try." Odile set down her pack with hands that would not quite stop shaking. From it she drew the four oath-stones, wrapped in wool and oilcloth and, at Bramble Hask's insistence, a layer of protective superstition involving red thread that she suspected had done nothing at all. They hummed against each other through the wrappings, a chord she felt in her teeth. "And you brought your whole horrible court. How flattering."

"They are not here for you." He glanced back at the treeline, and something flickered across his face — something almost like weariness. "They are here because I cannot tell them to leave. That is what exile is, Odile Villalobos. It is not walls. It is being made of a door that only opens outward."

Reyes shifted. "Stay behind me," she murmured.

"I'm touched," said Vesperin, without looking at her. "Truly. The last honest warden in the Crown's service, defending a thief. Your commander wept when he sold the ward-rituals, Captain Reyes. Did you know that? He wept, and then he counted the coin. Humans are my favorite tragedy."

"Why are you here?" Odile asked. Not rudely. She found, to her surprise, that she didn't have the energy for rudeness. Her feet ached. Her ribs ached where the rope bridge had nearly ended her. And beneath all of it, quieter and heavier than any wound, sat the absence where her memory of Reyes used to be — a gap she kept brushing against like a tongue finding a missing tooth. The gorge-spirits had taken it two nights ago, and Reyes knew, because Odile had confessed it to her afterward in a shaking voice, and Reyes had simply nodded and said, *"Then I'll remember enough for both of us."*

She wished she could remember why that had made her cry.

"Because I am tired," Vesperin said.

The words landed strangely. Reyes's sword dipped an inch. Even the wind seemed to pause over the dead hedges.

"When the covenant was forged," he went on, walking slowly along the fountain's rim, trailing one long finger through the snow, "the gardeners needed a darkness to define their light. Every border needs two sides. So they took the lord of the unlit stars — me — and they wrote him out. Cast him beyond the wards. Made him the story mothers tell to frighten children." He stopped. Turned. "I have been outside the light for nine hundred years, apprentice. And now the wards are broken, and the fifth stone lies empty, and I am standing in the garden that exiled me, holding the last key to my own prison door."

"The fifth stone seals the covenant," Odile said. "It doesn't open it."

"It can do either. It depends on who swears the oath, and what they ask for." His smile was thin and cold and terribly reasonable. "Swear it asking for my return, and the border stays open — gently, this time. No plague-shards. No falling stars. A tended crossing, a shared world. And you keep everything."

"Everything?"

"Your memories. All of them. The stones have been eating you alive — yes, I know, I have watched you grow lighter with every mile, the way candles do. The mistress you loved. Your mother's voice. Your happiest day. Your friend." His eyes flicked, deliberate and cruel, toward Reyes. "Whoever she was. Swear the covenant *my* way, and the fifth stone will take nothing. You will walk out of this garden whole. You will even be a hero. The realms will trade, the stars will shine, and the exiled lord will walk home in the spring thaw. Everyone wins."

The silence stretched.

And Odile, because she was her mistress's apprentice down to the marrow, asked the question that mattered:

"What's the catch?"

Vesperin laughed — a sound like ice cracking on a pond. "There is always a catch. The catch is that a border is a promise, and a promise has two ends. If I stand inside the light again, then the light stands inside *me*. My court walks free among your towns. My hunger walks free. I have spent nine hundred years learning to want things, apprentice, and none of what I want is good for gardens." He spread his hands. "But I am offering you honesty, which is more than your Crown ever offered. Choose. Keep yourself, and let me loose. Or lose yourself, and lock me out forever."

*Keep yourself,* whispered a voice in her that sounded like the drowned chapel, like the gorge-spirits, like every clever bargain she had ever struck. *You've paid so much already. What's left isn't enough to matter.*

But that was the lie, wasn't it. That was the exact lie.

Odile looked down at the grimoire in her arms. Somewhere inside its binding was a letter she had read by candlelight three nights ago, written in her mistress's cramped, loving hand: *I chose you because you steal things, dear girl — including, someday, I hope, my mistakes. Forgive me for what the stones will take. Memory is only heavy because it is carried alone. Carry mine lightly.*

She thought about her mother's voice, which she could no longer hear, and how she had learned to hum the lullaby anyway, from the shape of the silence.

She thought about Reyes, saying *"I'll remember enough for both of us,"* and about how Odile had trusted her with that — had handed a stranger her own grief like a package, and somehow known it was safe.

She thought about the children in burning Cindershade, who had forgotten their mothers mid-sentence, and how she had sung them the hedge-magic counting song until they slept, and how one small boy had called her "the lady from the song" and she had thought: *yes. Let that be enough of a name.*

"Here's my counter-offer," Odile said.

Vesperin's smile faded.

"You get nothing," she said. "No reversal. No open door. But I'll tell you something true, for free, because apparently I'm the only person in either realm who gives anything away anymore." She stepped forward, past Reyes's outstretched arm, until she stood at the foot of the dry fountain with the snow settling on her shoulders. "You're right that exile is a door that only opens outward. But doors aren't the only way through. Stories go where exiles can't. And for nine hundred years, every frightened child on both sides of the border has heard your name in the dark — which means you've never once been forgotten. You've just been remembered *wrongly.*"

"That is not—"

"It's the only mercy on offer," Odile said, "and I suggest you take it, because in about four minutes I'm going to forget how to be smug and I'd hate to waste it."

Then she unwrapped the stones.

* * *

The ritual was in the grimoire's final pages, in her mistress's hand, annotated in three colors of ink and one margin note that simply read: *stand straight, dear girl; the garden hates a sloucher.*

She placed the first stone in its socket at the fountain's base, and it woke with a sound like a struck bell heard underwater. The second, higher. The third. The fourth. With each one, the garden shuddered — black ivy twitching, snow sliding from the statues' smooth faces — and the humming climbed up through Odile's boots and into her bones, and she felt the four payments stir inside her like scars remembering rain.

The fifth socket waited at the top of the spire. There were no steps. Of course there were no steps. The original gardeners had probably grown a staircase out of living wood, and living wood did not survive nine hundred years of neglect and one very bad winter.

"Boost?" said Reyes.

"Boost," said Odile.

Reyes laced her fingers into a stirrup, and Odile put her boot in it, and for one absurd moment — the moment before everything — the two of them were just a thief and a warden doing the world's least dignified acrobatics in a frozen ruin, and Odile thought, with the part of her mind that wasn't terrified: *whatever she was to me, I bet she was wonderful.*

The spire's crown was narrow and slick with rime. Below her, Vesperin watched with his lightless eyes, and his court pressed against the treeline like a tide against a seawall, and far to the south the sky over the human realms glowed with the sick orange of Cindershade still burning.

Above her, through the broken pergola, the sky was full of holes.

That was the thing no one had said plainly, maybe because no one had seen it from high enough: the unlit stars hadn't gone out. They had *left*. The heavens over the Otherfell were peppered with dark places, wounds in the fabric of the night, and from each one hung a hair-thin thread of silver, dangling, like cut puppets' strings. Waiting to fall. Waiting to carry the forgetting down onto every town, every farm, every cradle.

Odile knelt on the spire's crown. She set the fifth stone in the last socket.

It did not hum. It *opened* — a soft unfolding, like a hand unclenching after years — and she felt it look at her. Not see. *Look.* The way her mistress used to look at her over the top of her spectacles when Odile claimed she definitely, absolutely had not eaten the last honey-cake.

"Well," Odile said aloud, to the stone, to the garden, to the sky full of strings. "You know what I am. You know what I've got left. Take the lot."

And because she was her mistress's apprentice, she added, firmly: "*Stand straight.*"

The stone took the mistress first.

It went gently, the way snowfall goes — no tearing, no theft, just a slow warm dissolution, and Odile felt the cottage go: the smell of ink and dried lavender, the spectacles pushed up into grey hair, the voice saying *again, from the top, and this time mean it.* She grabbed for it and it slid through her fingers like water, and she was crying before she understood what about, tears with no memory attached, grief outliving its object. *Carry mine lightly,* said a letter she could no longer read, in a hand she could no longer picture, and she carried it lightly, because it was all she could carry.

The stone took the theft — the night she'd run, the grimoire under her coat, exhilaration curdling into dread. Took her mother's lullaby's last echo. Took the drowned chapel and the clever half-truth. Took Cindershade burning and the children asleep. Took the rope bridge, the gorge-spirits, the debt. Took the campfire, the truce, the warden's rough voice saying *then I'll remember enough for both of us* —

Took her name.

It didn't hurt. That was the terrible part. It was like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was wearing. "Odile" went out of her the way a candle goes out — not violently, just *finished* — and the girl kneeling on the spire's crown was someone new, someone with no name and no history and four great aching absences she would never know to mourn, holding on to a warm stone with both hands because some deep animal part of her understood that letting go meant falling.

The fifth stone flared white.

Light poured out of the fountain like water running backward — up the cracked basin, up the dead spire, up into the sky — and the covenant caught fire along lines no living eye had ever seen: silver threads stitching themselves across the pass, over the peaks, between the realms, a seam of light closing a wound nine hundred years wide. The threads reached the hanging strings of the fallen stars and *gathered* them, drew them taut, pulled them home. One by one, the dark wounds in the heavens closed. One by one, the stars relit — not all at once but in a slow wave, like lamps being carried down a long street, until the whole sky over the Otherfell burned bright and the snow below glittered like spilled treasure.

In the garden, the black ivy greened from root to tip in a rush, like a held breath released. The fountain woke. Water rose singing in the cracked basin, and the cracks sealed with a sound like knuckles cracking, and the statues' worn faces — the keeper could see this clearly, though she no longer knew why it mattered — seemed, very slightly, to relax.

At the treeline, Vesperin's court dissolved into ash and drifting sparks, recalled to their lord like breath drawn inward. Vesperin himself remained a moment longer, standing in the new starlight, and the keeper — kneeling on the spire, blinking, empty, *light* — saw his expression and did not know it, could not have named it, but felt it land somewhere anyway, in the body's memory where names don't reach.

He looked like a man reading his own name carved somewhere very far away.

Then he bowed — low, formal, nine hundred years of bitterness folded into one precise gesture — and walked backward out of the garden, and the border shimmered where he crossed it, and did not break.

Below, a woman in a torn warden's cloak stood in the falling snow with her sword point-down in the earth, staring up at the spire, at the keeper, and the keeper stared back, polite and blank, the way one regards a friendly stranger.

"Hello," the keeper called down. Her voice sounded strange to her. New. "Is this your garden? It's lovely. I think I might work here."

The woman made a sound that was half a laugh and half something else entirely, and wiped her face with the back of her glove, and said, in a voice rough as gravel:

"Yeah," said Reyes. "I think you do."

And she picked up a book lying in the snow — a grimoire, its binding scarred, its pages soft with handling — and began to climb.
