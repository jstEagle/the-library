## 21. The Keeper Who Remembers Nothing

The road to the First Garden had been a smuggler's track once, then a warden's patrol route, and now — since the stars came back — it was simply a road again, which was the strangest thing about it. People walked it. Farmers, mostly, with carts of seed-grain and salt fish, trading across a border that had spent three hundred years as a wound. They nodded to Ilsa Reyes as she passed, because she still wore her captain's coat out of habit more than authority, and because a woman with a sword on her hip made farmers feel better about walking under mountains that had recently rained fire.

She did not correct them about her rank. There was no rank left to correct. The council had stripped it from her in a ceremony that lasted eleven minutes, most of which was taken up by her former commander reading charges he could barely pronounce. She had stood at attention through all of it, and when it ended she had saluted him, which had been the cruelest thing she'd done that year, and she had done some genuinely cruel things that year.

The coat still fit. That was enough.

The pass opened ahead of her the way it always did — too suddenly, the walls of rock falling away like curtains — and there it was: the First Garden, green and impossible in the high cold, spilling down the mountainside in terraces that no human hand had built. The frozen waterfall that had once hidden its door now fell open and unhidden, catching the afternoon light, and above it the sky was full of stars even though the sun was still up. Faint ones. Patient ones. They never quite went away anymore, not over the garden.

Reyes stopped where the path met the first terrace, out of an old soldier's instinct for thresholds, and looked for the keeper.

It took her a moment, as it always did. The keeper worked among the low hedges near the eastern wall, sleeves rolled, hands dark to the wrist with soil, and she moved through the rows with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never known any other life. Her hair was long now, tied back with a strip of green cloth. When she straightened and turned, shading her eyes against the light, her face did the thing it always did on these visits: a flicker of recognition that arrived without anything behind it, like a lamp lit in an empty house.

"You're back," the keeper said. Her voice was warm and slightly puzzled, the voice of a woman greeting a friend whose name sat just out of reach. "I knew you would be. I don't know how I knew."

"Good instincts," Reyes said. "You've always had good instincts."

"Have I?" The keeper smiled, wiped her hands on her apron, and came toward her across the grass. "You say things like that. Like you know me. It's very strange." She said it without any grief at all, which was somehow worse than grief would have been. "I've decided not to mind."

"That seems wise."

"It's the only thing I've decided," the keeper said, "and I'm rather proud of it."

* * *

Reyes had learned, over four visits, the shape of the days here. You did not ask the keeper questions about herself; the questions slid off her like rain off waxed canvas, and asking them put a small cloud behind her eyes that took hours to clear. Instead you let her talk, and she talked wonderfully — about the garden, about the soil, about the third terrace's stubborn refusal to grow anything but moonflowers, about the small grey fox that had taken up residence under the potting bench and considered itself management.

"The fox thinks the herbs are his," she said, leading Reyes along the rows. "I've told him otherwise. We've reached an understanding. He gets the chamomile, which nobody wants anyway, and I get everything else, and occasionally he brings me a dead vole, which I pretend is payment."

"A fair arrangement."

"He's a terrible businessman. Last week he brought me a dead vole I'd already buried." She laughed at this — a real laugh, head tipped back, entirely unguarded — and Reyes felt the old ache turn over in her chest like a stone in a streambed.

Because she knew that laugh. She had heard it on a rope bridge, over a campfire, in a flooded chapel with water rising around their knees. She had heard it directed at her, specifically at her, by a woman who had trusted her with a confession worth a happiest day, and the woman wearing that same face now looked at her with bright, courteous blankness and offered her tea.

The cottage at the garden's heart had been rebuilt — or perhaps grown; Reyes had stopped trying to distinguish — with a low roof and a chimney that smoked sweetly, woodsmoke cut with something green. Inside, two cups already waited on the table, which meant the keeper had known this morning that a visitor was coming, though if you asked her how, she would frown and say, "I set out two cups. I don't know why. I do it every few weeks and I don't know why," and then she would look at the cups for a long moment with an expression Reyes had learned not to name aloud.

"I brought things," Reyes said, unpacking her satchel on the table while the keeper poured. "News, mostly. And honey. Real honey, from the Cindershade side — Bramble Hask sends it, with instructions I'm to tell you he says hello, though he refuses to believe you can't remember him cheating you at cards."

"Did he cheat me?"

"Constantly."

"Then I'm glad I don't remember," the keeper said serenely, "because I suspect I'd want my money back, and he seems like a man who'd fight me for it."

"He absolutely would. He'd lose, but he'd enjoy it."

* * *

The news was this: the trade roads were holding. Three months of cautious caravans, warded at both ends by oath-stones that hummed contentedly in their new settings, and not one plague-shard found in the passes since midwinter. On the Crown's side, the council had been reorganized — half its members replaced after what the broadsheets were still calling the Candlelight Affair, a name Reyes privately resented, because it made it sound tidy, and it had not been tidy, it had been her standing in front of a hundred armed men with a stolen ledger in her hands and her career already ash in her mouth, saying *read it* until someone read it.

Her former commander was awaiting trial. The sacrifice decree was rescinded. The wardens' oaths were being rewritten — slowly, grudgingly, in the language of people rather than crowns — and Reyes had been asked, twice, to come back and help write them, and twice she had said no, and both times the asking officer had seemed relieved.

"And what do you do instead?" the keeper asked. She had tucked her feet up under her on the bench, tea cradled in both hands, listening the way some people listen — completely, as though the news were a story being told just for her.

"I walk," Reyes said. "Mostly. I check the stones. There are five of them, scattered from here to the coast, and they like to be visited. I talk to them. It's as mad as it sounds."

"It doesn't sound mad," the keeper said. "It sounds like tending. Things like being tended. My roses sulk if I skip a day." She paused. "Is that why you come here? To tend me?"

The question landed softly and went deep, the way her questions always did. Reyes looked into her cup.

"In a way," she said. "Yes."

The keeper nodded, satisfied, and asked nothing further, and Reyes loved her a little for that, and grieved a little more.

* * *

After the second cup of tea, as the light went long and gold across the terraces, Reyes did what she always did on these visits. She told the story.

She had refined it over the months. The first time she'd tried, she had made the mistake of telling it plainly — *there was an apprentice named Odile Villalobos, and she stole a grimoire, and she saved the world* — and the keeper had listened politely and then said, "That sounds like a very fine tale, Captain, but I don't see what it has to do with me," and had gone out afterward and weeded the same row for two hours, and Reyes had sworn never to cause that particular cloud again.

So now she told it as a story, the way Bramble told it in the taverns, the way it was becoming everywhere — a tale with a shape, a heroine at one remove, a brave apprentice who belonged to everyone and therefore, gently, to no one in particular.

"There was a girl," Reyes said, looking out the window at the moonflowers opening on the third terrace, "who ran away from the only home she'd ever known, carrying something that wasn't hers. She stole it, actually. From the woman who raised her. She felt terrible about it and did it anyway, which she later learned was practically the job description for saving the world."

"What did she steal?"

"A book. A grimoire. Full of the old covenant-lore — the stuff that held the border together, back when the border needed holding. Everyone wanted it. The Crown wanted it so they could seal the breach with a blood sacrifice. There was a man — not a man, something wearing a stolen face — who wanted it so he could unmake the border entirely. And this girl, this runaway thief with dirt under her nails and no plan whatsoever, was the only person alive who could read it properly."

"She sounds outnumbered."

"She was. Constantly. She got better at it." Reyes turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "There was a warden who hunted her. Captain Ilsa Reyes — you may have heard of her, she's moderately famous and insufferable about it."

The keeper laughed. "Was she handsome?"

"Very. Terrible cook, excellent at bridges."

"Bridges?"

"Later. Where was I. The girl gathered five stones — oath-stones, from the old garden-covenant — and each one took a price. Memories. That was the covenant's currency; it always had been. Her mother's voice. Her happiest day. And near the end—" Reyes kept her voice level, the way she'd practiced, the way she hadn't managed the first three times, "—near the end, the gorge-spirits collected a debt she owed them, and they took her memory of the warden. Of her friend. She woke up on the far side of that bargain and greeted her own friend like a stranger, and the friend had to stand there and smile and be a stranger, because that was the price, and the girl paid it without hesitation, because somewhere on a rope bridge she had decided that this warden's life was worth more than her remembering why."

The kitchen was quiet. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Out on the third terrace the moonflowers had opened fully, white faces turned toward stars that weren't quite there yet.

"What happened to her?" the keeper asked. Her voice had changed — smaller, careful, the voice of someone walking on ice and hearing it sing.

"At the last stone, she was offered a way out. Keep her memories. Seal the covenant a different way — a bargain that would have undone her enemy's exile, given him everything he wanted. All she had to do was say yes." Reyes looked at her. "She said no."

"Why?"

"Because it wasn't hers to give. Because the covenant was a promise made by other people, long ago, and she'd been chosen to keep it, and keeping it was the whole point of her." Reyes swallowed. "So the fifth stone took everything. Her mistress. Her name. Herself. And the covenant blazed back to life, and the stars relit, and every soul in two realms slept safely that night because a girl none of them would ever meet had paid for their sleep with everything she was."

The keeper's hands had gone still around her cup. She was staring at the middle distance, at nothing, at something.

"And the warden?" she said. "What happened to the warden?"

"She lost her rank for telling the truth at the worst possible moment. She gained a story she tells too often. And once a month, more or less, she walks up a mountain to drink tea with a gardener, and tell her about a brave apprentice named Odile, so that the girl isn't entirely gone." Reyes set down her cup. "So that somebody remembers her. Even if the somebody can't remember being remembered."

The keeper looked at her then — a long look, unblinking, and behind her eyes something moved. Not recognition. Nothing so kind. More like weather seen far off over a plain: a darkness gathering that might mean rain, might mean nothing at all.

"I don't know why," she said quietly, "but I think I might have liked her. This Odile."

"I think," Reyes said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it, because some prices deserved to be seen being paid, "she liked you too."

* * *

She stayed the night, as she always did, in the narrow bed the keeper kept made up without ever acknowledging that she knew why. In the morning there was bread and honey and the fox's insolent supervision, and the keeper walked her down to the first terrace through rows gone lush and riotous, past the stubborn moonflowers, past the little shrine-stone at the gate that hummed faintly when they passed, like a cat greeting a familiar footstep.

At the threshold the keeper stopped, and frowned, and said, "Wait. I have something for you. Or — no. Wait."

She hurried back to the cottage and returned breathless, carrying a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and held it out with both hands.

"This has been sitting on my shelf since before I can remember, which is not a long sentence," she said. "I keep almost giving it away and then not. Today I'm not going to not. Take it."

Reyes unwrapped it right there, because her hands were already shaking and pretending otherwise seemed pointless.

The grimoire.

Her mistress's grimoire — Odile's grimoire — the book that had started everything, bound in scarred leather, its spine stitched with thread that Reyes now knew concealed a letter. She had left it here on her second visit, against all sense, because the keeper had picked it up from her satchel and turned it over in her hands and said, *this feels like mine,* and Reyes had not been able to bear taking it back.

"You should keep it," Reyes said. "Really. It belongs—"

"With me. I know. I've always known, apparently, I just keep forgetting to know it." The keeper pressed it back into her arms and closed Reyes's fingers over it, and her touch lingered a half-second longer than courtesy required, and her brow creased. "But you take it today. I don't know why today. Something feels — imminent. Do you ever get that? Like the morning before weather?"

"More often than I'd like."

"Well." The keeper stepped back, wiping her hands on her apron, businesslike again, the flicker already fading from her face like a star settling. "Walk safely, Captain. Come back and tell me more about the girl. I find I'm greedy for her."

"You'll have the whole story next time. There's a part about a rope bridge you're going to hate."

"I hate all your stories," the keeper said warmly, "in the best possible way. Goodbye, Ilsa."

Reyes froze.

The keeper blinked. Tilted her head. "Did I — I don't know your name. Why do I know your name?" She touched her own lips, as if checking whether they'd acted without her. Then she laughed, uncertain and delighted and frightened, all three at once. "Well. How strange. How *wonderful.* Go on, go, before I start remembering things I haven't got."

Reyes walked down the mountain with the grimoire against her chest and her heart hammering like a fist on a door, and behind her, high in the terraces, the keeper stood watching her go — and above the garden, faint in the morning sky, one star that had no business being visible burned a little brighter than the rest.
