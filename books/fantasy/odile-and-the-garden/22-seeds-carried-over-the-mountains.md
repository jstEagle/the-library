## 22. Seeds Carried Over the Mountains

The border, these days, was a hedge.

Not a wall of iron, not a line of wardens with drawn blades — a hedge, waist-high and slightly ragged, running the length of the old Thornfell pass, planted by hand over one long spring by people who had every reason to hate each other and had decided, mostly, to plant anyway. Hawthorn and rowan and a stubborn groundcover of white flowers that no one could name and everyone agreed not to question. The flowers bloomed on both sides of the border at once, which the scholars said was impossible, and which the garden's keeper would have explained, if she could have explained anything at all.

She couldn't. She tended instead.

On the human side of the hedge, at a little post called the Tollgate of Two Bells, trade happened the way water finds its level: slowly, patiently, wearing grooves into stone. Fae honey in clay pots, sealed with wax that glimmered faintly. Human steel, honest and unenchanted. Bolts of moth-silk from the Otherfell looms that wove starlight into thread. In exchange: salt, seed-grain, candles, and — this had taken two years of negotiation and one small diplomatic crisis involving an offended dryad — wool socks. The Fae, it turned out, had never invented the sock. They had invented immortality, moonlit bargains, and songs that could unmake grief, but not the sock. The first shipment had caused something close to a riot in the Otherfell markets.

Bramble Hask had made a fortune on it, naturally.

* * *

In the tavern at Cindershade, which had been rebuilt twice since the burning and now had a sign reading THE UNLIT STAR — RESTORED, Bramble Hask stood on a table for the fourth time that week and told the story again.

"—and there she was," he said, arms wide, "cornered in the Drowned Chapel, up to her knees in black water, and Vesperin himself standing on the surface like it was a ballroom floor. And does she scream? Does she beg? No. She swears him an oath. 'I will give you the covenant,' says she, 'on my blood and my book' — and here's the clever part, friends, the part they don't teach you in any school — *she never said which covenant, and she never said when.*"

He paused, as he always did, to let the room lean in.

"Half a truth," someone called from the back. "Sworn whole."

"Half a truth sworn whole!" Bramble agreed, delighted, pointing his tankard at the speaker. "And it held him. It held him for a year while she walked the mountains gathering stones, because an oath is an oath even to the exiled, and he'd bound himself to wait for a gift she'd promised in words designed to mean nothing at all. Now—" he leaned down, conspiratorial, "—some of you have heard this before."

"*Twice*," said the barmaid, wiping a glass.

"Then you know it improves with telling." He drained the tankard and hopped down. "Because here's what I keep coming back to, all these months later. Everyone in that story gave something up. The warden gave her career. The apprentice gave—" he hesitated, as he always did here; the room went quiet, as it always did here, "—well. Everything. Her mistress, her mother, her happiest day, her own name. Every stone took its price and she paid it, right up to the last one, and do you know why?"

He didn't answer, because he never answered. That was the ritual. Someone else always did.

"Because somebody had to," said a miner near the fire, who had been in Cindershade the night it burned, who had watched a woman with soot on her cheeks sing children out of a forgetting-plague with hedge-magic and a stolen book.

"Because somebody had to," Bramble repeated softly. "So drink to her. Not to a saint — saints are boring, and she'd have hated being one. Drink to Odile Villalobos, thief, liar, apprentice, hero, who is not dead, whatever the Crown's records say, and who is tending a garden somewhere past the pass whether she knows her own name or not."

They drank. They always drank. And afterward, as the crowd thinned, the barmaid leaned across the bar and said, "You tell it different lately, Bramble. Softer."

"I got it wrong for a long time," he said. "Told it like a trickster's tale. All cleverness and escapes." He turned his tankard slowly on the wood. "But I knew her, you understand? Before. Sold her supplies, bought her pages, cheated her fair and she cheated me back. And what I remember isn't the cleverness. It's that she came back for my ledger when the plague hit Cindershade. My *ledger*, full of names of everyone I'd ever fenced goods for — half of them criminals, all of them mine — and she saved it off my burning shelf without being asked, because she knew I'd forget whose friend was whose." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Memory survives by being told. But somebody has to remember it first."

* * *

At the Warden Academy at Greywatch, Captain Ilsa Reyes — no longer captain, then briefly nothing at all, now Commandant, which was a longer word for the same trouble — stood before a hall of first-year wardens and did what she did every year on the first day.

She held up the old oath-book. Leather, gilt, older than anyone living.

"This," she said, "is the oath your parents swore. 'To the Crown, its borders and its laws, unto death.'" She let them look at it. Then she set it down on the table, picked up a single candle, and lit it. "This is the oath you'll swear instead. Say it after me: *I swear to the people behind me.*"

A hundred voices, uncertain, followed.

"*I swear to see clearly, even when the law is blind.*"

"*I swear to spend myself before I spend another.*"

"*And if ever the law demands a life to buy a border — I will find another way, or I will refuse, and I will bear the cost myself.*"

Silence. Somewhere in the back, a boy shifted nervously. Reyes found him with her eyes and held him there, gently.

"You're wondering what happens to a warden who refuses," she said. "Good. Wonder about it every day of your career. I'll tell you what happened to me: I lost my rank, my command, and eleven years of service, and I gained—" she paused, and the hall waited, because they always waited here too, "—a friend. A garden. And the knowledge that when the order came to put a girl on an altar, I didn't do it. That's the whole arithmetic. Write it down."

Afterward, her aide caught up with her in the corridor. "Commandant — dispatch from the pass. The autumn caravan crossed clean. First time both bells rang same-day since the reforging. Oh — and there's a package. From the Otherfell. Marked for you personally."

Reyes opened it in her office. Inside, wrapped in moth-silk, was a pair of socks. Finely woven. Starlight-grey. There was no note, but there didn't need to be; the stitching alone told her a Fae hand had made them, carefully, badly, learning. She laughed — a short, rusty sound, a door long unopened — and put them on, and wore them under her boots for the rest of the day for no reason she was willing to write in any report.

Her sister had gone beyond the old border thirty years ago, swallowed by the dark between the wards. Last spring, a trading party from the Otherfell had brought word of a woman in a far valley who remembered a human sister, a warden's badge, a promise made through the hedge before the world went dark. Reyes had not yet found the courage to cross and see her. She was getting closer. She kept the dispatch pinned above her desk, and every morning she read it, and every morning it got a little easier to breathe.

* * *

Autumn came to the First Garden the way it comes everywhere: gold, then rust, then rest.

The keeper walked the rows with her basket. She did not know her own name — there was no name in her anywhere, just a smooth warm place where one should be, like a stone worn blank by a river. She did not know she had ever been called anything else. She did not know the woman who had taught her magic, or the mother whose voice she'd traded away, or the happiest day of her life, or the warden whose memory she'd paid to the gorge-spirits, or the letter hidden in a book's binding that had told her *you were chosen, forgive me, forgive yourself.*

She knew the garden. That was the bargain's strange mercy: the stones had taken everything except this. The rows, the soil, the frost-timing, the way you talked to seedlings (firmly but kindly), the exact hour the light came through the eastern arch and woke the white flowers. She knew work. She knew the deep contentment of hands in earth.

And sometimes — this was new, this autumn — she knew other things, faintly, the way you know weather coming through a closed window. When traders crossed at the Tollgate of Two Bells, she felt a warmth pass through the hedge like a bell struck softly. When a story was told in a tavern far away — and stories traveled strangely along the covenant now, humming in the roots — she felt it as a kind of sunlight on her back, though she could not have said why, and did not try. Trying made her head ache. Not trying felt like standing in a doorway with the sun on one side and lamplight on the other.

Today a visitor had come. This was rare. Rarer still: the visitor was expected, though the keeper could not have said how she knew.

The woman came up the frozen path in a grey traveling cloak, wardens' boots, hair going silver at the temples, carrying a small pot with a seedling in it. She stopped at the gate and looked at the keeper for a long moment, the way you look at a house you grew up in.

"You won't know me," the woman said.

"No," the keeper agreed pleasantly. "But the gate opened for you. It doesn't do that for everyone."

"It doesn't do it for anyone else at all." The woman came in. Up close there were lines around her eyes that spoke of rope bridges and burning towns and hard choices, and also — newly — of socks worn under boots for private reasons. "My name is Ilsa Reyes. I'm told I was your friend."

The keeper considered this. "That sounds like something worth having been."

"It was." Reyes crouched, set the pot down, turned it so the seedling faced the light. "It still is, from my side. From yours—" she smiled, rueful, "—from yours it's a stranger showing up with a plant. Fair enough. I've done this before. I come maybe twice a year. I tell you stories. You don't have to believe them. Mostly you fall asleep around the third one, and I take that as a compliment, because you always did sleep through things you'd already decided were safe."

"Was I often safe?"

"With me?" Reyes looked out over the rows, the white flowers closing for the season, the hedge beyond, the mountains holding their peace. "Once, I fell off a rope bridge into a gorge full of spirits that collect debts. You caught me. Cost you more than either of us understood at the time. So yes. With me, you were safe. I intend to keep it that way in both directions."

The keeper knelt beside the pot. The seedling was a rowan — mountain ash, the tree that grows where nothing else consents to. She touched its leaves and felt, distantly, like a tune remembered from a room she'd never entered, that she had planted one of these before. Or been one. Or both.

"What's the story today?" she asked. "You said you tell stories."

Reyes settled onto the cold stone beside her, cloak tucked, comfortable, a woman planning to stay a while. "Today's the last chapter. The one where everything ends and begins. There's a garden, and a lord of ash, and a girl who gives up everything—"

"The girl in the stories," the keeper said. "Odile. Bramble Hask tells them too — I feel them come up through the roots, like sap. Everyone loves her."

"Yes."

"Do you think she was happy? At the end?"

Reyes was quiet a moment. "I think," she said finally, "that happiness isn't only remembering. I think it can live in the hands. In the work. In a gate that opens for a friend." She nodded at the seedling. "Plant that. It's from the Otherfell — a cutting grown from the rowan at the old crossing, the one your mistress planted the day the covenant was first sworn. Full circle. That's the whole point of gardens."

The keeper dug the hole with her hands, the way she liked to, feeling the cold soil give. She set the seedling in, pressed the earth around it, watered it from the winter-well. And as she worked, something stirred — not a memory, nothing so complete, but a warmth, a shape, the ghost of a feeling attached to no face and no name: *this has happened before, and it mattered, and someone stayed to watch it grow.*

She looked up at the stranger in the grey cloak, who was watching her with an expression the keeper had no word for but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a hearth in a window on a cold night.

"Will you come back in spring?" the keeper asked. "When it leafs out?"

"Every spring," Reyes said. "Count on it."

The keeper smiled. It was almost a smile — the way dawn is almost daylight, the way a seed is almost a tree. But it reached her eyes, and it stayed.

Above the peaks, the stars burned steady and bright, every one of them lit, keeping their ancient watch. And in the garden, in the dark soil, a seed that had crossed a border, a war, and a forgetting began, quietly and without any fuss at all, to grow.
