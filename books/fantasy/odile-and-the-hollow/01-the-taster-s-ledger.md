## 1. The Taster's Ledger

The seventh course was the killer.

Casper Iversen knew this before the silver dome even reached the high table, because he had counted the kitchen's knives that morning — forty-one, and one of them had been sharpened twice. A blade ground down twice in a single day meant a nervous hand, and nervous hands in the palace kitchens meant someone had been paid to be careful, which in Casper's long experience meant someone had been paid to be careless somewhere else.

He stood at the usurper's shoulder in his dove-grey livery, hands folded, face arranged into the pleasant blankness of a man who thought about nothing but sauces. The banquet hall of Vael's court blazed with two hundred candles and perhaps sixty honest faces; the arithmetic of the rest was less flattering. Courtiers leaned close to whisper behind fans and goblets, and every whisper in this room was a small transaction, a coin changing hands, a debt accruing interest.

Casper understood debts. He kept them for a living — two livings, if one counted honestly, which almost no one at this table did.

The plate arrived: quail in a dark glaze, its skin lacquered to a wet shine, arranged on a bed of candied roots dyed the pink of a healthy tongue. Beautiful. Beautiful food was the most dangerous kind, because it announced itself, and things that announce themselves want you looking at their face while their hands do the work.

Casper lifted the fork.

The first taste was fat and salt and something floral underneath — oleander honey, or an honest imitation of it. Fine. Then the second taste, from the meat near the bone, where heat pooled and concentrated whatever had been slipped into the cavity during roasting. There it was: a slow bitterness, green as unripe walnuts, with a cold edge that climbed the back of his throat like water running up a windowpane.

*Hellebore,* his body said, in the flat voice it used for such announcements. *And under it, something older. Something I don't have a name for.*

That second part was new, and Casper did not enjoy new things. His body was a ledger of poisons — twenty-two years in the taster's trade had written each of them into his blood, his liver, the gray callus of his nerves. Arsenic had taught him patience. Wolfsbane had taught him humility. The unnamed thing coiled beneath tonight's hellebore taught him nothing yet, only asked a question he could not answer, and questions without answers were how tasters died.

He swallowed anyway. That was the job. He set down the fork, inclined his head to King Vael — *the meal is fit, Your Radiance* — and watched the king take his first bite with the serene confidence of a man who believed the world owed him everything and had begun collecting.

Vael ate. The court exhaled. Somewhere in the rafters of the evening, the danger passed, or seemed to.

Casper's heart did not slow until well after the eighth course, and by then he was already elsewhere — already three rooms away and one floor down, in the part of himself where the other ledger lived.

* * *

The Hollow Guild kept no house anyone could find twice. Its vault was a cellar beneath a chandler's shop in the Sump, reached through a door that smelled of tallow and old rain, and Casper went there the way other men went to confession: alone, at night, carrying what they could not put down.

Inside, the vault breathed paper. Ledgers lined the walls in pigeonholes of blackened oak, thousands of them, each spine labeled in the Guild's cipher — not names, never names, but *commodities*. Bottled memories. Borrowed years. The last words of the drowned, sold by the vial to grieving families who wanted one more sentence from a mouth the river had closed. Impossible things, traded fairly, at ruinous prices, by people who understood that everything in the world was owed to something else.

Casper lit the lamp over the master desk, rolled his sleeves, and opened the current ledger to the night's entries.

His hand moved through the columns with the ease of long habit. *One memory of a mother's face, sold twice — flag the broker, the duplication will curdle.* *Three years borrowed against a cooper's son, interest compounding, due at midwinter.* *One name, purchased back in full.*

He paused at that last line, as he paused at it every time, though he would have cut off his own finger before admitting why.

*Name: Odile. Formerly: Odile of the Ninth Choir, priestess-broker of the Covenant of Salt and Smoke. Status: disgraced, unrecorded, erased from all rolls divine and mortal. Transaction: repurchase of own name, price agreed and paid across four years in installments. Balance remaining: one payment.*

Odile had come to the Guild the way broken people came to the Guild — quietly, hooded, with nothing left to pawn but the truth. She had not bought her name back to wear it. She had bought it back so that no one else could hold it, so that when she finally answered for whatever she had done — and Casper had read enough between her ledger lines to know she had done *something*, something large, something with gods in it — she would answer under her own weight and no one else's.

He respected that. He also feared it, the way one fears any debt large enough to change the shape of a life. And her final installment was due in nine days.

Casper dipped his pen. Beneath her entry he wrote the standard notation — *balance current, debtor reliable* — and then, because the vault was empty and the lamp was low and no one living would ever know, he added a smaller line beneath it in his own private shorthand:

*She pays her debts. Watch over the account.*

A foolish sentiment. Unprofessional. He told himself it was simply good bookkeeping to monitor a large outstanding balance, and did not believe himself, and wrote it anyway.

He was blotting the page when the lamps changed.

Not dimmed — *changed*, the flame bending sideways as though a door had opened somewhere in a room with no doors, and a smell moved through the vault that Casper knew from banquets and nowhere else: the cold green edge of the unnamed poison beneath the hellebore. It rolled through the paper and the oak and the dark, and every ledger in the vault shivered on its shelf like a flock of birds deciding together.

Then the master ledger turned its own page.

Casper did not move. Twenty-two years of discipline held him in his chair while the paper whispered over, and stopped, and lay open to a section he had never written in — the deep appendix at the back where the Guild recorded transactions not between mortals but *owed by heaven*: prayers made and never answered, miracles promised and withheld, the vast standing debt of the old gods, dormant now for a hundred years, since the covenant broke.

Someone had written in it. The ink was still wet. The hand was not his.

*Prayer received: ten thousand voices, Marrowgate district, offered to the Nine Who Sleeped. ANSWERED IN PROGRESS.*

Below that, in the same impossible script, a second line had begun to form itself, letter by letter, the way frost forms:

*Collateral called: one entry, taster's ledger, marked "watch over the account."*

Casper's blood went cold in the specific way it did around poisons, cataloguing the sensation even as it terrified him — the chill, the pressure behind the eyes, the taste of green walnuts rising from his own stomach. This was not hellebore. This was not anything a kitchen could brew. This was the vault itself reaching into his private notation, the small foolish mercy he had written for Odile, and holding it up to the light like collateral against a loan he had never knowingly made.

The gods were answering prayers again. And they had read his books.

The last letters finished themselves on the page, neat and patient as a moneylender's hand:

*Debt noted. Debtor located. Collection pending.*

Far above, through the floorboards, through the chandler's shop and the sleeping Sump, Casper heard bells begin to ring — not the alarm bells, not the temple bells, but all of them at once, every tower in the city, ringing out of time with one another like a heartbeat gone wrong.

Somewhere out there, ten thousand people were being answered. And somewhere closer, a woman with a repurchased name was about to learn that her account had been called.

Casper closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a room where something is breathing, and reached for his coat.

Nine days until Odile's final payment. He suspected none of them would get to keep them.
