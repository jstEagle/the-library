## 4. A Fairer Bargain

The crypt beneath Marrowgate had been drowning for three hundred years, and the water remembered everything it had ever touched.

Casper stood ankle-deep in the flooded nave, his boots long since ruined, his ledger held above the black water like a man holding a child out of a fire. The vaulted ceiling wept. Somewhere above, muffled by stone and silt and the bones of Marrowgate's drowned dead, Vael's army was hammering at the gates — and every blow landed with the sound of a stolen miracle, a prayer answered that no one had freely given.

Odile's lantern guttered at the far end of the nave, where the original covenant was carved into a slab of green basalt half-submerged in the flood. She was kneeling before it, sleeves pushed up, her hands moving over the old terms the way she might touch a wound she herself had opened.

"It's all here," she said, and her voice carried strangely in the water-hung air, flattened and holy at once. "Every clause. Every price. The gods bound themselves to answer only what was given freely — and mortals bound themselves to *ask* freely, without coercion, without desperation dressed up as devotion." Her fingers stopped on a line of script so old it predated the alphabet Casper knew. "And here is my clause. The broker's seal. I wrote this when I still believed a covenant could be kept by people."

"You were young," Casper said.

"I was ambitious," Odile corrected. "It isn't the same thing." She looked back at him across the water, and the lantern light caught the ruin of her face — the scarred cheek where the priesthood had burned off her name, the eyes that had watched him balance impossible accounts for two years without once flinching. "You don't have to do this, taster. There may be another way."

"There isn't." He said it gently, because he had learned from her that gentleness was not weakness; it was precision applied to soft things. "I've read the terms a hundred times since we came down here. The covenant needs collateral to be rewritten — something of equal weight to what the gods surrendered. Their freedom, weighed against—" He turned the ledger toward her, though she couldn't read it at this distance. He didn't need her to. "Mine. My immunities. Forty-one poisons, each one a small death I survived and carry. And the rest of my years. All of them. Paid forward as surety, so that every prayer answered under the new bargain is backed by a life already spent."

The water lapped at his shins. It was warm, which was worse than cold. Warm water in a crypt meant something living underneath it, something patient.

"Odile." He said her name — her true name, the one he'd bought back for her from the Hollow Guild two nights ago, the transaction recorded in his own hand: *one name, redeemed, paid in full.* "When I'm gone—"

"Don't." The word cracked. For a moment she was not the disgraced priestess who had brokered and betrayed the greatest bargain in history; she was only a woman standing in rising water, holding a lantern against the dark. "Don't give me a last speech, Casper. I've heard enough dying men talk. They always make it tidy. Nothing about you has ever been tidy."

"No," he agreed. "My ledgers are immaculate, but I am a mess." He smiled, and found, to his surprise, that it didn't hurt. "Then no speech. Just this: I spent my whole life tasting other people's food so they wouldn't die. Let me taste this too. Let me drink it down for them, one last time, and survive it the only way left to me — by becoming it."

* * *

He waded to the basalt slab and knelt in the flood beside Odile, close enough that their shoulders touched. The covenant rose out of the water around them like the ribs of a drowned god, and perhaps that was exactly what it was.

The bloodletting was simple. He had done it a thousand times in miniature — a nick behind the ear, a bead drawn into a vial, sold to the Guild as solvent for reading texts written in divine script. His poisoned blood could dissolve the old words; that was why the gods' original terms had hidden themselves from everyone but him. Every poison in his body was a key. Every immunity, a lock turned.

He opened his palm with the small knife from his boot — the one he'd used for twenty years to test whether the king's wine had been salted with nightshade — and let his fist hang over the submerged clauses.

The blood came slow and dark, threading down through the water like ink through milk. Where it touched the basalt, the old covenant *woke*. Lines of light crawled along the carvings, and the whole crypt exhaled — a sound like a cathedral sighing, like ten thousand years of unanswered prayers finally finding lungs.

"Terms," Casper said aloud, because contracts required witness, and there was no witness here but the water and the woman and whatever listened beneath. "Let the old gods hear. No prayer answered that was not freely asked. No miracle granted that purchases worship. No god compels, no mortal coerces. Divinity flows only where love or grief or honest need carries it — and the price of every answer falls on the giver and the god together, shared, as any fair debt must be."

He pressed his bleeding palm flat to the stone.

"And I post bond. Forty-one deaths survived, and every year I have left — taken as collateral against the gods' good faith. If they break these terms, they forfeit me. What remains of me. Which will be enough to unmake any god who lies."

The water went utterly still.

For a long moment nothing moved in the crypt — not the lantern flame, not the drip from the ceiling, not the black flood around their knees. Casper felt himself being *read*, the way he had spent his life reading dishes: swirled, sniffed, tested against the tongue of something vast. The forty-one poisons surfaced in his memory one by one, each with its night of fever, its hour of nearly dying, and he offered them up like coins counted onto a merchant's table.

*Paid,* said something that was not a voice so much as an agreement taking shape.

*Accepted,* said something older, deeper, almost tender.

The light in the carvings flared white, then sank into the basalt and vanished, and Casper felt the new covenant settle over the world like snowfall — silent, total, touching everything. Above them, distantly, the hammering at Marrowgate's gates changed pitch. Slowed. Faltered.

"What happened?" Odile whispered.

"The miracles just got expensive," Casper said. "Vael's been spending prayers he stole. Now every miracle costs the giver something real. And Vael—" He grimaced as a familiar sensation moved through him, the taste-tingle of a toxin meeting a body that knew it. "Vael has never given anything in his life."

* * *

They climbed out of the crypt as dawn came up gray over Marrowgate's walls, and they emerged into a world gone suddenly quiet in the way a fire goes quiet when the wind dies.

From the battlements they watched it. Vael's warcamp, vast as a city, began to come apart at the seams. The king rode forth wreathed in borrowed radiance — raw divinity leaking from his pores like smoke from a wet fire — and demanded the free cities kneel, and called upon his stolen wonders to prove him. And nothing answered.

The light guttered. The radiance peeled away from him in strips, revealing what Odile had seen in the warcamp and Casper had smelled in the tribute-wine: the rot beneath, the king hollowed out by everything he'd swallowed and never earned. Vael screamed at a sky that no longer owed him anything, and the sky, politely, did not listen. When the allied armies of the free cities swept down from the eastern hills, his soldiers looked at their miracleless king, looked at the dawn, and made the oldest calculation in the world.

By midmorning it was finished. By noon, Odile stood on Marrowgate's great gate with the council heads around her, and the crowd that had gathered did not see a disgraced priestess. They saw a woman who had confessed her betrayal publicly to save them, and who now, nameless no longer, raised her hands not in triumph but in invitation.

"We do not take back the old gods," she told them, her voice carrying to the walls and beyond. "We renegotiate. Fairly. Together. That work begins today, and it will outlast all of us — as all true bargains should."

Casper watched from the shadow of the gatehouse, leaning on the stone, feeling each of his remaining hours the way a miser feels coins slipping through a torn purse. Not painful. Just *counted*, now. Everything accounted for.

She found him there at dusk, when the celebrating had softened into singing. She didn't speak. She took his hand — the cut palm — and turned it over, and saw the skin already closing, already clean, as if even his wounds understood bookkeeping now.

"The final entry," she said quietly.

"In my coat. Left pocket." He managed the smile again. "I wrote it this morning. You'll want to check my figures. You always thought my margins were sentimental."

Odile laughed, and it broke in the middle, and she pressed her forehead to his and stayed there while the bells of Marrowgate rang out the first evening of a renegotiated world.

Later — much later, when the bells had gone quiet and the singing had thinned to embers — she reached into his coat with steady hands and unfolded the single page he'd left there. His handwriting, immaculate as ever. One line, centered, dated, signed:

*Casper Iversen. All debts settled. All poisons survived. All years given freely.*

*Paid in full.*

Below it, in fresh ink that had not been there when he wrote it — ink that gleamed faintly gold in the lamplight, as though the page itself had been answered — a second line had appeared, in a script older than alphabets:

*Received, and witnessed. The account stands open in your name, taster, for as long as the world keeps its bargains.*

*The first payment comes due at dawn.*

Odile looked up sharply toward the cot where Casper slept — really slept, deeply, impossibly, breathing slow and even, his pulse strong beneath her fingers when she checked it twice.

Outside the window, the sun was beginning to rise.
