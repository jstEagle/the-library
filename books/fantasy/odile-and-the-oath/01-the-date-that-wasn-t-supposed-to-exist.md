## 1. The Date That Wasn't Supposed to Exist

The whalebone had been complaining all afternoon.

Vesper Ishikawa held it up to the lamplight and turned it slowly, the way you'd turn a key in a stubborn lock. The bone was old — salvaged from a ship's rib that had come apart on the Greyhallow shoals forty years back — and the dead inside it were old too, or at least they sounded old, the way voices went grainy when they'd been rattling around in ivory for decades.

*Too cold,* one of them muttered. *Always too cold in this shop. When I was alive we heated our houses properly. Seal oil lamps in every room.*

"You didn't have seal oil lamps," Vesper said, not looking up from her knife. "You told me last week you were a fisherman. Fishermen don't heat anything."

*I was a fisherman with standards.*

She smiled despite herself. This was the trick of it, the thing no one warned you about when the gift arrived: the dead weren't terrifying. They were tedious. They gossiped, they complained, they repeated themselves, and after three years of listening to them through every scrap of bone she carved, Vesper had developed the particular patience of a woman who worked in a room full of chattering relatives she couldn't evict.

The charm in her hands was small — a luck-piece for a bride, no bigger than a thumbnail, carved from whalebone into the shape of a knot that had no beginning and no end. Bride's knots were her steadiest sellers. Nervous brides bought them by the dozen, and nervous brides paid well, and nervous brides never asked why the carver flinched sometimes when she touched her own work.

*It's crooked,* said the fisherman.

"It's not crooked."

*The left loop is thinner than the right. In my day a carver would have been ashamed.*

"In your day you fished with nets made of hair, according to you, which I don't believe either."

A different voice slid in beneath the first, softer, a woman's voice with salt in it: *Let her work, Tomas. She's doing fine.* This was Maren, who had lived in this particular piece of whalebone longer than anyone, and who had appointed herself Vesper's defender sometime during the second year. *The girl knows what she's about.*

"I'm twenty-six," Vesper said. "I'm hardly a girl."

*You're a girl,* Maren said serenely. *I was married at nineteen. Everything under thirty is a girl.*

Vesper set down her knife and blew bone dust from the knot. It was good work, even if she said so herself. The loops matched. The grain ran true. And threaded through the center, invisible unless you knew to look, was a single word she'd carved so fine it was more suggestion than letters: *stay*. Luck-charms worked better with a word in them. Everyone knew that. What nobody knew — what Vesper would take to her grave, or more likely to the Warden's pyre — was that the words only worked because she asked the dead to say them. A charm without a voice in it was just jewelry. A charm with a voice was a small promise the universe occasionally bothered to keep.

Three years she'd been careful. Three years of charms small enough to slip past the Wardens' notice, of never carving anything larger than a fist, of keeping her ledgers coded and her door locked and her gift folded up inside her like a knife carried edge-inward. Greyhallow burned bone-speakers. Everyone knew that. The magic laws hadn't been relaxed since the long winter began, and the long winter had begun before Vesper's mother was born.

Her mother. Vesper put the thought down the way you put down something hot.

*The bride will be happy,* Maren said. *Tell her to carry it over her heart, not in her pocket. Pockets are where lost things go.*

"That's actually good advice."

*I contain multitudes.*

There was a knock at the shop door — three quick raps, the pattern her regulars used, because a plain knock after dark meant Wardens and everyone in the Saltrow district knew it. Vesper wrapped the charm in waxed cloth, tucked it into her coat, and blew out the lamp before opening up.

Outside, the evening had come down blue and bitter. Marta Kell stood on the step in a wedding shawl that was doing heroic work against the wind, her fiancé hovering behind her with the expression of a man who had been talked into this errand and regretted everything.

"Marta." Vesper pressed the bundle into her hands. "Over the heart. Not the pocket."

"Pockets are where lost things go," Marta recited, because Vesper told every bride this and every bride repeated it back like scripture. "Oh, thank you, thank you — I couldn't sleep last night, I just kept thinking, what if the ice takes him on the crossing, what if—"

"He'll be fine." Vesper heard it before she said it — the faint chorus in the wrapped bone, already murmuring its little promise, *stay, stay, stay* — and she wondered, as she always did, whether she was lying. Whether any of it was real, or whether she'd spent three years selling anxious women the sound of her own dead. "The charm helps. But mostly he'll be fine because the crossing is four miles and he's too stubborn to drown."

Marta laughed wetly, paid triple the asking price because brides always did, and let her fiancé steer her away down the lane. Vesper watched them go, then closed the door and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the shop settle. Somewhere in the scrap bin, the offcuts whispered to each other. They always got louder at night. She'd learned to sleep through it the way sailors sleep through waves.

Tonight, though, tonight she had somewhere to be.

* * *

The night market on Tallow Street opened at moonrise and closed at suspicion, which is to say it had no fixed hours and dissolved whenever anyone shouted the word *Warden*. Vesper had been coming for three years, mostly to buy bone — legal bone, shipbone and cattlebone with clean papers, though half the stalls sold neither — and partly because it was the one place in Greyhallow where she could walk through a crowd and feel almost ordinary. Almost anonymous. Everyone at the night market was hiding something, which meant nobody looked twice at anybody.

She was haggling over a rib of elk — *you want how much? I've seen elk ribs with less worm in them sell for half* — when someone said her name.

"Vesper Ishikawa."

She turned with the ease of long practice, the polite blankness of a woman who was absolutely nobody interesting. The woman behind her was perhaps thirty, wrapped in a scholar's grey cloak that had seen better decades, with ink stains on her fingers and a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of her face, as if it had been sent ahead to scout.

"You have the advantage of me," Vesper said.

"Ilsa Vane." The woman stuck out an ink-stained hand. "I've been looking for you for six weeks. Do you know how hard it is to find a bone-carver in this city who doesn't advertise? You're like smoke. Your customers won't even say your name out loud."

"There's a reason for that."

"The laws, yes." Ilsa waved this off as though the laws were a minor administrative detail, a toll bridge, weather. "I'm not a Warden. I'm a reader at the Athenaeum — well." A flicker crossed her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Formerly of the Athenaeum. These days I consult privately. And I've been consulting my way toward you, because I think you're going to want to see something."

"Whatever you're selling, I carve my own."

"I'm not selling." Ilsa leaned closer. Up close her eyes were a pale, startling grey, the color of the sea before it decides to kill you. "I found a book."

"People find books constantly. It's a library city."

"This book has tomorrow's date written on its last page."

The wind came down Tallow Street and moved through the market, guttering lanterns, stirring the tarpaulins, carrying the smell of fried eel and tallow smoke. Vesper became aware that she was still holding the elk rib, and that the dead in it had gone quiet — which they never did, not unless something was worth listening to.

"What date?" she asked.

"Tomorrow's."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Ilsa agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Come have a drink and I'll give you the whole sentence."

Every instinct Vesper owned — three years' worth of careful, folded-up, edge-inward instinct — told her to hand back the elk rib, pay the man, and go home. Curious people died in Greyhallow. Curious people got noticed, and noticed people got named in reports, and named people got burned. Her mother had taught her that before she vanished, and her mother's vanishing had taught it again, harder.

But the dead in the elk rib had started whispering again, and what they were whispering was: *go, go, go* — and in three years of listening to bones complain about the temperature, Vesper had never once heard them agree about anything.

"All right," she heard herself say. "One drink."

* * *

Ilsa's rooms were above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, one large cold chamber stacked with books in towers that defied both gravity and fire code. She lit a spirit-lamp, poured something amber and illegal into two clay cups, and pulled a volume down from the third tower with the reverence of a priest handling relics.

It was unremarkable at first glance. Leather gone stiff and dark, corners worn round, the kind of book that had survived by being ignored. The title page read *Concerning the Hollow Crown: A True and Faithful Account of the Oath of Winter*, and beneath it a publication date a hundred years old.

"A chronicle," Vesper said. "Half the attics in Greyhallow are full of these. Everyone's grandfather wrote one during the famine years."

"Everyone's grandfather wrote one, yes. But look at the last page."

Isla opened it to the end and turned it around.

The page was dense with handwriting — not the printer's type of the front matter but a later hand, cramped and urgent, filling margins and then spilling onto a final leaf that had clearly been added long after binding. Dates marched down it in a column. Vesper read them without meaning to, the way you read a list of the dead:

*Third of Frostmoon, the king coughs blood.*
*Ninth of Deepwinter, the crown speaks aloud in court.*
*First of Thawmoon — nothing. Nothing happens. The winter holds.*

And then, at the bottom, in the same hand but slower, heavier, as if the writer had been pressing hard enough to carve:

*Tomorrow's date. The bone-speaker comes to Greyhallow. Odile. The choice is made.*

Below that, a name.

Not a description. Not a title. A name, written plainly, in ink that had dried a century ago:

**VESPER ISHIKAWA.**

The room did something strange. It tilted, very slightly, the way a ship tilts, and Vesper found she was gripping the edge of Ilsa's table with both hands and the illegal drink was untouched in front of her and the dead — the dead were silent. All of them. Every scrap of bone in her coat, every whisper she'd carried since childhood, gone quiet, as if they too were reading over her shoulder.

"That's—" Her voice came out wrong. She tried again. "That's impossible. That ink is old. You can see it's old. Someone wrote my name a hundred years ago."

"Yes," Ilsa said softly.

"But I wasn't—" She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I wasn't born. My parents weren't born. Whoever wrote this couldn't have known my name. Names aren't — you can't prophesy a *name*, names are arbitrary, my mother chose it—"

"Your mother chose it," Ilsa said, "because her mother chose hers. It's an old family name, Ishikawa. Bone-workers, going back generations. Going back, if the parish records I've spent six weeks reading are accurate, to exactly the generation that wrote this book." She tapped the page, gently, beside the name. "Whoever wrote this didn't see the future, Vesper. They saw a family. Yours. And they waited a hundred years for it to arrive."

Vesper stared at the name until it stopped looking like letters and started looking like a trap sprung shut.

*Odile,* the page said. *The choice is made.* She didn't know the word. She wanted, badly, to not ask.

But Ilsa was watching her with those grey eyes, patient as tide, and the silence in her bones had curdled into something worse than whispers, and tomorrow's date sat at the bottom of a dead man's page like a hook sitting in water.

"What's the choice?" Vesper asked.

Ilsa smiled — that smile that arrived early, sent ahead to scout — and reached for the bottle.

"Drink first," she said. "It's a long story, and I don't think you're going to sleep tonight anyway."

Later — much later, walking home through streets gone white-rimmed with frost, her breath hanging around her head like a veil — Vesper would realize she'd never actually gotten a straight answer. She'd gotten history, and genealogy, and a hundred years of a dead chronicler's increasingly frightened marginalia, and somewhere in the middle of it, the word *odile* had acquired a shape she couldn't yet see.

What she remembered most, afterward, was the last thing Ilsa said at the door, cheerfully, as if commenting on the weather:

"Oh — and you should probably know. The book says the bone-speaker arrives tomorrow. Which means whoever wrote those Wardens' reports about you, the ones that started circulating this week — they're ahead of schedule."

Vesper stood in the frozen street.

"My what?"

"Sleep well," said Ilsa Vane, and closed the door.
