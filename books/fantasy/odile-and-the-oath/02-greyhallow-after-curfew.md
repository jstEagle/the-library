## 2. Greyhallow After Curfew

The ledgers burned badly.

Vesper had expected them to go up like festival paper, all flame and glory, but old ink clung to the page. She fed the third ledger into her stove a sheet at a time, watching each entry curl and blacken — *Marta Voss, two charms, paid in bread. The cooper's boy, one tooth-charm, unpaid, forgive him. The widow Hesse, monthly, grief-work, do not ask what for* — three years of careful small lives going up in smoke that smelled like tallow and regret.

Outside her window, Greyhallow was doing what it always did at dusk: pulling its shutters in, banking its fires, pretending to be smaller than it was. Curfew bells would ring within the hour. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones, late, its driver hurrying.

She should have left this morning.

She knew that. She'd known it since Ilsa pressed the book into her hands last night and she'd seen tomorrow's date sitting beside her own name like a knife laid on a table. A sensible woman would have run at first light, out the Salt Gate before the toll-keepers finished their tea, gone east toward the river towns where nobody carved bone and nobody read prophecies.

But Vesper Ishikawa had spent three years building an ordinary life out of extraordinary materials, and ordinary lives don't come apart in a morning. There were clients expecting deliveries. There was the widow Hesse, who couldn't sleep without her charm. There was the stash of whalebone under the floorboards that would take hours to move, and moving it wrong would tell any Warden with eyes exactly what she was.

So instead of running, she was burning evidence and packing by lamplight, and telling herself the story careful people always tell themselves: that she had time. That the Crown's reach was long but slow. That a woman who sold luck-charms the size of a thumbnail could slip through fingers built for catching sorcerers.

The dead in the whalebone disagreed.

They'd been restless all day — not the usual murmuring complaints, the eternal petty grievances of people with unfinished business, but something sharper. Fear, maybe, or excitement. She'd learned to tell the difference the way you learn weather: not by listening to any single voice but by the pressure of the whole sky.

Tonight the whole sky said *run*.

"I'm running," she told them, wrapping a carving knife in wool. "I'm running as fast as a woman can pack."

The dead didn't answer in words. They never did, exactly. They answered in pressure, in the itch along her jaw where the bone touched her skin, in the way a particular voice would rise above the chorus when it wanted something. Right now the loudest voice belonged to a drowned fisherman whose femur she'd bought legally, he'd insisted on that even in death, from a licensed chandler — and the fisherman's fear tasted like brine and iron.

Like blood, almost.

She stopped packing.

Three years in Greyhallow had taught her to trust that taste. It meant someone nearby was bleeding, or about to be. Usually it meant nothing — a butcher's shop two streets over, a drunk falling down stairs. But tonight, with tomorrow's date written in a century-old book beside her name, with the dead pressing against the inside of her skull like water against a dam—

She blew out her lamp and went to the window.

The street below was emptying. Last stragglers hurrying home, a lamplighter making his rounds with his long pole, the amber glow of the corner shrine where old women left offerings to whatever saint protected doorways. Normal. All of it normal.

Except for the man standing in the shadow of the cooper's awning across the street.

He wasn't doing anything suspicious. That was what made him suspicious. He was just standing there, hands folded, patient as furniture, in the posture of someone being paid to wait. And when the curfew bell began to ring — six strokes, then silence, then the city holding its breath — he didn't leave.

Nobody stayed out after curfew except Wardens and fools.

Vesper stepped back from the window slowly, heart hammering, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all day. The scholar had found her too easily. Three years of careful anonymity, charms small enough to slip under the law's notice, no name on any register — and one night at a market, and a stranger with a book of prophecies knew exactly which stall sold bone-carvings, knew exactly who to ask for. Someone had pointed Ilsa to her. Or someone had pointed the Wardens.

Or both had been pointed by the same hand.

The fisherman's fear rose again, sharp as gull-cry. She pressed her palm flat against the wall and let herself hear him properly, the way she usually refused to, the way that cost her sleep for days after.

*"Below,"* the dead man said. *"They're coming up. They always come up."*

Wardens entered buildings from the roof when they could. Everyone knew that. Nobody remembered it until too late.

Vesper grabbed her satchel — not the packed one, the go-bag she kept under the bed for exactly this night, because a woman who lived illegally prepared for this night — and looked around her little room one last time. The stove still smoking with ledger-ash. The whalebone under the floorboards, impossible to take, impossible to explain. The half-finished bride's charm on the workbench, wings half-carved, waiting for its blessing.

She left it all. You can't outrun a prophecy carrying luggage.

The roof access was through the attic, a hatch her landlord didn't know existed because she'd cut it herself two years ago and patched the frame with wax every month since. She was halfway up the ladder when the front door below exploded inward.

Not knocked. Not politely forced. *Exploded*, hinges shrieking, boots on stairs, four men and a woman taking the steps two at a time while another pair — she heard them now, the scrape and thud — came over the roofline behind her like spiders dropping on silk.

"Roof," she breathed, uselessly, to nobody. To the dead. To herself.

A boot came through the hatch before she reached it, splintering wood, and a hand closed on her wrist — gloved, strong, impersonal as a manacle.

"Warden's business," said the woman attached to the hand, hauling Vesper up through her own escape route into the open air. "Don't make it worse than it is."

The rooftop was slick with frost. Two more Wardens stood by the chimney, lanterns hooded, faces shadowed. Below, through the broken hatch, she could hear the others turning her rooms inside out with terrible efficiency — drawers lifted, floorboards pried, the particular sound of her life being inventoried.

"The floorboards," one of them called down. "There's bone under here. Whalebone. A lot of it."

"Seal it," the woman said. She hadn't let go of Vesper's wrist. Her grip was professional, not cruel, which somehow made it worse. "Name?"

"You know my name," Vesper said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You came with it."

"Confirmation is procedure." The woman turned her slightly, studying her face in the hooded lantern light with the dispassion of a horse-trader. "Vesper Ishikawa. Bone-speaker. Daughter of—"

"Don't." The word came out of her like a reflex, fast and hard. "Don't say her name."

Something flickered behind the Warden's eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or the memory of recognition. "Someone knew your mother's craft," she said instead, quietly. "Someone who saw the work recognized the hand. That's how we found you. Not the market. Not the scholar. The *work*."

The betrayal landed sideways, the way real betrayals do — not through the front door but through some crack she'd never thought to guard. Not Ilsa, then. Or not only Ilsa. Some client, some friend, some neighbor who'd held one of her charms and thought: *I've seen this before, decades ago, in the hands of a woman who vanished.* And had sold that thought to the Crown for whatever the Crown pays for thoughts.

Three years. Undone by a carving style.

"Where are you taking me?" she asked.

"Where you're needed."

"That's not an answer."

"No," the Warden agreed. "It isn't."

They bound her wrists with cord, not iron — she noted that, filed it away in the part of her mind that never stopped working, the part that had already survived three years by noticing things. Iron was for criminals. Cord was for assets. You didn't shackle something you intended to keep intact.

They brought her down through her own ruined attic, past her ransacked rooms, past the widow Hesse's charm lying on the workbench where a Warden was turning it over with tongs like a dead rat. In the street, a black wagon waited, unmarked, its driver faceless behind a leather hood.

And leaning against the wagon's side, wrists also bound, face pale and furious in the lantern light, stood Ilsa.

"They took me from my lodgings," the scholar said, before Vesper could speak. "Twenty minutes ago. Same procedure. They were waiting for us both." Her laugh was thin and bitter. "I told you the book was dangerous. I didn't think I was in it."

"Were you?" Vesper asked. "In it? Or did you walk yourself into it and drag me after you?"

Ilsa flinched. Just barely. But enough.

The Wardens loaded them into the wagon without ceremony. It smelled of straw and old fear — other passengers had ridden in this dark before, and their terror had soaked into the wood. The door slammed. Bolts fell. The wagon lurched forward over the cobblestones.

In the darkness, Ilsa's breathing was quick and shallow. "Vesper," she whispered. "Whatever happens—"

"Not now."

"But—"

"*Not now.*"

Because Vesper was listening.

The wagon's walls were oak, old oak, and oak holds nails, and nails hold iron, and iron near a bone-speaker carries echoes the way a bell carries sound. There had been a Warden once — she could feel him in the fittings, in the brackets where lanterns used to hang, in the ring-bolt set into the floor. A dead man's bones had been ground into the iron of this wagon's making, or his relics sealed beneath its boards, some old practice from when the Wardens honored their dead instead of hiding them.

He was faint. Fainter than the fisherman, fainter than any voice she'd heard in years. But he was there, and he was a Warden, and Wardens know things.

*"...they want you alive..."*

The words came through like light through deep water, broken, refracted, but unmistakable. Vesper went very still.

*"...alive... the orders say alive... both of them... harm voids the writ... they want you alive, girl, and that is worse..."*

"What?" Ilsa whispered. "What is it? Your face changed."

Vesper stared into the darkness where the dead Warden's voice lived in the iron. Alive. Not killed on sight, not dragged to a pyre like the magic laws demanded. Kept. Preserved. Delivered somewhere intact, like a parcel, like a relic, like a thing with a purpose too valuable to damage.

The magic laws burned bone-speakers. Every child in Greyhallow knew the rhyme. So why did the writ say *alive*?

Unless the Crown needed a bone-speaker for something. Unless the prophecy book was real, and the date was real, and the name was real, and someone above the Wardens — someone with power over writs and wagons and the fate of kingdoms — had been waiting a generation for her mother's craft to surface again.

Her mother, who hadn't fled the city after all, maybe. Who had vanished the way people vanish when powerful people want them quietly.

"They want us alive," Vesper said aloud. The words tasted strange, like swallowing ice. "That's what the bones say. The dead Warden in this wagon — he says the orders are clear. Alive, both of us. Harm voids the writ."

Ilsa made a small sound in the dark. "Alive is better than dead."

"Alive is *worse*," Vesper said. "Dead, they'd have no use for me. Alive means I'm worth something to them. Alive means somebody wants something." She flexed her bound hands, feeling the cord bite. "And whatever they want, it has to do with why a hundred-year-old book has my name on its last page."

The wagon turned — she felt it in the sway, counted the turns automatically, mapping the route the way her mother had taught her to map everything. Down toward the river district first. Then not toward the Warden barracks, which sat on high ground to the west. Instead the road climbed. Climbed and climbed, wheels grinding on stone that grew smoother, older, better-made than any common street.

Toward the palace hill.

Ilsa felt it too. "That's not the way to the cells," she breathed. "The cells are downhill. Everything legal is downhill."

"Then we're not going somewhere legal."

Through the wagon's shuttered vent, a sliver of the city slid past — rooftops giving way to walls, walls giving way to gates, gates hung with banners she couldn't quite see in the dark but could feel, somehow, the way you feel a storm before it breaks. The dead in the iron had gone quiet, the dead Warden retreating into his fittings like a man stepping back from a threshold he daren't cross.

*Beyond this point,* his silence said, *even I cannot follow.*

The wagon stopped. Voices outside — formal, layered, the clipped exchange of passwords and seals. Then the bolts drew back, and cold clean air poured in, and torchlight, and the silhouette of a courtyard walled in pale stone that glowed faintly, faintly, like bone.

"Out," said the woman Warden. "Both of you. Hands where we can see them."

Vesper climbed down into the palace courtyard on legs that felt borrowed. Above her, towers rose into the freezing dark, windows lit gold, and at the highest tower — the old one, the crown-tower, the one every child in Greyhallow could name — a single window burned with a light that flickered wrong. Too blue. Too steady. Like frost given a wick.

Somewhere up there, she understood without knowing how, something was wearing a king.

The dead Warden's warning echoed in her bound hands, in the iron of her cuffs, in the marrow of her:

*They want you alive.*

And alive, Vesper thought, as the doors opened and the warmth of the palace swallowed her whole — alive was exactly what she was afraid of.
