## 3. The Inquisitor's Kindness

They gave her tea.

That was the first thing wrong with it. Vesper had spent three years learning what the Crown did to charm-sellers in Greyhallow: the holding cells under the Warden barracks with their drains in the floor, the fines that became indentures, the quiet disappearances of anyone whose craft touched bone. She had expected a cell. She got a room with a window — barred, but a window — and a table, two chairs, and a pot of tea still steaming when they sat her down at it.

The woman across from her was perhaps forty, grey at the temples in a way that looked earned rather than grown into, wearing the slate-and-silver of the Inquisitorial Office rather than a Warden's black. Her warrant chain was gold, which meant she answered to the palace and not to the city magistrates, which meant Vesper was no longer being processed as a petty criminal. Petty criminals did not get gold chains.

"I'm Serane Hale," the woman said. "Inquisitor Second Rank." She poured the tea herself, unhurried, and set the cup on Vesper's side of the table. "You were arrested an hour ago. You've been searched, your effects catalogued, and your companion is being held separately. None of that has to matter."

Vesper kept her hands in her lap where they couldn't shake visibly. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning that I have questions and you have answers, and if we get through them tonight, tomorrow can look very different from what you're imagining right now." Hale sat, folded her hands, and regarded her with an expression Vesper could not place for several seconds because she'd never been on this end of it before. It was patience. Genuine, unhurried patience, like a person watching bread rise.

The dead would have told her what this woman was. The dead always knew things about the living that the living didn't know about themselves. But there was no ivory here, no bone within reach except her own, and hers had never spoken to her — only to everyone else who'd ever worn it after. For the first time since childhood, Vesper Ishikawa sat inside a silence so complete it felt like a kind of nakedness.

She drank the tea. It was good. That was the second thing wrong with it.

* * *

"You carve," Hale said, opening a folder. Not a ledger, not a confession book — a folder, soft-backed, the kind scholars used. "Whalebone mostly. River-ivory when you can get it. You buy your stock from a fishmonger on Candlerow named Brannock who thinks you make buttons." She turned a page without looking at it. "You sell charms. Luck-knots for brides, fever-bone for mothers, small protections against small harms. Nothing the Wardens' schedule of proscribed arts covers. You have been, by any honest measure, careful."

"If you know all that," Vesper said, "why am I here?"

"Because last night you read a page you weren't meant to see, and the person who showed it to you has been watched for four months." Hale closed the folder. "Ilsa Verren. Or whatever her real name turns out to be once we finish digging. Do you know how long we've known about her?"

"I met her yesterday."

"We know. That's what made yesterday interesting." Something moved behind the inquisitor's eyes — not triumph, nothing so crude. More like a door opening onto a long corridor. "Do you understand what a bone-speaker is, Miss Ishikawa?"

"A story," Vesper said. "Something old women tell to frighten apprentices off grave-goods."

"It's something my office has hunted for twenty-six years." Hale said it flatly, the way people report weather or casualties. "I inherited the file when I took my rank. My predecessor inherited it from hers. Every generation, someone reports a carver whose charms work too well, and every generation we dig through the reports and find nothing — a fraud, a coincidence, a nervous imagination. Twenty-six years of nothing. And then four days ago a fishmonger mentions that the button-girl buys rib-bone, and one of my Wardens remembers his grandmother's stories, and we start pulling thread."

Vesper thought of Brannock's broad hands wrapping riverbone in oilcloth, his cheerful ignorance. *I hope they don't hang him,* some distant part of her thought absurdly. *He genuinely believes I make buttons.*

"And the thread led to me."

"The thread led to a name written in a book that predates your birth by eighty years, in a hand that predates your mother by sixty." Hale leaned forward slightly. "Tell me what you heard, Miss Ishikawa, when you opened that book."

"Nothing. There was nothing to hear. Paper doesn't speak."

"No," Hale agreed softly. "But you went white as tallow, knocked over Ilsa Verren's lamp, and asked her where she got it. We have the testimony of the tea-stall keeper across the lane. He thought you'd seen a ghost." A pause. "Perhaps you had."

The room was very warm. That was wrong too; cells should be cold. Vesper wrapped both hands around the cup and felt the heat ground her, the way the weight of a burin grounded her, the way the first thin voice out of a fresh-cut piece of whalebone had grounded her when she was nine years old and terrified of the thing growing inside her skull.

"My mother was a carver," she said, because refusing to say anything was its own kind of confession. "People talked. People always talk about carvers."

"Your mother was more than a carver. Your mother was taken to the palace eleven years ago and never came out, and the file on her is sealed above my rank, which is a sentence I have never before had reason to say about a bone-carver's daughter." Hale's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "You are not in trouble for selling luck-charms, Miss Ishikawa. You are in trouble because a hundred-year-old prophecy knows your name, and the Crown would very much like to know how."

* * *

They brought the book.

Two guards carried it between them on a tray, like something wounded. It was bound in vellum gone the color of old teeth, clasped shut with bone fittings that made Vesper's skin crawl before she consciously recognized why: they were carved in a style she knew, the tight spiral-work of the northern ateliers, the same grammar her own burin followed without her permission. Whoever had bound this book had learned from the same tradition her mother had. From the same tradition *she* had.

Hale unlocked the clasps with a small key from her chain and turned to the final leaf.

Vesper had seen it once already, lamplit, in Ilsa's shaking hands. Under the Warden lanterns it was worse. The ink had gone brown with age, but the letters stood sharp, a prophecy in the formal cadence the old ateliers used for oaths — the cadence that *bound*, if you believed half the stories, and Vesper had stopped disbelieving half the stories around the time she started hearing the dead complain about their funerals.

She read it again anyway. She couldn't stop herself.

*When the crown grows hollow and the king grows thin,*
*a speaker of bone shall come to the winter's door.*
*By her hand the Hollow King shall be unmade,*
*or by her hand he shall be wed.*
*There is no third road. The oath allows no third road.*

And beneath it, in the same ancient ink, the date. Tomorrow's date. And beside the date, in letters that had dried eighty years before Vesper Ishikawa drew her first breath:

*Ishikawa.*

"That's impossible," Vesper said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Inside, something was running in circles, screaming. "Names can be added later. Ink can be forged. Anyone could have—"

"The parchment has been tested twice this year. Once by the Royal College, once by an outside assessor we drowned in bribes and threats until we trusted him. The inscription is contemporaneous with the rest of the manuscript." Hale turned the page back with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Eighty years ago, someone wrote your surname next to a date that hadn't happened yet. Eighty years ago, someone knew a family called Ishikawa would matter."

"Then they knew my mother's family. This is about her—"

"Your mother arrived at the palace eleven years ago. The prophecy is older than that. Whatever the writer saw, they saw further back than her, and further forward than her." Hale folded her hands again. "Which brings us to the part I suspect you'll find less pleasant than the archaeology."

"There's a part I'll find pleasant?"

For the first time, something almost like humor touched the inquisitor's mouth, and vanished. "The Hollow King," she said, "is our term. Not the prophecy's. The prophecy says *the king beneath the crown*. Do you know what that refers to?"

Everyone knew the shape of it, even if no one said it aloud. Nine years ago, Aldric Corvane had been crowned in midwinter, in the old rite, the one with the oath-swearing that the modern court performed only as ceremony. Nine years ago the winter had broken early and held off ever since, and Greyhallow had prospered, and the king had grown thinner every year of it, thinner and quieter, until people joked nervously that the Crown ate its men. Everyone knew the shape. Nobody knew the substance.

"I know the stories," Vesper said carefully.

"The stories say he bargained." Hale watched her face while she said it, cataloguing, the way Vesper herself catalogued a new piece of bone for flaws before cutting. "The stories say the long winters were breaking us — the famine years, before your birth. Starvation in the lower wards, whole streets gone quiet. And the young king swore something at his coronation. Swore it on the old terms, with the old words, and whatever he swore to *accepted*, and it has been keeping the winter off ever since, and it has been eating him ever since, and the Crown has spent nine years pretending none of this is true because the alternative is admitting the throne of Greyhallow is currently occupied by two parties."

The tea had gone cold in Vesper's hands. She set it down so it wouldn't rattle against the saucer.

"Why are you telling me this? You're an inquisitor. This is treason to whisper, let alone—" she gestured at the book, the room, the gold chain, the whole apparatus of gentle horror around her.

"Because the prophecy gives us exactly two outcomes, Miss Ishikawa, and my office has been ordered to determine which one we're living toward." Hale's voice dropped, not for drama but for accuracy; she wanted every word precise. "*Unmade* means dead. It means the entity in the crown loses its host and the oath collapses and, per every account we have of the original famine-winters, the cold comes back within a season and it comes back hungry, because the bargain dies with the man. Half the court wants that outcome. They think a quick death and a hard winter is cleaner than whatever this is."

"And the other half?"

"Wants the wedding." Hale said the word without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "A binding marriage. The prophecy's phrasing suggests the speaker of bone can anchor the king — wed him, in the old sense, bind soul to soul the way the ateliers used to bind oaths into bone. The entity gets a stable host-pair instead of one failing body. The winter holds. The king lives. Everyone keeps everything." She paused. "It also requires a bone-speaker willing to be married to a stranger while a parasitic oath watches from inside his skull, but the court faction advocating for it has historically been light on details."

Vesper laughed.

It came out of her before she could stop it — a short, ugly, disbelieving sound, and Hale let it happen, waited it out with that terrible patience.

"You want me to choose," Vesper said. "Between killing a man I've never met and marrying one."

"I want you to understand the shape of the cage before we discuss the bars."

"I understand the cage fine." Her fear was still there — it was enormous, it filled the room like floodwater — but underneath it something else was rising, something older and harder, the same thing that had made a nine-year-old girl keep carving even when the voices frightened her, the thing that had built three years of careful ordinary life out of smuggled whalebone and a fishmonger's ignorance. "Here's what I understand. A dead king means a famine winter and everyone in the lower wards starves — I grew up in the lower wards, Inquisitor, I know what the cold does, I've *heard* what the cold does, the dead of those years don't stop talking about it. And a wedding means I become a tool. A component. Some ritual fixture the Crown screws into the throne so the machine keeps running." She pushed back from the table enough to sit straight, to take up space, because her mother had taught her that a carver's posture was a carver's spine. "So no. I refuse both."

Hale didn't move. "You refuse the prophecy."

"I refuse the *reading*. Prophecies are carvings. Someone cut these lines, someone chose these words, and every carver knows the material fights the design — you plan one figure and the grain shows you another. Your book says unmade or wed. Fine. But it was written by a hand, and hands can be wrong, and hands can be *answered*." She heard herself, heard the defiance coming out of her mouth like heat off a forge, and knew it was partly performance and knew, more dangerously, that it was partly true. "I didn't ask for any of this. I carved charms for nervous brides. I kept my head down. Whatever your eighty-year-old prophet thought she saw sitting at this table — I am telling you, as the person actually sitting here, that she got the grain wrong."

The silence stretched. Somewhere beyond the barred window, Greyhallow went on with its evening — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the ordinary machinery of a city that had no idea what was sitting in its inquisitors' back rooms.

"You realize," Hale said finally, quietly, "that I am required to report this conversation."

"Then report it."

"I will be instructed to pursue one of the two outcomes. And the instructions will not be gentle ones, whichever faction writes them." She began gathering the book, the folder, her composure, each in turn. "You have until tomorrow's date, Miss Ishikawa. After that, the choice stops being yours and starts being everyone's."

At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — the tea was my idea. The chair, the window. I read the file on your mother this afternoon. All of it I'm permitted to see." A beat. "She refused them too. Both outcomes. Word for word, according to the transcript."

The door closed behind her.

Vesper sat alone in the warm room with the cold tea and the barred window and the memory of brown ink, and pressed her palms flat against the tabletop, against wood that had never belonged to anyone dead, that had never once spoken, and tried to breathe.

*She refused them too.*

Her mother had walked into this room — or one like it — and said the same words, and then been taken upward into the palace and sealed away above everyone's rank.

And tomorrow, the date on the page would arrive, and Vesper understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that the prophecy hadn't been waiting eighty years for a *choice*.

It had been waiting for a daughter stubborn enough to make a third one.
