## 4. What the Crown Carries

Aldric woke the way he always woke: all at once, and not at all.

There was no clean line between sleeping and waking anymore. There was only the moment when he surfaced — like a man coming up through dark water into a room where someone else was already living his life. His eyes opened because *it* opened them. His lungs drew breath because *it* told them to. And Aldric stood behind his own face, hands pressed flat against the inside of his skull, watching.

The candles in the royal study had burned down to stubs. The hour was late — or early; the distinction had stopped mattering years ago. At the desk beneath the tall window, a man sat writing. Broad shoulders, grey threading the dark hair at the temples, a scar on the left knuckle from a fencing accident at fifteen that Aldric remembered getting. The man wore Aldric's face the way a well-made glove fits a hand: perfectly, and with no warmth in it at all.

The crown sat on the desk beside the inkwell. It never left the thing's reach. Iron and bone, old iron and older bone, plain as a farmer's ring and heavier than sin. When it was near, Aldric felt it the way a drowning man feels the weight of the sea — everywhere, patient, total.

The thing signed a decree. Aldric watched his own hand move, the familiar slight drag of the pen on the third stroke of the capital letter, a flaw in his handwriting that his tutors had beaten at him for two years and never cured. The signature came out flawless now. That was how he knew it wasn't him. He had never once managed to write his own name correctly, and this creature did it every time without thinking, the way it did everything without thinking.

*Charter of the Northern Granaries,* the decree said. *Rations reduced by one part in eight for the winter months.*

Aldric read it over his own shoulder. Somewhere behind his breastbone, something that was still entirely, stubbornly him wanted to scream. He didn't scream. Screaming cost energy, and energy was the only currency he had left, and he had learned — nine years of learning, paid for in the coin of everything he'd ever been — to spend nothing that wasn't necessary.

Instead he did what he always did first. He took inventory.

*Name.* Aldric Vane Coldharbour, fourth of that name. *Age.* Thirty-one. Nine years crowned, nine years tenanted. *Mother.* Dead. *Father.* Dead, and worse than dead, though he tried not to think about the worse. *Body.* Still his. Mostly. The thing used it but did not change it — could not, perhaps. It kept his scars, his calluses, the way his left shoulder sat lower than his right from an archery injury. It wore him but did not edit him, and in that refusal to edit there was, if you squinted, a kind of mercy. Or a kind of laziness. After nine years he had stopped trying to tell the difference.

*Self.* Present. Fading. Holding.

He held. He always held. That was the whole of his kingdom now: the narrow country of himself, population one, borders under permanent occupation.

The thing finished signing, blotted the page, and set the decree aside with the neatness of a clerk. Then it rose, crossed to the window, and looked out over Greyhallow — and Aldric looked too, because looking out was one of the few things they did together, the tenant and the landlord, both of them watching the city below the way a jailer and his prisoner might watch the same bird.

Snow was falling on the lower quarters. It fell in the strange, deliberate way it always fell near the palace, flakes descending in slow spirals, unhurried, as if the winter itself were being careful. The long winter. The winter that had begun the year of his coronation and never truly ended, that crept a little further down the mountains each year, that killed the vineyards first and then the orchards and then, last spring, the oldest oak in the palace courtyard, which had survived six kings and two sieges and could not survive whatever lived in the crown.

*You are awake,* the thing said.

It didn't speak aloud. It spoke the way cold speaks — not words so much as a temperature, a pressure against the inside of his thoughts. Aldric had spent years learning to parse it. Now he understood it better than he understood most living people.

*I'm always awake,* Aldric answered. He thought his answers rather than speaking them, and hoped — without much faith — that the thing couldn't tell the difference between a thought offered and a thought withheld. *You know that.*

*It is inefficient. Sleep would preserve you longer.*

*How considerate of you.*

The pressure shifted. If the thing had possessed a sense of humor, Aldric might have called the shift amusement. It didn't, so he called it what it was: attention, brief and total, like a searchlight passing over a hiding man.

*The granary reduction is necessary,* it said. *The winter extends. The stores must stretch.*

*People will starve.*

*People are already starving. This changes the rate, not the fact.* A pause, and then, with something almost like patience: *You feel this every year. You argue this every year. The argument changes nothing. Why do you keep having it?*

Because it was the last argument he owned. Because if he stopped arguing, even uselessly, even to an audience of ice, then some final door in him would close, and he would be nothing but a warm room the cold walked through. But he said none of that. He said:

*Because I'm the king.*

*You are the vessel,* the thing corrected, gently, the way one corrects a child who has confused a map with the territory. *The king is the oath. You are where the oath is kept.*

Aldric retreated. Not far — he couldn't go far; there was nowhere to go — but inward, down, into the cellar of himself where he kept the things the thing couldn't easily reach. His mother's voice singing in the kitchen of the old hunting lodge. The smell of cut grass before the winter came. His father's hand heavy on his shoulder the day before the coronation, saying, *Whatever they put on your head, boy, remember it isn't you.* He kept these the way a miser keeps coins: counted nightly, touched often, never spent.

The thing let him retreat. It always did, in the end. It needed him alive and reasonably whole, the way a house needs its foundations sound. It did not need him *present*. Presence was optional. Presence was, as far as Aldric could tell, purely sentimental — his own sentiment, hoarded against all reason.

The thing returned to the desk and began reviewing petitions. Aldric settled in behind his own eyes to watch, and to wait, and to do the only work left to him.

He waited for the frost.

* * *

He had discovered it by accident, three winters ago.

The thing slept — or entered whatever state served it for sleep, a stillness like deep ice, the pressure in his mind thinning to almost nothing. In those hours Aldric had a fraction more control of himself. Not enough to move. Never enough to move; the body was locked to him like a museum piece behind glass, visible, untouchable. But on that night, breathless with rage after the thing had ordered a charm-seller's stall burned in the Fishmarket — a girl of maybe nineteen, her wares scattered in the snow, her face when she ran — he had breathed hard through his nose, standing at the window, hating.

And the frost on the glass had moved.

Not melted. *Moved.* Swirled, gathered, crawled — and formed, for the length of one exhaled breath, a single crooked word:

**NO**

Then it was just frost again, and Aldric stood alone in the dark with his heart slamming, certain he had imagined it, hoping he hadn't, terrified of both.

He tested it the next night, and the next. He learned the rules the way he'd once learned swordwork: slowly, painfully, by failure. The frost answered only his breath. It answered only when the thing was dormant. It answered strongest on the coldest nights, when the palace windows bloomed white and the winter outside pressed close enough to lend him strength — which was a joke he appreciated even alone, because it meant the very thing devouring his kingdom was the only thing arming him against it. The long winter gave him paper. The enemy wrote his letters for him.

And the letters had to be small. One word, sometimes two, before the frost went blank or, worse, before the thing stirred and felt the shape of his intent moving through its house. He had nearly been caught twice. Once, early on, he'd written **HELP** across the whole lower pane in letters a hand high, and the thing had woken mid-stroke, and the cold that came off it had frozen the breath in his throat for a full minute while it examined him from the inside, curious, patient, asking without words: *What are you doing?*

*Nothing,* Aldric had answered. *Watching the snow.*

*You were doing something.*

*Breathing.*

It had watched him for three days after that. He had not written again for a month. He had learned economy. He had learned that a message worth sending must be short enough to survive and important enough to matter, and for three years he had found almost nothing that met both tests. Who would see the frost? Servants wiped the windows. Guards faced the doors. The study was sealed during the day. And even if someone saw — a scrawl of frost reading **I AM HERE** — what would they think? That the window leaked. That the king's rooms were haunted, which everyone half-believed anyway. That they had imagined it, and best not mention imagining things in a palace where imagination had a body count.

So the frost became what it had been ever since: not a message but a practice. A prayer with weather for a god. On the coldest nights, when the thing lay still, Aldric breathed against the glass and wrote small, useless, defiant words, and erased them himself by breathing elsewhere, and went back to being furniture.

*Tonight,* though — tonight was different. Tonight had started different.

* * *

It began with the dead kings.

They were always worst before midwinter. Aldric didn't hear them the way the thing heard them — or he assumed the thing heard them; it never spoke of them, which was its own kind of answer. What Aldric got was pressure. Weight. On bad nights it felt like standing in a crowded room with his eyes shut, bodies brushing past him, none of them speaking, all of them waiting. Nine men, he estimated. Nine kings before him who had worn the crown and hosted the oath and been, eventually, used up. His father had been the ninth. His father had lasted eleven years. Aldric was entering his tenth.

Tonight the crowding was fierce. The thing had been restless since dusk — pacing the study, standing at the window, summoning and dismissing servants over trivialities, a landlord walking his halls at night because the walls were making noises it didn't understand. Twice it had raised Aldric fully to the surface for no reason Aldric could detect, holding him up to the front of his own skull like a man checking whether a tool still worked.

*Why am I here?* Aldric asked, the second time.

*To observe,* the thing said. *Something is approaching.*

*What is?*

*Unknown. The oath is agitated. The dead are agitated. You will remain available.*

Available. As if he were a room that might need to be aired. Aldric stayed at the surface, though — gladly, in a grim way, because surface time was time he could see through his own eyes clearly, and seeing clearly mattered tonight, because the crowding in his chest had a direction to it. For nine years the dead kings' presence had been a fog. Tonight it was a compass needle. It pulled — faintly, insistently — toward the city. Downhill. Toward the low quarters, the night markets, the lanes where the snow lay grey with ash from permitted fires.

Toward something. Or someone.

The thing felt it too. That was why it paced. That was why it had woken him. Whatever was coming, the crown wanted to see it arrive.

Midnight came. The thing finally stilled — sank into its cold dormancy with unusual abruptness, like a man falling asleep mid-sentence, and Aldric wondered distantly what that meant, what it was conserving its strength *for*, and put the question away with all the other questions he kept in the cellar.

Then he was alone in his body for the first time in thirty hours, and the window was white with frost, and the night beyond it was the coldest of the season.

He breathed.

The frost woke like a flock of birds. It swirled up the pane, gathered, trembled — eager, almost, tonight, as if the winter outside were leaning in to listen. Aldric shaped his breath carefully, the way he'd practiced ten thousand times, and wrote the nightly liturgy, one word per breath, small letters, low on the glass where a kneeling servant might conceivably see them:

**ALIVE**

A breath. The word held, glittering.

**STILL**

Another breath. His chest ached with the effort of precision; writing frost was like threading a needle with his whole body. But the letters held, and the thing slept, and the dead kings pressed close around him like an audience leaning toward a stage.

And then the pressure *spoke*.

Not in temperature. Not in the usual dumb weight of them. For the first time in nine years, the dead kings did something they had never done: they pushed. All nine at once, a single concerted surge, and Aldric felt his arm — *his* arm, the locked one, the museum-piece one — twitch against the desk. Felt his breath hitch and reshape. Felt the frost take the next word out of him like a hook taking a fish.

He did not write it. They wrote it through him.

Four letters bloomed on the glass, sharp and deep-carved, in a hand that was not his:

**ISHIKAWA**

The name hung there, silver-white, utterly still. Aldric stared at it from inside his own eyes, and the cold that went through him had nothing to do with the crown.

*Ishikawa,* he thought. *Who is Ishikawa?*

The dead kings pressed again — softer now, urgent, a crowd murmuring behind a door. And beneath their pressure, for the first time, Aldric caught something else. Not a word. Not a voice. A *texture*, faint as a fingerprint on the far side of frosted glass: something young, something living, something that hummed at a frequency the dead recognized.

The dead kings knew this person. The dead kings were pointing at this person the way starving men point at bread.

*Ishikawa,* Aldric thought again, committing it to the cellar, to the vault, to the deepest room of the self he had defended for nine years. A name. A direction. A thread.

Behind him, in the marrow of his own bones, the crown stirred — a slow, cold turning-over, like something vast waking at the bottom of a lake. The frost on the window shivered. The name began to blur at its edges.

Aldric did the only thing left to do. He leaned close, cupped his palm against the pane, and breathed warmth over the letters until they ran — destroying the evidence with his own breath, erasing the first real message the frost had ever carried, keeping it the only way he could: carved into memory, in the one place the crown had never managed to search.

*Ishikawa.*

Somewhere downhill, in the grey lanes where the snow fell careful and slow, someone with that name was walking toward him — or being dragged.

He intended to find out which.
