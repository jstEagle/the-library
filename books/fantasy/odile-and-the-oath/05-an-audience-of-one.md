## 5. An Audience of One

They came for her at the hour when Greyhallow's bells went quiet and even the rats seemed to hold their breath.

Two Wardens, not three. They had taken her manacles off in the cell — Serane Hale's doing, Vesper suspected, though the inquisitor hadn't explained why — so her wrists were bare and cold as they walked her up through the palace's spine. The corridors changed as they climbed. The lower halls were stone and torchlight, practical, the architecture of people who expected to be attacked. But past the third landing the walls went to pale marble veined with grey, and the torches gave way to glass lamps that burned without flickering, and Vesper realized she was walking through money that had been old before her grandmother was born.

The dead noticed it too.

*Cold here,* murmured a voice from a lamp bracket — no, not from it, from the bone dust ground into the plaster behind it, because everything in this city was built on something that had once been alive, and everything that had once been alive left a residue, a sediment of voice. *Cold, and we were promised spring.*

"Shut up," Vesper whispered.

The Warden on her left glanced down. "Did you say something?"

"No."

He was young, this one, with chapped hands and the flat expression of a man who had learned early that curiosity was a tax he couldn't afford. The other Warden was older and walked like a door closing. Neither spoke to her. That was fine. She didn't want conversation. She wanted to count her heartbeats and make them last until whatever came next was over.

Except counting didn't work, because the dead wouldn't stop.

It had been like this since the arrest — worse than ever before, as if the palace itself were a struck bell and every ghost in Greyhallow had come to see what rang. In the cells beneath the Wardenhouse she'd heard drowned sailors complaining about the anchor chains in the foundation stones. In the corridor outside Serane's office, a hanged smuggler recited the names of his crew over and over, a rosary of guilt. And now, climbing toward the throne room, the voices thickened until they overlapped into a single low drone, like wind through a ship's rigging.

But threaded through the drone, one voice cut clean.

*Please.* A man's voice. Young. Hoarse, as if it had been saying this for years. *Please, someone, please—*

Vesper stopped walking.

The older Warden turned. "Problem?"

"I—" Her mouth was dry. "I need a moment."

"You don't get moments." He took her elbow, not roughly, and steered her onward. But she craned her neck as she went, searching the marble, the lamps, the floor, trying to find where the voice lived. It wasn't in the walls. It wasn't in the boneset of the building at all. It moved. It was *ahead* of her, growing louder with every step, and it sounded less like a dead man every minute and more like a living one who had been buried alive.

*Please. Please. I know you can hear me. I've been leaving messages. I know you can hear me.*

Frost letters. The thought arrived whole, uninvited: the stories about the king's windows, the frost that spelled words no scribe could explain, hushed about in markets for years and dismissed by sensible people as servants' pranks or damp. *Ishikawa,* the frost had said last night, according to the whisper-network that reached even into the Wardenhouse cells.

Her name. Or half of it.

Her hands began to shake, and she folded them together to hide it.

* * *

The doors to the throne room were black oak banded with silver, tall enough that a man could have stood on another man's shoulders inside the carved arch and still not touched the top. They opened without anyone visibly opening them — some counterweight mechanism, probably, but it felt like the room inhaling.

Vesper had imagined the throne room a hundred times since her arrest. She'd imagined gold, banners, ranks of courtiers. What she hadn't imagined was the emptiness. The hall stretched away from her, vast and dim, its far end lost in shadow, and the few courtiers present stood well back from the dais, clustered near the doors like people warming themselves at a fire that had gone out. No one wanted to be close to the throne. She understood why within four steps.

Because she heard it.

Not the king. Not yet. First the crown — and the crown was *screaming*, silently, in a register only she could hear, a chorus of dozens of voices braided into one sustained note like an organ pipe made of grief. Dead kings. She knew it instantly, the way you know the taste of salt. Every host the thing had ever worn was still in there, layered like rings in a tree, and none of them had finished dying properly. They sang their one long note and underneath it she could hear fragments — a coronation oath repeated in Latin-tongued cadence, a child crying, a woman's voice saying *hold on, hold on, my love, hold on* — and all of it bent around a central presence that was not a voice at all but a *hunger*, vast and patient and old, the way winter is patient.

Vesper's knees tried to buckle. The younger Warden caught her arm.

"Breathe," he said quietly, and there was something almost kind in it, and later she would wonder if he'd been told to say that.

She breathed. She put one foot in front of the other. Twenty paces of marble, each one louder than the last, the chorus swelling until her skull felt like a bell being rung from inside. She had spent three years carving charms small enough to slip under the Wardens' notice precisely because she could not bear large magic, large death, large voice. This was the largest thing she had ever heard. It filled the hall the way water fills a hull.

And beneath it — faint, frayed, nearly worn through —

*Please.*

The living voice. Closer now. Coming from the throne.

She looked up.

Aldric sat alone on the dais under a canopy of grey silk, and he was not what she expected either. The portraits in the market stalls showed him stern, bearded, remote, a king-shaped coin stamp. The man on the throne was thin. Younger than his coinage. Dark-haired, hollow-cheeked, dressed in black furs that seemed to wear him rather than the reverse. His hands lay on the arms of the throne with terrible stillness, fingers curled like a dead man's, and his eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance and did not move as she approached.

But the crown—

Gods, the crown. It sat on his head like a spider on a trapped fly. Black iron worked into shapes that might have been branches or might have been ribs, and it was *wrong*, it hummed against every law of metal, and the chorus poured out of it in a flood that made her teeth ache. The entity wore the crown the way a hand wears a glove, and the glove was Aldric, and the hand was centuries deep.

Serane Hale stood at the foot of the dais, hands clasped behind her back, and inclined her head as Vesper was brought to a stop six paces out. "Your Majesty," Serane said, "the bone-speaker."

No answer from the throne. The eyes didn't move. Somewhere in the hall a courtier coughed and flinched at the sound of his own cough.

"The Crown requires your assessment," Serane continued, level, professional, as if addressing a chair. "You will examine the regalia and report what you hear. You will speak to no one else of it. You will—"

"*Stop talking.*"

The words came out of Vesper before she decided to say them. Her own voice startled her — raw, too loud in the vaulted silence. Serane's head turned slowly. The Wardens went rigid. And Vesper stood there with her heart slamming and thought, *well, that's it, they'll hang me for lèse-majesté before lunch,* but she couldn't take it back, because the living voice was right there, right *there*, a drowning man an arm's length from shore, and she had spent her whole life pretending she couldn't hear the ones who begged.

*Please,* said the voice from the throne. Fainter now. Almost gone. *Is someone there? Is someone actually—*

"Yes," Vesper said aloud.

The sound she made in answering was small. The silence it fell into was enormous.

Every courtier in the hall turned. Serane's composure cracked — not into anger, Vesper saw, but into something more dangerous: attention. The older Warden's hand drifted toward his baton. And on the throne, in the space of a single heartbeat, the king's eyes *moved*.

They found her face and fixed on it, and they were not the empty eyes of a puppet. They were terrified. They were grateful. They were the eyes of a man watching a rope thrown across forty years of dark water, and they were wet.

"Don't," Aldric whispered. His lips barely shaped it. His voice came out rusted, a hinge unused for seasons. "Don't answer it. It listens. Everything I—everything I say near it, it learns, it uses, please, don't let it hear you—"

"He speaks," breathed a courtier near the doors, scandalized, delighted, horrified.

"Aldric." Vesper said his name without thinking, without permission, the familiarity of it a second crime, and the hall sucked in another collective breath. She didn't care. She was crying, she realized distantly, tears running hot down a face gone cold, and she didn't care about that either. "My name is Vesper Ishikawa. I hear the dead. I heard you. Do you understand? Three days ago I didn't know you existed and now I've crossed a city in chains to stand in front of you, and I *hear you*, so you are going to have to be brave for one more hour, because I am not leaving."

The chorus screamed.

It came off the crown like a wave off a harbor wall — a pressure, a cold, a soundless roar that staggered the nearest guardsmen though they couldn't hear a note of it. The candles guttered. Frost bloomed across the marble between Vesper and the dais, spreading in feathering white fractals, and the temperature of the hall dropped so fast her breath came out visible. The entity had noticed her. The entity had *turned its attention* on her, and it was like being looked at by an avalanche.

*What are you,* it said, and it wasn't words, it was the sensation of standing on ice and feeling it thin. *Little bone-speaker. Little thief of voices. We know your line. We ate your line.*

Vesper's legs shook. Her vision swam. Somewhere very far away Serane was shouting orders and the Wardens were moving and the courtiers were fleeing for the doors in a rustle of fur and panic, and none of it mattered, because the thing in the crown was speaking to her directly now, and it knew her mother's name, and it knew her grandmother's, and it was telling her, in the courteous tone of a landlord, exactly how long her family had been tenants of its patience.

And still — beneath it, muffled, wrapped in iron and oath and nine years of silence — the living voice held on.

*Run,* Aldric was saying, or trying to say, his body fighting him for every syllable, his hand lifting from the throne's arm and then being pressed back down by nothing visible. *Run, it will use you, it will wear you, run—*

"No," Vesper said.

She said it quietly. That was the strange part, looking back. With the frost crawling up her boots and the chorus howling and the whole court in flight, she said it the way she said things to frightened charms, to nervous brides, to the dead who crowded her workbench wanting one more minute of being remembered. Quietly. Like a person keeping a promise.

"No. I don't run. I carve."

She raised her hands — bare, unchained, scarred from three years of whalebone and antler — and showed them to the throne. To the crown. To both of them, the prisoner and the prison.

"And I am very good at my work."

The frost stopped.

For three full seconds the hall hung balanced on that word — *carve* — and Vesper felt the entity's attention shift, feel along her like a finger tracing a blade's edge, reassessing. Hunger met craft. Winter met the small stubborn heat of a woman who had survived Greyhallow by making things too small and too true to burn.

Then the king's body sagged, and the light went out of his eyes, and the chorus closed back over the living voice like water over a stone, and the moment was gone.

But not entirely. Because as the Wardens took her arms and turned her toward the doors, as Serane Hale fell into step beside her with a face like a drawn blade, Vesper heard one last thing, faint as a pulse under snow:

*Come back,* said the voice that was still alive. *Please. Come back.*

"I will," Vesper said, to the whole emptying hall, to the Crown, to the laws of Greyhallow, to the thing wearing the king. "Count on it."

Behind her, on the dais, frost crept up the throne's armrests and formed, in careful letters, a single word that no one else would see until morning:

*Soon.*
