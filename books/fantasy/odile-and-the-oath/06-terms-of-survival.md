## 6. Terms of Survival

Serane Hale had conducted one hundred and fourteen interrogations in her eleven years with the Inquisition. She knew them the way a surgeon knows scars: the ones that broke fast, the ones that lied beautifully, the ones that wept until she wanted to open a window just to give the crying somewhere to go.

The bone-carver was none of these.

Vesper Ishikawa sat in the interview room at Wardenhall with her hands folded on the table like a woman waiting for bread to rise. She had not slept — they'd taken her at midnight and it was now past noon — but she didn't fidget, didn't plead, didn't perform innocence or guilt. She simply watched Serane with dark, level eyes that seemed older than the face around them.

Serane set down the folder. She didn't open it yet. Opening it early was an amateur's move; it told the prisoner what you already knew instead of making them guess what you might.

"You're not asking me anything," Vesper said. Her voice was hoarse but steady. "Most of your kind would have asked something by now."

"My kind." Serane almost smiled. "And what kind is that?"

"The kind that burned the charm-sellers off Lantern Row before I was born." A pause. "Except you didn't finish. Someone always keeps carving."

Serane had heard defiance before. This wasn't quite that. It was inventory — the girl taking stock of her, cataloguing her the way she herself catalogued suspects. It unsettled her more than screaming would have.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Serane said, "and then I'm going to make you an offer. You can decide which part is the trap."

Something flickered across the carver's face. Interest, maybe. Or the recognition of another person who understood that everything was a trap, and the only question was which ones you could walk through.

"Tell the story," Vesper said.

*

Her sister's name had been Maren, and she had been dead for nineteen years, four months, and eleven days. Serane did not count this aloud, ever, but the number lived in her chest the way shrapnel lives in a shoulder: quiet on good days, loud when the weather turned.

Maren Hale had been a hedge-healer. Nothing dramatic — no grimoires, no blood-rituals. She could put her hands on a fevered child and know, without touching anything else, whether the sickness was in the lungs or the gut. She could feel where a bone had knitted wrong under skin and tell a surgeon where to cut. The village of Lowmarsh had loved her for it, right up until the winter the Wardens came through doing their census of the gifted, and a neighbor — a man whose son Maren had once saved from drowning — signed the affidavit naming her.

Serane had been fifteen. She remembered standing in the square with her mother's hand crushing hers, watching the pyre, and remembering most clearly — the detail she could never scrub out, the one that came to her at three in the morning like a debt collector — that her sister had not screamed. Maren had looked into the crowd, found Serane, and *smiled*. Not bravely. Not defiantly. Just smiled, as if to say: *it's all right, little sister, don't let them see you grieve.*

It had not been all right. Serane had spent the next two years hating the Crown, and then the year after that understanding that hatred was a luxury, and then making the coldest calculation of her life: that the only way to keep other Marens alive was to get inside the machine that killed them and slow its gears by whatever fraction her hands could manage.

Eleven years later she was Chief Inquisitor of Greyhallow, and the machine still ran, and she had slowed it by perhaps nothing at all. But she had learned things inside it. She had learned that the magic laws were not really about magic. They were about fear — the Crown's fear, old and structural, the fear of a throne that had been sick for nine years and could not afford to have it known.

She poured water from the pitcher and slid the cup across the table to the prisoner. Small kindnesses were also tools. But Serane had long ago stopped pretending to herself that every tool was only a tool.

"Drink," she said. "Then listen."

*

Vesper drank. Her wrists were bare; they'd confiscated her charms, all forty-one of them, each wrapped and labeled in Serane's own handwriting. On the table between them, in fact, lay the smallest one — a sliver of whalebone no bigger than a thumbnail, carved with a pattern like frost ferns. Serane had kept it back deliberately. An experiment.

"Do you hear anything right now?" Serane asked.

Vesper's eyes went to the bone sliver. Something moved behind her face — hunger, Serane thought, or grief. "You mean besides your pulse. You've been clenching your jaw since you sat down. Left side. Old injury."

Serane went very still. She hadn't said a word about her jaw. There were no bones in the room large enough to carry voices — she'd checked, twice, since the arrest.

"That's not hearing," she said carefully. "That's reading."

"It's the same muscle." Vesper set down the cup. "Ask your question, Inquisitor. The real one. You've been circling it since the door closed."

So Serane opened the folder after all, because there was no more point in theater, and turned it so the book's final page faced the carver. The prophecy page. The photograph of it, rather — the original sat in a vault beneath the Citadel, behind three locks and a priest's oath, and Serane had seen it exactly once, by lantern light, with her superior breathing down her neck.

*On the eve of midwinter's turn,* the old script read, *the speaker of bones shall stand before the Hollow King. By her hand he is unmade, or by her vow he is wed. The winter ends with one. The winter ends with both.*

"And tomorrow," Serane said, "is the eve of midwinter's turn. Nineteen years I've worked for the Inquisition. Fourteen of those, someone above me has been hunting a bone-speaker. Do you understand what that means? It means you weren't caught. You were *found*. We've been looking for you since before you picked up your first carving knife."

"They want me alive," Vesper said quietly. "A dead Warden told me."

Serane didn't blink, though it cost her. *A dead Warden.* Of course. Of course the girl heard the dead even here, even cuffed, even stripped of her craft. Somewhere in Wardenhall's walls, some poor soul who'd died in service was whispering warnings to a prisoner, and Serane could do nothing about it, and — a small, traitorous part of her noted — the warning had been true.

"Yes," she said. "Alive. Because the prophecy needs you living. And because His Majesty—" she chose the words the way she'd choose footing crossing ice— "is unwell. Has been unwell for nine years. And the Crown has run out of explanations."

Vesper studied the photographed page. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — smaller, careful, the voice of someone stepping onto a frozen river and testing each inch. "Unmade or wed. Those are my options?"

"Those are the options the prophecy allows."

"Prophecies are written by people." Flat now, hard. "People make mistakes. People make *lies*. I'm not marrying anyone. I'm not unmaking anyone. I carve charms for brides and sailors and children with bad dreams. That's the whole of my ambition, and your Crown burned the street I sold them on, and now you sit there telling me my entire life has been a footnote in some dead monk's poetry." Her hands, folded on the table, had gone white-knuckled. "Refused. Both futures. Refused."

There it was. Serane had expected it, had planned for it, and still felt something in her chest tilt — admiration, maybe, or its ugly twin, pity. She thought of Maren smiling into the crowd. *Don't let them see you grieve.*

"Here's what refusal gets you," Serane said, and made her voice the cold instrument it needed to be. "It gets you a cell in the deep archive until someone smarter than me decides how to use you. It gets the scholar — Ilsa Vey, your friend from the night market — a trial under the sedition statutes, because she showed a proscribed text to an unregistered practitioner, which carries a minimum sentence you don't want to imagine. It gets your mother's name dragged through the record — oh yes, we know whose technique that is, we've been matching your carving against samples for three years — and it gets every charm-seller in Greyhallow swept up in the net we cast around you. You are not a private citizen anymore, Ishikawa. You haven't been since the day you were born. The only question left is how much damage your refusal does to people who never asked to be prophecied at."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, a cell door slammed, and Serane saw the carver flinch — not at the sound, Serane realized, but at whatever the sound carried. The building was full of dead men's echoes, and Vesper Ishikawa could not close her ears to any of them.

Nineteen years Serane had dressed her loyalty every morning like a uniform, buttoning it up over the wound where Maren used to be. And in this room, facing this girl — this exhausted, stubborn, twenty-something girl who heard ghosts whether she wanted to or not — the uniform felt suddenly, unbearably tight.

So she took a risk. The largest one of her career.

"Maren," she said. "My sister's name was Maren."

*

Vesper blinked. Whatever she'd braced for, it wasn't that.

"She was a healer," Serane went on, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears, stripped of its official grain. "Hedge-craft. Feeling fevers, finding breaks. The kind of gift that saves children. The Wardens burned her in Lowmarsh when I was fifteen, on a neighbor's signature, and I joined the Inquisition eight years later because I decided — I *decided*, God help me — that the machine would kill fewer people if someone who hated it was holding the levers." She laughed, short and airless. "I've been holding them ever since. I've commuted eleven sentences. Out of a hundred and fourteen. Eleven."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you think I'm the wall." Serane leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for once let the tiredness show — all of it, eleven years of it. "I'm not the wall. I'm a crack in the wall. And cracks are the only thing that ever got anyone through."

She laid the whalebone charm on the table between them. The frost-fern sliver. Vesper's breath caught — audibly, involuntarily — and her hand moved toward it before she stopped herself.

"Here are the terms," Serane said. "Not the Crown's terms. Mine. You will come to the palace as my consultant — a legal fiction I can construct, barely, and only barely. You will examine what needs examining. If what I suspect is true, you will confirm it for me, in a form I can act on. In exchange: the scholar walks free tonight, charges quashed, no record. Your mother's file stays sealed. And the sweep of Lantern Row — I will delay it. Delay, not cancel. I don't have the authority to cancel."

"And if what you suspect isn't true?"

Serane held her gaze. "Then we're both fools, and I'll have spent the last coin of my credibility on a stranger, and you'll be back in this room by week's end. But it's true." She tapped the prophecy page. "This wasn't written a hundred years ago as poetry. I've read the marginalia. Whoever wrote it had seen the king's condition before — or a version of it. Hosts. Plural. Something has been wearing the crown of Greyhallow for longer than Aldric has been alive, and everyone in this city pretends the winters aren't getting worse because pretending is the only law we actually enforce."

Vesper was silent for a long moment. Then: "What do you suspect, exactly? Say it plainly."

"I suspect the King of Greyhallow hasn't spoken in his own voice for nine years," Serane said, "and that the thing speaking for him is hungry, and that the prophecy's last line is a countdown, and that in roughly thirty hours either you, or I, or several thousand people on Lantern Row are going to find out what happens when it runs out."

The lamp hissed. Down the corridor, boots rang on stone — the changing of the watch, punctual as ever, the machine grinding on regardless.

Vesper reached out and touched the whalebone sliver with one fingertip, the way a starving woman touches bread she's been told not to eat. When she lifted her eyes, the level darkness in them had shifted into something Serane recognized from her own mirror: the look of a person calculating the exact price of the only exit available, and deciding to pay it.

"Ilsa goes free tonight," Vesper said. "Before I examine anything. Not after."

"After. One hour after you confirm what's in the vault beneath the chapel — a relic, first Hollow King's era, that I'm not authorized to show anyone and will show you anyway."

"Tonight, then. Before midnight."

"Tonight."

Vesper exhaled slowly. "Your sister," she said, unexpected, quiet. "When they burned her. Was she scared?"

The question went through Serane like a blade finding the seam in armor. She thought of the smile. *Don't let them see you grieve.*

"No," Serane said, and discovered her voice had gone rough. "That was the worst part. No."

Vesper nodded slowly, as if something had been confirmed — some frequency, some truth in the marrow of the words themselves that Serane would never be able to check. Then she drew the whalebone toward her, curled her fingers around it, and closed her eyes.

For nearly a minute she was silent. Serane watched her lips move faintly, listening to something, answering something, in a conversation Serane could not hear and would spend the rest of her life wondering about.

When Vesper opened her eyes again, they were wet, and fixed on Serane with terrible clarity.

"Inquisitor," she said. "Who is Ishikawa?"

Serane frowned. "You are. That's your name."

"Not mine." Vesper's grip tightened on the charm until her knuckles paled. "Someone just said it to me. Through the bone. A woman's voice. She said — she said *tell the inquisitor's daughter the workshop is still there. Under the chapel. She should hurry.*"

The room tilted. Serane heard her own heartbeat, loud and stupid, in her ears.

"Say that again," she managed. "Slowly."

But Vesper was already leaning forward, urgent now, the bargain forgotten, the terms already obsolete — because the dead, apparently, did not wait for signatures.

"There's more," the bone-carver whispered. "She says she's been waiting eleven years for someone to ask."
