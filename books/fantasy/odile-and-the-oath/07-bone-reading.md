## 7. Bone-Reading

They brought the relic to her at dawn, which Vesper thought was either a courtesy or an insult — she hadn't decided which.

The cell they'd moved her to was not a cell anymore, or not only one. Someone had cleared the straw, set a table of pale pine by the high barred window, and put a lamp on it with a shuttered flame that burned without smoke. Warden oil, she guessed. The kind that cost more per drop than she made in a month selling luck-charms to nervous brides. The Crown could afford kindness when it wanted something.

Serane Hale stood at the door in her grey coat, hands folded behind her back like a woman attending her own funeral.

"You slept," Serane said.

"Barely."

"Good enough." She stepped aside and two Wardens came in carrying a box between them — iron-banded, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and heavy enough that their boots complained against the stone. They set it on the table and left without looking at it, the way you don't look at a thing you've been ordered never to look at.

"What is it?" Vesper asked.

"The reason this kingdom still exists," Serane said. "Or the reason it deserves to end. Depending on the day." She produced a small knife and broke the wax seal with a motion so practiced it looked bored. "You'll understand why we keep it locked when you touch it. If you can even touch it."

Inside the box, packed in wool gone grey with age, lay a bone the length of Vesper's forearm.

It was not shaped like anything. That was the first wrongness. Every bone has a logic to it — a femur knows it's a femur, a rib curves because ribs curve. This piece had been carved, once; she could see the ghost of tool-marks under the surface, fine as spider silk. But the carving had been *undone*. Not broken — smoothed. As if someone had taken a finished work and rubbed it back toward blankness, and the bone had resisted, and the argument between chisel and material had frozen mid-word a century ago.

Vesper's hands were shaking before she lifted it out.

Three years of working whalebone and antler and the cheap ivory the river traders brought down from the north, three years of listening to the dead murmur through whatever she carved — complaints mostly, small grievances, a fisherman's ghost grumbling about his nets, a grandmother correcting her stitch-count from beyond any reasonable need to correct stitch-counts. The dead in bone were quiet things. Distant things. Voices heard through a wall.

She touched the relic and the wall wasn't there.

*Cold.* Not temperature-cold. Cold the way a name is cold when it's spoken over your grave. The bone opened under her fingers like a door opening onto winter, and what came through was not a murmur but a *choir*, dozens of voices braided into one, and beneath the choir —

Beneath the choir, something vast turned over in its sleep.

Vesper snatched her hand back. The lamp-flame guttered in its shutter. Her breath came out white in a room that had been warm a moment ago.

"It does that," Serane said quietly. She had not flinched. Vesper noticed that — filed it away. Inquisitors who flinched didn't last. "The last reader we brought to it was a priest of the Quiet Order. He lasted four minutes before he started screaming about a vow he'd never made. He's still at the sanatorium in Lowmarsh. Very peaceful now. Doesn't speak at all."

"And you want me to do better than four minutes."

"I want you to do better than screaming," Serane said. "I want to know what's in it. The archives say it belonged to the first Hollow King — Edvard, they called him then, before the crown ate the name. This is all that's left of him that isn't legend. A fragment of the binding-oath, carved into his own arm-bone by his own hand, the night the bargain was struck." She paused. "You hear the dead in bone. That's what the prophecy says, that's what your arrest warrant says, and God help me, that's what I'm choosing to believe, because if I'm wrong about you then everything I've done this week is a crime and I'd rather not know yet."

"That's very touching," Vesper said. Her voice came out steadier than her hands. "And Ilsa?"

"Ilsa Verren eats breakfast in a warm room and complains about the tea. As agreed." Serane pulled a chair to the table and sat, unhurried, folding her hands. "I keep my bargains, Ishikawa. It's the only currency I have left. Now — four minutes. Start counting."

Vesper looked at the bone.

Every instinct she had honed in three years of careful, small, survivable life said: *put it down, walk away, be nobody.* But nobody was exactly what the prophecy had already refused to let her be, and besides — beneath the fear, beneath the choir, there was the other thing. The thing that had made her a carver in the first place. Curiosity. The dead always wanted to tell her something. She had spent her whole life pretending she couldn't afford to listen.

She put both hands on the bone.

* * *

The first thing she learned was that the voices were not separate.

In whalebone, the dead came one at a time, each with its own flavor of grievance. Here they were fused — nine voices, she counted, though the count kept sliding, eight, ten, nine again — melted together like candles burned too close, and what they were saying, all of them, endlessly, in round after round after round, was the same sentence:

*We swore.*

Not a complaint. Not a haunting. An oath, still being spoken, a century past any moment when speaking it could mean anything. She felt the shape of it settle over her like snowfall: hunger, terrible hunger, a city starving, fields black with frost in high summer, and people — the ancestors, the ones the histories called founders and she suddenly understood had been simply *desperate* — kneeling in a frozen square and giving voice to something too large for them.

*We will endure. We will hold the winter outside our walls. Take our king, take our sons, take our blood until the thaw — we swear it, we swear it, we swear—*

And the winter — or something wearing winter's face, something that had been listening in the dark beyond the walls the way wolves listen at a fold — had answered.

*Accepted.*

"Oh," Vesper said aloud. Her mouth was somewhere far away. "Oh, you idiots. You beautiful, desperate idiots. You *meant* it."

That was the horror of it. That was the whole architecture of the trap, laid open in her palms like a dissected bird. Oaths were real. Everyone knew oaths were real the way everyone knew the tide was real — you built your piers around them and didn't think further. But an oath sworn by a starving people, with nothing left to offer but themselves, sworn *in blood and bone* because they had nothing else to swear with — that kind of oath didn't fade. It didn't get kept and released. It *condensed*. It took the shape of the promise and went looking for fulfillment, forever, and it had found its instrument in a young king named Edvard who loved his city enough to kneel where his people knelt and say *take me instead.*

And the oath had. Gladly. It had worn him like a coat. And when Edvard wore through, it had taken the next king, and the next, each coronation a re-swearing, each host a fresh body poured into a vessel that never emptied, and the thing in the crown — the Hollow King, the entity, the chorus-with-teeth — was not a demon and not a god.

It was a *sentence*. A vow given flesh by the desperation of the people who spoke it, still trying, century after century, to fulfill terms that had stopped making sense before anyone alive was born.

*Hold the winter outside the walls,* the oath demanded. *Until the thaw.*

But the thaw never came while the oath held, because the oath WAS the winter's anchor — the bargain cut both ways, the long cold was the price of protection, and the protection required a living host, and the host would die, and when the host died—

When the host died, the oath would be unfulfilled. Broken contract. And the winter, no longer held *outside* the walls by the bargain, would come *in*.

Vesper surfaced gasping, hands numb, cheeks wet without her noticing.

"How long?" Serane asked.

"Four minutes," Vesper lied, and then, because lying to this woman suddenly seemed obscene, "Eleven. Maybe twelve." She wrapped her arms around herself. The room was genuinely cold now; frost ferned up the inside of the window bars. "Serane. How old is Aldric?"

"Thirty-one. He was crowned at twenty-two."

"Nine years." Nine years of being a tenant in his own body. Nine years of the chorus wearing his face. "How long did the others last?"

Serane was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had the flat care of someone walking across ice they already know is thin. "Edvard, eleven years. His son, six. Then four. Then seven. The records get worse the closer they come to the present. The archivists assumed the kings were growing weaker." She looked at the bone. "You're saying that's wrong."

"They're not weaker. The oath is *hungrier*." Vesper pushed the relic away from her, an inch, two — as much distance as she could bear. "It's not preserving them. It's eating them. Slowly, because slow keeps the bargain stable, but every host gives it less time than the last, and here's the part you need to write down, Inquisitor, the part that matters—" She made herself say it plainly, because plainly was the only way it would be believed. "When Aldric dies — when the oath finishes consuming him — the contract breaks unfulfilled. The winter comes off its chain. Not a hard season. The long winter. The one the founders traded a dynasty to postpone. It falls the day the Hollow King's current body fails, and it doesn't lift, because there'll be no one left alive inside the walls to re-swear."

Silence. The lamp hissed softly in its shutter.

"So the Crown isn't protecting a king," Serane said slowly. "It's maintaining a hostage."

"You're maintaining a *fuse*," Vesper said. "Aldric is the fuse. The oath lit it a hundred years ago and every ritual, every coronation, every generation of kings raised to be devoured — that's just the fuse burning down at the speed of a human life." She laughed, and hated the sound of it, brittle and small in the cold room. "Do you understand what the prophecy means now? Unmake the Hollow King, and you break the oath — but break it *wrong*, break it violently, and the same winter falls. Wed him, bind yourself into the contract as the next link in the chain, and the fuse burns slower. Those aren't two futures, Serane. They're the same future at different speeds. The book isn't offering me a choice. It's offering me a *schedule*."

Serane had gone very still. Vesper watched her — really watched her, the way she watched bone, reading the grain underneath. The inquisitor's face was composed, professional, correct. And underneath the composition, something was moving. Grief, maybe. Or arithmetic.

"My sister," Serane said at last, "was executed under the magic laws for less than what you just did in twelve minutes." She rose, straightened her coat with two precise tugs. "I'm going to bring you more of these. There are fragments in the vault — other pieces of Edvard's binding, recovered over the centuries. If the oath can be read, perhaps it can be *amended*. Contracts have clauses. Even divine ones."

"There are no clauses," Vesper said. "I felt it. It's one sentence, repeated forever. *We swore.* That's the entire text." She hesitated. And then, because the dead had taught her one thing above all others — that unfinished words fester — she said the rest. "But sentences can be *answered*. An oath is a call. It's been waiting a hundred years for a response. Nobody's ever tried to give it one. They've only tried to silence it, or feed it, or run from it."

Serane stopped at the door. Frost had crept along the floor to meet her boot and she looked down at it, then at Vesper, with an expression Vesper would spend a long time afterward trying to name. It wasn't hope. Hope was warmer. It was the expression of a woman recalculating the distance between the law she served and the truth standing in front of her, and finding the gap wider than her career.

"A response," Serane repeated. "From whom? The oath was spoken by the founders. They're dust."

"No," Vesper said. Her eyes went to the relic on the table, to the choir sleeping inside it, nine kings melted into one voice, each of them still down there in the dark, still bound, still *swearing*. "They're not dust. They're hosts. All of them. Edvard and the rest — the oath didn't release them when it moved on. It *kept* them." She swallowed. "The Hollow King isn't one thing wearing many crowns, Serane. It's a prison. And the prisoners are still in there."

She saw it land — saw the inquisitor's whole framework of possession and punishment and royal dignity crack down the middle like river ice in spring.

"The king," Serane said carefully. "Our king. Is he a prisoner too, or—"

"He's the newest one," Vesper said. "And he's the only one still alive."

Serane left without another word. The Wardens came and carried the box away, and Vesper sat alone in the cold room with her throbbing hands and the echo of the choir behind her eyes, and understood, with the terrible clarity the bone had left in her, two things at once.

First: the trap had rules, and rules could be learned, and what could be learned could be worked against. The oath wanted a host. The oath wanted fulfillment. The oath had been waiting a hundred years for an answer to a question nobody had dared ask it.

Second: sometime in the last hour, the frost on the window had changed.

It wasn't ferning anymore. It wasn't random. Across the glass, in letters written by a hand that had no hand — written, she realized, from the *inside*, from wherever the king's true self huddled behind his own eyes — the ice had arranged itself into a single word, small and crooked and unmistakable:

*Ishikawa.*

He knew her name. He had heard her, somehow, through a hundred years of dead kings and a foot of castle stone — heard her talking to the bone, talking about *him* — and he had done the one thing a prisoner in his own body could do.

He had written to her.

Vesper pressed her palm flat against the freezing glass, over the word, and whispered to the empty room:

"I hear you. Write faster."
