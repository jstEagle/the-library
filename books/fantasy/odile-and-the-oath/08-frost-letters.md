## 8. Frost Letters

The thing in my crown sleeps before dawn. That is the only hour that belongs to me.

I learned its rhythms the way a prisoner learns the footsteps of his jailer — which corridors it walked in me, when it grew heavy, when it thinned to a whisper and left me alone behind my own eyes. It slept deepest between the last bell of night and first grey light, sated on whatever it fed on while I watched from inside myself. Nine years I had kept this one small map of its habits, and for nine years I had done nothing with it but breathe.

Tonight, for the first time, I breathed *words*.

* * *

It began, as most dangerous things do, with something small.

Three nights after the audience — three nights since a bone-carver had looked up at my throne and spoken to the living man drowning under the dead kings' chorus — I woke at the dark bell and found my breath hanging in the air of my bedchamber longer than it should. The hearth was down to embers. Cold enough to crack stone. But my exhale hung there, white and patient, like a hand waiting to be held.

I had written in frost before. Small things, early years, when I still believed someone might be watching closely enough to notice. *Help.* Once, desperately, *I am still here.* The frost never lasted past sunrise. No one ever came.

But now someone had heard me. Someone who spoke to bones and listened to the dead — and if she could hear the chorus in my crown from across a throne room, then perhaps she could hear what I wrote into the cold.

I stood at the window, barefoot on freezing stone, and breathed against the glass. I shaped each word carefully, the way you shape anything when your hands are not entirely your own: slow, deliberate, mine.

*Ishikawa. Are you listening?*

The letters bloomed white, feathered at their edges, already dying. I watched them fade and felt the old shame rise — nine years a king, and all my rebellion fit inside a windowpane's worth of breath.

Then, below where my words had been, new frost crept up the glass from the bottom edge. Thin lines, precise as engraving. A craftsman's hand, not a ghost's.

*Every dawn. Same window. The Wardens rotate at the fourth bell; the east post is half-blind. Write small.*

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone, at four in the morning, in a palace that had forgotten the sound. It came out cracked and rusted, like a hinge opening after years.

She couldn't have received the message yet — the frost would be long gone by the time any note reached her cell or her quarters in the inquisitors' wing. But she had answered anyway. She had known. Which meant she had already been listening at my window before I ever thought to speak.

Or it meant the thing in my crown was cleverer than I knew, and this was the trap I'd finally walked into.

I decided, with the particular recklessness of a man who has been drowning for nearly a decade, not to care.

* * *

We built the correspondence out of whatever the winter gave us.

Frost, first. Every dawn, before the thing stirred, I pressed my palm and breath to the east window and wrote what I could not say aloud even in my own skull, because I no longer trusted my own skull. She answered in the same medium — I never saw how, at first, until I understood she had bribed or charmed some servant into admitting her to the gallery outside my chambers in the blind hours, a girl with cold-chapped hands and an astonishing talent for looking like she belonged nowhere and everywhere. Vesper wrote in frost the way she must have carved bone: few strokes, nothing wasted.

*Your father's ring,* she wrote on the third dawn. *The signet they buried him with. He says he's sorry. He says he didn't understand what the oath would take.*

I read it twice. Then I sat down on the floor with my back against the bed, because my legs had opinions about standing.

My father had died six months after my coronation. Officially: a fever. Unofficially — in the truth I kept locked beneath the truth — the oath had begun feeding, and the crown had needed a host young enough to survive the eating. My father had signed the renewal knowing it would pass to me. Knowing, or telling himself he knew. He had looked at me across the signing table with an expression I spent nine years refusing to name, and now a stranger was telling me the name of it: *sorry.*

*What else does he say?* I wrote.

The answer took longer to come. When it did, the letters were smaller.

*He says you were eleven when you first asked him why the crown was heavy, and he lied to you. He says the lie was the kindest thing he had, and it wasn't kind enough. He says — * the line wavered, refroze, continued — *he says he watches you sleep sometimes, and he hates what he sees wearing your face, and he cannot leave, none of them can, they are bound into the oath same as you. He says don't trust the gentlest voice in the chorus. The gentle ones are oldest.*

I pressed my forehead to the cold glass until it hurt.

All those years I had assumed the dead kings were my tormentors by nature — hungry things, cruel things, the way a wolf is cruel without meaning to be. I had never once considered they might be prisoners. That the chorus might be full of men like me, hosts who failed, still bound by a vow their ancestors made when the world was colder and more frightened.

*Tell him,* I wrote, and my breath shook so badly the letters came out ragged, *that I forgive him. Tell him I know what it costs to lie to someone you love. I've been doing it to a whole kingdom.*

Below my words, after a long moment, a single additional line appeared in that careful carver's hand:

*He heard you. He says — and I'm writing exactly what he said — "That's my boy." *

* * *

Frost melts. We both knew the dawn letters were a temporary architecture, one lazy guard away from disaster. So the tokens began.

They came to me hidden in ordinary things: the seam of a delivered cloak, the wax of a candle, once — audaciously — tucked inside the ceremonial fruit bowl, carved so finely into the skin of an apple that I only found it by feel. Each token was bone, small as a coin, worked with images too dense for a casual eye. And when I held them, I could almost hear them. Not the chorus — something quieter, warmer. Her voice folded into the carving like scent into cloth.

The first one showed a boy fishing off a frozen jetty. I stared at it for an hour before understanding that she hadn't invented him. That was me. That was the cove below the summer lodge at Greywater, before my father became king-in-earnest, before the oath, before everything. I had told no one about that jetty in twenty years.

*How?* I wrote in the frost that dawn.

*Your dead father talks,* came the answer. *He talks constantly. Mostly complaints about the plumbing in the crypt. But he told me about the jetty. He says it's the last year you were only yours.*

Only yours. As if I had ever stopped being anyone's but theirs.

I started sending things back. I had no craft, no carving — my rebellion remained limited to breath and borrowed pens. But I had memory, and memory turned out to be currency she accepted gladly. Through the tokens and the frosted glass, I gave her the boy I had been: the boy who was afraid of horses and hid it by naming all of them; who had broken his arm falling out of the library window trying to reach a book on dragons that turned out to be about taxation; who had sworn, at thirteen, that he would never wear the crown, and whose father had smiled sadly and said *yes, my love, of course*, in the voice of a man watching weather approach.

She gave me herself in return, in the narrow rationed language we could afford. That her gift wasn't chosen — the dead had simply always spoken to her, since childhood, since before she knew other people heard silence. That she had come to Greyhallow three years ago with nothing but a knife and her mother's technique, and had survived by making charms too small and too quiet for the law to notice. That her mother had vanished when she was fourteen, and that she had spent her whole life since assuming the woman had run.

*She didn't run,* Vesper wrote one dawn, and the letters were strange, stiff, as if her hand had resisted forming them. *I'm beginning to think she was taken. I'm beginning to think everything about me was arranged before I was born. Your father says the crown has always sought a bone-speaker. Says they used to keep one at court, generations back. Says the oath likes us. Says we're the only ones who can hear it clearly enough to serve it.*

I wrote back fast, anger burning through the fear: *Then we will make sure it regrets seeking you.*

And below my words, in her hand: *Careful, Majesty. That sounded almost like a promise.*

It was. I pressed my palm flat against the glass over her letters, over the place where her words were already fading, and let myself mean it completely.

* * *

The danger grew the way dangers do: gradually, then suddenly.

The thing noticed the missing minutes. It had always noticed them, of course — the dawn hours it slept through, the small gaps in my days it couldn't account for. For nine years the gaps had contained nothing but a sleeping man breathing in his own bed. Now they contained a correspondence. Now there was evidence, however perishable, and the thing in my crown had worn nine kings before me and knew precisely what evidence meant.

It began waking earlier. Testing the edges of the dark bell. Twice I felt it stir while the frost letters were still wet on the glass, and I had to stand very still and let it surface through me, let it take my hands and walk me back to bed like a child, wearing my body's obedience like a glove, while thirty feet of frozen glass held everything I had left.

The second time, it paused at the window.

I felt it look. I felt my own eyes move across the fading letters — *careful, majesty* — and felt it consider them, the way you consider weather. Then it made my mouth smile, closed my eyes, and sank back down.

In the morning the glass was clear, and I did not know whether it had dismissed the words as a king's idle dreaming or filed them away for later. That not-knowing was the worst part. That was always the worst part.

* * *

On the ninth dawn, she sent a token that was different from all the others.

It arrived in my breakfast bread, which was either carelessness or a message in itself — *they're searching the deliveries now; hide it where they'll never look*. I found it by breaking the loaf open with my hands, and it was warm from the oven, and it was not bone.

It was whale ivory, older-looking than anything she'd sent, yellowed at the edges, and carved with two figures: a crowned man kneeling, and a woman standing over him with a tool raised — not a weapon, a burin, a carver's blade — working at the base of his crown. Around them ran a border of letters in a script I didn't recognize, dense and deliberate.

Beneath the figures, in tiny characters I could read: *This is possible. This is the price. Think before dawn comes again.*

I held it a long time. The fire had gone out. My breath hung in the air, and for once I didn't waste it on words.

Possible. She was telling me it was possible — unbinding, freedom, an end to the thing that had eaten nine kings and was slowly eating me. And a price. Of course a price. Nothing in my reign had ever come free; why should my rescue?

At the east window, in shaking letters, I asked the only question that mattered:

*Whose price?*

The frost answered in her smallest handwriting yet, the words already thinning at the edges, already going:

*The carver's. Always the carver's. Ask me again when you've decided you can live with an answer you won't like.*

And then, lower, added quickly, as if she had hesitated a long time before allowing herself the extravagance:

*Aldric — the boy on the jetty deserved better than this. So did you. Hold on nine more dawns. I think I've found my mother's work, and if I'm right, it was always meant for you.*

I stood at the glass until the letters vanished, saying her name to the empty cold, over and over, silently, like a prayer to a saint who could actually hear me.

Behind my eyes, deep down where it slept, the thing in my crown stirred — and for the first time in nine years, I could have sworn I heard it dreaming.

Not of winter.

Of her.
