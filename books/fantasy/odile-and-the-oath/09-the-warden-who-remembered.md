## 9. The Warden Who Remembered

The old man sold roasted chestnuts outside the tannery gate, and he was the only person in Greyhallow who didn't stare at me.

I'd been in the palace eleven days by then — long enough to learn the rhythm of my gilded captivity. Mornings with Serane's questions. Afternoons with the relic of the first Hollow King and its terrible confession: an oath given flesh, a vow that outlived its purpose and now ate its hosts alive. Evenings alone in a room too fine for a prisoner and too watched for a guest.

Serane had started letting me walk the outer grounds with an escort. "You think better when you move," she'd said, which was either kindness or strategy, and with Serane it was always both. So I walked. And on the third day of walking, I smelled chestnuts and remembered being nine years old, and something in my chest cracked along a seam I hadn't known was there.

He stood at a brazier near the tannery wall, a barrel-chested man with a grey beard trimmed short and hands so scarred they looked like tree bark. He didn't look at me the way the palace servants did — the quick flick, the lowered eyes, the calculation of what I might be worth or cost them. He looked at my hands.

"You hold your knife wrong," he said.

I stopped. The escort — a young Warden named Pell, bored and half-asleep — drifted two steps behind me, close enough to hear everything and understand nothing.

"Excuse me?"

"Your knife." The old man nodded at my right hand, where I'd been turning a scrap of antler without realizing I'd taken it out. Nerves. I always carved when I was nervous; the dead found it soothing, which told you something about the dead. "Left thumb braced under the blade. Most carvers brace over. You brace under."

"It's just how I learned."

"Who taught you?"

"A woman in the Saltmarket," I said, because that was the story, the one I'd told Wardens and customers and myself for twenty years. A woman in the Saltmarket, no name given, gone before I could give one. "She died when I was small."

The old man looked at me for a long time. Chestnuts crackled in his brazier. Somewhere behind the tannery wall, a dog was losing an argument with a cart.

"No," he said quietly. "She didn't."

* * *

His name was Doss. Formerly Warden-Second Doss, retired nineteen years, formerly the man who had signed the ledger the night they took her.

We sat on an overturned crate behind his stall while Pell hovered at a distance that was technically supervision and practically eavesdropping. Doss poured tea from a dented tin pot into two cups, and his hands shook, and I understood the shaking wasn't age.

"I need you to say it plainly," I said. "Whatever you're about to say. Say it plainly."

"She didn't die, girl. She was taken." He blew on his tea though it wasn't hot enough to steam. "Taken up. To the palace."

The words went through me like cold water through cloth. I'd built my whole life on that absence — the woman in the Saltmarket, the story I told so often it had worn smooth as river stone. My mother fled. That was the story. Fled the city, fled the Wardens, left her daughter with a knife and a craft and no name, because running was what charm-sellers did when the law came. It was easier to be abandoned than hunted. Easier to be angry than afraid.

"When?" My voice came out flat. Dead-flat, like bone.

"Winter. Twenty-two years back." He stared into his cup. "I remember the date because it was the last arrest I ever made. I put down my warrant after that. Told them my eyes were going. They weren't. My eyes were fine."

"Why her? Why then?"

Doss set the cup down. His scarred hands folded around each other like they were holding each other still.

"Because the crown was dying," he said. "And someone in the palace knew there was a carver in Greyhallow who could hear the dead in ivory. Not charms — real hearing. Deep hearing. The kind that runs in blood." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and steady at once. "They'd been looking for years. Quietly. No warrants, no lists. Just old men like me asking questions in markets. And then we found her, and I signed the ledger, and she looked at me—" His voice caught. "She looked at me the way you're looking at me now. Like she already knew."

The dead murmured somewhere far off, in the whalebone pendant I wore against my skin — complaints, mostly, the eternal grumbling of people with unfinished business. But right then I couldn't have heard them if they'd screamed.

"My name," I said. "What did they write in the ledger?"

"Maren Ishikawa."

Maren. I mouthed it silently, tasting it. All these years I'd carried a blank where a mother should be, and now the blank had a shape, and the shape hurt worse than the blank ever had.

"They took her to serve the king," Doss said. "The old king. Aldric's father. The Hollow King before this Hollow King." He rubbed his face. "You've seen what sits in that crown. Whatever it is, it's hungry, and it's old, and the palace has spent a century feeding it whatever might slow it down. Priests. Scholars. Carvers." He paused. "Mothers."

"And it didn't work," I said. "It ate her anyway."

"I don't know." His voice dropped. "That's the truth, girl. Nobody knows. She went up those steps twenty-two years ago and never came down, and every year after, the palace paid my pension regular as rain, and I understood it was for my silence, and I took it, and I've hated myself every winter since." He finally looked at me straight. "Until three weeks ago. Until the whole city started whispering about a bone-speaker arrested off the street, and I heard your description, and I thought — no. It can't be. And then I saw you at the market, and you held your knife wrong, and I knew."

"Wrong," I repeated. "You said wrong."

"Under-braced. Left thumb under the blade." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile I've ever seen on a living face. "Only one carver in Greyhallow ever taught that grip. Said it gave you better control on curved bone. Said the dead liked curves." He reached across the space between us, slowly, giving me time to pull away, and touched the back of my hand. "She used to say that. *The dead like curves.*"

Somewhere inside me, twenty-two years of carefully constructed anger collapsed all at once, like a snow-laden roof, and underneath it wasn't anger at all. It was a child standing in a doorway watching a woman walk away with Wardens on either side, believing, because everyone told her so, that the woman had chosen to go.

"She didn't leave me," I said. It wasn't a question. I said it anyway, out loud, because some things need air.

"No," Doss said. "She fought. Kicked a hole in my knee I still feel in winter. She fought the whole way up the hill." He swallowed. "But she stopped fighting when she saw the palace gates. Do you know why?"

I shook my head.

"Because she'd read the same book your scholar friend carries," he said. "Or one like it. Prophecies keep copies, girl. The Crown keeps more." His hand tightened briefly on mine, then withdrew. "She told me, on the steps, before they took her inside. Last thing she ever said to anyone outside those walls. She said: *Tell my daughter, if I ever have one, that the grip is not wrong. Tell her the grip will bring her here.*"

The world went very quiet. Even the dog behind the tannery wall had stopped arguing.

"I don't have a daughter," I managed. "I don't— I was four. How would she—"

"The prophecy," Doss said simply. "Her page had tomorrow's date on it too. Her own name, and a line beneath it, and the line said the daughter would come after." He shrugged, helpless, ancient. "I didn't understand it then. I don't understand it now. But I've had twenty-two years to turn it over, and here's what I know: they didn't find you three weeks ago because you slipped. They found you because you were always going to be found. You were meant to be found. Your mother made sure of it — taught you the grip, left you the craft, carved the road into your hands before she ever walked up that hill."

Pell shifted behind me, and I realized distantly that my tea had gone cold in my hands, that I hadn't drunk any of it, that I was shaking the way Doss's hands shook.

"What was the rest of her line?" I asked. "On her page. What did it say?"

Doss hesitated. And I saw — really saw, the way I saw things in bone — that he'd been carrying this too, all these years, a message he'd failed to deliver until now.

"*The binding wants a bloodline,*" he recited, slow, like a man reading from a page burned into his memory. "*One to prepare the way. One to pay the price.*"

* * *

I walked back through the palace grounds without seeing any of it. Frost on the hedges. Guards at their posts. The grey sky pressing down like a lid.

One to prepare the way. One to pay the price.

My mother hadn't been dragged to the palace to fail. She'd been brought there to begin something — to carve, to listen, to learn the shape of the thing in the crown well enough to pass that knowledge down in the only language she trusted. Not letters. Bone. Grip. The angle of a thumb beneath a blade. Twenty-two years ago she'd started carving a message she knew she'd never live to deliver, and the message was me.

In my room, I took out the whalebone pendant and held it to my ear the way other people press shells, listening past the grumble of ordinary dead for anything older, anything patient, anything that sounded like it had been waiting.

Nothing. Just the usual murmur. Complaints about unfinished business.

But when I set the pendant down, my eye fell on my hands — my mother's grip, my mother's scars forming slowly on my palms — and for the first time since the scholar Ilsa had opened that cursed book in a night market, I wasn't afraid of the prophecy.

I was furious at it. There's a difference. Fear freezes. Fury moves.

Someone had taken my mother up a hill to feed a hunger, and the Crown had called it service, and the Wardens had called it law, and a tired old man had called it duty, and none of them had called it what it was. And now the same machinery had collected me, gently, patiently, exactly on schedule — because I was the second line of a sentence my mother had started writing before I could talk.

One to prepare the way.

One to pay the price.

A knock at the door. Serane's knock — two sharp, one soft, the rhythm I'd learned to read like weather.

"Vesper." Her voice through the wood, controlled, but with a hairline crack in it I hadn't heard before. "You need to come now. Something's happened in the east wing. Something's happened to the king."

I rose, and my mother's grip closed around the door handle, and I went.
