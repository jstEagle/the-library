## 10. Ilsa's True Page

They let Ilsa out through the Warden gate at dusk, into the smell of coal smoke and wet stone, and the first thing she did was throw up in a gutter.

Not from the cell. The cell had been clean, almost courteous — a cot, a blanket that smelled of cedar, three days of silence broken only by the sound of Serane Hale's boots passing her door twice a day, never stopping. No, Ilsa vomited because of the light. Three days underground, and Greyhallow's winter dusk hit her like a blow: the amber windows, the market criers shouting the hour, a girl selling roasted chestnuts on the corner with chilblains on her knuckles. All of it ordinary. All of it still standing.

*It stands because he does,* said the voice she'd been carrying since she was nineteen years old. Her teacher's voice, or the shape her teacher's voice had left behind. *And it will stand until he falls. Everything else is decoration.*

She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and walked north, away from the palace district, past the tanneries, to where the streets stopped pretending and the houses leaned together like conspirators. The Unbound kept a safehouse above a dyer's shop, behind a door painted the exact grey of forty other doors on the street. She knocked the pattern — two, pause, three, pause, one — and waited for the eye-slot to slide open.

"You were supposed to be quiet," said Maren Voss, when the door opened.

"I was supposed to be invisible," Ilsa said. "I was neither."

Maren stepped aside to let her in. Maren was fifty-one years old and had hands like tree roots, a former Warden who had quit the order the night her brother was burned for selling fever-charms, and she ran the Greyhallow cell the way winter itself ran things: patiently, inevitably, without hatred and without mercy. Behind her, in the long low room that smelled of dye-vats and tallow, six faces turned toward Ilsa. Six people who had spent three days believing she was dead or worse.

"Tell us," Maren said, and sat.

So Ilsa told them. She told it badly, the way you tell a thing when you've been rehearsing the honest version for three days and know you'll be executed by your own side for telling it. She told them about the night market and the bone-carver with the ink-stained fingers and the way the whalebone charm had seemed to *hum* when Vesper Ishikawa picked it up. She told them about the book — the century-old prophecy codex she'd stolen from a private collection in Corvane and smuggled across the border sewn into the lining of her coat. She told them about the last page. Tomorrow's date, written a hundred years ago, in a hand the archivists had authenticated down to the iron-gall oxidation of the ink.

And beside the date, two names. The Hollow King. And hers — no. *Vesper's.* Ilsa had shown her the page with her own thumb covering nothing, hiding nothing, and watched the color leave the woman's face.

"They took her," Ilsa said. "Three nights ago. Wardens, plainclothes, tipped off before we could move. They took me too because I was holding the book." She swallowed. "Then they let me go. Inquisitor Hale herself unlocked the door. She said the bargain was Vesper's cooperation for my freedom. She said—" Ilsa's voice caught, and she hated it. "She said Vesper asked for it. Asked for my release. First thing. Before food, before water, before they'd even told her what the charges were."

The room was silent. Somewhere below them, the dyer's vat turned over with a wet, rhythmic groan, like something breathing in its sleep.

"And?" Maren said. "What did you learn inside? What do they intend?"

Ilsa looked at her hands. They were shaking. She made them stop, the old discipline, the training she'd bought with a forged name and four years of borrowed scholarship — *Ilsa Veyranne, junior archivist, Royal Collegium at Corvane*, a woman who had never existed, whose credentials had cost her every coin she'd saved and one small crime of forgery that had felt, at nineteen, like the first true act of her life.

"They intend to confirm whether the king is possessed," she said. "Using her. Using whatever she hears in the bones. And then—" She stopped.

"Then," Maren prompted.

"Then there are two endings in the prophecy," Ilsa said. "You've read my translation. The king unmade, or the king wed. The Crown wants the second one. They want to *marry him off* to a bone-speaker and call the winter solved. A wedding as policy." She heard her own voice climb and forced it flat. "Serane Hale offered Vesper a bargain, and Vesper took it, and now she's in the palace being measured for a crown instead of—"

"Instead of doing what we sent you to make possible," Maren finished quietly.

The room's silence changed texture. It went from listening to weighing.

Ilsa stood. "Say it," she said. "Whatever you're deciding, say it to my face."

Maren didn't rise. That was worse. Maren rising meant anger; Maren sitting meant judgment. "We sent you to find the bone-speaker," Maren said, "because the codex says the speaker can end the oath. We assumed — you assumed — that ending the oath meant ending the king. That was the whole architecture of the plan, Ilsa. Find the speaker, put her in reach of the Hollow King, and let the prophecy take the road we've prayed for since before you were born. One death. One. Against thirty thousand who freeze every long winter while the Crown hoards grain and calls it providence."

"One death," Ilsa repeated.

"You hesitated just now," Maren said. "When you told us about the bargain. About her asking for your release. You told it like a confession, not a report. So I'm asking you plainly, in front of everyone, so there is no rot under the floorboards later." Her root-hard hands folded on the table. "Do you still want the king dead?"

* * *

Here is what Ilsa had not told anyone in the safehouse, and what she had not told Serane Hale either, though the inquisitor had looked at her across the interrogation table with those patient grey eyes as if reading an inventory of everything she owned:

She had engineered all of it. The meeting at the night market had not been chance. She'd tracked Vesper Ishikawa for eleven months — through charm-seller gossip, through a fence in Saltmarket who moved bone-work, through three false leads and one very expensive mistake. She'd learned the carver's routes, her prices, her habit of working late with the shutters cracked because she liked hearing the street. And on the appointed night, Ilsa had dressed in her scholar's borrowed authority and approached the stall with a rehearsed stammer and a genuine book, because the best lies are load-bearing truths, and the codex was real, and the date was real, and Vesper's name on the final page was real.

Only the framing had been forged. Only the friendship.

Except that by the third night in the cell, turning the word *friendship* over and over like a counterfeit coin looking for the flaw, Ilsa had begun to suspect the flaw was in the assay, not the metal.

Because Vesper had heard the dead. That was documented, verified by three days of Inquisitorial testing that Ilsa had witnessed through a doorway — whalebone held to her ear, her face going strange, her voice reciting a dead man's debts in his own cadence. And if the dead spoke through her, truly spoke, then Ilsa had to reckon with something her teachers in the Unbound had never prepared her for: what if the dead kings in the crown didn't want to be avenged? What if they wanted to be *released*?

There was a difference. The Unbound's whole theology rested on erasing it. Kill the host, break the vessel, end the oath — mercy as amputation. But release was different. Release required the thing to be unwound carefully, thread by thread, by someone who could hear the threads. Release required the speaker alive and whole.

Amputation required a corpse. And the prophecy, that maddening hundred-year-old page, offered both roads and refused to say which one was true.

"Do you still want him dead?" Maren asked again.

Ilsa thought about Vesper's face when she'd read the page — not fear, exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. Like a woman finding her own handwriting in someone else's diary. And she thought about the moment the Wardens came, how Vesper had shoved the luck-charm — the finished bride's charm, hours of work — into Ilsa's pocket before raising her hands. *Take it. Someone should get some use out of it.* Ilsa still had it. It sat against her ribs right now, warm as a held bird.

"No," she said.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Ilsa listened to it fall for a long time before she went on.

"I don't want him dead anymore. Maybe I never did. Maybe I wanted *something* dead — the oath, the winter, the arithmetic that trades thirty thousand lives for one man's pulse — and killing him was just the only tool anyone ever taught us to hold." She turned to face the whole room, all six of them, including Tomas the dyer's son who was seventeen and had already lost two siblings to a famine-winter, including Brother Anselm who had defrocked himself rather than bless the coronation. "But I'll tell you what I learned in that cell, holding this." She drew out the charm. "She heard his father apologize to him through a signet ring. His *father*. Dead nine years, sealed in the crown's chorus with the rest of them, and the first thing he spends his voice on is an apology. Do dead men who want vengeance spend their voices on apologies? Those kings aren't our enemy. They're hostages. Every one of them was a man who took the oath to save us, and we've spent a century planning to murder the latest hostage and call it liberation."

"So what are you saying?" Tomas demanded. He was on his feet, seventeen and furious, which was the correct age for fury. "You're saying we just — trust the Crown? Trust Hale? Trust the *prophecy's* nice version, the wedding version, and hope the winter breaks out of goodwill?"

"I'm saying the middle path exists," Ilsa said, "and it runs through a bone-carver who asked for my freedom before she asked for bread, and I will not be the reason it closes."

"It's narrower than you think," Maren said.

"Then we walk it single file."

Maren studied her for a long, cold moment. Around the room, Ilsa could feel the cell fracturing along lines that had always been there and never once named: those who wanted the oath ended by any means, and those who had joined because they wanted *someone*, finally, to pay. Tomas belonged to the second kind. So, if she was honest, had she, at nineteen.

"The Codex gives two roads," Maren said at last. "You've chosen a third that isn't written anywhere. Fine. But understand the cost of choosing unwritten roads, Ilsa Veyranne — or whatever your true name is." A flicker; even now, after four years, Maren only ever used the false one. "If the binding fails, if the speaker falters, the winter doesn't wait politely. The entity eats its host faster, the cold comes early, and every soul in this city pays for our experiment. There is no version of this where we get to be spectators. If we back her play, we're inside it. All the way in."

"I know."

"And if the Crown decides the speaker is more useful married than free — if they move her to the chapel crypt, or dose her, or simply keep her until the wedding and discard her after—" Maren's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Will you still be able to tell yourself you chose the gentle road?"

"That's why I'm here," Ilsa said. "That's what I'm asking you for. Eyes inside. Resources. Because in three days, maybe less, the Unbound's name is going to come up in that palace — mine, yours, all of ours — and when it does, I would rather we were a knife pointed at the oath than at her."

* * *

They argued until the candles drowned in their own wax. Maren rationed agreement the way she rationed everything: the safehouse would stay closed to Ilsa until she'd proven the palace wasn't watching her; two runners would be assigned, no more; contact only through the chestnut-girl on the corner, who was fourteen and unremarkable and the best courier in Greyhallow. It was less than Ilsa wanted and more than she'd expected, and she understood it for what it was — a rope thrown to someone the cell suspected of falling, thrown anyway because Maren Voss had buried a brother and knew the exact weight of a person teetering.

Near midnight, alone in the loft above the dye-room where they'd given her a pallet, Ilsa took out the bride's luck-charm and turned it in the candlelight. Whalebone, carved fine as lace, with a running knot at its heart that no blade had cut — Vesper had shaped it, somehow, the way water shapes stone. Ilsa pressed it to her ear, feeling foolish, feeling desperate, knowing she would hear nothing.

She heard nothing.

But she remembered the sound it had made in Vesper's hands. A hum, low and glad, like a struck string remembering music. The dead lived in the bone. The dead were *waiting* in the bone. And somewhere across the sleeping city, behind walls thick enough to swallow a woman whole, the same gift was pressed to the ear of a dying king's prison — listening to a chorus of dead kings — and Ilsa had to decide, tonight, with her forged name and her real heart, whether she trusted what they might teach her.

Below the window, the chestnut-girl passed on her rounds, and paused — barely a hitch in her stride, a shadow adjusting its hood — and left something wedged in the drainpipe's joint, the way they'd agreed.

Ilsa leaned out and retrieved it. A scrap of paper, folded to the size of a thumbnail, in the runner's blunt pencil-hand:

*Palace gossip moves fast tonight. The king missed his own council. Sat through it smiling and signed orders in a hand nobody recognized — even his chamberlain. They're whispering "possession" now, loud enough to be overheard. And the bone-speaker has been moved to the tower rooms.*

*One more thing. The old Wardens' roster was checked today. Somebody at court is asking after a charmer named Ishikawa — not the daughter. The mother. Records say she entered the palace service eleven years ago and never left.*

Ilsa read it twice. Then a third time, and the paper trembled, because the mother — the missing mother, the craft Vesper carried like an inherited debt, the woman who had vanished from Greyhallow's registers when Vesper was a child — hadn't fled at all.

She'd been taken to the crown. Taken to carve for the very thing they were trying to bind. Which meant the prophecy's road had been walked before, by another pair of hands, and had failed — or been stopped.

Ilsa folded the note small and held it to the candle flame, watching her options burn down to ash in a tin dish. By morning she had to choose which truth to carry across the city: the one that would harden the Unbound into killers, or the one that might, against all odds, aim them at the oath instead.

She thought of Vesper's voice in the dark of the wagon, after the arrest, when they'd both believed the worst: *Whoever told them about my mother's work — I hope they got paid well. It cost them their soul.*

Someone had. Someone close. And tomorrow, Ilsa Veyranne — forged scholar, failed radical, friend — would have to walk back through the palace gates and find out whether that someone was wearing a Warden's badge, a chamberlain's keys, or a crown.

Outside, the first snow of the deep cold began to fall — early, too early, weeks ahead of any winter in living memory — and did not stop.
