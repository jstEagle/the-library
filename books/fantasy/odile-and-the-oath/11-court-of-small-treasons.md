## 11. Court of Small Treasons

Aldric woke on the floor of his own bedchamber, cheek against cold stone, and knew before he opened his eyes that he had lost hours.

He could tell by the light. It came through the east windows at the wrong angle for morning—low and gold and already leaning—and by the ache in his knees, which meant he had been standing a long time. By the taste in his mouth, which was always the worst part: ink and tallow and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in a dead man's pantry.

*Four hours,* said a voice that was not a voice, from the crown that was not a crown. *Perhaps five. You missed nothing of consequence.*

"You would say that," Aldric murmured to the empty room, "even if you'd burned the city."

*I considered it.* The voice had no malice in it. That was the horror of it, after nine years. It never had. It spoke the way winter speaks—patient, procedural, without any opinion at all about what it buried.

He got up. His body obeyed him now; it always did, in the hours after, as if the thing in the crown were a landlord who kept the rooms clean between tenants. He walked to the desk by the window and looked at what his hands had done while he was away.

Three decrees, signed in his own hand. He read them standing very still.

The first remitted taxes on the salt trade—a sensible enough act, the kind of thing a king might do, though Aldric could not remember deciding it. The second ordered the eastern granaries opened early, which was generous, which was *kind*, and which made his skin crawl precisely because he could not find the reason for it anywhere in himself. Kindness was not in the oath's vocabulary. Kindness was bait.

The third decree was short. One line.

*Let it be known that all sellers of charms within the walls of Greyhallow shall present themselves to the Wardens for registry, upon pain of banishment.*

Aldric read it three times. Then he sat down slowly, because his legs had opinions of their own.

Charm-sellers. Small magics, bone-carved, whispered over—the only magic the poor of this city were permitted, the only warmth the long winter allowed them. And someone had signed an order to flush them into the open, in a hand indistinguishable from his own.

He thought of a woman with sawdust in her hair and a voice like a struck bell, who heard the dead in whalebone. He thought of her name written in frost on his own window, and how she had answered.

"No," he said aloud. "I won't let this stand."

*You have no say,* said the crown, gently. *You never did. You only ever had the terms.*

---

The chamberlain's name was Edmun Voss, and he had served the Crown for thirty-one years, through two kings and one coronation that still gave him dreams.

He noticed the gaps the way he noticed everything: late, reluctantly, and then all at once.

It began with the wax. A king's seal carried habits the way a face carried wrinkles—the pressure of the thumb, the tilt of the stamp, the little crescent where Aldric always let the wax pool a half-second too long before pressing. Edmun had catalogued these habits years ago, not out of sentiment but because a chamberlain who cannot tell a king's true seal from a forged one does not survive his office. For nine years the seals had been consistent. Whatever wore the crown, it had learned to press wax exactly as Aldric pressed wax.

But in the last month, the seals had begun arriving at odd hours. Midnight. The hour before dawn. Once, at the changing of the watch, when the king was known to sleep—or had been known, once, when sleeping was something he still did.

And the handwriting. Edmun held the third decree up to the light in his office below the council chambers, comparing it against letters from a year past. The hand was perfect. That was what troubled him. Kings' hands drifted. They grew hurried or drunk or old. This hand had stopped drifting two years ago and had been flawless ever since, and no living man's writing was flawless, and therefore—

Therefore what?

Edmun set the decree down and rubbed his eyes. There were questions a loyal servant asked, and there were questions a surviving servant did not. Thirty-one years had taught him the difference, mostly.

But last week he had seen the king in the gallery at dusk, standing at a window, breath fogging the glass. Ordinary enough. Except that the fog had not dispersed. It had hung there in lines, in strokes, deliberate as script, and the king had stared at it with an expression Edmun had no name for—hunger, maybe, or prayer—and then wiped it away with his sleeve like a man destroying evidence.

Kings did not write messages in frost. Kings did not need to.

Edmun took the third decree—the charm-sellers, God help them all—and locked it in the drawer where he kept the things he could not yet decide about. The drawer was getting full.

---

* * *

That night, Aldric stood at his window and breathed.

It was a ridiculous thing, a king of Greyhallow hunched at his own glass like a schoolboy, exhaling carefully, shaping each cloud of breath with a fingertip before it faded. But it was the only channel they had. The entity watched his hands. It watched his lips when he spoke aloud. It did not, apparently, consider frozen breath worth monitoring—which told Aldric something comforting and terrible about how thoroughly it believed it had already won.

*V. Are you safe. The decree tonight was not mine. If Wardens come to your door, run, and I will burn my kingdom down looking for you.*

He watched the words hang, silver in the moonlight, and hated how much of his heart he had just exposed to a pane of glass.

The answer came faster than usual, which meant she had been waiting too.

*A. I saw the decree. Serane showed me. She thinks it's a test of some kind—I think she's starting to wonder whose hand it really is. I'm safe. The dead in the market bones are singing something new. Not warnings anymore. Something like a wedding song. I hate it.*

Aldric laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, the first in days—and immediately pressed his hand over his mouth like a boy caught out. Down in the courtyard, a guard glanced up at the lit window. Aldric turned his back and waited until the footsteps moved on.

When he turned back, she had added more:

*My mother carved bindings. Did you know that? The old Warden told me. She tried to bind the crown once. There's a workshop under the chapel with her half-finished work in it, and one piece with a child's name on it that I can't stop thinking about. Yours. She knew about you before you were crowned. I don't know yet what that means. I'm afraid to know.*

He read it twice. His mother's workshop. Under the chapel. A piece bearing his childhood name—*Aldric, son of Maren, called Wren by his nurse because he sang to the ravens*—carved into bone by hands that had died trying to reach him.

*Ishikawa,* he wrote, slow, careful, the letters fat and clumsy in the freezing air. *Whatever it means, it means you were sent. Not by prophecy. By her. Finish her work. I will hold the doors as long as I can.*

*How long is as long as you can?*

He looked at his own reflection in the glass, ghosted over the fading words. Behind the reflection, deeper, something else looked back—the suggestion of a shape wearing his face the way a glove wears a hand.

*Less every day,* he wrote. *Last week it wore me for four hours. Yesterday, six. Today I woke on the floor. It is learning to be me faster than I am learning to fight it. Soon it will not need to put me down at all. It will simply keep me, the way you keep a coat you no longer wear but cannot throw out.*

Silence from the glass. Long enough that he thought the frost had taken her silence for agreement.

Then: *Then we are out of time. I need the vaults. The original oath—it has to be there, the first contract, the grammar of the binding. My mother's notes reference it. Can you get me access?*

Aldric closed his eyes. The vaults lay beneath the throne room, behind three doors, warded by oaths older than his line. Even he entered them rarely. Even he entered them *accompanied*.

*I will try,* he wrote. *Tomorrow. During the court session. It wears me in public now—I'll make sure it's me when it matters and let it have the ceremony. It likes ceremony. It performs beautifully.*

*That's the saddest sentence I've ever read.*

*Welcome to my kingdom.*

For a moment neither wrote anything, and the window filled with nothing but their shared breath, mingling, indistinguishable. Aldric found he liked that better than the words.

*One more thing,* she wrote at last. *The dead kings. I've been listening to them properly now. They're not what I thought. None of them are. When I understand the whole of it, you'll be the first to know. Sleep if it lets you. —V.*

*It doesn't,* he wrote. *But thank you for pretending otherwise. —A.*

The words faded. He stayed at the window a while longer anyway, breathing fog onto glass like a man writing love letters to winter itself, until the crown stirred at the back of his skull with something almost like amusement.

*Sentiment,* it observed. *Inefficient. But useful. Sentiment makes hosts predictable.*

"Get out of my head," Aldric said mildly.

*I live here,* it said. *You invited me. Remember?*

He did remember. That was the cruelest architecture of the whole prison: he had built it himself, one starving winter nine years ago, one desperate vow, one signature in a language he had not understood he was signing. Every host had. The dead kings in the crown had all, once, been young men at windows, bargaining with the cold.

---

* * *

Court convened at midmorning, and the thing wore him through all of it.

Aldric had learned to make the separations smaller. Instead of vanishing entirely, he learned to retreat to a room at the back of his own mind—a narrow place, cold, with one window—and watch the proceedings through it like a man watching weather. From there he saw his mouth pronounce judgments on boundary disputes and grain tariffs. He saw his hands gesture with a fluency that had taken the entity years to master and that he privately resented, since it gestured better than he ever had. He saw the court bow, and the lords smile, and none of them see anything wrong, because nothing was wrong, nothing had been wrong for nine years, and the performance was flawless.

Except today. Today there was a hitch.

It came during the petitions, near the end. A merchant from the salt quarter, kneeling, complaining of a Warden levy on his warehouse. Routine. The entity began its reply in Aldric's voice—and then stopped. Half a second. Less. A silence so small that anyone else would have called it a pause for thought.

But Aldric, watching from the back room of himself, felt the machinery stutter. Because in that half-second, the merchant's petition had brushed against something in the king's memory—the salt quarter, the night market, a stall of white bone charms, a woman laughing at something the scholar said—and the entity had reached for the memory to speak smoothly and found it *guarded*. Found it warm. Found it defended by a man who was supposedly asleep in a narrow room with one window.

The entity recovered instantly. The petition was granted, the merchant dismissed, the court moved on. But Aldric felt it turn its attention inward afterward, slow and considering, the way a man turns over a coin that rang wrong.

*You keep things from me now,* it said, in the space between sentences where no one else could hear.

"I keep nothing," Aldric said, aloud, smiling at the Lord Marshal as though mid-conversation. "I am a tenant. Tenants keep nothing."

*Precisely.* A pause. *Which is why I am revising the lease.*

That afternoon, the summons went out under the king's seal: the charm-sellers' registry would begin in seven days, enforced by the Wardens, beginning in the night markets of the low town. And a second order, small, easily missed among the day's papers: the palace chapel would be closed for renovation. All access suspended. Indefinitely.

Aldric read the second order from his narrow room with one window, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the thing in the crown had finally noticed the same thread he had. The workshop. The mother. The unfinished binding with a child's name on it.

It was going to wall off the past before anyone could use it.

---

* * *

Edmun Voss found the chapel order at dusk, in the stack of documents awaiting his countersignature, and he sat with it for a long time.

Closed chapels were not unusual. Renovations were not unusual. What was unusual was the timing—he had walked past the chapel that very morning and seen nothing amiss—and the phrasing, which was the king's phrasing exactly, down to the formal archaism Aldric used only in orders concerning the oldest parts of the palace. Which meant either the king had written it, or the thing that wrote like the king had written it, and Edmun had arrived at the place where the drawer full of undecidable things overflowed.

He thought about his oath. Thirty-one years ago, a younger Edmun had knelt on these same stones and sworn to protect the king's person, the king's secrets, and the king's peace, in that order, and had spent three decades discovering that the three were rarely the same thing and never, ever all three at once.

He thought about the frost on the gallery window. He thought about the sealed drawer, and the decree condemning charm-sellers, and the rumor moving through the servants' halls like smoke—that the king had begun signing papers in his sleep, that the king had been seen talking to empty air, that the king's shadow sometimes moved a half-beat late.

He thought, most of all, about the night nine years ago when the old king died and the young one was crowned in the middle of a blizzard, and Edmun had stood at the back of the chapel and watched Aldric—nineteen years old, gray-faced, shaking—recite an oath that no one had given him time to read.

Nineteen years old. No one had given him time to read it.

Edmun Voss rose, took the chapel order out of the stack, and did not countersign it. Instead he folded it into the drawer with the others, locked the drawer, and put the key in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he kept the things he had decided about after all.

Then he did the second thing, the thing he could not take back. He climbed the north stair to the royal apartments, past two guards who knew him and did not stop him, and knocked on the king's door with the triple rhythm reserved for the chamberlain alone.

The door opened. The king stood there, candlelit, exhausted, human—and behind his eyes, deep down, something else watched Edmun with the flat patience of a landlord counting his rooms.

"Your Majesty," Edmun said quietly. "Forgive the hour. I need to ask you a question, and I need you to answer me honestly, and I need you to answer as *yourself*."

The king's expression flickered—hope, terror, hope again—and when he spoke, his voice was rough, and it was his own:

"Ask it, Edmun. Before I'm not here to answer."

Down in the dark beneath the chapel, behind a door that was not yet bricked shut, something old waited in a workshop full of unfinished bindings—and seven days remained on the decree that would send Wardens hunting through every market in Greyhallow for a bone-carver named Ishikawa.
