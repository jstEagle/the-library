## 12. The Mother's Workshop

The old Warden's name was Corvane, and he walked like a man who had spent thirty years listening for footsteps that weren't his own.

He came to Vesper's cell in the grey hour before dawn — not her cell anymore, strictly; since Serane's bargain she was moved to a guest room with a lock on the outside and a window that looked out on the palace's inner court, where snow fell with the patience of something that intended to stay. Corvane didn't knock. He simply stood in the doorway holding a lantern, and when she looked up from the bone token Aldric had sent her — a small carved wolf, its jaws open around a blank space where a word should be — he said:

"You carve like her."

Vesper's hands went still. "You knew my mother."

"I arrested her." He said it flatly, without apology, the way men say things they've carried so long the weight has worn smooth. "Twenty-two years ago. Or I was meant to. She was gone before we reached the shop. Every Warden in Greyhallow believed she'd fled the city." His eyes went to the wolf token in Vesper's palm. "I believed it too. Until last week, when I saw your work through Serane Hale's reports and recognized the grip. The way you cut against the grain instead of with it. That's not taught. That's inherited."

"Where is she?"

Corvane set the lantern down on the table between them. Its light made his face into something older than it needed to be.

"Beneath us," he said. "She never left the palace at all."

* * *

They went down through the chapel of the Ninefold Oath, which no one used anymore because the Ninefold Oath had been folded into the Crown's business and business did not require kneeling. The chapel smelled of cold stone and old tallow. Corvane's lantern threw their shadows long across the floor, and Vesper noticed — the way she noticed everything now, her hearing sharpened by weeks inside these walls — that the bones in the crypt below were quiet. Not silent. Quiet. The difference mattered. Silence was absence. Quiet was restraint.

"She asked me once," Corvane said, stopping before the altar, "if I could keep a secret the way a grave keeps one. I told her yes. She said, 'Then you'll be the only man in Greyhallow who knows where I sleep.' And she showed me this."

He pressed his thumb into a carving at the altar's base — a swan, wings folded, small enough to be decoration. Something clicked. A section of floor beside the altar sank and slid, revealing stairs going down into dark.

Vesper stared at the swan. Her mother had carved swans. Little ones, all over everything — the handles of her knives, the edges of her workbench, once, memorably, the entire frame of a neighbor's mirror as payment for a debt. *Odiles*, she'd called them. Vesper had assumed it was habit.

It had been a signature. A map. A trail of breadcrumbs laid down years before Vesper was born, or at least years before Vesper could follow them.

"How long has this door been here?" she asked.

"Longer than me," Corvane said. "But she's the only one who ever opened it while I watched." He held out the lantern. "I can't go down with you. My post is above, and if anyone asks, I'm inspecting the crypt for structural rot. Which, incidentally, it has." A pause. "Your mother told me, the last time I saw her alive, that if a girl with my name for her hands ever came asking, I should let her in and ask nothing after. I've waited twenty-two years to be annoyed about how right she was."

"What happened to her? The truth."

Corvane's jaw worked. "The truth is I don't know. One winter she stopped coming up. I told myself she'd finished whatever she was doing and gone to ground somewhere far away. It was easier than the alternative." He looked at the stairs the way men look at water they've nearly drowned in. "Go down. Ask the dead. You're better at it than I ever was."

* * *

The stairs were narrow and worn in the center, hollowed by feet — many feet, over many years, though Vesper suspected most of those prints belonged to one woman making ten thousand trips. The air changed as she descended: warmer, impossibly, and smelling of cedar shavings and linseed oil and, beneath it, something mineral and sweet that she recognized from her childhood and had never been able to name. Bone dust. Old bone dust, aged like wine.

The workshop opened around her.

It was larger than the chapel above — the palace had been built in layers, century atop century, and someone had hollowed out a vault here long ago and forgotten to tell the architects. Lanterns hung from hooks, unlit but clean. A workbench ran the length of one wall, its surface scarred and grooved exactly the way her mother's bench in the city had been scarred, and Vesper felt something crack quietly in her chest, because the grooves were even in the same places. Same hands. Same habits. Twenty-two years of the same motions performed underground.

And the work.

Pieces covered every surface. Half-carved bindings, dozens of them, laid out in rows like patients in a ward. Vesper walked the length of the room slowly, her gift reaching ahead of her the way a hand reaches toward a stove to test its heat. Each piece murmured. Not the chatty complaints of market charms, not the gossip of whalebone and sheep knuckle — these were *bindings*, true ones, each one an attempt, each one unfinished, each one carrying the same exhausted refrain like a song stuck in a dying throat.

*Not enough. Not deep enough. Not willing enough.*

She picked up the nearest: a rib of some great beast, carved with interlocking script that made her eyes water to read, the grammar folding back on itself the way oaths do. It was beautiful. It was also, she understood within three heartbeats of holding it, a failure. The binding circled its purpose without landing. Like a knot tied by someone who'd learned the theory but flinched at the pull.

"They're all like that," said a voice behind her.

Vesper spun. Nothing there. But the voice hadn't come from the air — it had come from the pieces, from all of them at once, a chorus of failed bindings speaking with a single borrowed tone. She knew that tone. She'd heard it under the throne.

The dead kings.

No — not quite. Older. The tone beneath the kings, the way bedrock sits beneath soil.

"The oath itself," she whispered. "The original vow. It's in these."

"It is in everything she carved," the chorus agreed, patient as frost. "She listened to us for nineteen years. She learned our grammar better than any host before her. And still—" The chorus sighed, and the sound moved through every unfinished piece in the room like wind through a forest. "Still she could not finish. Do you know why?"

Vesper's mouth was dry. "Tell me."

"Because a binding must be given, not taken. She tried to bind the oath the way you bind a wound — from outside, with skill, with love even. But the oath only accepts what is surrendered. She was willing to spend her art. She was not willing to spend herself." A pause, and in the pause Vesper heard something almost like grief. "We do not blame her. We only note it. The work remains."

*The work remains.* Three words that had been waiting down here in the dark for two decades, patient as the winter they served.

Vesper set the rib down carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping. Then she turned to the far wall, because she'd seen it now and couldn't unsee it: a shelf apart from the others, higher, cleaner, holding a single piece under a cloth of white linen gone grey with age.

She lifted the cloth.

It was a crown. Small — child-sized, or ceremonial, or both. Carved from a single piece of bone she couldn't identify, pale as milk, worked so finely it seemed more grown than made. Around its band ran the binding grammar, complete this time, whole, finished — except for one section near the front, roughly the width of two fingers, left deliberately raw. Unfinished. Waiting.

And burned into the inner curve, in her mother's unmistakable hand — the slanted letters, the little odile tucked into the final stroke like a secret — was a name.

*Aldric.*

Not *the king*. Not *the Hollow King*. Not *His Majesty*. Just the name, the boy's name, the private name, the name a mother uses — no. Not his mother. Her mother. Written years before Aldric had ever worn the real crown, years before the oath had sunk its teeth into him, back when he was a prince nobody expected anything of, and a bone-carver in a hidden workshop beneath a chapel had already known what was coming for him and had started, in secret, in the dark, to build him a way out.

Vesper sat down on the floor. She didn't decide to. Her legs simply ended their employment.

Her mother had known. Before the coronation. Before the Hollow King. She had read the signs — in prophecy, in bone, in whatever sources a woman hidden beneath a palace could reach — and she had come here and spent nineteen years trying to save a child she'd never met, and she had failed, and then she had died down here or vanished or worse, and the work had remained, waiting, and now her daughter stood in the same room holding the same unfinished crown with the same hands.

"We were always meant to be found," Vesper said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the vault, flattened by stone. "Both of us. Me, Ilsa, the prophecy, Serane's bargain — none of it was accident. She built this. She built *toward* this."

"Not alone," the chorus said. "We helped her. As we will help you. It is the only freedom we have left — to help."

"Why?" Vesper looked up at the dark ceiling, at the weight of palace and winter and nine years of stolen kingship pressing down through the stone. "Why does an oath that eats kings want to teach the woman who'll end it?"

The silence that followed was long. When the answer came, it came from everywhere and nowhere, from the shelf and the bench and the hundred failed bindings, and it was very quiet.

"Because we were starving people once," it said. "Who made a vow to save our children. And the vow forgot the children and remembered only the hunger. We have been trying to remember for four hundred years." Another pause. "Your mother heard us say it. Now you have too. Finish the crown, bone-speaker. Finish what she could not. And when you learn the price — because you will learn it, it cannot be hidden, it is written into the grammar itself —"

"Yes?"

"—do not flinch."

* * *

Vesper worked until her fingers ached and the lantern oil burned low. She didn't finish the crown — she wasn't fool enough to try tonight, with her hands shaking and her mind full of her mother's ghost-shaped absence — but she read it. All of it. Every line of grammar, every folded clause, and near dawn she found what the chorus had promised she would find, the clause her mother must have found too, the reason nineteen years of genius had stalled at two fingers of raw bone.

The binding required a living hand. Not symbolically. Literally. The counter-oath had to be carved into the host's own bone — the crown-bone, the skull, the self — by a hand that gave up, in the act of giving, the thing the dead had granted it.

For her mother, that would have been her hearing. Whatever voices she'd heard, whatever gift had led her here — the binding would have taken it. And she had stopped. Two fingers short. She had chosen to keep the voices rather than free the king, and Vesper would never know if that choice had been cowardice or exhaustion or simple, human love for the only sense that had ever made her feel less alone in the world.

Vesper closed her fist around the small crown and pressed it to her forehead, and for the first time since the night market, since the book, since the Wardens broke down her door, she cried — not neatly, not briefly, but the way you cry in a place where no one living can see you and the dead are too kind to comment.

When she climbed the stairs at first light, Corvane was waiting with the lantern and a face that asked nothing, as instructed, twenty-two years ago.

"Well?" he said anyway. Because rules soften.

"She's dead," Vesper said. "Or she's gone somewhere even this door doesn't lead. Either way — she left me the work." She held up the crown. "And she left me the bill."

Above them, muffled by stone and distance, the palace bells began to ring the hour, and beneath the bells, faint but unmistakable, Veper heard the chorus of dead kings shift — not in grief, not in warning, but in something horribly close to anticipation.

Because far off, through walls and corridors and the frozen morning, another sound was rising. Boots. Many boots. And shouting in the vaults below the treasury, where the original oath-contract slept in its iron box.

Someone else had come for the work. And they hadn't come to finish it.
