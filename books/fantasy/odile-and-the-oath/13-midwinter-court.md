## 13. Midwinter Court

The Great Hall of Greyhallow had been built to make people feel small, and it did its work well. Serane Hale stood at the base of the dais steps with her hands folded behind her back and her face arranged into the expression the Crown paid her for: attentive, neutral, immovable. Above her, a thousand candles burned in iron cages. Below her feet, the flagstones were older than the kingdom, quarried from the same grey stone as the city walls, and she had always suspected they were meant to remind everyone — nobles included — that the palace was simply the first prison.

She hated midwinter court. She hated the furs and the false warmth, the way the lords of the northern houses drank too much and spoke too loudly about loyalty, as if volume were proof. She hated most of all standing where she could see the king's face clearly, because lately the king's face was the one thing in Greyhallow she could not read.

Three years ago she could have read it blindfolded. Aldric Vane had been twenty-four then, newly crowned, all sharp edges and sharper humor, a young man who treated his own coronation like a sentence he intended to appeal. Serane had liked him. That had been inconvenient.

Now he sat on the Hollow Throne with his spine very straight and his hands very still, and when he turned his head it moved with a smoothness that made the hair on her arms rise. Like something practicing being human from the inside.

*You're imagining it,* she told herself, not for the first time. *Grief does this. Vigilance does this.* Her sister had taught her that lesson differently — that the mind finds patterns because patterns are kinder than chaos — but Maren had learned it in a cell, waiting for the Wardens to come collect her for the crime of a salt-charm sewn into a hem, so perhaps her authority on the subject was compromised.

"Consultant." The word arrived at her elbow along with Vesper Ishikawa herself.

Serane did not look down at her. Looking down at Vesper Ishikawa in public was a mistake; it confirmed a relationship the Crown had not authorized. "You're late."

"The Wardens who escorted me took a scenic route," Vesper said. "I think they wanted me to appreciate the architecture."

She was dressed for court in borrowed grey, her dark hair pinned up in a style some lady's maid had imposed on her and which she had clearly fought to a draw. Around her neck, beneath the collar, Serane knew, hung no charm at all — she'd been searched twice, thoroughly, and had submitted to it with a serenity that Serane found more alarming than resistance would have been. A bone-speaker without her bones. A weapon surrendered voluntarily. Either it meant she was harmless, or it meant she didn't need the bones anymore.

Serane had stopped betting against her weeks ago.

"You'll stand behind me," Serane said quietly. "You'll speak to no one. If anyone addresses you, you'll say you're here to advise on relic authentication and nothing else."

"And if the king condemns an entire class of people to arrest while I stand there watching?"

Serane's jaw tightened. "Then you'll watch."

Vesper was silent for a moment. When Serane finally glanced at her, the woman's eyes were fixed on the throne, and there was something in her face that Serane recognized from mirrors — the look of someone cataloguing exits.

"He asked me to come tonight," Vesper said. Very low. Almost under the music. "Not you. Him. Through the frost on my window. Three words."

Serane kept her gaze forward. *Come. Trust. Endure.* She had seen the message herself, transcribed in Vesper's careful hand before the ice melted. She had burned the transcription and told no one, which was, by any honest accounting, treason. It was getting easier. That frightened her more than the frost did.

"Whatever happens tonight," Serane said, "you are my consultant. That is your armor. Don't take it off."

"And yours?"

"Mine doesn't come off." It was supposed to be a joke. Neither of them laughed.

* * *

The feast began the way feasts did: too much food, too little light, and a hall full of people performing contentment for an audience of one. Serane ate nothing. She watched the king.

He rose for the toast, as expected. He lifted the cup, as expected. His voice, when it came, was the king's voice — resonant, measured, trained since boyhood to fill a room without effort. But Serane had spent nine years listening to men lie for their lives in interrogation rooms, and she knew what rehearsed sounded like, and this wasn't rehearsed. This was recited. There was a difference. Rehearsed speech belongs to the speaker. Recited speech belongs to someone else.

"To Greyhallow," the king said. "To the winter we endure together."

The hall roared back the words. Somewhere in the middle of it, Serane saw his hand tremble — just once, just slightly, the cup's surface shivering — before it stilled again, corrected, smoothed over like water after a dropped stone.

Then he sat, and the herald called the first course, and for forty minutes nothing happened, and Serane almost let herself believe the night would pass clean.

It was during the second course that the king stood again, unbidden.

No herald announced him. No toast had been proposed. He simply rose, and the hall fell silent in ragged pieces, lord by lord, as each noticed and nudged the next. The musicians faltered and stopped. And Aldric Vane walked down from the dais alone, unhurried, his crown catching the candlelight, until he stood on the floor itself among them — which kings did not do, which kings had never done, which was either a gesture of marvelous intimacy or something else entirely.

His eyes found Serane first. Held for half a heartbeat. And in that half-heartbeat she saw — or thought she saw, or desperately wanted to have seen — something drowning.

Then his gaze slid past her shoulder, to the woman standing behind her, and everything in his face closed like a door.

"People of Greyhallow," the king said, and the hall bent toward him. "Winter has been long. Winter has been hungry. And I have watched, these three years past, as hunger made us foolish."

He began to walk. Slowly. Between the tables. The crowd parted before him the way crowds do, unwillingly, a tide pulled by something stronger than the moon.

"There are those among you who sell hope in small carved shapes," he said. "Charms against cold. Charms against grief. Charms against the dead." A pause, precise as a knife finding a joint. "Magic, sold in pieces small enough to hide from the law."

Serane felt Vesper go rigid beside her. Felt it through the air between them, a change in pressure, the way a room changes before lightning.

"I have been lenient," the king continued. "Leniency is a virtue in a father and a vice in a king. These trinkets do not hold back the winter. They feed it. Every unlicensed working draws the cold deeper into our walls, and I will not preside over a city that bargains with its own dying."

He stopped. Ten feet away. Close enough that Serane could see the candlelight move in his eyes and could not find Aldric anywhere in it.

"By order of the Crown, beginning tomorrow, every charm-seller in Greyhallow will be named, registered, and taken into custody. Those who surrender willingly will be treated gently. Those who run—" the pause again, that terrible evenness, "—will be found. The Wardens have their lists. The lists are complete."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere near the far doors, a young noblewoman — Lady Corvess, Serane noted automatically, daughter of a house with three licensed hedge-witches on its payroll — made a small sound and pressed her hand to her mouth.

And Vesper Ishikawa stepped out from behind her.

Serane's hand shot out and caught her wrist. Too late, too slow, too public. Vesper didn't pull away; she simply stood there, one step forward of her armor, in full view of two hundred nobles and every informer the Crown employed, looking at her king.

Looking at him the way no consultant looks at anything.

And the king looked back.

For three seconds — Serane counted them, she counted everything, it was how she stayed alive — the two of them regarded each other across ten feet of frozen air, and the hall watched them do it, and no one breathed. Whatever lived behind the king's eyes met whatever lived behind hers, and Serane saw the king's throat work once, saw his jaw tighten around something, saw his hand rise half an inch from his side and stop, arrested, as if a string had gone taut.

Then he smiled. It reached his eyes the way frost reaches a window: completely, and from the outside.

"The Crown thanks its loyal servants," he said, to the hall, to no one, to her. "Even those who counsel patience. Patience is a luxury winter does not permit."

He turned and walked back to the dais, and the hall exhaled, and the herald, bless his quick wits and his terror, called for the third course as though the fate of a hundred families had not just been signed away between a fish course and a meat one.

Serane hauled Vesper back behind her shoulder hard enough to hurt. "What in the name of every dead god was that?"

"He couldn't say it," Vesper whispered. Her voice was wrecked, scraped raw, barely a sound at all. "The whole speech. Every word. He was reading it off the inside of his own teeth. And at the end—"

"At the end of what?"

"He tried to apologize." Her eyes were shining and furious and wet. "In the only language left to him. He looked right at me and he *tried*, Serane."

"That was not an apology. That was a performance, and you nearly unmade it."

"It was a scream wearing a crown." Vesper's hand, still caught in Serane's grip, was shaking. "You've heard men beg before. You know the sound. That was it. That was exactly it."

Serane released her wrist. Looked at the throne, where the king now sat eating venison with perfect composure, discussing trade tariffs with Lord Asche, laughing at the correct moments.

She had heard men beg. Nine years of interrogation rooms. She knew the sound the way Vesper claimed to know it — better, probably, because Vesper's knowledge came secondhand through bone and Serane's came live, from throats, from eyes, from the specific quality of a voice that has given up on dignity.

And Aldric Vane had not begged tonight. But he had come closer to it than any king should ever come, in front of his whole court, while announcing a purge.

*You're imagining it.*

Maren had said that too, once. Right up until the Wardens came for her, and then she had stopped saying it, and started saying *you were right, you were right, why didn't I listen to you* — and Serane had carried those words for eleven years like a stone swallowed sideways.

"Stay behind me," she said. "And if you step out of line again, I will have you arrested myself, and I will not be gentle about it."

Vesper nodded. She didn't believe the threat. That was the worst part. Nobody believed Serane's threats anymore, and she couldn't decide whether that meant she'd gone soft or she'd finally gone honest.

* * *

The purge orders went out before midnight.

Serane signed the first batch herself, because that was her office and her duty and the machine did not run unless someone fed it paper. Forty-one names. Charm-sellers registered in the Warden rolls, most of them small operators, hedge-crafters, the kind of practitioners who worked in luck and weather and the settling of restless dead — the kind Maren had belonged to, the kind the magic laws had been written to strangle in their cribs a century ago and never quite managed to finish killing.

Her pen hovered over the forty-second name.

*Ishikawa, V. Bone-carver. Lantern Row. Status: consultant, Inquisitor's office.*

She wrote *exempted — Crown service* beside it, and her signature looked, to her own eye, like someone else's handwriting.

The hall was emptying. Servants snuffed candles in ranks. Through the tall windows, snow came down in the windless, deliberate way it had all season, as if the sky were no longer weathering but *deciding*. Serane gathered her papers and found Vesper waiting by the doors, wrapped in her borrowed cloak, watching the snow with an expression Serane was beginning to dread, because it was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic.

"Forty-one names," Vesper said, without turning. "I counted the lists over your shoulder. Forty-one families wake up tomorrow to Wardens at their doors. Because of a speech he didn't write."

"Yes."

"And you signed it."

"Yes."

Vesper turned then. Candlelight and snowlight, half and half on her face. "Which side of the law are you on, Serane? Honestly. Just once, honestly."

The question hung there. Serane had answers ready — she always had answers ready, that was the job, that was the armor that didn't come off — the law is the wall between order and the long dark; the law buried my sister and I serve it anyway because the alternative is a city where every hedge-witch becomes a warlock and every winter becomes the last one. She opened her mouth to deploy them.

What came out instead was: "I don't know anymore."

Vesper blinked.

"I watched my king condemn forty-one people tonight," Serane said, slowly, as if the words were being pried out of her rather than spoken, "and I watched him try to say *no* while his mouth said *yes*, and I have spent three months telling myself I imagined it. And tonight I watched you look at him like—"

She stopped. Recalibrated. Some doors, once opened, could not be quietly shut again, and she had learned that lesson at nineteen over her sister's body.

"Like what?" Vesper asked. Quietly. Dangerously.

"Like someone answering a question nobody else can hear."

They stood there in the emptying hall, the last candles guttering, the snow ticking softly against the glass, two women on opposite sides of a law that had eaten both their families in different ways.

"Tomorrow," Serane said at last, "the Wardens go to Lantern Row. First house on the list is a woman named Odile Ferrant. Sixty-two years old. Sells luck-charms out of a window box. She was Maren's teacher."

Vesper went very still.

"If the king is a prisoner," Serane said, "then these orders are not his, and obeying them makes me the instrument of the thing that took him. If the king is not a prisoner, then I am an inquisitor doing her duty, and my sister died for nothing worse than bad timing." She picked up her papers. "I need to know which it is, Vesper. Before dawn, if possible. Because tomorrow morning I ride to Lantern Row, and I would very much like to know, before I knock on that door, whether I'm serving my king or his cage."

Outside, the snow thickened against the windows — falling faster now, falling wrong, flakes rising as often as sinking, as if the winter itself had begun to doubt which direction was down.

And somewhere above them, in the sealed dark of the royal apartments, frost was already creeping across the inside of the glass, spelling out words no one had authorized:

*Tomorrow. Lantern Row. Come armed with truth, not steel.*
