## 15. The Unbound Move

The cold came for the city three days before it came for the palace.

Ilsa felt it first in the ink. She was copying out cell orders in the safehouse above a shuttered tannery when her pen began to drag, the ink thickening in the well like something drowning, and she had to hold the nib over the candle flame between strokes to keep the words legible. *Vaults at moonset. No fires after entry. The contract burns whole or not at all.* Her own handwriting looked foreign to her, as if someone else had written it years ago and she was only now reading it.

Below her, the tannery vats sat empty and frozen, and through the floorboards came the sound of the others preparing: whetstones on steel, the soft percussion of lockpicks being counted into cloth rolls, Merrin's voice running through the approach one more time in that flat recitation he used instead of prayer. Thirty-one of them. The largest action the Unbound had ever mounted. Years of quiet cells and burned ledgers and small mercies, all of it funneled toward one night.

*Burn the oath. Kill the king mid-binding. Let winter come honest.*

That was the doctrine. She had written half of it herself.

She set down the pen and looked at her hands. They were steady. They were always steady; that was the thing people misread about her, that steadiness meant conviction, when really it only meant she'd learned young that shaking changed nothing. Her forged credentials had gotten her into archives, into the Wardens' reading rooms, into — eventually — a night market stall across from a bone-carver with careful hands and dead men's voices in her ivory.

She had engineered Vesper Ishikawa. Selected her from a list of three candidates, approached her with a century-old book and a rehearsed air of scholarly wonder, and delivered her to the Crown's attention like a parcel. That was the mission. Find the bone-speaker, let the prophecy do its work, and when the Crown seized her, follow the chain upward until it led to the vaults.

It had worked perfectly. Every step.

And somewhere between the arrest and the bargain and the long strange weeks since — somewhere in Serane Hale's office watching that hard woman dress her grief in law every morning, somewhere in a frozen courtyard watching Vesper answer a voice no one else could hear and then look, afterward, like a girl who had touched a hot stove on purpose — the mission had come apart in Ilsa's hands like wet paper.

Because Vesper believed there was a third page. Not unmade. Not wed. *Rewritten.*

Ilsa had spent eleven years believing mercy was a luxury the cold didn't permit. Winter took what it took; the oath held back the worst of it at the price of one man per generation, devoured slowly in his own body. She had watched the arithmetic kill her family — not the frost itself, but the rationing, the triage, the quiet decisions about which villages got coal and which got prayers. The Unbound's founding text was a ledger of those winters. She could recite it.

But Vesper had stood in front of her once, wrists still chafed from Warden irons, and said: *The dead kings aren't the enemy. They're hostages. And you don't free hostages by burning the building.*

Ilsa had gone back to her cell that night and lain awake doing the thing she was best at, which was checking forgery against original. And the original — the doctrine, the ledger, the eleven years — kept failing inspection. There were erasures in it she'd never noticed before. Places where the founders had rounded the numbers down. Places where *mercy* had been written over something that looked, in certain light, a great deal like despair wearing mercy's coat.

She picked up the pen again. Below the cell orders, in the margin, she wrote one word — *third page* — and then scratched it out so thoroughly the paper thinned.

Moonset was two hours off.

* * *

They moved through the deep cold like smoke given purpose.

The palace had been built by people afraid of siege, and its builders had solved the problem the way builders always did: by making the servants' ways too narrow for armor and too crooked for torchlight. The Unbound went down through them single file, hoods up, breath freezing in their hoods until the fabric crackled. Ilsa walked fourth, behind Merrin, carrying the fire-oil in a double-wrapped jar against her chest like an infant. It was warm. That was the obscenity of it — the oil stayed warm while everything else died.

The plan was simple because it had to be. The vaults beneath the east wing held the Crown's sealed archive: treaties, confessionals of dead ministers, and at the bottom, in a case of black iron, the Oath-Contract itself — the original vow, signed in blood and bone by the starving ancestors four hundred years ago, the document that gave the entity in the crown its legal shape. Burn the contract, the entity lost its anchor. Kill the king while it was mid-unraveling, and it would have nowhere left to go. Two cuts, made together. The winter would fall within days, hard and total, and then break within a season, and after that — the Unbound's texts promised — the world would learn to be cold honestly. No more hosts. No more arithmetic.

Merrin picked the last lock with fingers that didn't tremble, because Merrin didn't believe in trembling either, though for different reasons than hers. The vault door swung open on air that hadn't moved in decades.

Inside, the cold was older. It had weight. Their lanterns shrank in it, flames pulling low and blue, and the shelves ran away into the dark like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. Somewhere below them, Ilsa knew, the binding work was already beginning — Vesper and the dead kings and whatever terrible grammar they were teaching her. They had perhaps three hours before dawn. Perhaps less. The raid was timed to land in the middle of it, when every Warden in the palace would be pulled toward the throne room and the vaults would stand empty as a promise.

Except the vaults weren't empty.

A woman stood at the bottom of the stairs. Small, gray-cloaked, holding a lantern that burned at full height in all that starving air, which should have been impossible and told Ilsa everything she needed to know before the woman spoke.

"You're late," said Inquisitor Serane Hale. "Or early. Depending on which side of this you think you're standing on."

Behind Ilsa, thirty-one people stopped breathing.

"I arrested my own superior tonight," Serane continued, conversationally, as if they were the only two people in the world. "Forged the order myself — you'd appreciate that, scholar. So the throne room is mine for a few hours yet, and these vaults are mine now, and I am asking you, once, formally, to put down the oil and walk out. You'll be released at dawn. All of you. My signature on it."

"And if we don't?" Merrin said.

"Then I stop you," Serane said. "And I'd rather not. I've buried enough people for laws that don't work."

Ilsa heard the words and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Serane Hale had chosen her side hours ago — and that the side she'd chosen wasn't the Crown's, exactly. It was the truth's, wherever the truth decided to live. Which meant the woman standing between thirty-one armed radicals and the end of four centuries of arithmetic was offering them the same thing Ilsa had spent eleven years refusing to believe in: a way out that wasn't a lie.

Merrin drew. Of course he did. Merrin had a dead daughter and a doctrine and no erasures in his text at all.

Steel rang along the line. Ilsa stood very still with the warm jar against her chest and did the last inspection of her life's work, quickly, the way you check a document before you sign it.

The doctrine said burn it. The doctrine said the king dies mid-binding, that winter comes honest, that mercy is a luxury. But the doctrine had been written by people who never met a bone-carver who answered dead kings gently, who never saw frost spell a name in hope instead of warning, who never sat across from an inquisitor and realized the law was just grief in uniform.

Vesper was down there right now, carving a counter-oath into a living man's skull-bone, paying for it with the only gift she had. Paying everything. Willingly. For a stranger. For a kingdom that had hunted her kind for a generation.

And Ilsa was supposed to make that worthless. To hand the world back its arithmetic because the numbers frightened her.

"No," Ilsa said.

Everyone turned. The lantern-light caught her face, and she found she wasn't shaking. For once, the steadiness meant exactly what people thought it did.

"The contract stays," she said. "The binding works. We walk out, all of us, and we take amnesty, and we spend the rest of our lives being wrong in public where everyone can see us." She lifted the jar of fire-oil and held it out — not to Merrin, but past him, to the gray-cloaked woman on the stairs. "Inquisitor. This is yours."

Merrin's blade came up between them, and the vault went quiet enough to hear the flames strain.

"You'd hand it to *her*," he said. "You'd hand *them* another generation of that thing wearing our kings."

"I'd hand them a chance," Ilsa said. "You'd hand them a corpse and a blizzard and call it mercy. I've read your ledger, Merrin. I wrote half of it. And I'm telling you the numbers were rounded down."

His eyes went to the jar in her outstretched hands, then to her face, and Ilsa saw the moment the fracture became a break — saw him understand that his own cell had split along a seam that had been there for years, invisible, waiting for the cold to find it.

"Merrin," she said quietly. "There's a third page."

He looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, one by one, uncertain in the blue light, blades began to lower — not all of them, but some, and then more than some.

Then the whole vault shuddered.

Dust sifted from the ceiling ribs. The lantern-flames bent sideways, all of them, toward the throne room, as if the air itself had been inhaled by something vast and wounded, and far above them — muffled by stone and distance and four hundred years of oath — came a sound none of them had ever heard and all of them recognized anyway.

The entity, screaming.

And beneath it, fainter, threaded through the stone like a pulse through a wrist: the sound of a chisel, striking bone, keeping time.

Serane Hale was already moving up the stairs. "Whatever you've decided," she called back, "decide faster. Dawn's coming, and she's running out of hearing to lose."

Ilsa set the jar down on the frozen floor, unlit, and followed her up into the dark.
