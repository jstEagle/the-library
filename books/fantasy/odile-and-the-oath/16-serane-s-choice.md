## 16. Serane's Choice

The order came at the worst hour of the night, which Serane had learned was the only hour the Crown ever chose for its dirtiest work.

She was still in her office above the Warden barracks, a cup of tea gone cold beside her elbow, when Captain Dreev brought the dispatch up with both hands, the way you carry something that might bite.

"From the Lord Protector's seal," he said. "Marked immediate."

Serane broke the wax. The letter inside was short, and it was written in the particular language that existed so that no one could later claim to have misunderstood it.

*By order of the Crown, the prisoner Vesper Ishikawa, detained on suspicion of collusion with the seditionists styling themselves the Unbound, is to be put to death before dawn. Method: beheading, public, at the palace gates. Execution to be carried out personally by Inquisitor Serane Hale, whose fidelity in this matter will be understood as proof of her loyalty to the throne.*

Personally. Proof of loyalty. They weren't asking her to kill a woman; they were asking her to sign herself over, body and record, one more time.

Dreev stood waiting by the door, pretending to study the wall map of Greyhallow's wards — the one with the old districts inked in faded brown, back when the Wardens had been called something gentler.

"Well?" he said.

"You read it?"

"I carried it. That's all I did." He set his jaw. "I carried it, and I'll say that at whatever inquiry comes after, if there is an after."

"There's always an after," Serane said. "That's the problem. It's everything before it that they make short."

She read the order again. *Collusion with the Unbound.* Of course. Ilsa's people had hit the vaults three hours ago — Serane had felt it through the floor of the city, the deep bell of the palace alarms rolling out over the rooftops like weather — and the Crown's answer, arrived at with the speed of panic dressed as decisiveness, was to execute the bone-speaker who had been sitting in a locked room under guard since sundown. The logic was impeccable and insane: the Unbound wanted the king dead; the bone-speaker wanted the king saved; therefore the bone-speaker had arranged the raid to look like salvation while dooming him. You could not argue with that kind of reasoning. It had no doors.

And underneath the logic, plain as a watermark, she could see the real reason. Dawn was coming. Whatever the girl intended to do at dawn — and Serane had spent two weeks watching Vesper Ishikawa work, listening to her talk about bindings and oaths and the grammar of dead kings' promises until the words started making a terrible kind of sense — the Crown did not want it done. The Lord Protector's faction needed the Hollow King exactly as he was: half-swallowed, pliable, signing purges with a hand that wasn't entirely his own. A king made whole would start asking why his regents had let the thing in the crown eat so well for nine years.

So: kill the carver before she could carve. Call it justice. Have it done by the inquisitor whose sister had burned under the magic laws, because the symbolism would be delicious and the loyalty unimpeachable.

They knew about Maren, then. Or someone remembered. Either way, the order was a test wearing an executioner's hood.

Serane put the letter down flat on her desk and smoothed it once with her palm, the way she used to smooth paper for Maren when they were girls copying out catechisms, before either of them knew what the laws would cost.

"Inquisitor?" Dreev said.

"Get me the duty roster for the palace gate. And find out where Lord Corvane is right now — actually where, not where his secretary says he is."

"He's in the vaults," Dreev said. "Has been since the alarm. He took personal command of the suppression detail."

Of course he had. Corvane, Lord Protector, keeper of the keys, the man whose signature sat at the bottom of every purge decree the Hollow King had issued this winter — Corvane would want to be standing in the vaults when they dragged the oath-contract out into the light, or better, when they quietly made sure it never saw light again. The original bargain, the founding document of the long winter, was down there behind three feet of granite. If the Unbound didn't burn it tonight, Corvane would bury it deeper than memory.

Unless someone got to it first.

Serane looked at her own hands for a moment. Steady hands. Her whole career had been built on steady hands: the arrests conducted by the book, the interrogations gentle as surgery, the sentences carried out without flinching because flinching was how inquisitors ended up on the other side of their own benches. She had dressed the wound of her sister's death in procedure every single day for eleven years, and the dressing had held, and she had told herself that holding was the same as healing.

It was not. She knew that now. She had known it since the night she'd watched Vesper Ishikawa stand in a freezing corridor and refuse — politely, precisely, with the flat calm of someone reciting a fact — to accept either future the prophecy offered. *I decline both,* the girl had said, as if futures were items on a menu and she'd found the kitchen wanting. Serane had gone back to her rooms that night and taken out Maren's letters and read them all, and had understood, somewhere around the third one, that she had spent her adult life guarding a law that had eaten her sister and was now eating a king, and calling it service.

"Captain," she said. "Two questions. Answer honestly, because honest answers stop being safe tonight and I'd like to get ahead of the fashion."

Dreev straightened. "Ma'am."

"Do you believe the bone-speaker colluded with the Unbound?"

He didn't answer fast, which was itself an answer. "No," he said finally. "I've seen collaborators. They're busy. She's been carving little birds out of mutton bones and giving them to the guards' kids through the grate. Collaborators don't do that."

"No," Serane agreed. "They don't." She turned the dispatch over. Blank on the back. Even the Crown hadn't bothered to justify this one to itself. "Second question. When the history of this winter gets written — and it will get written, one way or another, because winters like this always end with somebody writing — do you want your name in the chapter where we killed the one person who could have stopped it? Or the other chapter?"

"That depends," Dreev said slowly, "on whether the other chapter has us alive in it."

"Then here's what I know, and you can weigh it yourself." She rose, took her coat from the peg, and settled it across her shoulders with the small ritual care she brought to everything, because if the world was going to end tonight she intended to meet it buttoned correctly. "The palace is sealed. Snow's past the second-floor sills on the north face. The Unbound hold the vaults, or did an hour ago. The king is dying faster than any report admits — I've seen the frost messages, Captain, I've been carrying them to the girl in secret for a week and I am already guilty of everything they're going to hang me for regardless of what I do next. Which means the question isn't whether I burn my career. The question is whether I burn it on a beheading or on a chance."

Dreev was quiet a moment. Then: "The escort detail for the execution. Who do you want?"

"Whoever owes you. And Dreev — after tonight, no one owes anyone. We're all just people who were in the building."

He almost smiled. "Yes, ma'am."

* * *

The cells beneath the palace annex were warmer than the streets, which Serane supposed was the Crown's idea of mercy: kill people comfortably.

Vesper Ishikawa sat cross-legged on the cot with her wrists unbound — they'd stopped binding her days ago; the guards had gone soft around her the way garrisons go soft around anyone kind in a hard place — turning a sliver of bone over in her fingers. A swan, nearly finished. Its wings were half-open, caught between folding and flight.

She looked up when Serane stopped outside the bars, and her face did something complicated. Hope and dread sharing a chair.

"You're not smiling," Vesper said. "Inquisitors smile before bad news. It's a tell."

"The news is bad. But it's also good, depending entirely on how brave you're feeling in the next ten minutes."

"I've been feeling brave for two weeks in a hole. It's mostly just tired with better posture."

Serane unlocked the cell door herself, which drew a raised eyebrow from the escort — two young Wardens Dreev trusted, Hask and the Ondrey girl, both standing very straight in the way of people trying not to think. She stepped inside and handed Vesper the dispatch.

The girl read it twice. Serane watched her knuckles go pale around the paper, watched her set it down with exaggerated care on the cot, smoothing it, smoothing it — and recognized the gesture, and hated that she recognized it.

"They want it public," Vesper said. "At the gates. So the city sees the charm-seller die and stops hoping."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because I'm not taking you to the gates." Serane kept her voice level, conversational, the tone she used for confessions, which was what this was. "Because in approximately twenty minutes I am going to arrest my commanding officer on charges of treason, usurpation, and conspiracy against the person of the king, and I'm going to need my paperwork to be flawless, and then I'm going to walk you through a sealed palace full of armed men and one starving god to a throne room where you are going to do whatever it is you do to crowns. And I need you to understand the terms before we take a single step."

Vesper had gone very still. "You'd lose everything. Rank, pension, the law's protection. If this fails, they won't even give you a trial. Trials are for people the outcome doesn't embarrass."

"I'm aware. My sister got neither a trial nor an outcome. She got a sentence read off a list." Serane heard her own voice come out quieter than intended. "Eleven years I told myself the law was a wall that kept the fire in one place. Tonight I finally asked who built the wall, and where the fire actually was, and the answers were *us* and *the crown.* So." She straightened. "Terms. You go in, you finish it before dawn. Not because I order it — I can't order it; as of the moment I sign that warrant I'm no longer anyone who can order anything. Because if you fail, the winter falls for real, and there's no law and no Crown and no Greyhallow left to argue about. Finish it before dawn, or we all freeze honestly."

Something moved through Vesper's face — fear, yes, but under it a grim relief, the expression of someone who had been carrying a weight alone and had just heard footsteps falling in beside her.

"The dead kings taught me the grammar," she said. "The binding needs a living hand willing to surrender what the dead gave. For me that's—" She lifted her fingers toward her ear, didn't finish. "Aldric knows. We've planned it. The chorus will hold the edges. All I need is the room, the time, and nobody with a sword getting clever."

"Nobody with a sword getting clever is now my entire job description." Serane turned to the doorway. "Hask. Ondrey. You heard none of the last five minutes. What you heard was that Inquisitor Hale is transferring the prisoner to the throne room under direct authority of a warrant she's about to serve on the Lord Protector. If anyone challenges the transfer, you show them this." She held up the warrant she had written in her office an hour ago — written before she'd fully decided, because she had learned long ago that decisions made with a pen dry enough were easier to survive than decisions made with a shaking hand. "If anyone insists past that, Ondrey, you run for Dreev at the barracks and bring everyone who still remembers why we joined."

Ondrey swallowed. "And if the Lord Protector's men insist harder?"

"Then we find out," Serane said, "which of us freezes honestly first."

Vesper stood, tucked the little bone swan into her sleeve, and looked at Serane with those grey eyes that had heard too many dead things say too many true ones. "Your sister," she said softly. "Maren. She sold charms. Weather-charms, mostly. There's a whalebone comb in a shop on Tanners' Row that still carries her mark — the maker's mark, the little wave under the initials. The dead in it aren't angry. They're grateful. She eased a lot of pain before they took her."

Serane's breath went out of her like a struck bell. Eleven years, and no one — no priest, no colleague, no bottle — had ever given her back a single sentence about Maren that wasn't a case number.

"She heard them too," Vesper said. "Didn't she. That's why they executed her. Not for selling. For hearing."

"For hearing," Serane said, and her voice held, barely, because she refused to let it do anything else. "So you'll understand why I've decided the law can go to hell."

* * *

Corvane's men met them at the junction of the west gallery, six of them with poleaxes and the particular blankness of soldiers who had been told nothing and ordered everything. Their sergeant stepped into the center of the corridor, snowmelt dripping off the vaulting behind him, torches guttering in air that shouldn't have been cold indoors but was — the whole palace breathing winter now, the entity's hunger leaking through the stones.

"Lady Hale." The sergeant's eyes flicked to Vesper, shackled now, theatrically shackled, because Serane had insisted on the appearance of custody. "The Lord Protector orders the prisoner remanded to the vaults. Interrogation regarding the seditionists."

"How interesting," Serane said. "I have a writ ordering her remand to the throne room, under authority of a warrant for the arrest of Lord Corvane." She let that land. Watched six faces try not to react. "Treason, usurpation, conspiracy against the king's person. The evidence is substantial and growing. Your lord is currently in the vaults attempting to destroy or conceal the founding contract of the realm — the original oath — while the king lies dying upstairs. You may verify the writ with the Chancery, though I'll note the Chancery's seal-room is currently under the control of men wearing your livery, so I'd move quickly."

The sergeant hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a hairline crack, but Serane had spent a career reading hesitation the way Vesper read bone, and she leaned into it gently.

"Sergeant. Look at the windows." Behind him, along the gallery, frost had crawled up the glass in patterns that were not weather — letters, faint, repeating. *ISHIKAWA. ISHIKAWA.* The king's frozen handwriting, still spelling her name to an empty hall, hours old, refusing to melt. "That's your king writing. With the last of himself. Ask yourself who he's calling for, and then ask whose orders you'd rather answer to at dawn — a regent hiding in a basement, or that."

For a long moment the only sound was the torches and the wind pressing at the glass like a beggar.

Then the sergeant stepped aside. "Throne room's the long way," he said gruffly. "East stair's collapsed under snow weight. I'd take the chapel passage." He turned back to his men, loudly, as if closing a ledger. "Prisoner remanded per writ. Move along."

Serane walked, and did not run, and did not look back, and understood that she had just crossed a line drawn in something deeper than ice. Behind her lay rank and pension and the clean fiction of the law. Ahead lay a chapel passage smelling of candle-smoke and old stone, a girl walking beside her in borrowed shackles, and a throne room where a dying king waited for a carver to cut him free.

At the chapel door, Vesper touched her arm.

"Whatever happens in there," the girl said quietly, "when it's done — when I can't hear anymore — someone should tell me what the silence sounds like. From the outside. You strike me as someone who'd describe it honestly."

Serane thought of Maren's comb on Tanners' Row, its small grateful dead, its little carved wave.

"Honestly," she said, pushing open the door, "I intend to write it down."

Beyond the chapel, through the archway to the nave, the throne room doors stood open, and from within came a sound Serane had never heard in all her years of service: a chorus of voices, dozens of them, layered and ancient and alive — dead kings, singing a dying king free.

"Go," she told Vesper. "We'll hold the doors. Finish it before dawn."

Vesper pulled the shackles off — they'd never truly been locked — flexed her carved-rough fingers, and walked into the light.
