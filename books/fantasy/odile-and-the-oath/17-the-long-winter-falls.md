## 17. The Long Winter Falls

The pain woke him before the cold did.

Aldric had learned to map his own body the way a cartographer maps a coastline under siege — by what was lost each day. There had been years when the thing in his crown took whole provinces: his left hand for an afternoon, his voice for a week, once an entire month he could not account for except by the decrees issued in his name and the faces of ministers who flinched when he entered rooms they'd watched him leave as someone else.

But this was different. This was not occupation. This was consumption.

He surfaced inside himself like a diver coming up through black water and found the borders of his own skin burning. Not metaphor — he felt it as heat along every seam where Aldric ended and the oath began, and the seams were moving. Inward. Always inward now.

*You are wounded,* he thought at it, because after nine years he had stopped pretending they didn't speak. *The vault raid hurt you.*

The answer came not in words but in pressure, a slow tightening around the base of his skull, like a hand testing the fit of a ring. The entity did not waste breath on denial. It never had. That was the horror of it — the Hollow King's crown was honest in a way no living courtier had ever been. It wanted what it wanted, and it had wanted Aldric whole only because a dead host triggered the winter, and a dead host was a poor investment while the winter still needed tending.

Now the calculus had changed. Now there was a bone-speaker in the palace with carving tools and a chorus of dead kings teaching her grammar, and somewhere below them the vault doors hung broken where Ilsa's people had come with fire and iron to burn the original contract. The Unbound had failed — Serane's Wardens had held the inner vault, barely, bloodily — but they had wounded the oath in the trying. Aldric had felt it scream through his teeth at the moment the first flame touched the contract's case. He had felt it *reconsider.*

And what an oath reconsidered was not mercy. It was arithmetic.

*You'll kill me and the winter comes early,* he thought. *You'll kill everything you were made to hold back.*

The pressure tightened another degree. And beneath it, faint and terrible, something that might have been the closest thing the entity had to grief:

*The vow outlives its makers or it does not live at all.*

Then his left hand moved without him, reached for the inkpot, and Aldric went under again.

* * *

He learned the trick of minutes from his father.

Not deliberately — nothing about his father had ever been deliberate, which was rather the point. Corin Ashvale had been a mediocre king and a magnificent drunk, and on the long winter evenings of Aldric's boyhood he would sit by the hearth in the west tower and tell stories so rambling, so dense with useless detail — the name of the horse that threw him at fourteen, the exact shade of green of a girl's ribbon forty years gone, the smell of the tanneries before the river changed course — that no listener could hold all of it. The stories were unmemorable in their abundance. They were too much to digest.

That was the trick. That was what Aldric had discovered in the worst hours of the last nine years, alone inside his own skull while the thing wore him to councils and coronations and funerals: the entity could take his hands, his voice, his signature, his sleep. What it could not take — what it choked on, the way a throat chokes on gristle — was memory rendered in unbearable specificity.

It could hold the fact of his mother. It could not hold the fact that her left thumbnail always had a ridge in it, like a plowed furrow, and that she worried the ridge with her right thumb when she read, and that she died with a book open on her chest and the worry-thumb stilled mid-gesture, and that Aldric, nine years old, had closed the book and kept it — *Songs of the Rivermouth, third printing, water damage to the lower corner* — and had never told anyone he kept it because he couldn't bear the thought of the book being lonely.

The entity tried to eat that once, early on. It got as far as the title and recoiled, and for three days afterward Aldric had felt it sulking at the edges of him like a cat that had stepped in something.

So he bought minutes the way a gambler buys time at a losing table: one memory at a time, each more specific than the last.

The frost letters helped. Vesper had taught him the pattern — breathe against the glass, carve with a stylus of whalebone she'd smuggled to him disguised as a hairpin, wipe clean before the chamberlain's rounds. Six exchanges now. He had memorized all six. He recited them the way other men recited scripture.

*Your father apologized,* she had written, and he had stared at those five words until the frost melted them, because his father had been dead eleven years and had never once apologized alive.

Tonight, pinned beneath the oath's weight while snow climbed the windows of his bedchamber past the second pane, past the leaded diamond of the lattice, past any height snow had ever honestly reached in Greyhallow in living memory — tonight he needed more than memory. He needed a plan, and plans required hands, and his hands were not his.

He started with the small ones. The horse that threw him — Bittern, dun mare, blind in the right eye, thrown him because a snake had gotten into the paddock and no one believed a nine-year-old about snakes. The ribbon — not green, he'd misremembered it for years, it was the color of river glass, and the girl had been the cooper's daughter and her name was Maren and she'd married a fisherman and moved to Saltreach and good for her, genuinely, good for her. The tanneries. His mother's thumb.

Minutes. He counted them like coins. Each memory bought him back a finger's worth of control, a breath's worth of voice, and he spent them immediately, hoarding nothing, because hoarded minutes were stolen minutes and the entity noticed theft.

Breathe. Frost. Carve.

His hand fought him the whole way. It was like writing with a limb asleep, like signing his name during an earthquake, but the message was short and he had practiced it in his head for an hour before daring:

*SHE IS COMING TONIGHT. LET HER. TRUST THE DEAD KINGS. I WILL HOLD WHAT I CAN HOLD.*

He wiped it with his sleeve. Snow fell past the window in silence so complete it felt less like weather than like the world holding its breath.

Below, in the city, the bells had stopped ringing. He hadn't heard them stop. He'd simply realized, some hours ago, that the midnight bell had not come, nor the one after. Either the bell-ringers had frozen or fled or the cold had taken the bronze itself, and he found he could not decide which possibility frightened him most.

* * *

The entity came for him fully at what should have been dawn, though dawn had become a rumor.

It didn't announce itself. It never did. One moment Aldric was crouched by the cold hearth feeding splinters of a broken chair into flames that burned blue at their edges — wrong fire, oath-fire, the winter bleeding into even the wood he burned — and the next he was watching his own body stand up without him and walk to the mirror.

He hated the mirror. Nine years of hating it and the hate had never dulled. Because the face in the glass was his face and it was not his face; the eyes had gone pale as skimmed milk, the mouth sat wrong, and behind the eyes something looked out with the patience of geology. When the Hollow King looked through him, Aldric saw himself from the inside the way a man in a well sees the sky: distant, circular, closing.

"Report," said his mouth, in his voice, to the empty room — and then the door opened and it wasn't an empty room, and Aldric understood with a lurch that the entity had summoned someone and he had not felt the summons leave.

Chamberlain Ovesen. Old Ovesen, who had served since Aldric's father's reign, who had a limp from a hunting accident he described differently every time, who had begun — three weeks ago, four — to notice the gaps. Who had stood in this room ten days ago holding a decree for signature and watched the king sign it with the wrong hand and said nothing, and whose silence had been the loudest sound in the palace.

"The eastern granaries," the entity said through Aldric's lips. "Open them."

Ovesen blinked. "Your Majesty — the eastern granaries are the reserve. If we open them now, before thaw—"

"If we do not," said the Hollow King pleasantly, "there will be no thaw to wait for."

And here was the thing: it was right. That was the trap laid through the whole long winter, the cruelty folded into the mercy. Every measure that eased the city fed the oath's legitimacy. Every decree signed in Aldric's stolen hand kept people alive just long enough to need the next decree. The entity was not merely wearing him. It was governing better than he ever had, and it knew it, and some nights that knowledge cut deeper than the possession itself.

Ovesen bowed. At the door he paused — Aldric saw it, saw the old man's shoulders gather like a man deciding whether to step off a ledge — and turned halfway back.

"Sire. Forgive me." A beat. "You signed Lord Cassel's warrant with your left hand Tuesday. You have been right-handed since your coronation. I checked the portraits."

The room went very quiet. Inside his own skull, Aldric screamed *yes, yes, tell them, tell everyone* with the whole force of what remained of him, and knew it made no more sound than snowfall.

The entity smiled with Aldric's mouth. "You are imagining things, Chamberlain. Grief does that. Your wife has been dead two years, hasn't she?"

Ovesen's face did something complicated and terrible. He bowed again, precisely, and left, and Aldric understood that the entity had just done the cruelest thing available to it: it had made noticing madness. Anyone else who saw the gaps would be handled the same way. The king is ill. The king is grieving. The king is strange lately. The words built walls around the truth faster than any Warden could.

*That was unnecessary,* Aldric thought, shaking. *He suspects. He'll act on it.*

*He will act,* the entity agreed, *and I will be ready. As I am ready for her.*

*Vesper.*

The pressure around his skull became almost tender, which was worse than pain. *The bone-speaker. Yes. She has been busy below the chapel, little mouse, among her mother's unfinished things. Did you know? Her mother tried to bind me twenty-two years ago. She got half the grammar carved before I found her. Do you know what I did with the rest of her?*

Cold walked down Aldric's spine that had nothing to do with the room.

*I gave her to the winter,* the entity said, savoring it. *And now her daughter has come to finish the sentence. Families are so faithful. It makes them easy.*

*If you touch her—*

*What?* And the amusement dropped away all at once, and what looked out through his eyes then was the oldest thing in Greyhallow, older than the dynasty, older than the city, a vow that starving people had made in the dark ten generations ago and that had outlived every reason for its making. *You will hold what you can hold, little king. Hold this.*

And it took his memories.

Not all. It couldn't — that was the flaw in it, the crack Vesper's dead kings had promised her, the seam in the armor. But it took them in fistfuls, ravenously, wounding itself to wound him: it ripped away the smell of the tanneries, the color of Maren's ribbon, the name of the horse, and Aldric felt each loss like a tooth pulled, and in the space where each memory had been the entity poured cold. Filling him with itself. Making room. Preparing the house for a new tenant by evicting the old one piece by piece.

He fought the only way left. He stopped reaching for the pretty memories, the ones it could find easily, and went deep — down to the ones even he avoided. The night of his coronation, nine years ago, nineteen years old, kneeling before the archpriest while his father's coffin waited beside the altar, and the winter already six weeks old and the grain failing, and the old oath-bargain — struck by his grandfather's grandfather, renewed by every host since — coming due for renewal, and the choice put to him not in words but in the language oaths use, which is necessity: *take the crown and the crown takes its tithe, or refuse and let the winter have the city.*

He had said yes. He had been given an hour to decide and he had said yes in four minutes, and he had spent nine years telling himself it was courage, and tonight, stripped of every softer memory, he finally let himself know what it actually was: fear. He had been afraid, and he had said yes, and saying yes had not made him brave, it had only made him owned.

The entity recoiled from that one too. Truth in unbearable specificity — even ugly truth, *especially* ugly truth — it could not digest.

But it was faster now. Wounded things feed fast. The snow outside had passed the third pane. The blue fire in the hearth guttered. Somewhere far below, muffled through stone, he heard — or imagined he heard, or hoped he heard — the small rhythmic scrape of a carving tool against bone.

She was coming. Let her come. Trust the dead kings. Hold what he could hold.

He held.

* * *

Hours later — he thought hours; time had gone soft as the light — the door opened without a knock, and Vesper Ishikawa walked into the king's bedchamber carrying a satchel of tools and followed, impossibly, by the faint scent of chapel incense and old stone.

The entity had him standing at the window, hands clasped behind his back in the posture of a monarch surveying his domain, and it turned his head with perfect royal slowness.

"You should not be here," it said in his voice.

"I know," Vesper said. She did not bow. She looked at him — at the milk-pale eyes, the wrong-set mouth — the way she must have looked at bone before cutting: reading the grain, finding the flaw. "I'm not here for you," she told the entity. "I'm here for what's underneath."

And Aldric, buried alive in his own body, spending his last hoarded minute on one memory — her handwriting in frost, slightly crooked, the letters leaning like people listening — felt the entity's grip shift toward her, felt it gather itself to strike or seize or consume, and threw everything he had left against the inside of his own skull, screaming without sound:

*NOW. WHILE I'M STILL ME. NOW.*
