## 18. Carving the King

The throne room doors sealed behind her with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

Vesper stood on the long runner of white carpet, knife in one hand and her mother's unfinished binding in the other, and listened to the room the way she had learned to listen to bone: not with her ears but with everything else. The palace was screaming. Not people — stone. The walls had been raised by masons who sang their work-songs into the mortar, and now those songs were fraying at the edges as the cold climbed through them like water through cloth.

At the far end of the hall, beneath the great window of colored glass, Aldric sat on his own throne and was not in it.

She could see him — the shape of him, the hands gripping the armrests hard enough that the knives of his rings bit into the gilding — but what looked out through his eyes was older than him, wider than him, a winter given posture. The crown sat on his head like a mouth. It had been sitting there for nine years, and it was hungry, and tonight it had stopped pretending otherwise.

*You came,* said the chorus of dead kings, all together, the way they always spoke when the entity wasn't listening. *You actually came.*

"Of course I came," Vesper said aloud. Her voice cracked off the high windows and came back to her smaller than she'd sent it. "I'm late, aren't I."

*The snow is past the third-floor sills,* said the king who had died in the Year of Ash. *The host is holding. Barely. He is very stubborn, your king.*

"He's not my king," Vesper said, and started walking up the carpet.

The dead kings made a sound she had never heard from them before. It took her three steps to recognize it as laughter — thin, rusted, astonished laughter, the kind men make in trenches. *No,* they agreed. *He isn't. He's yours.*

She didn't correct them. There wasn't time to be honest about anything except the work.

* * *

The entity noticed her when she reached the dais steps.

It turned Aldric's head — a smooth, wrong movement, like a puppeteer adjusting a marionette's neck — and regarded her with eyes that had gone the pale grey of ice over deep water. When it spoke, it used his voice, and it used it well; that was the cruelest part, that after nine years it had learned to wear even his weariness.

"The bone-speaker," it said. "The little carver of luck-charms. You think you can bind me with a knife?"

"I bound a fever into a boar's rib when I was eleven," Vesper said. "I bound a murderer's name into a whalebone button so he'd never sleep warm again. I've been binding things since before I knew that's what I was doing." She climbed the last step. "You're just bigger."

"You are a candle," the thing in the crown said, "arguing with the dark."

"And you're an oath," she said, "that nobody living remembers making. That's worse than a candle. That's a debt with no debtor left." She set down her mother's workshop box and opened it, and the smell of the hidden chapel rose out of it — tallow, dust, and thirty years of patient failure. "I read the original contract, in the vault, while your friends were burning half the palace. Do you want to hear what it says? It says: *hold back the winter until we are forgotten.* That's the whole oath. Hold it until no one remembers why. And everyone's forgotten, crown. Everyone. Your makers are dust. Their grandchildren are dust. The only person left who remembers the terms is me, because the dead told me, and the dead are *inside you.*"

For the first time, something flickered across the entity's borrowed face that might have been uncertainty.

*Now,* said the dead kings. *While it wavers. Now, carver.*

Vesper reached into the box and lifted out the piece her mother had never finished: a circlet of antler-bone, thin as a finger, carved along its inner curve with the first third of the counter-oath. Her mother's cuts were fine and sure and stopped mid-stroke, the way a sentence stops when the hand writing it is taken away. Vesper had spent two days learning those strokes, tracing them blind until her fingers knew them better than her eyes did.

She had added nothing to it yet. Tonight she would finish it — not on the antler. On the crown itself. On the bone at its heart, the oath-bone, the thing the first starving ancestors had breathed their vow into a thousand years ago. Bind the vow into its own root, and the tree comes down.

But the crown was on Aldric's head, and Aldric was not alone in there.

"Aldric," she said. Just his name. Plain, human, unarmored.

The entity's grip tightened. She saw his jaw fight against it — saw the muscles in his throat move against the will inside them, a man drowning and shoving his face above the surface for one more breath.

"*Run,*" he said, in a voice scraped raw. "Vesper — the vault raid — they wounded it, it's feeding faster, I can't — I can't hold the edges much longer, it'll take my hands, it'll take my *hands*—"

"It won't," Vesper said. "Because I'm going to take them first."

She stepped onto the dais. This close, she could hear the whole architecture of the thing: the chorus of dead kings straining outward like men pushing against a door, and behind them the vast cold patience of the oath itself, which did not hate her, which did not fear her, which had simply been eating kings for a millennium the way a river eats a bank. You could not argue with it. You could not pity it. You could only change what it was made of.

And what it was made of was words.

* * *

"Dead kings," Vesper said. "Listen to me. All of you. I need you to hold its edges — every edge, every seam where it meets him. Can you do that?"

*We have been trying for nine hundred years,* said the king called Osric, who had worn the crown the longest and failed the slowest.

"You've been trying from inside. Pushing against a wall." She knelt beside the throne, close enough to see Aldric's chest rising too fast, too shallow. "Tonight you push *outward*. Tonight you're not prisoners holding a wall. You're a frame. You hold the shape of this moment still so I can carve inside it. Every scrap of yourselves you have left — spend it. All of it. When this is done, you go free, or you go out, but either way you stop carrying it."

Silence in the bone-ways. Then, quietly, the voice of the youngest of them — a boy-king, crowned at nine, dead at nineteen, whose whisper she had heard only once before:

*What does it cost?*

Vesper closed her eyes. She had known since the workshop beneath the chapel. She had known since the chorus taught her the grammar of the binding, and the grammar had a final clause written in a hand steadier than any living hand: *the hand that binds surrenders what the dead gave her.*

They had told her plainly. They had given her a night to decide. She had spent the night deciding, and then she had spent the deciding, and here she was.

"My hearing," she said. "All of it. Every voice. Forever. That's the price, and I pay it, and I'm telling you now so none of you waste a single death trying to spare me. Hold the edges."

*Carver—*

"Hold. The. Edges."

And the dead kings of Greyhallow, nine of them, a thousand years of hostages, threw themselves outward all at once.

* * *

She felt it happen the way you feel a storm front pass overhead — pressure, then stillness. The entity strained against its own contents. Aldric's body locked rigid, spine bowing off the throne, and the frost that had always crept from his breath now sheeted across his cheeks, his throat, the arms of the chair, blooming in patterns like fern leaves and feathered wings. But he did not rise. The dead held him to the shape of the moment, a frame of ghosts, and inside that frame there was, at last, room to work.

Vesper put her knife to the crown.

Not the metal — the metal was just the cage, gold worked by court smiths into leaves and points. Beneath the gold, woven through it like a splinter through flesh, ran the true crown: a core of ancient bone, dark as old honey, dense as a heart. She had felt it once before, through a glove, in the vault, and it had felt her back. Now she laid her bare thumb against it and the whole history of the oath poured up her arm — hunger, cold, the long gray centuries of hosts worn down and hollowed out and replaced.

"Don't listen to it," she told herself, out loud, because her mother had written that instruction on the workshop wall in charcoal and Vesper had laughed at it two days ago and was not laughing now. "Don't listen. Carve."

She set the blade at the point where her mother's unfinished stroke would have continued, if her mother had ever gotten within a mile of this crown, and she cut.

The counter-oath was not long. That was the terrible elegance of it. Oaths grow like coral, layer on layer, clause on clause, until they weigh more than the people who swore them — but to unmake a coral reef you need only break the first stone. Her mother had found the first stone. Vesper's job was to drive it home.

*"What was sworn in hunger,"* she carved, each character a small controlled violence, *"is unsworn in fullness."*

The entity screamed.

It screamed through Aldric's mouth and through the mouths of every portrait in the hall and through the throats of the gargoyles on the roof, and the stained-glass window above the throne crazed into a spiderweb of cracks, and the temperature dropped so fast that Vesper's breath froze in the air between one word and the next. But the dead kings held. She could feel them holding — nine wills braced against nine edges, spending themselves like coins, and with every second they spent, another voice in the chorus went quiet forever.

That was the part they hadn't warned her about. Or maybe they had, and she hadn't wanted to hear it: holding the entity's edges would burn the dead kings out. They were paying with the last of themselves so she could finish.

So she finished faster.

*"What was promised by the starving,"* — cut — *"is released by the fed."*

Her hearing began to die.

It did not go all at once. It went note by note, the way a tide goes out, and it went in order of distance. First the far sounds left her: the wind against the glass, the groan of the walls, the distant crash of fighting somewhere below where Serane was holding the stair. Then the middle distances: her own ragged breathing, the scrape of the knife. The world contracted around her like a closing fist, and in the shrinking circle of what remained, the voices of the dead kings rang clearer than they had ever been — as if the price were being collected in advance, sense by sense, to sharpen the last of it.

*Hold the line, carver,* said Osric, and his voice was already thinner.

*"What was taken as vessel,"* — cut — *"is returned as man."*

Aldric's eyes found hers. His eyes — really his, the grey of slate and morning, fighting up through the ice like a swimmer toward light. His lips moved. No sound reached her anymore except the bone-voices, but she read the shape of it, and the shape was her name.

"Not yet," she said, though she couldn't hear herself say it. "Talk later. Bleed later. Live later. Hold *still.*"

*"What was given without end,"* — cut, deeper, the bone resisting like a living thing, black sap beading along the groove — *"is ended without taking."*

The entity stopped screaming and started bargaining.

It came into her hearing the way cold comes under a door — sudden, total, intimate. *Bone-speaker. Little carver. Stop, and I will give you back the voices. All of them. Your mother's voice — I have it, she served me nine years, I kept her last words, I can let you hear them again, I can let you hear her say your name—*

Vesper's hand shook. One bad tremor, the knife skidding a hair's width in the groove.

Her mother. Taken to the palace years ago. Serving the crown. Dead, or worse than dead, and the thing wearing the king had kept her voice like a coin in a purse, and it was offering it back, and for one white-hot instant Vesper wanted it so badly her vision blurred.

Then she thought of the workshop. The half-carved bindings. The instruction on the wall in charcoal: *don't listen to it.* Her mother had known. Her mother had stood exactly here, metaphorically, years ago, and faced exactly this offer, and had refused it, and had been destroyed for refusing, and had left her daughter a map of the refusal in antler-bone.

"She'd tell me to keep cutting," Vesper said. "I know her. She'd say *keep cutting, Ves, and cut straighter than that.*"

She cut straighter.

*"What was sworn unto forgetting,"* — the deepest cut yet, the blade sinking to its shoulder into the oath-bone, and blood — hers, she realized distantly, from where the knife's guard had split her palm — running hot down the cold gold — *"is remembered, and thereby, ends."*

* * *

There was one stroke left.

One. A short downward cut, a period at the end of a thousand-year sentence, and Vesper discovered that her hand would not move.

Not from the entity. From exhaustion. Her arm was a column of fire, her palm was slick, the cold had gotten into her joints, and the last of her hearing was a single narrow channel in which the dead kings' voices still sounded — four of them now, maybe five, she couldn't count anymore, they were going out like candles in a draft, and the nearest voice, the boy-king's, was saying:

*Finish it. Finish it, carver, we are almost — we are almost out of—*

And underneath him, fainter than faint, threaded through the ruin of the chorus: one living voice. Aldric. He had no magic, no gift, no purchase on the bone-ways — but he had spent nine years learning to leave messages in frost, and a man who can write in frost can learn, at the point of death, to write in anything. He wrote his voice into the last of her hearing the way he'd written *Ishikawa* into a frozen window a lifetime ago:

*Ves. Whatever it costs — it's already worth it. Finish it and live deaf and free rather than hear one more day of this. I'll talk enough for both of us. You know I will. Finish it.*

Vesper laughed. Or she thought she did; she couldn't hear it. She wiped her bleeding hand on her apron, adjusted her grip, and thought about a nervous bride three years ago, and a luck-charm small enough to hide in a fist, and a life so carefully ordinary it had fit inside one room and one market stall and one rule: stay small, stay unheard, stay alive.

She was done staying small.

"Hey, crown," she said — shouted, maybe; she'd never know — "you're fired."

She drove the last stroke home.

* * *

The world broke open into silence.

Not quiet — silence. The difference was total and absolute, the difference between a room with no one in it and a room that has never held anyone. The chorus vanished mid-word. The entity's vast cold presence vanished mid-hunger. The wind, the fighting, the cracking glass, her own heartbeat — all of it, gone, as if someone had reached into her skull and closed a door she hadn't known was there.

Vesper knelt on the dais of a silent throne room and felt the crown come apart in her hands.

She felt it because feeling was all she had left. The gold grew warm, then hot, then sloughed away like spring ice off a branch, and the oath-bone beneath it whitened, aged a thousand years in a thousand seconds, and crumbled — not violently, but gently, the way a held breath lets go — into powder that drifted upward instead of down, catching light that had no source, and rose through the cracked window and out into the falling snow.

The dead kings went with it. She didn't hear them go. She would never hear anything go again.

The throne creaked.

She turned, and Aldric fell forward out of the chair and caught himself on his hands on the frozen floor, and stayed there, shoulders heaving, a man crawling out of deep water into air. Frost slid off him in sheets. Underneath, he was gaunt and shaking and flushed with actual warmth, and when he lifted his head his eyes were only his own — tired, terrified, alive, looking at her the way a man looks at the shore.

His mouth moved. Soundlessly, from where she knelt. He was saying something urgent, something repeated, and she watched his lips shape it twice, three times, before she understood that she would never again hear what he said, and that he didn't know yet, and that telling him was going to be the hardest sentence she would ever have to speak without speaking.

Then his gaze dropped to her hand — the knife still in it, the blood, the way her fingers wouldn't uncurl — and traveled up to her face, to her eyes, and whatever he saw there made him go very still.

He crawled to her. She let him. And the last thing Vesper Ishikawa heard in her life — no. The last thing she *felt*, because hearing was over, feeling was what remained: the last thing she felt was his hand, warm, human, unbound, closing over her bloody one as the darkness came up from the floor like a tide, gentle as snowfall, and took her under.
