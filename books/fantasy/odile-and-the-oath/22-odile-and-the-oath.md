## 22. Odile and the Oath

The workshop smelled of bone dust and beeswax, the way it had for a year now, the way it would tomorrow. Vesper stood at the long bench by the north window where the light came in clean and gray, watching three apprentices argue over a half-finished luck-charm as though the fate of the city hung on the angle of one gouge.

It didn't. That was the miracle of it. Nothing hung on anything anymore except what people chose to hang there.

"The grain runs wrong here," said Tam, the eldest, holding the whalebone sliver up to the window. He was fourteen, all elbows and certainty, the son of a fishwife from the Salt Rows who had never seen the sea until the thaw opened the harbor roads. "If I carve with it, the charm cracks in a year. If I carve against it, my hand slips."

"Then your hand learns," Vesper said. "That's the whole trade."

Mira, the youngest at eleven, looked up from her practice piece — a small round bead she'd been smoothing for two weeks, patient as a stone. "But how do you *know* when it's done? You always know. You look at a piece and you just know."

Vesper turned the question over the way she turned every question about the craft now: carefully, honestly, without the answer that used to live behind her teeth.

"I listen," she said. "Not the way you mean. Not anymore. I listen with my eyes and my hands and the part of me that remembers what listening cost." She took the bead from Mira's palm and held it to the light. Fine work. Better than fine. "You'll learn to hear it too. A different hearing. The kind that stays."

Tam frowned. He was too young to remember the years before, but old enough to have heard the stories — everyone had heard the stories, they were practically civic scripture now, told in taverns and schoolrooms with varying degrees of accuracy. The bone-speaker. The Hollow King. The winter that broke.

"You really heard them?" he asked, the way he asked every few weeks, unable to help himself. "The dead. In the ivory."

"Every day of my life until a year ago midwinter." She set the bead back in Mira's hand and closed the girl's fingers around it. "And every day since, I've been learning what silence sounds like. It sounds like this room. It sounds like enough."

Serane had told her once, over cheap wine in the rebuilt Warden barracks, that this was the strangest part — not the binding, not the raid, not even the snow rising past the palace windows like a tide coming in. The strangest part was that Vesper had given up the one thing that made her singular, and become more important for it. The first charm-shop in Greyhallow had opened in spring with Vesper's name over the door and no Wardens outside it. The second in summer. By autumn there were nine, and the Crown's new charter — Serane's charter, written in her own careful hand, signed by a king who insisted on reading every line twice — said plainly what the old laws had spent a century denying: magic was not a crime. It was a labor. It could be taught, licensed, protected.

And the teaching began with people who would never hear the dead.

That was the point. Vesper had understood it somewhere in the long convalescence, when the silence in her skull was still so vast she sometimes screamed just to prove sound existed on her side of it. The gift had made her a curiosity, then a prophecy, then a prisoner, then a sacrifice. What remained after — hands, patience, craft, love — that could be given away freely. Given away, it multiplied. Kept, it devoured. She had watched an oath do exactly that for nine years. She wasn't interested in repeating the lesson.

"So the swan," Tam said, because he always circled back eventually. On the bench between them lay the piece Vesper had been working for a month: a swan of pale antler-bone, wings spread wide, each feather a separate sliver fitted into the body with pins no thicker than eyelashes. It was nearly done. It was also, both apprentices knew, the most famous unfinished object in Greyhallow.

"It's not ready," Vester said, and caught herself, and smiled. Even now, even silent, the dead left their fingerprints in her grammar. *It isn't ready,* the bones would have murmured. *Wait for the light.* Some voices you kept because you chose to.

"What's it for?" Mira asked.

Vesper looked at the swan a long moment. Wings open. Not folded, not preening, not the closed elegant shape that carvers gave swans when they wanted them beautiful and safe. Open. Mid-beat. The moment before flight or the moment after landing — the moment that couldn't decide, and so contained both.

"It's an answer," she said. "To a question I haven't finished being asked."

* * *

Aldric came at dusk, as he did most evenings when the council let him, which was to say he arrived through the workshop's back stair wearing a plain wool coat and the expression of a man escaping something. The apprentices adored him with the unembarrassed ferocity of the young; Tam had once asked him, point-blank, whether being king was better or worse than being eaten by an oath, and Aldric had considered the question seriously for a full ten seconds before answering, "Better. But not by much some days."

Tonight he came in stamping snow off his boots — real snow, ordinary snow, the kind that fell in reasonable amounts and melted in reasonable time — and stopped just inside the door the way he always did, letting the warmth take him, letting the room be a room and not a throne room.

"They're still arguing about the harbor levy," he said by way of greeting. "I left Chamberlain Odo explaining fractions to a duke. I judged the kingdom safe in his hands for an hour."

"Reckless," Vesper said. "Fractions have toppled stronger crowns."

He laughed — the real laugh, the one that had taken months to come back, rusty at first, like a gate unused. For the first year after the unbinding he had flinched at mirrors, tested his own hands against his own will, woken at night reaching for frost that wasn't there. Slowly the flinching had gone. What remained was a man who knew precisely what his freedom weighed, because he had counted it out voice by voice, one grateful dead king at a time.

He crossed to the bench and looked down at the swan. His expression did the thing it always did around the piece — softened, and steadied, and waited.

"You've added feathers," he said.

"Three. The pinions were wrong. They said so." She tapped her ear, wry. "Metaphorically."

"Of course."

"Aldric." She set down her knife. "Ask me."

He had asked before. That was the trouble, and the tenderness of it. Once a month, sometimes more, he asked her the same question in different clothes: *What do you want next? Where shall we go? What shall we build? Shall we stay? Shall we leave? Shall we simply stop?* And once a month she answered with something small and true — *more windows in the shop, a trip to the coast, another apprentice* — and watched him accept it, and saw in his face the question underneath the question, the one neither of them had ever said aloud.

Because the prophecy was gone. Burned, officially; archived, actually, in Serane's sealed vault, its final page preserved as historical record. *The unmaker or the bride.* Two futures, written a century before either of them was born, and both of them false, because futures written by other people always are. They had proven it. They had walked out of the book entirely — not unmaker, not bride, but something the page had no word for. Two people who chose each other daily, without oaths, without prophecy, without any power in heaven or under the ice telling them to.

But Aldric had worn a crown that spoke. He knew better than anyone how words written down can start to feel like walls.

So tonight, instead of asking, he picked up the swan.

Held it in both palms, the way you hold something living. The firelight moved in the feathered wings and for a moment the whole piece seemed to tremble, mid-flight, suspended.

"This is what you've been making," he said quietly. "All winter. While I sat in council chambers arguing about levies."

"All winter," she agreed.

"Odile," he said. The old word, the scholar's word, the word Ilsa had first used a lifetime ago across a night-market table — *swan*, in the tongue of the southern courts, the word that had given the book its title. *Odile and the Oath.* Though the book was wrong about everything else, it had at least named itself well. There had always been an oath. There had only ever needed to be one.

"Wings open," he said. "Why open?"

Vesper took it from him. Held it up between them, turning it slowly, and found that the answer had been sitting in her hands for a month, waiting for the light.

"Because a swan with folded wings is a promise to stay," she said. "Beautiful, and safe, and small. Every carver makes that one sooner or later. I've made a dozen." She looked at him. "But wings open is a promise to *choose*. To fly and come back. To go anywhere and return because returning is the choice, not the cage." Her thumb traced the pinion she'd re-cut three times. "I spent my whole life hearing what the dead wanted. Then a year hearing what the prophecy wanted. Then a year hearing nothing at all, which was its own kind of lesson." She set the swan gently in his hands again, closing his fingers over it the way she'd closed Mira's over the bead. "This is the first thing I've carved that's only mine. No voices. No book. Just what I want."

"And what is that?"

She smiled. Outside, the snow fell soft and lawful on a city that had learned, slowly, imperfectly, to trust its own strange hands. Somewhere across the rooftops, Serane was redrafting a charter clause by candlelight; somewhere in the southern hills, Ilsa was teaching freed Unbound to farm instead of burn; somewhere below the chapel, a mother's unfinished bindings slept in the dark, forgiven.

"I want the next year," Vesper said. "And the one after. I want mornings that belong to no one's name on any page. I want to teach these children to make things that hold nothing but luck. I want—" her voice caught, not from grief, from fullness— "I want you. Not the king. Not the host. Not the bridegroom of a prophecy. You. Chosen daily. Wings open."

Aldric looked down at the swan in his hands for a long time.

Then he did the thing he had never done in all the months of asking: he set it on the bench between them, took out his own knife — a plain one, a councilor's penknife, nothing royal — and began, clumsily, carefully, to carve.

"What are you doing?" Vesper asked.

"Answering," he said. "In your language. Since apparently that's the one we speak now." Shavings curled away under his inexpert blade. It was going to be terrible, whatever it was — lopsided, overworked, unmistakably sincere. "A second bird. Worse than yours. So yours has company on the shelf, and neither of you has to be the answer alone."

Vesper watched his hands work — his own hands, his own will, steady and free — and thought of frost letters, of a name spelled in ice by a stranger begging to be found, and of how far a message could travel when someone finally listened.

The knife paused. He glanced up, boyish, uncertain, the ghost of the boy-before-the-coronation shining through the king.

"Is it done?" he asked. "How do you know when it's done?"

"You listen," Vesper said, and reached across the bench to guide his hand. "With your eyes, and your hands, and everything it cost to get here. And then—" she turned the little rough bird toward the window, toward the last gray light, toward the year ahead with its unwritten pages— "you let it fly."
