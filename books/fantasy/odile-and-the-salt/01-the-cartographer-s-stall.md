## 1. The Cartographer's Stall

The charm that hung at the front of Edmund Villalobos's stall was a knot of river-clay and twine, fired in a neighbor's kiln, strung on honest hemp cord. It did nothing. That was the point of it. It was the first thing every customer saw, and it said, in the language of objects: *here is a person who sells trinkets, nothing more, move along.*

Edmund had made forty of them over twelve years and replaced one each time the salt air wore one down to crumbs. They were, in their way, the most carefully crafted things in the stall.

The market of Saltmere woke around them the way it always did — in layers. First the fishwives, arguing prices with the flat certainty of women who had won arguments for thirty years. Then the tanners, whose corner of the square smelled like a wound and who were therefore given generous space by everyone. Then the fruit carts, the knife-grinder, the man who sold roasted chestnuts from a brazier he claimed had been lit continuously since before the Sundering War, which was either a lie or a small miracle, and no one in Saltmere cared to determine which.

Edmund arrived before all of them, as they always did, in the blue hour when the gulls owned the square. The stall came apart into six pieces and lived in a cellar beneath a chandler's shop on Rope Lane, and the walk between cellar and market took four hundred steps if you took the long way, which Edmund always did, because the long way passed no windows.

By full light the stall stood assembled: charms on pegboard hooks, maps in a slatted rack, a brass scale for weighing coin, and Edmund behind it all with ink stains to the second knuckle and an expression of mild, patient boredom that had taken years to perfect and cost more than any ward they'd ever drawn.

"Charms," Edmund would say, when asked what they sold. "Charms and maps."

Both statements were true. Neither was true. This was the trick of surviving in Saltmere: never lie outright, because liars got watched. Just arrange the truth until it looked like something harmless.

The charms were real charms — mostly. A knot of braided horsehair that kept a saddle from chafing. A wax seal pressed with a pattern that discouraged moths. Small domestic magics, so old and so diluted that even the Inquisition's own scholars classified them as "craft superstition," a phrase Edmund had read in a seized text and found deeply funny. The law banned *the gift*. It had never figured out what to do about craft.

The maps were the problem.

Not the ones in the rack. Those were honest work — coastlines of the Gray Reach, trade roads through the Vellum Hills, a lovely rendering of the Saltmere harbor that tourists bought because it was pretty. Edmund drew those with real care, because bad cartography was its own kind of suspicion. A mapmaker who couldn't map was a man pretending; a mapmaker who mapped too well was something worse.

The other maps lived nowhere at all. They lived in Edmund's head, in a memory trained since childhood to hold lines the way other people held grudges: the Veilroads. The paths between realms. The thin places where the world's fabric wore through and you could step sideways out of Saltmere and arrive somewhere older, rawer, unruled. Grandmother had taught them to see those seams before they could write their own name. She'd called it *reading the hem of things*, and she'd done it openly, in another country, in another life, before the Sundering War ended and the crowns decided that wonder was a form of treason.

Twelve years. Edmund measured everything in twelves now. Twelve years since they'd come over the mountains with a false name and a true gift folded up small enough to hide. Twelve years of selling moth-charms to grandmothers and drawing honest coastlines and going home each night to a room above the chandler's shop where the walls were lined — discreetly, under plaster — with wards that had never once been tested, and every night Edmund prayed they never would be.

*You are a shopkeeper,* Edmund told themselves each morning, hands flat on the stall's counter, feeling the wood's grain, its ordinary dumb reality. *You are a shopkeeper, and shopkeepers die of old age.*

* * *

The client came at midmorning, when the market was at its loudest and therefore at its safest. Loud markets swallowed conversations. Loud markets meant witnesses who saw nothing.

He was a clerk by his look — soft hands, ink on his cuffs, the anxious posture of a man employed by someone larger than himself. He'd bought from Edmund twice before, small purchases, cash, no conversation. Today he stood at the front of the stall longer than browsing required, and Edmund felt the old cold start behind the sternum, the one that arrived whenever a routine developed a flaw.

"Master Corvin," Edmund said, using the name the man had offered on his first visit. Names were currency; Edmund spent them sparingly. "Back for another moth-seal? I have fresh wax."

"No. No, I—" The clerk's eyes moved across the pegboard without landing anywhere. "My wife. She's been sleeping badly."

"Bad dreams?"

"Worse. Waking." He said it the way men said things they didn't fully believe themselves. "She wakes at the same hour. Between three and four. Says the room feels... watched." His gaze finally settled, on the clay-and-twine charm at the stall's front edge, and stayed there. "I thought perhaps—"

Edmund reached past him and lifted the charm down. Harmless. Fired clay, river twine, hemp cord. It had hung there through two winters and it weighed exactly what a lump of clay should weigh.

But when Edmund's fingers closed around it, the charm hummed.

It was very faint. A vibration in the bones of the hand rather than the ear, a bee trapped in a wall, a plucked string still ringing somewhere out of sight. Edmund's face did not change. Twelve years of practice lived in that: the face that did not change. But inside, everything went quiet and attentive, the way a forest goes quiet when something large moves through it.

Clay didn't hum. Clay had never hummed. Edmund had fired this piece personally, in a neutral kiln, with no working in it at all beyond the ordinary intention of making a thing look worthless. Which meant the hum wasn't coming from the charm.

It was coming through it. Something nearby, resonating. Something in the market, or beneath it, or walking toward it, that touched the world the way Edmund touched the world, and had brushed against this little dead object and set it ringing like a struck bell.

*Ward yourself,* said grandmother's voice, twelve years old and still the clearest voice in Edmund's head. *First. Always first. Panic is a beacon.*

Edmund breathed once, slow, and let the habit of a lifetime settle over them like a coat: the flattening, the folding-small, the gift tucked down behind seven mental doors that had names instead of locks. The hum faded under their fingers to nothing. Or almost nothing. Or perhaps they only wanted it to be nothing.

"For sleeplessness," Edmund said, and their voice came out level, warm, bored. A shopkeeper's voice. "This is a hearth-ward. Old country design. You hang it above the door, not the bed — above the door, mind, or it does nothing but collect dust." They turned the charm over, showing its plainness, its stupid innocent face. "It doesn't do anything mystical. It reminds the house of itself. Houses forget, same as people. A house that remembers itself keeps its people sounder."

It was a good patter. It was true, even, in the smallest sense — the charm *was* keyed, faintly, to the idea of boundary and keeping. Craft, not gift. The kind of thing a grandmother might sell honestly at a market in a kinder world.

The clerk turned it over in his soft hands.

And it hummed again.

Louder this time. Loud enough that Edmund saw the man feel it — saw his thumb pause, saw the small animal confusion cross his face, saw him begin, fatally, to open his mouth—

"It's the cord," Edmund said quickly. "Hemp holds tension. Roll it between your fingers and it sings a little. Folk used to say a singing cord meant the house wanted you home." A laugh, easy and hollow. "Superstition. But it sells."

The clerk laughed too, uncertainly, and the moment passed — passed the way a cart passes over a bridge you're not sure will hold. He paid four coppers. Edmund wrapped the charm in brown paper, tied it with plain string, and watched him go, and watched the way he walked: quick, hunched, glancing back once at the stall with an expression Edmund had seen before and hated seeing again.

Not suspicion. Worse than suspicion. *Wonder.*

Wonder was dangerous. Wonder made people talk. Wonder made a clerk go home to a wife who woke between three and four and say, *you'll never guess what the charm-seller's trinkets do.*

Edmund stood in the noise of the market and felt the day tilt, very slightly, off its axis.

* * *

They should have closed early. That was the rule they made afterward, in the long accounting of that night: the hum, the clerk, the glance back — close the stall, go home, ward the room, sit in the dark and listen. Twelve years of discipline said *when the world rings wrong, stop trading.*

Instead Edmund stayed. Because it was market day, because rent was due to the chandler, because a half-dozen regulars would come by and a missing stall raised questions that a present stall never did. Because fear, Edmund knew with the clarity of hindsight, is exactly the voice that tells you the rules don't apply today.

So they stayed, and sold a saddle-knot to a drover, and a coastline map to a honeymooning couple from upriver, and drew a small chart of the fishing shoals for a boy who paid in mussels, and the whole time some quiet chamber of their mind lay flat and listening, the way a hare lies flat in a field.

Nothing else hummed. By late afternoon Edmund had almost persuaded themselves the morning had been nerves — a bad night's sleep, a stray resonance off the chandler's stock, the imagination of a person who had been waiting twelve years for a knock and therefore heard knocking everywhere.

Then the criers came.

* * *

There were five of them, entering the square from the Customhouse side in a wedge, and the crowd's noise changed around them the way water changes around a dropped stone. Criers came for taxes, usually. For curfews, occasionally. For executions, rarely, and then everyone pretended to be sad.

These criers wore the gray and silver of the crown, and the man at their center held a scroll bound with a ribbon the color of dried blood, and Edmund's hands went still on the counter before their mind caught up to why.

"Hear ye!" The lead crier's voice was trained, enormous, indifferent. "By decree of the Chancellor of the Interior, speaking with the authority of the Crown of Saltmere: a General Gift-Hunt is proclaimed throughout the realm!"

The words went through the crowd like a blade through water — clean, silent, total. Edmund heard them and heard, simultaneously, the absence of reaction: the fishwife mid-haggle who stopped with her mouth open; the chestnut man whose brazier suddenly seemed very loud; the boy with the mussels, frozen halfway through counting his coins.

"Every soul possessing the forbidden gift shall present themselves for registration at the Customhouse within the turn of one moon," the crier continued. "Those who register shall be assessed, catalogued, and remanded to the crown's disposition. Those who conceal their gift—" the crier let the pause stretch, savoring it, a showman who knew his craft, "—shall be sought. And found. And taken."

Someone in the crowd cheered. One voice, then three, then a scatter of them — the always-cheerers, the men who cheered at executions, at fires, at anything loud. Most of the crowd said nothing. Most of the crowd did the arithmetic everyone in Saltmere had done in their heads for twelve years, the sum every gifted person carried like a stone in the belly: *how well do I hide? how well have I hidden? has it been enough?*

Edmund did the arithmetic too, and for the first time in twelve years, the numbers refused to resolve.

Because registration meant assessment. Assessment meant the sniffers, the iron tests, the questions asked in rooms without windows. And Edmund's gift was not a moth-charm, not a hearth-ward, not craft or superstition. Edmund's gift could open the hem of the world and step through it, and there was no category for that except the one at the top of every list the Inquisition kept, the one written in the dried-blood ribbon color: *warden-class. Take alive.*

And refusal meant being hunted. Being hunted meant the hounds, the sniffer-beasts, the neighbors paid in coppers to remember faces. Being hunted meant, eventually, the thing Edmund had fled at nineteen, the thing that had burned the orchard and the letters and the name they'd been born with.

*Odile.* The name surfaced unbidden, the way it did maybe twice a year, sharp as a splinter working out of skin. Somewhere east, across the flats, her sister was — what? Hiding the same way? Already taken? Odile, who had stayed behind when Edmund ran, who had sent no word in twelve years because word was a thread and threads got pulled. If the Hunt reached the countryside—

Edmund became aware that a customer was standing at the stall. The honeymooner's husband, holding his new coastline map, saying something. Edmund surfaced.

"—said is it a new edition?" the man repeated. "The coastline?"

"It's current," Edmund heard themselves say. Shopkeeper's voice. Bored, warm, level. "Drawn this spring."

The criers were moving on to the next square. The crowd was breaking apart into knots of conversation, and the conversations had a new texture, a new frequency, and Edmund stood among them selling maps of a world that was, as of this morning, rearranging itself.

They closed the stall early after all. Hands steady — the hands were always steady, that was the horror of it, the body had learned this work too well — they broke the stall into its six pieces and carried them down through the blue hour to the cellar on Rope Lane, and locked the door, and lit no lamp, and sat in the dark among the honest maps and the dishonest peace they'd built.

Somewhere across the city, a clerk was showing his wife a humming charm.

Somewhere in the dark below the Customhouse, lists were already being drawn.

And somewhere east, past the salt flats, in an orchard that should have died years ago, a woman who shared Edmund's blood was about to learn that the long hiding was over — for both of them.

Edmund sat in the dark and, for the first time in twelve years, began to plan a flight.
