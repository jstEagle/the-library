## 2. Twelve Years of Small Lies

The name on the stall's license read E. Vane. It had read E. Vane for twelve years, and Edmund had learned to answer to it the way a dog learns to answer to a whistle — instantly, without thinking, without ever quite believing the sound was meant for them.

They closed the stall at dusk and counted the day's coin into three piles: rent, stock, and the small iron box beneath the floorboard that held what Edmund privately called the escape fund, though they had never once said that word aloud, even to themselves, even in the privacy of their own skull. Escape was a shape. Caution was a practice. And practice, done long enough, became a kind of prayer.

Twelve years. They measured everything against it now — not years as the calendar counted them but as a body counts a scar. Twelve winters since Saltmere's bells rang the Purge. Twelve springs since the last warden's cottage outside the city went up with green fire in its windows, and a nineteen-year-old boy stood on the far side of a barley field and did not go back.

* * *

He had been Edmund Villalobos then. That was the first thing to understand about grief: it kept your old name like a coat you'd outgrown, hanging in a closet you could never quite bring yourself to empty.

The night of the Purge, he had been nineteen and furious, which is a way of saying he had been young. Grandmother Maren had spent the whole spring teaching him the Veilroads — not the drawing of them, which any fool with ink could attempt, but the *hearing* of them, the way you held a blank page up to the light at certain hours and saw the faint silver seams where the world had been stitched together by someone who hadn't expected anyone to look.

"You don't map places," she had said, her hands over his, both of them smelling of lamp oil and salt. "Places are already mapped. You map the *between*. That's the family work. That's the warden's work."

"I don't want to be a warden," he had said, because nineteen-year-olds say things like that, and because the word *warden* sounded like a cage with better furniture.

Grandmother had laughed — a short, dry sound, like a book closing. "No one wants to be a warden. That's how we know we can be trusted."

Three weeks later the king's riders came down the orchard road with torches and a list, and the word *warden* stopped being a title and became a sentence.

Edmund remembered it in pieces, the way you remember anything you've spent twelve years trying not to remember. The green light in the kitchen windows. Odile's voice calling his name from somewhere behind him — not *Edmund*, she wouldn't have risked the name, but the old whistle, two notes, the one they'd used since childhood to find each other in crowds. He remembered not turning around. He remembered the barley stalks whipping his hands bloody as he ran east toward Saltmere, toward the city where magic had gone to die, where a person with a gift could fold themselves small enough to survive if they were careful, if they were ruthless, if they were willing to become someone else entirely.

He remembered that he did not look back. That was the piece he kept locked deepest. Not the fire, not the soldiers, not grandmother's voice going silent somewhere behind the smoke. Just the not-looking-back. Everything else, in twelve years, had softened into something almost bearable. The not-looking-back stayed sharp as the day he'd done it.

Odile would have turned around. That was the thing about his sister. Odile ran *toward*. She always had — toward the wounded bird, toward the argument, toward the burning building, toward the truth. Edmund ran away, and he was good at it, and being good at the thing that saved your life was its own quiet damnation.

* * *

In the present, Edmund climbed the stairs to the room above the chandler's shop, lit no lamp, and stood at the window watching the market district settle into its evening rituals. A woman beat a rug. Two boys raced a hoop down the lane. Somewhere a fiddle started up, badly.

This was the ritual. Every night, the same inventory. *You are E. Vane. You sell charms for luck and love and safe travel, all of them dead things, wood and wire and dried lavender, nothing in them but craft.* The customers who bought them believed in them the way people believe in horseshoes, and that was fine, that was safe, belief without substance harmed no one. Once or twice a year someone touched a charm and frowned, sensing — what? A residue? An echo? Edmund had gotten skilled at laughing it off. *Old oak, friend. Old trees hold old stories.* People wanted to be reassured more than they wanted to be right.

And underneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, folded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth: the maps. Not the ones he sold. The real ones. The Veilroads of four kingdoms, charted in silver-thread ink that only showed itself to someone who could hear the between-places. Twelve years of nights, mapping the family work in secret, because he could not do it and he could not stop doing it, and this contradiction was the truest thing about him.

Grandmother would have understood. Or she would have boxed his ears. With Maren Villalobos the two were often the same gesture.

*You left her,* said the voice that lived under the floorboards with the maps. *You left her in the orchard with the soldiers coming, and you have never once written, never once sent word, because a letter is a thread and a thread can be pulled.*

*I saved myself,* said the other voice, the one he used at the market, bright and easy. *She'd have wanted me to live.*

*Would she?*

He didn't answer that one. He never answered that one.

* * *

There was a discipline to it, after twelve years. Edmund thought of it sometimes as an art form, though a crueler part of him knew it was just fear wearing a craftsman's apron.

Rule one: never use the gift where a soul could see. Rule two: never draw a Veilroad open, not even a crack, not even to breathe through it on the bad nights when the silence of the ordinary world pressed down like snow-load on a roof. Rule three: never say the name. Not Villalobos, not Maren, not *warden*, not — hardest of all — *Odile*.

That last rule he broke every night, but only inside, only in the dark behind his eyes, where he permitted himself the luxury of saying his sister's name the way a starving man permits himself to smell bread. *Odile.* She'd be thirty-one now. Did she still whistle when she worked? Had she married? Was she — and here the thought always caught, like a nail in cloth — was she alive?

He had tried, in the early years, to learn. There had been a peddler who came up from the southern valleys twice a year, and Edmund had asked him, casually, terribly casually, whether the villages out past Harrow Fen still stood, whether the orchards still bore. The peddler had said yes, mostly, there'd been a hard blight some years back, whole orchards died, though he'd heard tell of one farm where the trees bloomed out of season, every year, like they didn't know the rules.

Edmund had walked home with his heart hammering and told himself it proved nothing. Orchards bloomed out of season all the time. Weather was strange. Coincidence was a fact of the world.

But he had known. Even then, standing in his narrow room with his hands shaking, he had known. Because luck like that wasn't luck. Luck like that was a girl who had learned to hide her gift by dressing it up as fortune, the same way Edmund hid his by dressing it up as commerce. Two children of Maren Villalobos, each folding themselves smaller and smaller, each pretending the other was dead, because dead sisters couldn't be traced, and traced sisters got burned.

He had grieved her like a dead woman for twelve years. It occurred to him sometimes, in the hour before sleep, that grieving the living was the cruelest arithmetic there was. You got all the loss and none of the peace.

* * *

On the worst nights — and tonight was shaping into one — he took out the oldest map. Not a Veilroad chart. Something older, drawn in grandmother's hand, the ink browned to the color of tea. It showed the salt flats east of the city, and beyond them a region marked with a symbol Edmund had spent twelve years refusing to study too closely: a circle bisected by a line, the warden's sigil, and beneath it, in Maren's cramped script, a single line:

*It sleeps where it was spilled. It wakes when the moon has turned thirteen times. Keep the count.*

Keep the count. As if the moon were a loaf rising and someone needed to watch the oven.

Edmund had counted anyway. Everyone with a drop of the old blood counted; it was in the bones, like knowing which hand holds the knife. Thirteen turns of the moon from the night of the Purge. He was eleven turns in. Whatever grandmother had been keeping — whatever the wardens had died protecting — it was waking up, slowly, the way feeling wakes in a numbed limb, all pins and needles and dread.

He refolded the map before the thought could finish itself. Some doors you did not stand in front of, even locked ones. Especially locked ones.

Below the window, the fiddle player had given up or been driven off; the street was quiet. Edmund banked the cold hearth, lay down dressed as always — boots within reach, escape fund strapped under the shirt, the practiced choreography of a man ready to leave his own life at a moment's notice — and stared at the ceiling beams until the dark did what the dark did, which was to stop being the present and start being the past.

* * *

Nineteen again. The barley field. The fire behind him painting the clouds green.

He had made it to Saltmere on the third day, half-starved, and stood at the Westgate while a bored guard checked papers he didn't have. It was there, pressed against the gatehouse stone with a soldier's hand on his shoulder, that the gift had saved him for the first time — unprompted, unbidden, the page of his forged travel pass warming under his fingers until the ink rearranged itself into something official-looking, seals and all. The soldier had squinted, shrugged, waved him through.

He had vomited in the gutter ten paces inside the wall. Not from fear. From relief. And he had understood, kneeling there in the stink of the city's welcome, that he would spend the rest of his life doing exactly this: small magics, small lies, each one a brick in the wall between himself and the fire. Each one also a brick in the wall between himself and everyone he might have been.

A cartographer who could not publish. A warden with nothing left to ward. A brother with a sister he had chosen, actively, deliberately, in the space of one running stride, to abandon — because the alternative was turning around, and turning around meant dying with her, and dying with her meant the bloodline ended in smoke and the Salt woke for whoever came next, and grandmother's last instruction had been *keep the count*, and you cannot keep a count if you are ash.

He had told himself all of it, that night and every night since. He was very good at telling himself things. It was, he sometimes thought, the family gift — not the maps, not the hearing of the between-places, but the narrative. The ability to make the story come out with yourself still standing in it.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin. Edmund opened the stall at the usual hour, hung the charms in their usual rows, and performed the usual smile at the usual early customers: a fishwife wanting a charm against storms, a young mason wanting one against falling, a grandmother buying a birth-luck token for a daughter heavy with child. Dead charms, all of them. Wood and wire and lavender. He sold them honestly, which was to say he let people believe whatever got them through the day.

It was mid-morning when the criers began.

He heard the first bell from the Belltower of the Law — a deep, wrong-sounding toll, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause, the rhythm reserved for proclamations of state. The market noise thinned. Faces turned toward the square. And Edmund's hands, wrapping a charm in paper, went very still.

Three strokes and a pause. He had heard it once before, twelve years ago, from across a barley field, and he had run until his lungs bled.

The crier mounted the fountain steps, unrolled a scroll with ceremonial slowness, and pitched his voice to carry over the rooftops.

"By order of the Crown and the Office of Inquisition!" he called. "Let all persons possessing the forbidden gift present themselves for registration within one month's turn! Those who register shall be marked and released to supervised labor! Those who conceal themselves—" the pause was theatrical, expert— "shall be taken. Reward of fifty crowns for information leading to the discovery of the concealed gifted. The Hunt begins at dawn."

The crowd cheered.

That was the part Edmund would remember afterward, lying awake that night with the escape fund cold against his sternum. Not the words — he'd known those words were coming for twelve years, had felt them approaching the way you feel weather in an old wound. It was the cheering. The fishwife who'd bought a storm-charm an hour ago was cheering. The grandmother with the birth-token was cheering. Good people, kind people, people he'd served and smiled at for a decade, cheering the promise that people like him would be dragged into the light and marked like cattle.

Not malice. Worse than malice. Relief. The relief of a town that had lived so long beside a buried thing that any announcement of digging felt like safety, no matter what the digging turned up.

Edmund closed the stall early. His hands shook so badly he dropped the lockbox twice getting it from under the counter, and the coins inside made a sound like teeth chattering.

Fifty crowns. Someone in this market — someone who had shaken his hand, haggled over a love-charm, wished him good harvest — would sell him for fifty crowns before the month was out. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe today.

He carried the box upstairs and sat in the gray light with the maps spread around him like fallen leaves, and for the first time in twelve years he allowed himself to think the thought entire, end to end, without flinching:

*It's time to run again. And this time I know where I'm running to.*

East. Past the salt flats. To the place marked with the bisected circle, the sleeping thing, the inheritance he had abandoned along with the name. If the crown was going to dig up the whole country looking for the gifted, then the hiding was over, and if the hiding was over, then the only question left was the one grandmother had asked him when he was nineteen and stupid:

What are you, Edmund Villalobos? A warden, or ash?

He was reaching for the oldest map — the one with the sigil, the one with *keep the count* — when the knock came at the door below. Three strokes and a pause.

Three strokes and a pause.

His blood went to ice water. Then the knock came again, impatient, human, and a voice called up the stairwell, a woman's voice, dry and amused and utterly unfamiliar:

"Master Vane? I'm told you're the only mapmaker in this city worth the coin. I need a route to a place that doesn't exist."

Edmund sat very still among his maps, listening to his own heartbeat slow itself down by force, and reached for the mask he had worn for twelve years.

"Come up," he called, in E. Vane's easy voice. "Everyone's looking for a place that doesn't exist. The trick is the price."
