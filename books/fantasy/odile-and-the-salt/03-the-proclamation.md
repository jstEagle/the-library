## 3. The Proclamation

The morning the Gift-Hunt was announced began like any other morning in Saltmere, which is to say it began with Edmund lying about what things were.

They had been doing it for so long that the lies had worn smooth as river stones. The brass compass in the third tray was not a compass that pointed toward doors; it was a compass, full stop, and a decorative one at that, its needle prone to wandering because of a flaw in the housing. The blue glass bottle was not a captured piece of sky but a perfume vial, long empty. The iron nail bent into a ring was folk art from the eastern provinces, nothing more, and if you held it up to your eye and looked through it and saw the market as it had looked forty years ago — well, that was the trick of old glass, wasn't it, the way old glass warped things.

Edmund said all of this in the same warm, apologetic tone they'd perfected over twelve years behind the stall: *Curiosities, charms for luck, honest trinkets for honest coin.* The word "charm" was the great camouflage. In Saltmere, charm meant superstition, and superstition was permitted. Superstition was quaint. A woman could buy a knotted red thread against headaches and no one thought twice, because everyone knew knots did nothing.

That was the genius of it, if Edmund was honest with themselves, and they tried to be honest with themselves at least once a day, usually on the walk home when the stalls were folding and the light went long and amber across the cobbles. The Purge hadn't just burned the gifted. It had taught an entire city that magic was a thing of stories, and stories were harmless. Edmund's stall survived not despite the law but inside its blind spot, selling wonders disguised as nonsense to people who had been trained never to look too closely at anything that might be real.

So: a Tuesday in late autumn, gray overhead, the market smells doing their usual braiding — frying onions, wet wool, tallow, the mineral tang of the harbor three streets east. Edmund had sold four items by mid-morning. A fisherman's wife bought the red thread. A boy with a chipped tooth spent a stolen-looking copper on a tin whistle that played one note of tomorrow's weather (rain, obviously; it always played rain in Saltmere). An old man examined the compass for a long time without touching it, then put it down carefully, the way one puts down something hot, and walked away without meeting Edmund's eyes.

Edmund watched him go and told themselves it meant nothing. Old men walked away from everything. It was practically the definition of being old.

Then the bells changed.

Saltmere's bells had a grammar. Market bells rang quick and even. Alarm bells ran ragged. Funeral bells came slow, spaced like a mourner's footsteps. What rolled out of the Cathedral tower that morning was none of these — it was a pattern Edmund had heard only once before, at nine years old, standing on a chair at the window while their grandmother pulled them back by the collar and said, *Down. Away from the glass. Now.* It was the civic cadence, the proclamation peal, the sound the city made when the crown had something to say and wanted every ear in Saltmere to know it would be said whether they listened or not.

Edmund's hands stopped moving over the trays. For a moment the whole market seemed to hold its breath — not silence, exactly, but a thinning, a collective narrowing of attention, the way a field of grass all leans the same direction when wind comes through.

Then the crowd turned toward the square.

Edmund knew they should pack up now. That was the instinct, the animal instinct, the one that said *when the herd moves, be elsewhere*. But there is a kind of fear that pins you, that says you must see it, you must watch it arrive, because a thing unseen can still be imagined away and Edmund had spent twelve years imagining things away and some cold cellar of the mind understood that this morning the imagining was over.

Three criers came up Copperlane from the direction of the Magistrate's House, walking abreast, flanked by six soldiers in crown gray. The soldiers' pikes caught what little light there was. The criers wore the livery of the Chancery — black and bone-white — and the lead one carried a scroll bound in ribbon the color of dried blood, and Edmund thought, absurdly, of the red thread they'd sold the fisherman's wife, and how the same color meant such different things depending on who tied the knot.

The lead crier mounted the steps of the old weighing-house. Unrolled the scroll. And read.

"Hear ye, by decree of the Chancellor Vess, speaking with the voice of His Majesty's crown." His voice was trained, pitched to carry, each sentence landing like a coin dropped from a height. "It having come to the knowledge of the throne that practitioners of the forbidden arts persist within our cities, towns, and lands —"

*Persist.* Edmund felt the word go in under the ribs. Not *lurk*, not *hide* — those were words for monsters. *Persist* was a word for weeds, for vermin, for things that should have died and hadn't.

"— the crown does hereby declare a season of Registration and Reconciliation, to commence upon this day and to endure until the thirteenth turn of the moon."

Something turned over in Edmund's chest at *thirteenth turn*, some old half-remembered weight, grandmother's voice saying something about moons and turns in a tone she never used for anything else — but the crier was already going on, and there was no time to dig for it.

"All persons possessing or suspected of possessing the gift shall present themselves at any Chancery office within thirty days, there to be registered, examined, and reconciled to the law."

*Reconciled.* Everyone in the crowd knew what reconciliation meant. It was the gentle word the histories used for the pyres.

"Those who register freely shall be shown the crown's mercy."

A pause. The crier was good; he let the pause do its work.

"Those who are taken shall not."

And here was the thing Edmund would remember afterward, would turn over at night for weeks the way a tongue worries a broken tooth: the crowd cheered.

Not all of it. Not loudly, not all at once. It started somewhere near the fountain — two voices, maybe three, a scattered clapping — and then it spread the way warmth spreads through a cold room, the way laughter spreads at a funeral when someone finally breaks. People Edmund had sold threads and whistles and lucky horseshoe-nails to for a decade lifted their faces and clapped. The fisherman's wife. The boy with the chipped tooth, whistling his rain-note between his fingers. A woman who bought candles from the stall next door, shouting something bright and ugly that Edmund didn't catch and didn't need to.

They weren't cheering cruelty, Edmund understood. That was the terrible part. They were cheering safety — or the promise of it, the announcement of it, the theater of it. Twelve years since the last purge, and the city had grown comfortable again, and comfort always wants a fence around itself, and fear of the gift was the fence the crown had built, and today the crown was coming around to check the posts, and the neighbors were lining the road to wave.

*You've sold these people wonder,* Edmund thought, watching the applause ripple outward, *and they're applauding the men who burn it.*

Or maybe that was unfair. Maybe it was simpler and sadder than that. Maybe they applauded because everyone in the crowd had someone to protect, and the fastest way to protect your family was to point, eagerly, loudly, at anyone else's.

The soldiers fanned out along the edges of the square after the reading, unhurried, professional. One of them took up a post not twenty feet from Edmund's stall, pike grounded, face bored, eyes doing the thing soldier eyes do — moving, cataloguing, dismissing. They lingered on the awning. On the sign. *E. GALLOWS — CURIOSITIES & CHARMS.* Edmund had chosen the name twelve years ago, a small bitter joke, Gallows for Villalobos, a name buried in plain sight. The soldier's gaze passed over it like water off oilcloth.

Edmund smiled at him. It was the smile they used on everyone, mild and faintly apologetic, the smile of a person with nothing behind their eyes but inventory. The soldier nodded back, the nod one gives furniture.

Inside, behind the smile, Edmund was doing arithmetic.

Thirty days to register. Thirty days, or be taken. And registration meant examination, and examination meant — what? Iron filings pressed to the skin? The sniffer-beasts the old stories mentioned, creatures bred to taste magic on the air the way hounds taste fear? Grandmother had written about the examinations during the first Purge, in letters Odile kept and Edmund had left behind. She'd written that the examiners could find a gift the way you find a splinter: slowly, painfully, and always.

Twelve years of wards. Twelve years of suppression charms stitched into the lining of their coat, renewed monthly, sweating over each knot. Twelve years of never working anything larger than a whisper, never letting the gift rise above a simmer, until sometimes Edmund genuinely wondered whether they still had it at all — whether it had starved quietly in its cage, or gone strange and feral down there in the dark, or simply become Edmund, indistinguishable from the person, the way a river becomes the bed it has carved.

Would it show? Would the examiners taste twelve years of careful living and find only a nervous tradesman?

And beneath that question, quieter, more dangerous, the question Edmund refused to look at directly: *do you want them to find nothing?*

Because there were mornings — this was the shameful truth, the truth they only admitted on the walk home — there were mornings when the gift stirred at dawn like a second heartbeat, and Edmund woke already grieving, already hungry for it, the way you hunger for a language you've almost forgotten how to speak. There were nights they lay awake and mapped Veilroads on the ceiling of their mind, roads between realms, doors in the walls of the world, the great secret work of their life done entirely in imagination because doing it in fact was death — and the imagining was so vivid, so *real*, that sometimes they caught themselves mid-vision with chalk dust on their fingers and no memory of reaching for the chalk.

If the examiners found nothing, that life was over forever. If they found something —

The bell in the Cathedral tower rang the proclamation cadence again, a second reading for the latecomers, and Edmund moved.

They moved the way they did everything: without hurry, without drama, a shopkeeper closing early because of weather. They began setting the trays into their box, item by item. Red thread. Tin whistle. The blue bottle — they paused over it, thumb resting on the cool glass, feeling the faint pressure of the little piece of sky inside pushing against its prison, patient, uncomplaining, the way imprisoned things are patient when they don't know any better. Into the box.

The compass last. The compass that pointed at doors. Edmund wrapped it in cloth and their hands were shaking.

Not much. A fine tremor, barely visible, the kind a cold morning explains away. But Edmund could feel it in the wrists, in the hinge of the jaw, a vibration like a plucked string that wouldn't settle. This was the terror they knew best — not the screaming kind, not the kind that makes you run. The quiet kind. The kind that arrives the way the crier arrived: formally, with a scroll, at an hour already scheduled long ago. *We have always been coming,* it said. *You have always known. Today is simply the day the knock sounds.*

Grandmother had described this too, in one of the letters Odile kept. *Fear is a houseguest that arrives claiming to visit briefly,* she'd written, *but it unpacks. Feed it nothing and it stays anyway. The trick is not to stop shaking. The trick is to keep your hands busy while you shake.*

So Edmund kept their hands busy. Tray into box. Cloth over tray. Awning folded, poles slotted, the canvas with its faded painted sun going dark and dumb. Each motion smooth, practiced, performed for the soldier twenty feet away, who watched with mild disinterest and saw exactly what Edmund intended him to see: a vendor beaten by weather, packing up before the rain.

The lie was flawless. That was the horror of it. Twelve years of practice and the machinery of deceit worked perfectly even now, even with the proclamation still ringing in the stones, even with their pulse hammering so hard they could feel it in their teeth. Somewhere in Edmund there was a nineteen-year-old still running from the Purge with smoke in their hair, and that nineteen-year-old was screaming, and the scream made no sound at all, because Edmund had built the silence brick by brick over twelve years and it held everything now, grief and guilt and gift and all.

They locked the box. Lifted it. Nodded to the candle-seller next door — "Early day, Marta. Weather coming." — and Marta agreed cheerfully, glancing at the gray sky, agreeing with the fiction because the fiction was polite and true enough, and Edmund walked south out of the square with a box of illegal wonders under one arm and their heart going like a trapped bird.

Behind them, the crier's voice rose again for a third reading, and this time Edmund caught the final lines clearly, the ones that had blurred past on the first pass:

"And be it further known: any citizen harboring, concealing, or failing to report a practitioner of the forbidden arts shall share in that practitioner's penalty."

*Failing to report.*

Edmund did not break stride. Did not turn. Kept walking, steady, unhurried, a tired shopkeeper going home. But the words followed them down Copperlane like footsteps that matched their own exactly. Because Edmund had spent twelve years being concealed. Every neighbor who'd ever bought a charm and said nothing. Marta, whose stall backed onto theirs, who'd seen the odd deliveries at odd hours. Every customer who'd ever held something humming and chose, deliberately, to believe it was nothing.

The crown hadn't just declared a hunt. It had declared that everyone Edmund had ever been kind to was now an accomplice — and that kindness, tested, broke faster than loyalty. The crowd had proven that already, hadn't it? The cheers. The applause rolling across the square like weather.

At the mouth of Cooper's Yard, where the lane narrowed and the shadows pooled, Edmund finally allowed themselves to stop. Set the box down. Pressed both palms flat against the cold brick of the wall and breathed, once, twice, the way grandmother taught — *in through the wound, out through the roots*, her old nonsense phrase that had somehow kept three generations of Villaloboses from panic.

Their hands were still shaking. Fine tremors, plucked-string tremors. But busier than that, underneath — a warmth gathering low in the sternum, the gift stirring like a dog hearing its name called from another room. It had felt the proclamation too, Edmund realized. Or felt Edmund's fear. Fear and the gift had always been tangled together in them, twin ropes cut from the same hemp.

*Thirty days,* Edmund thought. *Register, run, or —*

The third option arrived not as a decision but as an image, sudden and complete: the compass in its cloth wrapping, pointing not north but *through*, toward the hairline seam in the world that only Edmund could see. The Veilroads. The paths between realms that existed in rumor and in Edmund's secret maps and nowhere else on this side of the veil. Roads out. Roads that led anywhere, everywhere — including, according to the oldest of the hidden charts, toward a destination Edmund had spent a lifetime refusing to name.

The Salt.

No. Not yet. Not thinking of that. First: home. First: bolt the door, ward the lintel, count the days. First: survive tonight.

Edmund picked up the box and went down into the yard, and behind them the bells rang the proclamation cadence a fourth time, and fifth, patient as the tide, making sure the whole city heard — and somewhere in the crowd they'd left behind, Edmund would learn soon enough, a sharp-eyed courier had stopped mid-stride, listening to the words *thirteenth turn of the moon* with an expression of great interest, and was already turning over in her mind an old coin stamped with a sigil, and deciding whom to visit first.
