## 4. A Client Named Wren

The woman came in at the hour when the market light turned the color of weak tea, and she did not browse.

That was the first thing wrong with her. Everyone browsed. Browsing was the ritual that let a customer pretend they had wandered into Edmund's stall by accident, that the charms and trinkets were incidental, that no one came here on purpose for anything but a lucky copper bird or a carved key that opened nothing. Browsing gave everyone plausible deniability. It was the social contract of Saltmere: we will all agree this is a shop like any other, and in exchange you will all agree I am a shopkeeper like any other.

This woman walked straight up to the counter as if it were a door she'd been told to knock on, set both hands flat on the wood, and said, "You're the one who does maps."

Edmund was re-inking a compass rose on a chart of the Amber Coast — a real coast, a safe coast, a coast any honest cartographer might sell. They didn't look up right away. Looking up too fast was a tell. Twelve years had taught them the choreography of innocence: finish your stroke, wipe your nib, then raise your eyes with mild, faintly bored courtesy.

"I do maps," Edmund said. "Coastal charts, trade routes, estate surveys. What did you need?"

"Something inland."

"Inland's mostly guesses. Nobody's surveyed the interior since the war."

"That's what I'm counting on."

She was perhaps thirty, lean in the way of people whose work was measured in miles, with wind-chapped cheeks and eyes the gray-green of harbor water — the kind of color that looked different every time you checked. Her traveling cloak was good quality but deliberately scuffed, the way expensive things were aged by people who wanted them read as ordinary. A courier, Edmund thought. Guild badge or private contract, hard to say. She wore no visible weapons, which meant either she was unarmed or she was very good at hiding it.

"I can draw you an inland guess," Edmund said. "It'll cost the same as a truth, though, so I want to be clear about what you're buying. Guesses are pretty. Truths are rare."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, filed away for later use. "Honesty from a charm-seller. The market's full of surprises today."

"The market's full of everything. That's rather the point of markets." Edmund capped the ink bottle. "Name?"

"Wren."

"Just Wren?"

"For now."

*Just Wren.* Edmund had been *just Ed* for twelve years, and before that *just Edmund*, and before that a name they kept folded small in the pocket of their heart where it couldn't snag on anything. People who offered single names were either hiding something or had already decided what they were hiding didn't matter. Both kinds were trouble. One kind was interesting.

"What place," Edmund asked, "are you trying to get to?"

Wren glanced over her shoulder. Not nervously — she moved the way cats check behind them, out of architecture rather than fear. Then she leaned in slightly, and her voice dropped into a register meant only for two people and the wood between them.

"There's a road," she said, "that starts at a standing stone in the Grayfell hills and doesn't end anywhere on any map ever printed. Walkers who take it come back three days later swearing they walked for a season. Shepherds won't graze flocks near the stone. There's a village at its foot called Harrow's Bend where the churchyard has graves with names nobody remembers giving them. And twice in recorded history — twice — someone has come back down that road carrying goods that don't exist anywhere else. Cloth dyed a color that isn't in any dyer's book. Fruit that keeps for years. Once, a knife that never dulled."

The stall felt smaller. Edmund was aware of the charms hanging above them — the copper birds, the knotted strings, the little glass bells — all of them silent, all of them dead weight and wire, and aware of how thin that silence suddenly seemed, like ice over running water.

"You're describing a Veilroad," Edmund said, and hated themselves for saying it.

Because that was the second thing wrong with her, or maybe the first thing right: she hadn't said the word. She'd circled it, laid out its feathers one by one, and waited for Edmund to name the bird. Which meant she knew the word herself. Which meant she wasn't testing whether such roads existed. She was testing whether Edmund knew.

Wren straightened. "I have no idea what that means."

"No," Edmund agreed. "Of course not."

They sat with that for a moment. Around them the market went on being the market — a fishmonger arguing about brine, children daring each other to touch the tanner's lime pits, somewhere a fiddle badly played. The ordinary noise of the world, holding its shape around them like a wall holds out weather.

"I'll pay fifty crowns," Wren said.

Fifty crowns was a year of rent. Fifty crowns was new boots, a winter of coal, the debt on the stall paid down to nothing. Fifty crowns was also, Edmund thought with cold clarity, exactly the kind of number that made a person stupid, and Wren would know that, because Wren had chosen it on purpose.

"Fifty crowns for a map to a road that doesn't exist," Edmund said slowly, "from a cartographer whose entire reputation rests on drawing places that do."

"Your reputation," Wren said, "rests on something else. I asked around. You've got a gift for coastlines. Your harbors are always right — depths, shoals, sandbars shifted after storms that happened before you drew them. The Guild thinks you've got informants. Informants cost money. Fifty crowns says you don't have informants."

The tea-colored light had gone thin and cold without Edmund noticing. Their hands, resting on the counter, were perfectly still, because stillness was the last skill left when everything else was burning.

"Who told you about my harbors?"

"A man named Corvus. Fat, rings on four fingers, sells salvage charts out of the Tidemark district. Pleasant enough. Talks too much when he drinks."

Corvus. Edmund's stomach performed a slow, deliberate rotation. Corvus bought three charts a year and haggled over every one, and last month he'd drunk too much plum wine and asked, laughing, how a man could know a channel had silted shut two days before the storm that silted it. Edmund had laughed too, and said, *good luck is good business,* and gone home and lain awake until dawn doing arithmetic on how much Corvus knew versus how much Corvus suspected versus how much Corvus had told anyone while drunk.

Apparently some portion greater than zero.

"He exaggerates," Edmund said.

"Probably. But here's the thing about exaggeration — it has to start with something." Wren reached into her coat.

Everything in Edmund tightened. In Saltmere, sudden movements near a charm-stall were how raids started, how accusations started, how the whole fragile pantomime came apart in a spray of shouted assumptions. But Wren only drew out a purse — soft leather, worn smooth — and from the purse, coins.

She counted them onto the counter one at a time. Ten. Twenty. Thirty. The sound of each one landing was a small bright bell-note, and the market noise seemed to bend around it.

Forty. Fifty.

Edmund looked down at the stack and stopped breathing.

They were old. Not antique-old in the way collectors liked, with polished faces and velvet cases — old in the way of things that had been buried and dug up and carried against skin for generations. The silver was dark, almost black at the edges, worn high on the centers where thumbs had rubbed them across centuries. And stamped into each face was a sigil Edmund knew the way you know your own handwriting, your own front door, the scar on your own knee:

A circle. Inside the circle, a salt crystal, six-sided. And threaded through the crystal, a single line — a road, drawn as if the crystal itself were a gate the road passed through.

The warden's mark.

Grandmother's mark. The mark that had been pressed into wax seals on letters Odile burned and Edmund fled. The mark on the clasp of the locket Grandmother had pressed into nine-year-old Edmund's hand the night before the Purge reached their valley, saying, *keep this, little mapmaker, and whatever happens, remember the shape of it, because shapes are doors and you are going to need every door there is.*

There should have been none left. The wardens' holdings had been seized, their coin melted, their name scraped from registers. That was half of what the Purge had been — not just killing the gifted, but unmaking the evidence that they had ever existed, that anyone had ever been trusted to stand between magic and the world.

And here were fifty of them, stacked in fives on a counter in Saltmere, in the year 214 of the Sundering Reckoning, being paid out by a courier who claimed not to know what a Veilroad was.

Edmund's mouth was dry. "These aren't legal tender."

"They're silver, same as anything. Silver doesn't care whose face it wears." Wren's harbor-water eyes were steady. "Do you recognize the stamp?"

"No."

The lie came out fast and flat and wrong, and Edmund heard it land wrong, heard it sit between them like a dropped plate. Twelve years of practice and when it mattered most they lied like a child with jam on their face insisting they hadn't touched the jar.

Wren didn't smile. That was worse. She simply watched, patient as tide, and let the silence do her work, and the silence said: *you recognized it instantly, your whole body flinched like I'd struck you, and now you owe me the truth of why.*

"I found them," she said at last, "in a strongbox under a floor in Harrow's Bend. Under the churchyard, actually, if we're being precise. Buried with papers. Most of the papers rotted. Some didn't." She paused. "Some of them mentioned a family. Villalobos."

The name went through Edmund like cold water through cloth.

No one had said it aloud in twelve years. Edmund had not said it, had trained themselves not even to think it in company, had worn *Ed* the way other people wore gloves. And here it was, spoken casually in a market stall, by a stranger, attached to warden coin pulled from beneath a churchyard in the Grayfells — and the world did not end, the criers did not cry out, the fishmonger went on shouting about brine, and Edmund stood in the wreckage of their own composure trying to remember how faces worked.

"Common name," Edmund managed.

"Is it?"

"Italianate. Old settler stock. Half the eastern provinces are full of Villaloboses."

"Mm." Wren gathered her coins back into the purse — all except five, which she left on the counter, deliberate as a signature. "Keep those. Consider it payment for the conversation. If you decide you want the other forty-five, you know where to find me. The Drowned Lantern inn, off Tanner's Row. I'll be there three nights."

"And if I don't come?"

"Then I'll find another cartographer." She shrugged. "But they'll only draw me a guess. And guesses get people killed on roads like that."

She turned to go, then stopped, one hand on the stall's corner post, and looked back. For a moment the courier polish slipped and something else showed underneath — tiredness, real tiredness, the kind earned over long miles alone.

"One more thing," she said. "The papers under the churchyard. Whatever family they mention — the ones who buried that box — the writing calls them *wardens*. Like it was a job. Like somebody hired them." Her jaw worked. "I've been carrying that word around for a month, and I can't make it fit anything. Warden of what? There's nothing left to guard. Everything worth guarding burned in the war."

*Not everything,* Edmund thought, and the thought arrived with a physical sensation, a warmth behind the sternum like a coal breathed upon.

Aloud, they said nothing at all.

Wren held their gaze a moment longer — reading it, cataloguing it, filing it wherever couriers filed things — and then she was gone, swallowed by the market crowd, moving through it the way water moves through a net: touched by everything, caught by nothing.

* * *

Edmund closed the stall early.

They never closed early. Closing early was a flag, a signal, the sort of thing neighbors noticed and remembered and mentioned later to men with official questions. Edmund knew this. Edmund closed anyway, latching the shutters with fingers that refused steadiness, and stood in the dim inside among the hanging charms while their heart attempted to leave through their ribs.

Five warden coins sat on the counter. Edmund didn't touch them for a long time.

Think. Think like a cartographer, which was to say: think like someone whose entire survival depended on distinguishing true terrain from plausible terrain.

Option one: coincidence. Old coins circulated; sigils repeated; a circle and a crystal could mean a dozen innocent things. This theory collapsed on contact with the rest of the facts. Wren hadn't shown the coins first. She'd built the whole encounter — the Veilroad story, the price, the pressure — and produced the sigil only after watching Edmund's face for a full minute. That wasn't commerce. That was interrogation dressed as commerce.

Option two: trap. Someone — the crown, the Inquisition, whoever fed on the gifted these days — had gotten a thread of Edmund through Corvus, and was pulling it gently, with bait instead of force. It was elegant. It was exactly what Edmund would design, in a colder mood. The warden coins were the perfect lure because no one living should have them, and only a warden's blood would react.

But traps wanted confession, and Wren hadn't pushed for one. A trap-setter would have leaned harder when the lie landed flat. Wren had done something stranger: she'd left money, left an address, left *time*. Three nights. That was not how you sprang a snare. That was how you made an offer.

Which brought option three, the one Edmund kept circling and refusing to land on: Wren Ashgrave — if that was even her name — genuinely had found a warden cache under a churchyard, genuinely carried questions, and genuinely needed a guide into territory that appeared on no map because the only people who could draw it had spent twelve years pretending the talent didn't exist.

And option three had a corollary, and the corollary was the thing making Edmund's hands shake:

If there were warden caches turning up under churchyards, then the wardens had hidden things. Deliberately. Broadly. Grandmother-style hiding — the kind with layers and contingencies and letters addressed to a future she wouldn't live to see. And if the wardens had hidden things, then the things were still there. Waiting. Marked with a circle and a six-sided crystal and a road through the middle of it.

Edmund picked up one of the five coins at last.

The metal was cold, then warm, then — and Edmund would spend years afterward wondering if this was memory or something else — it hummed. Faintly, at the very floor of hearing, the way a tuning fork hums after the strike, the way the air hums before weather changes. The copper birds overhead stirred on their strings though there was no draft.

*Put it down,* said twelve years of discipline, twelve years of small lies, twelve years of being *just Ed* with a stall full of harmless wire and glass.

Edmund put it down. Then picked it up again. Put it down. Picked it up.

Outside, the light failed. The lamplighter came along his rounds with his long pole, and the market packed itself away, and somewhere across the city a bell rang the hour, and Edmund stood in a shuttered stall holding a dead currency that wouldn't stop singing, doing the worst arithmetic of their life.

Three nights. The Drowned Lantern, off Tanner's Row. Forty-five crowns waiting, and a map to a road that shouldn't exist, and a word — *wardens* — spoken aloud for the first time in twelve years, tasting like ash and honey both.

If they went, they risked walking into a net. If they didn't go, they'd spend the rest of their life not knowing, and Edmund had learned the hard way that not knowing was its own kind of cage, with bars made of every question you never dared ask.

And beneath all the careful reasoning, quiet as groundwater, ran the thing that was neither reason nor caution: the pull. The old pull. The feeling of a door standing where the wall used to be.

Edmund wrapped the five coins in a scrap of chart linen and hid them inside the false bottom of the ink drawer, next to a locket they had not opened in twelve years.

Then they blew out the lamp and stood in the dark and listened to their heart slow down, and thought: *one night. I'll wait one night, and watch the Lantern from across the row, and see who else comes calling.*

It was a sensible plan. It was a careful plan. It was exactly the plan a survivor would make.

It lasted until morning, when the criers came.
