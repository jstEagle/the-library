## 5. What Odile Kept

The orchard had been dying for eleven years, and Odile Villalobos had been lying to it for ten.

She walked the rows at first light, before the mist burned off the low hills, because that was when the trees could almost be fooled. Frost clung to the black branches like a second bark. Eleven winters ago a blight had come through the valley — the neighbors called it the Gray Rot, and it had taken every orchard from Harrow's Bend to the coast road. Every orchard but hers.

That was the story, anyway. *Lucky soil*, the neighbors said. *Old Villalobos land, stubborn as its people.* They shook their heads at her trees, bare and gray while theirs rotted, and then they shook their heads harder in spring, when her dead-looking branches put out blossom in a green tide that followed her boots down each row.

Odile had learned early that the trick was not hiding the gift. The trick was giving people something else to see.

A woman who walked her orchard every morning was diligent. A woman whose trees bloomed where she'd trod was blessed by coincidence, or by good drainage, or by whatever explanation the village could invent that required no one to burn anyone. She let them settle on drainage. Drainage was boring. Boring was armor.

She crouched at the end of the third row, where an old Pippin stood with its roots in the stoniest corner of the hill, and pressed her palm flat against the trunk. Under the frost-gray skin, something answered — faint, sleepy, the way a dog answers a hand in the dark without waking. Warmth moved up through her wrist. Not much. Just enough.

"Not yet," she told it. "I know. I know."

The tree didn't argue. Trees never did. That was most of why she preferred them to people.

She straightened, wiped her hands on her apron, and allowed herself the morning's small dishonesty: she counted the blossoms that would come if she ever stopped rationing herself. If she walked every row and gave each tree what it asked for, the whole hillside would go up in white and pink so loud it would be visible from the market town, from the shrine on the far ridge, maybe from the sky. Some mornings she wanted it so badly her teeth ached. To stop being small on purpose. To let the thing in her blood stand up and say its name.

Her grandmother had called the gift *green-speech* and treated it like a debt rather than a treasure. *You don't spend it, Odile. You hold it. Holding is the work.* Maren Villalobos had held a great many things: the orchard, the letters, the old coin wrapped in waxed cloth at the bottom of a chest, and a silence about the family's past so complete it felt less like discretion than like a wall built one brick per year for seventy years.

Maren had been dead four years now, and the wall still stood, and Odile still kept bricks on it.

She finished the row, marked two trees for pruning, and was turning back toward the house when she saw the rider coming up the coast road.

*

He was a tinker by his cart, a peddler by his patter, and neither by his eyes, which stayed too long on the treeline the way men's eyes do when they've been paid to look. Odile met him at the gate with her arms crossed and her face arranged into the mild boredom of a woman with nothing worth stealing.

"Morning," he said. "Fine land."

"It's all right."

"Trees look half-dead." He nodded at the gray branches overhead. "Shame."

"They do that," Odile said. "Then they don't. It confuses everyone."

He laughed at that, a little too readily, and dug into his cart, and here it came — she'd known it would, word traveled faster than any horse, and she'd been waiting three days since the miller's boy mentioned criers in Harrow's Bend. The tinker produced a printed broadsheet, ink still sharp enough to smell, and held it up between two fingers like something he'd found on the road rather than carried.

"You'll want to hear this," he said. "Crown business. There's a proclamation out. Gift-Hunt, they're calling it." He read it badly, with relish, stumbling over the formal phrases: *all persons possessing or suspected of possessing the gift are commanded to present themselves for registration... those who conceal themselves will be sought... rewards offered for information leading to the discovery of the concealed gifted...*

Rewards. There it was. Not *punishment for sheltering them* — though that clause was in there too, smaller, the way teeth are smaller than the mouth that shows them. Rewards. Because the crown understood, as Maren had understood, that the surest way into a locked house was through the people inside it.

"Terrible business," the tinker said, watching her over the top of the paper. "You hear anything, you know, odd — growing things where they shouldn't, lights, weather behaving strange — you send word to the magistrate at Harrow's Bend. There's coin in it now."

"I'll keep my ears open," Odile said.

"Your trees," he said, and paused, and she watched him decide how to say it. "They bloom funny. Where you walk, I heard. Down at the mill they say—"

"Drainage," Odile said. "Spring water runs under the hill. It's well known."

He looked at her a moment longer than he needed to. Then he shrugged, tucked the broadsheet into her gatepost — *so you can reread it,* he said, which was either courtesy or a threat wearing courtesy's coat — climbed back onto his cart, and rattled off down the coast road without once looking back.

Which was worse. Guilty men look back. Men doing their job properly don't.

Odile stood at the gate until the cart was gone, then took the broadsheet down and read it herself, slowly, twice. The paper was cheap and the print was crooked, but the words were plain enough. Registration. Seeking. Rewards.

Twelve years ago, they hadn't bothered with registration. Twelve years ago they'd come with fire and lists already written, and the lists had names on them, and one of the names had been hers, age nineteen, and another name had been her brother's, and the reason neither of those names had ever been crossed out was that Edmund had run east with a stolen satchel of maps and Odile had stayed behind to bury their grandmother's strongest charms under the kitchen floor and become, overnight, a woman whose orchard bloomed because of drainage.

*Concealed gifted will be sought.*

She folded the broadsheet into quarters, went inside, and opened the chest at the foot of her bed.

*

There were forty-one letters.

She knew the number the way she knew the number of trees in the orchard, because she had counted both many times, in bad years especially. Forty-one letters in Maren Villalobos's narrow, upright hand, written across forty years to a granddaughter who wasn't born yet when the earliest ones were sealed. *For Odile, when she asks the right questions,* the bundle was labeled, and in twelve years Odile had asked herself a thousand questions and never once decided which of them were the right ones.

Some of the letters she knew by heart. The one about the war — the Sundering War, though Maren only ever called it *the bad time* — described it sideways, in terms of weather and bread prices, as if armies made of salt and light could be captured in the cost of flour. The one about Edmund's birth ran nine pages and mentioned the midwife's cat more often than the baby. The one Odile hated began *You will be angry with me for this, and you should be,* and explained, in Maren's patient terrible logic, why the truth had to be split between two grandchildren like seed grain, half to the one who left and half to the one who stayed, so that no single pair of hands would ever hold enough of it to matter.

So that no one would ever again be able to do what the last warden had done.

Warden. Even in her own head the word sat strangely, a key that fit a lock she'd never seen. Their grandmother had used it exactly once in Odile's hearing, drunk on plum brandy the winter before the Purge, sitting in this same kitchen with the wind worrying the shutters: *We were wardens, girl. Not owners. Wardens. Learn the difference or die of it.* In the morning Maren remembered nothing, or performed remembering nothing, and Odile had been nineteen and grieving and had chosen to let her.

Now the crown was offering coin for women whose trees bloomed where they walked.

Odile built the fire up high. Too high for cooking; she hadn't cooked since yesterday and wouldn't today. She took the bundle of letters from the chest and held it a moment, feeling the weight of forty-one small paper lives, and thought: *she wrote these to survive us. And now they can get us killed.* A letter that named the Salt, that named wardens, that named the thirteenth moon — she didn't know exactly what was in all of them, she'd never dared read past the surface of some — but she knew what an Inquisitor with a sniffer-beast would smell in them. Old magic soaks into paper the way lamp oil soaks into wool. You can't wash it out. You can only burn it.

"That's the trick, isn't it," she said to the empty kitchen, to the grandmother-shaped absence in it. "You taught me to hide things by making them look like luck. But you never taught me how to make forty-one letters look like drainage."

She fed the first letter to the fire.

It caught slowly, the way old paper does, curling at the corners before it gave. She watched the ink go brown, then black, then gone. Somewhere in that small flame was a description of a garden that had grown on the roof of a besieged city, feeding a street of refugees, and the name of the woman who'd grown it, and the price the woman paid. Odile had read it once, seven years ago, and wept, and put it back, and now it was heat and ash and nothing.

The second letter went in. Then the third. She worked steadily, without ceremony, because ceremony was a luxury and this was labor. Each letter she read fast — a line, two lines, just enough to say goodbye to it — before consigning it. The one about the roof-garden. The one about Edmund learning to walk on maps. The one that began *Do not trust the Ashgraves,* which she'd always assumed was paranoia and now, with a bounty hunt loose in the world, wondered about. The one that ended *the Salt remembers who carries it, even when you forget.*

By the time she reached the bottom of the bundle her eyes were streaming, and she told herself it was smoke.

Forty letters. One left.

It was the oldest, and the strangest, and the only one addressed not to *Odile* but to *whichever of you is reading this when the moon turns thirteen times.* She had found it years ago wedged separately beneath the false bottom of the chest, as if Maren had meant it to be found last or never, and she had never shown it to anyone, and she had read it exactly once, the night of the funeral, and had spent four years telling herself she'd imagined how much of it she understood.

She held it over the fire.

The paper warmed. The waxed fold softened. And underneath her fear, steady as root-pressure, rose the other thing — the thing that had kept her in this valley when running would have been smarter, the thing that made her palms itch to touch dying trees. Defiance, maybe. Or tenderness. With her they had always been the same muscle.

"No," she said aloud.

She pulled the letter back and the fire flared, offended, and settled.

She read it instead, standing, in full daylight, with the ashes of her grandmother's forty secrets cooling behind her. Most of it she had remembered true. The inheritance was not a thing. It was not buried anywhere, guarded by anything, hidden under any floor. It lived in the bloodline, sleeping, and it woke by degrees, and the moon's thirteenth turn — a turn of turns, a cycle the old wardens had tracked in star-charts — would wake it wholly in whoever carried it last. *Whoever holds it when the turn completes holds all of it,* Maren had written, *and everything the Salt has ever been will know that person's name.* And below that, in handwriting gone suddenly cramped, as if the pen had been gripped hard:

*If it is Edmund, tell him our mother's stories were true and I am sorry. If it is you, Odile, then listen: you were always the stronger holder, and I am sorry for that too. Either of you may burn this letter. Neither of you may burn the truth in it. It will find you anyway. It is already finding you. Look at your orchard and lie to yourself about drainage if it helps.*

Odile laughed. It came out cracked, half sob, and she let it, alone in the kitchen with the smoke.

*Look at your orchard.* As if Maren had watched her all these years from wherever stubborn grandmothers go. As if the whole performance — the dull routine, the boring explanations, the gift rationed out drop by drop like medicine — had been visible the entire time to the one person whose opinion mattered.

She folded the letter along its old creases and did not put it in the fire. Instead she went to the chest, lifted the false bottom, and slid it into the shallow space beneath, next to the roll of old coin stamped with a sigil she'd never let herself study closely — a circle broken by a line, the warden's mark — and closed the lid on both.

Then she stood in the middle of her kitchen and made a list, the practical way she made all lists.

One: the broadsheet said *sought*, and seeking meant sniffer-beasts, and sniffer-beasts meant the Hunt would reach this valley within weeks, maybe days. Two: forty letters' worth of soaked-in old magic had just gone up her chimney in a plume that any beast downwind might taste. Three: Edmund was somewhere east, alive as of four years ago, when a merchant passing through had mentioned a map-seller in Saltmere whose charms were unremarkable and whose hands were remarkable, and Odile had sent no word and received none, because that was the arrangement, that was the wall, that was the price.

Four: the moon was already climbing toward its thirteenth turn, whether she read charts or not. She could feel it the way she felt frost coming — not seeing it, just knowing, in the green-speech part of her that never slept. Something was waking. Something was starting to call.

She looked out the window at the orchard, gray and patient on its hill.

And there — she blinked, leaned closer, told herself it was light, told herself it was drainage — there at the end of the third row, in the frost, in the dead of the dormant season, the old Pippin she had touched that morning had put out a single blossom. White. Open. Facing the house.

Facing her.

Odile pulled her boots back on, took the letter out from under the false bottom, and tucked it inside her shirt, against her skin, where the fire couldn't reach it and the beasts would have to go through her first.

Then she went out to the barn to saddle the mule, because if the truth was going to find her anyway, she intended to be moving when it did — east, toward Saltmere, toward a brother who owed her twelve years of letters and was about to start paying.
