## 6. The Knock at Midnight

Edmund had always imagined discovery would come with trumpets.

That was the lie the old stories told — that ruin announced itself, that the gifted were taken in daylight, in front of crowds, with banners and proclamations and a name read aloud so the whole city could learn what happened to people who kept secrets. The stories wanted witnesses. The stories wanted meaning.

What came instead was a dog.

Or something shaped like one, if you squinted past the wrongness of it — too long in the body, too many joints in the legs, its skull smooth as river stone with no ears to speak of, only two shallow pits where ears should have been. It moved down Candlewick Row at midnight on soft feet that made no sound at all, and its nostrils — because it did have nostrils, wide and wet and working — flared at every doorway like a man reading letters addressed to someone else.

Edmund watched it from the loft window of their rooms above the stall, one candle pinched dead between finger and thumb, wax still warm against their skin.

They had smelled it first, actually, before they'd seen it. That was the thing nobody warned you about the Inquisition's beasts: you didn't see them coming, you *tasted* them coming. A copper tang at the back of the throat, like biting a coin, like blood you hadn't spilled yet. Iron filings and something older underneath, something that made the back of Edmund's teeth ache in a way they recognized from childhood, from the night the Purge came to their grandmother's house — the taste of a thing built to unmake what they were.

Twelve years, and the body remembered.

*Don't move,* Edmund told themselves, which was absurd, because they weren't moving. *Don't breathe. Don't think about the wards.*

The last instruction was the impossible one, because thinking about the wards was all Edmund could do. Three layers of them, woven into the doorframe and the stairwell and the floorboards of the stall below, each one small enough to pass for craftsmanship — a knot here, a carved sigil disguised as a maker's mark there, nothing that would hum or glow or do anything at all unless someone went looking with intent. Edmund had spent four years building them, one careful thread at a time, the way a miser stuffs coins into a wall. They were good wards. They were the best work Edmund had ever done, better than any map, because maps only had to survive being looked at, and these had to survive being *hunted*.

But the beast was going door to door, and it was not hurrying.

It stopped across the lane at Maren Ostrow's dye shop, and Edmund's stomach dropped through three floors of dark air, because Maren was not gifted — Maren was a widow with ink-stained hands who bought her mordants honestly and complained about the price of alum — but she had bought a charm from Edmund's stall eleven days ago. A little braided thing, blue thread over gray, sold as a *traveler's blessing*, which is what Edmund called them when they needed to sell magic to people who couldn't afford to know they were buying it. It did nothing grander than keep rain off a traveler's shoulders. It hummed, faintly, if you held it to your ear at night.

Maren's shutters were closed. Her shop was dark. The beast pressed its stone-smooth face to her door and breathed in, long and slow, the way Edmund had seen Wren Ashgrave study a map — not reading it so much as *listening* to it.

Then it moved on.

Edmund let out a breath they'd been holding since the solstice, it felt like, and hated themselves for the relief, because moving on meant the beast had found nothing at Maren's, and finding nothing at Maren's meant it was still working down the row, and the next door was Tam Beckett's chandlery, and after Tam's —

After Tam's was theirs.

* * *

They had known this night would come. That was the whole architecture of Edmund's life; everything else had been built on top of that single load-bearing certainty. Twelve years ago, at nineteen, they had run from a burning house with their sister's hand torn out of theirs, and they had landed in Saltmere and made themselves small and made themselves useful and made themselves, above all, *boring*. A cartographer of no particular renown. A stall-keeper of honest wares. A person whose name drew no attention because Edmund Villalobos was a name that had never existed anywhere before Saltmere — they'd built it themselves out of their mother's surname and a saint's name from a book of martyrs, and they wore it the way you wear a coat that doesn't quite fit: constantly aware of the seams.

And now the Gift-Hunt proclamation had gone up on every post in the city, and the criers had sung it in the market square, and the crowd had *cheered*, and Edmund had stood among the cheering with their hands folded and their face arranged into the mild, agreeable blankness of a man with nothing to hide, while inside them something twelve years old screamed and screamed and screamed.

So yes. They had known.

Knowing, it turned out, was worth exactly nothing. Knowing was a coin that purchased no passage. Edmund sat in the dark of their loft with their back against the wall beneath the window and listened to the beast taste Tam Beckett's doorway, and discovered that knowing the knock was coming did not prepare you for the sound of knuckles on wood.

Except there was no knock. That was the other thing the stories got wrong. There was no polite rap, no voice announcing *Inquisition, open in the crown's name.* There was simply the crack of Tam's lock giving way — a short, splintering sound, almost casual, the sound of a thing done ten thousand times before by men who had stopped counting — and then boots on stairs, and then light blooming yellow through Tam's shutter-slats, and then voices.

Tam's voice first. High, confused, protesting. *"I don't— I make candles, sirs, I make candles, there's nothing—"*

And then a voice Edmund had never heard before and knew immediately would live in their head forever. It was a woman's voice, low and even, with the particular flatness of someone reciting from a document rather than speaking to a person. *"Master Beckett. You are recorded as having purchased saltpeter in quantities inconsistent with your trade."*

*"For the wicks! For the— everyone uses it, ask anyone—"*

*"You are also recorded as having visited the market stall of one 'E. Villalobos, Cartographer' on no fewer than fourteen occasions in the past year."*

The silence that followed lasted perhaps three seconds. Edmund counted them the way you count the gap between lightning and thunder, except there was nowhere to be and nothing to measure and the strike was already inside the house.

*"Fourteen,"* Tam said, and his voice had changed — smaller now, and Edmund heard the exact moment he understood that his life had been arithmetic this whole time, that he had been summed up somewhere in a ledger without ever seeing the page. *"I buy maps. I'm a man who buys maps, that's all, I like to know the roads—"*

*"Bring him,"* said the even voice, and Edmund pressed both hands hard against their own mouth.

They heard Tam Beckett come down his own stairs. Not dragged — walking, stumbling, half-carried, the sounds blurring together the way sounds do when your body has decided that hearing is the only sense that matters anymore. They heard him say, once, clearly, in a tone of genuine bewilderment that was somehow worse than screaming: *"But I haven't done anything."*

And then they heard him say Edmund's name.

Not the way the Inquisitor had said it — clipped, administrative, a line item. He said it the way a customer says it. The way a neighbor says it. *"Villalobos!"* cracked up through the night air, ragged with terror, aimed at Edmund's dark windows like a thrown stone. *"Villalobos, tell them! Tell them what I bought! Tell them it was maps, it was only ever maps!"*

Edmund sat on the floor of their own room with their hands clamped over their mouth and did not answer, because answering was death, because answering was death *and* it wouldn't save Tam, because the ledger already had fourteen visits written in it and no charm sold as a traveler's blessing was going to explain away fourteen visits to a cartographer by a man who liked to know the roads.

The beast, standing in the lane below, turned its earless head toward Edmund's window.

Edmund felt it like a hand laid flat against their sternum. Not pain. *Attention.* The full, patient, bottomless attention of a thing designed for exactly one purpose, tasting the air above Tam's door, tasting the seam of the night, tasting — for one endless suspended moment — the three layers of wards woven through the doorframe downstairs, threads so fine they passed for carpentry, holding, holding, *holding*—

The beast exhaled. Moved on.

Down the row. Away. Its silent feet carrying it toward the harbor end of Candlewick Row, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the air was thick with tar and fish and a thousand honest stinks that must have been agony for a nose like that, a fog it would drown in, buying Edmund minutes, maybe an hour, maybe until dawn.

They stayed on the floor until the grey came up. Not hiding. Hiding required a plan for afterward, and Edmund had none. They just sat with their knees drawn up and listened to the absence of Tam Beckett's voice, which was very loud, and thought about fourteen visits, and thought about Maren's blue-and-gray braid humming softly in a drawer somewhere, and thought about how the beast had paused at their door for the length of three heartbeats and then walked on, and how *walked on* was not the same as *was fooled*, and how somewhere in the Inquisition's records there was now a line item that read *E. Villalobos, Cartographer*, and how lines items had a way of becoming appointments.

* * *

Dawn did not bring safety. Dawn brought the neighbors.

That was the cruelty of it — that Edmund's punishment for surviving was to stand in the lane at first light among the people whose street this was, watching the Inquisition's wagon roll away empty and Tam's door hanging open on its broken lock, and to be looked at.

Not accused. Nothing so clean. Just *looked at*. Old Sefa from the pie stall, arms crossed. The Ferro brothers, who bought their survey maps from Edmund twice a year and had always nodded to them in the lane. Young Pell, the ostler's boy, who had once spent an entire afternoon at Edmund's stall asking questions about the coast roads because he dreamed of the sea. All of them standing in the grey morning with their breath steaming, looking at Edmund with the particular expression of people doing arithmetic of their own.

*"He said your name,"* Sefa said at last. She was seventy if she was a day and had buried two husbands and feared nothing on earth except, apparently, nothing at all. *"Last night. Out loud. In front of soldiers. Why would a man do that, Edmund?"*

*"Because he was frightened,"* Edmund said. Their voice came out level. They were almost proud of it. *"Frightened people call for whoever comes to mind."*

*"You sold him something."* Not a question. Pell said it, the boy, staring at his own boots. *"Everyone knows you sold him something. Everyone buys something off you. That's not a crime."* He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and sixteen years old. *"Is it? Is it a crime now?"*

*"Buying maps is not a crime,"* Edmund said, and heard, distantly, with the detachment of a soul already halfway out of its body, how perfectly rehearsed the sentence sounded. How much it sounded like a sentence a guilty man keeps in his pocket.

Sefa held their gaze for a long moment. Whatever she saw there, she apparently decided it wasn't worth the price of saying aloud. She spat into the gutter — an old woman's commentary on crowns, inquisitors, and mornings generally — and shuffled back toward her stall, and one by one the others drifted after her, and the lane emptied, and Edmund was left alone with a broken lock across the way and the knowledge that the arithmetic had already started, and that Edmund Villalobos was a number in it.

They went inside and packed.

Not frantically. Frantic was a signature; frantic got people noticed. They packed the way they did everything now, slowly, in the order a careful man would pack if he were merely traveling to see a client — spare shirts, instruments, the false-bottomed case that had waited under the floorboards for twelve years like a loaded weapon. Into it went the charts that could not be allowed to burn in any fire the crown might set: the Veilroads, the crossings, the paths between realms that existed only in rumor and in Edmund's ink. And beneath those, wrapped in oilcloth, the stolen map. The one Wren Ashgrave had paid for in old coin stamped with a warden's sigil. The one that showed where the Salt slept.

Edmund had told themselves, for six days, that they had time. That the Hunt was theater, that registrations took weeks, that the machinery of the crown ground slow. Six days of telling themselves that, and the beast had come on the seventh night, and it had paused at their door.

Time, it turned out, had been spent while Edmund wasn't watching.

They were buckling the case when they saw it, on the desk by the window: the day's chart, half-finished, the coastline of some rumored northern reach taking shape under their pen. Edmund's gift lived in that work — the true gift, the one that let them see the shapes of places that didn't exist yet, that let their hand find coastlines no ship had ever traced. Twelve years of hiding it and still they worked every night, because a gift suppressed wasn't a gift destroyed; it just went underground, like water, and came up somewhere you didn't expect.

They picked up the chart. Held it. Watched, helplessly, as their thumb found the unfinished stretch of coast and *knew* it — knew the sound the sea would make against cliffs that weren't drawn yet, knew the smell of the pines that would grow there.

Somewhere out there, a woman with an even voice was reading names off a ledger.

Edmund put the chart in the case with the rest.

At the door, they stopped. Below, through the floorboards, the stall waited with its neat rows of harmless charms and honest maps, and Edmund understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that they were never going to stand behind that counter again. Twelve years of small lies, and the lies had finally added up to a sum, and the sum had a name, and the name had been shouted into the night by a candle-maker who had never done anything to anyone.

*"Tell them it was maps,"* Tam had said. *"It was only ever maps."*

"I know," Edmund said aloud, to the empty room, to the broken lock across the lane, to the man in a wagon rolling east toward whatever place the crown kept people who hadn't done anything. "I know it was."

Then they shouldered the case, blew out the lamp they had never lit, and slipped out the back stair into the waking noise of the market district — a man named after a martyr, carrying a kingdom's worth of rumor on his back, walking quickly without running, the way fugitives have walked since the first city learned to fear what its children could do.

Behind them, at the mouth of the lane, a courier in a gray cloak watched them go, sharp-eyed, unhurried, and smiled to herself like a woman whose letter had finally arrived.
