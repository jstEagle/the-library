## 7. Seraphine Dax

The iron filings went in first. They always did.

Seraphine Dax sat on the narrow cot in her quarters above the Inquisition garrison and poured the morning dose from a stoppered vial into her left palm. Gray grit, fine as sand, cold as riverbed stone. She held it there while she counted her breathing — four in, seven hold, four out — the way the handlers had taught her at eleven years old, when the shaking started and wouldn't stop and Mother had wept in the doorway of a room that smelled of lye and burned lavender.

*Control is redemption.* Chancellor Vess had said it to her once, early, when she was still new enough to believe that men in high collars said things because they were true. *Your gift is not a curse, Dax. It is a debt. And debts are paid through discipline.*

She tipped the filings onto her tongue.

The taste was always the worst part — like licking a nail, like blood that belonged to someone else — and then the numbness came, spreading down her throat and out along her arms, and the thing inside her that had been straining all night toward every sleeping mind in the garrison went quiet. Not silent. Never silent. But quiet, the way a dog is quiet behind a door it has learned not to scratch.

She swallowed, rinsed her mouth from the basin, and looked at herself in the small mirror above it.

A woman of thirty-one looked back. Brown hair scraped tight enough to hurt. A face people described as handsome when they wanted to flatter and severe when they didn't. Eyes the color of wet slate, which the other hunters joked were fitting, because nothing got past them and nothing warmed them either.

The joke was closer to the truth than any of them knew. That was the design of it. Vess had built her this way, filing by filing, the way a smith builds a blade — and a blade that forgot what it was for got melted down. She had seen it happen. Hunter Corvane, who'd hesitated outside a weaver's loft in Marrowgate three winters ago because the gifted woman inside had been singing to her baby. Corvane had stood in the rain with his hand on his sword and his gift straining toward that lullaby like a man leaning over a well, and he had let them walk out the back, and six weeks later Corvane had been reassigned to a border post where the fever ran, and no one had written to Seraphine about it because there had been no one left to write to.

*Control is redemption,* she told the mirror. The mirror, being a mirror, said nothing.

She dressed. Buckled the gray coat. Pinned the silver eye of the Inquisition at her collar, and felt, as she did every morning, the small dishonesty of it — the way a wolf might wear a shepherd's crook if a shepherd's crook were the price of not being shot.

Down in the yard, the sniffer-beast was already awake.

They kept it in an iron-barred pen near the kennels, though *kept* was a generous word. It was the size of a yearling calf, hairless, gray-pink, its skull too large for its body and its nostrils — it had six of them, arranged in two rows like the suckers on some deep-sea horror — flaring and closing in slow rhythm even in sleep. It had no name. Handlers who named the beasts ended up attached to them, and attached handlers made mistakes, and mistakes around the beast were measured in neighborhoods.

It ate magic. Not consumed it, exactly — *tasted* it, tracked it, and where it tracked long enough, the crown's men followed. It could smell a charm worked three days ago. It could smell a gift suppressed so poorly that it leaked, the way a bad roof leaks, one drop at a time into the joists of an ordinary life.

Sergeant Halloway was mucking the pen when Seraphine arrived, and he straightened fast, slopping the bucket.

"Inquisitor."

"Has it fed?"

"This morning, ma'am. Standard ration." He hesitated. "It's been restless since the raid. Since we brought the prisoner in."

"Restless how?"

Halloway wiped his hands on his apron, clearly wishing he hadn't been asked. "Pacing. Pressing at the bars toward the holding cells. Brannick says it's never done that for a low-talent before. Usually they're barely a smell to it." He lowered his voice, though the beast was ten feet away and deaf as far as anyone could tell. "Ma'am, it's acting like there's something *big* in the cells."

"There's a candle-dipper in the cells," Seraphine said. "A woman of sixty who charmed wicks so they burned slower. That's the whole of the crime."

"Yes, ma'am."

But she felt it too, standing there. Or rather, she felt the absence of feeling — the filings doing their work, pressing down on something in her chest that had leaned, very slightly, toward the holding cells the moment she'd come into the yard. Like a compass needle nudged by iron in a wall.

She turned away from the pen and went to see her prisoner.

* * *

The holding cells beneath the garrison were cut from the same salt-gray stone as everything else in Saltmere, and they smelled of damp and old fear, which never entirely washed out. Seraphine had walked these corridors for nine years. She knew which cell doors had been scratched by fingernails and which had been kicked, and she knew the difference between the crying of someone guilty and the crying of someone simply terrified, and she hated that she knew, and she did the work anyway.

Marta Quill sat on the bench in cell four with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a sermon to end.

She was sixty, broad-hipped, with flour still in the creases of her knuckles — they hadn't let her wash — and she had the particular stillness of someone who had decided, sometime in the last twelve hours, that terror was a currency she refused to keep spending. Her eyes were red but dry. When Seraphine unlocked the door and came in alone, as she always insisted on doing, the woman looked up without flinching.

"You're the one they call the hound," Marta Quill said.

"I'm the one they call Inquisitor Dax."

"The hound," the woman repeated, gently, as if correcting a child. "That's all right. I had a dog once. Best creature I ever knew. You do what you're made to do, and you can't be blamed for the hand that feeds you." She patted the bench beside her. "Sit, if you like. My legs are older than yours and I've been sitting all night, so I know it's no kindness."

Seraphine did not sit. She took out her notebook instead, and the little tin of iron filings she carried for close work, and set both on the bench between them like a boundary.

"Tell me about the stall," she said. "The one you named."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes — not guilt exactly. Grief wearing guilt's coat.

"I told the arresting officer everything."

"You told him a name. Names are cheap. Tell me what the name means."

Marta Quill was quiet a moment. Out in the corridor, boots passed and faded. Somewhere above them, the garrison bell rang the third hour, flat and toneless.

"It's just a stall," she said finally. "In the Tallowmarket. Charms, trinkets, love-lotteries, the usual harmless rubbish. The kind of thing that isn't even illegal anymore because it doesn't *do* anything." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, patient and sad. "Except that some of it does. You know that. You could smell it on my hands last night, couldn't you? Your beast could. It pressed its poor nose against the wagon bars the whole ride here, and I thought: that poor animal. Made to want a thing it can never have, and punished for wanting it."

Seraphine's hand, resting on the notebook, had gone very still.

"Who taught you the craft?" she asked, and heard her own voice come out level, cold, professional — and underneath it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, something else. Something that had leaned toward this woman in the yard and was leaning now.

"My grandmother. As her mother taught her. We dipped candles in the Hollowbrook district for four generations." Marta Quill unfolded her hands, studied them. "Slow-burning wicks. Nothing that would frighten a magistrate. A candle that should last a night lasting three. Who does that hurt, Inquisitor? Tell me who lies awake harmed by a candle that burns slow."

"No one," Seraphine said. "That's not the question the law asks."

"No," the woman agreed. "The law asks whether you have it. Not what you do with it. There's a difference between those questions, and everyone in this building knows it, and the difference has a body count." She looked up then, directly, and her eyes were dry and terribly clear. "You have it too."

The words landed in the cell like a dropped glass.

"I don't know what you—"

"Oh, hush." No anger in it. Almost tender. "I've been dipping charms since before you were born, girl. You think I can't feel it? You sit there wrapped in iron like a corpse in a shroud, and you think the shroud means there's no body?" She shook her head slowly. "I felt you the moment you came down the stairs. Felt you *straining*. Whatever they've got you swallowing, it's not working as well as you tell yourself it is. And you hate it. You hate that part of you the way I'd hate my own hands if they'd done what yours have done."

Seraphine picked up the notebook. Her handwriting, when it came, was steady. She made herself look at the page, not the woman.

"Name of the stall."

"You'll get it out of me either way. I'm old and tired and I won't give you the pleasure of a struggle." Marta Quill recited it like a litany: "The Quiet Compass. Tallowmarket row, third arch from the fountain, blue awning. The cartographer keeps it. Sells maps and charms and never once, in twelve years, gave me cause to fear them."

"Twelve years."

"Twelve years or more. I bought a luck-charm off them the first winter after I lost my husband. Paid too much for it, because I was grieving and grief makes fools of us. And the charm *worked*, Inquisitor. Not grandly. Not the way the stories say. Just — the winter was easier than it had any right to be. And I went back every year after, and every year I thought: here is a person more frightened than I am, spending their whole life pretending to be less than they are, so that people like me can buy a little warmth and nobody gets dragged anywhere." Her voice roughened, just slightly. "Until last night. When somebody got dragged somewhere, and screamed a stall's name into the dark, because that's what terror does. It spends other people's secrets."

Seraphine wrote: *The Quiet Compass. Proprietor unknown. Twelve-plus years operating.*

Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady. Inside, behind the iron, something pressed against the numbness like a face against frosted glass, and she did not let herself wonder whose face.

"Why did you keep going back," she heard herself ask, "knowing what it was? Knowing the risk?"

Marta Quill considered her for a long moment.

"Because of the way you just asked that," she said. "Because you wanted to know for yourself, not for the file." She leaned back against the stone. "Go on, then. Write your report. Send your men to the blue awning. And when you find them — and you will, you're the best they have, God help us all — ask yourself, girl, whether you're hunting a criminal or hunting the only other person in this city who knows what the inside of your head sounds like at three in the morning."

Seraphine closed the notebook.

She stood, and unlocked the door, and paused with her hand on the iron.

"The candle-wicks," she said, without turning. "How much longer did they burn?"

"Three times as long," the woman said softly. "Every time. Three times, exact."

*Three times, exact.* Precision like that wasn't luck. It was craft, honed across generations, careful as needlework. Careful as a map drawn by someone who could not afford a single wrong line.

"Thank you for your cooperation," Seraphine said, and heard how the sentence sounded, and left anyway.

* * *

The Tallowmarket at midday was all noise and steam — pie carts, fishmongers, a knife-grinder's wheel shrieking — and Seraphine walked it in plain clothes, coatless, hair loose, because a hunter in silver-eyed gray cleared streets like fire clears grass, and she needed the market to forget she was in it.

Third arch from the fountain. Blue awning, sun-faded to the color of a shallow sky.

The stall was shuttered.

Not abandoned — abandoned stalls gather dust and cobwebs and the slow collapse of things left behind. This one was *closed*: shutters snug, counter wiped, a chalkboard turned face-in against the wall. Someone had left in order, quickly, deliberately, the way soldiers break camp. She put her palm flat against the shutter wood and felt nothing, because the filings allowed her to feel almost nothing, and yet under the numbness, faint as heat through a wall, there was — something. Residue. Charm-work laid down over years, soaked into the grain of the counter like tallow into skin.

The sniffer-beast would lose its mind over this stall. She found she was glad she hadn't brought it.

"Closed since yesterday," said a voice at her elbow. A pie-seller, floury, wiping her hands. "Early. Middle of the afternoon, right after the criers. Never done that before. Twelve years I've traded next to them and never once seen them panic." She clucked her tongue. "Shame, that. Good trader. Honest prices. Sold my boy a chart of the coast roads when he took the fishing boats — he swears by it."

"Did you know them well?"

"Well enough to buy, not well enough to ask questions. That's the etiquette of the Tallowmarket." The pie-seller's eyes narrowed, just slightly, the wariness of a woman deciding how much a stranger might be planning to cost her. "You're not from the garrison, are you?"

"No."

"Hm." The pie-seller looked at the shuttered stall a moment longer. "Well. Whoever they were, I hope they run far. That's all I'll say, and I'll deny saying it." She went back to her cart.

Seraphine stood before the blue awning a while longer.

Then, because procedure demanded it and because she was, whatever else she was, thorough, she circled the stall to the alley behind it. Refuse bins. A drain. A back wall scarred with old posters for vanished fairs. And there, half-scuffed by cart wheels, pressed into the mud beside the rear door:

Boot prints. Two sets. One worn at the outer heel, deep-toed — a walker, someone who covered ground. The other lighter, quicker, overlapping, as if its owner had paced this alley many times, checking sightlines, counting exits.

And beside the door itself, at knee height, a mark. Small. Scratched into the wood with a pin or stylus, easy to take for a knot in the grain unless you knew to look.

Seraphine knew to look.

She had grown up in a house where her grandmother scratched such marks into doorframes — not this one, an older one, a spiral-within-a-circle that the family called the traveler's rest and that meant, in the private language of people who had once had gifts and lost them to the Purge, *safe here; nothing follows*. This mark was different. Angular. A compass rose collapsed into four strokes, north missing.

A warding sign. A professional's warding sign, renewed recently — the scratches still bright at the edges.

Someone had lived behind this stall in a state of permanent siege, guarding a door with symbols, pacing alleys, counting exits, for twelve years. And yesterday, when the criers read the Gift-Hunt proclamation, that someone had broken camp in an afternoon and vanished so cleanly that even the residue under Seraphine's palm felt like memory rather than presence.

She crouched and copied the mark into her notebook, stroke by stroke. Then the boot prints. Then she sat back on her heels in the dirty alley, behind a shuttered stall, in a city that cheered its own proclamations, and let herself think the thought she had been not-thinking since the holding cell.

*Marta Quill is in a cell below the garrison. She will be processed within the week. Registered, or taken. There is no third door, because I have spent nine years making sure there is no third door.*

*I am good at this. I am the best they have. And the reason I am the best they have is that I know exactly what I am hunting, because I swallow it every morning and press it down all day and hear it at three in the morning, scratching at the door.*

*Somewhere ahead of me is a person who has spent twelve years being careful in exactly the way I am careful. And the crown has sent me to bring them in. And when I bring them in, I will be doing to them precisely what was done to me — except that no one ever put me in a wagon, because I agreed to the leash. I hold it against my own throat and call it redemption.*

The filings numbed everything, but they had never once numbed that.

She stood. Brushed the mud from her knees. Closed the notebook, and in the act of closing it, made the decision she would spend the rest of this hunt neither confirming nor denying to herself: that she would find the proprietor of the Quiet Compass before the beast did. Alone. Because a hunt conducted by the sniffer-beast ended in a cage, and a hunt conducted by Seraphine Dax ended — for now, for reasons she declined to examine — in conversation.

Behind her, across the market, the garrison bell began to ring again — not the hour. The summons. Three strokes, pause, three strokes: *hunter to the Chancellor.*

Vess wanted a report.

Seraphine buttoned her plain coat to the throat, felt the silver eye in her pocket like a stone, and started walking — away from the blue awning, toward the Spire, toward the man who had built her filing by filing, carrying in her notebook a stolen compass-rose and in her chest, behind the iron, something that had begun, very quietly, to lean the other way.
