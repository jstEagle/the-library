## 8. Flight Across the Salt Flats

The salt flats began where the road gave up pretending.

Edmund had seen them once before, from a distance, as a child — a white so bright it looked less like ground than like the sky had fallen and refused to get up. Grandmother had covered their eyes and turned the cart around. *Nothing lives out there*, she'd said. *Nothing should.*

Now there was nothing behind them but Saltmere's smoke, and nothing ahead but the white.

"Keep your hood up," Wren said. She walked half a step ahead, which was her way of leading without admitting she'd agreed to lead. "The glare will cook your eyes by noon if you let it."

Edmund pulled the hood lower. The world shrank to a gray tunnel of wool and their own breathing. Behind that breathing, fainter than memory, they could still hear the market district screaming.

They had left at midnight, after the knock. After Marta Hale — who bought luck-charms for her goats and never once asked what was inside them — had been dragged past Edmund's shuttered stall with two inquisitors on each arm, shouting *Villalobos* into the dark like a curse she'd finally learned to pronounce. Edmund had stood in the back room with a pack half-packed and a knife in their hand and done nothing. There had been nothing to do. That was the whole architecture of twelve careful years: you built a life so small and so quiet that when the knock came, there was nothing to do but run through the hole you'd kept open in the wall.

Wren had been waiting in that hole. That was the part Edmund still hadn't decided how to feel about.

"You knew," Edmund said. The flats ate sound; even speaking felt like shouting. "Last night. When you paid me in warden coin. You knew the raid was coming."

"I knew the wind was changing." Wren didn't turn. "Knowing which way it blows isn't the same as starting it."

"It's close enough."

"Then arrest me." She gestured at the endless white. "Plenty of room for a trial out here."

Edmund said nothing. The truth was uglier: they needed her. Wren Ashgrave knew the flats the way Edmund knew the Veilroads — not from maps but from feet, from years of walking them for whoever paid. And right now the only person paying Edmund anything was the person keeping them alive.

The sun climbed. The white got worse. It wasn't light exactly; it was a pressure, pressing up through the eye sockets, making the skull hum. By midmorning Edmund had stopped seeing the ground as ground. It was a mirror laid flat under heaven, and they were walking across the face of it, leaving prints that would last until the next wind — hours, maybe. The most temporary home they'd ever made.

"We're being followed," Wren said.

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've been walking like a man at his own funeral. Look with your other eyes, cartographer. Tell me what you see."

Edmund hesitated. Then, hating how easily the old habit returned, they let their gift unfurl — just a thread of it, the way you'd crack a window in winter. The world shifted. Beneath the blinding surface, the flats were laced with old roads: trade paths, pilgrim ways, the ghost-tracks of armies, all of them faded almost to nothing, all of them readable. Magic ran along roads the way water ran downhill. It always had. And roads remembered what crossed them.

And crossing those ghost-roads now, three miles back and closing, came six bright smears of hunger.

"Hounds," Edmund said. Their mouth had gone dry. "Six. They're not tracking our scent. They're tracking—" They stopped.

"The charm in your pocket," Wren finished. "The one you've been too proud to throw away since the stall."

It was true. Edmund's hand had been resting on it for an hour without their noticing: a wayfinding charm, no bigger than a walnut shell, stitched from grandmother's patterns. It sat against their palm like a small warm heart. Every ward they'd woven into their stall was dead or dying back in Saltmere. This was the last working magic on their body, and the hounds could taste it the way sharks taste blood through miles of water.

"Throw it," Wren said. "Bury it. Give it to the salt. Whatever you do, stop carrying it like a candle in a cellar full of oil."

"If I drop it, we're blind. No roads, no landmarks, no idea where the safe crossings are—"

"You have *me*. I'm the map now. That was the arrangement." Wren finally turned, and even through the glare Edmund could see how tired she was, how much this cost her, whatever *this* was. "You keep treating your gift like a limb you can't afford to lose. It's not a limb. It's bait."

Edmund looked down at the charm. Warm. Patient. Twelve years of abstinence, and it still fit their hand like it had been poured into the mold of their palm.

They put it away inside three layers of cloth and did not throw it away. Wren watched them do it and said nothing more, but something in her expression filed the moment away, the way a courier files an address.

* * *

They walked until the sun stood straight overhead and the flats offered no shadow anywhere, not even their own — shadows drowned in glare, people reduced to walking silhouettes with the light eating their edges. Wren called a halt in the lee of a salt ridge, a low spine of crusted earth that broke the horizon like the keel of a buried ship.

"Water," she said, and passed the skin. "Small sips. You'll want to drink it all and then you'll want to die, so: small sips."

Edmund drank. Their lips had split somewhere in the last hour; the water found every crack and reported it. "How far to the crossing?"

"The Veilroad gate?" Wren squinted east, where the white blurred into a shimmer that might have been distance or might have been the end of the world. "Two days, if the flats hold. One day, if we cut through the Hollows."

"What are the Hollows?"

"That." Wren pointed south, where the crust of the flats gave way to a region of pits and broken ground, pale holes punched in pale ground like the scars of some enormous pox. "Sinkholes. The salt crust over old brine channels. Walk wrong and the ground opens and swallows you, and nobody hears the splash because there isn't one. Brine below. You'd go down like a stone in milk."

"So we go around."

"Around adds a day. A day is exactly how long those hounds need." Wren settled back against the ridge and closed her eyes, apparently able to sleep anywhere, which Edmund decided was either a skill or a symptom. "Your choice, cartographer. I get paid either way."

"Do you?" Edmund said. "Get paid, I mean. By whoever sent you. Do they know where we're going?"

One eye opened. "Careful."

"I'm being careful. I'm asking carefully." Edmund wrapped their arms around their knees. The warden sigil on the old coins — a circle broken by a single line, the mark of the line that kept the Salt — burned in their memory. Nobody minted warden coin anymore. Nobody had for sixty years. Which meant Wren hadn't been paid in it; Wren had been *given* it, recently, by someone who owned it, someone who knew what it meant, someone who wanted Edmund to see it and react.

Someone who was fishing.

"You paid me in sigils I haven't seen since my grandmother died," Edmund said quietly. "Either you don't know what they are, or you know exactly, and you were watching my face when I touched them."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. The flats ticked and creaked around them, salt expanding in the heat with sounds like distant knuckles cracking.

"You lie badly," she said at last. "Did you know that? Twelve years selling charms to gullible farmers and you never learned to do it well. It's almost admirable." She sat up. "I know what the sigils are. I know what they mean. And yes, I was watching your face. My employers watch everyone's face. It's how they got rich."

"And who are your employers?"

"When it matters, I'll tell you. Not before." She lay back down. "Sleep. We cut through the Hollows at dusk, while the crust is cool. And Edmund — if the ground opens, don't reach for the charm. Reach for me. I'm faster than magic."

"That's not true."

"No," Wren agreed, closing her eyes. "But it's kinder than the truth, and you look like you could use kindness."

* * *

They entered the Hollows as the light went long and gold, and the flats stopped being a mirror and became a wound.

The pits opened around them like mouths. Some were shallow dishes of glittering brine; others dropped away into darkness that the low sun couldn't reach, cold air rising from them smelling of minerals and deep time. Wren moved through it all with an ease that seemed less like knowledge than like instinct, reading the crust the way Edmund read roads — a discoloration here, a hollow sound underfoot there, a place where the salt grew in the wrong pattern.

"Step where I step," she said. "Exactly where I step. The crust lies. It looks strongest over the emptiest places. It likes to show off."

Edmund followed. Behind them, far back at the mouth of the Hollows, the light was failing — and in the failing light, Edmund risked the window-crack again, just a sliver of gift, just enough to check the road.

The hounds had entered the Hollows too.

Closer now. Two miles. The Hollows slowed them — even things that hunted magic had to mind the ground — but they didn't stop, and they didn't lose the scent, because Edmund was still carrying the charm, triple-wrapped, pressed against their ribs like a second heartbeat.

*Throw it away,* said the sensible voice, the voice of twelve years' discipline.

*I might need it,* said the other voice, older, hungrier, the one that sounded like grandmother's letters felt.

The dusk deepened. The pits filled with blue shadow. And then the ground under Edmund's left foot made a sound like a held breath being released.

There was no dramatic collapse. That was the horror of it. One moment the crust was holding; the next it simply wasn't, a circle of white tilting away beneath them like a trapdoor operated by patient hands, and Edmund dropped into cold and dark and the smell of ancient brine, arms pinwheeling, catching the lip of the hole with both hands as the rest of them swung free over a void that sloshed and echoed below.

"EDMUND." Wren's face appeared above, backlit by the last violet light, distorted by urgency. Her hand shot down. "Take it. Take my hand. Don't look down—"

Edmund took it. Wren was strong, stronger than her frame suggested, and for one glorious second the gap between them closed — Edmund's chin scraped the crust, their knee found a ledge—

And the ledge crumbled.

The fall was short and total. Edmund hit brine chest-deep in absolute blackness, the shock of it driving the air from their lungs, the cold closing over their shoulders like a door. The liquid was dense, buoyant, wrong — thick as oil, bitter as regret — and when they thrashed they sank slower than they should have, which somehow made it worse, made it dreamlike, made it a thing happening to someone else.

Above, a circle of fading sky. Wren's voice, thin and furious: *"Hold on. Hold on, I'm finding a rope, hold ON—"*

The walls of the pit rose sheer and slick. There was no climbing. There was no ledge. There was a circle of sky shrinking as the dusk became night, and there was the weight of the pack dragging Edmund down, and there was — against their ribs, through three layers of cloth, warm as a sleeping bird —

the charm.

*Don't,* said the sensible voice.

*You are going to die in a hole in the ground because of a rule you made to survive a war that ended,* said the other voice. *Twelve years. Twelve years of being small. Is this what smallness buys?*

Edmund's fingers found the charm through the wet cloth.

It would be so easy to tell themselves later that they'd had no choice. They would try to tell themselves that, in fact, many times. But in the moment, in the cold and the dark, with Wren's rope — a coil of cord, badly thrown, landing a body-length away — with death rising slow and salty around them, Edmund made a choice, and knew it was a choice, and made it anyway.

They cracked the charm open.

Light didn't pour out. It *rose* — out of the broken shell, out of Edmund's hands, out of somewhere deeper than their hands, a soft gold radiance that filled the pit like water fills a cup. And with the light came everything else. Twelve years of dammed river breaking its banks in a single instant: Edmund's gift woke all the way down, and the world *answered*.

They could see the pit's true shape — not a hole but a throat, an old brine channel running forty feet to a junction where three such throats met, and beyond that, faint as breath on glass, a road. An actual road, deep and old, running under the flats, a warden-way worn smooth by feet a hundred years dead. And along that road, coming fast, six smears of hunger pulling up short at the edge of the sudden light, whining, circling, afraid.

Magic feared magic. Even hungry things feared a lit hearth.

Edmund reached — not with hands, with will — and the brine itself responded, thickening, lifting, bearing them upward like a palm. They rose through the gold-lit dark, weightless, *held*, and for four or five seconds that lasted a season, Edmund Villalobos flew.

It was exhilaration beyond anything they had words for. It was the thing they had been amputated from at nineteen, reattached in a moment, nerve and fire and joy, the world suddenly enormous and legible and *theirs* — every channel, every current, every hidden road singing its name. This was what they'd been. This was what they'd given up. This was what the crown wanted to burn out of every living soul, and God forgive them, Edmund understood the burning now, understood why kingdoms trembled at it, because it felt like this, it felt like being the only awake person in a sleeping world—

Their hands cleared the rim. Wren seized their collar and hauled, and Edmund came out of the pit onto solid crust, gasping, soaked in brine, glowing faintly at the edges like something freshly forged.

The light died. The charm was ash in their palm. But the gift stayed awake, roaring, and Edmund knelt on the salt shaking with the aftershocks of it, laughing or sobbing or both.

Wren crouched in front of them. In the starlight her face was unreadable, which was its own kind of read.

"How long," she said quietly, "have you been able to do that?"

"Twelve years ago," Edmund managed. "Until about ninety seconds ago."

"And the hounds?"

"Gone to ground. The light spooked them. They'll circle and come back by morning, and now—" Edmund swallowed. Now it was worse. Now Edmund wasn't carrying a candle; Edmund *was* one. A lit beacon walking across the darkest country in the world, visible, he supposed, from the Inquisition's own towers. "Now they can smell me without the charm. Now I'm the loudest thing on these flats."

Wren was silent a moment. Then she did something Edmund didn't expect: she pulled off her own cloak and put it around their shoulders, brine-soaked as they were.

"Then we walk all night," she said, "and we pray the Hollows hide what the light showed. And Edmund—" she paused, and for once there was no irony anywhere in her, "—when we get wherever it is we're going, and whatever my employers want from you comes due — remember that I pulled you out of the hole. Remember whose hand that was."

Edmund looked at her. Under the exhaustion and the glare-burn and the professional blankness, Wren Ashgrave looked afraid — not of the hounds, not of the flats. Of something ahead.

"I will," Edmund said. And meant it, which frightened them more than the pit had.

They walked east through the whole of that night, two figures on a white immensity under a fat moon already swelling toward its thirteenth turn. And near dawn, cresting a salt ridge, Edmund — wide-eyed, unable to stop looking at the world with the window cracked — saw something that stopped them mid-stride.

Far to the southeast, where the flats met the orchard country, a line of green was moving.

Not marching. *Blooming.* A ribbon of blossom and new leaf advancing across dead land, unhurried, inevitable, following a path no map had ever shown — a path, Edmund realized with a jolt that dropped through them like the pit had, that only a warden could walk.

Only a warden. Walking warden-ways. Toward them.

"Wren," Edmund said. "Who else knows these roads?"

She followed their gaze. The green line caught the first sunlight and burned like a fuse.

"No one," she said slowly. "That's rather the point of them."

Edmund stood on the ridge with the gift still singing in their blood and their sister's flowering footprints drawing a line straight toward their hiding place, and understood that the flats had not been the trap.

The trap was family.
