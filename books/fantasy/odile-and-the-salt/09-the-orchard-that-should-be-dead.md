## 9. The Orchard That Should Be Dead

The orchard had been dead for eleven years, and Odile Villalobos had spent every one of them lying to it.

She walked the rows at dawn the way she always did, before the light got honest, her boots crunching over soil that should have been ash and wasn't. The trees stood in their long ranks like soldiers dismissed from a war no one had won — black-limbed, leafless, their bark split by frost and by the fire that had come through in the winter of the Purge, when the Inquisition's riders had burned everything on the Villalobos land that could be made to burn.

Except the trees hadn't died. That was the thing no one could explain, the thing Odile had spent a decade making sure no one tried to.

Where she walked, they bloomed.

Not all at once. Not showily. A single white flower here, opening out of season on a charred branch. A bud there, tight as a secret. By midmorning the blossoms would be gone — she'd learned to time her rounds so the evidence wilted behind her like a tide going out — but for an hour each day the orchard was impossibly, illegally alive, and it was alive because of her, and if anyone ever proved it, she would burn the way the trees had almost burned, the way her grandmother had burned, the way her brother had run rather than find out.

Odile crouched at the base of the oldest tree, the one Grandmother Sable had called the Mother, and pressed her palm flat against the root-flare. Under the bark, faint as a pulse taken through wool, something answered. Warmth. Recognition. The slow green attention of a living thing that had been waiting a long time to be touched honestly.

"I know," she murmured. "I know. Another day of pretending."

She straightened, brushed the dirt from her knees, and stopped.

Because the pretense had already been broken. Not by her.

There were tracks along the eastern fence line — she'd have missed them if the frost hadn't come in the night, laying its thin white paper across the ground for anything passing to sign. Boot prints. Two sets, one heavy and one lighter, coming off the moor where no road ran and no sensible person would walk, cutting straight through the bracken as though the bracken had been asked politely to move aside.

And that was wrong. That was wrong in a way that made the back of Odile's neck prickle, because the bracken *had* moved aside. She followed the prints with her eyes and saw it plainly: the ferns lay flattened outward in a corridor six feet wide, not trampled but *parted*, bent back at the stems as if a hand had combed through them, and the frost inside that corridor was unbroken except by the boots themselves.

Everyone else who crossed her land broke the frost. These prints sat inside a path that had been opened for them.

A warden-way.

The knowledge dropped into her stomach like a stone into a well, down and down, past eleven years of careful silence, past the ashes of the Purge, all the way to the bottom where her grandmother's voice still lived: *The ways answer blood, child. No one else can ask them to open.*

Someone had walked a warden-way across her land this morning. Someone whose feet fit boots, whose blood fit paths.

Odile stood very still among her dead-alive trees and let herself feel, for exactly three breaths, the wild and terrible hope of it.

Then she thought: *trap.*

* * *

She worked fast, because whoever it was had hours on her and the warden-ways didn't stay open for stragglers. But she also worked well, because this was the one inheritance she'd never let rust: the craft of hiding.

Her grandmother had taught both children the same lessons, back when the world still pretended magic was only rumor. How to read a hedge for what passed through it. How to set a snare that caught legs instead of rabbits. How to make a house look abandoned and a field look barren and a gift look like luck. Odile had kept the last lesson sharp for eleven years — she'd built a whole life on it, a whole self, the quiet woman at the edge of Fenlow village who was uncannily lucky with her garden, whose hens laid through winter, whose cider never soured, whose orchard everyone said must sit over some warm spring because how else did you explain the blossoms in a cold snap?

Luck. She had made her birthright look like luck. It was the loneliest work she'd ever done.

Now she turned it around. Now she made her luck look like a trap.

The first snare went at the gap in the drystone wall where the warden-path met the pasture — a leg-line of braided hemp, low, invisible under the frost-stiff grass, anchored to a stone that would take a running man off his feet and hold him thrashing until she came. The second was simpler and crueler: the bee-skeps. Her bees were ordinary bees, mostly, but they'd lived beside her gift for years and had grown strange about strangers, and Odile only had to lift the lids and whisper to them — *not me, not me, him, him* — and feel the hive-mind turn its attention outward like a drawn bow.

The third trap wasn't a trap at all. It was the house.

She went inside the low stone cottage, banked the fire to embers, and took down from above the lintel the tin box she hadn't opened since the night the riders came. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age: a knife with a blade of gray metal that drank the light, warden steel, the last thing her grandmother had pressed into her hands before walking out to meet the Inquisition with her chin up and her hands empty and open, the way you greet family.

*They'll want us afraid,* Sable had said. *So don't be. Be ready. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference.*

Odile belted the knife at her hip. It settled against her body with the familiarity of an old argument.

Then she sat at the table with her back to the wall, facing the door, and waited for her visitor the way the orchard waited for winter: patiently, and without any intention of dying.

* * *

The afternoon light was going copper when the stranger finally came up the lane — and it wasn't a stranger.

It was Wren Ashgrave.

Odile knew her by sight, the way everyone within twenty miles knew her by sight: the courier with the sharp eyes and the sharper rates, the woman who carried letters between Fenlow and Grayharbor and was rumored to carry other things between places that weren't on any map. She came up the lane openly, hands visible, walking with the deliberate unhurried gait of someone who has noticed the snares and would very much like the person who set them to know she's noticed too.

"Miss Villalobos," Wren called, stopping a careful ten paces from the bee-skeps. "Lovely orchard. I'd heard it was dead."

"It is," Odile said from the doorway, the warden knife loose in her hand, angled so the light-drinking blade looked like nothing at all. "You're standing on a dead orchard. Mind the wall gap."

Wren glanced down at the invisible snare and back up, and something like approval crossed her face. "I am. Thank you." She didn't move. "I'm not here for myself. Well. Partly. I'm here because my client paid me to deliver a message, and the message is: *she'll know what it means.*" From her coat Wren drew a folded paper and held it out. "That's it. That's the whole job. Though between us, I've been paid better for less interesting work."

Odile didn't move either. "Who's your client?"

"A courier doesn't—" Wren began, then seemed to weigh something, and shrugged. "Actually, a courier tells the truth when it costs nothing. I don't know. Dead drop outside Grayharbor, coin left under a loose brick, instructions in a hand I didn't recognize. Old-fashioned penmanship. Lots of flourishes. Someone educated a long time ago."

Something cold walked up Odile's spine. Flourishes. Her grandmother had written with flourishes, loops like climbing roses, ink-brown fingers smudging every page.

But Sable was eleven years ash.

"Leave it on the gatepost," Odile said. "Then leave."

"You're not going to invite me in? It's getting cold, and your bees are staring at me like I owe them money."

"The bees are right. Gatepost. Go."

Wren set the paper down, and Odile watched her go all the way to the end of the lane and out onto the moor road before she let herself cross the yard and take it. Her hands were steady. Her heart was not.

Inside, at the table, in the last of the light, she unfolded the page.

It was not her grandmother's hand. It was better than that, worse than that — it was *familiar*, the particular slanted capitals she'd known across a childhood of kitchen-table lessons, the handwriting of the person who used to dot his i's twice when he was nervous.

One line, in Edmund Villalobos's writing, after twelve years of nothing:

*Odi. They're hunting us. I'm sorry. I'm coming through the north warden-way and I need you to not kill me. — E.*

Odile read it four times. Then she read it a fifth, because the fourth time her eyes had blurred.

*Not kill me.* As if she might. As if the first thing she'd do, after twelve years of silence — twelve years of one letter, unanswered, that had arrived two months after the Purge and said only *don't look for me, I'm safer forgotten* — as if the first thing she'd do was stand at the warden-gate and put a knife in the only family she had left.

Well.

Maybe the second thing.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat up with the letter and the knife and the tin box, and she took out the one thing she'd saved from the burning that she'd never told another living soul about: a bundle of her grandmother's letters, forty of them, tied in string, hidden in the false bottom of the flour barrel these eleven years because burning them had felt like a second murder and keeping them felt like a slow one.

She'd meant to burn them tonight. When Wren's message came, she'd told herself: *this is the sign, this is the knock, burn them now before the Inquisition reads them over your corpse.* She'd even laid them in the grate. She'd even struck the match.

The match had gone out in her fingers. She couldn't tell you why. Cowardice, probably. Or the orchard's stubbornness leaking up through the floorboards into her hands.

Instead she untied the string now and read, and the letters were the old ones, the safe ones, full of kitchen wisdom and half-coded warnings — *the moon will be fat on market day, mind the east hedge* — and near the bottom of the bundle, the one she always avoided, the one dated nine days before the riders came:

*Odi, my heart. If you are reading this, then the thirteenth turn is close, or they are close, or both. Listen once and then decide whether to believe me. What we keep is not a thing. It does not sit in a box. It sleeps in our blood, yours and your brother's both, and it is waking the way all old things wake — slowly, and hungry. Your brother ran from it. You stayed and fed it flowers. Neither of you understands yet what you are. When the moon finishes her thirteenth turn, the Salt will call its own, and every gifted soul will hear it like a bell, and the ones who share our blood will hear it loudest of all. Guard the letters. Guard the orchard. And Odi — guard your brother, even from himself. Especially from himself. He was always the one who wanted to be loved more than he wanted to be good.*

Odile sat with that last line for a long time while the fire ticked down.

*He was always the one who wanted to be loved more than he wanted to be good.*

"That's not fair," she said aloud, to a dead woman, in an empty house. "He was nineteen. You sent him away yourself."

But even as she said it she remembered Edmund at fourteen, mapping the kitchen floor in chalk, drawing doors that weren't there and then opening one — just a crack, just wide enough to show a sliver of somewhere greener — and looking up at the family with that face, that shining, insufferable, starving face, waiting to be told they were wonderful.

She'd told them. She'd always told them. And then the Purge came, and Edmund ran, and the telling had stopped for twelve years.

Outside, the wind shifted, and Odile felt it — felt it the way she felt everything now, through the soles of her feet, through the roots under the yard. The orchard stirred. Every tree in those long dead rows turned its attention north, the way a dog turns toward a whistle only it can hear.

The warden-way was opening. Tonight. Hours early, and from the north, and fast.

Odile blew out the lamp, belted the knife, pocketed the letter and the bundle — all of it, every page, she was done choosing which pieces of her family to carry — and stepped out into the frost.

* * *

The north warden-gate stood in the hollow below the sheep track, a ring of standing stones that any passerby would take for an old shepherd's shelter, lichened and leaning and utterly unremarkable. Only family saw what the stones really were: a door. Only family could knock.

Odile reached the hollow ahead of the travelers — she knew the shortcuts of her own land the way Edmund knew the space between worlds — and positioned herself behind the largest stone with the knife drawn and her heart going like a hammer on a nailed-shut door.

The air in the ring began to change. Frost smoked upward off the grass. The dark between the stones deepened, thickened, became the particular velvet dark of a Veilroad mouth, and Odile's skin prickled with the nearness of it, the rawness, the smell of rain on hot stone from a country that wasn't anywhere.

Two figures stepped through.

The first was small and quick and armed — Wren Ashgrave again, of course, because apparently the woman was glued to this catastrophe now — and the second was tall and lean and hooded, and stumbled on the threshold like a man stepping off a boat after a long crossing, and caught himself against the standing stone with one gloved hand, and pushed back the hood.

Twelve years. Twelve years, and the face under the hood was older, thinner, carved harder by fear and distance, a scar through one eyebrow that Odile didn't know — that she hadn't been there for, that some stranger's blade or some stranger's kindness had given her brother while she was here keeping an orchard alive alone —

But it was Edmund. It was Edmund's face doing the thing it had always done, the flicker-fast calculation, the map-drawing, the eyes going everywhere at once and then, at last, landing on her.

On her, behind the stone, with the knife.

"Oh," Edmund said. All that cleverness, all that speed, and what came out was: "Oh, no."

"No," Odile agreed, and stepped into the ring. "Hello, E."

Edmund raised both hands slowly, palms out, the universal language of *please*, and Odile saw the tremor in them and hated how much it cost her not to cross the distance and grab them.

"You got the note," Edmund said.

"I got the note."

"And you're — holding a knife."

"I'm holding a knife," Odile confirmed, "because someone taught me that being afraid and being ready are different things, and I have spent twelve years being ready for you to come back, and I am *not* going to waste it on being afraid." Her voice cracked on the last word, which infuriated her. "You wrote one letter. One. *Don't look for me.* And then nothing. While they burned her, Edmund. While they burned her you were *gone*, and I stood in that yard and watched, and I have watered a dead orchard every single morning since so that no one would know what I am, and you —"

"I know." Edmund's voice was low and wrecked. "Odi, I know. I was nineteen and terrified and I told myself I was protecting you by disappearing, and that was a lie I told every day for twelve years, and I'm not asking you to forgive it tonight. I'm asking you to let me explain why I broke the one rule I left you — why I led them to your door."

"*Them?*" Odile went cold. "What them?"

"Inquisitors," Wren said quietly. "Sniffer-beast, hounds, the works. We lost them on the flats, but 'lost' is a word I use loosely when the thing chasing you navigates by the smell of your soul." She glanced at Edmund. "Tell her the rest. The part about the map."

"The Salt," Edmund said. "Odi — the Salt isn't extinct. It's real, and it's waking, and the crown is hunting for it, and the reason I came through *your* warden-ways, the reason I've dragged all of this to the one place I swore I'd never lead anyone —" Edmund stopped. Drew a breath. "Grandmother's letters. Do you still have them?"

Odile's hand went, without her permission, to the bundle under her coat.

Edmund saw the gesture and closed their eyes, and for a moment the clever cartographer's face simply crumpled, and they looked nineteen again, and lost, and hers.

"You kept them," Edmund whispered.

"Some of them," Odile said. "I burned most. I couldn't finish." She lifted the knife, and watched Edmund flinch, and turned the blade and drove it point-down into the frozen earth between them — a boundary, an offering, a door of her own. "So here's how this goes. You get until sunrise. You talk, I listen, and you start with the part where the Inquisition knows my name. Because if they're coming to my orchard, little brother—"

"They already know the way," Edmund said grimly. "I opened it for them. Every road I've run down leads here. That's what I came to warn you — it's not a hunt anymore, Odi. It's a convergence. Everything is pulling toward the Salt, and the Salt—"

Far to the south, beyond the dark line of the moor, the sky flickered. Once. Twice. Like lightning with no storm to blame, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

All three of them went silent. In the dead orchard above the hollow, every tree bloomed at once — thousands of white flowers opening together in the freezing dark, out of season, out of shame, out of hiding — and Odile felt her gift rise in her chest like a tide answering a moon she couldn't see.

"—is calling," Edmund finished softly. "And it just found out where home is."

Odile pulled the knife out of the earth.

"Then we'd better be gone before it finishes the sentence," she said. "Get off my land, Edmund. Both of you. Through the orchard, quick, before the blossoms fall — and pray to whatever's left of Grandmother that the hounds are slower than we are."

Behind them, in the north, faint but gaining, something began to bay.
