## 10. Wren's Bargain

Wren had been counting Edmund Villalobos's mistakes since the salt flats began, and she was up to eleven.

Mistake one: hiring a courier through a dead-drop instead of asking around first. Mistake two: paying in coin old enough to have a biography. Mistake three: lying about the sigil stamped on that coin with all the skill of a man who'd spent twelve years lying to no one but himself. Mistakes four through nine were navigational. Mistake ten was stopping at the ruined waystation on the flats' eastern edge, because it was the only shelter for six miles and therefore exactly where anyone hunting them would look.

Mistake eleven was trusting her.

She watched him now across the waystation's cold hearth — *them*, she corrected herself; the cartographer used they the way other people used a name they were still deciding on — hunched over the stolen map with a stub of candle, fingers hovering above the parchment without touching it, as if the thing might bite. Which, Wren thought, it probably would. Eventually. Everything connected to the Salt did.

The map wasn't inked so much as grown. Lines of silver-gray spread across the hide like frost on a window, and where they converged at the center there was a blank space the size of a thumbnail that seemed, when Wren looked too long, to be looking back. She'd seen a lot of contraband in her years with the Consortium. She'd smuggled relics out of drowned temples and carried letters that got three people killed and once transported, in a crate marked GLASSWARE, something that sang at night. Nothing had ever made her feel like this: like a mouse standing at the edge of a hole that went down much further than a hole should.

Edmund hadn't slept. That was mistake twelve, forming in real time — dark-ringed eyes tracking the door, the window, the gap under the eaves, cataloguing exits they wouldn't use because using them meant admitting the pursuit was real.

"You should sleep," Wren said.

"I'm fine."

"You're swaying."

"I'm thinking." Edmund rolled the map closed with exaggerated care. "You said you knew a route east that avoids the flats' open ground. I'd like to know why a courier knows routes at all. Couriers carry things. They don't chart."

"Charting is carrying," Wren said. "Just slower."

It was a good line. She'd had two days to prepare good lines, and she hated how few of them were going to survive what came next.

Outside, the wind moved over the salt with a sound like sand poured on a drumhead. Somewhere behind them — a day back, maybe less — Inquisitor hounds were tasting the air for the flavor of magic, and every hour Edmund spent not sleeping was an hour their fear-sweat soaked deeper into their clothes, and fear, Wren happened to know, smelled almost exactly like gift-work to a hound's nose. The crown's beasts couldn't tell terror from sorcery. It was one of the many elegant cruelties of the system she'd grown up inside.

She stood, stretched until her spine cracked, and made her decision the way she made most of them: quickly, and then lived inside it whether it fit or not.

"Before we go any further," she said, "you should know who you're running with."

* * *

Edmund's head came up slowly. In the candlelight their face did something complicated — the flinch of a person who has spent twelve years expecting betrayal and is almost relieved to finally be right.

"Wren Ashgrave," she said, before they could speak. "Yes, that Ashgrave. My grandmother founded the Consortium back when it was a shipping concern and a smuggling concern wearing each other's coats. My mother built it into the largest private ledger in the known realms. And me—" she turned her hand palm-up, showing the calluses, the old rope scar across the wrist, "—me, they trained from the age of eight to move anything anywhere, and to never ask what's in the box. So imagine my surprise when the box starts asking questions back."

"What box?"

"The one you're holding." She nodded at the map roll. "Three weeks ago my mother's factor handed me a commission. Anonymous client, premium rate, destination unspecified except by description: *a place that shouldn't exist*. Find someone who can chart it. Bring back proof. Standard Consortium acquisition protocol." She let that sit. "I found you because you were the only cartographer in Saltmere whose work didn't show up in any guild registry. Twelve years of being careful makes a man invisible, Edmund. Invisible men are exactly what people like my family collect."

Edmund's thumb moved against the map's leather casing, back and forth, a small nervous metronome. "And the coin."

"The coin." Wren laughed, short and without humor. "You recognized the sigil. I saw you recognize it — that little seizure you had at your stall, the bad lie about it being a family piece. So here's what I know, and you can check it against whatever your grandmother left you, if anything: the sigil is a warden's mark. Seven interlocked rings. The Consortium has bought and sold maybe forty objects bearing it over the last century, and every single time, our archivists logged the same notation in the provenance. *Recovered from Villalobos holdings.* Your family name has been in our ledgers longer than my family has. Isn't that something? We've been trading pieces of your inheritance since before either of us was born."

The silence that followed had weight. Wind worried at the shutters. Edmund sat very still, and Wren watched them do the arithmetic she'd watched a hundred marks do — the rapid recalculation of everything they thought they knew, the past rearranging itself around new information like furniture in a dark room.

"Why tell me this?" Edmund asked finally. "If you're Consortium, if you were sent to acquire the map — why confess? You could have waited. Taken it while I slept."

"Because I could have taken it while you slept last night," Wren said, "and didn't. Because I could have sold your location to the Inquisition for more than the whole commission pays, and haven't. Because somewhere between Saltmere and the sinkhole that nearly ate us both, I stopped working for my mother and started working out what I actually want." She crouched, bringing herself level with Edmund's chair, close enough to see the candle flame double itself in their exhausted eyes. "So. Here's the bargain."

* * *

"The crown wants the Salt destroyed," Wren said. "That's the official proclamation, anyway — *extirpate the last vestige of the Sundering corruption*, et cetera, et cetera. Don't believe it. Chancellors don't spend this kind of money destroying things. They spend it acquiring them. Vess wants the Salt because whoever holds the Salt holds every gifted soul in both kingdoms by the root. He doesn't want to end magic. He wants to be its only customer."

"And the Consortium?"

Wren smiled, and knew even as she did it that the smile wasn't reaching anything above her mouth. "The Consortium wants the same thing, minus the crown. We want to own it. Lease it, really — access, licensing, stewardship. A monopoly on wonder, administered responsibly, at competitive rates." She heard how it sounded. She'd rehearsed it anyway, because her mother would have wanted the pitch delivered straight, unashamed, and some stubborn part of Wren still believed in doing the job right even while burning it down. "My family believes power is safest in a ledger. Crowds are dangerous. Kings are worse. But a contract — a contract you can read, amend, enforce. The Consortium doesn't want to be tyrants, Edmund. We want to be the bank tyrants borrow from."

"That's not better."

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't. It's just better than Vess, which in the current market passes for virtue."

Edmund stood. The chair scraped. For a moment they loomed there, this hollow-eyed person who'd spent twelve years selling charms that hummed to farmers who pretended not to notice, and Wren braced for the speech — the righteous refusal, the *I'll die before I hand it to you*, the whole theater of it.

What she got instead was quieter and worse.

"You said *stewardship*." Edmund's voice was flat. "You know what the wardens called themselves? Stewards. Guardians. Keepers of the flame. And the last one of them—" they stopped. Swallowed. Started again from a different angle. "There's a reason the war is called the Sundering. There's a reason the word *warden* makes people spit. Whatever my grandmother's family kept, it got loose once, and the world is still counting the cost. And your family wants to put it in a vault with a price tag."

"My family wants a great many stupid things," Wren said. "I'm offering you a third option."

"There is no third option."

"There's always a third option. It's usually just expensive." She reached into her coat — slowly, telegraphing every inch, because Edmund's hand had already drifted toward the knife at their belt — and drew out a slim packet wrapped in oilcloth. She set it on the table between them and unwrapped it. Inside: papers. Good ones. Consortium travel writs under three names, none of them theirs; a letter of passage stamped with the Ashgrave seal, which opened doors in Grayharbor and closed mouths everywhere else; and a small square of pressed salt-crystal on a cord, which she'd paid a fortune for and which would blur a hound's nose for roughly six hours.

"Protection," she said. "Real protection — not charm-trinkets, but paper, money, routes, people who owe me. Enough to get you off every list the Inquisition keeps. In exchange: the map. Not the original — a copy, in your own hand, with whatever you're willing to show. And your word that when you find what's at the center of it, the Consortium gets first negotiation. Not possession. Negotiation. You keep the thing itself, whatever it turns out to be. We get a seat at the table."

Edmund stared at the packet like it might be a trap, which was correct of them. "Why would the Consortium settle for a seat?"

"Because I'm not telling my mother about this conversation," Wren said, "and by the time she learns otherwise, we'll be past the point where her opinion matters." She held Edmund's gaze. "Look at me. I've spent my whole life moving boxes I wasn't allowed to open. This is the first job I've ever opened the box on, and what I found inside is a person — tired, scared, in over their head, holding something that will get them killed by everyone who wants it. I can't give it back. I can't take it. All I can do is decide whose side of the hunt I'm standing on when the dogs close in." A breath. "I'm deciding now. That's the bargain. Not leverage — allegiance. Priced, because I don't trust anything free, including myself."

* * *

Edmund didn't answer right away. They picked up the travel writs, read them twice, set them down. They touched the salt-crystal amulet and their expression flickered — recognition, hunger, shame, all in the space of a blink, and Wren filed that away because it told her more than the whole confession had. The cartographer had used magic recently. Within days. And hated it.

"The copy will take a night's work," Edmund said at last. "And I'll show you the roads, the gates, the approaches. I won't show you the center. There are things on this map I don't understand yet myself, and I'm not handing anyone a key I haven't tested."

"Fair."

"And Wren." They looked up, and for the first time since the stall in Saltmere their eyes were steady. "If this is a longer con — if there's a second act where your mother's enforcers appear and I learn the friendship was scaffolding — I want you to know I'll understand. Truly. I've been the mark before. But I will also kill you, or try very hard to, and I'd rather not, because you're the only person in weeks who's talked to me like a person instead of a bounty."

"Noted," Wren said. "Same terms apply in reverse."

They shook on it. Her grip was dry; theirs was cold. Somewhere in the shake, Wren realized, a currency had changed hands that neither ledger in the world could record, and she felt the strange vertigo of having spent something she couldn't buy back.

Edmund took the map to the table's far end and began work on the copy, candle pulled close, pen scratching. Wren took the doorway watch, as always, back to the wall, eyes on the salt plain where the moon was rising — fat, and a little wrong-colored, and further along its slow count than any moon had a right to be.

Twelve turns gone, the old ledgers said. One remaining.

She was still watching it when she saw the light.

Far out on the white flats, maybe two miles, maybe less: a lantern, swinging in the rhythm of someone walking fast. Then a second. Then, along a low ridge she'd have sworn an hour ago was empty, a whole string of them, spaced like beads — a search line, sweeping east in perfect discipline, closing on the waystation the way water closes on a drain.

Behind her, the pen stopped scratching.

"Wren," Edmund said quietly. "How long does that crystal of yours last?"

"Six hours. Why?"

"Because I think we have about six minutes."
