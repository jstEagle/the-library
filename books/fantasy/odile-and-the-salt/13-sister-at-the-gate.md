## 13. Sister at the Gate

The warden-gate stood where the orchard ended and the world began: a ring of standing stones older than Saltmere itself, half-swallowed by briars that Odile had spent twenty years pretending were ordinary thorns.

They weren't ordinary. Nothing on her land was ordinary, and she had made a life out of not noticing it.

She crouched at the gate's edge in the blue hour before dawn, fingers pressed to the frost-stiffened grass, and read the ground the way her grandmother had taught her to read weather — by feel, by patience, by the small telltale wrongnesses that most people walked past without ever seeing. The warden-ways crossed here. Paths that only Villalobos blood could walk, threads through the hedge of the world that no mapmaker should have been able to find and no Inquisitor could follow.

Someone had used them. Last night. The grass along the eastern path lay flattened in a line too straight for deer, too deliberate for wind, and where the traveler had stepped between the second and third stones, the moss had turned briefly, impossibly green — a stripe of springtime in the dead of autumn, already browning at the edges like a lie caught fading.

Only three people alive knew those paths. One was Odile. One was buried under the apple trees with a name carved into slate that the rain had long since softened. And one —

One had run away twelve years ago and never once looked back.

Odile rose slowly. Her knees ached. Everything about her ached lately, since the letters, since the burning, since the one letter she'd kept folded against her heart like a coal she couldn't decide whether to warm her hands on or be burned by.

*The Salt is not a thing,* the grandmother's handwriting said, in the letter she'd read eleven times now. *It is a current, and you are its riverbed. When the moon makes her thirteenth turn, the river will rise. Guard the banks, my girls. Guard each other.*

*My girls.* As if they were still girls. As if they were still together. As if Edmund hadn't slipped out of the orchard one gray morning with a satchel of maps and a mouth full of promises — *I'll send for you when it's safe, Odie, I swear it on the salt itself* — and vanished so completely that for years Odile had wondered if she'd dreamed having a sibling at all.

Well. Dream or no dream, someone had just walked her family's secret roads in the night, and there were exactly two explanations. Either the crown had found a way to counterfeit warden-blood —

Or Edmund Villalobos had finally come home, twelve years late and running from something that smelled like smoke even from here.

Odile went inside and took down the traps.

Not iron traps. She'd never needed iron. Her gift — the one she'd spent a lifetime disguising as luck, as good soil, as coincidence — worked slowest and best on living things, and so her traps were living things: the blackberry canes that would knot themselves around an ankle; the hornet nest under the eaves of the gate-arch that she woke with a whispered word and aimed, gently, like a drawn bow; the old dog rose at the path's bend whose thorns lengthened overnight whenever she asked them to, which she did now, murmuring to the roots while the sky went from slate to pearl.

*Grow angry,* she told the rose. *Grow very angry, and very tall.*

Then she sat down on the gate-stone with a blanket around her shoulders and a kettle of cider warming over a spirit-lamp, and she waited for her sibling to walk into a wall of thorns.

It felt, she had to admit, almost festive.

* * *

Edmund came at midmorning, and they came badly.

Odile heard them before she saw them — the crash of the blackberry canes, a bitten-off curse in a voice that hadn't changed at all, still that same low roughness like a cart wheel on gravel. Then Edmund burst through the last of the briars onto the open ground before the gate, and Odile's breath went out of her all at once, because twelve years is a long time and also no time at all.

They were thinner. Gray threaded their dark hair at the temples, and there was a new scar splitting one eyebrow, and their coat was road-filthy and torn at the shoulder where the brambles had taken their tax. But they stood the same way, weight on the left foot, chin up, hands slightly raised as if perpetually about to explain themselves — and when their eyes found Odile across the frost-burned grass, they went wide and wet and utterly, catastrophically young.

"Odie," Edmund said.

"Don't," Odile said. "Don't you *dare* say it like that. Like you're greeting me at market."

"I —"

"You don't get the soft version." She was off the stone now, walking toward them, and she could feel her own gift rising in her blood like sap in spring, the grass greening in her wake, the dead orchard stirring, and she didn't care, didn't bother hiding it, because there was no one here to see but family and family had already seen everything. "Twelve years, Ed. Twelve years. Not a letter. Not a word. Grandmother died and I buried her alone and I burned her letters alone and I sat in that empty house every single night wondering if you were dead in a ditch somewhere, because the alternative — the alternative was worse, do you understand? The alternative was that you were *alive* and you simply *chose* —"

"I chose to keep you safe—"

"*Don't.*"

They flinched. Actually flinched, hands coming up, and some cold practical part of Odile noted that Edmund flinched like a person who'd been struck often and recently, and that part of her wanted to gather them up off the frozen ground and feed them cider until they stopped shaking.

The rest of her threw the first punch.

It wasn't a good punch. She was a farmer, not a fighter, and Edmund caught most of it on the forearm and staggered back anyway, more from surprise than force. "Odie — what —"

"That's for grandmother's funeral." Another swing. This one landed, glancing off the jaw, and Edmund's head snapped sideways. "That's for the letters." They grabbed her wrists then, both of them, and held on, and they were stronger than she remembered and shaking worse than she was, and for a moment the two of them stood locked together in the middle of the warden-gate like a knot that wouldn't come undone.

"The letters," Edmund panted. "What letters? Odie, stop, listen to me, there are hounds behind me that navigate by the smell of magic, we have maybe an hour, maybe less, and you're spending it hitting me?"

"An hour isn't nearly enough." But she stopped struggling. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "It would take a year to hit you enough. A decade."

"I know." Their grip gentled. They were looking at her the way people look at lighthouses. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry doesn't cover it, I know that too. But I need — I came back because I need —"

"What do you need, Edmund?" She pulled free and stepped back, arms crossed, and put every year of her absence into her voice. "Say it plainly. You always were bad at plain."

Edmund opened their coat. From an inner pocket wrapped in oilcloth they drew a roll of paper — no, not paper, she saw as they unrolled it across the flat top of the gate-stone, something finer, something that shimmered faintly like fish-scale — and weighted its corners with four stones, and Odile forgot how to breathe.

It was a map. Of course it was a map; Edmund had been drawing maps since before they could write. But this map moved. Rivers crawled. Coastlines breathed. And at its center, rendered in lines so fine they seemed stitched rather than drawn, lay a country Odile knew only from stories — the deep interior, past the salt flats, past the drowned abbey, to a place marked not with an X but with a small careful symbol she recognized from the spine of grandmother's oldest book.

A circle, quartered. The warden's sigil.

"This is why I ran," Edmund said quietly. "This is why I stayed away. Because I stole this, Odie, and the people who want it back want me dead, and the people who want it kept want me owned, and either way everyone who stands near me ends up in chains. I thought distance was protection. I was wrong. It's just a slower kind of danger." They looked up. "I need to know what grandmother left us. What the Salt actually *is*. Because I've read every book, bribed every archivist, and none of them know, and the letter you burned —"

"I didn't burn all of them."

Edmund went very still. "You kept one."

"One." Odile reached into her shirt and drew out the letter, creased and soft as cloth now from handling, and held it, and didn't give it over. "And before you touch it, you're going to answer me one question honestly. Not the polite honest. The ugly honest."

Edmund waited.

"Do you know what we are?" Odile asked. "Not what grandmother wrote. Not what the sigil means. Do you — in your gut, in whatever's left of the child who used to talk to the apple trees — do you actually know?"

Silence. Wind moved through the dead orchard, rattling branches that should have been bare and weren't, quite, because Odile's grief had been leaking into the land for twelve years and things grew here that had no business growing.

"No," Edmund said at last. It came out cracked. "I don't. I have guesses. I have a map that points at a place and a moon that's counting down and a hunter on my heels who looked at me in an alley in Grayharbor like she *recognized* me, Odie, like my magic called to hers across ten feet of frozen mud. I have a courier who betrayed me and then saved me and I don't know which betrayal was real. I have nothing solid except—" their hand hovered over the moving map, trembling, "—except that whatever the Salt is, it's waking up, and it thinks I'm its door."

The honesty of it undid her more than any apology could have. Odile looked at her sibling — thin, scarred, terrified, magnificent in the specific way of people who keep walking after everything has broken — and felt twelve years of calcified anger crack down the middle like river ice in thaw.

"We're wardens," she said. "We're not doors. We're *banks*, Ed. Riverbeds. Grandmother's letter says the Salt is a current and our blood is the channel it runs through, and when the moon finishes her thirteenth turn — six weeks, Ed, the solstice — whoever holds the inheritance when it wakes holds it *wholly*. All of it. Every drop of magic in the world, flowing through one person."

Edmund stared at her. "Wholly."

"Wholly." She let the word sit there, ugly and enormous. "Now think about who wants that. Think about Chancellor Vess and his Gift-Hunt theater. Think about your courier's Consortium. Think about the last person who held it, and what the histories call him instead of his name." She watched Edmund's face do the arithmetic. Watched them arrive at the sum. "Yes," she said softly. "*Him.* The Sunderer. That's the inheritance, Ed. That's what grandmother left us. A loaded weapon with our names on the handle."

* * *

They read the letter together, sitting on the gate-stone with the cider gone cold between them, Edmund's finger tracing the faded ink while Odile recited the passages she'd memorized and dreaded. The Salt as living inheritance. The thirteen turns. The warning at the bottom, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the page:

*If the river rises in one vessel, it will drown the world. Find the other vessel. My girls were always meant to carry it together. Split, you are two gardeners. Joined, you are a flood. Choose carefully which you wish to be.*

"Split," Edmund repeated. "Joined." They laughed, and it wasn't a funny laugh. "She wrote that like it was simple. Like we weren't —" they gestured vaguely at the space between them, at the twelve-year gap sitting in the frost like a third person. "Like this."

"This," Odile agreed.

A hornet droned past, lazy and harmless — she'd called the nest off an hour ago, though Edmund didn't know it, and she decided not to mention the thorns either. Some things you got to keep.

"So what do we do?" Edmund asked. "Run further? Bury the map? There's a vault, Odie — the letter mentions a vault beneath the drowned abbey, west, on the flats' far edge. Warden regalia. Records. Maybe answers about how the inheritance *works*, whether it can be refused, whether —" their voice dropped, "— whether it can be destroyed. I keep coming back to that. If we end the Salt, we end the hunts. We end Vess's whole design. No prize, no war."

"And we end every gifted soul in the world with it." Odile shook her head. "I've thought about it every night since I read the letter. I don't think it works like that. I think the Salt isn't *in* us, Ed. I think we're *in* it. Destroy the current and you don't kill the riverbed — you just make sure nothing ever grows there again." She looked out at her orchard, her beautiful half-dead orchard that bloomed only where she walked, and felt the truth of it in her bones. "Grandmother didn't leave us a weapon. She left us a *trust*. And trusts have trustees, plural, and rules, and —" she paused, "— and probably a vault full of instructions we haven't read yet."

Edmund was quiet a moment. Then: "You'd come? To the abbey. Through the flats. With hounds behind me and Inquisitors ahead and a Consortium that buys people like us by the pound."

"I've been waiting twelve years for you to ask me that question," Odile said. "You're lucky I answer it faster than I punch."

Something broke open in Edmund's face then — relief and grief and exhaustion all at once, the expression of a person setting down a load they'd forgotten they were carrying — and they leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the two of them just sat, shoulder almost touching shoulder, in the ruins of the family they'd been and the wreckage of the one they might still become.

"I missed you," Edmund said, barely audible. "Every day. I mapped the Veilroads for twelve years and I never once found a road that led home, so I told myself home was gone. And then I walked the warden-way last night and it knew me, Odie. The stones knew me. The moss went green under my feet like it had been waiting." Their voice frayed. "I don't deserve that."

"No," Odile agreed. "But the land doesn't keep score. That's rather the point of it." She stood, joints cracking, and offered her hand. "Come on. We leave within the hour, before the hounds catch your scent again. You'll eat first. You look like a scarecrow that lost a bet."

Edmund took her hand and let her pull them up, and for a heartbeat neither of them let go — two wardens at the warden-gate, joined at the grip, split everywhere else.

Then Edmund stiffened. Their head turned east, toward the flats, nostrils flaring like they'd learned to smell trouble on the road.

"What is it?" Odile said.

"That thread I mentioned," Edmund said grimly. "The hunter. Seraphine Dax. In Grayharbor she hesitated — let me go when every instinct she had screamed to take me. I've been telling myself it was a trick." They rolled up the map with quick, practiced hands, and their eyes were distant and troubled. "But hesitation in a hunter isn't mercy, Odie. It's a crack. And cracks in people like her either seal shut —"

"— or break wide open," Odile finished.

Somewhere far to the east, so faint it might have been imagination, a bell began to toll. Not a church bell. Not an alarm. Something older, deeper, ringing up out of the earth itself, and Odile felt it in her teeth, in her blood, in the sudden impossible flush of green that raced across the dead orchard grass from where she stood to the horizon.

The moon, counting down.

"Get your pack," she said. "We're going to the abbey."
