## 14. The Warden's Letters

The warden-gate closed behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Odile had not been inside the vault beneath the orchard since she was nine years old, and she had forgotten how small it was. A room no bigger than a kitchen, dug into the hillside below the dead trees, its walls lined with shelves of clay jars sealed in wax. Grandmother's preserves, she'd thought as a child. Apricots and quince for a winter that never came.

Now she knew better. The jars held salt — different salts, each one humming faintly at a different pitch when she passed her hand over them, like a choir of very patient singers waiting to be asked.

Edmund stood near the door, arms wrapped around themselves, looking like they still couldn't quite believe she hadn't killed them back at the gate. Fair enough. Odile had wanted to. She'd landed two good punches before the crying started, and after that it seemed pointless.

"Sit," she said, and dragged the single stool into the middle of the room. "You look half-dead."

"I am half-dead," Edmund said. "I crossed the flats on foot. There were hounds."

"There are always hounds." She found the candle stub where it always was, wedged behind the third shelf, and lit it with her thumb — a trick that would have gotten her burned in Saltmere, done casually, in front of her fugitive sibling. The flame leaned toward Edmund like a curious dog. "And now there's an Inquisitor with your scent, if what you told me at the gate is true. So we don't have time for you to be delicate about this."

"About what?"

Odile reached up to the top shelf, to the place she had kept empty for twenty years — kept *clear*, swept every season, dusted with a care she'd never once admitted to herself was hope — and took down the letter.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed thread, and it had not yellowed. That was the first strange thing. Paper should have gone brown and brittle in twenty years of damp hillside air. This paper was white as new snow, and when she took it in both hands, it was warm.

Edmund made a sound. Small, involuntary, the sound of someone touching a stove they'd been warned about.

"You feel it too," Odile said.

"I felt it from the door." Edmund's voice had gone thin. "Odile, what is that?"

"It came the winter after you left." She sat down on an upturned jar crate, because her legs had decided the stool was too far away. "A courier I never saw. No tracks. The letter was just — there, on the doorstep, on the morning after the first frost. Addressed to *the wardens who remain*. Grandmother had been dead eight months by then." She turned the letter over. The seal on the back was unbroken: a disc of grey wax stamped with a circle of thirteen marks, twelve filled and the thirteenth an open hollow. "I've read it eleven times. Once a year, on the anniversary. And every time I finish it, I can't remember wanting to read it again until the next year comes. It's like —"

"Like a dream," Edmund said quietly. "You can only hold it once."

"Yes." She looked up. "You've read something like this before."

"Not like this." Edmund lowered themselves onto the crate across from her, knees nearly touching hers in the little room. "But I know the feeling. From the Veilroads. Some things in the deep paths only let you carry them out one piece at a time." They nodded at the letter. "Read it aloud. If we're going to do this, we do it together. Whatever it says."

Odile almost laughed. Twenty years of anger, and here was Edmund asking for togetherness as if it were simple as breathing. But her hands were shaking, and she found she didn't want to read it alone either. She had read it alone eleven times. It had never once gotten easier.

She broke the seal.

* * *

The handwriting was Grandmother's. That was the second strange thing, and the worse one. Odile knew that hand the way she knew her own reflection — the tall ascenders, the way the letters leaned forward like runners at a starting line. She had seen it on recipe cards and seed packets and the margins of three generations of orchard ledgers. She had watched it go stiff and shaky in the last year of the old woman's life.

This writing was young. Strong. As if the letter had been written fifty years ago and waited all that time to be delivered.

"*To my grandchildren, whoever is left, whichever of you is reading,*" Odile began, and her voice caught on the word *grandchildren*, plural, spoken into a future the writer could not see. Edmund went very still.

She read on.

"*If you have opened this, then the Hunt has come, or the Salt has begun to call, or both — they arrive together, always; the crown smells the waking the way dogs smell rain. I will not waste your time with comfort. Comfort is for people whose problems end.*"

Odile heard Edmund exhale slowly through their nose. Recognition. Whoever their grandmother had been in these pages, she was exactly the woman Odile remembered.

"*You will want to know what the Salt is. Your parents were told and chose not to tell you, and I honored that choice, and I want you to understand it was not cowardice. We thought we had buried it deep enough that it would sleep past your lifetimes. We were wrong, but the hoping was not foolish. Hope is the only crop that grows in bad soil.*

"*Here is the truth, then, plain as I can make it. The Salt is not a thing. Stop looking for a thing. It is not in a vault or a mountain or a hoard under the sea. The Salt is a living inheritance. It moves down bloodlines the way color moves through eyes. It sleeps in some generations and wakes in others, and when it wakes, it wakes in a body — one body, one carrier, chosen by no one, explained by nothing. Our family were its wardens, which is a fine word for what we really were: its house. Its ground it grew in. For nine hundred years the Villalobos line has carried the Salt the way an orchard carries water — invisibly, entirely, and without ever being able to set it down.*"

The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

Odile stopped reading. Not because she wanted to. Because her voice had stopped working.

Edmund was staring at her. Or past her. Their lips were moving slightly, rereading the words she'd already said, and Odile watched the exact moment the meaning landed — watched her sibling's face go through disbelief, denial, and arrive somewhere worse: arithmetic. The kind of face a person makes when a sum they've been avoiding for years finally comes due.

"The orchard," Edmund said. "Your orchard blooms where you walk."

"My orchard is dead everywhere else," Odile said. Her own voice sounded far away. "Twenty-two years dead. Except under my feet."

"And mine —" Edmund pressed a hand flat against their own sternum, as if checking whether the organ underneath had changed shape. "I map places that don't exist. I've always assumed it was — a knack. A trick I learned. Odile, I taught myself to see the Veilroads when I was six. Who teaches themselves that? Nobody. You don't teach yourself to see. You either see or you don't, and I —" They laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "I thought I was clever. All this time I thought I was clever."

"You were hiding," Odile said. "Same as me. Cleverness is just hiding with better posture."

She made herself read on.

"*You will feel it begin soon, if you have not already. A pull. An appetite. The Salt waking wants things, and it will use your wants as its hands. Do not mistake its hunger for your own. This is the whole art of wardenship, children, the only lesson: learn the difference between what you want and what it wants through you. Nine hundred years of our family, and I can count on one hand those who learned it in time.*

"*And time is the shape of your problem. The Salt does not wake all at once. It wakes with the moon. Thirteen turns — thirteen months, marked from the first night the gifted begin to dream of white shores. On the thirteenth turn, at the turning itself, it comes fully awake. And here is the part you must hear with your whole spine, so I will say it plainly:*"

Odile's throat had gone dry. She swallowed and read the next words aloud anyway, because Edmund was watching her with an expression that said *together, whatever it says*, and she had agreed to that at the gate, with fists and tears, and apparently that agreement was binding.

"*Whoever carries the Salt when the thirteenth turn completes claims it wholly. Not shares it. Claims it. Every drop of magic in the world will answer to that one body, that one will, for as long as that body lives. The Sundering War was fought over the last claiming. Three kingdoms burned. Two hundred thousand dead. And the warden who claimed it then — Aldous Villalobos, your many-times grandfather — used his first year of power to salt the fields of everyone who had ever opposed him, which is why our family name is mud from here to the coast, and why he is remembered as the Tyrant of the White Shore. He was not evil when the moon began to turn. He was kind. He was generous. He loved his garden. The Salt did not make him into something else. It made him into more of what he already was, and what he already was, it turns out, included a man who could not bear to be defied.*

"*So. When the thirteenth turn comes, you will have three choices, and only three, and I will not soften them.*

"*One: claim it. Become the most powerful creature since the war. I know my grandchildren, and I know at least one of you is already imagining it — remaking the laws, undoing the Purge, raising the burned. Understand that the Salt will agree to all of it. That is the trap. It agrees with everything. It has no bottom.*

"*Two: destroy it. It can be unmade — the old rites exist, though they cost the carrier everything: gift, memory, self. You would end magic in the world forever, and the world would be safe and dimmer, and you would not be in it, not really. What walks away from that rite wears your face and cannot remember why it ever loved anything.*

"*Three — and this is the one I prayed over for forty years, and I never found it whole, only in pieces, and I leave you the pieces: give it away. Not to one heir. To everyone. Break it small. I do not know if it can be done. I believe it can, because the Salt is alive, and living things divide. But I never dared try, because a failed dividing might shatter it into nothing, and because I was afraid, and because I loved my life. I leave you my cowardice along with everything else. Spend it better than I did.*

"*One thing more. The moon is already counting. I felt the first turn last spring, in my bones, the way you feel a storm two counties off. By the time this reaches you, several turns will have passed. Count them. Know your number. Do not let the thirteenth turn arrive as a surprise — surprise is how Aldous lost himself; he didn't believe the letters until the sky changed color.*

"*I love you both. I have loved you since before you were born, and I mean both, and I mean the one who ran away too. Come home if you can. The orchard knows how to wait.*

"*— Your grandmother, Maren Villalobos, last warden of the Salt, keeper of a cowardice she hopes you'll forgive.*"

* * *

Silence filled the little vault the way water fills a cup.

The candle flame had righted itself again, but it burned very low now, as if the letter had taken something from the air to sustain itself. Odile realized her hands were numb. She looked down and found she had crushed the edges of the paper without leaving a mark on it. The paper declined to be damaged. Of course it did.

"Count them," Edmund said at last. Their voice was rough. "She said count them. The turns."

"Do you remember when they started?" Odile asked. "The dreams."

Edmund flinched. Which was an answer.

"White shores," Odile said. "Water that isn't water. A bell made of light. I've had them since —" She counted backward, months falling away like leaves. "Since last spring. Since before the Hunt was announced. Before Wren, before the flats, before any of it." She looked up. "Edmund. How many turns?"

Edmund's mouth worked. Odile watched them do the same arithmetic she had just done, and watched them arrive somewhere different, because they had been running for weeks and counting nights instead of months, and their number came out lower than hers and therefore worse.

"Eleven," Edmund whispered. "Maybe twelve. Odile, maybe twelve. I lost track on the flats — the sky was white day and night, I couldn't —"

"Then we have one turn," Odile said. "One month. Perhaps less."

The words dropped into the room and lay there between them like something with weight and temperature.

Edmund stood up abruptly, walked to the wall of humming jars, put their hand against the shelf as if steadying themselves against a ship's rail. "It's me," they said. "Isn't it. The carrier. It woke in me, not you. That's why the Veilroads — that's why the Salt-flats charm worked when I —" They turned around. In the guttering light their face looked ten years older than it had at the gate. "Why isn't it you? You're the one who stayed. You kept the land. You kept the *letters*. If inheritance means anything, it should be you."

"Because it doesn't choose the good child," Odile said. "Grandmother wrote that too, in the ledger margins, in code I spent ten years cracking. *It chooses the one who runs toward doors.*" She stood, legs stiff, and crossed to stand beside her sibling in front of the shelf of salt. "You've been opening doors your whole life, Ed. Roads between worlds. Doors in people. I stayed in one place and grew roots. The Salt doesn't want roots. It wants a key."

"That's a horrible thing to be."

"Yes," Odile said. "Welcome home."

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Edmund laughed — a broken, wet sound, halfway to sobbing — and Odile put her arm around her sibling's shoulders, and they stood together in the vault under the dead orchard, two grown adults holding each other up in a room full of their grandmother's patience.

When Edmund finally spoke again, their voice was steady in the way of someone who has decided to be steady by force.

"There's a mirror," they said. "In a drowned abbey, north of Grayharbor. Wren mentioned it — the Consortium's records reference a warden regalia site. If Grandmother left us pieces of a third choice, they'd be there. With the regalia. With whatever shows the previous holders." They pulled back and looked at Odile, and their eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "I need to see Aldous. I need to see what he looked like before the moon finished turning, so I know what to watch for in myself. Because Odile —" Their hand drifted to their chest again, unconsciously, the gesture of a person guarding a wound. "It's already talking to me. It's been talking to me for weeks. And it sounds *reasonable*."

Odile thought of her orchard. Twenty-two years of walking a dead field green, alone, stubborn, making luck out of defiance. She thought of the letter's warm paper, and the empty space on the top shelf she had kept clear for two decades without ever calling it hope.

"Then we go north," she said. "Tonight. Before your Inquisitor finds the trail, before the Consortium remembers you exist, before the twelfth turn closes." She pinched out the candle with her fingers, and in the sudden dark the jars of salt glowed faintly, all of them, a shelf-full of small pale stars. "And Edmund — pack the letter. It goes where we go. Grandmother waited twenty years to be read. She's not staying behind in a hole in the ground."

Above them, muffled by earth and root, the wind moved through the dead orchard — and for the first time in twenty-two years, Odile heard it carrying blossom-scent from the wrong direction. From the north. From wherever the moon was rising on its twelfth turn, counting down.
