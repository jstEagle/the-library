## 15. The City That Outlawed Wonder

Wren had been to Grayharbor twice before, both times for jobs she didn't like, and both times she'd left with the same feeling: that the city was a house where someone had died and no one would say so.

Now she came back with two fugitives in tow — one of them carrying an inheritance that could end or remake the world, depending on which moon-turn you asked about — and the feeling was worse. Because now she knew what had died here.

"Rules," she said, as the three of them crested the last hill before the city walls. The road below was wide and clean, lined with poplars that grew in perfect rows because nothing wild had been permitted to interfere with them. "No charms. No humming. No looking at anything too long. In Grayharbor, curiosity is a symptom."

Edmund squinted at the skyline. "It's beautiful," they said, and Wren heard the hesitation in it, the way you'd hesitate before calling a corpse beautiful.

"That's the point," she said. "Come on. Gates close at dusk, and I'd rather not explain to the harbor watch why we smell like salt flats and bad decisions."

* * *

The gate guards barely looked at them. That was the first thing Wren always noticed about Grayharbor — how little anyone looked at anything. In Saltmere, eyes slid and darted; everyone watched everyone because watching was survival. Here, nobody watched at all. The guards waved them through with the bored efficiency of men who had long ago concluded that nothing worth finding could possibly enter their city.

Inside, the streets were broad and cobbled and swept. Lamplighters worked the early evening rounds with brass poles, lighting lamps that burned steady and white and utterly without flicker. Wren watched Edmund notice that.

"The flames don't move," Edmund murmured.

"Grayharbor oil," Wren said. "Refined to burn flat. A flame that dances is 'unregulated,' according to the municipal codes. Section twelve."

"You're joking."

"I never joke about municipal codes. They're the only honest literature this city produces."

They walked. The market district was prosperous in the way a well-fed animal is healthy — glossy, groomed, dead-eyed. Merchants sold glassware and dyed cloth and clockwork toys wound tight enough to walk three steps before collapsing. Children played a game with chalk lines and stones, and when one of them laughed, the sound rang out strangely clear, because there was nothing else in the air to soften it.

Odile walked close to Edmund, her hood up, her hands buried in her sleeves. Wren had noticed over the past days that Odile's gift leaked. Where she stood too long, the moss between cobbles greened. Dead window-boxes leaned toward her like flowers toward sun. She'd learned to keep moving, to spread herself thin across the city so no single patch of street betrayed her.

Here, it was worse than anywhere. Because here, nothing answered her.

"Do you feel that?" Odile said quietly, as they crossed into the old quarter.

"No," said Edmund. Then, honestly: "Yes. It's like walking into a room where music stopped just before you arrived."

"It didn't stop," Wren said. "That's the thing people get wrong about Grayharbor. They think the magic quarter burned down, or faded, or got taxed out of existence. It didn't. It was cut." She turned down a narrow street where the buildings pressed close overhead, leaning toward each other like conspirators. "Seventy years ago, give or take. The Council of Nine decided that wonder was a fire hazard. So they amputated."

The street ended at a wall.

Not a ruined wall, not a collapsed one — a built wall. Fresh mortar over old stone, capped with iron, stamped every few feet with the seal of the city: nine interlocked rings. It ran the width of the street and up the sides of the buildings on either side, sealing doorways, bricking over windows, swallowing whatever had been here before.

Beyond it, Wren knew, lay six blocks of the old Glasswright Quarter, sealed since before her grandmother was born. Nobody went in. Nobody maintained it. And yet — and this was the detail that had kept Wren awake on her second visit, the detail she'd checked three times against the Consortium's own archives — the wall needed no maintenance. Seventy years, and not a single stone had shifted. Not a single weed had grown through the mortar. Nothing rotted inside there, and nothing grew.

Because the quarter itself was still holding its breath.

"They walled it up with everything inside," Edmund said softly. "People?"

"Some people. Some who wouldn't stop. Most of the gifted had already gone by then — fled east, or south, or underground. But some stayed. Believed the Council's promise that registration meant protection." Wren shrugged, and hated how casual she could make her voice sound when the subject deserved anything but. "The wall went up on a Tuesday. By Friday, the city records show the quarter's tax assessment dropped to zero. By Sunday, nobody mentioned it again."

"That's seventy years," Odile said. "Nothing grows for seventy years?"

"Nothing dies, either. Officially." Wren tapped the wall with one knuckle. The sound it made was wrong — dense, resonant, like tapping the hull of a ship rather than masonry. "There are stories. Dockworkers swear that on windless nights you can hear singing from inside. The Council calls it settling stone. My employers call it an asset, frozen in escrow." Her mouth twisted. "I call it the ugliest thing money ever bought."

Edmund laid a palm flat against the wall. Wren watched their face do something complicated — the cartographer reading a map that wasn't drawn on paper. Their fingers spread, pressing hard, and for a moment their expression went somewhere far away.

"What do you feel?" Wren asked.

"Streets," Edmund whispered. "Underneath. The grid is still there. Doorways. A fountain square. And something else—" Their hand flinched back as if the wall had bitten. "Something waiting. Like a held note that's been held so long the singer forgot there was ever a song."

Odile pulled Edmund's arm away from the wall, gentle but firm. "Don't touch things here," she said. "Not here."

But Wren saw it — the look that passed over Edmund's face as they stepped back. Not fear. Recognition. The look of a person seeing their own future sketched in brick and iron.

* * *

They took rooms above a chandlery in the merchant quarter — Wren paid in Consortium scrip, which opened doors in Grayharbor faster than coin opened them elsewhere — and Wren spent the evening doing what she did best: listening.

She came back to the room near midnight and found Edmund sitting by the window, watching the flat white flames of the streetlamps burn their motionless vigil below.

"Any trouble?" Edmund asked without turning.

"None. This city doesn't have trouble. It has incidents, and they're all insured." She dropped onto the other bed, boots off, and studied the cartographer's silhouette. "You want to know what I found out. Ask."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to sit there radiating questions until I volunteered. You've been doing it for three days. It's very effective and I resent it." She leaned her head back against the wall. "The Council of Nine still meets. Still rules. And every year, on the anniversary of the Sealing, they hold a public ceremony. Bells, banners, speeches about prosperity and order. They call it the Festival of Quiet."

"The Festival of Quiet," Edmund repeated.

"Sixty thousand people attend voluntarily. There's cake."

Edmund almost smiled. Almost. "And the gifted? The ones born here now? What happens to them?"

"That's the part I found interesting." Wren sat forward. "Officially, Grayharbor has none. Seventy years, zero registered gifts. Unofficially — and this comes from a midwife I talked to tonight, who talks to everyone and is paid by no one — children here are born different sometimes. Little things. A baby whose cries hush storms. A boy who mends broken cups by holding them. The families don't report it. They can't hide it either, not really. So they do what this city has always done."

"Which is?"

"They take the child to the wall."

Wren let the silence carry it. Edmund had gone very still.

"There's a gap in the patrol route along the north face," she continued. "A loose stone. Families leave offerings there — bread, small clothes, toys. The midwife told me the offerings vanish. Every single time. And the Council tells everyone the rats take them, and everyone believes it, because believing it is easier than the alternative."

"Which is that someone inside is alive," Edmund said. "Still. After seventy years."

"Or that the wall remembers what was put inside it." Wren rubbed her eyes. "Either way, Edmund — here's what I know about your possible futures. I've seen the one where you claim the Salt. I've read the Consortium's projections on what a living warden could do, and believe me, they dream about it in spreadsheets. But this—" she gestured at the window, at the motionless city beyond, "—this is the other one. The one where wonder loses. And the terrible thing, the thing I can't stop chewing on, is that it works. Nobody starves here. Nobody burns. Nobody gets dragged from their bed by sniffer-beasts. Grayharbor solved the problem of magic the way you solve a rotten tooth."

"You pull it," Edmund said quietly.

"And the whole mouth learns to chew around the gap. And after a while, nobody remembers what biting was for." She looked at them squarely. "So tell me, cartographer. When the thirteenth turn comes and the Salt offers you everything — is this what you're afraid of becoming? Or is this what you're afraid of wanting?"

Edmund didn't answer. Below the window, the lamplighter made his final round, and every flame he lit burned straight and white and perfectly, perfectly still.

* * *

They stayed three days, because Wren insisted they rest and resupply and because moving fast drew eyes. On the third day, she took them to see the harbor itself — the reason for the city's name and wealth. It was magnificent. Two hundred ships at anchor, cranes swinging cargo, money changing hands in seventeen currencies. The most prosperous port on the eastern coast, run entirely on ledgers, contracts, and the absolute conviction that nothing existed that couldn't be priced.

And then, as they walked the quay, Odile stopped.

Wren turned. The orchard-keeper was staring at the water below the pilings, where the tide lapped against stone dark with wet. And in the lee of a bollard, sheltered from foot traffic, a single flower had opened — a small white thing, salt-burned and improbable, growing straight out of a crack in the granite.

Odile hadn't touched it. Wren was sure of it. She'd been five steps away.

But the flower was turning, slowly, on its stem. Following Odile like a sunflower follows the sun.

"Odile," Wren breathed. "Move."

"I'm not doing it."

"I know. That's worse. Move anyway—"

"Look around," Odile said, low and urgent. "Look at the people."

Wren looked. And her stomach dropped, because the dockworkers nearest them — big men hauling rope, women tallying crates — had all gone quiet. Not alarmed. Not hostile. Just... attentive, the way animals go attentive. Heads turning by degrees. Eyes tracking Odile with a soft, unfocused hunger, like sleepers hearing a voice they knew from somewhere long ago.

One of them, an old man with rope-scarred hands, took a step toward her. Then another. His face was wet.

"My mother," he said, to no one, to Odile, to the flower. "My mother used to sing like that."

"I don't sing," Odile said carefully.

"No," the man agreed, weeping freely now, smiling. "No, but I remember it anyway."

Around the quay, more heads were turning. A woman set down her ledger. A child stopped mid-game. Thirty people, fifty, drifting inward with the slow tide-pull of a dream, and Wren's hand found her knife without her deciding it should, and Edmund grabbed Odile's arm—

—and the bell in the harbor tower began to ring.

Not the hour bell. The alarm pattern — three strikes, pause, three strikes. Somewhere in the city, something had been noticed. Something had been measured. And the soft attention on the quay shattered all at once, the crowd blinking awake like divers surfacing, confused, embarrassed, already explaining themselves to each other ("just felt dizzy," "stood up too fast," "what were we—").

Wren moved. "North gate. Now. Don't run — running is memorable. Walk like you're bored."

They walked. Behind them, watchmen were converging on the quay, and above the rooftops Wren could see them heading not toward the crowd but toward the flower — four of them, careful, gloved, carrying an iron box between them like men transporting a live coal.

Toward the flower that had bloomed in the shape of Odile's passing.

At the north gate, breath held, papers forged, Wren risked one look back at the city of quiet flames and sealed streets. Edmund was staring at the wall of the Glasswright Quarter, visible even from here, running away into the distance like a scar.

"Now you've seen it," Wren said. "Both futures. The one where you take the Salt, and the one where the world takes it from everyone. Prosperity or the wall. Those are the choices on the table."

"No," Edmund said. Their voice was strange — steady in a way Wren hadn't heard before, and it frightened her more than any panic would have. "Those are the choices *they* put on the table. Vess puts up walls. The Consortium writes projections. The Council of Nine throws festivals." They turned to her, and behind their eyes something vast and patient was waking up, and Wren thought, with a professional's cold clarity: *there it is.* "I'm done choosing between other people's options."

The north gate closed behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released.

And far behind, muffled by stone and seventy years of silence, Wren would have sworn — though she never reported it, never entered it in any ledger — that something on the other side of the wall began, very faintly, to sing.
