## 17. Charms for Strangers

The room above the chandler's shop on Tallow Row cost Wren three silver and a lie about how long they'd be staying. It had one window, a bed that sagged like a tired horse, and a view of Grayharbor's rooftops going gold in the late afternoon — a city of slate and smoke where nothing, Edmund had decided within the first hour, was allowed to be beautiful on purpose.

They had been there four days. Four days of keeping the shutters drawn, of speaking in whispers, of Odile pacing the floorboards with the restless stride of someone who had spent her whole life outdoors and now couldn't stand still indoors without feeling caged.

And four days of Edmund listening to the building weep.

That was what it was, though they'd never had a word for it before. Every house had a voice if you knew how to listen — not sound, exactly, but pressure. The groan of joists settling was just the body of the house talking to itself. And this one talked constantly, in the low anxious register of a structure that had been patched too many times by people who couldn't afford to do it properly. The third-floor beam over the stairwell was carrying weight it was never meant to carry. The chimney drew badly. Somewhere below them, in the chandler's own rooms, a wall had been breathing damp for years, and no one had ever thought to ask it to stop.

Edmund lay on the sagging bed with their hands behind their head and did none of those things. That was the discipline. Twelve years of discipline. You didn't fix the beam because fixing the beam meant reaching into the house's bones with something that wasn't your hands, and anything that wasn't your hands could be seen — not by neighbors, but by whatever else was out there looking. Inquisitors. Sniffer-beasts. The whole apparatus of a kingdom that had spent a generation teaching itself that wonder was a disease.

So they lay there and listened to the house suffer, and said nothing to it, and hated themselves a little more each day.

"You're doing it again," Odile said from the window, where she stood watching the street through a gap in the shutter the way she'd been watching it all afternoon.

"Doing what?"

"That thing with your face. Where you look like you're at a funeral for something nobody else can see."

"I'm resting."

"You're *listening*." She turned. In the slanted light her face was all sharp planes and suspicion, the orchard-keeper's squint she'd never lost even after years away from the orchard. "You've been listening since we got here. To the walls, the pipes, I don't know what. And you're going to keep listening until you can't stand it anymore, and then you're going to do something stupid."

"I haven't done anything stupid in four days."

"You once opened a Veilroad in a root cellar because a spider looked lonely."

"It was *trapped*, Odile. It was going to starve."

"It was a spider." She came away from the window and sat on the edge of the bed, and some of the sharpness went out of her, replaced by the older, wearier thing underneath. "Edmund. Listen to me. We're here because Seraphine Dax let us go once, and I don't believe for one moment she'll do it twice. Vess has half the Inquisition sweeping the coast roads. The Consortium knows we're in Grayharbor — Wren won't say how she knows, which means she knows very well indeed. We are two names on a list, hiding in a city that cut its own magic out of itself a hundred years ago and salted the wound." She took their hand. Hers was rough as bark. "You cannot fix the beam."

"I didn't say anything about the beam."

"You were going to. You've been going to for four days. I know that look. Grandmother had it too, every time someone in the village came to her with a sick goat or a bad harvest or a baby that wouldn't stop crying. She'd listen, and she'd ache, and then she'd do the small thing, the quiet thing, the thing that couldn't possibly hurt anyone." Odile's grip tightened. "And then the Purge came anyway."

* * *

It was the bread that broke him.

On the fifth morning, Edmund went down for water and found the chandler's wife, Marta, sitting at her kitchen table with her head in her hands. She was a broad, gray-streaked woman with wax burns up both forearms and the permanent stoop of someone who'd spent decades ducking under low beams. On the table before her sat three loaves, and even Edmund could see they were wrong — dense, gray-crusted, the crumb collapsed in on itself like something that had given up.

"The miller's flour," Marta said, when she saw him looking. She said it defensively, as if he'd accused her. "Third bad batch this season. Half the city's eating this. My Tam's got a gut like a knotted rope, and he's six." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, angrily, the way working women cry when crying is a luxury. "Six years old. He asks me why the bread hurts him and I don't have an answer that isn't *because the world is getting worse and nobody's coming to fix it*."

Edmund stood in her kitchen with the water jug in his hands and felt the old pull open in his chest like a door he'd nailed shut a thousand times.

He could taste the loaf from across the table. Not the flour — the *pattern*. Everything made by human hands carried the shape of its making, and this bread carried the shape of bad grain ground in a worn stone and baked in a hurry by a woman who'd stopped hoping somewhere around the second failed batch. It would take almost nothing. A breath of attention. A nudge at the pattern, the way you'd straighten a crooked picture frame. The yeast would rise true, the crumb would hold, the gut-knotting bitterness would smooth into plain honest food. No one would ever know. Bread was the least suspicious miracle in the world.

*"You cannot fix the beam."*

"Bad luck," Edmund said, and filled the jug, and went back upstairs, and sat on the bed with his hands shaking until Odile asked him what was wrong and he said *nothing* and she looked at him for a long time and, mercifully, said nothing either.

He lasted until evening.

Marta kept her old loaves in a crock by the stove, planning, he gathered, to soften them in broth rather than waste them. When she went out to the tallow shed, Edmund stood in her kitchen alone with the crock, and told himself he was only looking, and put both hands flat on the rim of the crock, and gave.

It was — gods, it was like unclenching a fist he'd been holding shut for twelve years. The pattern of the bread yielded to him the way a stiff lock yields to the right key, and warmth ran up his arms and settled behind his sternum like a coal, and for three seconds he was nineteen years old again, standing in his grandmother's kitchen while she showed him how to listen to dough rising, saying *the gift isn't in the doing, love, it's in the noticing; anyone can force a thing, but a warden asks first.*

The loaves came out of the pattern remade. Dense no longer. Whole. When Marta came back in, Edmund was upstairs, heart hammering, telling himself it was nothing, a drop in an ocean, invisible, harmless.

By noon the next day, Marta had brought up a plate of the good bread and a look of bewildered joy she tried to hide and couldn't, and said the strangest thing had happened, the old loaves had come right overnight, must have been the cool weather, and hadn't that been lucky, and would he like some?

*Lucky.* Odile's whole life, built on that word. Making the gift look like luck so thoroughly that even the beneficiaries believed it.

Edmund ate the bread. It tasted like forgiveness. That was the problem. It tasted like forgiveness, and he wanted more.

* * *

It went the way these things always go, which is to say: quickly, and with excellent reasons.

The lamp in the stairwell guttered every night because the wick was cheap and the draft was cruel, and old Hollis the chandler climbed those stairs in the dark with hands gone unsteady from age, and one night Edmund touched the lamp in passing and it simply — stayed lit. Steady as a held breath. Hollis noticed. Hollis told his wife. His wife told Marta it was the strangest thing, that lamp hadn't burned steady in twenty years.

The girl on the second floor — Pip, nine years old, all elbows and questions — had a cough that rattled her thin chest all through the cold snap, and her mother sat up with her nights, and Edmund heard it through the floorboards, that wet broken-bell sound, and on the fourth night he pressed his palm flat against the floor and sent the smallest thread of warmth down through the wood, just enough to ease the child's lungs, less than a candle's worth, less than a prayer. The cough broke two days later. Her mother told everyone the spring air had done it.

A widow on Tallow Row whose husband had drowned in the harbor: Edmund left a charm on her doorstep, a knot of braided cord he'd worked at midnight with his eyes closed, that would hold grief at arm's length — not erase it, never that, just keep it from crushing — and she wore it and slept for the first time in months and told the fishwife next door she didn't know what had come over her.

A boy with a stammer. A roof that leaked over a sickbed. A dog gone mad with fear of thunder. Each one small. Each one deniable. Each one, Edmund told himself, a drop, a breath, less than a candle, invisible.

Odile watched it happen the way you watch a friend drink.

She didn't say anything for six days. That was its own kind of eloquence. But on the seventh evening she came up the stairs quietly, closed the door, and stood looking at him where he sat at the little table finishing a charm — a simple one, a button-sized disc of gray clay that would keep moths from wool, for the widow with the good shawl — and her face was the face she'd worn at their grandmother's funeral, holding everything back by main strength.

"How many?" she said.

"Odile—"

"*How many?*"

He set down the clay. "Eleven," he said. "Twelve, counting the dog."

"Twelve miracles in seven days, in a city with more ears than people." She pulled the other chair around and sat facing him, knees nearly touching, close enough that he could see the exhaustion under her anger. "Do you know what I did today? I walked to the fish market, and I heard a woman telling another woman about a lamp that never goes out on Tallow Row. And the other woman laughed and said *someone ought to write to the Chronicle*, and the first woman said *no, seriously, Maren swears it*, and they stood there in the fish smell wondering. Wondering, Edmund. Wondering is how it starts. Wondering becomes talking, talking becomes a story, a story reaches someone who writes letters to people who pay for stories, and then one morning a man with gray gloves walks down Tallow Row and knocks on doors and asks cheerful questions about lamps and bread and coughs, and everyone answers, because they're proud of their luck, they want to share it—" She stopped. Breathed. "I burned our grandmother's letters to keep this from happening. Do you remember that? I sat in the orchard and fed forty years of her words into the fire, and kept one, and that one has cost me everything, and you are spending the same inheritance on *moth charms*."

"They're not moth charms," Edmund said quietly. "They're people. Marta's son eats without pain. Pip sleeps through the night. The widow on the corner doesn't wake up drowning anymore. You want me to apologize for that?"

"No," Odile said. "Gods, no. I want you to understand that I would do exactly the same, and that's precisely what frightens me." She reached out and took his hands, both of them, and held them in her lap like something rescued from a fire. "We are the same, you and I. Grandmother made sure of it. She taught us both that the gift exists to be given away — that hoarding it is the sin, that a warden who keeps their hands closed is no warden at all. And she was right. And it will kill us. Because the world we live in has made kindness contraband, and you—" her voice cracked, and steadied, "—you cannot stop being kind. It's the best thing about you and it is going to get us killed, and I will help you do it anyway, because the alternative is watching you become someone who *can* stop, and I'd rather die."

Edmund looked down at their hands in hers. Behind his sternum, the coal still glowed. He thought about the mirror in the vault beneath the drowned abbey, the tyrant's face wearing his own features, smiling. He thought about how it had started for that man, whoever he'd been. Not with armies. Not with thrones. Probably with a lamp. A lamp that never guttered, for someone who needed light. And then another. And then the slow discovery that giving felt like godhood, and godhood felt like purpose, and purpose felt like destiny, and destiny whispered *more*.

"I'll stop," he said.

"You won't."

"I'll be careful."

"You'll be careful," she agreed bitterly, "the way a river is careful."

He laughed despite himself, and she didn't, and then she did, briefly, hopelessly, the shared laugh of siblings who have survived the same childhood and know the argument is already lost. She squeezed his hands once, hard, and stood, and went to the window, and stood watch over the darkening street while he sat at the table and — because he was who he was — finished the moth charm.

Below them, Tallow Row settled into evening. Lamps were lit in windows. In one of them, a lamp that never guttered burned steady over the Hollis family's supper, and Marta set a loaf on her table that would feed her son without pain, and a widow fingered a braided cord and slept without drowning, and none of them knew they were candles in a window seen from a very great distance.

None of them knew that in a rented room near the harbor, a man in gray gloves was reading a letter by lamplight — a letter from a Grayharbor correspondent who dealt in gossip the way others dealt in fish, cheaply and in bulk — and that the letter contained, among items of no consequence, a single line:

*Tallow Row is lucky lately. Lamp that won't die, bread that comes right, a child cured. The locals are calling it a blessing. Some are asking who sent it.*

The man in gray gloves read the line twice. Then he rang a small brass bell, and told the servant who appeared: "Find out everything about a street called Tallow Row. Quietly. And send word to Chancellor Vess that I may have found his cartographer."

Upstairs, Edmund blew out his candle, and slept, and dreamed of bread.
