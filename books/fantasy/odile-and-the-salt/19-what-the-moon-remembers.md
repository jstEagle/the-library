## 19. What the Moon Remembers

The observatory had been a place of learning once, before the Sundering War made learning a kind of treason. Now it was a broken crown of stone on a hill above the salt flats, its dome split open like an egg, its brass instruments rusted into sculpture. Odile had known about it since childhood — their grandmother had drawn it in the margins of her letters, a little circle with rays, and written beneath it in her cramped hand: *when you need to understand the sky, go where the sky was understood.*

She understood now why Edmund hadn't come here first. Her brother — she still caught herself thinking *sister*, twelve years of habit wearing thin as old cloth — saw the world as roads and distances. Odile had always seen it as seasons. So while Edmund slept off the road in the shelter of the fallen dome, wrapped in a threadbare blanket with Wren standing watch at the broken doorway, Odile climbed the spiral stair into what remained of the observation deck.

The moon followed her up.

It hung low and swollen over the eastern flats, three-quarters full, and when she looked at it directly something turned over in her chest — that deep, tidal pull she'd felt all her life and had spent thirty years pretending was indigestion or grief or a weak heart. The orchard-gift answered it. She could feel the moss on the ruined stones quickening where she stood, tiny green threads uncurling in the frost, reaching toward her boots like roots toward water.

"Stop that," she murmured to it. To herself.

But the moon kept pulling.

* * *

The star-charts were exactly where grandmother's letter said they would be: in a lead-lined chest beneath the floor of the astronomer's chamber, sealed with wax stamped by the warden's sigil — the same sigil that had been pressed into Wren's old coin, the same one Odile had burned into memory from the letters she'd almost destroyed. The chest weighed nothing when she lifted it, which should have frightened her more than it did. Lead-lined chests did not weigh nothing. Magic paid for its conveniences somewhere; grandmother had taught her that much before the Purge took everything else.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay forty years of sky.

The charts were vellum, hand-inked, each one a month of the heavens rendered in gold leaf and iron-gall ink. Grandmother's work — Odile recognized the small, impatient asterisks she used to mark errors, the way she drew stars as flowers rather than points because, she'd once written to Odile, *a star is not a pinhole, child, it is a blooming*. There were marginal notes in three hands: grandmother's, and two older ones, faded brown, going back generations. Warden after warden after warden, each adding their observations, each passing the record down like a relay baton through the dark.

Odile carried the chest to the edge of the collapsed dome, where moonlight fell clean, and began to read.

* * *

It took her until well past midnight to find the pattern, and once she found it she wished she hadn't.

The notes were all about the moon. Turn after turn, year after year, the wardens had tracked not just its phases but its *behavior* — how it sat heavier in the sky some months than others, how its light seemed to lean, the way a plant leans toward a window. And every twelfth turn, the annotations changed hands. A different script. Tighter. Frightened, even in faded ink.

*The twelfth turn closes. It sleeps again.*
*Twelve turns done. We are safe for another cycle. May the thirteenth never come.*
*It leaned toward the north tonight. I felt my gift strain like a dog on a leash.*

And then, in grandmother's hand, dated the winter before the Purge — the last winter of the old world:

*Edmund was born under the ninth turn. Odile under the second. If the counting holds, the inheritance wakes fully in their lifetime. I have hidden the charts where only a warden would look. Forgive me for leaving you arithmetic instead of answers. But the answer has never been a thing you find. It is a thing you count.*

Odile sat back on her heels among the frost-bloomed moss, her breath steaming, and counted.

Grandmother had built a wheel diagram on the final chart — concentric rings of thirteen segments, each ring a cycle, each segment a turn of the moon. Twelve segments marked in silver. The thirteenth left blank, waiting. At the center, a single line: *When the thirteenth fills, the bell rings whole.*

Odile worked backward from there, cross-checking against the current sky, against the swollen moon leaning over the flats right now, visibly, unmistakably leaning — southward, toward the hills, toward *them*. She counted turns the way she counted frost-days before planting, the way she counted anything, patiently and twice.

Eleven turns closed. They were living inside the twelfth.

Six weeks. Give or take the length of a breath. At winter solstice, the moon would complete its thirteenth turn, and the blank segment would fill.

* * *

She must have made a sound, because Wren's voice came up the stairwell, low and wary. "Trouble?"

"Come see."

Wren climbed into the ruin with her knife already loose in its sheath, which Odile was learning was simply how Wren entered rooms. She glanced at the charts spread across the stones, at the moon, at Odile's face, and something in her courier's composure flickered. "That bad?"

"Depends." Odile tapped the wheel diagram. "Do you know what happens at solstice?"

"I know Vess wants whatever your family is hiding before then. He's moved half the Inquisition to the eastern provinces. That kind of want."

"The Salt isn't hiding." Odile heard her own voice go flat, the way it did when the orchard needed hard pruning. "The Salt is *waking*. It's been waking for eleven turns. And when the thirteenth completes—" She stopped. Started again, slower, because saying it aloud made it real in a way the ink hadn't quite managed. "Grandmother called it a bell. Every gifted person alive is going to hear it ring. Not the strong ones. Not the registered ones. *Everyone.* Every hedge-witch who's been hiding a green thumb. Every boy who can find lost things. Every child nobody's told yet. The call won't ask permission. It'll pull them — toward whoever holds the inheritance."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Below them, faint through the broken dome, came the sound of Edmund turning in their sleep, muttering maps to no one.

"Toward your sibling," Wren said.

"Toward Edmund." Odile looked out at the flats, white as bone under the moon. "Unless Edmund learns to hold it. Or unless someone takes it first. Or unless—" She didn't finish that. The third option lived in the letter too, in grandmother's careful euphemism about what the last warden had done and what it had cost the world. Some inheritances were better buried. She just no longer believed they could be.

"And the gifted," Wren said slowly. "The pulled ones. What happens to people who hear a bell like that and start walking? Mindless?"

"I don't know. Maybe they arrive and kneel. Maybe they arrive and burn everything between them and the door." Odile gathered the charts with hands that were steady, because her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook. "Either way, it won't be six people around a campfire arguing about destiny. It'll be a pilgrimage. Thousands. Maybe tens of thousands. Every soul the kingdoms ever made afraid of themselves, walking toward my brother at the same hour."

Wren exhaled through her teeth. "Vess doesn't need to win the race, then. He just needs to be standing next to Edmund when the bell rings, holding a leash he's had time to fit."

"Now you're counting too."

"It's what couriers do." But Wren wasn't looking at the charts anymore. She was looking east, where the moon leaned, and her face had gone strange — and Odile realized, watching her, that Wren Ashgrave had never once said whether she was gifted or not, and that the coin she'd paid with bore a warden's sigil, and that some questions deferred have a way of arriving anyway. Not tonight, though. Tonight there was arithmetic enough.

* * *

Odile went down the stair with the charts bundled against her chest like an infant, and found Edmund awake, sitting up in the dark with their eyes too bright. Gifted eyes. Waking eyes. She could see the Salt's tide in them now that she knew to look — a faint luminescence, like moonlight caught in shallow water.

"You've been reading the sky all night," Edmund said. "I felt you doing it. Like hearing someone turn pages in another room." They paused. "Tell me."

So Odile knelt in the frost beside her sibling and told them. About the wheel. The twelve turns. The blank thirteenth. Six weeks. The bell, and everyone it would call, and what they would become if they arrived and found no one worthy — or found someone *too* worthy — standing at the center of the sound.

Edmund listened without moving. When she finished, they were silent a long moment, and then they laughed — one short, cracked note with no humor in it at all.

"I thought we were fleeing *toward* answers," they said. "Turns out we're fleeing on a schedule."

"We have six weeks."

"We have six weeks, an Inquisitor who knows my face, a Consortium that knows your face, a Chancellor who apparently knows *everything*, and a growing suspicion that the answer involves me becoming something we swore our whole lives never to become." Edmund looked up at the broken dome, at the sliver of leaning moon visible through the gap. Their voice dropped. "Odile. Last night I dreamed I could see every Veilroad on the continent at once. All of them. Every door between every realm, lit up like veins. And in the dream it felt *good*."

Odile took her brother's hand. It was cold, and under the cold it hummed, very faintly, like the charm that had started all this — like the charm that hummed when touched.

"Then we have six weeks to decide what good means," she said. "Before the moon decides for us."

Above the ruined observatory, the moon leaned another degree south, patient as frost, and somewhere far below the salt flats, Odile could have sworn she felt something ancient roll over in its sleep — and smile.
