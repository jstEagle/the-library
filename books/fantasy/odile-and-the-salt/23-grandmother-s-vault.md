## 23. Grandmother's Vault

The drowned abbey announced itself as a smell before it was a sight: wet stone and old incense, the ghost of a thousand years of candle smoke soaked into rock that had been underwater longer than Odile had been alive.

"It's not supposed to be here," Edmund said, for the third time since dawn.

"Neither are we," Odile said. "Keep walking."

They had come down out of the hills two days after the observatory, following the letter in a way that made no sense on any map Odile had ever seen — which, she was learning, was rather the point. The letters didn't describe roads. They described *relationships*. Stand where the river remembers being a mountain. Walk until the water forgets your name. Turn toward the grief.

She'd thought it was poetry. It turned out to be navigation.

The abbey rose out of a valley that shouldn't have held it — a sprawl of black stone, half-swallowed by a lake that lay perfectly still around its walls like a held breath. No birds. No insects. The water was so clear it looked less like water than like glass someone had laid over a sunken world, and beneath it Odile could see roofs, arches, a bell tower leaning at an angle no honest tower would choose.

"The Sundering flooded this whole region," Edmund said. Their voice had gone thin and scholarly, which Odile recognized now as their fear-voice. They'd been doing it their whole life; she'd just never known what it was for. "The histories say the war broke the aquifers. Whole cities went under in a night."

"And grandmother sent us to one."

"She sent us to the one place Vess will never think to look."

Odile stopped at the waterline and looked at her brother — her sibling, she corrected herself, because twelve years of absence had sanded down some certainties and left others strangely sharp — and felt the old fury rise again, and set it down again, deliberately, the way you set down a heavy load you'll need to carry later.

They had fought at the warden-gate. Fists first, then words, then worse than words. But somewhere between the gate and the observatory, between the star-charts and the six-week clock ticking overhead, they had become something else. Not healed. Nothing about them was healed. But aligned, the way two broken halves of a seal can still make an impression.

"You're afraid," she said.

"I'm always afraid. It's a professional condition." Edmund crouched at the lake's edge and touched the water with two fingers, the way they touched everything — like a man checking whether a floor would hold. Then they went very still.

"What?"

"There's a road under there," Edmund said quietly. "A Veilroad. It runs straight through the abbey. Grandmother didn't hide the vault from the world. She hid it *between* worlds."

* * *

The way down was through the bell tower, which leaned into the lake at precisely the angle the letter had drawn it, and the door at its base opened onto stairs that descended into dry air — dry air, impossibly, below the waterline, the lake held back by nothing Odile could see except a faint shimmering, like heat off summer stone, that ran along the ceiling of every passage.

Her gift woke as they descended.

It always did this now, and she had stopped pretending it didn't. Where she walked, things grew. In the orchard it had been a curse disguised as luck — blossoms out of season, fruit on dead wood, enough to feed them but never enough to explain. Down here, in the dark, it was different. Moss bloomed green along the stones behind her in a trail like a written signature. Small pale fungi lifted their caps as she passed, turning toward her the way flowers turn toward sun. She was a walking springtime in a place that had been dead for three hundred years, and she could not turn it off.

Edmund kept glancing back at the glowing trail.

"Don't," Odile said.

"I wasn't—"

"You were going to say it's beautiful. Everyone says it's beautiful. Nobody says what it actually is, which is a beacon. If Seraphine Dax gets within ten miles of this abbey, she'll find me the way a moth finds a lamp."

Edmund was quiet for a few steps. Then: "That's why she let us go. In Grayharbor. You know that, don't you? She had us. And she looked at me and — I don't know how to describe it. Like recognizing a song you haven't heard since childhood."

"Then she's a fool."

"Then she's *like us*," Edmund said. "Which is worse. For her."

The stairs ended at a door.

It was small — smaller than any door in the abbey above, sized for someone slight, someone like their grandmother must have been. Black wood gone silver with age, and carved across its face in lines worn nearly smooth: the sigil from the old coin Wren Ashgrave had paid with. The warden's mark. A circle crossed by a river.

Odile had carried the last letter against her skin for weeks now, wrapped in oilcloth inside her shirt, and it had grown warm as they descended, warm as a sleeping bird. Now it was almost hot. She took it out, and the paper seemed to lean toward the door.

"She knew we'd come," Edmund said. "She knew *when*. That's the part I can't—" They pressed the heel of their hand against their eye. "Twelve years ago. Maybe twenty. She wrote instructions to a door she knew we'd reach days before the solstice, and she's been dead for nine of those years, and the letter is *warm*, Odile."

"Then she's not done talking to us," Odile said, and pushed the door open.

* * *

The vault was round, and it was lit.

Not by torches. By the walls themselves — veins of pale light running through the black stone like sap through wood, brightening as they entered, dimming behind them, following their movement the way her moss followed her feet. The room breathed light. Odile had the sudden, disorienting certainty that the vault was not a room at all but something alive, something grown rather than built, and that it had been waiting so long it had nearly forgotten how to be patient.

Regalia lined the near wall.

Robes first — seven sets, hung on stands of white bone or something like it, ranging from cloth stiff with antiquity to a grey traveling coat that might have been thirty years old. Crowns next, if you could call them crowns: circlets, bands, a single ring mounted on a stand like it was the most dangerous object in the room. Instruments whose purposes Odile could guess at and did not want to. A staff. A bell. A knife with a blade like frozen water.

And above them all, worked into the stone in silver wire, the same sigil repeated — circle, river, circle, river — dozens of times, each repetition slightly different, each one signed with a name.

"Read them," Edmund whispered.

Odile read. The names were in old scripts, some of them scripts she didn't know, but the newest were legible, and the newest of all was in her grandmother's hand, sharp and slanted:

*Maren Villalobos, Warden of the Salt, Keeper of the Thirteenth Turn.*

Below it, in the same hand, smaller:

*If you are reading this wall, then my grandchildren have found their way home. Good. Stubborn children. I hoped for nothing less.*

Odile laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised her more than the laughing itself. Grief did that sometimes. It waited years and then ambushed you in a hole in the ground.

"She's insufferable," Edmund said, and their voice broke on it, and they stood there in the breathing light with tears running freely down their face, and Odile put her hand on their shoulder and let them cry without saying anything, because there was nothing to say that the wall hadn't already said better.

When Edmund had mastered themselves, they turned to the far side of the chamber — and stopped.

"Oh," Edmund said. "Oh, no."

* * *

The mirror stood alone in an alcove at the vault's far end, framed in salt-white stone, taller than a person, its surface not silvered glass but something darker and deeper — polished obsidian, maybe, or still water standing upright. It did not reflect the room. It did not reflect them.

It reflected other people.

Figures moved in the dark glass like fish under ice. Odile counted a dozen before she stopped counting — men and women in the regalia from the wall, standing in poses formal and informal, some smiling, some stern, one ancient woman laughing at something outside the frame. Each figure wore the sigil. Each face, when Odile looked closely, carried a family resemblance — cheekbones, a set of the jaw, something around the eyes.

Their bloodline. All of them. Going back further than names went.

"It shows the holders," Edmund said. Their voice had gone flat and careful, the scholar-voice stretched to breaking. "Every warden. Grandmother's letters mentioned it — 'the mirror keeps the roll,' she called it. I thought it meant a list."

"It's a list," Odile said. "It's just alive."

She stepped closer. The figures watched her. Not moving to follow her — they couldn't; they were memories, or echoes, or whatever a mirror kept when it kept people — but watching, the way portraits watch, except these portraits had opinions, and most of the opinions looked like warnings.

One figure stood apart from the others.

He was positioned at the edge of the glass, half-turned away, as if even the mirror had tried to put him in a corner. He wore the regalia too — a version of it heavier than the rest, robes weighted with more silver than cloth, the crown less a circlet than a cage of metal around his brow. He was tall. He had their jaw. He had their hands.

And he was looking directly out of the mirror, directly at Edmund, and he was smiling.

"Edmund," Odile said carefully. "Don't."

But Edmund had already stepped forward, the way you step toward a precipice, the way you step toward anything that has your name in its mouth. The light-veins in the walls dimmed. The mirror's surface rippled once, like the skin of milk, and settled.

"That's him," Edmund said. "From the histories. The last warden before the Sundering. Aurelian." They said the name the way you'd handle a snake. "He's the reason the war happened. He's the reason magic is illegal. He's the reason we grew up hiding."

"He's the reason grandmother hid *us*," Odile said.

The figure in the glass tilted his head. It was such a human gesture, such a familiar gesture — Edmund did exactly that when they were listening to something no one else could hear — that Odile's skin crawled.

And then the mirror changed.

The other figures faded. Aurelian came forward in the dark glass, or the dark glass came forward around him, and suddenly he filled the frame, life-sized, close enough to touch, and Odile saw with terrible clarity that he was not a memory at all. Memories didn't blink. This man blinked. This man breathed. This man looked at her brother with an expression of recognition so warm, so *hungry*, that Odile grabbed Edmund's arm and pulled.

Because the face looking out of the mirror was Edmund's face.

Not similar. Not a family resemblance. Identical. The same narrow chin, the same tired eyes, the same way of holding the mouth as though perpetually about to apologize. Aurelian wore a crown and centuries and a smile, and underneath all of it he was wearing Edmund, the way a glove wears a hand.

"He sees himself," Edmund whispered. "Or I see myself. Or—"

"Step back."

"Odile—"

"*Step back.*"

Edmund stepped back. The mirror's surface shivered, and for one instant — one long, cold instant — the figure in the glass reached out, palm flat against the inside of the obsidian, fingers spread, and Odile would swear on their grandmother's grave that the stone warmed under her own hand where she'd braced against the frame, as if something on the other side had pressed back.

Then the glass went dark. Then the veins of light in the walls guttered low. Then, from everywhere and nowhere in the round stone room, a voice — dry, amused, patient as sediment:

*"You brought the map-bearer home, Maren. Clever old woman. Even dead, you cheat beautifully."*

The voice was not Aurelian's.

It was older. It was the vault itself, or the thing the vault had been built around, or the thing the vault had been built to *hold*, and it spoke with the tone of a landlord greeting tenants it had been expecting for a very long time.

Edmund's hand found Odile's in the dark. Her brother's fingers were ice.

"Grandmother's letters said the thirteenth turn wakes the Salt," Odile said into the darkness, because saying things aloud had always been how she refused to be afraid. "Said whoever holds it when the moon completes its turn claims it wholly. Is that you? Are you the Salt?"

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Four.

*"I am what the Salt dreams when it sleeps,"* the voice said, and now it sounded pleased, and pleasure was infinitely worse than menace. *"And child — I have been dreaming of your brother for a very long time."*

The mirror flared white.

And in the flare, before Odile could shield her eyes, she saw the glass show not the past anymore but something else entirely — a salt flat under a torn sky, a moon with thirteen rings, and a figure standing at the center of everything wearing Edmund's face and a crown, while armies knelt in rows to the horizon.

The vision cut out. The vault went dark as a closed eye.

In the blackness, Edmund's grip tightened on her hand, and Odile heard her sibling breathe — slow, deliberate, controlled — and then speak, and the voice that came out of the dark was calm in a way that frightened her more than the mirror had.

"Odile," Edmund said. "I need to tell you something. And I need you not to let go of my hand while I say it."
