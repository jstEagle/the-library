## 24. The Tyrant's Lesson

The mirror did not want them to sleep.

Edmund had tried. They had rolled their bedroll in the far corner of the vault, as far from the drowned abbey's dripping dark as the stone allowed, and Odile had banked the fire down to embers, and for an hour there had been only the sound of water finding its way through old wounds in the architecture — the patient drip of a building that had learned to live with being broken.

Then the mirror began to glow.

Not bright. That was the worst of it. A light like the last coal of a fire, like a candle seen through fog, like something that wanted to be noticed without wanting to be caught. It pooled on the vault floor in a thin silver wash and crept toward Edmund's bedroll with the unhurried confidence of a tide.

"Odile," Edmund said quietly.

"I see it." Their sister was already sitting up. She hadn't been sleeping either; her hair was loose and there were orchard leaves still tangled in it, souvenirs of a life three hundred miles and one lifetime ago. "It's been doing that since you stopped looking at it."

"That seems significant."

"Everything about that thing seems significant." She rose, crossed the vault in bare feet, and stood between Edmund and the mirror like a wall built of stubbornness. "We don't have to look."

"It woke when I looked away," Edmund said. "That means it wants me specifically."

"Or it wants anyone."

"Odile." Edmund sat up. In the ember-light and mirror-light their hands looked pale as salt. "The letter said the vault would show us what we needed to understand. Grandmother wrote *the glass remembers what the blood forgets*. We came here to learn. It's offering to teach."

"It's offering to show you your own face wearing a crown. I read the same letter."

"The letter also said the thirteenth turn is six weeks away and the Salt will wake whether we understand it or not." Edmund stood. Their legs were stiff from the flight through the Deep Veil, from the ambush, from the collapse that had nearly buried them all under six hundred tons of drowned abbey. Wren slept on, or pretended to, in the alcove where they'd dragged her after Odile stanched the wound in her side — a long shallow gash from Consortium steel that could so easily have been crown steel, could so easily have killed her. Her breathing was even. Her hand, even in sleep, stayed near her knife. "I'd rather walk into this knowing what's coming than be surprised by it at the solstice."

Odile didn't move. She was good at not moving; she had spent twelve years making stillness into armor, making her gift look like luck, making luck look like nothing at all. But finally she stepped aside — not because Edmund had won the argument, Edmund suspected, but because she had decided that watching her sibling walk toward the mirror alone was worse than walking beside them.

"Together, then," she said. "And if it shows you anything you like too much, you tell me. Immediately."

"You'll know before I do."

"That's why I'm here."

They approached the mirror side by side. Up close, it resolved out of its glow: a tall oval of glass in a frame of black iron worked with salt-crystals and stars, standing free in a recessed alcove behind the warden regalia. The surface was clouded, like breath held against it for centuries. Edmund raised a hand and stopped just short of touching it.

"Do it," Odile said. "Before we lose our nerve. I can feel mine going already."

Edmund touched the glass.

* * *

The world inverted.

Not fell — *inverted*, the way a reflection inverts, everything true becoming reversed and everything reversed becoming suddenly, horribly true. The vault fell away and Edmund stood somewhere else, though their body — they could feel it, distantly, like a coat left hanging in another room — remained kneeling before the glass with Odile's hand locked around their wrist.

They stood on a plain of white salt under a sky the color of a bruise healing.

"This isn't real," Edmund said aloud, and heard their voice arrive strangely, doubled, as though the salt plain were listening through them.

"No," said someone beside them. "But it happened."

The speaker was a woman. Or had been. She stood at Edmund's shoulder with the translucent quality of a figure seen through deep water — solid enough to cast a shadow on the salt, faint enough that Edmund could see the horizon through her chest. Her face was weathered and intelligent, lined around the eyes by decades of squinting into distance, and she wore robes that had once been magnificent and had been patched with the care of someone who valued things past their beauty. Around her neck hung a sigil Edmund knew intimately: the warden's mark, the same stamped into the old coin Wren Ashgrave had paid with, the same inked onto grandmother's letters.

"You're the last warden," Edmund said.

"I'm the first failure," the woman said. "The distinction matters less than you'd hope. Come. Watch. The glass remembers; I only narrate."

She turned, and the horizon turned with her, and Edmund saw the armies.

They came across the salt flats from both directions — two hosts, vast beyond counting, and every soldier of them made of salt and light. Not men carrying weapons. Men *made* of weapon and salt together, crystalline figures that glittered and burned, rank upon rank upon rank, marching with the terrible synchronized patience of a tide. Where the two fronts met, the sound reached Edmund a heartbeat later — a sound like bells shattering inside other bells, like mountains being sung apart.

"The Sundering War," Edmund breathed. "I thought— the histories say it was fought over magic. Over who would control it."

"The histories were written by the survivors, and the survivors needed to believe it was about principle." The warden-woman watched the collision of lights with an expression Edmund recognized with a sick lurch: not horror. *Regret*. The specific regret of someone watching the consequences of their own arithmetic. "It was fought over me. Over what I kept."

She lifted her hand, and Edmund saw it then — saw what she held, what she had always held, the thing the whole burning panorama orbited like moths around a lamp.

A heart. Roughly the size of two fists, roughly the shape of a heart, made of salt that moved like liquid and light that moved like thought. It beat. Slowly. Patiently. Each pulse sent a ripple across the salt plain, and each ripple fed the armies, and Edmund understood with the clarity that comes only in dreams and catastrophes that the soldiers themselves were *condensations* — the Salt breathing itself outward into shapes, giving power form, giving form to whoever asked loudest.

"The Salt isn't a source you draw from," the woman said. "It's a wellspring that flows to whoever holds the vessel. And for three hundred years, I was the vessel." She smiled, and it was the saddest expression Edmund had ever seen. "Do you know what it's like, child? Everyone comes to you. Every grief, every hunger, every ambition. The sick come to be healed and the powerful come to be armed and the desperate come to be saved, and the Salt will do all of it, gladly, endlessly — it *wants* to be used, that's the trap no history records. Using it feels like love. Every time. It feels like being loved by something enormous."

"And you gave it away?"

"At first. Freely. Healing, blessing, small wonders scattered like seed. And then—" the armies surged, and she flinched, an old flinch worn smooth by repetition, "—then the kings came. Both crowns, east and west. Each demanded exclusivity. Each promised protection in exchange for monopoly. And I thought myself clever, so I told each what the other wished to hear, and I kept the Salt, and I told myself I kept it *safely*, that I was the one person wise enough to hold what everyone else would abuse."

The scene shifted. The salt plain became a hall — the same hall, Edmund realized, the same vault beneath the drowned abbey, but alive, lit, crowded with petitioners whose faces blurred like faces in rain-streaked windows. And at the center of the crowd, the warden-woman sat enthroned — not a throne she had asked for, Edmund understood, but one that had accreted around her the way coral accretes, one granted favor at a time, until refusing a request meant ruining a family and granting it meant unbalancing a province and there was no longer any action available to her that wasn't violence against someone.

"They stopped asking," she said. "Did you notice? Somewhere in the third century, they stopped asking and started *bargaining*, and then they stopped bargaining and started scheming, and the moment two kings decided the problem was not how to serve the vessel but how to *own* it—" she gestured, and the hall burned away into the salt plain again, into the armies, into the war. "I could have ended it any day. Set the Salt down. Walk away. Let it pass to the wind. Do you know why I didn't?"

Edmund knew. Knew it in their teeth, in the place where the Salt now lived — because it lived in them now, waking turn by turn beneath their ribs like a second heart learning its rhythm.

"Because it felt like losing yourself," Edmund said.

"Because it felt like dying," the woman corrected gently. "And I had forgotten the difference between the two. Three hundred years of being needed. You cannot put that down. Your hands have grown around it." She turned to face Edmund fully, and her eyes were the exact gray of Edmund's own reflected back from dark water. "So I did the only thing left. When the armies closed in — armies my hoarding had called up, remember, never forget that — I shattered the flow. I broke the Salt's continuity. Scattered its essence into the bloodlines of those who served me faithfully, so that no single vessel would ever hold it whole again. So that it would sleep, and dilute, and perhaps, in some kinder century, wake as something small enough to be safe."

"But it didn't dilute."

"No. Blood remembers. Blood *concentrates*. Generation by generation the gift narrowed instead of spreading, until it funneled down to a single line." She smiled again, terribly. "Yours. And the moon counts thirteen turns, and on the thirteenth, whatever sleeps in your blood wakes *whole*. All of it. Everything I hoarded, returned with interest, offered freely to whoever carries the line." She leaned close, and her voice dropped to the register of confession. "It will feel like love. Remember that. Whatever it offers you, however right it feels — it felt exactly like this to me, on the day I chose the vault over the wind."

The armies collided one final time, closer now, close enough that Edmund could see individual soldiers — and froze. Because the soldiers had faces. Blurred, rain-streaked faces, but among them, sharp as a needle through cloth: Odile. Wren. Seraphine Dax, her iron-filing discipline cracked open, her gift blazing. Strangers from the market of Saltmere. The neighbor who had screamed Edmund's stall name as the sniffer-beast dragged her away. Everyone Edmund had ever failed to save and everyone they might yet save, all of them marching, all of them made of salt and light, all of them *offered* —

The mirror let go.

* * *

Edmund came back to themselves on their knees on the cold vault floor, gasping, with Odile's arms around them and Odile's voice saying their name — their true name, the one they hadn't used in twelve years, which meant they must have been gone longer than they thought.

"—mund. Edmund. Look at me. Look at *me*."

"I'm here." Their voice came out scraped. "I'm here."

"What did it show you?"

Edmund sat back against the base of the regalia case. The mirror had gone dark again — smugly dark, Edmund thought, dark the way a cat goes after knocking a cup off a table. Beyond it, the vault was unchanged: dripping stone, dead embers, Wren's steady breathing from the alcove. The ordinary world, holding its shape around the hole the vision had punched in it.

"A woman," Edmund said. "The first warden. She held the Salt for three hundred years and it started the Sundering War, and when the armies came for her she broke it apart and hid it in bloodlines. Ours. She's the reason we exist the way we exist." They laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Grandmother's letters said *inheritance is a burden carried best in company*. She was quoting a woman who destroyed the world trying to carry hers alone."

Odile knelt in front of them. In the dimness her face was mostly shadow, but her eyes caught the mirror's residual glow, and they were fierce and frightened and entirely present. "What else."

"She showed me the war. Made of salt and light. She showed me—" Edmund's throat closed. They made themselves go on, because Odile had said *you tell me immediately*, and because the alternative to saying it was letting it grow in the dark, and they had spent twelve years learning what things became when you let them grow in the dark. "She showed me everyone I've ever failed, marching in the armies. And Odile — I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you say anything."

Odile waited. She was good at waiting. Twelve years of tending a dead orchard taught a person to wait.

"Since the Deep Veil. Since the vault. There's a—" Edmund pressed their palm flat against their sternum, over the place where the second heartbeat lived. "It talks. Not in words. More like — you know how you can feel a storm coming in an old scar? It's like that, except the storm has opinions. And it's been showing me things. What I could fix. Vess's apparatus, unwound in an afternoon. The Purge lists, reversed. Everyone who burned, standing up out of their ashes, because I decided they should." Their voice cracked and they pushed through it. "And the horrifying thing, Odile, the thing I can barely make myself say out loud, is that it doesn't feel like temptation. Temptation is wanting something you know is wrong. This feels like *remembering* something. Like the world is currently wrong and I'm the only one who can feel how wrong it is, and fixing it would be the most natural thing in the world, like straightening a picture frame. Every time I think about putting it down — about what the warden woman did, setting it loose — something in me flinches like I'm imagining my own death."

The silence after was long. Water dripped somewhere in the dark. Wren murmured in her sleep and went quiet.

When Odile finally spoke, her voice was careful, the way she handled the last living branch of a dying tree.

"Good," she said.

Edmund stared. "*Good?*"

"Good that you said it. Good that it scares you." She took their face in both hands, the way she had when they were children and Edmund had nightmares about the Purge, the way that meant *listen to me and only me*. "Listen. I've hidden a gift my whole life by making it look like luck. You know what I learned? The gift never whispers anything you disagree with. That's the trick of it. It only ever says things you already believe — justice, mercy, repair — and then it waits for you to notice that you're the only one strong enough to act on them. It doesn't corrupt you with lies, Edmund. It corrupts you with truths only you can hear." Her thumb traced the cheekbone where their mother's features lived on in them both. "The tyrant in that mirror probably started exactly here. On a cold floor, telling someone honest what the whisper sounded like. The ones who become monsters aren't the ones who feel the pull. They're the ones who decide the pull makes them special."

"I know." Edmund's eyes stung. "I know, and I still want it. That's what I'm telling you. Understanding the mechanism doesn't disarm it. Knowing it feels like love doesn't make it feel less like love."

"No," Odile agreed softly. "It doesn't. I've watched my orchard die for twelve years and known exactly why, and it died anyway, because knowing isn't the same as curing." She rested her forehead against theirs. "So here's what we do. You don't get to carry this alone — that's the whole lesson of that woman in the glass, yes? She hoarded the secret along with the Salt. The whisper grew fat on privacy. So we starve it. Every time it speaks, you tell me. Out loud. Even the ugly parts. *Especially* the ugly parts. And when the thirteenth turn comes, whatever you choose — claim it, break it, scatter it, set it on fire — you choose it in front of witnesses. Me. Wren, if she lives. Maybe even that Inquisitor, if half of what I saw in that alley was real. Power that has to explain itself is power that stays human."

"And if explaining myself isn't enough? If the wanting grows faster than the telling?"

"Then I'll be here." Odile smiled, and it was the orchard smile, the one that made dead trees bloom. "And I am very, very stubborn, little brother. I out-waited a drought. I can out-wait a god."

Edmund laughed despite everything — a wet, cracked laugh, but real. They held on to their sister in the dripping dark of their grandmother's vault, under the blind eye of the mirror, and for a few minutes the second heartbeat under their ribs was just a pulse, just a body, just a person afraid of the future in the ordinary mortal way.

Then Wren's voice came from the alcove, rough with pain and adrenaline in equal measure.

"Siblings. Lovely moment. Hate to interrupt." She was sitting up, one hand pressed to her bandaged side, the other pointing at the vault entrance — the flooded corridor through which they'd entered, now glowing with torchlight that none of them had lit. "But either the dead are very interested in our family reunion, or somebody just found the door."

Edmund rose, pulling Odile up with them, and felt the Salt stir beneath their ribs like a dog hearing its name — and this time, deliberately, out loud, in front of the witness Odile had appointed, they said:

*"Not yet. Not ever, if I can help it. But if they've come for a fight—"*

The torchlight rounded the corner, and Edmund stopped talking.
