## 25. Ambush at the Abbey

Wren had been in exactly eleven ambushes before this one, and she'd survived all of them by obeying a single rule: the first person to move is the first person to die.

So when the drowned abbey began to shake, she stood very still and counted.

The vault beneath it was lit by warden-light — a soft gold that came from nowhere and clung to nothing, the kind of illumination that made everything look like memory. The mirror at its center still showed the tyrant's face fading from its surface like breath off glass. Edmund stood before it with both hands pressed flat against the frame, and Wren didn't need to see their face to know what was happening behind their eyes. She'd watched greed work on better people than Edmund Villalobos. She'd watched it work on her own family, which was a longer and more expensive education.

Odile had a hand on her brother's shoulder. "Edmund. Look at me."

"I'm looking."

"You're not. You're looking at him."

That was true enough. In the mirror's depths, where the Salt's previous holders drifted like fish in dark water, the tyrant remained. He did not fade the way the others had. He was tall, hollow-cheeked, crowned in salt-crystal, and he was smiling — and Wren would have bet every coin in her ruined career that he was smiling at Edmund specifically.

Then the ceiling groaned.

Not the settling of an old building. Not water shifting through the flooded galleries above. This was a sound Wren knew from sieges, from collapsing towers, from the one time she'd watched a Consortium safehouse come down around her ears because she'd been three minutes too slow clearing out: stone being broken *on purpose*.

She counted four heartbeats. Then five.

On the sixth, she moved.

"Down!" She caught Odile by the collar and hauled her behind the great stone plinth that held the mirror, dragging Edmund along by the sleeve as she went. "Both of you, against the wall, hands flat, don't touch anything—"

The eastern wall of the vault exploded inward.

It did not crumble. It *detonated*, blocks the size of coffins tumbling into black water that surged up through the floor grates, and through the breach came lantern-light and the smell of oiled leather and iron — the specific iron-and-ash stink of crown inquisitors that Wren could have identified blindfolded in a crowd of ten thousand.

Six of them. No — eight, spreading through the gap in pairs, crossbows level, boots splashing in the shallows. And behind them, tall and straight-backed with her hair bound back for war, walked the woman whose name had been following them across two hundred miles like a debt collector.

Seraphine Dax.

"Villalobos," Dax said, and her voice carried through the vault without effort, pitched the way commanders pitch things. "You are taken alive. That is the order. Whether you arrive breathing is negotiable."

*Inquisitors,* Wren thought. *Crown. Which means Vess knows about the vault, which means someone talked, which means—*

The western wall answered the eastern one.

It went the same way — a crack like lightning, then ruin — but what came through the western breach was not crown livery. It was grey and black, unmarked armor, faces behind half-masks, and they moved with the drilled economy of people who had done this a hundred times in a hundred places where no one would ever write down what happened.

Consortium enforcers. Her enforcers. Or they had been, forty-eight hours ago, before she'd burned her credentials in a brazier and walked out of Ashgrave House with nothing but a knife and a decision she hadn't finished regretting yet.

At their head stood Master Corvane, the Consortium's chief of acquisitions, a man built like a well-dressed door. He looked across the flooded vault, past the inquisitors, directly at Wren, and inclined his head with terrible courtesy.

"Mistress Ashgrave," Corvane said. "You've cost us a great deal of money."

"You'll invoice me," Wren said.

For one long moment, nobody moved. Eight inquisitors on the east side. Ten enforcers on the west. Three fugitives pinned between them like meat in a sandwich that had gone spectacularly wrong, and above all of it the warden-light burning steady and gold, and beneath all of it the thing they'd all come for, humming in Edmund's blood so loudly now that Wren could almost feel it through the wet stone under her boots.

Dax spoke first. "Chancellor's warrant. The gifted and all materials pertaining to the Salt revert to the Iron Court."

Corvane smiled inside his half-mask. "There's no such substance as the Salt. Crown law says so. Has said so for twelve years." He gestured lazily at the mirror. "We're here to recover stolen Consortium property. The map. Perhaps the cartographer, if she comes quietly."

"The cartographer is under arrest."

"The cartographer is under *contract*. There's a difference. Contracts survive governments."

And Wren, crouched behind the plinth with Odile's arm hard across her chest and Edmund's breath coming fast against her ear, thought: *there it is.* The whole architecture of her life laid bare in one sentence, delivered by a man who genuinely believed it. Contracts survive governments. Families don't. Promises don't. People certainly don't.

She'd spent nine years working for people who thought like that. Nine years telling herself she was different because she knew it was ugly.

Someone fired first. Later — much later, in the weeks after, when she lay awake listening to her own heartbeat and the small sounds of a world remade — Wren would try to remember whether it was an inquisitor bolt or an enforcer quarrel, and she never could. History, she decided, probably wouldn't bother recording it either. History only ever recorded who won.

What she remembered was this:

The first volley crossed the vault and hit nothing, because everyone had already dropped, and the bolts and quarrels met in the middle air with a sound like a flock of birds striking a window. Then both sides charged, and the drowned abbey became a butcher's floor.

Wren fought the way she always fought: cheaply. No flourishes, no dueling stance, nothing that wasted a motion or announced itself. She took the nearest inquisitor — a young one, barely old enough to shave properly — and broke his wrist against the plinth's edge, took his crossbow, put a bolt through the knee of the second one coming around the corner, and rolled left as a sword came down where her head had been.

"Odile!" she shouted over the din. "Edmund! The gallery — northeast stairs — go!"

"We're not leaving you—" Odile began.

"*Go!*"

They didn't go. Of course they didn't go. Wren registered it distantly, furiously, even as she parried and retreated and gave ground toward them — the siblings standing back to back in the churned shallows, and Odile doing something with her hands that made the water itself rise up and slap two enforcers backward like the hand of a very annoyed god, and Edmund with their palms out and eyes shut, whispering, and the warden-light bending toward them like wheat toward sun.

*Magic,* Wren thought, cutting low and taking an enforcer's hamstring. *Twelve years hiding it and now they're using it like a lantern in a cellar full of gunpowder.* Every gifted soul within fifty miles could probably feel that flare. Every sniffer-beast in the chancellor's kennels was, right now, turning its head east.

But that was a problem for later. Later was a country Wren had learned to live in.

A quarrel hissed past her cheek close enough to part her hair. She answered it with a thrown knife that buried itself in an enforcer's shoulder, and then the press of bodies carried her sideways, away from the siblings, toward the base of the mirror's plinth, and she got one clear look upward —

— and saw the tyrant's face in the glass, watching.

Not drifting. Not faded. *Watching*, turned toward the battle like a man at a window, and his smile had widened, and the salt crystals of his crown were growing, branching, reaching up out of the mirror's surface into the air above the vault like frost climbing a pane.

"Oh," Wren said quietly. "That's new."

The vault shuddered again — deeper this time, structural, the sound of something below the foundations giving way. Water geysered up through a grate and knocked three fighters off their feet. Somewhere overhead, in the drowned galleries, an entire ceiling section let go with a roar.

Dax cut through the melee toward the siblings — toward Edmund — moving like the answer to a math problem, economical and inevitable. An enforcer stepped into her path and she put him down in two motions without appearing to hurry. Wren saw it all from twenty feet away with the horrible clarity of someone who has studied another fighter closely enough to read their handwriting.

*Dax isn't fighting to win,* Wren realized. *She's fighting to reach them. And she doesn't care who's in the way.*

Which meant whatever Chancellor Vess had done to her — and Wren had heard rumors, iron filings and catalysts and other words she'd pretended not to hear — it had turned Seraphine Dax from a hunter into something with a heading.

Wren did the arithmetic. Edmund and Odile, backs nearly to the northeast stair. Dax, six seconds out. Corvane regrouping his survivors near the west breach, and Corvane wanted Edmund alive too, which meant he wanted them *unharmed*, which meant—

Which meant the safest place in this entire collapsing room was the exact spot where everyone's orders collided, and the most dangerous person in it was her, because she was the only one on this floor with no orders at all.

She ran.

Not toward the stairs. Toward Dax.

She hit the inquisitor from the side, shoulder-first, and they went down together into waist-deep water. Dax was stronger than she looked — far stronger — and faster, and within a heartbeat she'd reversed the tackle and had Wren by the throat with a forearm across her collarbone, pinning her under the black water, and her eyes above were wide and strange, the pupils blown, and Wren understood suddenly that the woman wasn't entirely home. Something was driving her. Something was calling her, and Edmund's flaring gift had given it a direction.

"Dax," Wren choked, bubbles streaming. "Listen to me. Vess sent you to fetch a *thing*. Not a person. You know what he builds out of things."

Something flickered in those blown pupils. A hairline fracture. Wren had seen that exact expression before, on her own face, in mirrors, in the years before she finally stopped pretending the Consortium was a family.

Then the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. One gallery section — tons of it, black stone and drowned timber and centuries of silt — punching through into the vault corner where the fight had been thickest. The shockwave of displaced water picked up fighters and furniture alike and threw them. The warden-light guttered for the first time since they'd arrived, and in the sudden half-dark the mirror blazed, salt-white, and the tyrant's silhouette stood up out of it like a man rising from a chair.

Wren surfaced coughing, deafened, tasting blood and plaster. The vault was half-choked with rubble now. She couldn't see Edmund. Couldn't see Odile. Couldn't see Dax.

Then she saw Odile — on her knees in the shallows, dragging rubble, screaming her brother's name into the dust — and beyond her, teetering on a tilted slab above, a chunk of vault-rib the size of a rowboat, balanced on nothing, beginning its slow and terrible decision to fall.

Wren had survived eleven ambushes by standing still and letting other people make the first mistake.

This was not going to be one of those times.

She crossed the distance without deciding to. There was no thought in it, which frightened her afterward more than the falling stone did — just her legs, and Odile's face, and some wordless arithmetic about which lives she had decided, somewhere in the last few days without filing the paperwork, were hers to protect.

She hit Odile at the waist and drove them both sideways as the slab came down. It caught Wren across the back and left shoulder — a blow like being struck by the concept of weight — and then they were rolling in the shallows and the pain arrived, white and total, and the world went briefly to static.

When it came back, she was on her back in six inches of water, and Odile was leaning over her, saying her name — *Wren, Wren, look at me* — and pressing both hands against Wren's shoulder, and there was green light leaking through Odile's fingers, moss-light, orchard-light, the gift doing in seconds what would take a surgeon weeks.

"That's cheating," Wren managed.

"Shut up." Odile's voice cracked clean down the middle. "Shut up, you absolute — you could have died, you don't even — why?"

Good question. Wren filed it away with the others.

The vault was ending around them. Half the ceiling was open to night sky now, rain falling through in grey sheets, and the mirror stood alone amid the wreckage, untouched, blazing, and Edmund was on their feet before it with blood running down one side of their face and their hand raised — not toward the mirror, Wren noticed. Toward *it*, yes, but also *away*, holding it at arm's length like a snake they couldn't stop looking at.

And rising out of the mirror's surface, realer than it had any right to be, the tyrant. Salt-crowned. Smiling. Extending one crystalline hand toward Edmund across the boundary of the glass, and Edmund's hand trembling, drifting, traitorously, toward it.

"No," Odile breathed beside her. "Edmund, *no*—"

Movement at the breaches. Both of them. Inquisitors hauling themselves out of the water, enforcers reforming ranks, Dax upright and swaying with rain running down her face and something newly awake behind her eyes — and through the eastern gap, unhurried, dry despite the flood, came a figure in chancellor's grey whom Wren recognized from a hundred briefings she'd sat through pretending to take notes.

Not Vess himself. Worse. Vess's shadow. The man who did what the chancellor preferred not to be seen doing.

He looked at the mirror. He looked at Edmund's outstretched hand. And he raised a slender black rod that hummed with a frequency Wren felt in her teeth — an artifact, old war stock, the kind of thing the Consortium kept lists about and never sold.

"Thank you all," the chancellor's shadow said pleasantly, over the rain and the ruins, "for opening the vault for us."

The tyrant in the mirror began to laugh.

* * *

Rain hammered the open wound of the ceiling. The rod in the grey man's hand climbed in pitch until the water itself shivered into patterns, rings spreading from his feet, and every fighter in the vault — inquisitor, enforcer, deserter — staggered as if the floor had tilted.

Except Wren, who was lying down anyway, and except Odile, whose hands were still glowing green against Wren's shoulder, and except Edmund, who had frozen with their fingers inches from the mirror's surface, caught between two calls like a compass between magnets.

"Wren." Odile leaned close, rain streaming off her chin, her voice low and fierce and absolutely steady. "Can you walk?"

"Define walk."

"Good enough."

The grey man turned the rod toward Edmund. The tyrant in the mirror reached harder. And Seraphine Dax, thirty feet away, soaked and bleeding and no longer anyone's instrument, took one step — then another — placing herself, deliberately, unmistakably, in the line between them.

"Well," Wren said, and started laughing, which hurt enormously and felt correct. "Now it's a party."
