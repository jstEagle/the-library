## 27. The Chancellor's Audience

They dressed me for the audience the way you'd dress a corpse for viewing: carefully, and with no expectation that I would say anything.

The clothes were fine — too fine. A coat of dove-gray wool with silver thread at the cuffs, a shirt soft as moth-wing, boots that had never known mud. Someone had measured me in my sleep, or guessed well from a distance. The collar closed at my throat like a decision already made.

The cell they took me from had been comfortable, which was its own kind of message. A real bed. A basin of clean water. Bread with butter on it. Three days of that, after the wagon, after Seraphine's hands binding my wrists not cruelly but not gently either, her face turned away the whole time as if looking at me cost her something she couldn't afford. Comfort was the message. Vess wanted me to understand that I was not prey. Prey got a cage. I got a guest room with a lock on the outside.

Now two guards walked me through corridors of white stone and iron-banded doors, and I did what I had done in every room since I was nineteen years old and running: I mapped it.

Left at the second arch. Stairs down, then up — they were taking me higher, into the keep's throat. Torch brackets every six paces, unlit; the walls here glowed faintly on their own, some old working the Inquisition hadn't bothered to strip because it was theirs. Four guards at the landing, halberds grounded. A door of black oak banded in silver, worked with the crown's sunburst, and beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic, a second sigil I recognized from Wren's old coin: the warden's mark, defaced. Scratched out by hand, long ago, by someone who wanted very badly to make a point.

I filed all of it away. It was the only thing I still owned.

* * *

Chancellor Aldous Vess received me in a round chamber at the top of the Iron Court's spire, and the first thing I understood about him was that he had arranged the room to be disappointing.

I had expected menace. What I got was a study. Bookshelves. A desk of pale wood, immaculate. A window behind him showing the whole gray sprawl of the capital, the river cutting through it like a seam of tarnished tin. Vess himself was smaller than his reputation — a narrow man in a plain black robe, silver at the temples, spectacles he didn't need, the kind of face you forgot while it was talking to you. He looked like a man who had spent forty years being underestimated and had decided, somewhere along the way, to make it a weapon instead of a wound.

"Edmund Villalobos." He said the name correctly. All of it. Not the market name, not the alias I'd worn for twelve years like a second skin. My true name, spoken softly, the way you'd speak to a horse you didn't want spooking. "Sit, please."

There was one chair. One. Positioned across the desk from him, angled so that whoever sat in it would have to look past him at the window — at the city, small and far below, the city I had spent half my life hiding inside. I sat. I looked at the city. I let him think the view intimidated me.

"You're wondering why you're alive," Vess said. He poured tea from a pot on the sideboard — actually poured it, actually handed it to me, the cup warm in my palms. "Inquisitor Dax had orders to take you living, but orders can be misread. She chose not to misread them. That interests me. It should interest you too."

"I'm told gratitude is expected," I said. "I'm bad at expectations. Ask anyone who's ever bought a charm off me."

He smiled. It reached his eyes, which frightened me more than if it hadn't. Men whose smiles don't reach their eyes are performing. Men whose smiles do reach their eyes mean them, and men like that are the dangerous ones.

"Your sister," Vess said.

The tea went cold in my mouth. I hadn't touched it yet and it went cold anyway.

"She was taken four days ago, near the salt road. Resisting, of course. She always resists — the reports on your family are quite consistent on that point. Your grandmother resisted the first Purge. You resisted the last one, though you called it something politer. Fleeing." He folded his hands. "Odile is in a cell much less comfortable than yours. Chains, in fact. Iron ones. We've found iron does lovely things to a gift like hers — all that growing, all that blooming. Iron teaches it patience."

Every instinct I had screamed at me to react. To stand, to shout, to give him the flinch he had built this whole room to harvest. Twelve years of practice held me in the chair. I set down the tea. I looked at the window again, at the city, and I made my face into the face of a man doing arithmetic.

"How is she," I said. Flat. Bored, almost. As if he'd mentioned a business associate.

Something flickered behind the spectacles. Recalculation. He had expected grief or rage, and I had given him bookkeeping, and for a moment — just a moment — Chancellor Aldous Vess did not know what he was looking at.

Good, I thought. Stay confused. Underestimating me is the smartest thing you'll ever do.

* * *

He rose and walked to the window, hands clasped behind his back, and gave me his back the way other men give a bow — an offering of trust that wasn't trust at all.

"Do you know what the Sundering War was actually about?" he asked. "Not the version in the schoolrooms. The schoolroom version is that mad kings fought over magic until the world broke, and wise men outlawed wonder to save what remained. It's tidy. Children like tidy." He turned his head, profile against the gray light. "The truth is that the war was fought over scarcity. One source. One inheritance. Whoever held the Salt could make every other gifted person a beggar. So everyone fought to hold it, and when the fighting ended, the victors did the only thing left that guaranteed no one else could ever hold it again." He tapped the windowsill once. "They pretended it was dead. They outlawed the gift itself, burned the bloodlines, salted — forgive the phrase — the old warden lands. And for three hundred years it worked, because the Salt slept, and sleeping things are easy to call extinct."

"And now it's waking," I said.

"Now it's waking." He came back around the desk and sat, and leaned forward, and the lamplight caught his spectacles and blanked his eyes for a heartbeat, so that he seemed briefly to have no face at all. "And every power in the world is scrambling to be holding it when it finishes. The crown. The Consortium — yes, we know about your courier friend, Miss Ashgrave plays both sides of every table she sits at, it's rather her whole personality. Foreign courts. Cults. Desperate little cabals of the gifted who think waking it will set them free." He spread his narrow hands. "All of them wrong. Because the Salt isn't a treasure, Villalobos. It's a throne. And thrones don't care who sits in them. They only care that someone does."

"I've seen what happens to people who sit on thrones," I said. "There's a mirror under a drowned abbey that shows it rather clearly."

"Yes. The vault. Our people reached it two days after you fled." His smile again, reaching his eyes. "You left excellent tracks. Both of you. Sentimental people always do."

The word *sentimental* landed where he wanted it to land. I let it. Let him believe he'd scored. A man who thinks he's winning stops checking whether the game has changed.

"So here is my offer," Vess said, "and I'll make it plainly, because I find plainness disarms people better than any threat. Hand over the inheritance. Willingly. Formally, before the court, with witnesses — there are rites, old ones, the wardens used them themselves. Renounce your claim and transfer it to the crown, and in exchange: clemency. Full pardon, both kingdoms' bounties struck. Land. Title. A position — cartographer royal, if you like; we've never had one worth the name, and I'm told you see roads that don't exist. Your sister freed within the hour, her record burned, her orchard returned to her, iron off her wrists before the ink dries." He paused, delicately. "You would live. She would live. The world would go on having maps."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the Inquisition continues doing what the Inquisition does. Slowly. Thoroughly. Starting with the parts of the world you love most and working inward." He picked up his own teacup, unhurried. "I want to be clear that I hope you refuse nothing. I am not a cruel man, whatever the pamphlets say. Cruelty is inefficient. But I am a thorough one, and thoroughness, given enough time, looks identical to cruelty from the outside."

* * *

He showed me Odile then.

I want to say I was ready for it. I want to say twelve years of calcified grief had armored me against anything. But he didn't bring me to her, and he didn't summon her in, and he didn't even use a scrying glass — he simply opened a drawer in that pale immaculate desk and took out a sheet of paper, and that was somehow worse than any sorcery. A report. Written. Filed. My sister reduced to a clerk's handwriting.

He read it aloud, in the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest.

"*Subject O.V., taken near the salt road, offered no resistance during transport but required full restraints thereafter. Subject's gift manifests as accelerated growth — moss, lichen, flowering — even under iron suppression, though greatly diminished. Note: subject caused the lock mechanism of her cell door to rust through overnight. Lock replaced. Subject has since begun work on the replacement.*"

He lowered the paper. "She's been in that cell nine days," he said, "and she has destroyed four locks. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Iron slows her. Nothing stops her. Your family does not know how to stop." He folded the report away, gentle as a priest closing a missal. "Imagine what she could do unfettered. Imagine what you could do together. Or — imagine neither, because you gave it all up, and she plants her orchard, and you draw your maps, and the crown keeps its leash on the world's one wild thing, and everyone lives."

The trap was beautiful. I'll grant him that. Every exit led to him. Take the offer and I surrendered the Salt to exactly the monopoly the war was fought to prevent — I became the warden who handed the tyrant his crown, politely, with witnesses. Refuse, and Odile rotted in an iron cell while I ran, and everything in me that had spent twelve years running knew precisely how that story ended: alone, eventually, with a name I couldn't use and a debt I couldn't pay.

He watched me weigh it. I could feel him watching, patient as rust, certain that weight would tell.

So I gave him what he wanted. Carefully. A little tremor in the hands — not much, a craftsman's amount. A long look at the window, at the city, at the life he was offering back to me. I let my breath go uneven. I thought about Odile's actual face, which made it easy, because the fear underneath was not performance at all; I was only choosing where to put it.

"If I agree," I said, and heard my own voice crack on the second word, and hated how good it sounded, "the rites. Would she be present? At the transfer?"

"If you wish it."

"I wish it." I looked down at my hands. "I won't say the words without her there. If this is mercy, Chancellor, let it look like mercy. Let her see me choose it. Otherwise she'll spend the rest of her life thinking I abandoned her twice."

Silence. Then Vess nodded slowly, and I watched him file me away under a heading he liked: *the sentimental one. The brother. Leverage confirmed.*

"The rites require preparation," he said. "Three days. The moon turns soon — you'll understand why that matters better than anyone at this court. On the third day, you and your sister will stand before the altar together, and you will speak the renunciation, and this whole ugly hunt ends with everyone breathing." He stood, and offered his hand, and I took it, and his grip was dry and cool and entirely in control, and I shook it like a man agreeing to his own salvation.

At the door, guarded again, waiting to be walked back down into comfort, I paused.

"Chancellor," I said. "One question. The mirror in the vault. The last warden — the one who became the tyrant. When you looked into it. What did you see?"

Vess's smile reached his eyes again.

"I saw a man who hesitated," he said. "I resolved, some time ago, never to be one."

The door closed behind me. The guards fell in. And I walked back down through the glowing corridors of the Iron Court with my heart hammering a rhythm I hadn't let it hammer in front of him — not fear, not anymore, or not only — counting torch brackets, stair turns, guard rotations, the exact number of paces between the spire and the cells below, where somewhere a woman was patiently, methodically, destroying her fourth lock.

Three days. He'd given me three days, an audience with Odile, and a stage before the whole court.

He thought he'd bought my surrender.

What he'd actually done was put me in the same room as my sister, in front of every powerful person in two kingdoms, with the Salt awake in my blood and the moon one turn from full — and no idea, yet, that the map he'd confiscated from me in the wagon had been a forgery I'd drawn in the dark three nights before I was ever caught.

I had been underestimated by experts for twelve years.

It was finally going to be useful.
