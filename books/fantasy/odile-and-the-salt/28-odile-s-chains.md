## 28. Odile's Chains

The cell smelled of iron and old prayers.

Odile had been counting the hours by the sound of boots in the corridor above — a guard's circuit, roughly every forty minutes, the tread heavier on the left side, favoring a bad knee. She had been counting for what she judged to be two days, though the dark made liars of all measurements. Two days since the wagon. Two days since the hood came off and the door slammed and her world shrank to six feet of stone in every direction.

Two days was long enough to grieve. It was not long enough to despair. She had decided that on the first night, lying on the straw pallet with her wrists still raw from the manacles, staring up at a ceiling she couldn't see: *You get one night, Odile Villalobos. One night to cry about it. Then you get to work.*

She had used her night. She had wept for Edmund — for the fool courage of it, walking into Vess's audience chamber as if being underestimated were armor instead of bait. She had wept for the orchard, which would die unattended, its last true blossoms falling on ground no one would walk. She had even wept, briefly and against her will, for herself — nineteen years old again, watching soldiers burn the letters, except this time she had been the one burning them, and keeping only one had felt like both treason and salvation.

Then morning came — or what passed for morning, a gray seam of light under the door — and she got to work.

* * *

The first thing she did was nothing at all.

That was harder than it sounded. Her whole life, her gift had been a thing of motion — bloom following footstep, green rising where she lingered. In the orchard she had learned to think of magic as movement, as generosity, as the world leaning toward her when she leaned toward it. A cell offered nothing to lean toward. Stone did not want to grow. Iron did not want to flower.

But Grandmother's letters — the one letter, the letter folded so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth — had said otherwise. *The Salt does not live in what you touch,* the old woman had written, in a hand that shook but never hurried. *It lives in what you tend. Tending is slower than touching. That is why they will never fear it, and why it will outlast them.*

So Odile tended.

She began with the floor beneath her pallet, because it was the one place in the cell no guard ever looked closely. Each time the boots went by overhead, each time the gray seam brightened or dimmed, she pressed her palm flat to the stone and thought about soil. Not wished — thought. Wishing, she suspected, was for people whose gifts worked fast. Hers had always worked slow, patient, disguised as luck. An orchard did not erupt. An orchard *accumulated.*

Nothing happened the first day. Nothing happened the second. On the third day, when she ran her fingers along the crack between two flagstones, she found something that might have been dust and might have been the ghost of moss, dry as an apology.

She almost laughed aloud and had to bite it back, because the guard outside shifted at the sound, and his keys chimed.

*There you are,* she told the invisible green. *Slow is fine. Slow is how I've done everything my whole life.*

* * *

They fed her twice a day, through a slot in the door, and once a day they took her to a yard the size of a kitchen to walk in circles under armed watch. The yard was the worst part, because the yard had sky, and the sky reminded her that somewhere east of here Edmund was sitting across a table from Chancellor Vess, pretending to bargain, and that the thirteenth turn was coming whether any of them were ready or not.

The guards didn't talk to her. That was policy, she gathered; she'd heard the sergeant instructing a new man on her first evening — *"No conversation with the warden-blood. Not even insults. The Chancellor says words are doors."* Vess again, sealing every crack. He was thorough. She would give him that. Thorough men made narrow prisons, and narrow prisons had walls you could learn by heart.

She learned hers. On the walks she counted paces, noted where the mortar had crumbled, where a drain grate sat loose enough to ring when her heel caught it. In the cell she mapped the dark by touch until she could navigate it blind — which, given that she lived in the dark, meant navigating it entirely.

And every day, twice a day, she tended.

By the fifth day there was real moss in the floor-crack, a coin-sized patch of it, soft as velvet and impossibly green in a place that had never seen green. By the eighth day it had spread along the wall's base in a thread-thin line, following the mortar like a road. She kept it small — smaller than it wanted to be — pruning with her attention, because a patch of moss could pass for damp, but a garden could not.

The locks were harder.

Her cell had two: the door lock, heavy black iron, older than the building; and the manacle bracket bolted to the wall beside her pallet, unused now but present, a reminder of what she was for. Iron resisted her the way stone never had. Iron had been forged angry — quenched in fury, shaped by hammers, and it remembered. When she laid her hand on the door lock and *tended* at it, tending rust into the hinges, tending decay into the bolt-works, it was like talking to someone who had decided, generations ago, exactly what he thought of her family.

But everything rusts eventually. Even anger. Especially anger left out in the weather.

She worked the lock the way she'd once coaxed a blighted tree back from rot — not fighting the metal's nature but joining it, offering it the one future it already believed in. Decay. Oblivion. Rest. *You don't have to hold anyone anymore,* she whispered to it, palm flat against cold iron, breath fogging in the chill. *No one has asked your opinion in three hundred years. You're allowed to be tired.*

On the eleventh day, the keyhole's inner lip flaked red when she touched it.

On the fourteenth, the bolt's spring gave a soft, tired *click* when she pressed it with her thumb — not open, not yet, but no longer certain either. She felt the uncertainty like a heartbeat.

She pulled her hand away and lay down on the pallet and smiled at the ceiling she couldn't see, and for the first time since the wagon, slept without dreaming of chains.

* * *

The visits began on the sixteenth day.

Not visits — interrogations, though the first one barely qualified. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs sat across a scarred table from her in a room lit by a single lamp and read questions off a list in a bored voice. Where was the vault. What did the letters say. Had Edmund spoken of the thirteenth turn. Odile answered with the truth, or near-truths, or pleasant nonsense, calibrated to waste his time without provoking him. She had spent thirty years making a miracle look like luck; making a lie look like fatigue was easy work.

The second visitor was worse. A woman in Inquisition gray who said little, watched much, and left a mark on the room like frost on glass. She never touched Odile. She didn't need to. When their eyes met, Odile felt something lean toward her across the table — something hungry and grieving at once, a dog straining at a leash held by someone else's hand.

*"You feel it too,"* the woman said quietly, before the guards walked Odile back. *"The calling. Don't pretend you don't."*

Odile said nothing. But she carried the woman's face back to her cell, and turned it over in the dark the way she turned seeds before planting — looking for the split where the life was. There had been a split. She was almost sure of it. Somewhere under the iron discipline, that woman was holding something down with both hands, and things held down that hard always, eventually, come up.

*Patience,* Odile reminded herself, tending the moss. *Everything comes up. That's rather the whole point of gardens.*

* * *

On the twentieth day they took Edmund past her corridor.

She knew it by the sound before she knew it by anything else — a different tread among the guards' boots, uneven, dragging slightly, and then, unmistakable, her brother's voice saying something low and mild to whoever was marching them, the way they'd talk to a horse they were trying not to spook. Alive. Whole enough to be flippant. She was at the door with her palms against it before she'd decided to move, and the moss-line along the wall's base pulsed warm under her bare feet like a struck bell.

"Edmund." She kept her voice level, pitched just loud enough to carry. "Edmund Villalobos. If you're listening — the letter. Page three. *Count the turns backward.*"

The footsteps stopped. A beat of silence, rich as ripe fruit. Then Edmund's voice, closer than she expected — right outside the door, muffled but clear: *"Backward. Yes. I knew you'd keep the good parts."*

Boots closed in, voices barked, the footsteps moved on. But Odile stood with her forehead against the iron for a long time after, feeling the lock's tired mechanism tremble faintly under her hands, as if it had heard too.

*Page three.* There was no page three. The letter had two pages and a scrap of wrapping paper Edmund had once used to sketch a map of nowhere. But Edmund would understand: *the part you think is nothing is the part that matters.* And more than that — Edmund would simply understand that she was alive, and thinking, and waiting. Siblings needed very few words. They had spent their whole childhood inventing shorthand out of silence.

That night the bolt's spring clicked all the way into its housing, loose as a milk tooth.

She didn't use it. Of course she didn't use it. An open lock in a locked corridor led nowhere but an alarm. But knowing it was there — knowing that if she pressed the iron for another week, maybe two, the whole assembly would give up its argument — changed the shape of the dark. A cell with a dying lock isn't a cell. It's a seed casing. You just have to wait for the conditions.

She tended. She waited. She refused, daily and deliberately, to despair — not because despair wasn't reasonable, but because it was a crop she knew how to starve. You didn't fight despair head-on any more than you fought a drought by yelling at the sky. You planted something else in its rows and watered it and let the weeds go untended until they thinned on their own.

What she planted was small and stubborn: the memory of the orchard blooming under her feet. The sound of Edmund saying *I knew you'd keep the good parts.* The image of an Inquisitor's eyes with grief behind them, a leash around something wild. The click of a bolt learning to rest.

* * *

On the twenty-seventh day, the moss reached the door frame.

It had taken nearly a month to travel eight feet of wall, and she had pruned it back a dozen times, but now she let it run — up the jamb, into the hinge-gaps, threading the iron like ivy claiming a fence. Moss in a hinge looked like neglect. Neglect looked like nothing at all. By the time anyone thought to inspect the door of cell nine, the hinges would be soft as bread crust, and soft iron screams differently than strong iron when it breaks.

She was tracing the growth with one fingertip, checking her work, when the boots stopped outside her door and stayed stopped.

A pause. Then the slot scraped open, and a voice she hadn't heard before — young, nervous, human — said, "You're the sister."

Odile rose slowly from the pallet. Through the slot she could see half a face: a boy, seventeen at most, Inquisition gray hanging wrong on narrow shoulders.

"I'm nobody's sister," she said mildly. "Ask anyone."

"The prisoner they brought in yesterday. Gray coat. Burned credentials. She wouldn't stop asking about you — wouldn't give a name, wouldn't answer anything else. They've got her in the yard cell." His eye darted down the corridor and back. "She said — she said to tell the orchard woman that the *gate's already paid for.*"

The words landed in Odile like rain on soil that had been waiting all season.

Wren Ashgrave. Caught — burned — paying for gates. Which meant the courier had chosen, finally and irreversibly, and the price of choosing was a cell one corridor away, and whatever came next would come soon, because men like Chancellor Vess did not keep burned spies and warden-blood in adjacent cells out of administrative accident. Someone wanted them close. Someone was arranging a board.

Or someone had made a mistake. Odile had lived thirty years on the difference between those two sentences, and she trusted mistakes far more than arrangements.

"Thank you," she said to the boy, and something in her voice made him step back from the slot as if the words had thorns.

When his boots faded up the corridor, she knelt at the base of the door and put both palms flat against the iron — against the hinges wrapped in green, against the bolt gone soft as an old promise — and she tended, properly, fully, for the first time letting the gift run open through her like water through a broken sluice.

The moss surged. The hinges sighed. Somewhere deep in the lock, iron that had been angry for three hundred years finally, quietly, gave up.

Odile stayed kneeling in the dark of her finished work, heart steady, hands green-dusted, and listened to the corridor — to the bad-knee guard's receding tread, forty minutes until the next circuit.

Thirty-eight minutes to decide what kind of garden she was going to plant in the middle of the Iron Court.
