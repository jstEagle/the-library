## 29. Wren's Reckoning

The Ashgrave Consortium kept two ledgers.

The first was the public one — bound in gray calfskin, audited quarterly, the kind of ledger that made Grayharbor's magistrates nod approvingly over their wine. The second was the real one, and it lived nowhere at all. It lived in the memory of three people: the Consortium's head of acquisition, its head of security, and Wren Ashgrave herself, who had spent nine years learning to be both.

Wren had memorized her first ledger entry at fourteen, standing on a stool in her uncle's counting room while he dictated: *One courier, expendable, assigned to the Saltmere inquiry. Cost of replacement, negligible.* She hadn't known then what "the Saltmere inquiry" meant. She knew now. It meant a cartographer with a stolen map and a sister with dirt under her fingernails and an inheritance sleeping in their blood like a banked fire.

It meant, as of six days ago, the most valuable asset the Consortium had ever nearly acquired — and the only asset Wren had ever wanted to steal for free.

She sat in the third-tier gallery of the Iron Court's administrative wing, wearing the Consortium's slate-gray traveling coat with its silver ash-leaf clasp, and she watched Chancellor Vess's clerks process prisoners the way other men processed grain. Names into books. Books into files. Files into sentences. The machinery was beautiful, in the way that millstones are beautiful — all smooth turning surfaces and total indifference to what went between them.

*One prisoner, female, taken at the drowned abbey. Value: leverage against the warden inheritance. Disposition: hold.*

That was Odile Villalobos. That was the line Wren had written herself, three days ago, in a hand so steady it frightened her.

"Courier Ashgrave." A clerk with ink-stained cuffs beckoned her toward the processing desk. "Your credentials."

Wren produced the letter of passage. Consortium seal, Consortium signature, Consortium authority to move freely through the Iron Court's facilities in pursuit of contractual interests. Nine years of favors and double-booked deliveries and one very compromising evening with the Undersecretary of Seals, all distilled into a single sheet of heavy paper.

The clerk examined it, examined her, and waved her through.

And that was the whole horror of it, Wren thought, walking the corridor toward the holding cells. That was how easy it was. No one stopped her because no one needed to. The world had agreed long ago that a woman with the right paper could walk anywhere, do anything, be anyone — and the world never asked what happened when the woman decided the paper was a lie.

Her uncle had told her once, when she was still young enough to ask honest questions: *"We don't take sides, Wrennie. We own the table."*

Tonight she was going to flip it.

* * *

The cells beneath the administrative wing were not dungeons. That was Vess's particular genius — she'd understood it fully only after sitting across from him twice, watching his soft hands fold and unfold while he explained, gently, why terror was inefficient. Dungeons bred martyrs. The Iron Court kept its prisoners in clean rooms with whitewashed walls and adequate food and a single window set too high to see out of, and it let the absence of sky do the work that racks used to do.

Odile Villalobos was in cell eleven.

Wren knew this because she'd bought the knowledge honestly, or as honestly as anything came in her profession: two days of listening, one bribe paid in Consortium scrip that would bounce spectacularly by morning, and one conversation with a guard named Pell who owed her from a smuggling run in Grayharbor's amputated quarter, back when Wren had still pretended her sympathies were negotiable.

Pell met her at the junction stair, keys already in hand, his round face slick with sweat despite the cellar cold.

"You're sure about this," he said. Not a question. A man reciting the words he needed to hear so he could keep moving.

"I'm sure I'm doing it," Wren said. "Whether I'm sure about anything else is my problem, not yours."

He almost smiled. "You always were the worst liar in your family. Your uncle lies with his whole face. You lie like you're apologizing to the truth on the way past."

"That's why I'm good at it," Wren said. "People trust an apology."

Cell eleven's door was iron, but the lock was brass, and brass was a language Wren spoke fluently. She had Pell stand watch at the corner while she worked — two pins, a tension wrench cut from a hatpin, ninety seconds of the old rhythm, the meditative click-and-give of tumblers yielding. She'd learned locks before she'd learned letters. Her uncle had called it the family's first alphabet: everything in the world had a mechanism, and every mechanism opened for someone patient enough.

The lock gave.

Odile sat on the narrow cot in the cell's center, and she did not look surprised. She looked like a woman who had been waiting for a specific knock and had finally heard it. Her wrists were unbound. There were no bruises Wren could see. Vess didn't bruise his assets; he filed them.

"You," Odile said.

"Me." Wren stepped inside and closed the door behind her out of habit, which was absurd — the door was open now, that was the entire point — but some habits were load-bearing. "Edmund is out. Seraphine Dax turned the whole Inquisition inside out yesterday; half the Iron Court is rubble and the other half is lying about it. There's a window. It won't stay open."

"I know about the window." Odile rose. Up close she was thinner than the orchard, thinner than the gate, thinner than the woman who'd caught Edmund at the warden-door with fists and tears. Prison had been sanding her down. But her eyes were the same eyes, and they fixed on Wren with the flat patience of someone assessing weather. "I know because the moss told me. The walls here are old stone underneath the plaster. Old stone remembers being alive. Everything in this building is slowly coming back to life around me, and nobody noticed, because who looks at moss?"

Wren blinked. "You've been—"

"Three days," Odile said. "I'd have had the door off myself by tomorrow. Rust is slow, but it's obedient." Something moved at the edge of her mouth, not quite a smile. "But I'm glad you came, courier. Because I have questions, and you're going to answer them while we walk."

"There's no time—"

"There's exactly as much time as it takes to walk to the window you mentioned," Odile said, "and you're going to spend all of it talking."

They went. Through the cell block's service corridor, past the guard station where Pell stood with his back turned and his heart visibly hammering through his uniform, up the stair where the lamplight thinned, along the gallery where Wren had sat an hour ago watching the ledger-machinery turn.

And Wren talked. Because Odile was right, and because there was a confession lodged in her chest like a swallowed hook, and because — though she'd never have admitted it under torture — she had wanted, for six days, to say these things to someone who would understand the weight of them.

"The Consortium sent me after Edmund in Saltmere," she said, keeping her voice low and even, courier's cadence, information delivered without decoration. "Not to help. To acquire. The map first, the cartographer second, the inheritance if it could be held. My uncle's instruction was explicit: the Salt doesn't go to the crown and it doesn't get destroyed. It goes into the vault under Consortium House, and the Consortium becomes the third kingdom — the one that owns the other two."

"And you took the job."

"I took the job. I'm very good at taking jobs." They reached the ground floor. Beyond the barred window at corridor's end, the night was wrong-colored — orange flicker in the west, where the Inquisition barracks still burned. "I told myself it was just work. Everyone says that. Everyone always says that. The sniffer-beast handlers say it. Vess's clerks say it. It's the prayer of people whose hands are dirty and whose ledgers are clean."

Odile walked beside her, unhurried, matching a pace Wren hadn't realized she'd set. Outside the barred window a bell began tolling — not alarm, something older, the solstice bells maybe, or the city simply grieving in its sleep.

"My grandmother kept bees," Odile said.

Wren faltered a step. "What?"

"Honeybees. Before the Purge, before they burned the farm. And she told me once that a hive will forgive almost anything — robbing, smoke, even a careless hand — except one thing. If a bee starts taking honey and giving nothing back, the others will kill it. Not out of cruelty. Out of arithmetic." Odile looked at her sidelong. "You've been taking honey for nine years, courier Ashgrave. What changed?"

Wren reached the end of the corridor, the door to the yard, and stopped with her hand on the bar.

What changed. She could give the easy answer — Edmund's face in the sinkhole light, the first charm cracking open twelve years of abstinence, the wonder of it, the shame of envying it. She could give the professional answer — Vess's design revealed, the Hunt as theater, the realization that the Consortium wasn't buying an asset but arming a rival, that she'd been a pawn in a game with stakes no vault could hold. Both answers were true. Neither was the true one.

The true one was smaller and worse.

"A child sold me a candle," Wren said. "In Grayharbor, while we were hiding. Four coppers. It never gutters. She'd bought it off some stranger — Edmund, obviously, working charms out of a rented room like a fever they couldn't sweat out. And I bought it because it seemed practical, and then I carried it for a week, and every night it burned the same, and every night I thought: *this is what magic actually is.* Not the Salt. Not kingdoms and inheritances and thirteen-turn moons. A candle that doesn't gutter, sold to a frightened child for four coppers." She pushed the bar. The door swung open onto cold air and burning sky. "My family wants to own the sun. And I couldn't stop thinking about a four-copper candle."

Odile was quiet for three steps across the yard.

"That's the truest thing anyone's ever bribed me with," she said.

"I didn't bribe you."

"You freed me. That's better than a bribe. That's a debt, and I intend to collect." Odile nodded toward the wall — the low section, the drainage arch, the route Wren had mapped and paid for and memorized. "Walk. Talk later. Your window's closing."

* * *

It closed faster than either of them expected.

The drainage arch let out onto the embankment road below the administrative wing, and the embankment road should have been empty — Wren had timed the guard rotations, had paid for the gaps, had built the whole escape on a foundation of purchased silence. But the fires had scrambled everything. Half the garrison was fighting the other half, and the confused halves had started inventing duties, and one invented duty was a patrol on the embankment with lanterns and a sniffer-beast on a chain, its wet black nostrils flaring at air that still tasted of Seraphine Dax's unbinding, of burning iron, of a thousand small unregistered magics waking across the city like frost flowers.

Wren saw them first. She grabbed Odile's sleeve and pulled them both off the road into the shadow of the embankment wall, into a recess that smelled of river mud and old rope.

"Patrol," she breathed. "Sixty seconds. We wait, then we cut down to the water stairs."

Odile pressed back against the stone beside her. Close enough that Wren could feel the strange warmth of her — the orchard-warmth, the living warmth, the thing that made dead stone remember moss. And Wren watched, in the lantern-light leaking around the wall's edge, as the moss on the old stones nearest Odile thickened almost imperceptibly, green deepening like breath returning.

"They'll smell us anyway," Odile whispered. "That beast tastes magic. And I've been growing things in a prison for three days. I'm probably the loudest quiet person in this city."

"Then we don't wait." Wren was already re-planning, discarding the careful architecture of her escape, falling back on the oldest tool in the courier's kit: speed and nerve. "Water stairs, straight down, there's a barge—"

Lantern-light swept the wall above them. A voice: "Hold! Who's there!"

And Wren made the calculation she'd been avoiding for nine years, the one her uncle had designed her entire life to prevent, the one with only one honest answer.

If they ran, the patrol would raise the alarm, and the alarm would close the river, and the window shut. If they stayed hidden, the beast would find them in moments, and the window shut. If—

If she stood up. If she walked out into the lantern-light wearing the slate-gray coat with the silver ash-leaf clasp. If she showed the patrol her credentials and her name and her family's seal, and drew their eyes, and gave Odile the thirty seconds she needed to reach the water alone.

"Wren." Odile's hand closed on her arm. Hard. Stronger than she looked. "Whatever you're calculating, stop it."

"It's not a calculation," Wren said. "That's the problem. I've done nothing but calculate since I was fourteen. This is the first thing I've ever done with the other part." She pulled the letter of passage from her coat and pressed it into Odile's hand — the seal, the authority, the paper that could walk anywhere. "Service tunnel under the granary, third arch, it comes out at the tannery docks. Pell's cousin runs a barge. Give them this; they'll believe the Consortium seal even now, especially now — everyone's still afraid of it. That fear has about an hour left in it. Use it."

"What are you going to do?"

"What I'm best at," Wren said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "Being seen."

She stepped out of the recess.

"Lanterns here!" she called, walking up the embankment road with her chin up and her stride long, every inch the Consortium courier, the woman who owned the table. "Courier Ashgrave, Ashgrave Consortium, official business — I need to report unauthorized activity on the water stairs, there's someone—"

The patrol converged. Six guards and the beast, the beast lunging at the end of its chain with its nose full of Odile's fading trail and Wren's deliberate noise. Wren talked. She talked the way she'd been trained, layered and plausible, a courier's report with just enough panic in it to be credible, and she watched over the lantern-glow as a slim figure in a stolen prison smock slipped along the base of the embankment wall, quick and low, toward the granary arch.

Ten seconds. Twenty.

Then one of the guards — young, sharp-eyed, the dangerous kind — looked past Wren's shoulder at the wall, at the dark, at the smear of green that shouldn't have been growing there in midwinter, and his face changed.

"There's someone—" he started.

And Wren, with the last of her old craft, did the one thing she had sworn she'd never do for the Consortium and found, to her astonishment, she could do easily for its opposite.

She tore the ash-leaf clasp from her own coat and threw it into the river.

"There's nothing there," she said, loudly, clearly, in the voice she used for closing contracts. "There's nothing there, and I am Wren Ashgrave of the Ashgrave Consortium, and I am ordering you to search the water stairs—"

"*Ashgrave,*" said a new voice, and the night curdled.

Chancellor Vess stood at the top of the embankment road in his plain dark coat, flanked by two hunters whose uniforms were scorched and whose faces were blank with the particular exhaustion of men serving a regime that was visibly dying. He descended the slope with his soft hands folded, unhurried, and the patrol parted around him like water.

"Courier Ashgrave," he said pleasantly. "Or should I say, former courier? We received a message from your Consortium this afternoon. Very curious document. It seems you've been dismissed. Embezzlement, they claim. Sabotage. Your uncle was quite thorough." He stopped an arm's length away and regarded her with the mild interest of a man reading a file. "Burned credentials. Cut loose by your own family. And yet here you are, in the middle of my crisis, standing between my patrol and an escaped prisoner." He tilted his head. "You've chosen your side rather late in the war, haven't you?"

"The war isn't over," Wren said. Her voice didn't shake. She was almost proud of that. "Ask your artifact. Ask the moon. Ask the woman who just walked out of your prison carrying a letter with your own seal on it — because you never guarded against us, Chancellor. Nobody ever guards against the table."

Something flickered behind Vess's mildness — the first hairline crack Wren had ever put in it, and she intended to die remembering it.

"Take her," he said quietly.

Hands seized her arms. The letter of passage was gone from her pocket, gone with Odile, gone down the granary tunnel toward the tannery docks and the barge and Edmund. The ash-leaf clasp was sinking somewhere in the black river. Nine years of ledgers, closed.

As they marched her up the embankment road, Wren looked back once — not at the prison, not at Vess, but at the granary arch, dark and empty and successful. Somewhere beyond it, a woman with dirt under her nails and a forged future in her fist was running toward her brother, and the debt Wren owed the world was, for the first time in her adult life, being paid down instead of accrued.

It cost more than she'd expected. Most honest purchases did.

They took her to a cell with a whitewashed wall and a window set too high to see out of, and Wren Ashgrave sat down on the narrow cot, and smiled at the ceiling like a woman who had finally, at long last, gotten the exchange rate right.

* * *

Two floors above, Vess stood at the barred window and watched the fires.

His aide waited respectfully with a dispatch case. "The prisoner Ashgrave — interrogation at dawn?"

"No," Vess said. "No interrogation. She's told us everything worth hearing, whether she knows it or not." He turned from the window, and his mildness had reassembled itself, seamless as ever — but underneath it, for the first time, something moved with urgency. "The sister escaped with Consortium papers. Which means the Consortium's infrastructure is compromised, which means the siblings will move on the Salt within days, using roads we don't control." He handed the aide a slip of paper bearing a single word. "Wake the relic-keepers. All of them. And send word to the flats — I want the artifact positioned before the twelfth turn ends."

The aide hesitated. "Sir. The hunters loyal to Dax—"

"Are traitors, and traitors burn with the rest." Vess smoothed his coat. Outside, far to the east, past the burning barracks and the grieving bells, the moon was rising — swollen, pale, one turn from finished. "Everything converges at the salt flats, gentlemen. Make certain we arrive first."

Below in the cells, Wren lay awake in the dark, counting the tolling of the solstice bells, and did the last calculation of her old life:

*Twelve turns done. One remains.*

*Hold on, Edmund. Hold on, Odile. I bought you a head start — spend it well.*
