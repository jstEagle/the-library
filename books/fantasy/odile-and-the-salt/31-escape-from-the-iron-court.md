## 31. Escape from the Iron Court

The bells began at the wrong hour.

Edmund knew the Iron Court's schedule the way they had once known the tides of Saltmere harbor — by necessity, by survival, by three days of listening through a cell wall while pretending to sleep. The bells rang for shift changes, for executions, for the Chancellor's audiences. They did not ring now. Now they rang all at once, every tower, every pitch, a sound like the city itself screaming.

Then came the light. Not firelight. Not lamplight. Something silver and furious pouring through the high slit of Edmund's cell window, and beneath it a pressure in the air that Edmund felt in their teeth, in the old scar on their palm, in the place behind their sternum where something vast had been waking turn by turn for weeks.

Seraphine Dax had stopped being their weapon.

Edmund pressed their ear to the door and heard what they'd been waiting three days to hear: not order, but its absence. Boots running in two directions. Shouted commands dissolving into shouted questions. A man's voice cracking mid-sentence — *she turned them, she turned the whole rank* — and then a sound Edmund had never heard before and would remember forever: iron armor falling in a courtyard, dozens of pieces at once, hunters setting down their arms not because they were defeated but because they had finally decided what they were.

*Good*, Edmund thought, with a ferocity that frightened them. *Burn it down.*

That was new. That was the Salt talking, probably. Twelve years of smallness did not vanish cleanly; it curdled. Edmund put a hand flat against the stone wall and felt the answering hum come up through their palm like a heartbeat under the world's skin.

*Not yet,* they told it. *First we walk out.*

The lock was already rusting. They could feel it — had been able to feel it since yesterday, since Odile's gift worked slow and patient somewhere below, moss and time doing what crowbars couldn't. But the mechanism needed one more push, and Edmund had spent twelve years refusing to push. They closed their eyes, found the small bright thread of themselves that mapped things, that saw doors where other people saw walls, and laid it over the lock like a hand over a wound.

The tumblers turned with a sound like a sigh.

The corridor outside was chaos in the best possible way. Torch brackets torn from walls, a sniffer-beast's empty chain dragging along the stones, an inquisitor slumped against the wall with his helmet off and his face wet, whispering *I didn't know, I didn't know* to no one. Edmund stepped over him. Somewhere above, Seraphine's voice rang off the vaulted ceiling — not the cold command voice from the prison wagon but something rawer, a woman reading her own indictment aloud:

"—and you let him! You let Vess dose you, leash you, point you at children—"

Edmund ran the other way.

Down. Always down in this place, then up; the Iron Court had been built as a fortress and remodeled as a courthouse and nobody had ever reconciled the two, which meant there were stairs that went nowhere and doors that opened onto drops and, if you knew how to look — and Edmund had always known how to look — a servant's passage behind the records room where the mortar had crumbled just enough for a thin person with a cartographer's sense of negative space to squeeze through.

They were halfway into the gap when hands closed on their ankle.

Edmund kicked, connected with something soft, twisted around — and found themselves looking up at Wren Ashgrave, gray-faced, one arm cradled against her chest at an angle arms don't go, blood dried black down her sleeve, grinning like this was the best day of her life.

"You're hard to buy," she said. "I've decided to steal you instead."

"Wren—" Edmund got a shoulder under her good side, hauled her up. She weighed nothing, which meant she'd lost blood she couldn't afford. "You're supposed to be in the cells."

"I was. Then the scary lady started tearing the roof off, and my escort got religious about self-preservation." She winced as they moved. "Odile's in the yard. East gate. She has horses and a plan and she's very angry at both of us, mostly me, some you."

"The map—"

"Is in your head, isn't it? Where it always was." Wren's grin sharpened. "That's the thing about you cartographers, Edmund. You can confiscate the paper. You can't confiscate the person."

* * *

The east yard was a study in controlled catastrophe. Half the garrison had defected with Seraphine and was holding the west wall against the half that hadn't; between those two fires, the east gate stood open and almost unguarded, and Odile Villalobos stood in front of it with two horses and a face like winter deciding whether to become spring or stay winter forever.

She saw Edmund and something in her broke open — relief first, fierce and unguarded, and then immediately after it the anger, because Odile had never in her life let relief arrive without an invoice.

"You," she said. "Three days. Three days I sat in a cell growing moss through solid rock, and you were upstairs *negotiating*—"

"I was surviving, Odile. It's different."

"It is not different." She grabbed their collar with both hands, and Edmund saw that her fingers were green-stained to the second knuckle, living wood grown through prison stone, and that she was crying without any of the usual shame. "Don't you ever — I burned everything, Ed. Everything but one letter, and that letter says the thing inside you wakes in *two turns*, and if you die in this building I will dig you out of the rubble and kill you myself."

Behind her, Wren made a noise that might have been a laugh or a cough of blood. "Family reunions," she said. "I see where you get the charm."

Odile looked past Edmund at Wren — at the ruined arm, the gray lips — and whatever she'd planned to say to her died. She crossed the yard in four strides and took Wren's weight against her side with a gentleness that would have surprised anyone who hadn't grown up watching her carry dying saplings indoors for the winter.

"Hold on, courier," she said. "You don't get to bleed out owing me money."

"That's — the only reason — I'm alive," Wren managed.

They went through the gate together, three fugitives in a city eating itself, and the last thing Edmund saw of the Iron Court was Seraphine Dax standing on the wall above the west gate with her arms raised and her gift blazing off her like heat-shimmer, holding back a volley of crossbow bolts with a shield of pure force while hunters on both sides of her looked up at her with the expressions of people watching a religion collapse in real time.

Their eyes met across the distance. Edmund didn't know what passed between them — acknowledgment, warning, promise, none of these — only that Seraphine gave the smallest nod, and turned back to her war, and Edmund rode east with the Salt singing louder in their chest than the bells had been.

* * *

They rode until the Court's fires were embers behind them and the road became the salt flats' northern edge, scrubland giving way to white crust that glowed faintly under the fat, nearly-full moon. Two turns left. The sky kept count better than any calendar.

They made camp in the lee of a dead watchtower, and Odile boiled willowbark over a fire no bigger than two fists while Wren slept propped against her saddle, breathing shallow but steady, her arm splinted with a wagon shaft and bound in strips of Edmund's shirt.

"She shouldn't ride tomorrow," Odile said quietly.

"She'll insist on riding tomorrow."

"I know. I've known her four days and I know she'll insist on riding tomorrow." Odile fed the fire a twig. "Tell me what happened up there. All of it. You've been talking to everyone but me since the wagon."

So Edmund told her. The audience with Vess — the clemency, the mercy dressed in silk, the chains around Odile's wrists held up like a merchant displaying wares. The way Edmund had smiled and nodded and asked careful questions while inside their skull the Salt whispered *say yes, say yes, take his offer, take his palace, take everything.* The way they'd walked out of that room knowing they'd have said yes eventually, given another week, another month, another turn of the moon.

"And the mirror," Odile said. "In the vault. You saw yourself in the tyrant."

It wasn't a question. Edmund watched the fire.

"I see him more every night," they said. "He's not a ghost, Odile, he's — a shape. A shape my life fits into if I stop fighting it. Every time I use the gift to save us, it gets easier to imagine using it to rule us. Every charm I make, I think: what would ten thousand of these be worth? What would one big one be worth? What could I build—" They stopped. Their hands had curled into fists without permission. "Grandmother didn't leave us a treasure. She left us a test. And I'm failing it slowly, in private, where nobody can see."

Odile was quiet a long moment. Then she reached across the fire and took Edmund's fist and pried it open, finger by finger, the way she used to when they were children and Edmund clenched their hands during storms.

"Look at your palm," she said.

Edmund looked. In the center, where the scar had been for as long as they could remember — a childhood burn neither of them could account for — the skin had changed. Lines had surfaced, fine and silver, branching like frost or like river deltas seen from very high up. Like a map.

"Oh," Edmund said. Their voice came out very small. "Oh, it's not *in* me like a coin in a pocket. It's in me like — like water in a watershed. Like roads in a country."

"The mirror showed holders," Odile said. "But grandmother's letter said wardens. Wardens aren't owners, Ed. A warden holds the bridge so others can cross." She squeezed their open hand once and let it go. "The question was never whether you're strong enough to hold it. It's whether you're honest enough to give it away. And you've been giving things away your whole miserable life — maps, charms, chances. You just never counted it as strength."

Edmund stared at the silver lines in their palm until their eyes blurred.

"The resting place," they said finally. "The mirror showed it. It's not under the flats. It's not in a vault anywhere. It's—" They laughed, and it came out cracked. "It's wherever I am. It moves with me. Grandmother didn't hide the Salt from the world, Odile. She hid it in her family, and it's been walking around buying apples and selling charms for thirty years."

"Two turns," Odile said. "When the thirteenth completes, it wakes fully. And every gifted soul within a thousand miles feels the call already — you've seen the pilgrims on the roads. If you're still carrying it whole when it wakes..."

"Then whoever stands nearest claims it. Or it claims them." Edmund looked east, toward the deep flats, toward the place where the Sundering War had begun and where, in six weeks, everything would end or begin. "Then we don't run from the Court anymore. We don't even run from Vess. We walk straight into the middle of the flats, and we decide — publicly, in front of everyone the call drags there — what happens next."

"That's insane."

"Yes."

"They'll all come. The called, the Consortium, what's left of the Inquisition, Vess's artifact—"

"All of it. Yes." Edmund fed the fire the last twig. "We spent twelve years learning to hide, Odile. Hiding is over. It ended the moment the mirror showed me my own face. There's nowhere left to run except forward, and the only direction forward is inward." They flexed the silver-mapped hand. "I need to understand what I am before the moon finishes counting. And the only way to do that is to stop running and start listening."

Across the dead camp, Wren stirred, opened one eye, and croaked: "If you two are done having your moment, someone should know the Consortium knows about the flats too. Ashgrave's whole board is converging. And Vess's artifact — the thing he unleashed — it's not following roads. It's following *you*. Straight line. Doesn't eat, doesn't sleep." She sagged back. "Whatever you're planning to do in six weeks, you've got maybe four before it arrives."

Silence, except the wind moving over the salt.

Odile looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at their own mapped palm, and felt, deep beneath the ribs, the vast patient thing turn over in its sleep — closer to waking, closer to speaking, closer to asking the question that had waited thirty years and two wars to be asked.

"Four days," Edmund said, "to learn what I am."

Above them, the moon slid one degree further along its thirteenth turn, and far out on the white flats, something answered.
