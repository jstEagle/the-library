## 32. The Calling Grows Loud

The first of them arrived on a Tuesday, though Odile would remember it later as the day the road began to sing.

She was in the kitchen of the safehouse — a collapsed miller's cottage two days' walk from Grayharbor, chosen by Wren because its cellar had three exits and no history — when she heard the footsteps. Not one set of footsteps. Many. A soft, shuffling percussion, out of rhythm with each other, like rain starting on a tin roof.

She went to the window and saw them coming up the lane.

There were perhaps thirty at first. Later she would learn the number had been closer to eighty by dusk, and by the following morning there were hundreds strung along the roads for miles, like beads drawn toward a magnet buried in the earth. But that first hour she counted thirty: farmers in their muddy boots, a woman still wearing her baker's apron dusted white with flour, an old man in a nightshirt who had clearly walked out of his house mid-sleep, bare feet split and bleeding on the frozen ruts. They were not running. That was the thing Odile could not shake, the detail that made her skin crawl even now, weeks after — they were not running. They walked the way people walk to church. The way people walk to a funeral of someone they loved very much. Reverent. Unhurried. Certain.

Their eyes were open but their faces were empty of everything except a kind of terrible contentment.

"Edmund," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

Her brother was already in the doorway behind her. She heard their breath catch — a small sound, quickly swallowed. Twelve years of practice at swallowing sounds.

"I felt them at dawn," Edmund said quietly. "Like... warmth on the back of my neck. I thought I was dreaming."

"They're walking *toward you*."

"I know." Edmund's hands had gone to the doorframe, gripping the wood as if the cottage itself might slide. "I know, Odile."

* * *

By midday there were more, and Wren had climbed onto the millstone wall to watch the road with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the particular way that meant she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Two turns left," she said. "That's what your letter said. Thirteen turns total, eleven done, solstice six weeks out." She glanced back at Odile. "So why are they here *now*?"

"Because the bell doesn't wait until it's struck to begin humming," Odile said. Grandmother's voice, coming out of her own mouth. She hated that. "It hums from the moment someone decides to pick up the hammer."

Wren snorted, but without humor. "Lovely. Very comforting." She hopped down from the wall. Below them, the crowd had stopped — maybe forty people now, arranged in the lane before the cottage not like a mob but like an audience waiting for a play to start. Some had laid down bundles at their feet. Odile squinted and saw bread, a jug of cider, a hen in a wicker crate, a small carved horse, a folded blanket. Offerings.

"Gods," Wren breathed. "They've brought gifts."

"They've brought everything they have." Odile recognized the woman with the flour-dusted apron now — Maren, from the village of Twosprings, four days east. Odile had bought seed stock from her husband two winters ago. Maren's face wore the same placid smile as all the others, and that smile was worse than weeping would have been. "Look at them, Wren. These aren't zealots. This is a schoolteacher. That's a shepherd. That girl can't be more than fourteen."

"Then what do you call it?"

Odile didn't answer, because she didn't have a word yet. She would find one eventually — she'd call it *the Gentle Horror*, in the account she never wrote down — but standing there on the wall with the winter wind pulling at her coat, all she had was the sight of Maren's flour-white apron and the hen complaining softly in its crate, and the understanding settling into her bones like cold water into a well: these people had not chosen anything. Something had chosen them. And it had used her brother's heartbeat as the hook.

* * *

Edmund refused to go out to them.

For three days Edmund refused, and Odile learned what refusal looked like on her brother: it looked like sitting very still in the corner of the cottage with their palms pressed flat against the floorboards, as if holding down a lid. It looked like not eating. It looked like answering questions two beats too late, because somewhere underneath, some vast patient tide was whispering through the Salt in their blood, and every word spoken aloud had to be hauled up from a long way underwater.

"You have to send them home," Odile said on the third evening. She'd said it every day. She said it again anyway, because that was what she did; she was the sister who kept saying things.

"I can't."

"You can tell them to go. You don't have to command them — just speak to them. Tell them the calling is a lie."

"It isn't a lie, Odile." Edmund lifted their head, and in the firelight Odile saw something in her brother's face that frightened her far more than the crowds did: exhaustion, yes, and fear, yes, but underneath both, faint and shameful and unmistakable — *pleasure*. "That's the horror of it. It's not a lie. They feel what I feel. The world waking up. Every gifted soul within a hundred miles has been half-deaf their whole lives and doesn't know it, and now something is humming the note they've been missing since birth. Of course they're walking toward it. I'd walk toward it too."

"You ARE walking toward it. You haven't moved from that floor in three days, but you're walking toward it just the same."

Edmund flinched. The lid pressed down harder; Odile watched her brother's knuckles whiten against the boards.

"That's not fair."

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing about this is fair. Fairness died with Grandmother's orchard." She crouched in front of Edmund, took their chin in her hand — the way Gran used to, when one of them had lied about something small and needed to be looked at properly. "But listen to me. Listen. Whatever it's offering you, whatever it's letting you feel through them — their devotion, their warmth, all those hearts pointed at you like compass needles — that is not love. Love walks up to a door and knocks and waits to be let in. This kicks the door in while singing hymns. Do you understand the difference?"

For a long moment Edmund said nothing. Then, very quietly:

"When I look at them, I feel like a god, Odile. And when I stop looking at them, I feel like a drain that everything in the world is pouring into. There's no third feeling. I've checked. I've checked for three days."

"Then we make a third feeling." She stood, joints cracking in the cold. "We feed them, we warm them, and tomorrow morning we walk out among them and tell them the truth: the thing calling you belongs to a person, and that person says go home. And if they won't go home—" her jaw tightened— "then we move. We take the Veilroads and we put fifty miles between you and them, and then another fifty, and we keep doing it until the thirteenth turn passes or the moon falls out of the sky."

"And if moving makes it worse? If every mile I run, the call gets louder, reaches farther?" Edmund finally rose, swaying slightly, twelve years of discipline visibly fraying at every seam. "You read the letters, Odile. You deciphered the star-charts at the observatory. When the moon completes its turn, it won't matter where I am. It will call, and everyone gifted will hear it, and either I'll be standing somewhere ready to choose—or I'll be dragged to the choice by ten thousand hands that love me and have never met me."

Outside, in the dark, the crowd sang.

They had started at dusk — a low wordless drone, dozens of voices finding the same pitch without instruction, without leader, without any agreement Odile could detect. It wasn't a hymn she knew. It wasn't a hymn anyone knew. It sounded, horribly, like the note a wineglass sings just before it shatters.

Edmund closed their eyes. Odile watched her brother's face soften despite everything, watched the pleasure rise again like oil through water, and felt the ground shift under everything she believed about the person she'd crossed a kingdom and burned an orchard's worth of grief to find.

* * *

On the fourth day, Odile went out among them alone.

She told herself it was strategy — Edmund couldn't risk proximity, Wren was scouting the eastern roads, so it fell to her. But honesty, that old unfashionable virtue, made her admit as she stepped over the cottage threshold that part of her simply needed to see their faces up close. Needed to confirm they were people and not puppets. Needed, in some stubborn orchard-keeper's corner of her heart, to believe that growing things could not be commandeered this way.

Maren was nearest. Odile knelt in front of her in the frost-browned grass.

"Maren. From Twosprings. Your husband sells seed stock. Your daughter has a crooked tooth she's proud of."

"Yes," Maren said, smiling. Her eyes focused somewhere past Odile's shoulder, past the cottage, past the horizon — focused, Odile realized with a lurch, on nothing visible at all. On something only she could hear.

"Maren, listen to me. What called you here — did it ask? Did it give you a choice?"

"Oh, it gave me a choice." Maren's smile widened by a degree, serene as a painted saint. "It asked if I wanted the world to be *alive* again. It asked if I was tired. Everyone's tired. Aren't you tired?"

"What happens when you reach him? Did it say?"

"We'll rest," Maren said, with the simple certainty of a child describing heaven. "We'll be near. That's all anyone wants. To be near."

Odile reached out and touched the woman's hand. It was freezing — Maren had stood in the lane for four days and nights, apparently neither eating nor sleeping nor noticing — and beneath the cold, Odile felt something else. Her gift woke in her palm the way it always did near living things, that green thread of attention, moss-minded and patient. And what she felt in Maren was not emptiness. That was the worst discovery of all. The woman wasn't hollowed out. She was *full* — brimming with a borrowed radiance that poured off her like heat off a stone, a radiance Odile recognized, because she'd felt its source across a campfire, asleep, breathing, ordinary: Edmund. The Salt in her brother's blood had overflowed, and these people had come with cups.

And none of it — none — had been given freely. It had poured out of Edmund unasked, the way a wound bleeds, and the crowd had drunk without knowing they were drinking.

Odile stood up. Her legs shook.

Behind her, she heard the cottage door, and then Edmund's boots on the frost, and then a sound she had never heard before in her life: her brother weeping in front of strangers.

"I'm sorry," Edmund said to them, voice cracking across the silent lane. Eighty faces turned toward the sound with identical tenderness. "Oh, gods, I'm so sorry. Go home. Please. Please go home. Whatever you think you're hearing, it isn't asking permission, and I am asking. Go home to your houses. Eat something. Sleep in beds. Forget the song."

The crowd smiled. The crowd did not move. Somewhere in the middle distance, on the eastern road, new figures crested the hill — twenty more, thirty, a thin dark line of the gentle and the desperate converging out of the winter haze.

Edmund turned to Odile, tears freezing bright on their cheeks, and said the sentence she had been dreading since the observatory, since the letters, since the mirror in the drowned abbey showed them a tyrant's face wearing their own:

"I don't think I can hold it in anymore, Odile. It's leaking. Every hour it leaks more, and they drink it, and they come, and I can't—" their voice broke entirely. "I'm going to become the bell whether I want to or not. Unless I choose first."

"Then we choose first," Odile said, and took her brother's frozen hands in hers, and over Edmund's shoulder watched the eastern road fill with walking pilgrims, and felt, deep beneath her own ribs, in the quiet garden-place where her small green gift lived, a faint and distant hum begin to answer.
