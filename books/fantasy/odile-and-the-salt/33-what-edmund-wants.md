## 33. What Edmund Wants

The camp was asleep, and Edmund was not.

They sat at the edge of the salt flats where the scrub gave way to white, with the moon two turns from full and hanging low enough that its light lay on the ground like spilled milk. Behind them, the fire Odile had built ticked and settled. Wren slept with her wounded side toward the flames and a knife under her hand even in dreams. Somewhere out in the dark, the called were still walking.

Edmund could feel them. That was the part no one had warned them about — that the Salt's waking would not be a sound but a gravity, a slow pull they could sense through the soles of their feet, like standing on the deck of a ship and knowing, without looking, which way the water ran. Hundreds of gifted souls had felt the bell toll when the observatory's charts said it would, and they had gotten up from their lives and begun to walk. Farmers. Midwives. A boy of eleven who'd been hiding a gift for lighting candles with his breath. They walked toward Edmund the way iron filings walk toward a lodestone: not because they chose to, but because choosing was no longer part of the arrangement.

*Two turns,* Edmund thought. *Then thirteen.*

They took the mirror-shard out of their coat.

It was no bigger than a playing card now — a fragment broken off the vault mirror during the collapse at the abbey, carried out in the chaos by Edmund's own hand without any conscious decision to do so. Odile had asked about it once, early on, and Edmund had lied, and then had lied about lying, and since then neither of them had spoken of it. The shard showed nothing when anyone else looked into it. When Edmund looked, it showed the tyrant.

He was there tonight, as he always was. A warden of some earlier age, his face half Edmund's and half something older and harder, dressed in robes that might have been white once and had gone the color of old bone. In the vault's great mirror he had been whole; here, in the shard, he was a portrait seen through frosted glass. It didn't matter. Edmund knew every line of him by now.

"Say it," the tyrant said. He always said it first. He never made Edmund go first. That was the courtesy of it, and the cruelty — he offered the opening the way a confessor offers absolution before the sin has even been named.

"I'm not going to say anything," Edmund whispered. "You're not real."

"I'm as real as your wanting." The bone-colored face tilted. "I'm just the shape it takes when you let yourself look at it. You've been looking at it all week."

Edmund turned the shard over, but the reflection came through the back of it anyway, the way truth comes through a wall you've built too thin.

Because it was true. And because there was no one awake to hear it, and because twelve years of silence had become a weight they could no longer carry alone, Edmund said it.

"I want it."

The words went out over the salt flats and did not echo. The salt drank them.

"I want it," they said again, louder, and something in their chest unlocked like a door that had been barred since the Purge. "I want Vess to kneel in the ash of his own Iron Court. I want every law that ever put a neighbor in chains struck from every book in every kingdom at once, so hard that the ink burns off the vellum. I want the burned ones back — grandmother, and Master Corvin who taught me my first glyphs, and the girl in Saltmere whose name I never learned, the one they dragged away screaming our stall's name while we hid behind wards and let it happen." Their voice cracked and they kept going, because stopping now would have meant dying a little. "I want the Sundering undone. I want the Veilroads open and the realms stitched back together and every child born with a gift to grow up thinking it's as ordinary as brown eyes. I want—" and here the honesty turned on them, sharp as a hook, "—I want them all to *see*. Everyone who ever made me small. I want to stand in the market square of Saltmere with the thirteenth moon overhead and let them understand what they spent twelve years hunting."

The tyrant smiled.

It was not a cruel smile. That was the horror of it, the thing Edmund had come to understand over these long nights with the shard: the tyrant smiled the way a teacher smiles at a student who has finally grasped the lesson. Warmly. Proudly. With love, even. His face was half Edmund's, and when he smiled, Edmund felt their own mouth try to follow.

"You see?" he said gently. "Every want on that list is just. Every single one. That's how it works. The Salt doesn't tempt people with wickedness. It tempts them with justice. It hands you your grief and says *this is fuel*, and it isn't even lying."

"I know," Edmund said.

"Do you? Because you're still talking about it like it's outside you. Like it's a coin someone might put in your hand." The tyrant leaned closer, and the frosted glass of the shard seemed to clear, and for one vertiginous moment his eyes were Edmund's own eyes, looking back at themselves from inside. "It isn't outside. It woke in you the day you opened your first Veilroad. Everything since has just been deciding what to do about it. And you have decided, haven't you. You decided the night you broke the seal on the vault. You decided when you started carrying me around in your pocket like a keepsake. All that's left is the announcement."

Edmund closed their fist around the shard until the edges bit.

That was when they heard the footsteps in the scrub, unhurried, familiar — and knew, with the sick lurch of someone caught mid-confession, exactly how long they had been there.

* * *

Odile came and sat down beside them without asking. She had a blanket over her shoulders and dirt on her hands, always dirt on her hands now; the orchard-gift had grown restless since the calling began, and things sprouted wherever she knelt too long. Even here, on the margin of the salt, a thread of green moss had followed her across the stones like a cat that wouldn't be left home.

She didn't look at the shard. She looked at Edmund, and waited, the way she used to wait at the orchard gate when they were children and Edmund had done something wrong and hadn't confessed yet. She had always been better at waiting than Edmund was at lying.

"How much did you hear?"

"Enough." She pulled the blanket half around Edmund's shoulders too, an old habit, unthinking. "The part about Vess kneeling. The part about the burned ones coming back." A pause. "The part about everyone seeing."

Edmund stared out at the flats. Far away, at the limit of sight, a tiny orange point moved against the white — one of the called, still walking, still coming. "Then you heard everything worth hearing."

"I heard a person being honest for the first time in twelve years," Odile said. "Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how long I've watched you lie? To clients. To Wren. To inquisitors, to neighbors, to me, to yourself. You lie the way other people breathe." She said it without heat, the way she reported weather. "And tonight, alone, in the dark, you told the truth. To him, but still. You told it."

"To *him*," Edmund repeated bitterly, and held up the shard between two fingers. "Your brother sits up at night having conversations with the man who started the Sundering War. Congratulations on the family reunion."

"He didn't start it." Odile took the shard — Edmund didn't resist; their fingers simply opened, and later they would think about that, about how easily they surrendered it, about what that ease meant — and held it up to the moonlight. Her reflection showed only herself: a tired woman with their grandmother's jaw. "Grandmother's letters said he inherited it same as us. Same blood, same burden, same bell ringing in his bones. He just answered differently." She turned the shard so the tyrant's face caught the light, and Edmund saw him flinch — actually flinch, the smile dropping for an instant into something rawer. Odile noticed too. Of course she did. "He doesn't like me," she observed.

"He doesn't like being *seen*."

"No," Odile said. "He doesn't. Because he's not a ghost, Miri. He's a possibility. And possibilities can't survive being looked at straight."

She said the name. *Miri.* Nobody had said it in twelve years. Edmund had crossed it out of their own mouth the night they fled home — a cartographer needed a name that left no trail, and *Mirabel Villalobos* was a trail a mile wide. They had been Edmund for so long that the old name sounded like it belonged to someone dead. Hearing it now, in their sister's voice, on the edge of the salt, did something to their chest that they refused to examine.

"Why are you here, Odile?" they asked quietly. "To talk me down? To take the shard and throw it in the flats? Wren would. Wren's been giving me looks for a week. She thinks I'm going to turn."

"Wren thinks in knives. It's her best tool and her worst one." Odile folded the shard into her own pocket, deliberately, where Edmund could see her do it — not confiscating, she seemed to say, just holding. "I'm here because you need to hear this while it's still tonight and not history. I need you to listen, and I need you to believe that I'm not afraid of you."

"Aren't you?"

"Yes," she said. "Terrified. Since the observatory. Since I watched three hundred strangers stop eating because something in them wanted to walk toward my little brother more than they wanted to live." She met their eyes. "But fear isn't the same as doubt. I'm afraid of you the way I'm afraid of the sea. Not because it means harm. Because it's bigger than I am, and it doesn't know its own strength."

"The called don't want *me*," Edmund said. "They want—"

"They want what you're carrying. I know. And here's the question I've been chewing for six weeks, so chew it with me." She turned to face them fully, knees drawn up, the way she'd sat in the orchard rows a hundred years ago, a hundred lifetimes ago, planning raids on the pantry. "If the Salt makes everyone walk toward its holder — if it pulls the gifted like a tide — then tell me why it pulled *you* to the vault. Why it led you across my land, through the warden-gates, down to grandmother's letters. Why every step of this road has been *yours*, chosen, stumbling, arguing the whole way. If it were simple possession, you'd have marched straight and quiet like the called do." She tapped their chest, once, over the heart. "Something in there is still steering. Something in there still gets to choose. That's what I'm betting on."

"And if you lose the bet?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. Out on the flats, the far-off walker's light paused, then resumed.

"Then I'll do what gardeners do," she said. "When a tree grows wrong, you don't burn the orchard. You prune. You stake it. You tie the crooked limb to a straight one and wait a few seasons, and usually — not always, but usually — it grows true. I've been tying myself to you since we shared a crib, Miri. I'm not going to stop now because the limb got heavy."

"That's not—" Edmund's throat closed. They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "You should be holding a knife. Wren would hold a knife. Anyone sane would hold a knife."

"Anyone sane burned orchards in the Purge too, and look how that worked out for the world." She took their wrists and lowered their hands, gently, the way you calm a spooked animal. "Look at me."

They looked. And it was unbearable, because there was no steel in her face at all — only their sister, only the girl who had hidden her gift by making the orchard's blooming look like luck, who had stayed behind when Edmund fled, who had tended a dying grove for twelve years on the chance that the family would someday need somewhere to come home to. Love without armor. It was the most dangerous thing anyone had ever pointed at Edmund, and they had faced sniffer-beasts and inquisitors and the Consortium's enforcers, and none of it had come close.

"I want those things you said," Odile told them. "All of them. Vess kneeling. The laws burning. Grandmother back. I want them so badly I can't sleep either. The difference between us isn't that you're corrupt and I'm pure. The difference is that I never had the option, so I got very good at carrying the wanting without feeding it." Her voice softened. "You're going to feed it, though. Aren't you. Just a little. Just to see."

Edmund couldn't deny it. That was the honest horror of the last weeks: not that they had wanted the power, but that they had *tasted* it — the sinkhole charm on the flats, the Veilroads opening at a touch, the called turning toward them in their hundreds like flowers toward sun — and the taste had been the finest thing they had ever known. Twelve years of abstinence had been discipline. One month of inheritance had nearly undone it.

"Just to see," they admitted.

"Then let's make a bargain. Sibling to sibling. No wards, no knives, no lies." She held out her open hand, palm up, the way wardens sealed oaths in grandmother's stories. "Two turns left. When the thirteenth completes, you don't decide alone. Whatever the Salt offers — whatever it whispers, however beautiful — you bring it to me first, and we look at it together in the daylight. And if I say it's the tyrant smiling through you, you trust me over your own certainty. Can you do that? Can you agree to distrust yourself?"

"It's the hardest thing you could ask."

"I know." Her hand stayed open. "I'm asking it anyway. Because the man in the glass never doubts himself, Miri. That's the whole lesson. That's the entire war, right there. Certainty."

Far out on the salt flats, the little orange light stopped.

Edmund noticed it because the noticing had become instinct — they felt the called the way they felt the Veilroads, in the teeth, in the marrow. This one had walked without pause for two days. Now it stood still. And then, slowly, it wasn't alone anymore. Another light kindled beside it. Then a third, further out, and a fourth, and Edmund's stomach dropped as they understood: the scattered walkers were finding each other. Converging. Organizing into something with shape and direction, like drops of mercury rolling together into a bead.

"Odile," they breathed.

She followed their gaze and went very still. "That's not how the calling works. They walk. They don't gather."

"I know."

Unless, Edmund thought, with a cold sliding feeling beneath the sternum, unless the calling had changed. Unless the bell had rung again, higher, sharper, and this time it wasn't calling the gifted home.

Unless something had called *them*.

The lights on the flats began to move — all together now, a constellation of small fires wheeling eastward, away from Edmund's camp, away from Edmund entirely, toward the horizon where the sky above the old war-fields had lately been prone to flicker with light that no storm could explain. Toward the direction, Wren's scouts reported, where the Iron Court had been moving wagons under canvas. Toward whatever Chancellor Vess had unearthed from the vaults of the old war, and was learning, in his desperation, how to wake.

The Salt turned over in Edmund's chest like a sleeper disturbed by thunder, and for the first time, it spoke without being asked:

*Hurry.*

Odile's hand was still open between them. Edmund took it, and gripped it hard, and stood.

"Get Wren," they said. "Wake the camp. We ride east within the hour — and Odile?" Their sister looked back, firelight on her face. "Whatever happens next, remember the bargain. Daylight. Together. Don't let me forget it either."

Out on the flats, the called were running now, all of them, toward a dawn that was coming from the wrong direction.

And above the eastern horizon, low and red and wrong, the moon began to bleed.
