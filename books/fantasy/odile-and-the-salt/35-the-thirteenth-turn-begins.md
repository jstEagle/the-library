## 35. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The moon did not rise so much as arrive.

Edmund felt it before they saw it — a pressure behind the sternum, like a hand pressing flat against the inside of their ribs. They had been walking the salt flats for two days, following a road that existed only because they remembered it, and now, as the sun drowned in the west, the thirteenth moon came up over the eastern haze like a coin flipped by some enormous indifferent god.

It was wrong-colored. That was the first thing everyone noticed. Not silver, not white — a deep bruised gold, the color of candlelight through honey, and it made the salt flats glow as though the whole world were lit from beneath.

"It's early," Odile said beside them. Her voice was steady, but her hands were not; she kept flexing them, opening and closing, as if she could shake something out of her fingers.

"The turn completes at true midnight," Edmund said. "The charts were right."

"I hate that the charts were right."

Behind them, Wren rode slung across a pack-mule like cargo, her bandaged shoulder seeping through the linen in a slow brown bloom. She had refused to be left behind at the observatory with a tone that suggested she would crawl after them on her elbows if necessary, and so here she was, gray-lipped and furious about it, which Edmund had come to understand was simply how Wren Ashgrave expressed gratitude for being alive.

"Tell me again," Wren said, "why we're walking toward the thing every sane person in two kingdoms is running from."

"Because it called us," Edmund said. "And because if we don't answer, Vess will."

"And that's better?"

"No," Edmund admitted. "But it's ours."

The flats stretched to every horizon, blinding even in dying light, cracked into vast pale plates like the floor of a dried sea — which, the old letters said, was exactly what it had been, once, before the Sundering War boiled the water out of it and left the Salt behind. Somewhere out there, under six feet of crust, lay the place where everything began. Edmund could feel it pulling at them with every step, a low tidal ache, patient as geology.

And they were not alone in feeling it.

They crested a rise of crusted salt an hour past sundown and stopped, all three of them, because the horizon ahead was moving.

Not wind. Not heat-shimmer. People. Hundreds of them, then, as the light of the strange moon strengthened, thousands — a dark tide flowing across the white flats from every direction at once, converging on the same invisible center. Edmund could hear them now, faintly: no war-cries, no drums. Just footsteps. Thousands upon thousands of footsteps, unhurried, unhesitating, the sound of a congregation arriving.

"The called," Odile whispered.

They had seen the first of them three nights ago — a baker from Grayharbor who had walked out of his shop mid-loaf and simply started east, eyes soft, smiling faintly, humming a tune no one had taught him. Since then the roads had filled. Farmers. Fishwives. Children. Old soldiers of both armies. Anyone with a drop of gift in their blood, however thin, however long suppressed — the Salt's call had reached into them like a bell-rope and rung, and they had answered without knowing why.

"They're not being dragged," Edmund said quietly. It was important to say it aloud, to test whether it was still true. "Look at their faces."

Odile looked. Her jaw tightened. "No," she said. "Worse. They're *glad*."

That was the horror of it, Edmund thought. Not compulsion — invitation. The Salt was waking after twelve hundred years of sleep, and its first act was not conquest but homecoming. Every gifted soul in the world was being told, in a voice only they could hear, that they were wanted. That they could come home. And the gentle ones came weeping with relief, and the desperate ones came running, and none of them — not one — had chosen this.

Except Edmund. Except Odile. The wardens' blood heard the call too, but louder, and underneath it, something else: a question. *Will you take me up?*

Edmund's hands ached. They had ached for six weeks, since the mirror in the drowned abbey, since the tyrant's face had worn theirs. The ache had been growing ever since, filling them like water fills a vessel, and tonight, under the thirteenth moon, Edmund could feel the vessel nearly full.

"Edmund." Odile caught their arm. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"They're doing the thing. The mirror thing." She said it flatly, refusing to flinch, and Edmund loved her desperately for the refusal. "Gold at the edges. Like the moon."

Edmund turned away and looked at their own reflection in a pane of salt-crusted ice and saw that she was right. Their eyes had gone the color of the sky tonight, gold bleeding inward from the rims, and when they breathed, the breath tasted of lightning about to become a decision.

*Soon,* said something vast and warm at the base of their skull. Not words, exactly. More like the memory of words, the way you remember sunlight after years underground. *Soon, warden.*

"Not yet," Edmund muttered.

"You're talking to it again," Odile said.

"I'm negotiating with it. There's a difference."

"There really isn't."

* * *

They made camp on a ridge of uplifted salt — a low spine of rock and crystal that gave them sightlines in every direction — and that was where they saw the sky tear.

It began as a smudge above the northern flats, near the old battlefield the letters called the Anvil, where the last stand of the Sundering War had been fought and lost. A smudge like heat-haze, except there was no heat in the night air. Then the smudge brightened. Then it *opened*.

The sound reached them a full breath later: a crack like the world's largest bone breaking, rolling across the flats, and every one of the thousands of marching figures below stumbled, moaned, clutched their heads — and then resumed walking, faster now, drawn toward the wound in the sky as well as toward Edmund.

Through the tear, Edmund could see another sky. A red one. And things moving in it.

"Oh," Wren said from her bedroll, propping herself up on her good arm, her courier's composure finally cracking clean through. "Oh, that's bad. That is *spectacularly* bad. What is that?"

"That's what Vess unleashed," Edmund said. Their mouth had gone dry. "The bound artifact. From the old war. Grandmother's letters mentioned it — the Sunderers didn't just fight each other. They fought *that*. And when the war ended, someone chained it behind the sky and threw away the key."

"And Vess found the key."

"Vess found the key."

Below the tear, lights were moving — lanterns, torches, the cold blue glare of inquisitor-lamps. Edmund could make out ranks now, organized movement amid the wandering tide of the called: the Iron Court had come in force, and at their center, on a platform of black iron that had not been there yesterday, stood a figure in chancellor's white, arms raised, conducting the tearing of heaven like a symphony.

And between the Court's ranks and the platform, scattered and outnumbered and burning like sparks on iron, moved a smaller force. Defected hunters. Seraphine's people. Edmund could see their gifts flashing in the dark — small things, domestic things, a hunter who made walls of hardened air, a hunter whose hands shed cold, a hunter riding a construct of woven shadow — and at the point of the wedge, a figure in a torn grey coat moving with terrible economy, striking down inquisitor after inquisitor, and even at this distance Edmund knew the way she moved.

"Seraphine's attacking the platform," Odile said. "She's trying to reach Vess before he finishes."

"She won't make it." Edmund could see it with the new sight, the gold-rimmed sight: the artifact's tear widening by inches every minute, fed by something Vess was feeding it, and the Chancellor standing inside a lattice of stolen wards that would burn anyone who touched them. "She needs a distraction. She needs the Court looking somewhere else."

Odile looked at them. Edmund looked back. Neither of them said it, because saying it would make it true.

Then Wren said it. "You'd have to call them. All of them. Every called soul on these flats would turn toward you if you raised your hand, and Vess's entire army would have to deal with ten thousand people who won't stop walking." She was pale, sweating, grinning like a woman watching a card game she'd already lost. "You'd have to use it, Edmund. Just a little. Just enough."

"Just enough," Edmund repeated. The words tasted like the first sip of wine after a long fast. That was the terrifying part — how good the taste was.

"Or," Odile said, very quietly, "we walk down there as ourselves, three nobodies from nowhere, and we find another way."

"The tear's going to finish opening in an hour whether we find another way or not."

"Then we spend the hour finding it anyway."

* * *

They spent the hour descending toward the Anvil along a dead riverbed, Wren navigating by stars she'd charted in a dozen realms, Odile walking point with her gift coiled tight — though even coiled, it leaked. Grass sprouted in Edmund's footprints. Actual grass, green and impossible, pushing up through salt crust in a trail behind them like a wake. The called noticed. Some of them turned to follow the greening path instead of the bell-call, and Edmund understood with a lurch that Odile's small stubborn gift was *competing* with the Salt's summons, and winning, at least for a few.

"You're doing that on purpose," Edmund said.

"I'm doing nothing on purpose. My gift has always been a weed." But her chin was up, defiant, and more stragglers joined the green trail, blinking, confused, *themselves* again for a few steps at a time.

The Anvil resolved out of the dark as they drew close: a plain of fused glass-salt where the old war's final blow had melted the ground, and rising from its center, the iron platform, and above it the tear — huge now, a ragged mile-long gash in the night, through which the red sky pressed like something leaning its weight against a door. Chains of light still held it, ancient chains anchored at seven points around the battlefield, but Edmund could see four of the seven anchors dimming, corroding, Vess's engineers crawling over them like white ants.

And the armies of the called were arriving. They poured onto the glass plain from every side and stopped there, forming a silent ring miles wide, faces upturned, waiting. For midnight. For the moon. For whatever came next.

For Edmund, though none of them knew it.

"We can't get through that ring," Odile murmured. "There's no gap."

"There's a gap." Edmund pointed — not at the ring, but at the western anchor, where the fighting was thickest. Sparks rained off the chain of light. Seraphine's wedge had almost reached it. "There."

"That's not a gap, that's a battle."

"It's a gap with a battle in it. We go in behind her people while the Court's busy." Edmund flexed their aching hands. The Salt surged eagerly against the inside of their skin, offering — always offering — and for one vertiginous second Edmund let themselves feel the whole shape of it: every called soul on the plain, waiting to be aimed. One gesture. One word. Ten thousand devoted soldiers who would march into the tear itself if asked.

The mirror-tyrant smiled through the thought, and Edmund slammed the door on him so hard their teeth clicked.

"Edmund?" Odile's hand on their sleeve. "Where'd you just go?"

"Nowhere," Edmund lied. "I'm here. Let's move."

* * *

They crossed the ring of the called at a dead run, and the called parted for them.

That was the worst moment yet. Edmund had braced for grasping hands, for the mindless tide, but the thousands of faces simply turned as they passed, and the crowd *opened*, gently, reverently, the way water opens around a keel. An old woman reached out as Edmund went by and brushed her fingers against Edmund's coat hem and sighed like a pilgrim touching a shrine, and Edmund felt her gift — a tiny thing, bread that rose twice as high, nothing, a crumb of the Salt's attention — flicker toward them like a moth toward a lamp.

*They know,* the Salt purred. *They know their warden walks among them.*

*I'm not your warden yet,* Edmund thought back, and hated that *yet* had slipped in.

The battle at the western anchor was chaos given choreography. Inquisitors in white-and-gold held a perimeter around the dimming chain-anchor, and Seraphine's defectors hammered at them in waves, and above it all the anchor-chain groaned and spat light like a live wire. Edmund, Odile, and Wren came at the perimeter from the blind side, through a smoke-bank one of the hunters had conjured, and hit the line where it was thinnest.

What happened next happened too fast for dignity. Odile's gift bloomed — vines, actual vines, wrenched out of dead ground by sheer furious will, tangling inquisitor legs and dragging two of them down — and Wren, one-armed, fought with a courier's knife and a smuggler's dirty pragmatism, and Edmund —

Edmund used it. Just a little. Just enough.

A gesture, half-formed, barely a decision: the nearest inquisitor's iron wards *unlocked*, every latch and buckle and binding-thread coming open at once, armor falling off him like fruit off a shaken branch. He stared at Edmund in naked horror, and Edmund felt the pull to do it again, to do it to all of them, to do it *bigger*, and the gold in their vision flared—

—and a wall of cold slammed into them, and they were on their knees in the salt, gasping, and Seraphine Dax was standing over them with frost smoking off her bare hands and her unbound gift roaring around her like weather.

"Don't," she said. Her voice shook. Her eyes — Edmund saw, with a strange calm, that her eyes were also rimmed in gold now, the catalyst's work, the calling's work — her eyes were terrified. "Whatever you were about to do next, don't. I can feel you from here. Do you understand? I've been able to feel you for six weeks. You're not a person to me anymore, most days. You're a *bell*. So don't ring."

"Good to see you too," Edmund wheezed.

"Inquisitor Dax!" One of her hunters, bloodied, pointing north. "The third anchor's gone! The tear's accelerating!"

Seraphine looked at the sky. Everyone looked at the sky. The tear had doubled, and through it the red otherworld leaned closer, and shapes were gathering at the edge of the wound — vast, patient, *hungry* shapes, the things the Sunderers had loosed on each other once before, waiting for the door to open wide enough.

Midnight was twenty minutes away. The thirteenth moon hung directly overhead now, fat and golden and impossibly close, and Edmund could feel the turn completing inside their own chest, gear by gear, click by click, the inheritance waking to its final degree.

*Now,* said the Salt, gentle as a mother. *Now, warden. Take me up, and I will close the sky myself. I will give you the strength. You need only say yes.*

And Edmund knew — with the absolute clarity of the last hour before everything changes — that it wasn't lying. Say yes, and the tear would seal. Say yes, and Vess would kneel. Say yes, and ten thousand called souls would sing. All it cost was everything, forever, and Edmund stood there on the broken glass of an ancient battlefield with the offer glowing in their veins like swallowed dawn, and Odile was shouting their name, and Seraphine was shouting it too, and somewhere below the salt the primordial heart of the world beat once, twice, three times, counting down—

The fourth anchor died.

The sky screamed.

And Edmund opened their mouth to answer.
