## 36. The Choice at the Center

The moon finished its turn above the salt flats, and the sky tore open like wet paper.

Edmund stood at the center of everything — the exact middle of the white plain, where the light of Vess's artifact bled down in ragged ribbons and the Salt rose through them like breath through a keyhole. They could feel it now without trying. That was the horror of it. For twelve years they had built walls against the gift, brick by careful brick, and tonight every wall had come down not because Edmund broke them but because the Salt had simply walked through, the way a tide walks through a child's sandcastle.

*You are mine,* it said. Or perhaps Edmund said it. The distinction had stopped mattering an hour ago.

Below the flats, the called had gathered. Thousands of them — farmers with dirt still under their nails, weavers, dockhands, a whole village of fisherfolk who had walked out of Grayharbor barefoot across the ice-crusted shallows. They stood in the falling light with their faces upturned, gentle and empty-eyed, waiting for Edmund to tell them what they were for. Seraphine's defected hunters formed a thin line between the crowd and Vess's remaining inquisitors, iron drawn but wavering. And past them all, at the edge of the flats where the artifact's light met the ordinary dark, Chancellor Vess himself stood inside a cage of bound glass and old war-iron, his hands raised, his voice carrying that terrible reasonable calm.

"You see what they are," he was saying. "A mob. A flood. Magic unregulated is a plague, Edmund Villalobos. I am offering you order. Take the Salt, hold it, and no one ever has to be afraid again."

"That's what the last one said," Edmund murmured. The mirror-tyrant's face floated behind their eyes, patient as frost. *He also believed he was the cure.*

"Edmund." Odile's hand found theirs. Her palm was cracked from the chains, her knuckles split, and she held on anyway. "Whatever you're hearing — don't answer it alone."

"I'm not alone," Edmund said. "That's rather the point."

Wren leaned against Odile's shoulder, gray-faced, her wound bound in strips of Wren's own shirt, still standing because Wren Ashgrave did not know how to do anything else. "For the record," she rasped, "if you become a god, I want a temple. Small one. Good acoustics."

Edmund laughed — actually laughed, a broken sound that startled the Salt into silence for one full second. In that second they saw clearly: the whisper was not evil. It was only enormous. It was a hunger the size of a sea being offered a single mouth, and any mouth would fail, and the failing would look exactly like Vess's order or the tyrant's glory or even Odile's orchards multiplied over all the world until nothing wild remained anywhere.

The Salt wanted to be held. That was its nature. It had waited under the flats since the Sundering War for hands strong enough to close around it, and every generation it grew more persuasive, and every generation the world answered by outlawing wonder, which was just another way of holding on — gripping so tight nothing could move.

*Then don't grip,* Edmund thought. *Open.*

"Seraphine," they said.

She came up the flat at a run, her unbound gift crackling off her like heat lightning, her eyes wide and furious and terrified in the way of someone who has just burned her whole life down and is deciding whether to regret it. "If you're about to do something stupid—"

"Probably. Catch." 

And Edmund reached into their own chest — there was no other way to describe it; the Salt lived inside them now, waking with each turn of the moon, and reaching for it felt like reaching between one's own ribs — and broke off a piece.

It came away like warm glass, humming, the size of a plum. The flats shuddered. Every face below turned upward. Vess stopped speaking mid-sentence.

"What did you—" Seraphine started.

Edmund pressed it into her hands. "Yours. Not the crown's. Yours. It'll quiet your gift to a voice instead of a scream — you'll finally sleep, if you want. Or it'll wake it gently, if you'd rather. Your choice. That's the whole design. Choice."

Seraphine stared down at the charm as though it were a live coal and a drowning rope at once. Her jaw worked. "This is madness," she whispered. "You're handing out the Sundering War in pieces."

"I'm handing out the end of it," Edmund said. "There's a difference. It took me thirty-nine chapters to learn it, but there is."

They turned to the crowd then, and raised both arms, and began to break.

It did not hurt, exactly. It hurt the way birth hurts — it hurt the way anything does when something larger than you insists on coming through. Edmund reached inward again and again, cracking the Salt loose in pieces small enough to hold, small enough to hide in a pocket, small enough to be lost down a well and forgotten for a hundred years. A light that never gutters. Bread that doesn't stale. Locks that rust open for the grieving and stay shut for the cruel. Each piece went out of their hands and into the crowd, and Edmund felt each one go, felt their own edges going with it, the map-gift dimming like a lamp running low on oil.

The called woke as the pieces touched them. The emptiness left their faces first, then the devotion, and what replaced it was better than either: ordinary bewilderment, ordinary wonder, people looking down at their own palms and asking each other, *is this yours? No — whose is this? Mine? Really?*

Vess understood before anyone else did. Edmund heard him screaming across the flats — not the reasonable calm anymore, but the raw thing underneath, the hoarder's panic. "Stop them! Stop them, you fools, it's slipping away — it was supposed to be *ours*—"

His artifact flared. The sky's wound widened. But there was nothing left up there to seize, no single prize, no throne-shaped hole in the world for his ambition to fill. The Salt was already ten thousand small loyalties scattered among ten thousand strangers, and no army could confiscate a thing everyone owned and no one controlled. His coalition, seeing the prize dissolve, began — Edmund noted with distant satisfaction — to quietly reorganize itself around the question of who had betrayed whom.

Odile caught the last large piece. Edmund saved it for her — a seed-sized shard, dense and green-hearted. She held it a long moment, tears cutting tracks through the salt dust on her face.

"The orchard," Edmund said. "Plant it. Anywhere. Everywhere."

"And you?" she demanded. "What's left of you?"

Edmund considered. Inside, where the Salt had been, there was a quiet like a room after furniture has been carried out. Their gift was nearly gone — they could feel the Veilroads fading even now, the paths between realms closing like eyes going to sleep. What remained was a body, a sister, a friend bleeding cheerfully on the salt, a hunter holding a plum of warm glass like it might hatch, and a lifetime of maps yet to draw the slow way.

"Enough," Edmund said. "More than my fair share."

The last of the Salt they did not give to any person. Edmund crumbled it fine as flour and threw it to the wind, and the wind took it east and west and north and south, sowing the flats, the cities, the drowned abbey, the dead orchard country — magic returning to the world not as inheritance or weapon but as weather, as luck, as small grace falling everywhere and belonging to no one.

Vess's artifact guttered, its purpose dissolved, and the tear in the sky closed with a sound like a book shutting.

In the sudden ordinary dark, Seraphine Dax looked at the charm in her hands, then at Edmund, and said the first honest thing of her life:

"I'm going to have to register myself somewhere, aren't I."

Edmund grinned, swaying, caught by Odile's arm. "I know just the person to found the office."

Far off across the flats, the first of the called — a fisherwoman from Grayharbor, holding her little shard of morning — began to laugh.

* * *

But the wind carries both ways.

Later — much later, when the crowds had gone home and the medics had taken Wren and the stars had come back out over a scarred and quiet plain — Edmund stood alone at the center one last time and felt the absence where the Salt had been. It was a good absence. A chosen one. They were already composing the story they would tell, the way cartographers compose coastlines: simplified, survivable, mostly true.

Which is why it took them a moment to notice the hum.

Faint. Familiar. Coming not from within but from the ground beneath their boots — from deep under the flats, where the vault-mirror had shown them armies made of salt and light. One thread of the Salt had not scattered. One piece had sunk too deep, too fast, straight down through the white earth toward whatever old dark the Sundering War had left unfinished.

And it was humming Edmund's name.

"Odile," Edmund called, not taking their eyes off the ground. "How do you feel about one more map?"

Behind them, soft as snowfall, the salt began to shift.
