## 37. Aftermath of the Scattering

The morning after the end of the world, Odile Villalobos woke to birdsong.

Not the thin, wary birdsong of the salt flats — the scrub-wrens and ash-larks that sang with one eye open, ready to go silent at a footstep — but a full-throated, shameless chorus, the kind she had only ever heard in her grandmother's letters, described as something from before the Sundering War. She lay on her bedroll in the ruined observatory and listened to it the way you listen to a language you almost remember.

Below her, in the camp that had grown up around Edmund overnight — because that was what it was now, a camp, hundreds of people who had walked out of their villages and garrisons and prison wagons following a call only they could hear — someone was laughing. Really laughing, the unguarded kind. And when Odile sat up and looked down the hillside, she saw why.

The grass was blooming.

Little white flowers, pushing up through ground that had been scalded white by the flats' runoff for as long as anyone could remember, opening in the dawn like small hands unclenching. And where a girl of maybe ten crouched with her palm flat against the earth, the flowers came faster, crowding toward her fingers, turning to follow her hand when she moved it.

The child looked up at Odile with an expression Odile knew from the inside. The look of someone who has spent a lifetime hiding a thing and has just been told, by the whole world at once, that they can put it down.

Odile went down the hill and knelt beside her and said, "Careful. It's addictive."

"I know," the girl said, grinning. "I don't care."

* * *

They called it the Scattering, though Edmund hated the word, because it sounded like something spilled rather than given. Ten thousand charms, more or less — no one had counted, because in the last hour there had been no time for counting — flung out over the salt flats on solstice night while the sky tore above them and Vess's bound artifact screamed itself apart against a power it had been built to swallow whole.

Odile remembered it in fragments, the way you remember any loud ending. Seraphine Dax standing in front of the Inquisition's own ranks with her gift blazing off her like heat-shimmer, saying words Odile couldn't hear over the wind but understood anyway: *No.* Wren Ashgrave, gray-faced with blood loss, propped against a fallen pillar, directing the defected hunters' retreat with a courier's crisp hand gestures even while half-conscious. Vess himself, at the center of it all, reaching into his artifact's opened heart for the Salt he believed would be waiting there —

— and finding Edmund instead, holding it, and letting it go.

That was the part Odile returned to most often in the days after. Not the light, though the light had been terrible and beautiful, a slow-motion shattering that turned the whole horizon into falling glass. Not the sound, which was less a boom than a chord, held and then released. What she returned to was her brother's face in the last moment before the Salt left them: not triumphant, not afraid. *Relieved.* Like a person setting down a weight so heavy they'd forgotten it was a weight, and discovering their body still worked without it.

And then the light went through everyone, and everything changed, and nothing changed, which was the miracle of it.

Because the Salt did not die. That had been the other road, the one Edmund refused with the same stubbornness they refused the crown. It *scattered* — broke itself, or let itself be broken, into pieces small enough to live inside ordinary people without owning them. A charm's worth each. A candle-flame's worth. Enough to make bread keep, to make locks rust kindly, to make an orchard bloom where you walked. Not enough to raise armies of salt and light. Not enough to build a tyrant. Not enough, Odile thought, watching the little girl coax flowers out of dead ground, to be worth hunting anyone for.

She tested that thought against the old fear, the one calcified into her bones across twelve years of making her gift look like luck, and found — carefully, suspiciously, the way you test ice in spring — that it held.

No one was coming for the girl on the hillside.

No one was coming for any of them.

* * *

Vess fell the way rotten trees fall: slowly, creakingly, and then all at once, and mostly onto himself.

Word reached the camps within a week, carried by couriers who no longer had to pretend to be anything but couriers. The Chancellor of the Iron Court had stood before the assembled coalition that had followed him to the flats — inquisitors, guildmasters, border lords, the whole uneasy machine he'd spent a decade building — and announced that the Salt had been destroyed by traitors, and that the Hunt would continue, and that greater measures would be necessary.

And the coalition had looked at him, every gifted and ungifted soul among them, and felt the same thing at the same moment: that the thing he'd promised them was gone, and had perhaps never existed, and that they had burned a lot of very good people for a very bad idea.

Seraphine told Odile about it later, sitting on the observatory steps with the iron filings finally washed out of her hair, looking younger and older at once. "He didn't understand," she said. "That was the strangest part. He stood there giving the speech, and I could see him expecting them to rally. He'd built his whole life on the assumption that fear compounds. That if you scare people enough, they'll follow you anywhere." She turned a plain tin cup in her hands. "But fear doesn't compound. It *evaporates*. All it takes is one morning where the sun comes up and nobody's dragged anybody away, and people start asking what exactly they were afraid of."

"What happened?"

"His own guards arrested him. Quietly. No drama — he'd have hated that most of all. There'll be a trial." Her mouth twisted. "A real one, I think. Though half the judges will be people who signed his proclamations, so we'll see."

"And the Hunt?"

"Dissolved. Officially. Unofficially—" she shrugged, "—you can't un-teach twelve years of fear in a week. Some hunters will keep hunting until someone makes them stop. But the register—" She stopped, and Odile watched her decide whether to say the next part, and say it anyway. "I'm building a new one. Voluntary. Not a list of the gifted for the crown to harvest. A list of teachers. People willing to show children what their gifts are before the gifts grow wild and frightening." She glanced sideways. "Your grandmother would have understood the difference."

"She'd have made you write it out three times before she trusted it," Odile said, and Seraphine laughed — actually laughed — and Odile understood that this too was part of the aftermath: enemies becoming colleagues, terror becoming paperwork, the loud ending settling into the long quiet work of building a world where wonder didn't have to be outlawed or hoarded because it belonged to everybody.

It would fail sometimes. She wasn't naive. Somewhere, right now, some petty lord was probably working out how to corner the market on charms, some frightened town would burn a healer before the news reached them. Monopolies on power didn't die in one night; they died slowly, of irrelevance, starved by ten thousand small lights they couldn't tax or conscript.

But that was the point. That had always been the point. You couldn't hunt what everyone had. You couldn't tyrannize a kingdom of gardeners.

* * *

Odile went home in the second week.

Home was a word she used loosely. The orchard outside Saltmere had been dying for years — frost-bitten, salt-scoured, half its trees standing like skeletons that hadn't gotten the message. She'd tended it out of stubbornness and grief, walking its rows alone, letting the green rise behind her heels and fade ahead of her face, hiding the miracle inside the ruin so thoroughly that even she sometimes forgot it was there.

Now she walked the rows openly, in daylight, with her hands bare, and the orchard came back like a held breath released.

Blossoms first — absurd, out-of-season, blinding white and blush-pink, breaking from black branches in drifts that caught the wind and moved across the rows like weather. Then leaves, unfurling with an audible creak and sigh. By the third day, green fruit swelled on branches that should have been months from bearing, and the bees — where had the bees even come from? — arrived in golden clouds and got to work with the industry of creatures who had been waiting a long time for the invitation.

Neighbors came to watch. That was the part that undid her. For twelve years, if anyone had seen the grass green where she walked, she'd have been in a wagon headed for the Iron Court, and the neighbors would have been the ones pointing the way. Now they came with baskets and questions and their children on their shoulders, and an old woman named Marta who had sold Odile flour for a decade and never once met her eyes said, "My hands. Look." Her palms were cupped around a small warmth, a glow like a coal wrapped in wool. "Since solstice night. I thought I was dying. I thought it was a fever."

"It's not a fever," Odile said.

"What do I do with it?"

"Whatever you want," Odile said. "That's the whole point. Whatever you want."

Marta looked at her glowing hands for a long moment, and then she pressed them to the trunk of the nearest dead tree, and the bark softened, and somewhere deep in the wood a new bud decided, against all reason, to exist. Marta cried a little. Odile pretended not to notice, because that was the neighborly thing to do.

By week's end there were seven households keeping small charms alive in the village — Marta's warmth, a boy whose whistling mended fences, a fisherman whose nets never tore, twins who could find anything that was lost, including, embarrassingly, Odile's reading glasses. Nobody registered anywhere. Nobody asked permission. They just... had things, the way people had good singing voices or steady hands, and life arranged itself around the having.

Odile planted new trees at the orchard's edge. Proper ones, saplings from Grayharbor stock, because the magic could do a great deal but it couldn't do the one thing that mattered, which was the slow ordinary work of putting a root in the ground and letting it choose to stay. She planted forty of them, one for each year of her grandmother's life, and she talked to them as she worked, because her grandmother had talked to her trees and Odile had never seen the sense in breaking a family tradition just because the family had been hunted for it.

"You're late," she told them. "Everything's late. But you're here."

* * *

The grief came at odd hours.

That was the thing no one warned you about, after a loud ending. Everyone expected the relief — and the relief was real, vast, a sky-sized thing she breathed like air. But underneath it ran a quieter current, and it caught her at strange moments: cracking eggs, folding laundry, walking the rows at dusk.

Edmund had given away everything.

Not just the Salt — that had been the choice, the famous choice, the one the balladeers were already mangling in taverns from Saltmere to Grayharbor. But the gift had gone into the scattering too. The map-gift, the Veilroad-sight, the thing that had made her brother a cartographer of realms that existed only in rumor. Twelve years of hiding it, and then one night of wielding it at a scale no warden had ever attempted, and then — gone. Spent. Poured out with everything else into ten thousand strangers' hands.

Edmund said they were fine. Edmund said it lightly, with the particular lightness Odile had learned to read across a childhood shared and a adolescence divided and twelve years of silence between. They drew maps now the ordinary way — walking, asking, listening, squinting at horizons and arguing with innkeepers about distances — and they claimed to love it, and maybe they did, and maybe some days they did.

But Odile had seen them stop, once, at the edge of the salt flats, staring at empty air with an expression she recognized from mirrors. The expression of someone listening for a voice that isn't there anymore. Of someone reaching, out of pure habit, for a door that has been bricked over.

You couldn't mourn out loud for a gift your brother had chosen to give away. That was the cruelty of it. Grief wanted a villain, and there wasn't one. There was only a choice, and the choice had been right — she believed that down to her bones, she'd have fought anyone who said otherwise — and it had cost exactly what it cost, and the cost was her brother's whole self, the self they'd spent twelve years protecting and one night spending.

So she mourned in the odd hours, quietly, while kneading bread or pruning deadwood, and she kept an eye out — the way you keep an eye on a wound that claims it doesn't hurt.

Some days the loss was a wound. She could see it. And she had learned, from a lifetime of tending dying things, that wounds like that didn't heal by being ignored.

They healed by being tended.

* * *

On the last evening of the month, she found Edmund at the stall.

Their old stall, in the Saltmere market — reclaimed, dusted out, the awning patched with a scrap of Wren's Consortium-blue canvas because Wren had insisted on contributing something and this was what she had. The sign above it still read VILLALOBOS FINE CHARMS, HARMLESS, GUARANTEED, in Edmund's careful cartographer's lettering, and beneath it someone — Odile suspected the flower-girl from the hillside, who had turned up in Saltmere with her family like half the scattered world had — had chalked a small addition: *and mostly true.*

Edmund was packing up for the day, maps rolled and twine-tied, and doing it slowly, the way people do things when they're thinking about something else entirely.

Odile leaned against the stall post and waited, because she'd gotten reacquainted with her brother's silences these past weeks and had learned they opened faster if you didn't knock.

"They're saying the moon's starting its first turn again," Edmund said at last, not looking up. "New cycle. The astronomers at the observatory are very excited. Thirteen turns to the next solstice." A pause. "The Salt's quiet now. Scattered. But it's still... in things. In people. Growing, maybe. Slowly." They set down the twine. "Someone asked me today what happens in thirteen years. Whether it all comes back together. Whether someone's born who can hold it again."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I'm a cartographer, not a prophet." Edmund smiled, but it was the light smile, the one with the listening-behind-it. "Odile. Do you ever miss it? Your gift — before, when it was just yours? When it was small enough to hide?"

Odile considered the question honestly, because her brother deserved honest answers even when they cost something. "I miss being afraid of losing it," she said. "Isn't that strange? Twelve years clutching it like a coal in my fist, and now that I can open my hand, I keep flexing the fingers." She looked out at the market square, where a lamplighter was touching flame to wicks with a small unnecessary flourish that made each lamp bloom a half-second early, showing off for no one, for everyone. "But no. I don't miss the hiding. I spent twelve years being a rumor of myself. I'd rather be a person."

Edmund nodded slowly. Then, in a different voice — lighter, too light, the voice Odile had already learned meant *change the subject before I have to answer anything true*:

"Anyway. I've been asked to map the eastern Veilroads. The ones that are left. On foot." A crooked grin. "Wren says she knows a courier who owes her a favor and a guide who lies about distances. Apparently it's a long journey."

"How long?"

"A year, maybe more. Maybe—" Edmund stopped, and Odile watched her brother stand in the doorway of the next chapter of their life, one hand on the frame, not yet stepping through. "Maybe longer than that. Maybe I don't come back the same person who leaves. Walking roads instead of seeing them. Learning the world the slow way, the way everyone else does." They looked at her. "Come with me?"

And Odile, who had spent twelve years rooted in one dying orchard like a tree herself, who had just spent a month learning to bloom in the open, felt the question land in her chest like a seed in spring soil — terrifying, inevitable, alive.

She looked at the stall. At the chalked sign. At the lamplight coming on across the square, one small flame at a time, in a city learning wonder again.

"When do we leave?" she said.
