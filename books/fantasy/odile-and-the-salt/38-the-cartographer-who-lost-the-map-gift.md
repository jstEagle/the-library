## 38. The Cartographer Who Lost the Map-Gift

The first morning after the scattering, Edmund woke and reached for the Veilroads the way a man reaches for a wall in the dark.

They had done it every day for thirty years — since before the Purge, before Saltmere, before the stall with its harmless-looking charms. It was not a gesture of the hands but of the mind: a small turning inward, a listening, and there the roads would be, silver threads stitched through the fabric of everything, waiting to be walked. Edmund had opened them in the dark beneath the drowned abbey. Had run them ahead of Seraphine's hounds. Had stepped through one on the solstice night with the sky tearing open above the salt flats, carrying ten thousand small charms in a satchel against their chest like a second heart.

Now Edmund turned inward and found only Edmund.

It was not silence, exactly. Silence would have been kinder, or crueler — Edmund couldn't decide which, that first morning, lying in Odile's spare room above the rebuilt orchard house while frost feathered the window. It was more like the absence of a sense. The way a person born blind does not miss color because they have never been given it to lose. Edmund had been given it, and lost it, and so the world now had a hole in it shaped exactly like everywhere else.

They lay there for a long time, testing the emptiness the way you test a tooth that's gone, pressing your tongue into the gap.

Then they got up, and made tea, and began to draw a map by hand.

* * *

The map was of the valley below the orchard, three miles of river and hedgerow and the ruins of a mill nobody had claimed since the war. Edmund drew it from memory first, then went out and corrected it against the world, which was a thing they had never once needed to do.

That was the joke of it, if you wanted to laugh. Edmund Villalobos, who had mapped realms that existed only in rumor, who had charted the Veilroads the way other men charted coastlines, who could have drawn you the shape of a country they'd never set foot in just by standing still and listening — Edmund Villalobos now had to *walk*.

They walked the riverbank and found their memory had put the ford forty yards too far east. They walked the hedgerow and discovered a gap where a gate had rotted away, a gap no gift would ever have shown them because no gift needed to. They talked to the miller's widow, whose name was Anselma and who had lived in the valley her whole life, and she told them about the flood year, and the year the eels came, and the path her husband used to cut across the high meadow before his knees went. Edmund wrote all of it down. The ink smudged in the cold. Their fingers ached from holding the pen instead of the charm-knife.

Some days the loss was a wound. This is the honest accounting, and Edmund had promised themselves an honest accounting, because they had watched what happened to people who lied about power — Vess, hoarding; the last warden in the mirror, hoarding; even the Salt itself, patient as a debt, waiting thirteen turns to collect.

Some days the loss was a wound, and Edmund bled it privately, in the mornings, in the gap between sleeping and tea.

But some days — and these grew, slowly, the way Odile's orchards grew, which was to say stubbornly and against all advice — some days it was something else.

* * *

In February a woman came up from Grayharbor looking for the cartographer who'd broken the moon.

Edmund was at the market stall — the old stall, reassembled, though it stood now in the orchard village rather than Saltmere, because neither sibling had any appetite for Saltmere's cobbles yet. The stall sold maps. Real ones, surveyed on foot, with the surveyor's errors honestly left visible in the margins, because Odile said the errors were the best part, they proved a human being had been there. It also sold charms. Small ones. A light that never guttered. Bread that didn't stale. Nothing that hummed anymore, nothing that whispered, nothing that wanted anything. The Salt, scattered, had settled into ordinary objects and ordinary people like sediment into still water, and what rose from it was modest and strange and mostly kind.

The woman from Grayharbor stood at the stall turning a folded map over in her hands without opening it.

"You're them," she said. "The one from the stories."

"I'm the one who sells maps," Edmund said. "The stories do most of the rest themselves. They always have."

"My daughter," the woman said, and stopped, and started again. "My daughter has something. Since the solstice. She can — when she sleeps, she knows things. Small things. Where I've put my keys. Whether the letter coming is good news or bad." Her jaw tightened. "Under Vess, I'd have burned the house down before I let anyone write her name in a register."

"Vess is gone."

"Vess is gone." She said it like a woman testing ice. "But habits aren't. So I'm asking you, cartographer, the one who gave it all away — was it worth it? Would you take it back? If you could."

Edmund thought about the question honestly, because the woman deserved honesty and because Edmund had spent a year practicing it.

"No," they said. "I don't think I would. But I want you to understand that's not the same as saying it doesn't hurt. It hurts most mornings. Some evenings too. What I gave up was the largest part of myself, and I did it on purpose, and I'd do it again, and all four of those sentences are true at once. That's what a choice is. If it cost nothing, it wouldn't be one."

The woman looked at them for a long moment. Then she bought two maps — one of the valley, one of the road south to Grayharbor — and asked, quietly, whether the register Seraphine Dax was building was really voluntary.

"Ask her yourself," Edmund said. "She's coming through next week. She'll stand right here and let you look at her, and you can decide what you see."

* * *

Seraphine came through nine days later, traveling without escort, wearing plain wool instead of Inquisitor gray. She looked thinner than she had in the prison wagon, and easier, the way a bow looks easier unstrung. She brought a crate of paper and ink for the school Odile was starting, and she stayed three nights, and on the second night she and Edmund sat up late by the fire doing the thing they did whenever they met now, which was talk about the calling.

"It's quieter," she said. "Since the scattering. Mine, I mean. Before, it was a shout. Now it's — someone humming in another room. I can almost ignore it."

"Almost."

"Almost." She turned her cup. "Yours is truly gone? You don't hear anything?"

"I hear the fire settling. I hear Odile moving around upstairs. I hear my own knees when I stand up." Edmund smiled. "It's the quietest I've been since I was six years old. Do you know what I've learned this year? Most people live their whole lives in this much quiet. And they build things anyway. Roads. Maps. Laws. Without hearing a single silver thread. I find that — I don't know the word. Humbling isn't right. It's more like discovering the floor was holding you up the whole time and you never once thanked it."

Seraphine studied them across the fire. "You know what I keep thinking about? The alley in Grayharbor. When I had you cornered and I let you go."

"The first hesitation."

"My whole life before that, control was redemption. Iron filings and discipline. Feel nothing, want nothing, and maybe the crown will let you keep living." Her mouth twisted. "And then I stood in front of you and your gift called to mine like — like a tuning fork — and I understood that everything I'd called control was just fear wearing armor. Letting you go was the first thing I ever did that wasn't fear. It cost me everything and it was the cheapest thing I ever bought."

Outside, an owl crossed the yard. Edmund heard it — plainly, ordinarily, with ears alone — and found, to their surprise, that this was enough.

* * *

Spring came early to the valley, or perhaps Odile simply refused to acknowledge that it hadn't.

She was everywhere in the orchard those weeks, walking the rows, and where she walked the blossom broke open days ahead of schedule, so that by mid-March the whole hillside stood white and loud with bees and the air smelled sweet enough to make grown travelers stop their carts and stare. She did it openly now. That was the part Edmund still couldn't watch without something catching in their throat — not the magic, but the openness. Twelve years of luck-that-wasn't-luck, of hiding the gift inside coincidence, and now their sister walked her own land trailing bloom like a bride trails ribbon, and dared anyone to say a word.

"You're staring," Odile said, not turning around.

"I'm allowed. It's a free country now. Allegedly."

"You're staring because you're doing the thing again. The counting-up-the-cost thing." She snapped a dead branch off a young tree and tossed it aside. "Say it out loud or it'll go septic."

Edmund leaned on the fence. Below them the river ran bright with snowmelt, and beyond it the new road — the one Edmund had surveyed on foot, in the rain, arguing with Anselma about the ford — curved toward the market village, already rutted with cart wheels.

"All right," they said. "Here's the ledger. On the left side: I can't see the Veilroads. I can't step between realms. I can't feel the shape of a country I haven't visited. Half of what I was for forty years is simply gone, and some mornings I wake up reaching for it like a phantom limb, and the grief is real, and I'm done pretending otherwise. On the right side:" they counted on their fingers, "I chose it. Nobody took it. Vess didn't win, the mirror-tyrant didn't win, the Salt didn't get a warden to hollow out and wear. Ten thousand strangers are carrying little pieces of wonder in their pockets, and not one of them can raise an army with it. And I am — " here they paused, because the sentence surprised even them — "I am a better cartographer than I was. Slower. Wrong more often. But I ask questions now. I listen to millers' widows. My old maps were true and bloodless. These ones have mud on them."

Odile came and stood beside them at the fence, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd stood at the warden-gate with split lips and shared blood.

"And the bad days?" she said.

"The bad days are bad. I won't dress them up. Last month I dreamed I opened a Veilroad and stepped through and woke up crying." Edmund shrugged. "But grief for a thing you gave away on purpose is a different animal than grief for a thing stolen. One eats you. The other one teaches you. I intend to be taught."

Their sister was quiet a moment. Then: "Grandmother would have said something insufferable about this."

"She would have said the inheritance was never the gift. The giving was."

"There it is." Odile smiled despite herself. "Insufferable."

* * *

In April, a letter arrived that Edmund read four times before showing anyone.

It was from a student of Seraphine's register — a girl of fifteen in the hill country north of Grayharbor, gifted since the solstice with a small, specific talent: she could see old roads. Not the Veilroads. Ordinary roads, dead roads, roads abandoned two hundred years ago — they showed to her faintly, like scars under skin. She had written to the register, and the register had written to Edmund, because the girl had a question no one else could answer.

*There is a road near our village,* the letter said, *that shows brighter than the others, and it doesn't go anywhere. It starts at a standing stone and walks straight into the hillside and stops. Everyone says it's nothing. But it shines like it's waiting. Did roads used to wait?*

Edmund sat with the letter in the afternoon sun and felt something turn over in their chest that was not the Salt, and not grief either. It was older and simpler than both. It was the feeling of a question worth walking toward.

Because here was the truth they had come to, slowly, the way water comes to a low place: the gift had never actually been the mapping. The gift had been a shortcut. It had shown Edmund the shape of the world without requiring Edmund to love it — and a map made without love is only information. The walking, the asking, the listening, the ford forty yards wrong and the miller's widow's flood-year stories and the mud drying on the margins — that was the mapping. That had always been the mapping. Everything else had been a faster way to arrive somewhere without being changed by the journey.

Edmund wrote back to the girl that evening, by firelight:

*Roads do wait. All the old ones do. Come spring I'll walk out to your village, and we'll go and stand at that stone together, and I'll teach you what I know, which is less than it used to be and more useful than it sounds: how to draw what you see, how to admit what you don't, and how to knock on doors. Because a road that waits is a door, and doors are answered by people, not gifts.*

*P.S. Bring boots. There will be mud. The mud is the best part.*

They sealed it, and set it on the table for the courier, and stood a moment looking at the map pinned to the wall behind — the valley, hand-drawn, error-honest, alive with marginal notes in three different hands because Odile kept adding things and refused to apologize.

Somewhere out past the hills, a road was shining in a girl's eyes, waiting to be walked.

Edmund reached for their boots.
