## 39. Seraphine's Register

The register began, as most honest things do, as a lie.

Seraphine Dax stood in the shell of the Inquisition's registry hall in Saltmere — the same hall where, for twelve years, names had been written into ledgers and the people behind those names had been written out of their lives. The shelves still smelled of iron filings. She could taste the old suppression in the air, faint and metallic, like a coin held too long on the tongue.

The clerks had burned the ledgers in the first week after Vess fell. She had watched them feed the pages to the fire herself, one name at a time, because it seemed wrong to let someone else do it quickly. Some of those names she had written. Some of them she had hunted. One of them was a woman named Maren Colm, who had been dragged from her stall screaming the word *cartographer* while Seraphine looked through her like weather.

Maren had survived. Most hadn't.

So when the city council asked what should be done with the hall — tear it down, consecrate it, turn it into a granary — Seraphine said: *a school*. And when they asked what kind of school, she heard herself say words she did not know she had been carrying:

"A register," she said. "But not of the gifted."

She set a single ledger on the great scarred table where the old ones had stacked like tombstones. This one was thin. New paper, unruled, waiting.

"Of teachers."

* * *

The idea had come to her in the prison wagon, though she hadn't known it then. Edmund Villalobos, wrists bound, hands folded with terrible calm, had talked to her for three days about how magic worked — not the Salt, not the grand inheritance, but the small stuff, the ordinary stuff. How a gift left alone grows crooked. How a child who can hear doors before they open learns to flinch from every room. How suppression doesn't kill a gift; it just teaches it to bite the hand that holds it down.

"Iron filings and discipline," Edmund had said once, looking at her hands — at the faint tremor that never fully left them after Vess's catalyst burned her suppression away. "You didn't destroy your gift. You trained it to hate you."

And Seraphine, who had built an entire life on the architecture of control, who had believed control was redemption, had found no argument. Because it was true. Her gift — the deep listening, the sense of other gifts like heat on skin — had spent twelve years pressed under iron, and when it finally broke free it came up screaming, and she had aimed all that scream at Vess's ranks and called it justice. It had been justice. It had also been twelve years of pain given permission, all at once, in one direction.

That wasn't mastery. That wasn't even health. That was a dam failing gloriously.

A child's gift shouldn't have to fail to be free.

That was the whole philosophy of the register, and she wrote it on the first page in her blunt, clerk-trained hand: *No more hunts. No more hiding. If your child shows a gift, you may write here — not to be recorded, but to be taught. No name is required. Only a willingness to learn.*

Below it, the rules, which she kept deliberately short:

1. Registration is voluntary. Always.
2. Teachers are volunteers, paid by the city, answerable to the families.
3. Nothing written here may be used by any court, crown, or council.
4. Any teacher who betrays a student is struck from this book forever, and everyone will know why.

Rule four had been her addition. Chancellor Vess had used registers as nets. Seraphine intended hers to be a door that only opened inward.

* * *

The first month, no one came.

She sat in the hall six hours a day with the ledger open, and the city walked past the tall windows and pretended the building was empty, the way cities pretend about everything that frightens them. A few children pressed their faces to the glass until their mothers pulled them away. Once, someone threw a stone. It cracked a pane and she left the crack there, unrepaired, because covering it would have felt like lying.

The second month, an old woman came in — Halda Brune, who had once been a hedge-healer in the marsh villages before the Purge, seventy years old, hands like tree roots. She didn't sign. She stood over the ledger a long time and then said, "I'll teach. But I'm not writing my name in anything again. Not ever."

"You don't have to," Seraphine said. "I can put a mark. An X. The families will know it's you."

Halda studied her with wet, furious eyes. "You were Dax. The hunter."

"Yes."

"My brother was taken in the third year of the Hunt."

"I know. I read the file." She had read all the files. It was part of the penance she hadn't yet figured out how to perform — reading every case, learning every face, so that the dead would at least have one witness who remembered them individually rather than as a number.

Halda's jaw worked. Then she said, "An X, then. And if you ever betray anyone in this book, Dax, I will come back, and I am old enough not to care what happens after."

"Fair terms," Seraphine said, and drew the X.

By winter, the ledger had forty marks. By the thaw, a hundred and twenty. Carpenters who could find the true line in warped wood without measuring. Girls who made bread rise with a touch and needed to learn why, so it wouldn't frighten them. A boy who glowed faintly when he slept and whose parents had spent years nailing blankets over the windows of his crib.

None of them were registered by name unless they chose to be. Most chose the mark. Seraphine understood. A name had been a death sentence for so long that freedom would need time to make names safe again.

Some evenings she stayed late, alone in the hall, and listened — really listened, with the gift she no longer suppressed — to the hundred small warm signals drifting through Saltmere like embers on wind. Before the Scattering, she'd have counted maybe a dozen such lights in the whole city, all hidden, all frightened. Now there were hundreds. Ten thousand small charms, scattered among ordinary people by a cartographer who had given away godhood like bread. The world hummed now, everywhere, gently. It sounded nothing like power. It sounded like a kitchen at dawn.

It sounded, she thought, the way it probably sounded before anyone thought to hoard it.

* * *

On a grey morning in early spring, she closed the ledger, told the day's volunteers she had an errand, and walked across Saltmere to the market district.

The market had rebuilt itself the way markets do — messily, stubbornly, with new stalls crowding the scorched footprints of old ones. Maren Colm had gotten her stall back and sold candles now, honest tallow ones, and always gave Seraphine one for free no matter how many times Seraphine tried to pay. Today Maren pointed, wordlessly, toward the far row, where a familiar awning had gone up between two fruit sellers: faded canvas, hand-painted letters, a display of maps pinned flat under strips of glass.

Edmund Villalobos stood at the stall, arguing cheerfully with a farmer about whether a certain river actually bent or merely appeared to.

They had aged. Or perhaps they had simply stopped hiding. The hunch was gone from their shoulders; the constant flicking glance toward the street corners — the surveillance habit every gifted person developed during the Hunt — had faded to almost nothing. Their hands moved over the maps with the same precision she remembered from the interrogation wagon, but loosely now. Relaxed. Hands that weren't waiting to grab something.

Seraphine waited at the edge of the stall until the farmer shuffled off with his map, and then she stepped forward, and Edmund looked up, and she watched the recognition move through them like a slow tide — wariness first, then surprise, then something gentler settling over both.

"Inquisitor Dax," Edmund said. "Or is it just Dax now? Teacher Dax?"

"Just Dax works fine." She set a small object on the counter between them. "I've been meaning to return this."

It was a charm. A little carved stone on a leather cord, no bigger than a thumbnail — one of the harmless-looking pieces Edmund had sold by the dozen back when selling it had been a crime. She had bought it in her first week in Saltmere, twelve years ago, working undercover, cataloguing the market's quiet traffics. She'd never turned Edmund in. She'd told herself it was because the charm was legally ambiguous, technically inert, not worth the paperwork. She'd told herself a lot of things in those years.

"This one still hums," she said.

Edmund picked it up and went very still. "That's impossible. These were inert. I made them inert — that was the entire point—"

"It hums," Seraphine repeated. "Faintly. It's been humming since the solstice. Since—" she gestured vaguely at the air, at everything, at the scattering. "I think some of you got into it. When you broke the Salt apart. I think things you touched got... seeded."

Edmund turned the stone over twice, held it near their ear, and laughed — a short, startled, wondering laugh. "Twelve years I sold these telling myself they were nothing. And the whole time they were seeds." They looked up. "Does it bother you? The humming?"

She considered the question honestly, because Edmund deserved honesty and because she was trying, these days, to practice it. "A year ago it would have terrified me. Humming meant magic meant the Hunt meant someone burning." She shrugged. "Now it sounds like the register."

"The register." Edmund's eyebrows rose. "I heard rumors. A voluntary book. Teachers instead of hunters. I assumed it was rumor. Rumors about you tend to run large."

"They run both directions. Half the city thinks I'm a saint for turning on Vess. The other half correctly remembers what I did before that." She glanced at the maps. "How's the mapping? I hear you lost the—"

"The Veilroads, yes. Gone. Went into the scattering with everything else." Edmund tapped their own chest lightly, wry. "I can't see the paths anymore. I map the way ordinary people do now. Walking. Asking. Getting lost." A pause, and then, quieter: "Some days it's a wound. Most days it's just work. Good work. Honest work." They met her eyes. "You'd know about losing a way of seeing and finding another one."

"I would," she said.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Around them the market roared its ordinary roar — haggling, laughter, a cart wheel grinding, someone singing badly two stalls down. A year ago, sound like this had been cover for fear. Now it was just sound.

"I owe you an accounting," Seraphine said. "Not an apology. Apologies are cheap and I've issued too many to believe in them. An accounting. Of what I did. To Maren Colm. To the others whose files I can recite from memory. I've started doing it in person, house by house. It takes a long time. It should."

Edmund studied her for a long moment. "Odile says the same thing about grandmother's letters. That inheritance isn't what you're given. It's what you do with what you're given." They slid the humming charm back across the counter. "Keep it. It's yours — it's been yours longer than it's been mine. And come to dinner. Both of us. Odile makes bread that refuses to go stale, which she claims isn't a gift anymore, just good baking, and she gets furious if you suggest otherwise."

"She sounds formidable."

"She's terrifying. You'll like her."

Seraphine took the charm and, after a moment, put it around her neck where the iron filings used to hang — a different weight in the same place, and lighter. "One more thing," she said. "The register needs teachers. Real ones. People who understand gifts from the inside — what they cost, what they want, how they grow wild when you're afraid of them. There's no one better qualified in this city than the two of you. No obligation. But the ledger's open, and the marks are anonymous, and rule four is absolute."

Edmund looked at the crowd, at the market they had hidden inside for twelve years and nearly died escaping. Then they smiled — the tired, luminous smile of someone who had once held everything and chosen to give it away.

"We'll come," they said. "We'll bring the maps. Children should learn geography anyway. It teaches you that the world is bigger than whoever's ruling it."

"That," said Seraphine Dax, "is exactly the kind of dangerous lesson we're trying to teach."

* * *

She walked home along the harbor road as the afternoon light went gold on the water, and the charm hummed against her collarbone — faint, steady, patient, like something planted long ago finally deciding it was safe to grow.

At the top of the register hall steps, a girl was waiting. Eight, maybe nine, barefoot despite the cold, hair full of salt-wind. Seraphine knew immediately — felt it in the deep listening, a bright unfamiliar note — that the child was gifted, and unregistered, and utterly unafraid.

"You're the teacher-lady," the girl said. Not a question. "My gran says you used to hunt people like me."

"I did," Seraphine said.

The girl considered this with terrible gravity. Then she held up a small, grubby hand, and above her palm, wobbling slightly, a tiny sphere of light bloomed — soft and gold and guttering, like a candle flame learning to fly.

"Gran says somebody has to teach me before I burn the curtains down again," the girl said. "Are you taking students?"

Seraphine looked at the light. At the child. At the tall windows of the hall where names had once been written as sentences, now open wide to the evening.

"Yes," she said, and opened the door.
