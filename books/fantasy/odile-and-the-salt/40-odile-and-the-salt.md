## 40. Odile and the Salt

The stall went up on the first morning of spring, in the same corner of the Saltmere market where it had always stood, between the fishmonger who sang off-key and the woman who sold candles that smelled of nothing at all. Odile hammered the last peg herself, because Edmund's hands were full of maps, because Edmund's hands were always full of maps now, and because someone in this family had to know how to build a thing that would not fall down.

"You're hitting it too hard," Edmund said, without looking up.

"I'm hitting it exactly hard enough."

"That's what you said about the fence at the orchard."

"The fence held."

"The fence leaned."

Odile set down the hammer and looked at her brother — thinner than they had been a year ago, grayer at the temples in a way that no twenty-some-odd years should allow, but standing straighter too, the way a tree stands after the storm has passed and it has decided, against all advice, to keep growing. They smoothed one of the maps across the counter with both palms, careful as a priest laying out relics, and Odile felt the old ache move through her chest, the one that never quite left, the one shaped like everything Edmund had poured into the wind.

"Stop staring," Edmund said.

"I'm inventorying you."

"I'm fine."

"You're always fine. That's how I know when you're not." She took the maps from them and began arranging: coastlines first, then the mountain passes, then the new ones, the ones drawn by hand and foot and asking, the ones that had taken Edmund eleven months to make because there was no gift in them anymore, only work. "We charge double for the Grayharbor road. It floods."

"It doesn't flood."

"It flooded twice in March."

"That was weather, not the road."

"Then we charge double for the weather."

Edmund laughed — really laughed, the sound still surprising them both sometimes, like a bird returning to a burned forest — and turned to hang the sign. Odile had painted it herself over the winter, by lamplight, while Edmund slept: *VILLALOBOS & VILLALOBOS — HONEST MAPS, SMALL CHARMS, NO QUESTIONS ANSWERED BEFORE NOON.* The last part was Edmund's addition. They thought they were funny. Some days they even were.

The market filled around them the way water fills a basin, slowly and then all at once. And this was the thing Odile still could not get used to, a year on, the thing that made her stop mid-task some mornings just to watch: nobody hid anymore. Not here, not quite. A girl at the next stall over coaxed her bread to rise with a touch and a hum, openly, while customers waited and chatted and did not cross themselves. An old man sold bottled light for a copper apiece, guaranteed never to gutter, and when a child asked him how it worked he told her the truth — *I don't rightly know, it just likes me* — and she nodded as if that were the most reasonable answer in the world, which, now, it was.

There had been a hard winter first. Odile did not let herself forget that. There had been towns that burned their own gifted rather than share a street with them, and registers kept by frightened clerks, and Seraphine Dax riding from city to city for half a year, alone, unarmed, arguing with magistrates and mobs and once, famously, talking an entire garrison into standing down by showing them what her gift could do and then setting it down in front of them like a weapon surrendered. The register she built afterward — the voluntary one, the teachers' one — had been laughed at in three capitals before it worked in four.

But Saltmere had come around faster than most. Saltmere, Odile suspected, had always been ashamed of itself, deep down, for outlawing wonder in the first place, and shame makes people quick to forgive when forgiveness is offered cheaply enough.

Their first customer arrived before the sun cleared the rooftops: a farmer wanting a map to the lowland markets that avoided the toll bridges, and paid in eggs. Their second wanted a charm against nightmares, and Edmund made one while the man watched — a twist of salt-thread and riverstone, nothing grand, nothing that would call to anyone or pull at anyone or wake anything sleeping in anyone's blood — and when the man asked what it cost, Edmund said, "Whatever you think it's worth, and if that's nothing, tell your neighbors I'm bad at my trade."

"He'll tell them you're good at it," Odile said, after he'd gone. "That's worse."

"How is that worse?"

"Because then everyone comes, and you'll stay up all night making charms for strangers, and we'll be right back where we started, with you glowing like a beacon in a dark field."

Edmund was quiet a moment. "I can say no now," they said finally. "That's the difference. Before, I couldn't help it. Now I choose it. It turns out those look the same from the outside and feel completely different from the inside."

Odile looked at them for a long moment, and then reached over and straightened their collar, which was crooked, which was always crooked, which was the kind of small domestic grievance she had missed so fiercely during the year of running that she had once caught herself, in a prison cell, worrying about it.

"Your collar," she said.

"I know."

* * *

Midmorning brought the crowds, and with them the questions.

This was the other thing Odile had not anticipated: people wanted stories. Not charms, not maps — stories. They came to the stall and bought a map they didn't need so they could stand there a few minutes longer and ask, in the casual tones of people fishing, whether it was true about the salt flats, about the sky tearing open, about the ten thousand lights that had gone out over the heads of the gathered armies and settled instead into the pockets and hands of ordinary folk. Whether it was true about the Chancellor. Whether it was true about the Inquisitor who had turned her gift against her own ranks in front of a thousand witnesses and then walked away from the ashes without looking back.

Edmund deflected most of these. Edmund had gotten very good, in the past year, at saying *that was a strange season, and I mostly remember being cold.* But Odile found she didn't mind the questions. She answered them plainly, or as plainly as the truth allowed, and the truth was strange enough that plainness served it well.

A woman asked if the Salt had really been alive. "It was more alive than anything I've ever touched," Odile said. "And less alive than my grandmother. Both of those surprised me."

A boy asked if Edmund Villalobos was really the one who broke it. Odile looked at her brother, who was very intently reorganizing maps that did not need reorganizing. "They're the one who gave it away," she said. "Breaking is easy. Anyone can break a thing. Giving it away — that took doing."

And then, near noon, when the crowd had thinned and the fishmonger had moved on to his third song of the day, a child came to the stall.

She was small, maybe seven, with mud on her boots and her hair braided badly, the way hair gets braided when a child does it herself out of defiance rather than necessity. She stood at the edge of the stall for a long time, working up her courage, and Odile let her take the time, because children deserve the same patience as seedlings.

Finally the girl said, "My gran says the stories about the thirteenth moon are true. That the moon finished turning and the Salt woke up and a cartographer split it into ten thousand pieces and threw it to the wind. She says she was there. She says she caught one."

"Did she?" Odile said.

"She keeps it in a tin with her sewing things. It doesn't do anything. She says that's the point." The girl frowned, gathering her real question the way you gather stones to throw. "Is it true? All of it?"

Odile crouched down behind the counter until she was at the girl's eye level, elbows on the wood, chin on her hands.

"Only the good parts," she said.

The girl considered this with the total seriousness of the young. "Which parts are the good parts?"

"Well." Odile glanced at Edmund, who had stopped pretending to work and was listening with the expression of someone who has learned, at great cost, to be present for these moments. "The part where nobody owns magic anymore — that's true, and it's good. The part where a little bit of it lives in almost everybody now, quiet, like a seed under snow — true, and good. The part where the bad men lost — true, and very good, though it cost more than we like to say out loud." She tapped the counter. "The parts where it was frightening, and people got hurt, and my brother cried in a swamp —"

"I did not cry in a swamp," Edmund said.

"— those are also true," Odile continued, "but they're not the good parts, and your gran knows it, and that's why she tells the story the way she does. Stories are like orchards. You prune them so the best branches grow."

The girl absorbed this. Then: "Did you cry in the swamp?"

"Twice."

"*Once,*" Edmund said.

"Once in the swamp and once thinking about the swamp later," Odile said, and the girl laughed, sudden and bright, the laugh of a child who has just discovered that adults are people, and ran off toward the candle stall with her coins clutched in her fist.

Edmund watched her go. When they spoke again their voice was quieter. "Do you ever miss it? The old gift. The big one. What you could have been."

Odile took her time answering, because the question deserved it, and because she had spent a whole winter learning to answer honestly instead of quickly.

"I miss knowing," she said at last. "I miss walking through the dead orchard and feeling certain. But certainty nearly ate us both, Ed. What I have now — the moss that grows where I walk, slow, ordinary, mine — it's smaller. But it's *mine*. Nobody called me to it. Nobody woke it in my blood and told me what I owed. I planted it myself." She shrugged. "Turns out I like things better when I plant them myself."

Edmund nodded slowly, and reached under the counter, and came up with something wrapped in cloth, which they set between them without ceremony.

"What's this?"

"Open it."

Inside the cloth lay a charm. Small, unremarkable, a twist of salt-thread around a chip of pale stone — except that Odile knew stone, and this was salt-flat stone, and the thread was the thread Edmund had spun on the solstice night from the last of what remained in their own hands. It hummed faintly against her palm, the way all the scattered ones hummed, the note that every gifted person in the world could feel now if they held still long enough to listen.

"This is yours," she said. "You kept one."

"I kept two." Edmund's voice was steady, but their eyes weren't, quite. "One for each of us. I've been waiting a year to give you yours, because I wanted to wait until giving it didn't mean anything except that I love you. If I'd given it to you in the ruins, it would have been an apology. If I'd given it to you in Grayharbor, it would have been a bribe. Today it's just Tuesday, and the stall is open, and you built it straighter than I could have." They pushed it across the counter. "So. Happy Tuesday."

Odile picked it up. It sat in her hand like a bird deciding whether to stay. And she understood, suddenly, completely, what Edmund had understood on the flats with the sky coming apart above them: that the Salt had never been power. Power was just the shape it took when someone tried to hold it. Held loosely, given freely, it was only this — a small warmth, a small song, a small proof that the world intended to keep being wonderful whether or not anyone deserved it.

She hung it around her neck and tucked it under her shirt, against her skin, where it hummed its one low note like an orchard humming in April.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't make it weird."

"Too late. I'm going to make it extremely weird." She came around the counter and hugged her brother, hard, in front of the entire market, and Edmund endured it with the martyred patience of the deeply loved, and over Edmund's shoulder Odile saw the candle-seller watching, and the bread-girl, and the old man with his bottled light, and none of them looked away, and none of them crossed themselves, and somewhere in the crowd a child was already telling another child about the cartographer who cried in a swamp.

* * *

They closed at dusk, as the lamps came on — real lamps now, though half the city had stopped needing them — and Odile counted the day's coin while Edmund rolled the maps, and the market emptied around them in its unhurried way.

It was Wren who appeared at the closing, as she appeared everywhere these days, leaning against the next stall with her arms crossed and her courier's coat brushed clean and the Consortium sigil scraped off her belt-buckle, replaced by nothing at all. She had come to dinner four nights out of seven for the past three months, and had never once admitted this was what it was.

"Slow day?" she said.

"We sold out of river maps," Edmund said.

"Everyone's traveling north this season. Something about the northern lights being brighter than they've been in a century." Wren's mouth twitched. "Wonder why."

"Complete mystery," Odile said.

Wren pushed off the stall and came closer, and her voice dropped into the register she used for actual news, the one Odile had learned to trust more than any map. "Seraphine's register reached its hundredth teacher today. There's a ceremony in Grayharbor next month. She sent word — she wants the two of you there. Says the program wouldn't exist without what happened at the flats, and she's terrible at ceremonies and wants familiar faces in the crowd."

Edmund went still, the way they always went still at that name, and Odile watched them work through whatever they worked through — the alley, the hesitation, the wagon ride, the strange understanding between hunter and hunted that neither of them had ever fully explained to her and that she had decided, wisely, never to ask about.

"We'll be there," Edmund said.

"And there's one more thing." Wren hesitated, which was unlike her, and dug in her pocket, and brought out a scrap of paper, folded small and soft with handling. "A letter came for the stall. No sender. Smells like orchard blossom, which — correct me if I'm wrong —"

"Means it came from someone whose gift grows things," Odile said quietly. She took the letter. Her heart was doing something complicated. "Who else knows about us here?"

"Nobody official. Which is why I thought you should see it before anyone else does." Wren paused at the edge of the lamplight. "Dinner Sunday?"

"Sunday," Edmund said.

Wren vanished into the evening crowd, and Odile unfolded the letter under the lamp.

The handwriting was old-fashioned, careful, the letters of someone taught penmanship by a grandmother who had been taught by a grandmother — and Odile knew that lineage of teaching, had burned most of it in a hearth two years ago, had kept one page of it in a tin box through fire and flight and prison and war.

*To the wardens of the scattered Salt, whoever you have become.*

*I write because my garden woke this winter. Nothing grand — roses out of season, that's all — but my mother's mother had the same gift, and hers was burned for it, and I buried mine so deep I forgot I had it. Until the moon finished turning, and something under the snow remembered for me.*

*I am writing to ask what I owe. The stories say the Salt was given, not taken, and that someone chose to give it. I don't want to owe a debt I can't see. Tell me what the gift asks. Tell me what the wardens ask. Or tell me there are no wardens anymore, only gardeners, and I will tend what I've been given and pass seeds along and trouble you no further.*

*Yours in bloom,*
*M. Ashgrave*

Odile read it twice. Then she held it out to Edmund, who read it once, very fast, and looked up with an expression she recognized from a lifetime of watching — the expression of a cartographer who has just discovered that the map ends and the country goes on.

"There are no wardens anymore," Edmund said slowly. "Only gardeners."

"Then that's what we tell her." Odile folded the letter and tucked it beside the humming charm against her skin. "And then we tell the next one. And the one after that." She looked out over the darkening market, at the lamps and the laughter and the small, scattered, stubborn lights of a world learning to hold its wonder loosely, and she smiled. "It's a big garden, Ed. Somebody ought to map it."

Edmund laughed, and blew out the lamp, and together they walked home through a city that glowed.
