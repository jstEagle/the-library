## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There are three rules to surviving as the crown's food-taster, and I wrote them myself, which is how I know they're good ones.

One: never be interesting. Interesting people get promoted, and promoted tasters end up tasting for the wrong table — the kind where the poison is political and the antidotes are theoretical. Two: never refuse a dish. Refusal looks like accusation, and accusation at court is a disease with a mortality rate. Three: never let them see you enjoy anything. Enjoyment implies appetite; appetite implies a body with opinions; and bodies with opinions are bodies that can be bought.

I had followed my own rules for eleven years. Eleven years of swallowing small deaths so that Queen Maren could eat her pheasant in peace. Eleven years of being furniture with a tongue. It was, all things considered, a good arrangement. The pay was steady, the quarters were warm, and nobody asked a taster to dance.

Then came the night of the Ambassadors' Supper, when the third rule nearly killed me by breaking the second.

* * *

The kitchens beneath the palace smelled the way ambition smells: browned butter, scorched sugar, and under it all something faintly metallic, like fear left too long on a hot pan. I arrived an hour before service, as always, because the tasting begins long before the food reaches the table. A taster who only tastes at the table is a taster who dies surprised.

Cook-Master Berl met me at the pass with his sleeves rolled and his hands flour-white to the elbow. Berl was a mountain man gone soft in the capital, thick through the middle, with a mustache he groomed more attentively than most men groomed their children. He liked me, or he liked what I represented, which in a kitchen amounts to the same thing: I was the reason nobody had ever been dragged out of his kitchen in chains.

"Quintana." He said my name like a password. "Big night."

"They're always big nights, Berl. That's rather the point of them."

"Bigger." He wiped his hands on an apron that had given up hope long ago. "Ambassador Vessic's people are in town. The eastern delegation. And you know what they say about the east."

I knew what they said about everywhere. Every province, every duchy, every foreign court had its reputation for poison, and every one of those reputations was maintained by people like me, who made sure the reputations stayed rumors. "What are we serving?"

"Pheasant in plum glaze. Chestnut soup. The honey cakes she likes, the ones from the southern hives. And the wine — the Vellorine red, the ambassador brought a case himself. Gift."

A gift of wine from a foreign ambassador to a queen with a nervous court. In my trade, that sentence was less a courtesy than a riddle, and riddles were how tasters got buried.

"I'll want the wine opened now," I said. "Decanted. And I'll taste it before anything else goes near her table."

Berl nodded without argument, which told me he'd already thought of it. Good. Kitchens that thought ahead kept their cooks.

The cellar stair went down farther than the palace admitted to having. Down there, among casks older than the dynasty, I found the ambassador's case sitting alone on a shelf like a guest who'd arrived early and didn't know anyone. Six bottles, wax-sealed, each stamped with Vessic's crest — a coiled serpent eating its own tail, which I have always felt was less a crest than a confession.

I broke the wax on the first bottle. Poured a finger's worth into the tasting glass I carried everywhere, a heavy-bottomed thing scarred with eleven years of small survivals. I held it to the lamp. Color fine, deep garnet, legs slow down the glass. I swirled it. Smelled it.

And there it was.

Not in the wine. Under the wine. Beneath the black cherry and the smoke and the long cellar dark of it, something else — thin, green, almost sweet, like the air after lightning strikes a hedge. Most noses would have called it terroir. A clever nose would have called it nothing at all. My nose called it by name before my mind caught up, the way you flinch from a stove before you remember the stove is hot.

Hemlock water-dropwort, or something wearing its clothes. Not enough to kill outright. Enough to make a queen ill over weeks, ill in ways that looked like age, like grief, like a body quietly deciding to stop. The slow poisons were the fashionable ones now. Anyone could kill a king; it took real sophistication to make him fade.

Except — and here is where I should have been frightened, and instead was merely puzzled — except that I shouldn't have smelled it. Nobody should have. The compound was masked under a tannin profile that would take a master vintner a week of study to find suspicious. I had no training in master vintnery. What I had was eleven years of swallowing things designed never to be noticed, and somewhere along the way, my body had apparently decided to become an expert on the subject whether I consented or not.

I stood in that cold cellar holding a glass of murder and thought, not for the first time: *this is getting strange.*

* * *

Let me explain how a tasting actually works, because the ballads get it wrong. There's no dramatic collapse, no dying gasp of loyalty. Mostly it's arithmetic. You learn your doses. You learn your symptoms. You learn how much of a thing will make your mouth numb for an hour versus how much will stop your heart between the second course and the music. You keep a ledger in your head of everything you've survived, because survival is data, and data is the only inheritance a taster gets.

My ledger was long. Arsenic, twice — once in a fish pie, once in someone's idea of a joke at a midwinter feast. Belladonna, once, which cost me three days of seeing the world through gauze. Aconite, which made my fingertips feel like they'd been dipped in snow and set near a fire. Various organics whose names I never learned because the cooks who used them died before they could be interrogated. And hemlock itself, years ago, in a bad vintage from a bad year, back when my detection skills consisted mostly of noticing that the wine tasted like regret.

Each time, the dose was calibrated to sicken, not slay — the crown preferred its tasters alive, since dead tasters tell no tales and also require paperwork. Each time, I recovered. Each time, I recovered a little faster than the textbooks said I should. I'd mentioned this once, early on, to the Master of Household Security — a gray man named Corvane who smelled of ink and disappointment — and I watched his face go carefully blank in a way that taught me my second-most-valuable lesson: never again mention anything about yourself that cannot be explained by diligence.

So I stopped mentioning it. But I kept count privately, the way you keep count of debts you don't intend to pay. The arsenic that should have laid me up a fortnight took four days. The aconite that should have numbed me to the elbow barely reached my wrist. And lately — this past year — the poisons had begun announcing themselves to me before I swallowed them at all. First by smell, faintly. Then clearly. Now, apparently, through wax and cork and a foot of still air above the glass.

Bodies aren't supposed to do that. Bodies are supposed to endure, not learn. A sword doesn't get better at cutting because it's been cut.

I finished the arithmetic anyway, because sentiment is for people whose dinners don't try to kill them. The dose in the decanter would sicken the queen within the month and kill her within the season, slowly enough that three different factions could plausibly deny involvement and one of them could plausibly claim credit. Classic Vessic work, if it was Vessic work. It might not be. That was the other thing about slow poison: it was the one weapon at court that everyone had a motive to use and no one had to be present for.

I poured the bottle out into the gravel drain, rinsed the glass twice, and went up to ruin Berl's evening.

* * *

"The wine's off," I told him, in front of his sous-cooks, because a taster's verdicts are public or they're worthless. "Vellorine red, the ambassador's gift. All six bottles, if the others match. They'll need to serve the reserve stock."

Berl's face did something complicated. "Off? It was sealed—"

"Wine goes off. Corks fail. Happens to the best cellars." I said it lightly, breezily, with the practiced blandness of a man who has learned that the word *poison*, spoken aloud in a kitchen, travels upward faster than any tray. If I said *poisoned*, then by morning there would be an investigation, and investigations have a way of tasting everyone in the room, including tasters, and I had spent eleven years making sure there was nothing about me worth tasting.

He grumbled but complied. The reserve was sound — I checked it twice, out of habit and something less comfortable than habit. Service proceeded. The soup was unremarkable, which is the highest compliment I pay. The pheasant was excellent, which meant I ate three bites of it with genuine pleasure and then hated myself for the pleasure, per rule three.

At the table itself, I performed the little theater they expect of me. I stood behind the queen's right shoulder while the ambassadors watched, and I took my measured portions with a visible calm that was itself a message: *your tricks are known, your dinner is safe, go home.* Ambassador Vessic watched me chew the honey cake with an expression of polite interest that I chose to read as professional respect between colleagues. We were, after all, in the same business. His just had better funding.

Queen Maren ate everything. She was not a woman who enjoyed eating — she ate the way she signed documents, efficiently, with her attention elsewhere — but she ate, and she did not die, and therefore I had done my job, and therefore I could go home and sleep the dreamless sleep of the successfully paranoid.

Except.

As the plates were cleared, as the musicians tuned, as the whole glittering machine of the court ground onward toward dessert and diplomacy, the queen said something. Not to me — never to me; queens address their furniture through stewards — but near me, to her chamberlain, in the carrying half-voice that royal women use when they want a thing overheard.

"The eastern delegation grows bold, sending gifts ahead of their grievances."

And the chamberlain, a soft-spoken horror named Pell, inclined his head and said, "Your taster found nothing amiss, Majesty."

"Your taster," the queen said, "finds nothing amiss every year. One begins to wonder if he's tasting the food or simply eating it."

Laughter, light and terrible, around the table. I bowed at the correct depth. Inside, some cold careful part of me took note: *she's bored of you. Bored is the first stage of disposable.* Tasters who bored their queens got replaced, and replaced tasters got retired to country estates or shallow graves depending on how much they'd seen, and I had seen quite a lot, thank you, eleven years' worth.

But that wasn't the thing that kept me awake that night. The thing that kept me awake was smaller and stranger and mine alone.

Because when I'd lifted that spoon of chestnut soup to my lips, in full view of two delegations and a bored queen, I had smelled — not tasted, *smelled*, from a hand's breadth away, before the spoon touched my mouth — a trace of something bitter in the soup's steam. Faint. Almost nothing. Probably nothing: a scorched herb, a bad nut, the kitchen's ordinary ghosts.

Probably.

Eleven years ago, Edmund Quintana would have swallowed it and moved on. The Edmund Quintana of tonight had stood there in front of the entire court, spoon halfway to his lips, and *known* — the way you know a road is washed out before you see the gap — that if he swallowed it, his body would take the bitterness apart, catalog it, survive it, and file it away for next time. And next time it would be faster. And the time after that, faster still.

I drank the soup. Of course I drank the soup. Rule two.

But walking back through the cold corridors afterward, past guards who never saw me and servants who never greeted me, I caught my reflection in a window — a thin, forgettable man with a taster's stoop and a taster's pallor, the most invisible creature in the palace — and for the first time in eleven years, I didn't look away quickly. I looked, and I wondered what was accumulating under the skin of me. What eleven years of small deaths had been building in the dark, the way water builds in a wall, patient and invisible, until the day the plaster gives and everyone finally sees the shape of what was there all along.

My grandmother had a word for people like me. I hadn't thought of her in years — deliberately hadn't; memory was another thing a taster couldn't afford — but she came back to me that night, standing in her kitchen in the Thornfell foothills, stirring something that smelled of pine and iron, telling me in her flat mountain voice:

*"Some folk swallow the world's harm and keep it, child. And the mark finds them. And when the mark finds them—"*

She'd stopped there. Mothers calling, a door banging, the ordinary interruptions of a life. I'd never asked her to finish. Children don't ask grandmothers to finish the frightening parts.

In my narrow bed in the tasters' quarters, I turned my wrist over in the dark and looked at the inside of it, where the skin was pale from a lifetime indoors. Nothing there. Of course nothing there. Just a tired man's vein, and a pulse keeping its dull faithful time.

Just wait, said the quiet part of me, the part that had learned to smell hemlock through sealed wax. Just wait and see.

Three days later, the heralds rode through the city gates with the queen's colors flying, and the proclamation they nailed to every post in the capital began with words I would spend the rest of this story trying to outrun:

*Let it be known throughout the realm: by order of Her Majesty, all persons bearing the old sigil-gift shall present themselves for registration...*
