## 2. Proclamation at the Gate

I have stood in a great many crowds in my life, and I have learned to stand in them the way a stone stands in a river: present, unremarkable, and offering the current nothing to push against. This is not modesty. It is craft. A food-taster who draws attention dies of it eventually — usually quickly, usually badly, and always in front of witnesses who will describe the death as *regrettable*.

So when the queen's herald came to Thornfell on the first grey morning of autumn, I did what I always do. I arrived early, found my spot at the edge of the market square, put my back against the wall of Odile Vane's warehouse where no one could come up behind me, and made myself furniture.

The square filled anyway. That was the trouble with proclamations; they were one of the few things that could drag a whole town out of its beds before the bread was even out of the ovens. Farmers with hay still in their collars. Fishwives with their sleeves rolled. Children perched on barrels, delighted, because children love a herald the way they love a fire — all noise and color and no understanding whatsoever of what burns.

The herald was a young man on a chestnut horse, wearing the crown's blue and silver, and he had the voice of a man who had been taught to make announcements the way siege engineers make breaches. He unfurled a scroll the length of an arm and read.

"By order of Her Majesty, Queen Isolde the Second, rightful sovereign of the realm and its territories—" he paused there for the traditional silence, which we provided, "—let it be known throughout her lands that the gift of the sigil, that old and corrupted sorcery of the mountain folk, is hereby declared contraband in flesh as in deed."

*Contraband in flesh.* I remember thinking it was a strange phrase, and then remembering that whoever wrote it had probably been paid by the word, and cruelty is very economical with language. It says a lot in few words.

"All persons bearing the mark of the sigil upon their bodies," the herald continued, "shall present themselves to the crown's officers within thirty days for registration. Registration shall include examination, record, and—"

He said something else after *record*, but the wind took it, or perhaps I simply stopped hearing. There is a particular quality to dread that I know better than most people know their own hands. It does not arrive like fear arrives, all at once, a slammed door. Dread arrives like water finding a crack. It seeps. It takes its time. And long before you notice it, it has already reached the foundations.

"—and those who fail to register shall be declared harboring enemies of the crown, subject to inquiry, seizure, and such penalties as the law provides."

*Such penalties as the law provides.* The law, I could have told him, provided quite a lot. The law had been providing generously ever since the last war, when the crown finished putting down the sigil-bearers of the mountains and discovered that victory is expensive and must be paid for afterward, forever, in vigilance.

The herald rolled up his scroll. The horse shifted. A pigeon expressed its opinion from the eaves of the chandlery. And then the square did the thing that squares do, the thing I have watched a hundred times and never once gotten used to: it began, quietly, to organize itself around the new fear.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about purges, because the histories are written by people who were safely elsewhere when they happened: they do not begin with soldiers. They begin with neighbors.

Marta Ellery was the first in line. I knew Marta — everyone knew Marta — a broad, competent woman who sold cheese and had once, years ago, thrown a drunk soldier out of her stall by his collar without spilling a drop of her own cider. She marched up to the clerk's table beneath the gate like she was going to lodge a complaint about grain prices. I watched her lean over and say something to the crown's man, and I watched the crown's man write it down, and I watched Marta walk away looking lighter than she had in years.

I did not hear what she said. But I saw whose name she pointed at, because the man she pointed at was standing three feet from me, and I saw him understand, all at once and publicly, what had just happened to him.

His name was Tam Broadfoot. Cooper. Widower. The kind of man who fixed other people's cart wheels in winter and never sent a bill. He had a birthmark on his forearm — I'd seen it a hundred times, a dark splash shaped like nothing at all, like a spilled cup — and apparently Marta had decided, sometime between this morning and the herald's arrival, that a birthmark was worth reporting.

Tam didn't argue. That was the worst part. He looked at the clerk, and the clerk looked back with the flat patience of a man being paid by the day, and Tam rolled up his sleeve himself and held out his arm like a man showing a physician a growth. The clerk examined it, wrote something, and handed him a paper. A summons. Registration within thirty days. Examination.

Tam folded the paper with great care, the way you fold something you intend to keep, and walked past me without seeing me, and I understood that I was watching a man rehearse his own funeral arrangements.

By midmorning the line was forty deep. Not all of them were denouncing anyone. Some had come, God help them, to register themselves — old mountain families, descendants of the folk who'd never left the foothills, people whose grandmothers had married into valley blood two generations back and left them nothing but a story and maybe a mark. They came voluntarily, heads down, holding their arms out like Tam had, because the proclamation had said *register or be hunted*, and hunted is a word that gets into a body the way cold does.

And some came to trade. That was the part I couldn't look away from, though I tried. A purge is a marketplace, if you're built for it. Old grudges become currency overnight. The miller reported the tanner. Two brothers reported each other over a disputed fence line that predated the queen's grandmother. A woman I recognized from the fish market gave the clerk four names in the time it took me to drink half a cup of cider, and got a receipt for her trouble, and walked off with the particular brisk satisfaction of a person who has just converted an inconvenience into an asset.

I stood against Odile's wall and drank my cider and made myself stone.

That is the discipline, you see. That is the whole of the taster's art, underneath the palate and the nerve: you learn to watch terrible things happen at close range and keep your face doing nothing at all. I have swallowed hemlock with a courtier making jokes beside me. I have watched a fellow servant convulse on the kitchen flags while carrying on a conversation about the price of plums. The face is a door you learn to keep shut, and behind it you can scream all you like, so long as the screaming doesn't reach your hands.

My hands were fine. My face was fine. Behind the door, I was doing arithmetic.

* * *

Because here was the sum, and it would not resolve to anything comfortable.

Registration meant examination. Examination meant a trained officer looking closely at your skin, and possibly more than your skin — the old texts spoke of bearers whose marks lived under the surface, rising only under certain conditions. Grief. Fever. Poison.

Poison.

I set down the cup.

Nine years I had served the crown's table. Nine years of swallowing what was meant for others, because a dead taster is a warning and a living one is a service, and because the pay was good and the work was quiet and quiet was the entire point. I had taken things in those years that should have killed me twice over. Everyone said I had a strong constitution. My physician — the crown's physician, who checked me quarterly like a piece of equipment — called it remarkable resilience and noted it approvingly in his ledger, right next to my weight and my pulse, because everything about me was crown property, including the parts I hadn't known were parts.

But I had felt it for months now, the thing underneath the resilience. The way poison sat in me lately like a guest rather than an invader. The way my tongue had begun to taste truths it had no business tasting — the arsenic in the duke's wine last spring, identified before the silver needle touched the glass, faster than training allowed, faster than *should* be possible. I had told myself it was experience. Experience explains a great deal if you don't examine it too hard.

And now the crown was going to examine it for me.

I looked at my wrist. Bare, ordinary, tanned at the edge of the sleeve where I'd rolled my cuffs that morning. Nothing there. Nothing visible, which is not the same as nothing, and I had stopped believing it was nothing somewhere around midsummer, when I'd woken with a faint ache in the bones of my forearm that felt less like pain and more like pressure — like something written on the inside of the skin, waiting for ink.

Grandmother's voice, in my head, the way it always came at the worst moments: *A bearer who swallows enough of death learns to carry it, little bird. The mark comes up through the harm. That's how you'll know.*

She had said a great many things like that, my grandmother, sitting by her fire in the foothills, telling stories she claimed were only stories. I had been a child, and then a young man eager for the capital, and I had filed her warnings under superstition along with her charms against storms and her insistence that the mountain roads remembered who walked them. It had taken nine years of professional poisoning to make me reconsider the archive.

Thirty days. That was what the herald had given us. Thirty days to present myself for examination, or thirty days to become a fugitive in the only country I had ever worked for, hunted by the institution that fed me, clothed me, and owned my pulse.

Or — and here the arithmetic turned ugly — thirty days to find out whether there was anything on my wrist worth examining at all.

* * *

The crowd thinned toward noon. The clerk packed his table. The herald rode out the far gate toward the next town, trailing his scroll and his damage behind him, and Thornfell went back to its business the way a household goes back to its business after a death in the family — moving carefully around the empty place.

I bought bread I didn't want, because a man standing in a square buying nothing draws the eye, and I was turning to go when I felt someone fall into step beside me.

Odile Vane. Warehouse-keeper, fence, lender of money to people who preferred not to be asked why they needed it. She was perhaps fifty, built like a door, with a face that gave away exactly as much as she chose, which on most days was weather information and nothing else.

"You didn't get in line," she said.

"Neither did you."

"I'm not interesting." She took the bread from my hand, tore off a piece, and ate it, which was either familiarity or an audit, and with Odile it was usually both. "Half this town lined up to sell each other before the herald finished his second sentence. You stood against my wall for three hours and your face never moved. That's interesting."

"My face never moves. Ask anyone."

"I am asking." She chewed, watching the square rather than me, which was her way — she conducted conversations sideways, like a smuggler checking a road. "You know what examination means, of course."

"I imagine it means needles."

"It means needles, and ledgers, and a copy of every name going to the capital, and a copy of every mark going to the inquisitors' college, where they keep drawings of the old sigils the way a huntsman keeps tracks." She brushed crumbs from her fingers. "They're not registering people, Edmund. They're inventorying them. There's a difference, and the difference has a use, and the use isn't kind."

I said nothing. With Odile, saying nothing was also a form of speech, and she read it fluently.

"The mountain folk used to say the sigil wasn't a weapon," she went on, mildly, terribly. "Used to say it was a memory. That the marks rose on people who'd survived what should have killed them. Poison, mostly. Cold. Grief, if you believe the songs." She glanced at me then, briefly, one appraiser's flick of the eyes, wrist to face and back. "Folk who survive a lot tend to accumulate."

"You're telling me fairy stories."

"I'm telling you what the line at that table was really for." She handed me back the rest of my bread. "Thirty days. Long enough to think. Try to think somewhere other than against my wall — a man who stands in one place too long becomes a landmark, and landmarks get reported."

She walked off toward her warehouse, and I stood in the square with my bread going stale in my hand, doing the arithmetic again, and this time it resolved.

Registering meant testing. Testing meant revealing. And if there was anything rising in me — anything at all, any silver thread of the thing my grandmother had spent her whole life pretending not to believe in — then the crown that had fed me its poisons for nine years was about to discover that it had been cultivating the very thing it had sworn to burn.

I looked down at my wrist one more time, in the emptying square, in the flat noon light.

For half a heartbeat — I would swear to this later, though I'd swear it quietly, and only to myself — the skin there seemed to hold a shadow of a pattern, faint as breath on glass.

Then it was gone, or I blinked, or I wanted it gone badly enough to see it gone.

Thirty days.

I threw the bread to the pigeons and went home to decide how to disappear.
