## 3. What the Poison Left

The first thing you learn as a taster is that your body is not yours. It belongs to the crown, like the uniform you don't wear and the title no one says aloud. You are a piece of palace equipment with a heartbeat. When I was twelve and freshly apprenticed, Master Corvane told me this over a breakfast of deliberately spoiled eggs.

"Your tongue is a lock," he said. "Your stomach is a vault. Your opinions are a broom closet nobody visits. Keep them swept."

I had kept them swept for nineteen years. I had swallowed henbane at a border delegation's welcome feast and walked it off in the herb garden, sweating politely. I had taken arsenic meant for the queen's cup — twice, though the second time was an accident of catering rather than malice — and spent three days gray as dishwater while the physicians marveled that I lived. I had built up, dose by dose, year by year, what Corvane called "a working tolerance," which is the polite way of saying my body had learned to eat murder and ask for seconds.

What Corvane never mentioned — because he didn't know, because nobody knew — was that all that poison had to go somewhere.

It went into me. Into whatever I was underneath the equipment.

And sometime between the proclamation at the gate and the third sleepless night after it, it had begun to show.

* * *

I found it on the morning of the fourth day, washing my hands in the basin by the window, when the light came through the water at the right angle and caught something on the inside of my left wrist.

A mark. Silver, or silver-adjacent, the color of moonlight through smoke. Not a bruise — bruises don't shimmer. Not a scar — scars don't *move*. It sat just below where a pulse would be if I were the sort of person who checked their pulse, and it was shaped like nothing and like everything: a curl, a hook, half a letter in an alphabet I'd never been taught, drawn by someone whose hand trembled with purpose.

I turned my wrist toward the window. The mark drank the light and gave back more than it took.

"Well," I said to the empty room, "that's new."

That is the official record of my reaction, and I stand by it. A man in my profession learns early that panic is a luxury item, right up there with honey wine and honest grief. You note the anomaly. You assess the threat. You decide whether to report it, conceal it, or eat something bland and lie down.

I did all three, in order.

Then I sat on the edge of my narrow bed with my wrist held out like a beggar asking alms, and I let myself remember what I had spent nineteen years not remembering.

* * *

My grandmother raised me in the taster's quarters, in the years before the quarters were called that, back when they were just rooms above the old kitchens where the crown housed its expendables. She was not a taster herself. She was a laundress, which in the palace hierarchy is one rung below expendable and two rungs below furniture, and she had hands like tree bark and opinions she kept in a locked box somewhere behind her ribs.

She was also, though she'd have denied it under torture and probably had been, a bearer.

I know this now the way you know things in dreams — without evidence, without sequence, with total certainty. At the time, I only knew her habits. She wore long sleeves in every season. She flinched from silver. And once, when I was seven, she caught me tracing patterns in spilled flour on the table and went so still that I thought she'd died sitting up.

"What did I tell you about making marks?" she said.

"It's flour, Gran."

"Flour remembers." She swept it away with the side of her hand, fast, like killing something small. "Everything remembers. That's the trouble."

I laughed, because I was seven, and she looked at me with an expression I couldn't read then and can read now, having seen it since on the faces of people watching a door they hoped would stay shut.

Later — much later, the night before she died, when I was sixteen and already swallowing poisons professionally — she held my wrist in both bark hands and said the thing I have spent nineteen years pretending she didn't say.

"You'll want to know why you survive what should kill you," she said. "Don't ask the physicians. They'll call it constitution. Constitution is what clever men call a miracle they can't tax."

"Then what do I call it?"

"Nothing," she said. "You call it nothing. You keep your sleeves down and your mouth shut, and if ever — *if ever*, Edmund — you see silver on your skin, you come find the mountain folk. The ones who trade in the high passes. Don't go to the crown. Don't go to any priest. The mountain folk remember what it's for."

"What what's for?"

But she was tired, and dying, and she squeezed my wrist hard enough to leave marks of the ordinary kind, and said only: "Sleeves down, mouth shut. Promise me."

I promised. I was good at promises; they're a form of inventory, and I've always kept my inventory straight.

Then she died, and I buried her promise in the same grave as everything else she'd given me, and I got very good at not thinking about it, which is a skill, and like most skills it fails exactly when you need it most.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the sigil-gift, as of four days after the proclamation, sitting on my bed with my sleeve pushed up and my heart doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional:

I knew the stories every child knows. Once, before the crown was the crown, there had been people marked with silver who could hold things in their bodies that would kill anyone else. Poisons, mostly. Some said plagues. The village tales made them saints — the Bitter Saints, the ones who drank the well-water so the village could live. The court versions made them traitors, naturally, because the court version of any story makes someone a traitor. There had been a war about it, generations back. The crown won. The bearers were purged, registered, burned, or scattered, depending on which historian you paid, and the gift itself was declared extinct, which is the word governments use instead of *murdered*.

I knew the proclamation now hanging in every square: registration within thirty days, testing mandatory, harboring punishable.

And I knew — with the flat, cold certainty of a man reading his own autopsy — that if I registered, they would test me. And the test would be simple. They'd give me something bitter in a cup and watch whether I died.

I hadn't died from anything in nineteen years.

I was, by the crown's own definition, a bearer. I had been one for years. I had eaten their poisons at their table, in their service, wearing their invisible livery, and every swallow had been writing something on me in ink I couldn't see until now.

The mark on my wrist wasn't new. I understood that, lying there. It was a ledger, and ledgers don't appear overnight. Every drop of henbane, every grain of arsenic, every "working tolerance" — it had all been recorded. My body had been keeping books the whole time, patient as any clerk, and now, apparently, it had decided to publish.

The question — and here I sat up, because even dread gets bored of horizontal — was *publish to whom?*

* * *

Flashbacks are supposed to arrive in soft focus, I'm told, with music. Mine arrived in the smell of almonds.

Almonds mean cyanide, in my trade. Bitter almond means the cook has been careless or the guest has been ambitious, and either way you spit, rinse, and make a face that says *the sauce is over-salted* so the kitchen can save face and the assassin can save his deposit. I had spat a great many almonds in my career. But that morning, four days after the proclamation, I caught myself standing in the pantry holding a vial of almond extract — part of my legitimate stock, used for masking — and breathing it in like perfume.

It smelled like nothing. Like water. Like a memory of a smell.

I put the vial down carefully, the way you put down a snake.

That was the other thing the poison had left me, besides the mark. My senses were going strange. Not dulled — sharpened past usefulness, past sanity. I could taste the copper in the pipes. I could smell the difference between two batches of flour from the same mill. Three nights ago I had dreamed in colors that don't exist, and woken with the taste of a flower I couldn't name on my tongue, and it had taken me half the day to stop tasting it.

Corvane would have called it palate development. Corvane is dead now, peacefully, in a way I envied him.

The truth was less comfortable. The truth was that the sigil-gift — if that's what this was, and the silver curl on my wrist had stopped being deniable around the second pot of tea — didn't just survive poison. According to the oldest stories, the ones the mountain folk supposedly kept, the gift *held* things. Kept them. Remembered them. A bearer who drank poison didn't destroy it; they carried it, changed it, made it harmless the way a river makes silt harmless — by carrying it somewhere else.

Which meant that everything I had swallowed for the crown was still in me, somewhere, transformed. Nineteen years of the court's worst intentions, filed away in my blood like letters in a dead man's desk.

I was not a food-taster. I had not been one for years. I was an archive that chewed.

* * *

On the fifth night I did what my grandmother had forbidden and my survival demanded: I went looking for the mountain folk.

Not literally — I'm reckless, not stupid, and Thornfell's passes in autumn are closed to everyone except smugglers and corpses, categories with considerable overlap. But I went looking in the only ledger I trusted, which is to say the mental one, the one where I keep every debt, favor, route, and secret I've collected in nineteen years of being furniture that listens.

Furniture hears a lot. Furniture in the kitchens hears everything.

I knew that Odile Vane ran the softest smuggling operation through the eastern passes — soft meaning quiet, quiet meaning bribed, bribed meaning expensive but reliable. I knew her caravans moved grain down from the high valleys twice a season, and that her carts sometimes carried things grain carts shouldn't, and that the mountain folk who worked her routes were paid in coin and silence and asked no questions in either direction. I knew that once, three years ago, a palace guard had whispered to another guard over the wine I'd already tasted that Vane's people could move anything through Thornfell, "even things that ought to burn," and had glanced over his shoulder when he said it, and had not said it again.

Even things that ought to burn.

I sat with that phrase for a long time, turning my wrist in the candlelight, watching the silver catch and hold the flame's reflection like it was drinking.

Thirty days. Twenty-five now. If I ran without help, I'd die in the passes — I knew the roads, but knowing roads and surviving them alone in the shoulder season are different skills, and the mountains kill experts as a hobby. If I stayed and hid, the inquisitors would come eventually, because they always come eventually; patience is the one weapon the crown never runs out of. If I registered —

If I registered, I'd die in a test chamber, politely, having followed every rule, having kept my sleeves down and my mouth shut for nineteen years, and the crown would enter me in a ledger as a confirmed bearer, terminated, threat resolved, and my grandmother's promise would turn out to have been worth exactly what obedience always turns out to be worth.

"Sleeves down, mouth shut," I said aloud to the empty room. "You forgot to mention the part where it doesn't work, Gran."

The mark on my wrist caught the light. It had grown since morning. Barely — a hair's width at the curled end, the way frost grows on glass, too slow to watch and impossible to deny. Whatever my body had been keeping all these years, it was done keeping it private.

I blew out the candle. In the dark, the mark glowed faintly, patiently, like a door left open for someone expected.

I dressed for travel. I counted out coin. And I began composing, in the privacy of my own skull, the opening terms of a negotiation with Odile Vane — a woman, by all reports, who charged by the secret and executed by the lie, and who was therefore, in my professional judgment, the most honest person in the province.

There was a grain caravan leaving for the eastern passes in six days. I intended to be on it.

I did not yet know what — or who — I would find in the bottom of those carts. But the mountains were already calling, in the voice my grandmother used, and for the first time in nineteen years of swallowing other people's poisons,

I was going to answer.
