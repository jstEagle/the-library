## 4. The Child in the Grain Cart

There are three rules to moving contraband through Thornfell, and I had learned all of them the expensive way.

One: never travel the same road twice in a season. Two: never carry anything you can't eat, burn, or bury. Three: never, under any circumstances, open a sealed shipment before you deliver it — because the moment you know what's inside, it becomes *yours*, and everything that's yours can be taken from you.

I broke rule three on a Tuesday, somewhere past the second switchback above Miller's Hollow, because the grain cart was riding wrong.

Not broken-wrong. Not axle-deep-in-mud wrong. It was riding wrong the way a purse rides wrong when someone has slipped an extra coin into it without your leave. I'd hauled this cart up and down these mountains for six years; I knew its moods like I knew my own pulse. And its moods were saying: *you are carrying more weight than barley.*

I told myself it was damp grain swelling in the sacks. I told myself the road had tilted since autumn. I told myself several other lies with increasing creativity until the cart lurched over a root and something inside it made a sound no sack of barley has ever made.

A sneeze.

Small. Muffled. Absolutely, catastrophically human.

I reined the mule to a stop and sat there for a long moment, listening to the wind move through the pines and doing arithmetic I did not want to do. If there was a person in my cart, then someone had put them there, which meant someone had known my route, which meant either Odile Vane had sold me out or the crown had gotten clever, and both possibilities ended with me hanging from the same beam.

Better, then, to know. Knowledge is the one cargo I've never regretted carrying.

I climbed down, walked around to the tailgate, and drew my knife — not to threaten, but because a man who opens mysterious shipments unarmed is a man who deserves what he finds — and cut the wax seal on the canvas flap.

Two amber eyes stared back at me from between sacks of barley.

They belonged to a child. A girl, maybe eight years old, thin as a winter branch, with soot ground so deep into her skin that at first I thought her hair was gray. She didn't scream. She didn't cry. She looked at my knife, looked at my face, and did the thing that frightened me more than any of it: she went perfectly still, the way small animals go still when they've learned that stillness is their only trick.

"Well," I said, because someone had to say something. "You're not barley."

She said nothing.

"You're also not mine," I continued, mostly to myself, "which is a problem, because as of about four seconds ago, you're in my cart, on my road, in my mountains, and every one of those things is now a crime I'm committing."

Still nothing. But her eyes flicked — just once, quick as a fish turning — toward the treeline behind us. Toward the way she'd come.

That was when I smelled the smoke. Not hearth-smoke, not kiln-smoke. The fat, sweet, terrible smell of a burned village, which travels for days and clings to everything, including, apparently, eight-year-old stowaways.

I put the knife away.

* * *

Here is what I knew, standing in that road with my hand still on the cart rail: I should have put her out. Right there, given her water and half my bread and pointed her down toward the valley road where travelers still passed, and ridden on. That was the smart move. That was the move that kept me invisible, and invisibility was the only wealth I'd ever built, coin by careful coin, over eleven years of swallowing poison at the queen's table and memorizing smuggler roads through the Thornfells so that someday — soon, soon — I could buy a cottage with a garden and be nobody at all, permanently, on purpose.

Every instinct I owned said: *not your problem. Not your crime. Not your child.*

But I have a confession to make, and I'll make it here where no tribunal can read it: I looked at that girl sitting among the barley sacks with soot in her eyelashes, and I did not see a problem. I saw my grandmother's kitchen, and the way she used to bar the door at night even though we lived three days' ride from anywhere, and the way she said, when I asked why:

*"Because doors are only wood, Edmund. What keeps us safe is that nobody remembers we're worth finding."*

And then I looked at the smoke smell clinging to this child, and understood that somebody had remembered she was worth finding after all.

"Can you talk?" I asked.

A nod. Small.

"Are you hurt?"

A pause. Then another nod, smaller, the kind of nod that means *yes but not in the way you're asking.*

I climbed into the cart and sat down across from her, which was a mistake, because up close I could see what the soot was hiding. There was a mark on her left wrist — or rather, there had been a wound on her left wrist, recently cauterized, badly, and in the ruined flesh beneath the crude scarring something glowed. Faintly. Like a coal buried in ash. Like a letter written in light that only wanted to be read.

I knew that glow.

I had spent three weeks telling myself the silver tracery blooming along my own wrist was a rash. An allergy. A taster's occupational hazard, some interaction of arsenic residue and hard mountain water. I had been very persuasive. My own eyes, however, remained stubbornly unconvinced, and now they looked at this child's wrist and finished the argument I'd been losing.

The sigil. The old gift. The thing the heralds had spent the last month screaming about from every gate in the kingdom — *register or be hunted, register or be hunted* — the thing I had watched my neighbors line up to denounce each other for bearing.

This child hadn't registered. This child had run. And whatever village had raised her —

The smoke smell finished that sentence too.

"What's your name?" I asked.

She considered me for a long time. I let her. You don't rush a creature deciding whether you're predator or weather.

"Ilsa," she said finally. Her voice was hoarse, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in weeks.

"Ilsa. I'm Edmund. I drive this cart. I'm going to ask you two questions, and I need true answers, because true answers are the difference between me helping you and me being unable to." I kept my voice level, the way I kept it when reporting to the queen's chamberlain — flat enough to hide in. "Who put you in my cart?"

"No one."

"No one? You climbed into a sealed grain cart in Miller's Hollow by yourself?"

"I hid in the hollow under the sacks. At the mill. When the carts came through." Her chin came up a fraction, and underneath the terror I caught the first glimpse of a spine that would serve her well if she lived long enough to grow into it. "You checked the seal too late."

Despite everything — despite the smoke and the glowing wrist and the gallows doing slow arithmetic in the back of my mind — I almost laughed. "So I did. Second question. Who burned your village?"

Her whole body changed. The stillness cracked, and what came through it wasn't crying, exactly. It was worse. It was the sound a person makes when they've cried themselves empty and what's left is just air moving through grief.

"The gray men," she whispered. "With the white hands. They came at night and they made everyone stand in the square and they asked who carried the mark. Old Tam did. He showed them. He thought showing them would save him." Her breath hitched. "It didn't save anyone. They took the marked ones into the tithe barn and then the barn was fire, and then they started asking everyone else who *knew* the marked ones, and Mama pushed me into the flour cellar and said don't come out till the carts come, and—" She stopped. Her hand found her wrist, found the ruin there, covered it like a wound, which is what it was. "The carts came," she said. "So I got in."

Gray men with white hands. Inquisitors. Wren Halloway's order — though I didn't know that yet, wouldn't learn it for another week, and God help me, when I did learn it, part of me would keep hoping I'd misheard.

I sat in the barley dust and did the last of the arithmetic, and this time I didn't lie to myself about the total.

If I turned her out, she died. Maybe not today, maybe not tomorrow, but a soot-marked eight-year-old with a fresh-cauterized sigil wandering the Thornfell roads alone had a life expectancy I could measure in weather. If I turned her in, I saved myself nothing, because delivering a sigil-bearer meant explaining how I'd found her, which meant explaining why I'd been on an unregistered smuggler road with a sealed cart, which meant the noose either way — and besides, I'd sooner have swallowed the queen's whole poison cabinet than hand a burned child to the people who'd lit the fire.

And if I kept her?

Then I stopped being invisible. Then the careful life — the tasting, the routes, the cottage-with-a-garden dream I'd been saving toward like a miser counting copper — all of it became collateral. Everything that's yours can be taken from you. Rule two, the oldest one.

I looked at Ilsa. She was watching me decide, and she knew it, and she was too tired to beg, and that — more than the sigil, more than the smoke — is what decided me. Nobody should have to watch their fate get weighed while they're too empty to argue for it.

"Here's how this works," I said. "You stay in the cart. You stay quiet. When I stop, you stay hidden unless I say otherwise. You don't tell anyone your name, your village, or what's on your wrist — not anyone, not ever, not even people who seem kind. Especially people who seem kind." I dug in my pack, found the water skin and the heel of black bread, and handed them over. "In exchange, I take you somewhere the gray men can't follow. That's the whole bargain. No thanks required. No promises beyond it. I'm not your family and I'm not your cause. I'm a cart driver."

She drank half the water in four swallows, tore the bread with startling precision — saving the larger piece, I noticed, wrapping it in her sleeve for later, a child who had already learned famine mathematics — and then looked up at me with those amber eyes and destroyed my entire speech with six words:

"You checked the seal," she said. "Nobody checks the seal."

Meaning: *you looked. You opened the cart knowing what opening it might cost, because something sounded wrong.* Meaning: *I chose right, hiding in yours.*

I climbed back onto the bench, took up the reins, and clicked the mule forward, and I will admit — privately, silently, in the ledger where I keep the accounts I can't balance — that the road felt different with her in it. Heavier. Warmer. More dangerous. All three at once, the way the truest things usually are.

* * *

We made the high camp at dusk, in a hollow below the old shepherd cairn where the trees close in thick enough to swallow lantern-light. I gave Ilsa stew from the pot I'd buried that morning, and she ate like a small polite wolf, and afterward she slept curled against the barley sacks with her bandaged wrist tucked against her chest.

I sat up keeping watch, which was a fiction — nothing moved in those woods but owls — and really I sat up looking at my own wrist in the last gray light.

The silver tracery had grown again. Three new branches since the proclamation, fine as frost-fern, and where they crossed the older lines the skin gave off that faint buried-coal warmth I'd been pretending not to feel. Every poison survived, my grandmother had said once, near the end, when her mind wandered backward through the years. *Every poison leaves something behind, Edmund. The body keeps accounts the tongue swears it doesn't.*

I had thought she was talking about bitterness. About grudges. The things survivors carry.

Sitting in the dark with a sleeping fugitive ten feet away and the crown's purge rolling through the valleys below like weather, I understood she had been talking about something else entirely. Something the heralds called abomination and the mountain folk, if Odile Vane's rumors were true, called something much older and much kinder.

The body keeps accounts.

Mine had been keeping them for eleven years, one swallowed cup at a time, and tonight, holding a stolen child's future in the same hands that held the queen's poisoned cups, I finally read the sum.

Below us, far down the valley, a thread of torchlight moved along the king's road. Too orderly for smugglers. Too numerous for merchants.

Gray men with white hands, coming up into the Thornfells.

I banked the fire, woke Ilsa softly — she came awake instantly, completely, the way hunted things do, and hated that I noticed — and began breaking camp by starlight, because tomorrow's route ran through country no inquisitor's map had ever bothered to record.

Roads that remember, my grandmother used to call them.

I was about to find out how well.
