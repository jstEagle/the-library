## 5. Wren

The road into Thornfell smelled of pine resin and wet stone and, faintly, of someone's supper — onions, I thought, and something gamey that had no business being this far from a proper kitchen. My mule, Bitterroot, had opinions about the grade. She expressed them by stopping every forty paces to consider her life choices, and I let her, because a woman who has spent six years interrogating farmers learns patience the way other people learn prayer: slowly, resentfully, and with occasional lapses.

I was Wren Halloway, Inquisitor Second Class, bearer of a writ signed in the queen's own hand, and I was riding up a mountain to hunt children.

That last part sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. The official language was cleaner. *Locate and register all persons bearing the old sigil-gift.* Register. As though the sigil were a birthmark you could note in a parish book and be done with. As though registration were mercy.

My brother had registered. Tam had walked to the magistrate's office in Aldermoor with his chin up, because he was honest the way some people are tall — structurally, unavoidably — and he'd put his palm on the reader's plate and let the silver light crawl up his wrist where the mark lived, and he'd come home and told me it was fine, Wen, it's just paperwork now, we're safe, they said so.

They said so.

I hadn't heard from him in four months. His letters came irregular before; now they didn't come at all, and when I wrote to the registry office for confirmation of his status, I received back a form letter with a seal on it and a sentence that managed to be reassuring and ominous in the same breath: *Your inquiry has been noted and filed.*

Filed. Like evidence.

So yes. I rode up the mountain with a stone in my chest and a writ in my saddlebag and a list of names in my head, and at the top of the list, in the place of honor where I'd tried very hard not to put anyone, was a name I had known since I was nine years old.

Quintana.

* * *

We had grown up in the taster's quarters behind the palace kitchens, Edmund and I, which is a sentence that requires explanation for anyone who wasn't raised in the particular shadow of the crown's table. Tasters were servants, but strange ones — half honored, half feared, because their whole trade was dying instead of other people. Their children played in the herb gardens among the hemlock beds (labeled, even then; the palace was thorough about its poisons) and learned early not to lick their fingers after touching anything.

Edmund's mother had been a taster before them. Died of it, eventually — not poison, ironically, but a fever that swept the quarters the winter I was eleven. I remember Edmund going quiet for a season, the way small things go quiet when they're deciding what shape to be. And then Edmund went careful. Careful became the whole architecture of them: careful hands, careful face, careful words that told you everything except anything true.

I had been the loud one. I'd talked enough for both of us, climbed the kitchen roof, stolen honeycomb from the stores and split it with Edmund on the slates while the whole court glittered below us like a spilled jewelry box. We used to play a game called Poisoner and Taster, which in retrospect says something bleak about our childhoods. One of us would hide a pebble in one fist — the "poison" — and the other would have to guess which hand. Edmund always won. Always. Not because they cheated, but because they watched. They'd read some flicker in my wrist or my breathing or the way I shifted my weight, some tell so small I never found it myself, and they'd point at the right fist every time, calm as Sunday.

"You're cheating," I'd say.

"I'm paying attention," Edmund would say. "There's a difference."

There was a difference. There always had been. I'd gone into the Inquisition because I believed in order — genuinely, stupidly, at fourteen, watching the city burn in the Bread Riots and thinking *someone should make the rules make sense.* Edmund had gone into service because service was invisible, and invisibility was safety, and safety was the only inheritance a taster's child could count on.

Two answers to the same fear. That's what we were, I think, looking back. Two different answers to the same fear.

* * *

Thornfell's garrison commander was a man named Brase, who received me in a room with a map of the province pinned to one wall and a brazier going against all sense in the mild autumn air, because Brase was cold-blooded in every meaning of the word and liked the room hot enough to wilt flowers.

"Inquisitor Halloway," he said, and looked me over with the frank appraisal of a man pricing a horse. "Young."

"Thirty-one," I said. "The writ will confirm my authority regardless."

"Oh, I don't doubt your authority." He poured wine without offering any. "I doubt your numbers. You've come alone?"

"Alone is sufficient."

"For a purge?" He smiled, showing teeth like fence posts. "Inquisitor, I've got two hundred men on this mountain and I can't keep the smuggling roads closed. Every village up here has a cellar the tax assessors can't find and a shrine the last purge missed. You want to register sigil-bearers? Half these people can't write their names. The other half will swear on their mothers' graves that grandmother's silver wrist-mark is just a burn from the still."

"Then we'll test them."

"With what? The reader-plates are in the capital. I requisitioned three in spring." He tapped the map, on which Thornfell township was marked with a neat ink cross. "I was told the plates were needed elsewhere. So until the church deigns to send equipment, your purge is a matter of rumor, rumor, and more rumor."

Rumor, as it happened, was doing excellent work already. That was the thing about proclamations: you didn't need soldiers to deliver them. Fear traveled faster than cavalry and required no fodder. The herald had read the queen's decree from the Thornfell gate nine days before I arrived, and in those nine days the town had performed its usual miracle of self-mutilation. Neighbors denouncing neighbors. A miller who'd reported his own brother-in-law over a disputed fence line, dressing the grudge in patriotic linen. Three families fled into the high passes in a single night, leaving doors open and bread going stale on tables.

"The fugitives," I said. "From the village of Lowmarsh. The ones who ran after the herald came through. Where did they go?"

Brase shrugged with magnificent indifference. "Into Thornfell. Mountains are full of holes. Ask the smugglers."

"I intend to."

"Good luck with that too." He leaned back. "You know what I know about the sigil-bearers up here, Halloway? I know that the mountain folk never stopped keeping their little shrines. Never stopped the remembrance days. Crown banned the practice sixty years ago, burned the chapels, and these people went right on lighting candles in sheep barns and calling it nothing. You don't stamp out a faith by burning its buildings. You just teach it to live smaller."

He said it lightly, a man describing weather. But something in it snagged on me, the way a nail snags silk. *You don't stamp out a faith by burning its buildings.* I turned the sentence over later, in the room they gave me above the garrison stable, and found it heavier than it had first seemed. Because that was precisely what we were attempting, wasn't it? Not a census. An erasure. Registration was only the polite first stroke of it, the way a surgeon's first cut is polite compared to everything after.

And Tam. Tam had registered. Tam had trusted the paperwork.

I lay on the narrow garrison cot and listened to the stable boy below singing something tuneless and old, an air I half recognized from my grandmother humming it over her loom, and I did not sleep.

* * *

The work itself, I'll describe honestly, because dishonesty about this kind of thing is how inquisitors sleep at night, and I had decided I would rather not sleep.

We began with the town. No reader-plates yet, so we worked the old way: testimony, record, confrontation. I interviewed forty-one persons in five days. Most claimed ignorance. A few claimed knowledge generously and inaccurately, pointing fingers at everyone they disliked. Two broke down weeping and admitted to nothing at all, which was somehow worse.

On the sixth day a woman named Sera Voss came to me privately, at dusk, hood up, hands shaking. She was a weaver, mid-fifties, with the cracked knuckles of someone who'd worked a loom through thirty winters.

"My granddaughter," she said. "She was born with the mark. On her shoulder blade, silver, like frost ferns. My daughter hid it. Registered the babe as born clear." Her voice frayed. "But the girl is eight now, and she glows, Inquisitor. When she dreams, sometimes. When she's frightened. The mark lights up under her skin like a lamp behind cloth, and last month the schoolmistress saw."

"What happened to the child?"

"They took her." Voss's jaw worked. "Not the garrison. Men in gray, no colors, no papers shown. Took her and took my daughter too, for the concealment. Three nights ago. I want to know where. I want to know if—" She stopped. Couldn't finish the sentence. Didn't need to. We both knew what the sentence ended with.

"Men in gray," I repeated. "Did they give a name? A warrant?"

"No papers. That's what frightens me most, Inquisitor. Even the crown shows papers when it comes for you. These men showed nothing." She gripped my sleeve with fingers like roots. "You're the law here. Tell me the law did this. Tell me it was the law, because if it was the law, then at least there's a record somewhere, a ledger, a name. If it wasn't the law—"

If it wasn't the law, then her granddaughter had vanished into a gap between jurisdictions, and gaps were where people went to stop existing.

I promised her I would inquire. I inquired. Brase knew nothing, or claimed to. The garrison log showed no such action. Gray men, no colors, taking a marked child off the mountain without warrant or record — and I stood in the garrison yard that evening feeling the first real crack go through the load-bearing wall of my certainty, because I had built my whole self on the proposition that order, even harsh order, was better than chaos. Rules, even cruel rules, beat no rules. But what do you call men who take children without papers? What rule were they serving?

Unless the cruelty *was* the rule, and the paperwork was theater for people like me.

I wrote to the registry office again that night. Third letter. *Requesting status verification: Tammas Halloway, registered bearer, Aldermoor district.* I sealed it and set it with the dispatches and felt the stone in my chest shift its weight.

* * *

It was on the ninth day that I heard the name properly for the first time.

A drover, brought in drunk and belligerent after a dispute at the Woolmarket, decided to purchase his release with information. Most of what he sold was worthless — smuggler gossip, grudges, a tale about a ghost in the old quarry that I filed under folklore. But buried in the dregs of his talk was a nugget:

"—and the Vane woman's holding, up past the chapel ruins, that's where the mountain folk send what they want kept. Odile Vane keeps everything. Debts, secrets, contraband, children, if the price is right. Why d'you think nobody touches her? Half the garrison owes her money and the other half owes her silence." He leered. "Even got a taster up there not long since. Palace man. Quintana. Came through with a grain cart, regular as taxes."

Quintana.

I made my face stay administrative. "This taster. What business does a palace food-taster have running grain carts through Thornfell?"

The drover shrugged. "Palace eats too, don't it? Mountain grain, mountain cheese. Somebody's got to haul it." He grinned, gap-toothed. "Though folk say Quintana knows the passes better than the shepherds do. Say there's roads up there even the smugglers forgot, and Quintana walks 'em like a Sunday stroll."

I released him with a warning and went back to my rooms and sat down hard on the cot.

Because I knew. Of course I knew. Edmund had always known the mountains — had spent years, even in the capital, taking leave each season and vanishing into Thornfell, and when I'd teased them about it once, years ago, over cheap wine in a tavern near the river gate, Edmund had said, with that careful flatness that was their version of a locked door: "I'm mapping routes. Someday I'll want out of the city fast, and I intend to know the way."

I'd laughed. I'd thought it was a joke, or a fantasy, the way servants fantasize about cottages and freedom. Edmund hadn't laughed. Edmund had looked at me across the table with those patient, cataloguing eyes — the eyes that always found the right fist — and said, quietly, "You should think about your own way out, Wren. Institutions are kind to you right now. Kindness from institutions is a lease, not a gift."

I'd thought them paranoid. I'd thought: *how like a taster's child, to see poison in everything.*

And now I was riding up the mountain with a writ, hunting their kind, and Edmund was hauling grain carts past a fence's hold that kept secrets for sale, and the question I could not stop turning over was the oldest one in my trade:

What was in the other fist?

* * *

Here is what I told myself, that night and several nights after, arranging the facts like evidence on a table: Edmund Quintana was a servant of impeccable record. Fifteen years a taster, sworn to the crown's table, throat offered daily to the queen's enemies. No one with the sigil-gift would choose that life — the testing alone, the medical examinations, the proximity to inquisitors. It made no sense. Edmund was careful, and the sigil-gift was the least careful thing a person could carry. Therefore Edmund was clean.

It was a good argument. It was even, mostly, true.

But underneath it, in the cellar of myself where I kept the things I didn't examine, another argument sat in the dark, patient as a spider. *You haven't seen them in three years,* it said. *People change. Bodies change. And you, Wren — you requested this posting, didn't you? Out of every province, you asked for Thornfell. Why?*

Why. Because the fugitives had run here. Because the trail led here. Those were the reasons I'd written on the request form, and both were true, and neither was the reason.

The reason was that if Edmund was ever going to surface in my files, I wanted to be the one who found them. Before the gray men found them. Before Brase's two hundred boots found them. Because whatever else the years had done, whatever offices we'd grown into, I remembered a rooftop and stolen honeycomb and a voice saying *I'm paying attention, there's a difference*, and some stubborn root of me believed — needed to believe — that attention ran both ways. That Edmund had been paying attention to me all these years too. That they knew what I was now, what I did, what I served.

And if they knew, and had still sent no warning, no letter, no whispered word through the smuggler grapevine that surely connected a fence's hold to a palace kitchen — then either Edmund had forgotten me, or forgiven the crown too thoroughly, or was protecting themselves the only way left, which was distance.

Or — and here the spider moved — Edmund was protecting *me*. Staying away from me. Keeping their name out of my mouth because my mouth was now an instrument of the registry, and anything it touched got written down, and written-down things, in this new country we lived in, had a way of ending up crossed out.

I thought of Tam. I thought of the form letter. *Your inquiry has been noted and filed.*

I got up, lit the lamp, and pulled out my casebook. Under *Persons of Interest — Unconfirmed*, I wrote: *E. Quintana, taster, palace household. Observed in Thornfell vicinity, engaged in provisioning trade. No indication of sigil-gift. Recommend routine interview upon next capital rotation.*

Routine. The word looked absurd even as I wrote it. Nothing about Edmund had ever been routine. But I wrote it anyway, because writing *recommend immediate surveillance* would have been an admission — of suspicion, of hope, of the fact that I had ridden up this mountain carrying two lists, the official one and the other one, and on the other one there was only ever one name.

I blew out the lamp. Below my window, the stable boy was singing his tuneless song again, and I recognized it now beyond doubting: it was a remembering-air, one of the old ones, the kind the crown had banned sixty years ago and the mountains had never stopped humming. He sang it the way children sing anything — without knowing what it carried.

*The roads remember, the stones remember,*

*the wind writes it down where no hand can burn it—*

I lay in the dark and listened, and told myself the crack in my conviction was fatigue.

Three days later, the reader-plates arrived from the capital. Six of them, crated in straw, with a supplementary writ: priority districts, mandatory testing, effective immediately. And with them, a dispatch summary of open inquiries across the province — and at the bottom of the list, flagged fresh, an item that stopped my breath:

*Unregistered minor, female, approx. 8 years, sigil-mark confirmed active. Village of Ashford Hollow destroyed by fire during apprehension attempt; subjects escaped. Fugitives believed moving north through Thornfell high country toward Vane's Hold. Gray company detached. All inquisitors to coordinate.*

Ashford Hollow. Burned. A child of eight, running for the holds, with the whole machinery of the hunt closing behind her — and ahead of her, the one person on this mountain who knew every forgotten road, every hole Brase couldn't close, every path the wind wrote down where no hand could burn it.

I closed the dispatch and sat very still in the lamplight, and understood, with the terrible clarity of the right fist finally opening, exactly where Edmund Quintana would be.
