## 6. Smuggler's Arithmetic

Odile Vane kept her counting house in a place that had been, in some previous and more honest century, a shrine to a river god. The river had since been rerouted by royal engineers to fill the palace cisterns, and the god had been struck from the rolls of permitted divinities, which left the building available for rent to anyone whose business preferred not to be counted. This was, I suspect, the only joke the crown ever made on purpose, though I doubt they knew they were making it.

I had done business with Odile for eleven years. I knew her the way you know a knife you've carried a long time: precisely, gratefully, and without any illusions about what would happen if you gripped the wrong end.

The girl waited in the treeline above the shrine with a blanket around her shoulders and my last apple in her fist. I had told her to run if I whistled twice. She had told me, with the flat certainty only eight-year-olds and emperors can manage, that she wouldn't run. I decided not to argue with either of us about it and went down the goat path alone.

The door was answered by a boy of perhaps fourteen with a crossbow held the way boys hold crossbows — as though it were a large and disappointing cat.

"She's expecting me," I said.

"She's expecting a taster." He looked me over. "You look like a man who's been sleeping in a ditch."

"Then she's expecting exactly the right man. Complimentary, even."

He grunted and let me in, which in the Vane household constituted a warm embrace.

* * *

The counting room smelled of tallow, dried herbs, and old stone — the stone smell underneath everything, the smell of a building that had outlived three religions and intended to outlive the fourth. Odile sat behind a table scarred with knife-marks that predated her grandmother, doing sums in a ledger with a quill sharpened to a point that could have opened a vein. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a barrel that had been in a fight and won, and her eyes had the particular flat patience of a woman who had heard every lie the lowlands produced and found most of them derivative.

"Quintana." She didn't look up. "You're three days early and one cart short."

"My cart met with an accident."

"Your carts frequently meet with accidents."

"I attract misfortune. It's a professional hazard of honesty."

That earned me the look up. Her quill stopped. "You're alone."

"I'm accompanied."

"By?"

"By a debt," I said, "that I'd like to convert into passage."

Odile set down the quill with the care of a woman who had learned that sudden movements preceded most of the bad news in her life. "Sit," she said, "and start from the beginning, because I can see the beginning is going to be the part you'd rather skip."

So I sat, and I told her. Not all of it — I am a taster, concealment is my trade, and old habits die with more dignity than most men do — but enough. The proclamation at the gate. The registry. The testing. The mark on my wrist that I had been pretending for a month was a birthmark, a scar, a stain of walnut ink, anything other than what it was. And the child in the grain cart, and the village that had burned behind her, and the inquisitors who were even now asking questions in the valley towns with the patient, terrible courtesy of people who had all the time in the world and intended to spend it on us.

Odile listened the way stone listens. When I finished, she was quiet long enough that I heard the boy outside shift his weight, and the wind worrying at the old river-god's empty niche, and my own heart conducting its usual business at an unscheduled tempo.

"Say the mark again," she said. "The wrist. Left or right?"

"Left."

"Show me."

I will admit I hesitated. Eleven years of trade buys you a kind of trust, but it is trust the way a bridge is trust — you only learn its true opinion of you when you put weight on it. I pushed back my cuff.

The sigil had grown since the chapel. It was no longer a smudge; it was a statement. Silver, branching like frost on glass, like a river delta seen from a height, like — and this was the comparison my grandmother had used, in the one story she ever told me twice — like a root system, reaching down into the dark for water no one else could taste.

Odile looked at it for a long moment. Then she did something I had never seen her do in eleven years.

She stood up.

Not quickly. Deliberately, the way you stand for a funeral or a magistrate, and she came around the table, and she took my wrist in both her hands — her fingers dry and strong and cool — and she turned my arm toward the window light, and she read it.

That is the only word. *Read* it. Her thumb traced one branch of the silver and then another, and her lips moved slightly, and I understood that she was not looking at the mark the way I looked at it, the way Wren would look at it, the way the inquisitors would look at it — as a thing to be found, proven, punished. She was looking at it the way a priest looks at scripture. Or the way I look at a plate set before a queen.

"You've survived poison," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Professionally. For sixteen years."

"How many?"

I blinked. "How many what?"

"Distinct toxins. Swallowed, not merely tasted. Tasting is nothing. Swallowing is the arithmetic that matters."

I thought about it. I am, as it happens, very good at this particular kind of arithmetic; I had kept the ledger in my head for years, the way other men keep debts. "Copperleaf, twice. Widow's milk, three times. Graverot, once — that was the bad winter. Salt-of-the-vein, four times, though twice it was diluted. Nightshade in its three usual costumes. A thing in a fish course in the Year of the Double Harvest that the palace physicians never did identify, and I stopped asking after the third physician died of something unrelated." I stopped. "Forty-one," I said. "Forty-one distinct poisons, swallowed whole, survived."

Odile let go of my wrist and sat back down, and for the first time in our long acquaintance, she poured me a drink without being paid for it. It was plum brandy, harsh enough to strip paint, which in the mountains counts as hospitality.

"Forty-one," she repeated. "And you never wondered why you lived?"

"I wondered. I concluded I was lucky, and then I concluded that luck was a poor thing to build a career on, so I concluded nothing and kept a vomit kit in my boot."

That startled a sound out of her that in a lesser woman would have been a laugh. "And the mark?"

"Appeared after the graverot. Grew after everything since. My grandmother—" I stopped, because my grandmother had been dead thirty years and I had never once spoken of her to Odile, and yet the words came up like water finding a crack. "My grandmother told me once that our family had a debt to the mountains. I thought she meant money."

Odile drank. She looked at me over the rim of the cup with an expression I had never seen on her face, and it took me a moment to name it, because it was so entirely out of its usual costume.

It was grief. Old, folded-away, kept-in-a-drawer grief.

"Quintana," she said, "do you know what the sigil is?"

"The priests say it's a weapon. The heralds say it's a plague. The inquisitors say it's treason wearing skin."

"The priests, the heralds, and the inquisitors," said Odile, "are all employees of the same kitchen." She set down the cup. "It's a ledger."

I waited. She did not elaborate, which from Odile was itself an elaboration; she wanted to see whether I could do the sum myself.

"A record," I said slowly. The thought arrived the way poison knowledge arrives — not learned but remembered, something already in the blood. "The mark on my wrist — it's not a scar. It's an entry. Forty-one poisons, and the sigil kept the account."

"Kept more than the account." Odile's voice had gone quiet, the voice she used for prices that could get people killed. "You've been tasting for sixteen years, Quintana. Tell me — when you taste widow's milk in a stew, do you detect it, or do you *recognize* it?"

I opened my mouth to say *detect* and found the word wouldn't come. Because it wasn't detection. Detection is a stranger at the door. What I had, what I had always had, was recognition — the flavor arriving already known, already catalogued, as though some part of me had met it before I did. As though some part of me had been keeping the file.

"The sigil remembers," Odile said. "That is all it is, and that is everything. Every bearer who carried it carried what had been given to them — every poison, every wound, every grief the body survived. It's a ledger of endurance, written in flesh, passed down. The mountain folk kept it alive for six hundred years because a people who remember what was done to them are harder to do it to twice."

"And the crown—"

"The crown burned the memory out of the lowlands three generations ago and called it peace." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "They didn't purge the sigil because it's dangerous, Quintana. They purged it because it's *evidence*."

I sat with that. Outside, the wind came down off the high passes and moved through the old shrine, and for a moment I would have sworn the building remembered the river — a smell of cold water and wet stone that had no business being there, in a room that hadn't touched a river in a century.

"The child," I said. "Ilsa. Her mark isn't like mine. Mine is silver. Hers—"

"Was burning?"

"Was. Is, when it flares."

Odile was quiet again. When she spoke, she spoke carefully, the way you walk on ice that you have reasons to believe will hold. "A burning sigil is a new entry. A first line in a fresh ledger. The old bearers' marks filled up and went silver with what they carried." She looked at me. "A child born with a burning sigil, in this generation, after the purge — that isn't a bearer, Quintana. That's a beginning. And beginnings are the one thing both the crown and every frightened man in the lowlands will burn a village to stop."

* * *

We came to terms over the brandy, in the manner of our kind: no oaths, no sentiment, only numbers.

Passage for two through the high passes, by roads the crown's maps showed as goat tracks or showed as nothing at all. Provisioning from Vane stores. Shelter at way-stations whose existence I would continue not to know about, in keeping with our long and fruitful tradition of mutual ignorance. In exchange: my palace secrets.

Not the trivial ones — anyone with a bribe and a laundry maid could get the trivial ones. I gave her the load-bearing walls of the queen's household. Which physician watered down the poppy syrup and pocketed the difference, and therefore which doses were trustworthy and which were theater. Which of the queen's tasters had been bought by the Duke of Carris and by how much. The rotation schedule of the gate watch, the true one, not the one posted. The fact — worth more than all the rest together — that the crown's granary ledgers were falsified by a fifth, and that the coming spring would find the capital's bread more expensive than its silence.

Odile wrote none of it down. She listened, and she nodded, and once she closed her eyes for the length of a slow breath, the way a woman does when a debt she'd feared unpayable comes in at par.

"You understand," she said at the end, "what you've done. You've just made yourself worthless to the crown and priceless to everyone it's at war with. There is no road back from that arithmetic."

"I did that arithmetic a month ago, in a grain cart, holding a sleeping child who'd watched her village burn." I turned my cup. "The numbers came out the same way."

"Sentiment," said Odile, with the contempt of a professional.

"Arithmetic," I said. "A child is a debt every grown person owes forward. I'm simply paying mine."

She studied me for a long moment. "Your grandmother," she said, "was Mara of the Hollow Chapel. Did she ever tell you that?"

"No," I said. "She told me almost nothing. That was rather her whole method."

"It was everyone's method, for a while. It kept more of us alive than the swords did." Odile stood, and this time it was only Odile standing, a fence closing a ledger. "The girl stays here tonight. The boy will watch her. Tomorrow at dark, a cart goes up the Vane road with wool, and the wool will be heavier than it looks. You'll drive it."

"And your price?" I asked. "Beyond the secrets. I know there's a beyond. There always is with you."

Odile looked at my wrist, still bare where I'd pushed the cuff back, the silver branches catching the lamplight.

"The price is this," she said. "One day — not today, maybe not this year — someone will ask you what the sigil remembers. Not what it *is*. What it *remembers*. And when that day comes, you'll answer them honestly, whatever it costs you. That's the price. Memory for passage. It's the oldest trade in these mountains, and I'm merely the current shopkeeper."

"That's not a price," I said. "That's just a promise."

"Everything I've ever sold," said Odile Vane, "was just a promise. It's the delivery that costs."

* * *

Ilsa was where I'd left her, in the treeline above the shrine, sitting with her back against a pine as though she had been posted there since the founding of the world. The apple was gone. The blanket was still around her shoulders. She watched me come up the path with those too-old eyes, and I saw her cataloguing my face for the verdict before I reached her, the way children in burned villages learn to read adults the way I read plates.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"Did she say yes?"

I sat down beside her in the pine needles. Below us, the counting house's single window glowed, and somewhere behind it Odile was no doubt entering tonight's transactions in a ledger I would never see.

"She said the roads up here remember things," I said. "And that we'd better remember faster."

Ilsa considered this. Eight years old, and she considered it — turned it over, weighed it, filed it. Then she leaned her small burning-wristed weight against my arm, briefly, the way a bird touches a branch, and said, "I'm tired."

"I know," I said.

"I mean in my *bones*."

"I know," I said again, and did not tell her that her bones were keeping a ledger older than either of us, and that the first entry had already been written in fire, and that the crown was coming to collect the account.

Far below, on the valley road, I saw it then — a line of lanterns moving through the dark in perfect, patient order, the way inquisitors travel. Too far to be certain. Too orderly to be anything else.

"Get up," I said quietly. "Time to learn the road."

Ilsa looked where I was looking, and did not ask questions, and got up.

Which was, I was beginning to understand, the whole curriculum of the mountains: the roads remember, the ledgers are kept, and the children learn too fast.

We went up into the dark, three days ahead of the lanterns, if we were lucky.

I had stopped believing in luck somewhere around the graverot. But I had a promise to Odile, a cart full of heavy wool, and forty-one poisons' worth of memory in my blood.

For the first time in my careful, invisible life, it felt almost like enough.
