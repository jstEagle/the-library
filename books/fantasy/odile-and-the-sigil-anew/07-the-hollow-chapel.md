## 7. The Hollow Chapel

The chapel had no roof, which was the first thing to recommend it. No roof meant no smoke lingering, and no smoke meant no lanterns on the ridge lines, and no lanterns meant we would see the dawn arrive before anyone came to arrest us for witnessing it.

It stood in a fold of the mountain like a cupped hand that had forgotten what it was holding. The villagers below — the ones who hadn't burned — called it the hollow chapel, which struck me as a name arrived at by committee. The walls were grey stone furred with lichen, and the windows had gone to holes long ago, and inside there was nothing left of holiness except a floor of flat slates worn smooth by knees that were two hundred years dead.

Ilsa slept against the wall where an altar might have been, if altars could be stolen. Hers had been. There was a scar in the stone where someone had pried something out with a chisel and a clear conscience.

I sat up through my watch and counted poisons. It's what I do instead of praying. Graverot, thirty-seven. Widow's milk, thirty-eight. The thornapple tincture I'd swallowed at the autumn feast while smiling at a duke who thought he was flirting with me, thirty-nine. Forty and forty-one had come since the proclamation, since Odile's hold: two doses of something in the well water at the waystation that I'd tasted a half-second before Ilsa drank, and knocked the cup from her hands hard enough to bruise her wrist. She'd cried. I'd apologized. Neither of us had said what we were both thinking, which was that the poison had been meant for sigil-bearers, and only one of us was supposed to be able to taste it coming.

My body had known anyway. That was the part I didn't say.

Forty-one. My grandmother used to count her rosary; I count my toxins. Same posture, same muttering, roughly the same odds of being answered.

* * *

Ilsa woke at grey light the way children wake — all at once, like a door kicked open. "Are they here?"

"No."

"Are you sure?"

"I'd be running if they were. Running is louder than this."

She considered the logic and found it sound, which put her ahead of most of the queen's council. She ate the last of the smoked goat Odile had pressed on us, chewing with the grim thoroughness of a child who has learned that food is not a promise but a loan. Then she looked around the chapel with the particular hunger of the displaced — cataloguing walls, exits, corners big enough to sleep in. Eight years old, and she already had the eyes of a smuggler.

"Was God in here?" she asked.

"Different god, maybe. Or none. The crown pulled whatever was in here out before my grandmother was born."

"What was it?"

"Nobody wrote it down," I said, and heard the lie in my own mouth even as I said it, because of course somebody had written it down, or remembered it, or carved it into a stone that some chisel-happy clerk had carted off to a capital archive where it lay in a drawer labeled HERESY, MISCELLANEOUS.

"That's stupid," Ilsa said. "If it mattered enough to take, it mattered enough to remember."

Out of the mouths of fugitives. I told her to check the snares, mostly because I needed her out of earshot of what happened next.

* * *

It happened at midmorning, while I was hauling water from the spring. I came back into the chapel and Ilsa was standing in the middle of the worn floor with her sleeve pushed up, and her arm was burning.

Not burning-burning. Not fire. Light — a silver tracery under the skin of her forearm, the sigil uncoiling like something written in water, in ink, in veins. She wasn't screaming. That frightened me more than screaming would have. She was standing very still with her face tipped back toward the roofless sky, and her lips were moving, and she was saying words I did not know in an accent older than the kingdom.

"Ilsa." I dropped the waterskin. "Ilsa, look at me."

Her eyes opened. They weren't entirely hers. There was silver in them, a shimmer like moonlight on a ledger page, and when she spoke again it was in the trade-tongue, but not with her voice's usual wobble. This was level. Old.

"You carry the count," she said. "Forty-one. You keep the count in your blood."

Every hair on my body stood up and took notes. "What are you?"

"I am the record," she said — no. *It* said, through her, the way wind speaks through a crack in a door. "We were never swords. Do you hear me, taster? We were never weapons. That is the first lie, and the oldest."

The light flared, and the chapel went away.

* * *

I don't know how to describe what I saw without making it small. I'll try anyway, because trying is the whole trade of memory.

There was a war — I knew there'd been a war; everyone knows there was a war, three hundred years ago, the Unification, the founding, the blessed peace purchased at a price every schoolbook names in gold ink. What the schoolbooks do not tell you is what the losing side was doing while the winning side sharpened steel.

They were writing.

I saw bearers — men and women with the silver mark climbing their arms like frost — standing in burning fields and *recording*. Not fighting. Recording. A woman knelt beside a dying soldier and held his hand, and everything he was — his mother's kitchen, the smell of his village bread, the name of his dog, the joke his brother always ruined — poured into her sigil and stayed there, silver-bright, held. Another bearer walked through a plague town touching doorframes, taking in the names of the dead so the dead wouldn't go down nameless. They were living ledgers. Living archives. When a bearer died, the memories passed — I felt this, felt it like a current, hand to hand, generation to generation, three hundred years of harvests and weddings and grievances and songs stored in human flesh and passed along like a flame from candle to candle.

That was the gift. That was the whole of it. Not a weapon. A *memory*.

And then I saw the other side of the vision, and here is where awe curdled into something hotter.

I saw the first queen's clerks. I saw the edicts drawn up in a beautiful hand: the gift declared sorcery, the bearers declared traitors, the archives declared arsenals. I saw the logic of it, and the logic was immaculate, which is the most frightening thing about cruelty — it is almost always tidy. A people who cannot forget cannot be lied to. A kingdom built on an official story cannot tolerate a rival archive walking around in mortal bodies. So the story became: they were weapons. Dangerous. Burn them before they burn us.

I saw bearers hunted. I saw them, at the end, choosing — choosing — to burn their own stores rather than let the crown seize them. Whole generations of memory set alight in a mountain fastness rather than surrendered to men who would have edited it. And the last keepers scattered into the Thornfells, carrying fragments, teaching their children in whispers, letting the gift thin with each marriage to the unmarked until it surfaced now and then, in a child like Ilsa, like a coin turning up heads after centuries in the dirt.

The vision let me go.

* * *

I came back to myself on the chapel floor with Ilsa kneeling over me, ordinary-eyed, terrified, shaking my shoulder.

"Mara—" No. Edmund. She said, "Edmund. Edmund, you fell down. Your nose is bleeding. Are you dying?"

"Not yet," I said, and found that my voice worked, more or less. "It showed me things."

"The lady?" she whispered. "The lady in the silver?"

I looked at her. "You've seen her before."

"When it burns. Sometimes." She hugged her knees, suddenly eight years old again and smaller than that. "She holds people's hands when they die so they're not alone. Is that wrong? The soldiers said it was wrong. They said we were weapons and weapons have to be broken."

I wiped my nose on my sleeve and sat up, and I want to be honest about what I felt, because it wasn't the noble grief you'd find in a ballad. It was anger. Clean, cold, clarifying anger — the best kind, the kind that organizes.

Because I understood, lying on those knee-worn stones, exactly what I was looking at. I had spent eleven years as the crown's instrument of certainty. Every dish the queen ate, I tasted first; every death I didn't die made her dinner safer. My whole profession was the royal family's refusal to trust anything it hadn't verified through a disposable body. And here was the mirror of it: the crown's whole history was the same appetite scaled up. Nothing may be true unless the crown has tasted it first. Anything it cannot digest must be poisoned, and the poisoner promoted.

Three hundred years ago they hadn't destroyed a weapon. They had burned the *library*. They had burned the library and written a book about how dangerous libraries are, and every generation since had nodded along over its schoolbooks, and I — I who prided myself on tasting truth before swallowing it — had swallowed that one whole, years ago, without ever noticing the flavor.

"They lied," I said aloud.

Ilsa blinked at me. "About the lady?"

"About all of it." I got up. My legs held. "Your gift isn't a weapon, Ilsa. It never was. It's a memory. You're not a sword the crown has to break. You're a witness it has to silence, because witnesses are the one thing a lie can't survive."

She absorbed this with the terrifying literalism of children. "So why did they burn my village?"

"Because they believe their own lie," I said. "That's the trick of it. Nobody burns a village over something they know is false. You need true believers for that kind of work."

I thought of Wren Halloway as I said it — her face, the last time I'd seen it, eleven years old in the tasters' quarters, sharing stolen honeybread with me and swearing we'd both grow up honest. I wondered what the crown had fed her since. I wondered whether she knew she'd been eating poison all these years, and whether she'd ever developed the tongue to taste it.

Probably not. Most people never do. That's the entire business model.

* * *

We spent the rest of the day doing the mundane work of staying alive, because visions notwithstanding, the body still wants lunch. Ilsa checked the snares — empty; we'd eat wool-trader's biscuit again, hardtack that could stop a crossbow bolt — and I walked the chapel's perimeter and thought about contraband.

Here is what I concluded, walking the perimeter of a robbed church: history itself had become contraband. Not documents — documents the crown could burn. Not relics — relics it could melt. But a living memory, carried in blood, surfacing in children like springs coming up through rock no matter how much weight you put on the ground. You cannot confiscate a memory without killing the carrier. So the crown killed carriers, and called the killing safety, and three hundred years later it was still at it, still burning villages over a lie so old nobody alive remembered inventing it.

Except somebody did remember. The mountain folk did. Odile Vane had looked at Ilsa's scar and named its price without flinching, which meant somewhere beneath Thornfell there were people who had kept the ledger going, whisper by whisper, wedding by wedding, for three hundred years. The crown had won the war and lost the argument, and it knew it, and that knowledge was the engine of the whole purge.

I stood in the roofless chapel with the afternoon light coming through window-holes like searchlights, and I understood something else, too — something about myself, unwelcome as most understanding is.

The forty-one poisons in my blood weren't just immunity. The vision had shown me that. Every toxin I'd survived had been *recorded* — held in me, silver-threaded, the way the old bearers had held the dying soldier's last morning. I was an archive too. An accidental one, compiled by the crown itself, one deliberate poisoning at a time. Eleven years of the queen's enemies' chemistry, all of it filed away in my flesh, and the crown had no idea it had spent a decade building the very thing it was hunting.

The irony was rich enough to serve at a state banquet.

* * *

At dusk, Ilsa came and sat next to me on the chapel steps — steps leading nowhere, the path below long collapsed — and asked the question children ask when adults think they haven't noticed anything.

"Edmund. Am I going to end up like the lady? Holding people's hands when they die?"

I could have lied. I'm good at lying; it's most of my job description. Instead I said, "Maybe. If you choose to. That's the difference between you and the soldiers' version. The gift doesn't make you anything. It remembers. What it *means* is up to whoever carries it."

"Then what does yours mean?" she asked. "You carry the count. The lady said so. Forty-one."

I looked out at the darkening ridgelines, at the valleys where inquisitors were lighting evening fires under other roofless chapels, other fleeing children, and I gave the only honest answer I had.

"It means nothing yet," I said. "Ask me again when I decide what to do with it."

She nodded, satisfied, and leaned against my shoulder, and was asleep within minutes, the way the young can sleep anywhere, even in the ruins of erased things.

I stayed awake. Somewhere past midnight, on the eastern ridge, I saw it: a line of lanterns, moving with the unhurried discipline of people being paid by the day. Too far to reach us tonight. Close enough by tomorrow.

I woke Ilsa, and we doused the fire, and slipped out the back of the hollow chapel into the dark — leaving behind, on the worn floor where she'd stood, a single scorch-mark in the shape of a sigil, silver-edged, already fading.

But not gone. And I knew, with the certainty of a man who has spent his life reading traces, that the inquisitors would read it too.

And I knew whose boots would be first through that door by tomorrow's dusk. I'd have known that handwriting anywhere — the careful, orderly, grieving hand of Wren Halloway, who had once sworn over stolen honeybread that she'd grow up honest, and who was about to find out what honesty cost, three hundred years late.
