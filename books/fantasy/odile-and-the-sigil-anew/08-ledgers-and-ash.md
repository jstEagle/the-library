## 8. Ledgers and Ash

The village was called Bracken Hollow, which struck me as a cruel joke of naming, because by the time I finished with it, there would be neither bracken nor hollow — only ash where both had been.

I stood at the edge of the square with my ledger open on my forearm, the way my father used to hold his prayer book, and watched my soldiers do what soldiers do when they've been told a thing is necessary: they did it quickly, and then they pretended it had been quick. The granary went first. Then the longhouse with its carved lintel, some grandmother's great-grandmother's work, gone in eleven minutes of good dry pine. Smoke has a way of making even the most reluctant man feel efficient.

"Names," I said to Sergeant Corvel, who was warming his hands over a burning fence post as though it were a hearth and this were a pleasant evening. "Every registered bearer accounted for?"

"Eleven of twelve." He didn't look at me when he said it. That was how I knew the twelfth was going to be a problem.

"And the twelfth?"

"A woman. Maren Ostwick. Registered four years ago, sigil on the left collarbone, per her own declaration." He finally turned, and his face had the particular blankness men develop around me, the one that says *you are the inquisitor, this is your arithmetic, not mine.* "Her neighbors say she went into the hills three days past."

"Went," I repeated. "Or was sent."

Corvel shrugged, which from him was practically testimony.

Here is what no one tells you about being an inquisitor: it is mostly paperwork. The fires make the ballads, but the ledgers make the law. Every name I recorded was a name that had been *processed*, and processed meant safe — safe from the mob, safe from the panic that had burned three villages before the queen's edict ever reached them, safe from the kind of justice that doesn't check its spelling. That was the argument I made to myself every night, and I made it well, because I have always been an excellent arguer, especially against an opponent who is tired and alone and shares my bedroll, so to speak. Order prevents worse bloodshed. A name in my book was a life with a shape around it. Unregistered sigil-bearers died badly and anonymously; registered ones, at worst, died documented.

At worst. I turned the page and did not look too closely at that phrase, the way you don't look too closely at a stair in the dark. You just keep walking and trust the house.

* * *

They brought the survivor to me at dusk, when the smoke had settled into the valley like a guest who intended to stay the winter. An old man, half-carried between two soldiers, with a burned sleeve and the unbothered eyes of someone who had already decided what he was willing to lose.

"Name?" I asked.

"Tomas Rell." He sat because his legs made the decision for him, not because I offered. "You'll want to know if I bear the sigil. I don't. Never did. My wife did. She's in your book — third page, Maren's mother, registered the same day. Died last spring, natural causes, which I understand is becoming rare."

I wrote nothing down yet. Interrogation is a craft, and the first rule of the craft is that silence does more work than questions. But Tomas Rell was not a man who feared silence. He looked at my ledger with something worse than hatred — he looked at it with recognition, the way a farmer looks at weather he can't stop.

"You burned my village today," he said conversationally. "The registered folk were already gone — taken east in the wagons, all proper, all signed for. So what exactly was the fire for?"

"The structures were condemned under the Edict of—"

"I know the edict. I helped carry the herald's chair up from the ford." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You're young to be doing this work. Younger than my daughter. Do you sleep?"

"Sergeant," I said, "water for the prisoner."

Corvel brought it, and Tomas drank like a man who had earned the right to be unimpressed by everything else in the room. When he set the cup down, he studied me the way one studies a mark on a wall — trying to decide if it's damp or something older.

"You'll want Maren Ostwick," he said. "That's why you're here instead of at your supper. Ask your real question."

"Where did she go?"

"The hills. As I said." He folded his hands. "But that's not the question underneath, is it? The question underneath is: who taught her to run, who drew her a map, who told her there were roads your patrols don't watch. And you won't get that from me, so let me save us both the evening."

I should have pressed him. I want that noted somewhere, by someone, in whatever ledger exists for inquisitors: I should have pressed him, and instead I asked the question that mattered more, because I am a professional and professionals are ruined by curiosity.

"You said your wife registered the same day as Maren. Who brought the registry to Bracken Hollow? Who explained the sigil-gift to people who'd hidden it for generations?"

Tomas's eyebrows rose a fraction. "The crown's own taster, years back. Before your time, girl. Came through collecting food customs — poison lore, they called it, though we called it sense. Knew more about our old ways than we did, near enough. Grandchild of one of ours, gone south to serve. The family was proud and frightened about it in equal measure." He squinted at me. "Quintana, the name was. Edmund Quintana. Polite creature. Ate a dish of bitterroot tea at my table once to prove it was clean, and thanked me for the recipe after."

The ink on my pen had gone still. I made myself move it.

"Edmund Quintana," I said. "The taster."

"That's the one. Sweet tooth. Terrible at cards." Tomas leaned forward, and there was nothing conversational in him now. "Why do you ask, Inquisitor Halloway? Is the crown's own tongue on your list?"

"No," I said. "The taster's office is tested yearly. It's the safest body in the kingdom."

"Mm," said Tomas Rell, in the tone of a man filing something away for later, possibly for a court that hasn't been built yet.

* * *

I dismissed him at full dark — released, technically, though a man whose village is ash and whose daughter is in the hills is released into nothing, and we both understood the mercy was for my benefit, not his. Then I sat with my ledger and my lamp and did the thing I do every night, which is tell myself the story that lets me sleep.

*Order prevents worse bloodshed.* Eleven names processed today, eleven lives given legal shape before the fire took their houses. Without the registry, the mob comes first, and mobs don't keep records. My brother's name is in this same book — registered in the first season, when I still believed the registration was protection — and whatever became of him afterward, at least it happened inside the law, at least there is a line in ink that says he existed and was accounted for. That is not nothing. I have built a whole life on its not being nothing.

But tonight the story kept snagging, the way cloth snags on a nail you can't see.

Because here is the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing: Maren Ostwick ran three days ago. Someone told her to run. Someone showed her roads. And the survivor who knew those roads best — who knew the *old* roads, the smuggler ways through Thornfell that appear in no crown survey — had just handed me a name with a smile on his face, the way a fisherman hands you a hook wrapped in bait.

Edmund Quintana. My Edmund. The boy from the taster's quarters who taught me which mushrooms lie, who split stolen honeycakes with me behind the scullery, who cried at my brother's registration and then laughed it off and said *the law knows what it's doing, Wren, the law always knows.* Who swallowed poison for a living and never once flinched, never once sickened, never once — I realized now, sitting very still while the lamp guttered — never once showed the least effect of it, year after year, dose after dose, in a profession that kills a man every decade or so.

I had admired that. I had thought: *there is a person the poisons cannot touch.*

And the sigil-gift, as every keeper of the old lore knew and every inquisitor's manual confirmed, begins in exactly one way: a body that survives what should kill it, until survival becomes something else.

I opened my ledger to a fresh page. I did not write Edmund's name. I wrote *Bracken Hollow — follow-up inquiry: origin of flight routes, Thornfell passes,* which is the sort of entry that looks like diligence and functions like a door left open.

Then I closed the book and told myself the story again, louder this time: order, bloodshed, prevention, law. Somewhere in the hills a woman named Maren was running along roads drawn by hands I couldn't see, and somewhere south of here a taster was sleeping soundly, and I lay awake doing sums I refused to finish.

By morning I had requested transfer of my commission to the Thornfell circuit.

The paper gave no reason. Ledgers rarely do.
