## 9. Roads That Remember

The first thing I taught Ilsa was how to walk like a thief.

Not run — walk. Running is what the body wants to do when it's frightened, and the body is a terrible strategist. Running kicks up scree, snaps branches, announces you to every ear within a mile of your panic. Walking says: *I belong here.* Walking lies on your behalf.

"Left foot first," I told her, "then roll onto the outside edge, then down through the ball. Like you're apologizing to the ground for disturbing it."

She looked at me the way children look at adults who have said something obviously insane — which is to say, with total attention and zero belief. Then she tried it, and her boots went *crunch* anyway, and she frowned at me as though I'd sold her a defective product.

"You're doing it wrong," she announced.

"I've been doing it wrong for twenty years," I said. "That's how I know it's wrong."

We were two days out of Odile's hold by then, up past the tree line where Thornfell stops pretending to be gentle and starts being honest about itself. The official roads ran along the valley floors, wide enough for carts and patrols and the particular optimism of people who believe the mountains can be administered. My roads went everywhere else. They were smuggler roads, goat paths, deer trails, one stretch that was technically a riverbed for three weeks of the year and a footpath for the rest — and they were mine in the way that anything becomes yours: by repetition, by error, by nearly dying on them enough times that they leave an impression.

I had walked them for years carrying sealed crates and unasked questions, memorizing them the way other men memorize scripture. It hadn't occurred to me until this week that I'd been building an escape route my whole life without ever once planning to escape. There's a word for that kind of foresight, probably, but I don't know it, and honestly I suspect the word is *cowardice*, and I've decided not to look it up.

Ilsa walked behind me because I told her to, and she stayed there because she wanted to. Eight years old, hair cut raggedly short — someone had done it in a hurry, maybe herself, maybe a neighbor with more kindness than skill — and a scar on her forearm that glowed faintly in the dark like a banked coal. She didn't talk much. When she did talk, it was in the flat, practical voice of a child who has already learned that feelings are a luxury item.

"The soldiers burned our house," she told me on the second morning, apropos of nothing, while we ate hard cheese and watched frost burn off the grass. "Then they burned the church. Then they burned the man who wouldn't tell them where my mother went."

I said nothing useful. There was nothing useful to say. I gave her more cheese instead, which is the food-taster's answer to everything: if you cannot fix the world, at least make sure no one starves on your watch.

She took it and said, "My mother went toward the coast. She told me to go into the grain cart." A pause. "It was a good hiding place."

"It was," I agreed. "Terrible luck that I found you."

"Luck," she repeated, tasting the word like I taste wine, checking it for poison. She decided it was safe, mostly.

* * *

The patrol found us on the third day, or rather we found the patrol, which is the correct order of operations and the entire reason my roads exist.

There were six of them, crown livery, moving up the switchbacks below us with the unhurried confidence of men who are paid whether or not they catch anyone. I heard them before I saw them — a good taster learns to listen the way he tastes, for the thing underneath the thing — and I pulled Ilsa off the trail into a fold of rock that I had found eleven years ago while fleeing a customs officer named Brakke who is almost certainly dead now of drink, and whose memory I therefore honor regularly.

We crouched in the shadow and I put my hand over Ilsa's mouth, gently, and whispered, "If I take my hand away, it means be quiet. If I press harder, it means don't breathe."

"That's the same instruction twice," she whispered back.

"No. One is a request. One is a survival strategy."

Below us, the patrol stopped. Their sergeant — a broad man with the posture of someone who has been promoted past his competence — stood in the middle of the path and consulted a map, which struck me as optimistic, since the map was drawn by the Office of Roads and would show a lovely straight route directly through terrain that had never once permitted anything to travel in a straight line. The mountains do not read the Office of Roads. This is, I have come to believe, their finest quality.

One of the soldiers, younger than the rest, turned his face upward toward our rocks. Just a glance. Just a soldier's idle scan of high ground, which every manual tells him to do and no soldier actually does unless bored.

Ilsa's sigil flared.

I felt it before I saw it — a warmth against my palm where my hand covered her mouth, a heat rising through her skin like fever, like the moment just before a poison announces itself in my own blood. The mark on her arm lit orange through her sleeve, and the boy below us opened his mouth to say something—

—and then a ptarmigan exploded out of the scree ten yards to his left, all thunder and feathers and outrage, and he yelped, and the sergeant swore at him, and the moment passed the way moments do: stupidly, narrowly, thanks to a bird who will never know what it prevented.

The patrol moved on. We stayed in the rocks another hour, because I am paid to be patient and Ilsa was learning to be.

Afterward she held up her arm and looked at the fading glow and asked me, "Does yours do that?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"When it wants to. That's the problem with gifts. Nobody asks you first."

She considered this. "Yours didn't light up just now."

"No. Mine's quieter. Older poisons teach you to keep things quiet." I hesitated, then told her the truth, because she was going to see it eventually anyway and better from me than from surprise: "Mine doesn't flash. It hums. It's been humming for years, under the skin, and I spent most of those years telling myself it was nothing, because 'nothing' was safer than the alternative."

"What was the alternative?"

"That I was what the queen says I am."

Ilsa looked at me with the merciless clarity of children. "Are you?"

And here is the thing I could not explain to her, standing in the cold with a fugitive's warmth still fading against my hand: I didn't know. I knew what my body was. I did not yet know what that made me. Those are different questions, and only one of them gets answered by looking at your wrist.

"We should move," I said instead. "The bird won't cover us twice."

* * *

That night we sheltered in a shepherd's hut that wasn't on any map, including mine, because I'd found it drunk once, years ago, and had the good sense to come back sober and memorize its location. It had a roof, mostly, a hearth, and a smell of ancient sheep that no amount of burning brush would ever fully evict. Ilsa pronounced it "the worst house I've ever seen," which I took as a compliment, since she'd clearly decided it was ours.

I built a small fire — small, screened, the kind that reads as starlight from any distance — and while it caught, I started teaching her things. Not because I'd decided to teach her. Because she kept asking.

"How do you know water's safe?"

"If it comes off snowmelt above the sheep, it's safe. If it runs through a village, boil it. If it's standing still, assume something died in it, because something did."

"How do you know which plants?"

"You don't. You learn six plants and eat only those, and you let the other four hundred go on being beautiful and mysterious." I showed her the six. Sorrel, wild garlic, two berries whose names are regional arguments I refuse to referee, a root that tastes like punishment but keeps you alive, and pine bark, which is less a food than a formal protest against hunger.

"Why do you know all this?" she asked. "You're a king's man. You live in a castle."

"A palace. And I live in a corridor next to the kitchens, which is not the same as living in the palace. The palace belongs to people who've never once wondered what's safe to eat." I fed the fire a stick. "I grew up between worlds. My grandmother kept a tavern at the mountain's foot, and my mother married a clerk and moved us inside the walls. I've spent my whole life translating. Poison for the queen. Routes for Odile. Silence for everyone."

"My grandmother kept goats," Ilsa said.

"Goats are honest," I said. "Worse company, better character."

Something happened to her face then — a crack, quick as the ptarmigan, there and gone. The first almost-smile I'd seen on her. And I understood, sitting in a stolen hut smelling of dead sheep, that I had crossed some line without noticing it. You become a guardian the way you become poisoned: gradually, then all at once. No single dose does it. It's the accumulation. Every meal shared, every lesson given, every night you sleep between the child and the door.

I had not decided anything. Deciding is for people with time. I had simply woken up responsible, the way you wake up sick — the condition preceded your consent.

* * *

On the fifth day we came to the pass road, and Ilsa saw the valley beyond it, and the sight undid her a little.

Green. Actual green, running down and away, fields stitched in stone walls, smoke rising from chimneys that were, today, ordinary chimneys and not funeral pyres. She stood at the lip of the pass with the wind pushing her hair around and stared like a person reading a letter from someone believed dead.

"People live down there," she said.

"People live everywhere. That's the trouble."

"Will they burn too?"

I knelt down so we were eye to eye, which is a thing I'd found myself doing more often, and which my knees had opinions about. "Here's what I know," I said. "The crown burns villages that hide sigil-bearers. So the question isn't whether people down there are good. It's whether they're brave, and whether anyone gives them the chance to be both." I pointed down-valley, to a fold in the hills where no smoke rose. "Odile's folk are that way. They remember things. Old things. Things about that mark on your arm that the queen would very much like everyone to forget."

"Is that why she wants us? To forget us?"

"Queens don't want to forget, child. Forgetting is easy. Queens want everyone else to forget, while they keep the ledger." I stood, knees cracking like green wood. "Come on. We've got light left, and the pass closes early this time of year."

But we didn't take the pass road. I took her down the smuggler's way instead — a crack in the mountain's flank that dropped through three hundred feet of switchback so narrow that in places we walked sideways, Ilsa in front now, because the descent is the part where you want to fall forward and I preferred to catch her than to describe catching her.

Halfway down, her boot dislodged a stone the size of a skull. It went bouncing and booming into the dark below, and we both froze, listening, while the mountain filed its complaint.

Nothing answered. No voices. No dogs. No horns.

"They're not following us," Ilsa said.

"Not tonight."

"But they're still—" She stopped. Children stop mid-sentence when the sentence is too big. "They're still looking. Even if we get down there. Even if we're fast."

"Yes."

"Forever?"

I thought about lying to her. I want that noted somewhere, by someone, in whatever ledger records such things: I thought about it, and I chose not to, because she'd already been lied to by everyone with power over her life, and I refused to join the list.

"For a while," I said. "Longer than we'd like. But 'forever' is a word grown-ups use when they're tired. Nothing is forever. Not the queen, not the purge, not even the mountains — though the mountains come closest, and they're on our side, or at least indifferent in a way that helps."

She nodded slowly, filing that away, and we finished the descent in silence, and at the bottom, where the smuggler's crack opens into a stream meadow, she did something I didn't expect. She reached up and took my hand. Not because she needed steadying. Because she'd decided to.

Her fingers were warm. Warmer than they should have been. The sigil, banking its coal again beneath the skin.

* * *

We made Odile's outer holdings by dusk of the sixth day — a cluster of stone barns and a longhouse where the mountain folk took one look at Ilsa and, without discussion, set another bowl at the table. Nobody asked questions in front of her. Questions, I was learning, were also contraband; the mountain folk had gotten very good at smuggling those too.

It was there, eating barley stew with a child asleep against my shoulder, that a boy arrived from the low country, mud to his knees, with a folded paper he would not hand to anyone but me.

I read it by rushlight. The seal was familiar. The handwriting was worse.

*Edmund Quintana. Her Majesty requires her taster. Return within the fortnite. — by order of the Undermaster of the Household.*

They wanted me back at the table. Of course they did. Tasters are rare; loyal ones rarer; and I had been gone long enough to be noticed, which for a man whose entire profession is not being noticed is its own kind of alarm bell.

I sat with the paper in my hand for a long time. Ilsa slept against me, breathing slow, her marked arm curled in her sleep the way children curl anything precious.

Somewhere south of us, Wren Halloway was reading the same absence I'd left behind. Somewhere north, the passes were filling with patrols. And the crown, with its terrible patience, had simply sent for its instrument — because instruments come when called, don't they? Instruments always have before.

I looked at the fire, and at the sleeping weight against my side, and at the door of the longhouse, open to the cold and the stars, and I understood that the easy part of the arithmetic was over. Whatever I did next, I would be doing it as two men at once: the taster the queen believed she owned, and whatever it was I'd become on these roads, walking beside a child who held my hand because she'd chosen to.

The paper waited. The fortnight would not lengthen itself.

In the morning, I would have to decide which of my lives to feed — knowing, the way I know poison, that feeding one means starving the other, and that the crown had just invited me to bring the starvation home.
