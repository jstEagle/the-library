## 10. The Queen's Table

I left Ilsa with Odile on a morning so cold the well rope had frozen stiff as a corpse's arm, which is the kind of detail a taster notices because we spend our lives cataloguing the ways things die.

"She stays in the cellar rooms," Odile said, not looking at me while she said it. She was counting coin into a ledger, or pretending to; the ledger had been open to the same page for an hour. "Nobody goes down there but the girl who brings her meals, and the girl owes me more than her wages. If anyone asks, the child is my sister's granddaughter, sick with lung-rot. Nobody asks after lung-rot. It's bad manners."

"And if they ask after her wrist?"

Odile finally looked up. She has the kind of face that makes lying look like a moral failing, which is useful in a fence, since it means everyone assumes she's telling the truth when she isn't. "Then I'll tell them she fell against a forge. Children do." A pause. "You'll come back."

It wasn't a question, but it wasn't entirely confidence either. It was the tone of a woman placing a bet she'd already counted the odds on and found them ugly.

"I have no choice," I said. "The queen's summons doesn't accept refusals. It barely accepts excuses, and it executes delays."

"Then come back quickly," she said, "and don't eat anything you don't have to."

I laughed. It came out wrong, too sharp, like a bone cracking. "That's rather the whole of my profession, Odile."

"Yes," she said, going back to her ledger. "I've often wondered how you sleep."

* * *

The road south to the capital took six days, and I spent all of them rehearsing.

This is what nobody tells you about double lives: the exhausting part isn't the lying. Lying is easy. Lying is just arithmetic with a straight face. The exhausting part is the maintenance — keeping both ledgers balanced in your head at once, remembering which version of yourself each person has been introduced to, making sure the taster Edmund never says anything that only the road-Edmund would know. I had done this before, in small ways, for years: the smuggler routes I kept out of my reports, the extra coin I moved through Thornfell, the little economies of concealment every servant learns young. But there's a difference between hiding a purse and hiding a child, and between hiding a child and hiding the fact that your own body has become the exact thing the crown has sworn to burn out of the world.

By day three I stopped rehearsing answers and started rehearsing stillness instead. Because that was the real danger now, wasn't it? Not what I said. What my body did.

The mark on my wrist had opinions about travel. When I rode hard it warmed. When I passed certain places — old waystones, the foundations of bridges older than the crown, one particular bend of the Greywater where the reeds grow in patterns too regular to be accident — it woke like a dog hearing its name, a slow pulse under the skin, almost pleasant, which was somehow worse. In Odile's longhouse I'd learned to keep a sleeve over it and my mind off it, the same discipline I used to keep my hands steady at the tasting table. But discipline is a muscle, and muscles tire.

Somewhere past the last Thornfell outpost, at a shrine to a road-god whose name the priests had chiseled off two centuries ago, I unwrapped my wrist just to look.

The silver had spread. Not much. A finger's width further up the forearm, branching now, fine as frost on glass, and where the branches met they made — I told myself I was imagining it — letters. Or the ancestors of letters. Shapes that wanted to mean something.

My grandmother had a word for people who let the sigil spread unchecked. I hadn't remembered it until that moment, kneeling in the snow at a nameless shrine, and when it surfaced I laughed again, alone, in the middle of nowhere, like a madman. *Tasters,* she used to say, when she talked about the old bearers. *They taste everything twice.* I'd thought it a joke about caution. It was not a joke. Nothing my grandmother ever said was a joke; I simply hadn't lived long enough to get them.

I wrapped the wrist. I rode on. I practiced being one man.

* * *

The capital smelled the way it always smells in late autumn: woodsmoke and horse dung and river rot, with a thread of roasting chestnuts cutting through it near the Gate of Chains, sold by the same one-legged veteran who'd sold them when I first arrived, eleven years ago, a thin boy with a careful tongue and no family anyone could check on. He didn't recognize me. Nobody recognizes a taster; that's the entire point of us. We are furniture that swallows.

The palace had changed more than the city had. There were guards at the kitchens now — actual soldiers, not the porter with his ring of keys — and a clerk at the scullery door holding a ledger, checking names against names.

"Quintana," I said. "Summoned by warrant. Fortnight-old."

The clerk found me, ran his finger down the entry, and looked up with the particular blankness clerks cultivate, which is the blankness of men who write down everything and understand nothing. "You're late."

"The mountain passes were closed by weather."

"They're always closed by weather." He made a note anyway, which meant he'd record the excuse without recording whether he believed it, and waved me through.

Inside, the kitchens were the same and wrong. Same copper pots, same great spits, same Master Corvel presiding over the pastry bench like a general who'd won all his battles by shouting. But quieter. Two of the under-cooks I'd known were gone, and nobody would say where, and their stations had been filled by new boys who worked with the exaggerated care of the recently frightened.

Corvel saw me across the room and his face did something complicated — relief and reproach fighting to a draw. He came over wiping his hands, which meant he intended to talk, which meant trouble.

"Quintana. Good. Good." He drew me aside, behind the hanging hams, out of the new boys' earshot. "You heard about the purges."

"Everyone's heard about the purges."

"You weren't here." He lowered his voice. "Vessel got taken. And Marta, from the stillroom. They came at night, very polite, very official, asked them to show their arms." His eyes went to my sleeves, briefly, reflexively — the glance of a man checking his own hands are clean — and away. "Marta had a birthmark on her forearm. Just a birthmark. They took her anyway. Said ambiguous marks go to the examiners." He swallowed. "So. Keep your sleeves down, keep your head down, and be grateful the queen wants her taster. Whatever else you might be, Quintana, right now you're too useful to test."

*Too useful to test.* I smiled the smile I use for ambassadors' jokes and said something agreeable, and inside me something cold turned over, because Corvel had given me the shape of my survival in five words, and shapes like that never hold. Useful today. Tested tomorrow. That was the whole history of servants who knew too much.

* * *

The tasting was set for the evening of my second day back, a private supper — the queen, her Lord Chamberlain, two ministers whose factions I could smell the way I smell arsenic, and a visiting envoy from the coastal principalities whose fish course I would need to attend to personally, since the coastal principalities had tried to poison this queen's mother twice and considered it, in their way, a family tradition.

I worked the table the way I always work it. Bread from three ovens, tasted in order of suspicion — the third oven served a household whose steward owed gambling debts, and I noted the debt months ago, because a taster's real work happens far from the table. Wine, white then red, small sips held on the tongue, waiting. The soup, which was venison and barley and smelled of thyme and nothing else, which is itself worth noting; soup should smell of something.

Nothing. All of it nothing. Eleven years of nothing, and I had begun, somewhere in the last year, to miss the nothing less than I trusted it.

The sigil had grown restless since the capital. That was the thing I couldn't say aloud even to myself. On the roads it had been a warmth, a pulse, a dog waking. Here, in the palace, under the candles and the gilt, it felt like thirst. As though years of swallowed poison had taught my blood a hunger, and the hunger remembered the palace the way a man remembers the house he was born in.

The fish course arrived.

I did what I always do with fish from the coast: broke it apart with a silver knife, checked the dark flesh along the spine where the old poisons concentrate, lifted a flake, held it, tasted. Salt. Butter. Dill. A ghost of smoke from the curing.

And then — faster than thought, faster than training, faster than the eleven years of craft that were supposed to make detection slow and methodical and boring — my tongue said *no.*

Not bitter. Not metallic. None of the textbook signatures. Something underneath the dill, underneath the salt, a wrongness in the flesh itself, as though the fish had died angry. My mouth knew before my mind did. My mouth had known before I put the fork in it, I realized — had known from the moment the dish entered the room, a prickle at the back of the jaw, a coldness in the teeth. The sigil hadn't waited for me to taste. It had smelled the poison coming the way Odile's dogs smelled weather.

I spat, delicately, into the tasting napkin, the way one does — a taster's spit must always look routine, bored, administrative — and said, "The fish."

The room went quiet the way rooms do when the taster speaks, all those powerful faces turning toward the furniture.

"Something in the dark flesh," I said. "Not enough to kill a healthy adult in one serving, I think. Enough to do damage over a week of suppers. Enough to make a queen ill in ways her physicians would call a wasting humoral imbalance while the true cause sat smiling at her table." I looked at the envoy. He was looking at the window. Ambassadors are terrible at windows; guilt always looks at windows.

The queen said, "You are certain?"

Eleven years, and Her Majesty had spoken to me perhaps forty times, mostly to ask how I was feeling after incidents. I bowed. "May it please Your Majesty to have the dish fed to a condemned animal and the animal watched. Three days will tell you more than my certainty can."

It pleased her. It also, I saw, unsettled her — the speed of it, the sureness. A taster is supposed to work like a clerk, methodically, and report conclusions, not certainties. Certainties belong to magicians and to liars.

The supper concluded. The envoy departed with remarkable haste for a man on holiday. Guards escorted the kitchen staff responsible for the fish somewhere for questioning, and the sounds that came from that direction later were brief and businesslike, and I drank watered wine in the servants' hall and did not think about them, because thinking about them is a luxury the living can't afford, and I intended, stubbornly, to stay among the living.

* * *

It caught up with me at midnight, in my narrow room above the stillroom, and it nearly killed me.

I had felt it building through the evening — the warmth climbing my arm, the branching silver prickling like a limb gone to sleep, the strange thirst getting worse with every hour since the fish. I told myself it was fatigue. I told myself many things. I am very good at telling myself things; it's most of what composure is.

Then I stood to trim the candle, and the floor tilted, and the wall met my shoulder harder than walls usually meet shoulders, and I slid down it to sit on the cold boards while my heart attempted to leave through my throat.

Here is what was happening, as best I understood it afterward, sitting in the dark with my teeth chattering: the poison had been in the fish in quantities too small for any ordinary detection — I had been right about that, right in the way that mattered, and wrong about the rest. Because it wasn't designed for the queen. Or rather — and this arrived in my head with the clarity of a struck bell — it wasn't *only* designed for the queen. It was a compound I half-recognized from the smuggler routes, something the Thornfell alchemists call *hollowroot*, rare, expensive, and famous for one property: it kills sigil-bearers swiftly and leaves everyone else merely queasy.

Someone had seasoned the royal fish with a poison aimed at me.

Or aimed at whoever at that table carried the sigil. And the crown's own examiners would run their tests on the leftovers, find hollowroot, and draw exactly one conclusion: there is a bearer in the palace, close enough to the queen to taste her food, and the taster who flagged the dish is either the bait or the bearer.

I sat on the floor and shook, and the shaking was only partly the poison working itself out of me — my body burning it off with an efficiency that would have interested any physician and terrified every examiner. The silver on my wrist had spread another inch. I could see it even in the dark, faintly luminous, frost-patterns crawling toward my elbow, and beneath my skin a feeling I can only describe as *recognition*, as though the gift in my blood had met its opposite number in the fish and filed the encounter away, learning, always learning, growing stronger with every attempt to kill me.

They were feeding me, these poisons. Every dose survived made the mark brighter. Whoever had laced that dish — the envoy, a minister, someone who wanted a bearer flushed out of the palace shadows — had handed me the very thing I needed least and feared most: proof. Proof growing up my arm at the speed of ivy, visible, testable, damning.

And I had to be at the tasting table again tomorrow. And the next day. And every day until the queen dismissed me, smiling, sleeves down, hands steady, while under the linen my own skin wrote out my confession letter by letter.

Toward dawn the fever broke and I lay on my cot listening to the palace wake up below — boots in corridors, the clatter of the kitchens, the ordinary music of a house that fed on secrets — and I made myself think, because terror unexamined is just terror, but terror examined becomes arithmetic.

Whoever sent the hollowroot would be watching. Watching for a death, or watching for a survivor who suddenly couldn't hide what he was. Either way, they would expect me to run. Running confirms. Running signs the confession.

So I would not run. I would stand at that table tonight, pale but steady, and thank Her Majesty's cooks for their diligence, and swallow whatever came next, because swallowing was the one thing I had ever been genuinely excellent at.

But I needed help, and there was only one person in the capital who might give it, who knew both halves of me, who had once shared a pallet in the tasters' quarters and sworn we'd look out for each other, and who now wore the grey coat of the Inquisition and hunted children with marks like mine and Ilsa's across three provinces.

Wren Halloway had arrived in the city two days before I did. Everyone in the kitchens whispered it.

There was a knock at my door.

One knock, soft, deliberate — not the porter's impatient double-rap, not the clerks' bureaucratic drumming. One knock, the way she used to knock on our barracks wall as girls, when the dormitory lamps were out and she wanted to talk about running away to the mountains.

I looked at my sleeve, at the faint silver light bleeding through the linen like moonlight through a shutter, and I went to answer it.
