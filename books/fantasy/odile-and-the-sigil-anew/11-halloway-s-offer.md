## 11. Halloway's Offer

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the taster's quarters of the royal palace, and I had forgotten about it for eleven years until I stood in Edmund Quintana's old room in the Vane's Hold guest wing and recognized it again.

It is not an empty silence. It is a held one. The silence of people who have learned that sound carries, that walls have opinions, that the wrong word spoken at the wrong volume can end up in someone's mouth at dinner as a joke and in someone's report by morning. Edmund and I grew up inside that silence like two plants grown in the same cracked pot, roots tangled before either of us knew what roots were for.

I had come to Thornfell to hunt sigil-bearers. I had a warrant, four riders, a ledger with too many names in it, and the queen's seal pressed into wax soft enough to take any shape I asked of it. What I did not have was a reason to be standing in Edmund's rented room at midnight, running my thumb along the mantel and finding no dust, because Edmund Quintana had never in their life tolerated dust.

The room told me things the way rooms always do. A washbasin used but recently. A coat brushed and hung with the collar turned out — worn often, then. And on the table, a map of the Thornfell passes, folded once, carelessly, except that the fold ran precisely along the route no crown surveyor had ever charted, the smuggler roads Edmund used to describe to me when we were nineteen and stupid and certain the world would reward cleverness.

I sat down on the bed that wasn't mine and put my head in my hands and laughed, quietly, because the alternative was something less useful.

I knew. Of course I knew. I had known since the second week, when the grain merchant in Lowmark described a stranger who bought flour for two and paid for three, and I had heard Edmund's name in his mouth and felt my stomach drop through the floorboards of my certainty. I had known more certainly since the shepherd boy who saw a tall figure carrying a child up the goat track above the burned chapel — a child with her hair tucked under a cap, walking with the careful exaggerated steadiness of someone who has been told *don't run* so many times the words have grown into her bones.

I had written none of it down. That was the thing I could not explain to Commander Ashe, who believed a fact unwritten was a fact unowned. Some facts you carry instead. They get heavy. You get strong or you get crushed, and I had spent eleven years getting strong, and here in this held silence I discovered that strength and crushing are not opposites after all. Sometimes they are the same weight, differently carried.

I heard Edmund coming back up the stairs — I always heard Edmund coming, some specific rhythm in their step, half soldier and half apology — and I made a choice I would spend a great deal of time afterward failing to regret properly.

I blew out my lamp and waited in the dark.

* * *

The door opened on darkness and Edmund stopped in it the way water stops at a lip. I felt them take in the room: the shape of me on the bed, the cold lamp, the absence of everything where an intruder should have been making noise.

"Wren," they said. Not surprised. That was the worst part. Not even a little surprised.

"You still walk like a man sneaking home past his wife," I said, and lit the lamp, and watched eleven years arrive all at once on their face.

They looked older. Everyone looks older; that is the one mercy and the one cruelty time grants uniformly. But Edmund looked older in the specific way of someone who has swallowed a great deal and shown very little, which, given the profession, tracked. There were new lines around their mouth. Their eyes went to my hands first — habit from the tasting quarters, checking for the little vials we used to palm — and then to my face, and whatever they found there made them close the door behind them softly, which was its own answer.

"You're sitting in my room in the dark," they said. "Inquisitors usually knock."

"I knocked at the hold's front door yesterday. You were conveniently at market."

"I was conveniently at market."

"Edmund." I said their name and it came out wrong, too much history in it, a key that no longer fit the lock it was cut for. I started over. "I'm going to say some things now, and you're going to let me finish, because if you interrupt me I will lose my nerve, and I did not ride three days through freezing mud to lose my nerve in your rented room."

They leaned against the door. Waiting. They had always been good at waiting. It used to infuriate me.

"The child," I said. "Red hair, eight years old, scar on her left forearm that she keeps covered. She was in the chapel above Hollowmere when it burned. She's been seen on the goat tracks. She's been seen buying bread in a village where nobody buys bread for strangers. And every road she's been seen on is a road only one living person knows."

Edmund said nothing. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself.

"I've kept it out of my reports," I said. "Three weeks of reports. Do you understand what that costs? Commander Ashe reads my field notes the way a moneylender reads a debtor's letters — looking for the lie between the lines. Every day I don't write the child's description is a day I'm committing treason in ink I haven't spilled yet. And I keep doing it. I keep doing it and I don't fully know why, and I've decided the why doesn't matter, because here is what matters—"

I stood up. My knees crackled. Eleven years of saddles.

"—here is what matters. In six days, Ashe brings forty men into Thornfell. Not four. Forty, with dogs, with a fire-wright who can read heat traces off stone, with orders to burn the holds that shelter bearers and question everyone else in ways that leave marks. He doesn't need your roads. Forty men can be wrong in forty directions and still cover the passes by winter. Your arithmetic fails, Edmund. Whatever sum you've been doing in that clever head, the numbers change in six days."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I have papers." I took them out of my coat then, because there was no longer any point in ceremony. Two documents, sealed, genuine in every particular except the truth. Travel writs under a merchant name I had built over four years of quiet forgery practice that I told myself was professional development. Passage stamps for the southern ferry. A registration exemption stamped with the queen's own cipher, valid for one adult and one dependent minor.

"One set of papers," I said. "One adult. One child. Out of Thornfell, south past the ferry, gone, free, alive. Nobody hunts you. Nobody hunts her. You disappear into the low country and plant turnips and grow old and I never see either of you again except in the part of my memory I've already reserved for things I refuse to think about."

Edmund looked at the papers for a long moment. Then at me.

"What does it cost?"

And there it was. That was the moment I had been dreading, the moment I had rehearsed on the ride up and flubbed in every rehearsal, because Edmund Quintana had never once in our shared life accepted a gift without finding the hook in it, and I loved them for it and hated them for it in roughly equal measure.

"The child," I said. "Not to me. To the registry. You bring her to me, alone, and you sign the exemption as her guardian, and she goes into custody under my authority — which means she registers, she's documented, she's under crown protection, and *protected* means alive, Edmund, it means fed and housed and not burned, it means the law stands between her and everyone who wants her dead, including—" my voice did something I didn't authorize, "—including the parts of the law that want her dead. I can't stop the purge. I can't. I've tried the channels that exist for trying and they are all walled. But I can stand between one child and it. That's the offer. That's the whole offer. Her, surrendered to my custody, in exchange for both your lives."

Silence. The held kind. We were both of us raised in it; we knew how to make it last.

"That's not protection," Edmund said finally, and their voice was gentle, which was worse than anger. Gentle meant pity, and pity meant they'd already understood the shape of me better than I'd finished describing it. "That's a cage with your name on the door. You've seen what happens to registered bearers, Wren. You burn the villages of the ones who don't register. What happens to the ones who do?"

"They're catalogued. Watched. Left alone unless—"

"Unless." They smiled, and it was an awful smile, the smile of the tasting quarters, the one we wore when the food was fine and the company was poison. "Unless someone senior needs a demonstration. Unless policy shifts. Unless a ledger gets crowded. Wren. You keep lists for a living. Tell me honestly: has anyone ever been removed from one of yours?"

I thought of my brother. I did not think of my brother. Both happened in the same instant, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

"The law is the only thing standing between these people and a mob," I said, and heard how it sounded, the way a plank sounds when you step on it and feel it give.

"The law is the mob with better handwriting," Edmund said. "You taught me that. Don't you remember? The night Tammas Ferrow was taken. You were fourteen and furious and you said the difference between the crowd and the court was that the court wrote down whom it killed, and you said it like it was a comfort, and I remember thinking even then — even at twelve — that a list is just a mob that remembers."

"I was a child."

"You were right." They pushed off the door and crossed the room, and stood close enough that I could smell woodsmoke and mountain air and underneath it the faint bitter ghost of the tasting quarters, the mineral tang of someone whose body has processed more poison than most pharmacies stock. "You were right and it broke something in you and you spent eleven years building a career on top of the break, and now you're here offering me a bargain you know is rotten because it's the only version of saving us that lets you keep believing the break was load-bearing."

"Don't psychoanalyze me, Edmund. You'll find nothing but scaffolding."

"I know. I helped you build it."

We stood there. The lamp did its small work. Outside, wind moved through the pass with the long patience of weather that has all winter ahead of it.

"She's eight years old," Edmund said. Quietly. "She hums when she eats. She's afraid of the dark and she pretends she isn't by asking questions until dawn. She asked me last week whether mountains dream, and when I said I didn't know, she said *then who remembers for them*, and I have thought about that sentence every day since, Wren, every single day, because I think it might be the truest thing anyone's said to me in twenty years." Their hands were steady. Their voice nearly was. "You're asking me to hand her to the thing that burned her village. Her whole village. She watched people she loved die for the mark on her arm — the mark *she* carries, not them. And your offer is that if I give her to you, you'll keep her in the machine that did it, and call that mercy because the machine will feed her."

"Yes," I said. "That's the offer."

"It's a good offer," Edmund said, and closed their eyes. "That's what makes it unforgivable."

* * *

We talked until the candle guttered. I won't render all of it here; some conversations belong to the room they happen in. But near the end, when both of us were hoarse and the argument had worn down to its skeleton — surrender her, or flee without papers and let Ashe's forty men do their arithmetic — Edmund asked me a question I have turned over every night since.

"Why did you really come yourself?" they said. "You have riders. You could have had the summons delivered. An inquisitor of your rank doesn't climb to a smuggler's hold in person to offer forged papers. You wanted something you couldn't put in a report. So say it."

And I said, because I was tired and because it was true and because some debts get called in whether or not you can pay:

"Because when they took my brother, you were the only person who didn't look at me like I was contagious. Everyone else in the quarters stepped back — politely, kindly, the way you step back from a widow — and you walked across the yard and stood next to me and said nothing for an hour, and it was the best hour anyone ever gave me. I have owed you an hour of silence for eleven years. I came to pay it. And instead I opened my mouth, because I am constitutionally incapable of the one gift you ever gave me."

Edmund laughed — a real laugh, brief, startled out of them, the first I'd heard since the palace. It hurt more than anything else that night.

"There's still time," they said. "The hour's not going anywhere. Neither am I, tonight."

So we sat in the held silence of that rented room, two people raised in the same cracked pot, while outside the wind counted down six days neither of us mentioned aloud. And I want to record, because my ledger-keeping soul demands it, that for one hour I did not try to persuade them of anything. I just sat next to them, the way you sit next to someone at a funeral, which is what it was — a funeral for the version of us that could have taken those papers and run.

Near dawn, Edmund stood, stretched, and looked down at me with an expression I couldn't read then and can read now, having had time to study it.

"You should know," they said, "that Odile Vane keeps records too. Older ones than yours. And the mountain folk have a saying about people who come up here carrying the queen's seal and leave carrying doubts." They paused at the door. "They say the mountain remembers them. Whether they want it to or not."

Then they were gone, down the stairs, into the grey morning, and I sat alone with my forged papers and my brother's name and the growing, sickening suspicion — arriving like weather, undeniable, from the west — that somewhere beneath Thornfell there was a record with my grandmother in it, and that everything I had built my life on top of was built on top of that.
