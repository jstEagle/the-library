## 12. Odile's Price

Odile Vane kept her ledgers the way other people kept grudges: carefully, in a locked room, with a candle burning low so no one could read over her shoulder.

I knew this because I'd spent three days in that room, and because I'd once watched her threaten to break a man's fingers for dog-earing a page. The man had been her own nephew. The page had been an inventory of smuggled brandy. Priorities, in Vane's Hold, were very clear.

"Read it again," she said now, tapping the paper in front of me with one blunt finger. "Slower this time. You tasters are supposed to be good at noticing things."

"I'm good at noticing things that might kill me," I said. "This is worse."

The paper was cheap stock, the kind mountain scribes used when they didn't want what they wrote to outlive them. The ink was brown-black, iron-gall, the same recipe my grandmother had mixed in a chipped cup on a stove I could still smell if I let myself. And the words on it were simple enough for a child to read, which was fortunate, because a child was going to ask.

*There is a place beneath Thornfell where the sigil can be set down. It cannot be taken. It must be given.*

"That's it?" I said. "That's the whole message? Where's the rest of it? The part with the price?"

Odile leaned back in her chair until it creaked. She was a broad woman built like a doorframe, with grey shot through her black hair and hands that had done every kind of work except honest work. Around her neck, on a cord I'd pretended not to notice for three days, hung a small stone disc carved with a spiral — the old sigil, worn smooth as a river pebble by someone's thumb over decades of worry.

"You've been in my hold three days," she said, "and you haven't asked about the necklace. That's either politeness or fear, and I don't trust either one in a business partner."

"It's not a partnership yet. It's a negotiation."

"Same thing, with better manners." She unclasped the cord and laid the disc on the table between us, next to the note. "You know what this is."

"I know what the crown says it is. Heresy. Sedition. A mark that makes you a target." I turned the disc over with one finger. The spiral caught the candlelight. "My grandmother wore one like it. Under her clothes. She told me never to touch it, which of course meant I touched it constantly."

"And what did she tell you it *was*?"

"A story. About remembering." I'd been avoiding this conversation for three days, ever since Odile had looked at the silver mark creeping up my wrist — visible now past my cuff if I wasn't careful — and gone very still in the way of a woman recalculating every number in her head. "She said the sigil-bearers were keepers. That they held things. Memories, histories. Things the kings wanted burned, so they burned the keepers instead. I thought it was a grandmother's story. The kind you tell children so they feel special before bed."

"And now?"

"Now there's a mark on my arm that glows when Ilsa has nightmares," I said, "and I'm less inclined to call anything a bedtime story."

* * *

Ilsa was asleep in the loft above the stable, which was where Odile had put her, on the theory that a child who'd watched her village burn slept better near animals than near people. I understood the theory. Some nights I'd have traded the whole royal palace for a warm horse and no conversations.

The girl had been with us nine days. Long enough for the burns on her hands to scab over. Long enough for her to stop flinching at Odile's laugh, which was loud enough to startle the dead. Not long enough for her to stop asking questions, because Ilsa asked questions the way rivers ran downhill — constantly, quietly, and finding every crack.

Her sigil sat at the base of her throat, a raw red spiral that looked fresh as a brand and had been there since birth, according to the story she told in fragments: born marked, hidden by her mother, discovered when the inquisitors came through testing every child in the village with cold iron and hot questions. She'd run. She'd hidden in a grain cart bound over the pass, which was how she'd found me, or how I'd found her — the distinction mattered to neither of us anymore.

I climbed up to check on her, because checking on her had become something I did without deciding to, the way breathing was something I did without deciding to. She was curled against the mare's stall, the horse's breath stirring her hair, and the sigil at her throat pulsed faintly in the dark. Not glowing, exactly. Breathing.

Edmund, I told myself, you are in trouble.

I had been in trouble before. I'd been in trouble professionally, you might say. A food-taster's entire career is a controlled emergency: you stand at the queen's table, you put the king's wine on your tongue, and you wait to find out whether your body is going to betray you. I'd survived eleven poisons in fourteen years of service. Each one had left me a little stranger — faster to detect, harder to hurt, and marked, though I hadn't known the marks meant anything until the proclamation went up on the gate and the word *bearer* stopped being history and started being a hunting license.

But all my troubles had been mine alone. That was the arrangement. I swallowed the poison; I carried the risk; I took the coin home to a small room and locked the door behind me. Nobody else stood at the table.

Now there was a child whose nightmares lit up my arm like a lamp, and a woman downstairs with a ledger full of prices, and somewhere south of us an inquisitor named Wren Halloway who had known me since we were both small enough to steal plums from the taster's quarters, and who was very good at finding things.

I went back down to the ledger room.

* * *

"Tell me about the conclave," I said.

Odile poured two cups of plum brandy without asking, which was its own kind of answer. "So you do want the rest of the message."

"I always want the rest of the message. That's why I'm alive."

"The conclave is real." She said it plainly, no theater, which from Odile was more convincing than any performance. "Beneath Thornfell. Older than the crown, older than the passes, older probably than the mountains themselves if you believe half of what they say down there. The keepers. Last of them. They keep what the bearers carried."

"Memories."

"Everything." She turned her cup but didn't drink. "Every bearer who died carrying the sigil — what they saw, what they knew, what was done to them — it goes into the stones. Into the archive. The crown burned the villages and the books and the people, and thought that was the end of it. But you can't burn what lives in blood, Edmund. The keepers have been keeping the receipts for two hundred years."

"And the note. *It cannot be taken. It must be given.*"

"That's the ritual." She set the disc down between us again, deliberately, like a card played face-up. "Unmaking. A bearer can give the sigil up — pour it out, release everything it holds, everything they hold. It disperses. No more mark, no more gift, no more target on your skin. The crown can't weaponize what doesn't exist. Neither can anyone else."

My heart did something complicated that I chose not to examine. "That's what Ilsa needs. She's eight years old, Odile. She shouldn't be a key to anything. Give it up, and she's just a girl again."

"Yes." Odile watched me with those flat merchant's eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "Except."

"There's always an except. Merchants and poisons, that's the rule."

"The giving has to be willing. Truly willing. Not frightened, not coerced, not doing it because the grown-ups decided for her. The ritual reads the bearer, not the words. If the will isn't clean, it fails — and a failed unmaking, from what the keepers say, is worse than no unmaking at all." She finally drank. "And an eight-year-old can't give a clean consent to something she doesn't understand. Not really. Not the way the ritual means it."

"So she waits."

"She grows up marked. Hunted. Every year the purges get wider, and every year she gets easier to find." Odile shrugged, brutal as arithmetic. "Or someone takes the sigil from her first. There are ways. Ugly ways. Ways that leave the giver hollowed out. The keepers won't do them, but the keepers aren't the only ones who know the old craft."

"No," I said, before I'd decided to say it. "Nobody touches her."

"Mm." Odile refilled my cup, which I hadn't emptied, which told me she wanted my hands busy. "Then here's the shape of it, taster. The conclave can take Ilsa's sigil — but only into another bearer first. Sigil calls to sigil. One bearer can receive another's gift, hold both, carry the weight of it. And then, if that second bearer chooses — freely, fully, knowing the cost — they can give it all up. Both. Unmade together. Gone into the wind where nobody gets it."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, the way flames do when the room has gone quiet enough to hear your own blood.

"And the cost," I said. My voice came out level. Fourteen years at the queen's table will teach you that much, at least: nothing shows on your face while you're waiting to see if you die. "What does the giver lose?"

"Everything the sigil holds in them. Which, for a bearer your age, with your history—" she nodded at my wrist, at the silver spiral I'd been hiding under wool for months, "—is considerable. Eleven poisons, they tell me. Each one you survived woke the gift a little more. Your body's been keeping records since before you knew there was a library." She tapped the table. "Give it up, and you give up all of it. The resistance. The tongue that tastes arsenic in watered wine. Maybe more than that — the keepers say some bearers come out of the unmaking lighter, and some come out empty. Like a house with the fire swept out. You wouldn't know which until after."

"After being a generous term."

"After being the only term. There's no tasting this one first, Edmund. That's the joke of it. The one thing a taster can't test."

I laughed. It surprised both of us, a short ugly sound, and then the room was quiet again and the arithmetic sat on the table between us like a third cup neither of us would drink.

Because here was the sum, and I could do it as well as Odile: Ilsa couldn't choose. She was eight. She'd spend her whole life hunted unless the sigil left her, and the sigil couldn't leave her unless someone carried it out, and whoever carried it out had to give it up willingly, wholly, eyes open, with no guarantee of walking away as themselves.

There was exactly one other bearer within a hundred miles.

There was exactly one other bearer, full stop, as far as either of us knew.

"You're not asking me," I said slowly. "You're telling me the price and letting me pretend I'm choosing."

"I'm a fence, not a priest." Odile's mouth twitched. "But yes. That's the trade on the table. The mountain folk have kept the memory alive two hundred years, taster — passed down in songs, in knots tied in rope, in names whispered at funerals. We remember what the sigil was *for*. It was never a weapon. It was a promise: no one gets erased. Not the dead, not their stories, not their names." She looked at the disc on the table, and for a moment the merchant fell away and something older and wearier looked out. "And now the crown wants the last bearers dead, and the last keepers buried, and the last of it finished. If you walk into that conclave and give it all up — freely — then it's finished on *your* terms. Nobody wins it. Nobody owns it. It just ends, the way a debt ends when it's paid."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you run. You're good at roads; you'd likely make the far passes. And Ilsa grows up running beside you, marked, until someone catches her. Or until she's old enough to choose for herself, if she lives to be old enough." Odile spread her hands. "I don't sell hope, taster. I sell passage. But I'll tell you what the mountain folk say, since you seem determined to make me say it: sometimes the bravest thing isn't keeping the gift. Sometimes it's putting it down so the next generation doesn't have to pick it up."

* * *

I went up to the loft after midnight and sat in the straw with my back against the stall, and the mare breathed her warm hay-smell over me, and I listened to Ilsa sleep.

She talked in her dreams sometimes. Names, mostly. Her mother's name, her brother's name, the name of the village, which I had written down in my own ledger-memory along with the names of every other thing the crown had burned that I'd witnessed. Fourteen years of tasting had taught me that memory is a discipline. You hold the flavor of the poison in your mouth long enough to describe it to the physicians, and you learn to hold terrible things that way too — precisely, calmly, without spitting them out.

I thought about my grandmother's chipped cup. I thought about Wren Halloway at nine years old, laughing with plum juice on her chin, before the crown got its hooks into both of us in different ways — her into the law, me into the kitchens — and taught us both that survival meant swallowing.

I thought about what Odile had said, *like a house with the fire swept out*, and about whether I would mind being empty, if empty meant safe, if safe meant hers.

The sigil at Ilsa's throat pulsed in her sleep. On my wrist, unbidden, the silver spiral warmed and answered, the way it always did now, two small lights calling across the dark like lamps on opposite shores.

Eight years old. She couldn't consent to staying up past bedtime, by law. The ritual would see straight through any choice made for her — and worse, it would *take* it, would bind her forever to a sacrifice she never agreed to. I knew what that was. I'd been swallowing decisions other people made for my body since I was nineteen years old.

Which left the arithmetic with one variable, and the variable was me.

"All right," I said to the dark, to the horse, to the sleeping child, to my grandmother wherever grandmothers go. Quietly. Testing it on my tongue the way I tested everything, holding it, waiting to see if it killed me.

It didn't kill me. It settled instead, heavy and clean, like a coin dropped into a deep well.

In the morning I found Odile in the ledger room and told her I'd need a guide who knew the way beneath Thornfell, and supplies for four days, and no arguments.

Odile wrote it all in her book, in her small fierce hand, and then she looked up at me and said, "You should know the keepers sent word yesterday. Someone's already asking after the conclave. An inquisitor, traveling light, asking about a taster and a child."

I closed my eyes. Of course. Wren had always been fast.

"Then we leave tonight," I said.
