## 13. The Conclave Beneath Thornfell

The door to the under-mountain was a dead shepherd's hut with a floor that lied.

Odile Vane knocked on the hearthstone three times, paused, then twice more — a rhythm I recognized from a smuggler's counting song I'd once heard in a tavern in Lowmark, back when I thought it was just a tavern song. The stone swung inward on greased hinges older than the crown itself, and cold air breathed up out of the dark, smelling of wet granite and something else. Something green. Like a cellar where someone had once stored living things and never quite stopped.

"Mind the third step," Odile said. "It minds you."

Ilsa gripped my hand hard enough to grind knuckles. She'd been quiet since the Hollow Chapel — quieter than an eight-year-old should ever have to be — but her scarred wrist had been glowing faintly all morning, through the wrapping Odile's people had bound around it, like a coal that refused to accept being banked.

"How far down?" I asked.

"Far enough that the queen's writ doesn't reach," Odile said. "Which is the only distance that matters."

We went down.

* * *

The stair was cut by hands that predated any mason's guild I could name, and I have tasted for men who employ every mason's guild worth naming. The steps were worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet, each one dipping toward the center like a spoon, and I found myself thinking — in the way my mind does when it's frightened, reaching for arithmetic instead of feeling — that this was what poison had taught me about stairs. You learn surfaces. You learn which stones sweat and which ones lie dry right up until they don't. These steps were honest. Old things usually are; it's only new things that bother learning deception.

The light came before we saw its source. A pale gold glow seeped around a bend in the passage, and Ilsa's grip tightened.

"It knows me," she whispered.

"Everything down here is going to know you," Odile said, not unkindly. "That's rather the point."

Then the passage opened, and I stopped doing arithmetic entirely.

The cavern was vast — cathedral-vast, though I've stood in the capital's great nave and it would have fit inside this place four times over with room left over for the choir. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some impossible organ, and every one of them was lit from within. Not torchlight reflected. Lit. A slow golden pulse moved through the stone the way blood moves under skin, traveling up one column and down another, and where the light gathered thickest, the air itself seemed to hold its breath.

And there were shelves. That was the detail that undid me, later, when I tried to describe it to myself. A cavern full of miracle, and along its walls: shelves. Wooden, plain, carpentered by mortal hands, and stacked with objects — stones mostly, smooth river stones and rough-cut chunks of quartz, each one cradled in a niche like an egg. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The sigil-light pulsed through them too, fainter than the stalactites, patient as banked coals.

"The archive," said a voice.

An old woman stood between the shelves. She was small and brown and weathered as a fence post, wrapped in layers of wool despite the cavern's strange warmth, and she looked at me the way I look at a dish presented to the royal table — completely, without hurry, missing nothing.

"You're the taster," she said.

"I'm nobody," I said, which is what I've said to everyone from customs officers to inquisitors for fifteen years.

"Mm." She turned to Ilsa and crouched, slow, keeping her eyes level with the child's. "And you're the newest page in a very old book. My name is Sera Voss. I keep what's kept here." She held out her hand, palm up, and waited. Didn't reach. Didn't press. Just waited, the way you wait for a cat or a traumatized child, letting them come to you.

Ilsa looked at me. I nodded — I don't know what I intended the nod to mean, permission or apology or just *I'm here* — and she set her small hand in the old woman's palm.

Sera Voss turned the wrist gently. The wrappings glowed through their weave.

"Yes," she said softly. "Yes, little one. We've been waiting a long time for anyone at all."

* * *

They fed us first. This, I approved of instinctively; any organization that negotiates before feeding you is an organization I don't trust, and I've sat across tables from enough of them. The keepers — there were eleven, which Sera Voss delivered without being asked, in the tone of someone reporting a wound — fed us barley soup with mountain onions and dark bread, and the eleven of them ranged in age from a wiry man who might have been fifty to a girl no older than sixteen whose name was Brannoch and who watched Ilsa eat with an expression I recognized because I wear it sometimes in mirrors.

"There used to be hundreds," Sera Voss said, when the bowls were empty. "Keepers, I mean. Once, there were enough that we rotated seasons in the high caves and no one carried more than their share. Now there are eleven, and we carry everything."

"What is everything?" I asked. "Truly. I've heard stories — Odile says the folk remember the sigil as memory-keeping, but the crown teaches it was—"

"The crown teaches it was a weapon," Sera Voss said. "Of course it does. It's easier to burn a library if you tell yourself it was an armory." She rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and beckoned. "Come. You should see what a weapon looks like. Then you can judge whether you've been carrying one."

She led us deeper, past the shelves, to a hollow in the cavern wall where a single stone sat on a pedestal of raw granite. It was larger than the others — fist-sized, veined with gold — and its light was different. Steadier. Older.

"This bearer died two hundred years ago," Sera Voss said. "Her name was Maren of the Ashfall valleys. She was a midwife. She attended four hundred births and she remembered every one of them — every mother's name, every child's weight, every death she couldn't prevent and every life she saved. When she was dying, the keepers of her time performed the transfer. Her whole remembering went into this stone." She looked at me. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, taster? Not power. Not spells. *Record.* The sigil-gift is a ledger written in flesh. Every bearer who lived carried what their people could not afford to lose, and every bearer who died passed it on, and so nothing — no famine, no war, no burning — nothing was ever truly forgotten."

"And the crown," I said. My voice came out flat. I could hear how flat it was, the way I can hear a poison I can't name. "The crown destroyed—"

"The crown burned bearers for three generations," Sera Voss said. "And here is the thing your queen may not even know, buried as it is under her grandfather's grandfather's justifications: when a bearer dies badly — afraid, alone, unmourned — the memory doesn't transfer. It scatters. Two hundred years of executions, and with every one, a piece of everything we knew went into the wind. Names of villages. Songs. How to read the old script. Which plants stop bleeding and which start it. How to build the terraces that kept the high valleys fed through the Long Winter." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it. It stayed level as a surveyor's line. "Your crown didn't just kill people, Edmund Quintana. It killed *knowing*. And now there are children starving in valleys that wouldn't starve if anyone alive remembered how their great-grandmothers fed them."

I stood there in the gold light and did the thing I do instead of weeping, which is count. Eleven keepers. Tens of thousands of stones. And against them, three generations of ash.

Ilsa tugged my sleeve. "Is Maren still in there?" she asked. "In the stone?"

Sera Voss crouched again. "Would you like to meet her?"

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only craft I have, and because afterward I will need to know it wasn't a dream or a trick or a poison-hallucination. I have hallucinated before. I know its texture. This was not that.

Sera Voss lifted the stone and held it out to Ilsa. "Bearer to bearer," she said. "Touch it. If anything feels wrong, let go. Nothing here can hold you that you don't agree to hold."

Ilsa looked at me again. I realized I was holding my breath, and made myself release it, because children calibrate off the adults near them and I refused to teach her fear in a room full of the dead who deserved better.

She touched the stone.

The light in the cavern — all of it, every stalactite, every shelf, every banked coal in ten thousand niches — flared. Once. Like a great indrawn breath. And then I felt it, felt it *in my wrist*, in the silver mark that has been growing there since the first poison survived it: a pressure, a presence, something vast and gentle turning over in sleep.

And then — I swear this on whatever is left of my soul to swear on — I heard humming. An old woman's voice, cracked and warm, humming a lullaby in a dialect I didn't know and understood anyway, the way you understand a mother's tone in any language. Ilsa hummed along. She knew it. Of course she knew it. Her village burned, but her grandmother had sung it, and her grandmother had learned it from hers, and somewhere in that chain, two hundred years deep, Maren of the Ashfall valleys had sung it over a newborn while the stone that held her memory sat warm on a shelf behind her.

Ilsa pulled her hand back. Her eyes were streaming. She was smiling.

"She had forty grandchildren," Ilsa reported, with the absolute authority of an eight-year-old delivering intelligence. "And she was scared of thunder. Even when she was old. She said it sounded like the sky falling on the barn."

"That's her," Sera Voss said quietly. "That's exactly her."

I turned away and pretended to examine the shelves, because my face was doing something I didn't authorize.

* * *

Later — hours later, I think; time moves strangely down there, measured in pulses of light rather than bells — Sera Voss found me sitting alone among the stones, running my thumb over the mark on my wrist.

"Yours is unusual," she said, sitting down beside me with the groan of a woman who has earned every joint complaint. "May I?"

I gave her my arm. She unwound the bandage Odile's people had applied and studied the silver sigil blooming there — branching lines, like frost, like lightning frozen mid-strike, like the roots of something.

"Poison," she said. "You built it with poison."

"Fifteen years of it. Occupational hazard."

"No." She tapped the mark, gently, and it stung like a struck nerve. "Occupational hazard is what happens *to* you. This is what you *did*. Every toxin you survived, your body wrote down. That's the gift, taster — it was never about magic words or bloodlines. It's about a body that refuses to forget what was done to it. Most bearers inherit it sleeping. You woke yours the hard way, dose by dose, year by year." She looked up at me. "Do you know what that makes you?"

"Tired," I said.

"A record of every attempt to kill you," she said, "and the shape of every hand that held the cup. There are poisons in your blood history that haven't been brewed in a century. Recipes the crown thinks it owns. You're not just a bearer, Edmund Quintana. You're evidence."

I laughed. It came out wrong, jagged, closer to the other thing. "Evidence. Yes. That's what Wren Halloway would call it too. She'd say it kindly. That's the worst part about Wren — she'd weep while she filed me."

"Tell me about her."

So I did, there in the gold-lit dark, more than I'd told anyone. About the tasters' quarters where we grew up, the orphan children the crown raised on arsenic tolerance and gratitude. About Wren teaching me cards and me teaching her which mushrooms in the palace garden would put a grown man to sleep. About her brother Tam, registered years ago, compliant, cooperative, and gone into the crown's custody with paperwork in order. About the proclamation at the gate, and the neighbors lining up to denounce each other, and the way dread tastes — I know how everything tastes; dread tastes like copper and cold tea.

When I finished, Sera Voss was quiet a long moment.

"Eleven keepers," she said finally. "One child. One poisoned evidence-of-crown-crimes. Against the whole machinery of the realm." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of someone doing sums she intends to win. "But here is what the crown has never understood, and what Wren Halloway cannot file: you cannot execute an idea you've already let escape. Every person Ilsa meets, every story she carries out of these caves, every valley family that remembers why the terraces are built the way they're built — the archive stops being a place and becomes a people. That's what the purge was always really for. Not the bearers. The *remembering*."

"So what do we do?" I asked. "Hide down here until there are ten keepers, then nine?"

"We do what archives have always done," she said. "We copy ourselves. And you—" she took my wrist again, turned the silver mark to the light, "—you are going to be the most dangerous copy we've ever made. Because you know the roads. You know the kitchens. You know which cup at the queen's table holds what, and which hand holds the cup." Her eyes were bright and terrible. "The crown trained its own witness, taster. All we have to do is keep you alive long enough to testify."

Above us — far above, through a hundred fathoms of stone — I heard, or imagined I heard, or perhaps the stones themselves relayed it down: boots. Many boots. And beneath them, faint as a struck bell, the particular resonance of a sigil-stone being handled by someone who had no right to touch it.

Sera Voss went still. So did the light.

"They've found the mountain," she said.
