## 14. A Name in the Ledger

The mountain did not want us.

I don't mean that in the poetic way the chaplains meant it when they spoke of the wild places as godless and hungry. I mean it mechanically, practically, the way a door does not want a boot: the tunnels fought us at every turn, narrowing where maps promised width, flooding where the surveyors had marked dry stone, and twice simply ending in walls of collapsed rock that no one could account for. Three men broke ankles in the first hour. A fourth was stung by something in the dark that left his arm swollen to the shoulder and his mouth full of prayers he'd clearly been saving for years.

I kept my lantern low and my thoughts lower, and I told myself — as I had told myself since the order came down from Commander Aldric Vess — that this was justice proceeding on schedule. The conclave beneath Thornfell was an archive of sedition. The people who tended it were criminals by definition, because the queen had defined them so. That was how law worked. You didn't get to disagree with a definition and keep your commission.

I was very good at telling myself things. It was, if I'm honest, my principal talent.

"Master Halloway." Vess's voice carried down the tunnel with the flat authority of a man who had never once wondered whether he was right. "Your prisoner. Bring him forward."

My prisoner. That was one way to describe Coram Ash. Another way was: a grandfather of sixty-one years with bad knees and a good memory, whom we'd taken at dawn from a shepherd's hut above the valley, because a frightened keeper named Pell Harrow had sold the conclave's location for his family's safety, and Coram Ash had been the name in Pell's mouth when the inquisitors arrived to collect their purchase.

Coram walked with his hands bound and his chin up, which annoyed me more than struggling would have. Struggling I knew how to answer. Dignity required paperwork.

"You didn't have to come down here," Coram said quietly, as I steered him past a seam of rock that glittered faintly, wrongfully, with light that had no source. "You could have waited above. Let the soldiers do the breaking."

"The commander requested me."

"He requested your name," Coram said. "It opens doors. Mine closes them." He glanced at the glowing seam, and something in his face softened into grief so old it had gone smooth, like a stone in a riverbed. "You should know what you're walking into, Halloway. Not the trap. The library."

"I've burned libraries before."

"I know," he said. "That's what frightens me about you. You say it like a chore."

* * *

We found the conclave an hour before midnight, and it was not what the reports had described.

Reports — mine included, in earlier months — had painted the sigil-keepers as a cell: fanatics hoarding weapons, a cabal of the marked plotting restoration of some pre-crown kingdom. What we descended into instead was a cavern the size of a cathedral, ribbed with pale stone, lit along its walls by sigil-marks carved into the rock itself — thousands of them, each one giving off a light like late afternoon through honey. And between the lights: shelves. Rows upon rows of them, cut directly into the living stone, holding stones. Sigil-stones, each one humming faintly, each one — though I did not yet understand this — a person. Or rather, what a person leaves behind when the crown takes everything else.

The keeper they called Sera Voss stood in the center of it with perhaps a dozen others, unarmed, arranged not in a battle line but in a semicircle around a single stone dais, as if they were a congregation interrupted mid-service. Which, I suppose, we were making sure they were.

"Sera Voss," Vess announced, in the tone of a man reading a menu. "By decree of Her Majesty, this assembly is dissolved. The archive is forfeit to the crown. Resistance will be met—"

"There's no resistance," Sera said. She was old, but her voice was young; it hadn't learned to be careful. "There never was. Look at us, Commander. We're librarians."

Vess looked. I watched him look. And I saw the moment his eyes moved across the shelves and found nothing there that matched the word *arsenal*, and decided — I saw him decide it, the small muscular twitch of a jaw setting itself — to proceed anyway. Because the orders said arsenal, and a man who has built a career on orders cannot afford to find a library where his superiors promised him knives.

"Bind them," he said. "Then begin cataloguing. Master Halloway — the ledgers. Every record of bearers, registrations, names. I want the archive's index on my desk within the day."

"And then?" Sera asked.

"And then the archive burns," Vess said, "and the mountain is sealed. Her Majesty's mercy is that she allows the unmarked to walk out."

One of the younger keepers made a sound — not quite a sob, not quite a laugh. Sera put a hand on his shoulder without looking at him.

"Catalogue quickly, then," she said to Vess, mild as milk. "Librarians always tell you: read them before you burn them. Afterward is too late."

I took four soldiers and went for the ledgers.

* * *

They kept their records in a side chamber off the main cavern, a low room where the sigil-light came through a single carved vein in the wall and pooled on a table scarred by two centuries of elbows. The ledgers themselves were ordinary — leather, string-bound, ink gone brown with age. That was the thing I noticed first, and it unsettled me more than the glowing walls had. I had expected the contraband history of Thornfell to look dangerous. It looked like bookkeeping.

I am, by training and temperament, a woman of lists. My whole career is a ledger: names in, names accounted for, the arithmetic of the purge balancing nightly in my report. So I did what I do. I opened the oldest volume and began to read.

The early entries were registration rolls — the same format the crown used now, which means the crown stole the format, or the keepers invented it and the crown borrowed it back without citation. Names. Birth villages. The nature of the gift, recorded in the keepers' own taxonomy, which was nothing like ours. Where our registry listed *offensive capacity*, theirs listed *what was carried*. Not what a bearer could do. What a bearer held.

Because that was the truth of it, laid out in entry after entry in a hand that grew shakier decade by decade: the sigil-gift was memory. Each bearer's mark stored what they witnessed — truly stored it, whole and uncorrupted, the way no human mind and no paper record ever could. Bearers were living archives. When one died, the stone-cutters of the conclave would carve their mark into a sigil-stone, and the memory would pass into the rock, and the shelf, and the mountain. Generations of them. Weddings and famines and songs and the exact color of a sky over a village that no longer existed. The crown hadn't destroyed a weapon when it began the purges, three hundred years ago. It had burned a library of everything it wished forgotten, and then written histories saying there had never been a library at all.

I sat with that for a long time. Around me, the soldiers stacked stones into confiscation crates with the cheerful efficiency of men loading wine for transport, having understood none of it, or understanding all of it and preferring not to.

I told myself: this changes nothing about your duty. History is one thing; law is another. The queen's peace is bought at a price, and every peace is. Someone must be willing to be the hand that holds the ledger while better minds than yours decide what deserves to be remembered. You are not the thinker, Wren. You are the pen. Pens don't weep over ink.

I was halfway through composing this comfort for the fourth time when I reached the volume covering the years of my own childhood, and found the conclave's copy of the crown registry — copied out, entry by entry, by keepers who apparently believed even their enemies' books deserved preserving — and my own handwriting looked up at me from the page.

No. Not my handwriting. But the same training, the same academy drills, the same abbreviations. An inquisitor's hand, twenty years older than mine. And beside it, in the margin, in different ink — the keepers' ink, the brown ink — annotations. For every name the crown registered, someone down here had recorded what became of them. A parallel ledger. A conscience with columns.

I turned pages. Registered. Registered. Registered — status unknown. Registered — fled north. Registered —

Tam.

My brother's name was written in the crown's ink: *Tamasin Halloway, registered under decree, year of the Vale Purge.* And beside it, in the brown ink, dated eight months after the registration I still kept folded in my breast pocket like a talisman, like proof that our family had complied, had cooperated, had done everything right:

*Executed. Ashford garrison. No trial. Body returned to family with fee deducted for the rope.*

The room went very quiet. Not actually quiet — the soldiers were still stacking, still joking somewhere behind me about the glowing rocks — but the kind of quiet that happens inside a person when a load-bearing wall comes down.

There had been no crossed-out name in the crown's copy. I knew the crown's copy by heart; I had helped compile the regional annex. In our ledger, Tam Halloway was *relocated*. Resettlement. A administrative euphemism I had repeated to our mother at her graveside, because it was kinder than the truth and I had believed — I had needed to believe — that it was the truth.

Eight months. He had survived eight months after signing the register. He had done what I told him to do. I had held his hands in the taster's quarters — no, that was Edmund; Edmund had been there too, that last night, the three of us sharing stolen honey-cakes — I had held his hands and told him the register was protection. Sign it, Tam. Signed names are safe names. The crown doesn't hurt the compliant. That was the entire architecture of my life: that compliance was a roof, and I was a roofer, and the rain fell only on the careless.

And the crown had taken my brother's signature and used it to find him faster.

I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps such things. I sat in a chair in a dead library beneath a mountain, with my brother's execution priced out to the cost of a rope, and I did not give Vess the satisfaction — though he wasn't there, though no one was watching, which is perhaps the more damning detail — of tears. What I did was worse. I started laughing. Quietly. The way you laugh at a joke that has taken twenty years to land.

"Wren Halloway," Coram Ash said from the doorway. They'd brought him in to identify records; I'd forgotten he was in the room. His bound hands made the gesture awkward, but he touched my shoulder anyway, one warm weight, brief. "Now you've read them."

"How long have you known?" My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "About the executions. About the register being a hunting map."

"Since the Vale Purge. Since your brother." He lowered himself onto a crate with the care of a man whose knees had opinions. "We write every name down, inquisitor. Yours. Your brother's. The ones you've burned and the ones you've saved. We thought someday you'd come down here and read yourself."

"That's manipulative."

"It's bookkeeping," Coram said. "You'd know the difference better than most."

I looked down at the brown ink. *Body returned to family with fee deducted for the rope.* Our mother had paid that fee. She had never told me. She had died carrying it alone, because I was the one wearing the uniform, and she had loved me enough to let me keep my roof.

Somewhere beyond the chamber, in the great lit cavern, Vess's voice rose, giving orders for the burning. Torches were being distributed. I could hear the soldiers' cheerfulness, the particular brightness men get when destruction is sanctioned — permission is an intoxicant, and I had been drunk on it for fifteen years.

"Coram," I said. "If I asked you what happens to these memories when the stones burn — honestly, no sermon — what would you tell me?"

"They don't burn," he said. "Not all at once. A sigil-stone releases what it holds when it breaks. That's why the crown grinds them in sealed furnaces, far from any bearer." He studied my face. "But there are hundreds of bearers' worth of stone in this room, and your commander intends open torches, and there is one bearer within a mile of here that I know of, and she is eight years old, and she is not in this room."

Ilsa. The child Edmund Quintana had spirited away — the child whose trail I had followed into these mountains, half-hoping, in the dishonest corner of myself I never audited, that I wouldn't catch it.

"If the stones break uncontained," Coram went on, watching me, "every memory in this mountain goes looking for a bearer to land in. All of it. Centuries. Into a child."

Vess's voice again, closer now: *"Halloway! The index, if you please!"*

I closed the ledger. I did not put it in the confiscation pile. I put it inside my coat, against my ribs, where the folded copy of my brother's registration had lived for twenty years, and I found that the two fit together as if they'd been cut for each other.

"Coming, Commander," I called, and picked up my lantern, and walked toward the light of the torches — already lit, already moving toward the shelves — doing the fastest arithmetic of my life, and knowing, with the certainty of a woman who has finally read her own ledger to the bottom of the page, that whatever number I arrived at, I would have to betray someone to balance it.

Behind me, in the dark of the record chamber, Coram Ash said softly, to no one: "Read them before you burn them. Afterward is too late."

The first shelf caught, and the mountain began, very quietly, to remember out loud.
