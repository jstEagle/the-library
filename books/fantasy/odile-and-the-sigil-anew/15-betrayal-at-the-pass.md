## 15. Betrayal at the Pass

The first sign that something had gone wrong was a jar of honey.

I want to be clear about this: I have survived assassination attempts disguised as soup courses, wines, and once — memorably — a candied plum intended for a visiting duke. I have developed an instinct for danger that most people would call paranoia and I call Tuesday. But nothing in twenty years of swallowing other people's malice prepared me for the particular dread of seeing a sealed honey jar sitting on Odile's kitchen table with no note attached.

Odile Vane did not give gifts. Odile Vane conducted transactions, and every transaction had paperwork, even if the paperwork was just her saying "you owe me" in a tone that made debt feel like a physical weight. A gift was not generosity. A gift was a message.

"Where did this come from?" I asked.

"The lower gate," she said, not looking up from the ledger she was pretending to read. She had been pretending to read it for some time; I could tell because she'd been on the same page since I came down, and Odile did not linger over anything except invoices and grudges. "Boy left it. Said it was from Tam Verrin."

Tam Verrin. One of the keepers. A mild, stooped man who smelled of lamp oil and apology, who had wept quietly during the third recitation of the archive when the names of his own ancestors came up. The kind of man you'd trust to watch your coat and feel guilty about it afterward.

"And where is Tam now?"

"That," said Odile, "is the question, isn't it."

Ilsa was upstairs, asleep in the loft above the stable, curled around a carved wooden bird one of the conclave's old women had made for her. She slept like a child who had learned that sleep was something you did while you could, quickly and completely, the way soldiers ate. When I'd checked on her an hour ago, the sigil at her collarbone had been quiet — a faint silver shimmer, banked coals rather than open flame.

I picked up the honey jar. Good mountain honey, dark, the kind sold at the autumn markets. Heavy glass. Sealed with wax.

I held it up to the light the way I'd been trained to hold wine, looking for sediment, for cloudiness, for anything that shouldn't be there.

There was nothing wrong with the honey. That was what was wrong with it. Because under the wax seal, pressed into the softness before it cooled, someone had marked a sigil-glyph — not one of the archive's true signs, but a crude imitation, the kind a frightened man might scratch out from half-remembered drawings. And beneath it, so small I nearly missed it, scratched into the glass itself with something sharp:

*They know. Run.*

* * *

"You're certain it's his hand?" Odile asked.

We stood in her counting room, which smelled of tallow and old paper, while outside the hold's evening routine went on — cart wheels, a dog barking at nothing, someone singing badly. Normal sounds. The sounds of a place that didn't yet know it was about to become a battlefield.

"I've seen his writing in the archive margins," I said. "He annotates everything. Can't help himself." My voice sounded steadier than I felt. Inside, something was doing arithmetic I didn't want finished. "Odile, how long has Tam's family been down in Lowmark?"

"His wife's sister lives there. Why?"

Because Lowmark was where the inquisitors had set their waystation after the pass skirmish in autumn. Because Wren Halloway's people had been pulling travelers off the road there for weeks. Because a frightened man with a family in reach of the crown's mercy would do almost anything, and *almost anything* had a very specific shape: a location, a name, a child described in detail.

"He sold us," I said. It came out flat. Facts are easier flat. "Or he's about to. The honey is him trying to warn us without — I don't know. Without signing his own death warrant twice."

Odile closed the ledger. When Odile closed a ledger, men had been known to leave the room. "How long do we have?"

"If he told them yesterday? They're already moving. If he told them this morning?" I looked toward the window, toward the grey line of Thornfell rising behind the hold, riddled with the tunnels the keepers had used for centuries. "Then we have until dawn, maybe less. Inquisitors travel fast when they think God's on their side. It's the one advantage of certainty."

"And the conclave?"

The word sat between us. Beneath our feet, three hundred feet of rock down, the last keepers of the sigil were keeping their vigil among stones that remembered everything the crown had burned. Old Mother Casselin. The twins, Ferro and Annet. A dozen others whose names I'd learned in the dark, along with their histories, because that was what the place did to you — it made you carry names.

"They can't evacuate in a night," I said. "Some of them can barely walk. And even if they could — where? Every road out of these mountains has a checkpoint on it now."

"So we wake them anyway."

"We wake them, and we run, and some of us die tired instead of dying surprised." I rubbed my face. "Yes. Fine. Wake them anyway."

It says something about the state of my life that this counted as optimism.

* * *

We went down through the keeper's entrance behind the hold — the crack in the hillside that Odile's grandfather had walled with a door disguised as a root cellar, because the Vane family had been hiding things from the crown for four generations and had gotten professionally good at it. Ilsa came with us because leaving her above ground, alone, with inquisitors possibly descending, was unthinkable, and because she refused anyway with the specific immovable quality of an eight-year-old who has already lost everything once.

"I'm coming," she'd said, standing in the stable loft in her too-big coat, chin up, eyes red-rimmed and furious. "You always try to leave me places. It never works."

She wasn't wrong. It had never worked. There was a lesson in that about my judgment that I chose not to examine.

The tunnels took us down in long spirals, past walls where sigil-light lived in the stone itself — pale silver veins that brightened as bearers approached, dimmed when they left. Ilsa's mark woke as we descended, glowing through her shirt collar, and the walls answered her, and she walked with her hand trailing along the rock like a girl greeting a herd of enormous sleeping animals.

"They're humming," she whispered.

"They're always humming," said Ferro, meeting us at the second junction, lantern in hand though he hardly needed it. He was ninety if he was a day, built like a knot in old wood. "You only hear it when you're scared. The stone knows you're scared, child. That's not a flaw. That's the whole point of it. It keeps what we feel."

"Ferro," I said. "We need everyone up. Now. Tonight."

He looked at me for a long moment. Old men's faces take bad news slowly, the way stone takes rain. "Tam didn't come to evening recitation," he said.

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "When did you notice?"

"An hour ago. Casselin noticed before me. She says—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Old men learn to deliver grief in installments. "She says the stone went cold near the eastern gallery two nights back. Cold spots mean a bearer has gone beyond the deep ways. Out. Tam went out two nights ago."

Two nights. Two days of travel, two days of whatever bargain he'd struck, two days for the information to reach Lowmark and the machinery of the purge to grind into motion.

"Then we may already be out of time," I said. "Get them up. The ones who can walk go up and scatter to the holds that will take them. The ones who can't—"

"Will not leave," Ferro said simply. "Casselin hasn't left this mountain in forty years and won't start for inquisitors. We'll carry what we can of the archive-stones to the inner vault and seal it. The rest—" He shrugged, a small terrible motion. "The rest is memory, Edmund. You can burn shelves. Memory goes where it goes."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to grab the old man by his ancient coat and shake sense into him, tell him that memory doesn't stop a blade, that dead keepers remember nothing. But I'd spent enough time in the conclave by then to understand what he was actually saying: that they had already done the math, decades ago, and the answer hadn't changed. Some people are the message. You don't evacuate the message.

So instead I said, "Take the western galleries. If it goes wrong, the collapse shafts behind the archive still work?"

"They work," Ferro said. "They've worked for six hundred years. Whether they'll work tonight is between you and whatever you believe in."

"I believe in load-bearing masonry," I said. "It's never lied to me."

* * *

It began, as catastrophes often do, with a sound too small to matter.

We were in the great gallery — the wide cavern where the archive-stones stood in rows like a forest turned to mineral, where Ilsa had first touched a stone and gasped at whatever centuries-old stranger had spoken through it. Forty-odd keepers were moving, carrying, wrapping stones in wool and leather, an operation with all the speed of a funeral procession, which is essentially what it was.

Then I heard it. Faint. Above us. Through sixty feet of rock.

Boots. Many boots. And beneath the boots, thinner than a needle, a sound I knew from the palace guard drills I'd watched from servants' corridors: the metallic kiss of a ram's head being fitted to its carriage.

"They're at the upper entrance," I said. "Everyone stop. Listen."

The gallery went silent. Forty faces turned toward me, lit silver from below by the stones, and I thought, absurdly, of the royal table — all those faces waiting on my verdict, my nod deciding whether a dish lived or died. I had spent my whole life being the pause before catastrophe. Apparently the skill traveled.

Casselin came forward, leaning on Annet's arm, her blind white eyes somehow finding me exactly. "The eastern shaft," she said. "Not the main way. They'll have the main way blocked within minutes. The eastern shaft comes up behind the shrine ruin, above the scree field. It's narrow. It's falling apart. It's perfect."

"It's also directly under the ridge line," said Annet. "If they bring fire-oil down the main gallery and it catches the resin seams—"

"Then we should not be here when they do," said Casselin. "Go. All of you. Edmund—" Her hand found my wrist, found the silver mark beneath my sleeve, and gripped it with shocking strength. "The child goes first. Then the stones you can carry. Then you. In that order, taster, or I will haunt you, and I have been practicing haunting for eighty years."

For one strange suspended moment, with boots audible overhead and old hands gripping my arm, I almost laughed. There was something in the absurdity of it — the crown's food-taster, promoted by apocalypse to general of a retreat of the blind and the ancient. My grandmother would have said the sigil has a sense of humor. My grandmother said a lot of things, mostly warnings, all of them correct.

Then the first blow landed.

The sound came down through the rock like a heartbeat played on a drum the size of a cathedral. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Somewhere in the rows of stones, one of them flared bright — answering, I realized, the fear flooding the room, recording it, keeping it, the way it had kept everything for six hundred years. Even this. Especially this.

"MOVE," I said, and forty people moved.

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have sides. This was a flood with a schedule.

Ilsa went first, as ordered, with Annet guiding her. Then the carriers, strings of keepers hauling wrapped stones on sledges of oiled wood down the eastern shaft, a passage barely shoulder-wide, sloping up through rock that groaned when the ram struck again. Behind us, deeper in, Casselin and five others who could not make the climb withdrew toward the inner vault with the oldest stones — the first-generation records, the war-era archives, the memories nobody living could carry.

"Casselin—" I started.

"Don't waste breath arguing with a dead woman, boy," her voice floated back, dry as the dust falling around us. "Spend it running."

I ran.

The eastern shaft was everything promised and worse. Six hundred years of seep water had softened its joints; the ram blows above were shaking loose what patience had preserved. I climbed with my palms bleeding, following the silver glow of Ilsa's sigil ahead of me like a lantern that occasionally sobbed. At the third bend, a section of wall let go entirely — not dramatically, not with cinematic grandeur, just a wet grinding sigh as a slab the size of a cart slid down and took the passage's floor with it.

Annet screamed from somewhere ahead. Dust. Darkness. The glow ahead vanished, then reappeared, weaker.

"Ilsa!" My voice cracked off the stone. "Talk to me!"

"I'm here!" Small, terrified, alive. "Annet pushed me! The rocks — Edmund, the rocks fell where she was standing—"

Silence. Then, muffled, pained, furious: "I'm under it. Not crushed. Pinned. Go on, girl, don't be stupid—"

I got to the gap. Beyond it, the passage continued upward, and across the fresh rubble Annet lay pinned from the knees down, grey-faced, waving us on with both hands. Ilsa stood frozen on the far side, caught between obedience and love, which is the worst intersection in any world.

I did the arithmetic. It was the same arithmetic I'd done my whole life — weight, time, distance, cost — but for once the variables included a person I could not put a price on, and the sum came out simple anyway.

"Hold the light high," I told Ilsa, and went over the rubble.

Moving the slab was not possible. Moving *enough* of the slab was merely improbable. I wedged myself into the gap beside her, got my shoulders under the edge where it had caught on an older stone, and lifted with legs and back and the special strength of people who have decided not to calculate anymore. Something in my lower back made a sound I chose to interpret as encouragement. The slab rose an inch. Annet hauled herself free by her elbows, dragging useless legs, swearing in three dialects with impressive fluency.

"Go," she gasped. "I'll follow. Crawl, if I must."

"You'll crawl behind me," I said, "where I can see you."

Above us, muffled by everything, came a new sound. Not the ram. Voices. Shouting, echoing down through the broken main gallery — and then a smell threading through the stone that stopped my heart colder than any blow.

Fire-oil. Burning.

The resin seams. Annet had warned us. The crown's people had found the old maps' weakness or simply gotten lucky, and fire was coming down the mountain's throat behind us, and the archive — the stones, the stored centuries, Casselin and her five companions in the inner vault—

I did not look back. Looking back is a luxury of people with time.

* * *

We came out of the shrine ruin into night air so cold it felt like rescue. Below us, the scree field glittered under a half moon, and beyond it, the black shoulder of the ridge, and beyond that, the pass road where — I prayed, I bargained, I offered the universe terms — Odile's people were waiting with carts and lies.

Behind and below, the mountain breathed smoke through a dozen cracks. Orange light pulsed in the fissures like embers in a banked fire. Once — I will swear to this until I die — once, through the roar, I thought I heard singing. Casselin's voice, or the stones', or just wind finding a hole in the world. The keepers had a song for exactly this, Ferro had told me once, laughing: the song they'd sing when the last archive closed. He'd called it a joke. Nothing about it had been a joke.

Ilsa was crying without noise, the way children cry when they've learned crying costs something. I crouched, pulled her against me, felt the sigil at her collarbone burning hot through the cloth — grieving, I thought. The stone grieves with us. That was the whole point of it. It keeps what we feel.

"Did they get out?" she whispered. "The old ones?"

I thought about lying. I thought about how many lies I'd swallowed in my life, how expert I was at administering comfort in measured doses, tasting everything before anyone else had to.

"Some of them," I said. "And the ones who didn't — they're not gone, Ilsa. That's the thing the crown never understood. You can't burn what's already been given away. Casselin gave everything she carried to everyone who ever listened. We're carrying it now. Both of us. That's how it survives. Not in stones." I tapped her chest, gently, then mine. "In here. Where fire-oil doesn't reach."

She nodded against my shoulder, unconvinced, which was honest of her. Grief doesn't take comfort on the first offer. It takes it eventually, the way mountains take rain.

Then I raised my head, and the night got worse.

Torches on the pass road. Not Odile's people — Odile's people didn't travel in formation. A column, winding up from the valley, moving with the unhurried confidence of an army that believes the work is already done. And at its head, riding, a figure I knew by silhouette alone, the way you know a scar.

Wren Halloway had come in person.

Of course she had. Tam Verrin's information had bought more than a location; it had bought the purge's highest prize — the last conclave, the living key, and, if Wren's suspicions had finally hardened into certainty, the taster who'd been feeding her gentle lies for months.

I gathered Ilsa up. Behind me, Annet dragged herself from the tunnel mouth, took one look at the torches, and said a word in old keeper-tongue that needed no translation.

"There's a path," I said. "Off the scree, west, the one that doesn't exist unless you've smuggled contraband through these mountains for fifteen years. It exists. I've used it. It's awful. We're taking it."

"And Wren?" Ilsa asked, small voice, sharp eyes. "Your friend?"

I looked at the torches. Somewhere down there was a woman who had once shared stolen sweets with me in the taster's quarters, who had registered her own brother believing it would save him, who had spent her whole life mistaking order for mercy. Somewhere down there was the person sent to collect us.

"She was," I said. "Come on. We're going to be very difficult to find."

We went down into the dark, three fugitives and everything we carried, while behind us the mountain burned its memory into the sky — and ahead, on the road below, a rider detached from the column and turned her horse toward the scree field, toward the shrine ruin, toward the smoking hole in the world where a tunnel mouth still breathed.

Toward us.
