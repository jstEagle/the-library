## 16. What Wren Saw

The conclave ruins smelled of burnt cedar and old stone, and beneath both, faintly, of something sweet — like honey left too long in a hot room. I had been in enough burned buildings by then to know the smell of a life interrupted. This was different. This was the smell of something that had been interrupted on purpose.

I stood at the mouth of the collapsed tunnel with my lantern held high and my ledger under my arm, and I told myself I was doing my job.

That was becoming a difficult sentence to finish without laughing, or weeping, or both. My job. Three weeks earlier my job had been simple: find bearers, register them or take them, keep the count clean, keep the peace the way a dam keeps a river — badly, and only until it doesn't. Then I'd found my brother's name crossed out in Commander Aldous Verrick's private ledger, dated four months after his registration, four months after he'd done everything right, everything the crown asked of him. *Processed.* That was the word. Tam Halloway, processed, like grain through a mill.

So now my job was to walk into the wrecked heart of the sigil-folk's last sanctuary, alone, off the record, and pretend I didn't know why.

The tunnel had come down during the raid — Verrick's sappers, quick and thorough, sealing whatever the conclave kept below before anyone could catalog it. Most of his men were already back at camp drinking to a purge well done. Verrick himself had ridden for the capital that morning with dispatches and, I suspected, with Edmund Quintana's name written large in his mind. Which left me, technically, with orders to secure the site.

Securing a site meant burning what couldn't be carried. That was doctrine. Doctrine and I were no longer on speaking terms.

* * *

I went down instead of up, which is to say I went where the collapse hadn't quite finished its work. A seam of open passage ran along the eastern wall where the blast had sheared the rock and left a gap barely wide enough for a woman who had spent her girlhood squeezing through fence gaps in the taster's quarters to escape her brother's chores. Some skills you never lose. Tam would have laughed at me, wedged sideways in the dark, lantern scraping stone, whispering curses our mother would have boxed my ears for.

Tam. Everything came back to Tam lately. Every corridor I walked, every name I wrote, every fire I did not light because I could not make myself light it — all of it bent toward that one crossed-out line like iron filings toward a lodestone.

The gap opened into a chamber that the sappers' fire had touched but not consumed. Sigil-light still lived in the walls.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the last professional habit I had left. The carvings on the stone — spirals, knots, lines of script in no alphabet I knew — glowed. Not reflected my lantern light. Glowed. Faintly, unevenly, the way coals breathe hours after the fire seems dead. The air in the chamber was warm when the mountain around it should have been cold as a tax collector's heart.

My training said: destroy it. My hand said otherwise. My hand had been saying otherwise since the ledger, and lately I had learned to listen to it, the way you learn to listen to a horse that wants to bolt — not because you trust it, but because it knows something you don't.

There were stones set into niches along the walls. Dozens of them. Palm-sized, flat, each carved with a single spiral, each holding its own small coal-glow. An archive, I thought. Or a graveyard. With these people, from everything I'd read in the confiscated fragments, the two words might have been synonyms.

I should have called for the sappers. I should have marked the chamber, climbed out, and reported it for destruction, and gone home to my narrow bed and my narrow conscience and slept the sleep of the obedient.

Instead I picked one up.

* * *

It was warm. That's the detail that undid me, more than the light, more than the script. Warm as a living hand. I turned it over and found, scratched small on the base in careful common letters — someone had wanted it legible to outsiders, or to children, or to the future — a name:

*Maren Halloway.*

I did not drop it. I want credit for that. Every instinct in my body screamed to drop it, to crush it, to throw it against the wall and run, and I stood there in the breathing dark holding my grandmother's name in my palm like a coal that refused to burn me.

Maren Halloway did not exist. That was the first thing my mind offered, the clerk's reflex, the registry reflex. I had spent two years building the bearer registries for the Thornfell district; I knew every family record worth knowing. The Halloways were tasters' folk going back four generations — sworn servants of the crown's table, tested clean at every generation, that was the family pride, that was the story Mother told at every funeral and every wedding: *we are the ones who swallow the poison so the realm need not.* Clean stock. Proven loyal. Maren Halloway appeared nowhere in any record I had ever touched.

Except once. Once, when I was nine, I had asked Mother about the woman in the miniature portrait that hung in the stairwell — the one with the sharp eyes and the mountain brooch, older than any portrait had a right to be. Mother had taken it down the next week and told me it had always been broken, and sold the frame, and never spoken of it again. I had forgotten. You forget what your family teaches you to forget. That is practically the definition of a family.

The stone pulsed in my hand. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat remembering itself.

I knew what it was by then. I had read the seized fragments — the ones Verrick let me see, anyway. The gift was never a weapon. That was the heresy at the center of everything, the thing the crown had spent three centuries burying under the word *witchcraft*. It was a keeping-magic. Bearers didn't cast spells; they *held things* — memories, records, the testimony of the dead — and passed them on, and the stones were how they stored what a single life couldn't carry. Every bearer who died carrying the sigil left behind an account no fire could fully erase.

Which meant the stone in my hand was not a relic.

It was testimony.

"Whoever you were," I said aloud, to the dark, to the glow, to my own grandmother apparently, "you'd better be ready for a hostile audience."

I pressed my palm flat against the spiral.

* * *

They tell you nothing about what it's like, because there is no one left to tell you. So I will tell you, whoever finds this account, if accounts survive anymore: it is like being remembered by someone else. Not remembering — being remembered. You are the page. Someone else holds the pen.

Snow. A kitchen with a low ceiling and a copper pot gone green at the rim. A child crying — no, a child *not* crying, a child biting her lip bloody so she won't cry, because crying brings questions and questions bring the testers. Hands wrapping the child's wrist in linen to hide the new silver mark blooming there, the mark shaped like a spiral, the mark that had shown itself the day the girl survived the fever that took her two brothers.

A voice, low and fierce: *"You will never show them. You will marry into the taster's quarter, where they test you every year and find nothing, because I will teach you to hold it down, hold it deep, hold it like breath underwater. And when you have a daughter you will teach her the same, and she will think it is only a trick her mother taught her, and she will teach her daughter, and the sigil will sleep in our blood like a seed under snow until the world is safe for it again."*

*"And if it never is?"* The child's voice. My mother's voice. Nine years old, maybe ten, lip bleeding, eyes furious.

A pause. In the pause I felt what Maren felt, which was the whole weight of the burned village behind her, the names of forty-one neighbors she carried in her skin because their keeper had died at the pyre and passed them to her in the last minute of his life, forty-one names pressing on her ribs like stones in a sack. I felt her decide, in that moment, that the truth was heavier than comfort.

*"Then we hold it until we die,"* she said, *"and we die unrecorded, and that is our victory. They cannot burn what they cannot find. Remember that, little one. Forgetting is their weapon. Memory is ours. As long as one of us remembers, none of us is truly ash."*

The vision folded. Snow again, years later — a road over the pass, a bundle of papers sewn into a coat lining, a young man with kind eyes and a forged registry seal walking beside her. She was teaching him the roads. She was teaching him which poisons left no trace and which left silver marks. She was calling him *Edmund*, and he was fifteen, and he adored her, and he had no idea yet what she was sewing into his inheritance along with the roads.

She was dying even then, slowly, the way bearers died when they carried too much — I understood that now, felt it in the ache behind her eyes, the names stacking up year over year, the fever-victims and the drowned and the disappeared, a whole district's unwritten history pressing her further underground. And near the end — the very end, the stone's final entry, made in a cellar with shaking hands — she had pressed her wrist to a blank stone and whispered:

*"For whoever comes looking. My granddaughter serves the crown now. She thinks she hunts monsters. Let her find me instead. Let her find all of us. We were never monsters. We were the ledger they were afraid of."*

* * *

I came back to myself sitting on the floor of the chamber with the stone in my lap and tears freezing on my face, and I could not remember getting down there, and I did not care.

Here is what I had believed, and I want to state it fairly, because if you cannot state your own sin fairly you have not repented of it, you have only redecorated it: I had believed the purge was mercy. Truly. The registries, the tests, the relocations — ugly instruments, yes, but instruments aimed at preventing worse. Because the alternative story, the old story, was that bearers were weapons waiting to be aimed, and a weapon that waits gets used by somebody eventually, and better the crown controls it than a warlord, better a controlled burning than a wildfire. Order prevents worse bloodshed. I had written that sentence in my own hand at the front of my first field ledger, underlined twice, the way seminary students underline scripture.

And underneath that belief, load-bearing and invisible, had been a smaller one: *my family was clean.* The Halloways stood on the crown's side of the line because we had always stood there. Loyalty was our inheritance, the way the sigil was theirs. It had to be true. If it wasn't true, then Tam —

Tam, who registered the moment the proclamation went up, who walked to the inquisitors' tent with his hat in his hands like a man arriving at church, who did everything right. Tam, whose name sat in Verrick's ledger with a date and the word *processed* and no explanation, because explanations were for people and Tam had stopped being one the moment his paperwork was complete.

He hadn't been processed. He'd been *erased.* The same operation, performed with different tools, that had erased Maren Halloway from a stairwell wall. The same operation I had performed on forty-one villages' worth of names, writing them into ledgers that fed fires, telling myself each time that order prevented worse bloodshed.

We were never monsters. We were the ledger they were afraid of.

I looked down at the stone glowing in my lap — my grandmother's patient, furious, loving voice still ringing in my skull — and I understood, finally, with the clarity that only arrives after the cost has already been paid, what the conclave had been. Not an arsenal. Not a war council. A library of the damned, staffed by people who had chosen to die unrecorded rather than let their memory become ammunition. And the crown hadn't feared them because they were dangerous.

The crown had feared them because they were *witnesses.*

* * *

I sat in that chamber for a long time. Long enough for the lantern to gutter. Long enough to open my field ledger to a fresh page and understand, with a strange calm, what I was about to do and what it would cost.

I did not report the chamber. I noted in the site log that the lower galleries were "collapsed and unrecoverable," which was even mostly true. I pocketed Maren's stone. There were others — dozens, each one a voice, each one a witness — and I could not carry them all, and leaving them felt like leaving people in a burning house, so I did the only thing I could: I rolled a boulder across the niche wall, piled rubble against it, and memorized the way in. If the conclave's keepers survived — if Edmund Quintana and a stolen child were somewhere ahead of me on the mountain, and I was increasingly certain they were — then they would come back here. And when they did, I would not be standing between them and their dead.

I would be standing beside them. Whatever that made me. Traitor, probably. The word arrived on schedule and found, to its visible disappointment, that I had already furnished the room and moved in.

Because here was the arithmetic I could no longer avoid: I had served a law that executed the compliant and called it order. I had hunted a child with a scar and called it mercy. I had crossed out my own grandmother — not with ink, worse, with *ignorance*, the tidy ignorance families build like furniture — and called it loyalty. Verrick's ledger had cracked me. Maren's stone finished the job. The law I served was not a dam holding back a flood.

It was the flood. It had always been the flood. It just wore a uniform and filed reports.

I climbed out through the gap in the rock into grey afternoon light, and the mountain wind hit my face, and somewhere south of me — I didn't know how I knew, I simply knew, the way you know a storm is coming by the taste of the air — Edmund Quintana was moving a child over the passes, and Verrick was riding to intercept with two hundred men and a writ that would let him do anything.

I checked my pistol. I checked my pen. Both were loaded; only one of them was pointed the right way now.

Then I turned north, away from camp, away from my orders, away from everything I had been at dawn, and started walking — toward the passes, toward the hold where Odile Vane sheltered fugitives, toward whatever was left of my name on the other side of this.

Behind me, deep under the collapsed rock, a chamber full of small warm lights breathed on in the dark, keeping its accounts, waiting to be believed.

I intended to believe every one of them. Starting with hers.
