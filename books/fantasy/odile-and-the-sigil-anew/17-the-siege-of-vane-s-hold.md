## 17. The Siege of Vane's Hold

I smelled the smoke before I saw the hold, which was the first wrong thing, because Odile Vane did not permit smoke on her mountain. She had a standing rule about it, the way other people had rules about shoes in the parlor. No fires after dusk, no green wood ever, no chimney lit unless the wind agreed to carry the evidence east toward the crown's garrison and not west toward her neighbors. Smoke meant either carelessness or siege, and Odile Vane had never once in her life been careless.

So: siege.

Ilsa felt me change pace and matched it without asking, which is a skill children learn fast when the world has spent a year teaching them. She'd stopped asking questions somewhere around the third burned village and started instead doing a kind of quiet arithmetic of her own — counting exits, counting soldiers, counting the seconds between my face going calm and my feet deciding something. She was eight years old and better at threat assessment than half the palace guard I'd eaten breakfast beside for eleven years.

"Odile's in trouble," she said. Not a question either.

"Maybe she's just burning dinner."

Ilsa looked at me with the flat gray patience of a child who has watched adults lie badly her whole short life. "You're doing your tasting face."

"I don't have a tasting face."

"You do. It's the one where you look like you're swallowing something that tastes like nothing." She considered. "You're wearing it now."

The girl was going to be insufferable at fourteen, and I intended, against all evidence and odds, to be there for it.

We came up through the scree below the hold as the light went long and coppery, and I got my first proper look at what "trouble" meant when the crown decided to collect a debt. Vane's Hold wasn't much to look at even in peacetime — a squat stone manor stitched onto the bones of an older watchtower, terraced gardens climbing its back like a green cloak, a single road in and a great many roads out if you knew which goat tracks counted. Odile had bought it cheap from a family that had died out inconveniently, the way families around Thornfell kept dying out lately.

Now there were tents below it. Not many — forty men, maybe fifty, two supply wagons, a pair of those newfangled arquebuses the quartermasters loved because they made noise like God clearing his throat. A picket line across the road. And above it all, nailed to the hold's own gatepost, a banner I recognized from too many proclamations: the crowned eye of the Inquisition, staring down at the door it intended to come through.

Fifty men. Odile held with maybe a dozen — gardeners, a fence's muscle, two old keepers who'd survived the conclave collapse by being too slow to run and therefore too far behind to be caught. Fifty against twelve, with artillery, and the arithmetic only worked if you were very good at subtraction and didn't mind what got subtracted.

I crouched in the rocks with Ilsa and did what I had done at ten thousand royal meals: I looked at the table and asked what had been put in the food.

* * *

Here is a thing nobody tells you about poisoners' work, and I include the poisoners themselves, who are mostly romantics about their own cleverness: every poison is a supply chain. You cannot kill a king with a theory. You need hemlock or arsenic or distilled crowshade, and crowshade does not grow in the capital, and arsenic comes from smelters in the lowlands, and hemlock has to be cut fresh and kept wet or it loses its teeth. Every dose that ever crossed the queen's table had traveled a road, sat in a storehouse, been signed for by a clerk with cold hands and warm opinions about his own importance.

And I had spent eleven years memorizing those roads, because a taster who doesn't know where the poison comes from is just a man who dies professionally.

More than that. I had helped *build* three of the storehouses. When the purge began and the kitchens grew nervous about sabotage, the Master of Household had asked me — me, invisible Edmund Quintana, whose tongue was worth more than his name — to advise on secure storage. I had walked him through it like a proud parent. Ventilation here. Sealed lead-lined casks there. And, because I am thorough and because paranoia is simply professionalism applied to fear, I had noted exactly which poisons were stored where, in what quantity, and — this was the part I'd never said aloud to anyone — exactly how each of them behaved when it met heat, water, or time.

Arsenic, heated, gives off a fume that will drop a room of men before they understand they've been robbed of breath. Crowshade steeped into a well keeps killing for six days. Distilled hemlock, if a cask cracks near a cookfire, burns with a sweet smoke that smells like almonds and lies.

I knew the location of the nearest crown cache to the last step. Two days' march south, in a converted granary at Coldford, guarded by four men and a clerk. Enough stock to provision an army's worth of quiet deaths — or, if one were so inclined, enough stock to make fifty besiegers very sorry about their choice of campsite.

I had spent my whole life making sure nothing poisonous reached anyone who didn't deserve it, and here I was, lying in scree with a child's hand in mine, planning to spend that knowledge like a drunkard spends his last coin.

Well. Coin exists to be spent. That's the entire point of coin.

* * *

Getting into the hold was the easy part, which tells you how bad everything else was. Odile's people had left the postern open for us — Wren's desertion from the inquisitor column must have bought us hours, though I didn't know yet whether Wren had deserted or merely fallen behind, and the difference between those two things was currently the difference between my heart beating and not.

A gardener named Brannic hauled us over the wall, looked at Ilsa, looked at me, and said nothing, which in Odile's household was practically a speech of welcome.

She was waiting in the cellar, of course. Odile Vane conducted her life from cellars the way other women conducted theirs from drawing rooms. She stood amid her ledgers and her casks of smuggled brandy with her sleeves rolled and a crossbow leaning against a barrel within reach of her hand, and she looked at me the way she always did, like a merchant appraising goods that had arrived late and slightly damaged.

"You brought the child back," she said. "Through a war zone. On foot. In winter."

"She walks well."

"She walks well." Odile pinched the bridge of her nose. "Quintana. There are fifty soldiers at my gate and a man downstairs with a writ signed by the Lord Inquisitor himself, and you are telling me about the child's walking."

"There's a writ?"

"There's a writ. They read it through a speaking-trumpet at noon. Very theatrical. 'Surrender the sigil-bearer and the harborer of sigil-bearers, and the rest may go free.'" Her mouth went thin. "They even spelled my name right. That's how you know they mean it."

"And the man downstairs?"

"A captain. Came under flag of truce an hour ago to repeat the offer in person, in case we were illiterate. He's drinking my second-best brandy and pretending he isn't afraid of me." She almost smiled. "He should be more afraid. But he's young, and young men think numbers are the same thing as victory."

I set down the pack. My shoulders ached in the specific way that meant I'd been carrying fear rather than weight, though with me the two had always shared a spine. "How long can you hold?"

"Hold?" Odile laughed, short and real. "Against arquebuses? Until they get bored, or until they stop being polite. The polite phase ends tomorrow morning. After that they burn the granaries and wait for us to starve, and starving takes weeks, and weeks is time they have and I don't." She turned and looked at me fully then, and beneath all the ledger-keeper's iron I saw the thing she would never say: *I gave you passage twice, Quintana. I named your price once. This was not the debt I thought I was buying.*

"I need to talk to your captain," I said.

"Why?"

"Because I know where his army keeps its poison."

* * *

Captain Aldery Voss was twenty-six years old, freckled, and drinking my hostess's brandy with the guilty enthusiasm of a boy stealing jam. He had a writ in a leather tube and a sword that had never been drawn in earnest and a face that wanted very badly to believe in the righteousness of his orders and couldn't quite find the purchase for it. I knew the type intimately. For eleven years I had been the type.

He stood when I entered, out of reflexive courtesy, then caught himself and sat back down, remembering he was the one with fifty men.

"You're the taster," he said. "Quintana. Your description's on half the notice boards between here and the capital."

"I know. It flatters my jaw."

"It says you're to be taken alive. Priority bearer." He said it carefully, watching me, the way a man watches a dog he's been told bites. "That's unusual. Most of them they just want dead."

"Most of them aren't useful alive." I sat down across from him without being invited, because the whole trick of negotiating from weakness is to sit as though the chair belongs to you. "Captain, how long have you been posted to Coldford?"

His eyes flickered. "What makes you think—"

"The granary. South road, converted two years ago, four-man watch plus a clerk named Pell who waters the wine and hates his posting. Am I close?"

Now he was very still. "That's a state secret."

"It's a storehouse. I designed its ventilation." I let that land. Watched him do the arithmetic — taster, kitchen access, the Master of Household's famous security reforms, the reforms everyone joked about because they'd been authored by a servant. His face went through disbelief, then the slower, worse journey to belief. "You have, sitting two days south of this mountain, enough refined arsenic to kill a city, enough crowshade distillate to salt a watershed, and roughly forty gallons of hemlock spirit that some clerk has been storing next to the lamp oil because the inventory sheets ran out of columns. I know this because I wrote the inventory system. I also know that your commanding officer requested transfer of six casks of crowshade to this operation — for the wells of noncompliant villages, presumably — and that the transfer order is sitting in his dispatch box right now, unsigned, because Captain, and I say this with respect, your army is slow."

Voss's hand had drifted to his brandy glass and stopped there, hovering, as if the drink itself might now require testing. I almost smiled. Old habits.

"What are you saying to me?" he said quietly.

"I'm saying that I could take that poison. Tonight, tomorrow, whenever I like. I know the guards' rotation because I trained two of them. I know which window the ventilation shaft feeds, and I know that arsenic fume, properly introduced to a sleeping barracks, does not leave survivors to write reports." I leaned forward. "I am not going to do that. Here is what I am going to do instead: nothing. And the fact that I *could* do something, and that you now know I could, is worth more to you than anything I might actually do. Because if one hair falls on one head in this hold — if your men storm these walls tomorrow and so much as bruise a gardener — I walk south to Coldford, and I introduce your empire's own arsenal to your empire's own soldiers, and I do it with the clear conscience of a man who warned you first."

Silence. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked; Odile listening, obviously, taking notes for the memoir.

"That's blackmail," Voss said.

"It's negotiation. Blackmail is when you want something for yourself." I spread my hands. "I want you to withdraw. Send to your commander and tell him the hold is fortified, supplied for months, defended by bearers whose gift you've seen demonstrated—" (this was a lie; Ilsa's sigil hadn't flared in days, and mine I kept wrapped and gloved) "—and that the cost of assault exceeds the value of the prize. Tell him whatever gets your fifty men marching south. In exchange, I give you my word: the stores at Coldford stay untouched, and I stay away from them, forever."

"And your word is worth something? You're a fugitive."

"My word is the only currency I've ever had, Captain. Eleven years at the queen's table. Do you know what happens to a taster who lies? Nothing, the first time. The second time there's no taster." I held his eyes. "I have swallowed death for that crown more times than you've drawn your sword. My word is the most expensive thing in this room, including the brandy."

He looked at the brandy. He looked at me. I watched him weigh it — the career risk of reporting up the chain that a servant had threatened the empire's poison supply versus the career risk of assaulting a hold and losing men to an enemy who'd promised nothing of the sort. Young officers survive by passing hard decisions upward. It's practically the job description.

"I'll send a rider tonight," he said finally. "But if my commander refuses—"

"Then tomorrow morning you'll learn what I learned years ago, Captain, which is that everyone at the table is eating poison. Some of us just taste it first."

* * *

He left under flag of truce with his rider dispatched and his dignity approximately intact, and Odile found me afterward on the garden terrace, where I was teaching Ilsa to identify which of the wintering herbs would keep a wound clean and which would stop a heart, on the principle that a child ought to know both sides of the garden.

"You threatened the crown's poison stores," Odile said, settling beside me on the cold stone bench. "Using knowledge they gave you. Knowledge they trusted you with."

"Yes."

"And you'll keep that word. Even if they refuse. Even if they come anyway."

"Yes."

"Then you've spent your old life for nothing." She said it without cruelty — Odile was incapable of cruelty in matters of trade; cruelty distorted prices. "They'll march regardless, Quintana. Commanders don't retreat because a servant made threats. You've bought a day, maybe two, and paid for it with the only thing that made you safe: the fact that they still believed you were theirs."

I turned Ilsa's sprig of crowshade over in my fingers. Even dormant, winter-stunted, it smelled faintly of the queen's banquet hall, of eleven years of swallowing on command and smiling after.

"No," I said. "I paid with the thing that made me *theirs*. What made me safe was knowing things they didn't know I knew." I handed the sprig to Ilsa, who took it solemnly, a small grave-eyed apprentice of the dangerous garden. "And now they know I know. Which means the price goes up every hour we sit here. I've bought us a day, Odile. Tomorrow I start spending faster."

"Spending what? You've given them your secrets. What's left?"

Below us, the campfires of fifty men pricked the dark like a spilled purse of embers. Above us, the first stars, indifferent and excellent company. And in my chest, under the ribs, under the scar tissue of a thousand small survivings, the sigil pulsed its slow silver patience — centuries of dead bearers' memories coiled in my blood, waiting, keeping their accounts.

I had told Wren once, before everything, that a taster's job was to die slowly so that others wouldn't have to die quickly. I had thought, walking north from the collapsed conclave, that I was done paying that particular bill.

"Everything," I said. "There's everything left."

From the south road, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a horn — not Voss's truce-horn, but the deeper call of reinforcements arriving. More than fifty now. The night had brought the empire's answer early.

Odile stood, unhurried, and picked up her crossbow. "Then we'd best decide what it buys," she said, "before morning decides for us."
