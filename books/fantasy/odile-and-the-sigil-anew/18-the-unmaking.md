## 18. The Unmaking

There is a particular arithmetic to being cornered, and I have always been good with numbers.

The count went like this: forty crown soldiers in the yard below, six on the wall, two trebuchets — crude things, more spite than engineering — and one inquisitor-commander whose patience had the texture of a man who had already written his report and was merely waiting for the world to agree with it. Against that: Odile Vane, one hold with walls older than the queen's grandmother, eleven mountain folk who had chosen memory over safety, a child of eight, and me.

I had spent my whole life learning what bodies could survive. I had never once learned how to make a body survive forty soldiers.

"You're doing the face," Ilsa said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're counting." She sat cross-legged on the granary floor, dust in her hair, her sleeve pushed up so the sigil showed — that silver branching mark, alive now, faintly luminous, like frost that had decided to grow inward instead of out. "You did it at the chapel too. Before you told me we were leaving."

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting."

She was right, which was the most irritating thing about her. Eight years old and she read me like one of Odile's ledgers. Somewhere along the forgotten roads she had stopped being cargo I was smuggling and become a person I was responsible for, and no one had asked my consent for either transition.

Below us, the commander's voice carried up through the shuttered window. He was making offers. They were generous offers, the way a knife is generous when it promises to only cut once.

*The child comes down. The rest of you disperse. The crown remembers its friends.*

The crown remembered its friends the way fire remembered wood.

* * *

Odile found me an hour past midnight, in the cellar where the keepers' last archive-stones were packed in wool like eggs someone might still hatch. She set down two cups of something that smelled like pine tar and courage and sat across from me without ceremony, because Odile Vane had never once done anything with ceremony in her life.

"The tunnel under the east wall collapses in three days," she said. "Maybe four if we're lucky, and we haven't been lucky since spring."

"I know."

"Then you know the arithmetic better than I do. We can't run them all through. We can't hold. And they won't starve us out because—" she gestured vaguely upward, at the hold, at the smoke already rising from our burned fields, "—they've started eating our winter for us. Clever bastards."

"They learned it from watching us," I said. "Smugglers invented siege economy."

"Smugglers also invented knowing when a shipment's lost." She turned one of the cups toward me. "Drink. You look like a man pricing his own funeral."

"I'm pricing everyone's funeral. It's a bigger market."

She almost smiled. Then she didn't, and what replaced it was worse — the flat, level look of a woman about to say the thing she'd climbed all these stairs to say.

"There's another way. The keepers told me about it years ago, back when there were enough of them to argue. The sigil isn't fixed to a body. It moves. A bearer who's willing can take another bearer's mark into themselves — carry both — and a bearer who's willing can let go entirely. Release it. All of it. Every stored year, every swallowed memory, out of the flesh and into the air."

"And then?"

"And then it's wind." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her something; I could see it in her hands. "No crown can arrest the wind. No army can burn it. No ledger can cross out its name, because it won't have one anymore."

I drank the pine-tar swill and let that settle in me like a stone dropped down a well.

Because here was the thing Odile wasn't saying, the thing underneath the thing, the way poison sits underneath sweetness in a badly made dish: the conclave had shown me what the sigil held. Centuries of bearers. Grandmothers and smuggler-kings and children who died with names in their mouths. The archive-magic wasn't a weapon — it was a *record*, the memory of a whole people the crown had spent three generations trying to erase, kept alive in silver marks on living wrists because paper burns and stone crumbles and flesh, apparently, endures even what it shouldn't.

And I could unmake all of it. In an afternoon. With a choice.

"It disperses," I said slowly. "It doesn't die?"

"That's the theology of it, yes. The keepers disagreed about the details. Keepers always disagree about details. It's how you know they're keepers." She leaned forward. "But here's the detail that matters, Edmund. It has to be willing. Both parts. The taking and the letting go. You can't force it, can't trick it, can't drink your way around it — and believe me, I asked whether you could, because I know how you think."

"You do know how I think."

"I've fenced with you for nine years. I know exactly how you think. Which is why I came down here before you thought of it yourself, because I wanted you to hear it from a friend first." Her voice roughened. "A willing bearer. There are two in this hold, and one of them is eight."

The well in my chest filled all the way up and overflowed.

"No," I said.

"She's too young to choose—"

"*No.*" It came out harder than I meant, and Ilsa was three floors up and asleep and couldn't hear it, and I said it again anyway, quieter, to myself, to the stones. "No. She doesn't get handed this. Not by me. Not by anyone. The whole rotten reason we're in this cellar is that people keep deciding what children owe to other people's wars."

Odile watched me for a long moment. Outside, a sentry called the hour, low and soft, the mountain way.

"So it's you," she said.

It wasn't a question. That was the worst part. She'd known before she opened the cellar door. Maybe she'd known at the conclave, when I stood in the sigil-light and wept over names I'd never heard, and she'd seen what I saw: that I was the only bearer in Thornfell who had ever wanted to put the thing down.

Every poison I'd ever survived had fed the mark. Twenty years of the crown's table, twenty years of swallowing death for wages, and my body had turned all of it into — this. Silver branches. Stored centuries. A library written in a language only suffering could read.

I had earned it by accident. Which meant, perhaps, I could give it away on purpose.

* * *

I went up to tell Ilsa at dawn, because I am not a coward about the important things, only about the small ones.

She was awake — she was always awake before me now, some instinct she'd developed on the roads, sleeping light as a bird — and she took one look at my face and pushed her sleeves up before I'd said a word.

"You're going to take it," she said. "Aren't you."

"How do you—"

"Because you do the face." She pointed at me, accusatory. "The counting face. But this time you're not counting soldiers."

I sat down next to her on the granary floor. Dust motes turned in the light from the arrow slit. Below, the enemy was quiet, which frightened me more than noise; sieges go quiet right before they stop being sieges.

"Ilsa. What I'm going to tell you, I need you to listen to all of it before you argue. You're good at arguing. I respect it. But listen first."

She folded her arms. Listened.

"The sigil in your arm holds memories. People who lived and loved and died carrying the same mark. If you keep it, the crown will hunt you until one of those memories is the last thing anyone remembers of you. If you give it to me — willingly, both of us willing, that's the rule, the old rule, the true one — then I can carry it for a little while. Long enough to let it go the right way. Into the wind. Where nobody owns it and nobody can burn it and nobody has to be hunted for it ever again."

"Does it hurt?"

That was her question. Not *will I lose it*, not *is it fair*, not *what do I get*. Eight years old, hunted half across a mountain range, village burned behind her, and her question was: *does it hurt.*

"A little," I said honestly, because lying to her had stopped working around the third week. "Taking it will feel like cold water filling up your arm. Letting it go — I don't know. Nobody living knows. The keepers said it feels like setting down something very heavy that you've carried so long you forgot it was heavy."

"And then it's gone? Forever?"

"From our arms, yes."

"But not gone-gone." She had the keeper logic already; I hadn't taught her that either. It grew in mountain children like moss. "Wind goes somewhere. Wind carries things. Seeds. Smells. My mother's bread, when she baked — the whole valley knew."

"Not gone-gone," I agreed. "Changed. From a secret into a story. Stories are harder to kill than magic. Believe me. I've tried to kill both."

She looked at her arm for a long time. The sigil pulsed faintly, silver against sun-browned skin, and I realized — with a pain I hadn't budgeted for — that she was saying goodbye to it. To the last thing her village had given her that the fire hadn't touched.

"Okay," she said. "But you have to do it today. Because yesterday the commander's man offered Odile gold for you, and she spat at him, and he laughed, and laughing men with trebuchets are worse than angry ones. They've stopped waiting."

* * *

We did it at noon, in the cellar, among the archive-stones, because if the sigil was going to leave the world I wanted it to leave from a room full of everything it had saved.

Odile stood witness. So did four of the mountain folk — the ferryman's widow, the two brothers who ran the high passes, and old Marta, who was ninety if she was a day and claimed to remember the last unmaking attempt, though Marta claimed to remember a great many things and roughly a third of them were true. We lit no candles. The sigils provided their own light.

Ilsa sat across from me, knees touching mine. Her arm lay palm-up between us, the mark bright as a vein of ore.

"Do I do anything?" she asked.

"You say yes."

"I already said yes."

"Say it again. Apparently the magic likes paperwork."

She grinned — gap-toothed, fearless, infuriating — and said, "Yes. I give it. Willingly. To Edmund, who counts things." And then, softer, just for me: "Take care of it. It's got people in it."

*I know,* I didn't say, because my throat had closed. *That's why it has to go free.*

I laid my hand over her mark and said yes too — yes to taking, yes to carrying, yes to whatever came after — and the cold came up through my wrist like water finding an old channel.

It did not hurt. That surprised me. It felt like recognition. Like a lock meeting the key cut from its own teeth. The silver branched out from my palm, up the veins of my forearm, and where it passed it left a coolness behind, a clarity, and in the clarity —

— snow on a shrine roof, three hundred years ago. A woman singing a harvest song in a language the crown had outlawed. A boy in a plague year carrying names door to door so the dead wouldn't be buried unknown. My grandmother, younger than I'd ever known her, laughing in a kitchen, telling a child — telling *me*, some version of me, some self I'd been before the palace scrubbed it out of me — *the gift is not a weapon, little wren-heart, it is a remembering, and they fear it because you cannot hang a remembering.*

Twenty centuries poured into my arm and settled there like sediment in still water, and I understood, finally, completely, why the crown feared it. Not because it was power. Because it was *evidence*.

Ilsa slumped against me, asleep or near it, her arm bare and ordinary, and old Marta caught her before I could, and Odile looked at me and said, "Now the second part. While you're still willing. Willingness curdles, Edmund. I've watched it curdle in better people than you."

"I know." I stood. My legs held, which seemed rude of them, given everything. "Open the roof. Or the highest window. Whatever this hold has that faces the sky."

* * *

They took me up to the wall walk, because the hold had no roof worth the name and Odile refused to punch a hole in architecture she'd defended for thirty years. The afternoon was clear. Wind came off the peaks in long, clean pulls, the kind of wind that had carried smuggler rumors and refugee songs over these passes for longer than any crown had existed.

Below, the soldiers stirred. They'd seen movement on the wall. The commander's voice rose, making new offers, and somewhere in the middle of them I stopped listening, because I was looking at my arm — at both marks now, mine and hers, braided together, centuries deep — and I was doing the counting face one last time.

Not soldiers. Not exits. Names.

I counted what I was holding. The singer. The boy with the plague-names. The grandmothers. The smuggler-kings. The keepers who'd died in the tunnels last week buying us our head start. Wren Halloway's brother, whose name I'd never learned but whose weight I'd carried anyway. Everyone the crown had crossed out of every ledger, still here, still warm, still *insisting*.

And I understood the last thing, the final lesson of twenty years of poisons: a body can hold anything. It's the holding that kills you. The only mercy any body ever gets is to open its hand.

"Edmund." Odile's voice, steady as a fence's handshake. "Whenever you're ready. And for what it's worth — it was worth it. All of it. The roads, the secrets, the whole crooked trade. Worth it."

"For what it's worth," I said, "you overcharged me in year three and I've never forgiven you."

"Noted. Now jump off the cliff of your own personality and finish this."

I raised my arm into the wind.

I did not say a spell. There wasn't one. The keepers had been clear: willingness was the whole liturgy. So I said the only true thing I had, quietly, into the wind, where it belonged — *I carried this long enough; it belongs to no one; take it where crowns can't follow* — and I opened my hand the way you open a fist around a bird you've been gripping too tight, and I let go.

The silver lifted out of me like heat-shimmer off summer stone.

For one heartbeat it held its shape — the branch-work, the braid of two lifetimes and twenty centuries, hanging in the air above the wall like a signature written in frost — and then the wind took it, and it came apart, and it *went*. Not dying. Dispersing. I felt it leave the way you feel a fever break: the sudden absence of a weight you'd mistaken for your own skeleton. Threads of light unwound over the yard, over the soldiers, over the trebuchets and the burned fields and the far white shoulders of Thornfell, thinner and thinner, everywhere and nowhere, until there was nothing left to see and everything left to imagine.

The soldiers below had gone silent. Some of them made signs against evil. One man fell to his knees, and I heard him say, bewildered, "It went *through* me," and I thought: yes. It went through all of us. It's been going through us the whole time. That was the entire secret.

The sigil-light faded from my skin. My arm was just an arm. Twenty years of scars and swallowed deaths and one impossible inheritance, and now — empty. Lighter than I had been since I was a child in my grandmother's kitchen, before I knew what tongues were for, before the palace taught me that survival was a profession.

Ilsa woke in Marta's arms downstairs, later, and demanded to know if it worked, and I told her the truth: ask the wind. She frowned at me for a full ten seconds and then nodded, satisfied, because that was apparently an acceptable answer when you were eight and mountain-raised, and I envied her that with my whole emptied heart.

The secret was dead. The people were not. That had been the trade, and I would make it again, and again, and again, in every life I might have had.

* * *

The commander did not attack that evening. I like to think the wind unsettled him. I know for a fact the missing sigils unsettled his report — you cannot execute a purge against a gift that no longer exists, and the paperwork alone would take a season.

What happened instead, near dusk, was stranger.

A single rider came up the switchback road under a parley flag, and Odile squinted at the figure through the wall-slit and made a sound I had never heard her make — half laugh, half grief, all disbelief.

"That's the Halloway woman," she said. "Alone. Unarmed." A pause. "Edmund. She's carrying a ledger, and she's holding it out in front of her like a confession."

I went down to the gate.

Wren Halloway stood in the last light with her inquisitor's coat unbuttoned and her rank pinned to the ground beside her boot like something she'd dug out of herself, and the ledger in her hands was open to a page I could read upside down from ten feet away, because I had spent my life reading things upside down.

Her brother's name. Crossed out. In the commander's own hand.

"Edmund," she said, and her voice was not the inquisitor's voice, and it was not quite the girl from the taster's quarters either, but something new being built out of both, live and unfinished. "I need to show you what I found. And then I need you to help me bury them with it."
