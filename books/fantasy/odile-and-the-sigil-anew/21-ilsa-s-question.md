## 21. Ilsa's Question

Odile had not exaggerated. She never did — exaggeration costs a fence money, and Odile Vane could smell a wasted word the way I could once smell arsenic.

Ilsa was sitting on my grandmother's stool.

That was the first thing I noticed, before her face, before the question already loaded behind it like a crossbow. The stool had come with me out of the capital in pieces, wrapped in sacking at the bottom of a grain shipment, because a man who tastes poison for a living learns early that you cannot keep anything you love in plain sight. It was a small thing, three-legged, older than me, older than my mother, worn pale where two generations of hands had gripped its edge. My grandmother had sat on it to shell peas and tell lies about the sigil — protective lies, the kind you tell a child so the child keeps breathing.

And now an eight-year-old girl sat on it, swinging her feet, looking at me like I owed her something.

Which, as it turned out, I did.

"Odile says your grandmother was a keeper," Ilsa said. "A real one. Before."

"Odile says a lot of things," I said. "Most of them while charging me for them."

"She said you'd try to be clever." Ilsa folded her arms, a gesture so precisely modeled on Odile that I nearly laughed. "She said when I got tired of waiting, I should say *the sigil is gone but the question isn't*."

"That sounds like Odile."

"It worked, didn't it?"

I set down the axe by the door, hung up my coat, and gave in. There was no point pretending I hadn't seen this coming. Children who survive burning villages do not forget what they survived; they carry it around inside them like a stone in a boot, and eventually they sit you down and make you look at it.

Supper was still an hour off. The kitchen smelled of onions and woodsmoke and the particular iron-and-lichen scent of Vane's Hold itself, which I had decided after three days was simply what safety smelled like when it had been left unwashed too long. Outside, the light was going gold on the pass, the way it did in the last week before true winter closed the high roads. Inside, a child waited with her stone in her boot.

"All right," I said, and pulled up the second stool — mine, the ugly one. "Ask."

Ilsa looked down at her own wrist, where the sigil had been. The scar there was faint now, a ghost of silver, fading since the unmaking the way a bruise fades, or a name fades from a ledger nobody updates. She touched it with one finger, the way she'd been touching it all week, checking.

"When it was gone," she said slowly, choosing words the way she chose footing on scree, "when you took it out of me and let the wind have it — where did the people go? The ones inside it. The memories. The keeper women and the men whose names were crossed out and—" Her voice caught, steadied. She was practiced at catching it; that practice made my chest hurt worse than any catch would have. "My mother. Was she in there? And did we lose her twice?"

There it was. Not *where does magic go*, not *will I get powers back*. A child's question shaped exactly like an old woman's grief.

I had spent four days dreading this conversation, and in those four days I had learned something about dread: it is almost always worse than the room it keeps you out of. The room, it turned out, was warm and smelled of onions and had a child in it who deserved better than a lie and better than a shrug.

"Sit properly," I said, because my grandmother used to say it, and because some rituals hold you up when answers can't. "This takes telling."

* * *

Here is what I knew, and what I had learned beneath Thornfell, in the conclave caverns lit by sigil-light, from the last keepers of a memory the crown had spent three hundred years calling treason:

The sigil-gift was never a weapon. That was the crown's story, and the crown told it loudly enough that even the persecuted half-believed it. No. The gift was a ledger. Every bearer who died carrying it passed their memories into it — not their souls, whatever the priests said, but their *knowing*. What they saw. What they loved. What was done to them. The sigil was an archive written in living bodies, and the purge, the burnings, the registries — none of it was ever about stopping a weapon.

It was about destroying evidence.

When I let the sigil disperse into the wind above Thornfell, I had felt it go. All of it. Centuries of stored remembering loosening out of my body like breath in cold air. I had chosen that, deliberately, gladly, because the alternative was letting a queen or a rebellion or an inquisitor's ambition own it. A secret that valuable gets people killed. I had watched it happen from both sides of the hunt.

But Ilsa hadn't asked whether it had been the right choice. She'd asked if her mother had been in there. And whether losing the archive meant losing her again.

So I did the only honest thing available to me.

"I don't know," I said. "I'll start there, because you've earned honesty and everyone else will give you comfort instead. When the wind took it, I couldn't read it anymore. Whatever was written in that silver — your mother's face, my grandmother's voice, the names Wren Halloway keeps in her ledger of the dead — I couldn't tell you if it went somewhere, or became something, or simply stopped being held."

Ilsa's chin came up. She wasn't going to cry. She was going to be furious, and she had every right.

"But," I said, and held up one finger before the fury could land, "here is the part the keepers understood, and the crown never did, and it's the part I'm going to spend the rest of my life making sure you understand too. The sigil was never the memory. It was only the *vault*. The memory lived in people. It always did. Your mother carried it. My grandmother carried it. Every burned village carried it, and every survivor who walked out of a burning village carrying a child carried it double."

I leaned forward on the ugly stool.

"The vault is broken. Fine. Vaults break. But you know what a vault is for? Keeping things safe from fire and thieves. And you know what the crown discovered, over three hundred years of trying? You cannot burn what lives in mouths. They killed the bearers and the memory kept walking. They burned the archives and grandmothers kept telling stories in kitchens exactly like this one, in voices exactly low enough. That's why the purge failed before it ever started, and why it will fail again. Memory doesn't need magic, Ilsa. Magic needed *it*."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. Her feet had stopped swinging.

"So she's not gone," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"She's not gone," I said. "She's just moved house. Into you. Which means—" and here I had to stop for a second myself, because grief will ambush you through the strangest doors, "—which means the job of keeping her is yours now. And it turns out that's a job you don't need silver scars to do."

"How?"

"You ask questions," I said. "You remember answers. And when someone asks you — someday, some child on some stool — you tell it true."

* * *

We started that same evening, because children do not wait for curriculum and neither, apparently, do I.

After supper — Odile's stew, heavy on barley, light on meat, priced accordingly — I cleared the table and Ilsa dragged her stool close to the fire and said, "Tell me about her. Your grandmother."

"Mara," I said. "Her name was Mara. Named for her own grandmother, which is how keeper families did it — names went backward down the line like rivers running uphill. She shelled peas on that stool you're stealing and lied to me beautifully for years. Told me the mark on her wrist was a birthmark. Told me the songs she hummed were just songs. Told me never to taste anything twice, because the first taste tells you if it's poison and the second taste tells the poison where you live."

Ilsa giggled. Good. Grief needs laughter mixed in or it sets like concrete.

"She was wrong about that last one, by the way. Or lying. Probably lying. The second taste is how you learn the poisons, and knowing poisons is how I stayed alive long enough to ruin everything." I fed the fire a split log. "But here's a true thing she told me, late, near the end, when she'd stopped bothering with lies because I'd swallowed enough of the palace's secrets to be trusted with hers. She said: *Edmund, they think our gift was power. It was patience. We were the people who agreed to remember, and remembering is slow work.*"

"What did she remember?" Ilsa asked. "Specific things. Not lessons. Things."

Children cut straight through abstraction the way a blade goes through broth. Lessons are for adults; adults need lessons because we've learned to dodge. Ilsa wanted the actual dead woman, the actual life.

"All right. Specific things." I closed my eyes. Some of this came from the weeks the sigil had lived in me, flashes of borrowed memory I'd caught like sparks off a grinder — and some of it was simply mine, a boy's ordinary love for an extraordinary old woman. By then I couldn't always tell the difference, and I had decided that was fine. An inheritance doesn't come with receipts.

"She remembered the smell of the conclave caverns — wet stone and tallow and, underneath it, rosemary, because the keepers burned rosemary when they recorded a death, so that the smell of grief would never be confused with the smell of fear. She remembered a man named Aldric Fen who could hold a whole harvest in his head — every field, every yield, every debt — and who starved one winter giving grain away and laughing about it. She remembered the night the first registry burned, and how the keepers' response was not to fight but to scatter, deliberately, like seeds, so that no single blow could ever take all of them. She remembered being afraid for sixty years, Edmund—"

"That's you."

"That's me borrowing her voice, yes. She said it to me and now I say it to you and someday you'll say it to somebody, and that sentence will have been alive for over a century without a single drop of magic in it." I opened my eyes. "She also remembered that honey spoils slower if you never dip a wet spoon, which has nothing to do with anything except that she'd want it preserved too."

Ilsa laughed again, then sobered with the terrible quickness children have, grief and joy sharing a body without negotiation.

"My mother sang," she said. "Not keeper songs. Just — market songs. Silly ones. About a pig that wouldn't die." Her hand found her wrist again. "If I sing them, is that the same as what the keepers did?"

"It's smaller," I said. "It's exactly the same."

* * *

We built it into a ritual without naming it a ritual, because naming things gives the crown a handle to grab.

Every evening after supper, firelight, two stools. Ilsa asked. I answered — and when I didn't know, I said so plainly, and then we invented nothing and lost nothing, we simply marked the hole: *unknown, ask Odile, ask the keepers who survived the tunnels, ask Wren Halloway when she comes through in spring.* Because Wren would come through. A woman who kept a ledger of every name she'd failed had become, whether she'd admit it yet or not, the best historian the mountain folk had. Guilt, properly aimed, makes an excellent archivist.

And then Ilsa would teach me. That was her idea, arrived at on the fourth evening with the sudden ferocity of someone who has located a grievance and intends to farm it.

"You got my mother's stories secondhand from me," she said, "and I got your grandmother's secondhand from you, and that's how mistakes happen. So I tell them back. Every night. And you correct me if I get it wrong."

"That's—" I stopped. "That's exactly what the keepers did. Two bearers reciting to each other. Verification. You've reinvented a three-hundred-year-old discipline, Ilsa, and I'd charge Odile for the lesson if she weren't listening at the door."

"I am NOT," said the door.

Ilsa grinned at me, gap-toothed, firelit, eight years old and carrying more dead than most gravediggers. And I thought about my grandmother on that same stool, telling her beautiful lies, doing the slow patient work of agreeing to remember, and I understood at last what she'd actually left me. Not the gift. The gift was gone, scattered on the wind above Thornfell, and good riddance to it — it had been nothing but a vault, and vaults attract thieves.

What she'd left me was the *work*. The nightly, stubborn, unhurried work of saying names out loud in kitchens where the crown couldn't hear.

"Again," I said. "From the top. The pig that wouldn't die."

Ilsa took a breath, sat up straight on my grandmother's stool, and began — and outside, far up the pass, the first snow of the season came down soft and thick over the roads, filling in every track, erasing every path, covering everything except what we carried, which was, as it turned out, the only cargo that mattered.

And on the seventh evening, boots crunched in the yard, and a knock came at the hold's door — a knock I knew, because I'd heard it across twenty years and one war, and Ilsa looked up mid-verse and whispered, "Is that the ledger lady?"
