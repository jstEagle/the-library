## 22. The Road Goes On

*Edmund*

The thaw came late that year, the way it always does in Thornfell — grudgingly, like a debtors' argument settled in installments. One morning the snow on Vane's Hold roofline went soft and gray; two mornings later the eaves were dripping in a rhythm Odile complained was keeping her books awake at night. By the third morning, there were people at the gate.

Refugees. That word had a taste to it now, one I knew better than any poison I'd ever swallowed for the crown: bitter at the back of the throat, with an aftertaste of iron. They came up from the lowlands in ones and twos and small ragged processions — families who had registered and regretted it, families who had never been given the chance, a cooper whose brother had been taken by Halloway's predecessor and who had finally worked up the courage to run. They came because word travels in the mountains the way water does, finding every crack, and somewhere in those cracks it had been said: *Vane's Hold takes them in. There's a taster there who knows the roads.*

I could have corrected the rumor. I could have said I wasn't a guide anymore, that my tasting days were done, that I was just a woman with a scarred wrist and no sigil and no office and no particular talent beyond knowing which mushrooms lie. Instead I found myself standing in Odile's courtyard with a leather satchel of dried plums and a map drawn nowhere but in my head, looking at eleven strangers who all seemed to have decided, without consulting me, that I was their way over the passes.

"You could say no," Odile said beside me, arms crossed, watching me watch them.

"I could."

"You won't."

"I won't," I agreed. "It's a character flaw. You should write it down in your ledger, under *assets*."

She snorted, which from Odile is roughly equivalent to a sonnet of affection, and went inside to count what we could spare.

One last time, she'd said when I told her. One last crossing, and then I'd stay put through summer like a sensible person. I believed myself when I said it. That's the trick of roads — they don't argue with you. They just wait.

* * *

*Wren*

I arrived at Vane's Hold on foot, which felt appropriate. A year ago I would have arrived with twenty riders and a writ. Six months ago I couldn't have arrived at all — I'd have been arrested on sight, or worse, welcomed, which in some company is worse.

They let me stand at the gate for most of an hour before anyone spoke to me. I understood. I had burned villages. I had kept a ledger of names, and I had learned, too late, what the crossings-out meant. The fact that I had eventually turned that ledger against my own commander before the crown's tribunal — the fact that it cost me my rank, my pension, and every friend I'd made in the service — none of it unburned anything. Justice, I had discovered, is not a scale that balances. It is a stone you carry, and you carry it because putting it down would mean pretending you never picked it up.

A girl came out eventually. Small, dark-eyed, eight going on ancient, with a faded mark on her wrist where a sigil used to live. She looked me up and down with the frank appraisal of someone who has already survived the worst thing that will ever happen to her and finds everything since mildly boring.

"You're the inquisitor," she said.

"I was."

"Edmund says you're a free woman now." She said *free woman* the way children repeat words they've been told are important, testing the weight of them. "Edmund also says you snore."

"I do not snore."

"That's what Edmund said you'd say." She turned and walked back toward the hold, then paused. "There's soup. If you're staying."

I stayed. It was, I reflected later, the first order I'd followed in a year that hadn't come with a writ attached, and it was the only one I'd ever obeyed gladly.

* * *

*Edmund*

Wren Halloway walking beside me up a smuggler's trail was such an absurdity that I spent the first mile waiting for the mountains themselves to object. They didn't. Mountains, I've found, are less sentimental than people. Stone doesn't care who carries you over it.

We had nineteen souls this crossing. The cooper and his family. Two sisters who'd been seamstresses to a minor lord until he decided registration was a formality he could skip on their behalf. An old man named Perrin who claimed, loudly and often, that he'd once dined with the queen, a claim I did not dispute because I had tasted her food for nine years and could confirm the queen would dine with anyone if the alternative was eating alone. And Ilsa, who had refused to be left behind with a ferocity that ended the discussion before it began.

"She needs to see it," Wren had said to me the night before we left, by which she meant the passes, the paths, the whole geography of escape that I'd spent a decade memorizing and a year teaching. "Not be told about it. See it."

"She's eight."

"I was seven when my father first took me into the low courts," Wren said. "You were younger than that when your grandmother started teaching you which poisons smell like almonds and which ones smell like nothing at all." She looked at me across the fire. "Nobody asks children whether they're ready for the world, Edmund. We only decide afterward whether to tell them the truth about it."

So Ilsa came, walking at the front of the line more often than the back, asking questions with the tireless appetite of her age. Why did the path bend here? Because the rock shears in wet weather. Why do we cross the stream three times? Because following it looks natural to a tracker and three crossings look like indecision. Why did you learn all this? And there I usually found something urgent to attend to among the pack straps.

But once, high on the shoulder of the pass where the wind strips everything down to essentials, I answered her.

"Because I wanted a quiet life," I said. "And it turns out quiet isn't a place. It's a thing you make for other people so they can stop running long enough to breathe."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "That's not quiet, though. That's work."

"Yes," I said. "Welcome to adulthood. The pay is terrible."

* * *

*Wren*

On the fourth day we crossed the high shelf above the Greywater valley, and Edmund called a rest because the view demanded it, the way certain views do — not as scenery but as testimony.

Below us the snowmelt ran silver through a valley just beginning to green. Somewhere down there, a year ago, I had stood with a column of riders and watched a village burn, and I had written the names of its people in my ledger because writing them was the only protest available to me, and I had told myself that order prevented worse bloodshed. I had needed very badly to believe it. Belief, I've learned, is a room you furnish yourself, and you can furnish it so comfortably that you forget there's a door.

"What are you thinking?" Edmund asked. They have a way of asking questions that makes evasion feel like bad manners rather than privacy.

"I'm thinking that I know exactly where we are," I said. "Within half a mile. Maybe less."

Edmund nodded slowly. They'd known, of course. They'd known since we set out which route skirted the burned places and which didn't, and they'd chosen this one anyway, and I understood that too. You don't heal by avoiding the wound. You heal by learning to look at it without flinching.

"The registry office in Thornfell keeps records," I said. "Names, dates, dispositions. I'm going to spend the summer copying them. All of them. Every name that came through my hands and everyone else's."

"And then?"

"And then I'm going to take them to the families. However many are left. Someone should say the names out loud, in the right places, to the right people. Not as evidence." I looked down at the valley. "As memory. The keepers' magic held memory for centuries and the crown destroyed it, and now there's just — us. Mouths and paper. It seems insufficient."

"It is insufficient," Edmund said. "Do it anyway."

Below us, Ilsa was showing Perrin how to skip a stone across a meltwater pool, and failing, and laughing, and trying again. The stones sank. She threw another. I thought: that's the whole discipline of it, really. The stones sink and you throw another. The names are heavy and you say them anyway. The road goes on because someone walks it.

* * *

*Edmund*

We brought them over on the ninth day, all nineteen, through weather that behaved itself out of what I can only assume was professional courtesy. On the far side, past the last ridge, the lowland road ran soft and green toward towns that had begun, cautiously, shamefacedly, to open their doors again now that the tribunals were done and the worst of the inquisitors were answering for their own ledgers.

The cooper shook my hand until I feared for the hand. The sisters kissed Wren on both cheeks, which visibly astonished her; she stood there afterward looking like a woman who has been handed something fragile and doesn't trust herself to hold it. Perrin embraced me, announced that he had dined with the queen, and set off down the road singing something off-key and defiant.

And then it was the three of us, and the mountain behind us, and the long walk home.

Ilsa walked between us, holding a hand of each, swinging them like a child half her age, which is to say like a child her actual age, permitted at last to be one. Halfway down the first slope she stopped and pointed back at the pass, where evening light was pooling gold in the saddles between peaks.

"Will you bring more people next time?" she asked me.

"No," I said. "I'm retired. Ask anyone."

"Odile says you'll bring more people."

"Odile," I said, "talks too much."

"Odile says you talk too much."

Between us, Wren laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind I hadn't heard from her since we were children stealing honey cakes from the taster's quarters, back before the crown got its hands on both of us and bent us into tools. Hearing it, I understood something I'd been circling all year: that we had both survived being weapons, and that survival, shared, turns into something else. Not forgiveness — that was still a road under construction, and might be for years. But companionship. Company on the walk.

"So," Wren said, as we started down. "Summer. The registries. I'll need help reading the old hands. Half those clerks wrote like spiders drowning in ink."

"You're asking me to spend my retirement squinting at government paperwork."

"I'm asking you to help me say the names."

I looked at the road ahead — the lowland road, then the mountain road, then all the unnamed tracks between, the ones that existed only in the memory of people who'd walked them. The roads remember. My grandmother used to say that, back when I thought it was a figure of speech. Now I knew it was a job description.

"One condition," I said. "When we get to the coast towns, someone has to teach me the fish. I've tasted for queens. I've never once tasted for fishermen, and I suspect they eat better."

"It's a deal," Wren said.

"Also I want it noted that I'm doing this under protest."

"Noted," said Ilsa solemnly, on my other side. "Odile will want it in the ledger."

And we went down together, the three of us, into the green and waking world, while behind us the pass stood open in the evening light — not a wound, not a wall, just a road, waiting, the way roads do, for whoever comes next.
