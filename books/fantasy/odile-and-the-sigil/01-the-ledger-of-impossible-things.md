## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There were three rules to keeping the ledgers of the Vesper Guild, and Quentin Zhou had written two of them herself.

The first was that every entry be made in the same hour it occurred, in ink mixed that same morning, so that no transaction could be backdated by wish or by lie. The second was that the ledger-keeper sign her initials at the foot of each page before the wax cooled on the day's seal — not because anyone doubted her arithmetic, but because a ledger without a witness was only a diary, and diaries could be burned. The third rule had come down from the first Guildmaster Vesper, three hundred years dead: *the gate must never open twice.* That one she had not written. That one she had merely learned to obey, the way one learns not to touch a stove, or to look too long at the sealed door beneath the counting floor.

She had kept the guild's books for nine years, and in nine years she had never once found an error.

This was not pride, though others mistook it for that. It was simply the shape of her mind. Numbers went where they were put. A debt entered on the left would remain on the left through fire, flood, and the small treacheries of merchants, and when it moved to the right — paid, forgiven, forgiven *wrongly* — the movement left a trail as legible as footprints in snow. Quentin trusted nothing else in the world the way she trusted a well-kept column. People lied with their faces and their voices and even their kindnesses; ink only lied if a hand made it lie, and a hand always trembled somewhere.

So on the evening of the ninth of Frostmonth, when she found the error, she did not at first believe it was an error.

* * *

The counting room of the Vesper Guild occupied the top floor of the ledger-house, under a roof of slate so old it had gone soft at the edges, green with lichen in summer, silver-rimed in winter like now. Four tall windows faced the harbor, where the fishing fleet rode at anchor in a sea the color of unpolished pewter. The light came up off the water at this hour, late afternoon in the deep of winter, and laid long pale bars across the desks — eleven of them, ten empty, hers nearest the stove. The other clerks had gone home to their suppers an hour past. The guild did not require her to stay. The guild had never once required her to do anything, which was itself a kind of requirement, and she felt its weight most keenly in the quiet hours, when the building settled around its secrets like an old man settling around his pains.

The winter accounts lay open before her in their prescribed order: trade first, then tithe, then tolls, then the category the clerks called *irregulars* and the founding documents called, in a hand more cautious than any used today, *transactions of the second gate.*

The Vesper Guild traded in impossible things.

That sentence, spoken aloud in the market square, would have earned a listener a fine or a cell, depending on the magistrate's mood, but within these walls it was simply the business. A widow's letter delivered to a husband drowned forty years — impossible, and therefore guild work. A key cut to fit a lock that had never existed. Snow gathered from a winter that had not yet fallen. The guild did not make such things; the guild *brokered* them, which is a humbler and a darker trade, standing between those who wished and whatever it was on the far side of the gate that granted. The guild took its commission in coin where coin was offered, and in stranger currencies where it was not. All of it was recorded. All of it was balanced. For three hundred years the columns had come out even at the bottom of every page, because the world, however strange, was still a world that added up.

Quentin ran her finger down the irregulars and stopped at line forty-one.

The entry was dated the third of Frostmonth, six days past. It recorded a prayer received and answered — the first such entry in the irregulars since before her time, since before Oren Vesper's time, since a year whose ledger she had seen only once, in the forbidden dark of the archive, and had thought about for a month afterward. The commodity column read, in the terse cipher of the trade: *one petition, granted in full.* The consideration column read: *memory, unspecified, weight assessed at collection.* And in the final column, where the verifying initials of the recording clerk belonged, stood two letters she knew better than her own reflection:

Q.Z.

She sat back. The stove ticked. Out on the harbor a bell buoy clanged twice against something unseen, and the sound came through the glass thin as thread.

She had not recorded that transaction.

She remembered the sixth of Frostmonth perfectly — she remembered every working day of nine years with the undramatic completeness of a woman who had trained her memory the way other women trained needlework. On the sixth she had risen before dawn, mended the stove's flue, audited the salt-toll receipts, eaten lunch standing at the window because the herring boats were coming in with their sails the color of dirty ice, and spent the afternoon reconciling the tolls against the harbormaster's book, finding a discrepancy of four coppers that had turned out to be a smudged four, not a stolen one. She had signed the day's page at dusk. She had gone to bed. At no point on the sixth of Frostmonth had she sat at this desk and entered a prayer into the irregulars.

And yet there it was, in her own handwriting.

Not *like* her handwriting. It *was* her handwriting — the small severe numerals, the way her sevens carried a slight forward lean like a person hurrying, the crossbar of the Q that she made a fraction too long because a teacher in another life had told her a Q should look like it was holding its tail up out of the mud. No forger alive could have reproduced it. She had tried, years ago, as an exercise, to forge her own hand and failed; the forgery had been accurate and dead, a photograph of a living thing, while the true strokes carried some small animal warmth that could not be counterfeited. The letters on line forty-one were warm. They were hers.

She checked the ink. Rule the first: ink mixed the same morning as the entry. She tilted the page to the window's last light and saw what she had feared and half expected — the iron-gall brown of her own batch, the one she had mixed on the sixth, the one with the faint ash-gray undertone from the well water that month. Not store ink. Not old ink. Ink from her own pot, dried six days, sitting on a line she had never written.

Quentin Zhou did not believe in omens. She believed in discrepancies. An omen asked you to feel your way to a meaning; a discrepancy asked you to count. So she counted.

She pulled the day-book for the third of Frostmonth — the clerk's rough log, kept in pencil, transcribed nightly into the fair ledger. The third's pages recorded a routine day: two salt contracts, a dispute over a love-token pawned twice, a request from a chandler for *rain, one barrel, off-season,* which the guild had declined per policy because weather was crown business. Nothing about prayers. Nothing about petitions. The gate log — the slim volume kept by whoever drew door-duty, though the gate below had not been opened in her tenure, not once, not a crack — showed no activity on the third. The seal on the counting-floor hatch was intact; she had walked over it that very morning and felt, as she always felt, the faint wrongness of the air above it, like the silence above a held breath.

Every record said: nothing happened on the third of Frostmonth.

Except the fair ledger, in her own hand, said otherwise.

* * *

She worked until full dark, and she did not light the lamp, because lamplight invited the eyes of passing clerks and she wanted no witnesses yet, not even innocent ones. She verified the entry against the ledger's own internal logic. Page 118, lines one through forty, all in her hand, all dated the third, all ordinary. Line forty-one followed them without pause, the same pressure of nib, the same spacing, as if it had been written in the same sitting — as if she had sat down on the evening of the third and recorded a prayer she had never heard, from a petitioner she had never met, granted by a gate that had not opened.

The consideration column troubled her most. *Memory, unspecified, weight assessed at collection.* That phrasing was archaic. It appeared in ledgers from two centuries past, when the guild's brokers had dealt in considerations the modern clerks preferred not to name aloud. Oren Vesper himself, in thirty years as Guildmaster, had never entered such a line. The guild's current practice was to decline transactions whose price could not be fixed in advance — it was practically doctrine, *no unfixed weights,* and Quentin had enforced it with the zeal of the converted. Someone had reached back across two hundred years of house style to write that line. Or something had.

She turned to the index at the back of the volume, where each entry was summarized in a single word. Trade entries said *salt* or *cloth* or *bone.* Tolls said *toll.* Line forty-one said *prayer,* and beneath it, in the margin, in the same impossible hand, someone had written a number.

Nine.

Just the numeral, alone, small, tucked against the ruled edge like a footnote. Quentin stared at it for a long time. Nine years since she had come to this house. Nine winters since the plague winter she did not remember — and that absence itself was a fact she had audited many times in the privacy of her own skull, turning it over like a coin with one face worn smooth. Everyone had losses from that winter. Hers was simply total, a clean zero where a season should have been, and she had learned to live on the far side of it the way one lives on the far side of a river, without wading back to check the depth.

But she did not write *nine* in margins. She had never written a marginal note in her life. Margins were where doubt lived, and her whole discipline was built on keeping doubt out of the columns.

The stove had gone cold. She hadn't noticed. Her hands, she realized, were cold too, and had been for some time, and she was still touching the page, her fingertip resting just beside the initials Q.Z., not quite on them, as though contact might confirm something she was not ready to confirm.

Outside, the city was lighting itself against the dark, window by window, and somewhere beyond the rooftops the great bell of the cathedral rang the hour — six, muffled by snow-cloud, each stroke arriving a half-beat late as though the sound itself were reluctant. In the streets people were saying the thing people had begun saying that week, in fishmarkets and shrine-nooks and the warm corners of taverns: that prayers were being answered again. That old Marla Candery had lit a candle for her son's fever and the fever broke by morning. That the shrines that had stood silent for a hundred years were, in the phrase the fishwives favored, *waking up.* Quentin had noted the rumor in the guild's intelligence file with the flat annotation *unverified, folk enthusiasm, watch for fraud.* Fraud was the rational explanation. Fraud was almost always the explanation. The guild existed precisely because the gods had gone quiet a century ago and left a market gap, and the guild filled it honestly, or as honestly as a trade in impossibilities permitted.

She looked at line forty-one again.

A prayer, received and answered. Recorded in her hand, in her ink, on a day she could account for to the minute — except for this. Weight assessed at collection. A memory, taken from someone, priced after the taking. And a nine in the margin, in her writing, meaning nothing she could name and everything she feared.

Quentin closed the ledger. She locked it in the iron cabinet with the day's other volumes, turned the key, and stood a moment in the dark counting room with her hand flat on the cabinet's cold door, listening to the building. Beneath her feet, down through two floors of stone and one floor of sealed doors, the gate slept the way it had slept for nine years — silently, supposedly, absolutely.

But as she stood there, in the last moment before she lit the lamp and became again the composed and meticulous keeper of the Vesper accounts, Quentin Zhou heard — or imagined she heard, and the distinction mattered enormously, and she refused, for one more night, to decide which it was — a sound rising through the floor.

Not a rumble. Not machinery. The gate made no sounds; that was doctrine.

It was more like a hum. More like a voice humming, low and patient, the way a woman hums at a cradle. And though she would deny it in the morning, and audit the sound into a pipe, a draft, a settling joist, some flaw in the old house and not in the world — in that dark, with her palm on the iron and the city ringing its reluctant bells above her, Quentin could have sworn the hum was shaped like a name.

Her own.
