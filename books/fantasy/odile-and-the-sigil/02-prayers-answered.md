## 2. Prayers, Answered

The rumor reached the ledger-house the way most rumors did: sideways, in fragments, carried on the coats of men who came to pawn things they could not afford to lose.

Quentin heard it first from a tanner's apprentice who had come in with a bolt of dyed leather and a story he clearly thought was worth more than the goods. The gods were answering prayers again, he said. Down in the Saltrow, a woman had prayed for rain for her husband's nets, and it had rained — only over her roof, only for an hour, silver as coin. A lamplighter had prayed his sick daughter well and woken to find her fever broken and a year missing from his own memory, though he didn't mind, he said, he *didn't mind*, because what was a year against a child.

Quentin recorded the leather, counted out the apprentice's coin, and said nothing. But she wrote the rumor down that night in her private book — the thin one bound in grey calfskin that no one else in the guild had ever seen — because Quentin Zhou had learned long ago that the difference between a rumor and an omen was simply whether anyone had bothered to enter it into evidence.

*Prayers answered,* she wrote. *First instance: Saltrow, rain. Second: lamplighter, fever, cost one year.*

The guild had rules about impossible things, and rule the first was this: nothing is given. Everything is traded. If prayers were being answered after a hundred years of silence, then someone was doing the answering, and someone was doing the paying, and Quentin wanted very much to know which column those entries belonged in.

* * *

By the third day the city had gone strange with hope.

She saw it in the queues outside the shrines — the old shrines, the ones that had stood cold since before she was born, their altars furred with dust and pigeon filth, now scrubbed by volunteers who worked with the frantic tenderness of people afraid the miracle would notice them slacking. She saw it in the way strangers touched each other in the street, brief superstitious brushes of the sleeve, as if luck were contagious. She saw it in the guild-house itself, where Oren Vesper stood at the tall window of his office with his hands folded behind his back, watching the smoke rise from a hundred newly lit votive candles, and said, without turning around:

"Have you been listening to the talk, Mistress Zhou?"

"I've been recording it," Quentin said. "That's not the same as listening."

Oren smiled at the window. He was a narrow man, dry as a winter reed, with the patient face of someone who had spent fifty years waiting for other people to finish speaking. "And what does your ledger say?"

"It says the Vesper Guild trades in impossible things through a gate that must never open twice," Quentin said. "It says we have held that monopoly for two hundred years by being the only ones who understood the arithmetic. It says that if prayers are being answered city-wide, free of charge, then either the arithmetic has changed—" she paused, "—or someone is keeping books we can't see."

Oren turned then. His eyes were pale, the color of watered milk, and they rested on her with an attention that always felt slightly too warm, like standing near a fire that was considering you. "You think there's a price."

"There's always a price."

"And yet the lamplighter doesn't miss his year. And the fishwife in Saltrow—" he stopped himself, delicately, the way a man stops himself from stepping on a crack.

Quentin went still. "What fishwife?"

"The one whose husband came back." Oren unfolded his hands. "I assumed you'd know. You know everything that moves through this city's ledgers." He took an envelope from his desk — good paper, guild paper — and held it out. "Her name is Maren Ostwick. Widow, eleven years. Her husband Toma drowned off the Gullspit in the spring tide of '91. Last Tuesday evening, he walked into her kitchen, dripping wet, and sat down to supper." A beat. "Go and see what it cost her. Write me a report. Discreetly."

Quentin took the envelope. It was heavier than paper should be, or perhaps that was only her hands. "And if the price turns out to be something the guild should be worried about?"

Oren returned to the window. Below, the candle-smoke climbed into the grey sky like a forest of thin black trees. "Then I will worry about it," he said, "in my own time, and in my own way. That is what the guild pays you for, Quentin. To find the numbers before they find us."

* * *

Saltrow smelled the way it always had — brine and tar and the sweet rot of the rendering yards — but on the morning Quentin came it smelled underneath of something else, something floral and wrong, like lilies laid on a coffin that hadn't been dug yet.

Maren Ostwick's house was the blue one at the end of the row, salt-scabbed shutters, nets mended and hanging on the wall like the ribs of some enormous patient animal. Quentin knocked, announced herself as an auditor of accounts — which was true, if not of the kind Maren would assume — and was let in by a broad-shouldered woman in her fifties with windburnt cheeks and hands that wouldn't stop moving, folding and refolding a cloth as though it were a net and the conversation were fish that kept escaping.

"You're the fourth one this week," Maren said, pouring tea without asking whether Quentin wanted any. "A priest from the Lantern Order. Two scholars. A woman who claimed she was from the palace, though she had ink on her fingers like a scrivener and no ring worth mentioning." She set the cup down hard enough to slop. "They all want to know about Toma."

"And do you tell them?"

"I tell them what happened. Whether they believe it's another matter." Maren sat across from her, and for a moment her hands went still, pressed flat on the table as if holding down a lid. "He came back. My husband. Eleven years dead. He came back for one evening, and now I—" Her jaw worked. "Now I'm told I paid for him. So ask your questions, auditor. Ask them all. Because the ones who came before couldn't tell me what I lost, and I'd like to know if anyone in this city can."

Quentin opened her notebook. She was aware, distantly, of the professional machinery of herself engaging — the neat hand, the ruled lines, the questions asked in order of increasing intimacy — and equally aware that beneath the machinery something older and colder had begun to turn over, the way ice shifts on a river before it breaks.

"Tell me about Tuesday," she said. "From the beginning."

"There wasn't a beginning. That's the thing nobody understands." Maren's voice dropped, went rough. "There was supper on the table — herring, bread, the pickled cabbage he used to complain about — and I was setting his place out of habit, the way I have every night for eleven years, because I never could stop doing it, and then the door opened and he was standing there. Dripping. Seawater on my floor. He looked exactly as he did the day they buried what the sea gave back of him, except—" she hesitated, "—except wetter. Colder. Like something pulled out of deep water rather than shallow."

"What did you do?"

"What would you do?" Maren almost laughed; it came out as a cough. "I ran to him. I held him. He was cold as a mooring post but he held me back, and his arms remembered me, auditor. That's what I keep coming back to. His *arms* remembered me." She looked down at her hands. "We ate the supper. He complained about the cabbage. He told me about the nets — he could see them from wherever he was, he said, or whatever he was, he said it was like looking up through green glass. He stayed until the candles burned down. And then he started to go, and he knew it, and he said—" her voice cracked clean through, "—he said, *don't follow me out, Mare, you'll lose the shore.*"

The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere outside a gull screamed once and quit.

"And then?" Quentin asked. Her pen had not moved for some time. There was a small dark spot where the nib rested on the page, bleeding outward like a slow thought.

"And then I woke Wednesday morning and the chair was empty and the floor was dry and I went to the wall where our wedding portrait hangs—" Maren turned her face away, toward the window, toward the grey light and the smell of lilies. "There's a man in that picture. I know there's a man in that picture, because the frame says so. Underneath, in my mother's handwriting, it says *Toma and Maren, Midsummer '88*. But when I look at him, auditor, I see a shape wearing my husband's coat. I remember that I loved him. I remember that loving him *happened*, the way you remember a sum you once did correctly. I cannot remember him." Her hands resumed their folding, faster now. "They say that's the price. The priest said it plainest: the old gods take payment in memory. One evening with the dead costs you the dead. He said it like it was fair."

"Do you think it's fair?"

Maren was quiet a long moment. When she turned back, her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level, and Quentin recognized the look — she had worn it herself, once, in a room she could not remember, signing something she could not remember.

"No," Maren said. "But I'd pay it again tomorrow. That's the trap of it, isn't it? That's how they get you. They don't rob you. They just leave the door open and let you walk through it yourself, and thank them after." She leaned forward. "So here's my question for you, since you're the one with the book. Eleven years I prayed to anything that would listen. Nothing. Then last month the whole city starts praying and things start *answering*, and everyone's calling it mercy, calling it the gods coming home. But mercy doesn't take payment. Mercy doesn't need payment." Her voice hardened. "So what is it, auditor? What's answering us? Because whatever it is, it keeps books too. I felt it. Like something writing my name down while it handed him back."

Quentin looked at her notebook. At the dark bloom of ink where her pen had rested. At the entry she had begun and not finished:

*Subject: M. Ostwick. Transaction: return of deceased spouse, duration one evening. Payment: [blank].*

She found, suddenly, that she could not write *memory of the deceased* in that blank. It seemed too small a thing to hold what was sitting across the table from her — this woman who remembered that love had happened and could not remember love, who was grieving a grief she could no longer locate, mourning a man reduced to a caption under a portrait. It was not a line item. It was not a number. And yet somewhere, Quentin was certain, in some ledger she could not see, it had been entered as one. Neatly. Ruled. In a hand that believed everything had a price.

"I don't know what's answering," Quentin said quietly. It was the first fully honest sentence she had spoken in days, and it tasted strange, like cold water. "But I intend to find out. And I intend to find its ledger."

Maren studied her for a moment, and something passed between them — not comfort, nothing so kind, but recognition, the grim fellowship of two women who had both signed things in the dark.

"When you find it," Maren said, "ask it what it gave back to me along with him. Because here's the last thing, the thing I haven't told the priests or the scholars or the ink-fingered woman from the palace." She lowered her voice, and the room seemed to lean in with her. "When Toma was leaving — when he was going thin at the edges like smoke — he gripped my wrist and he said, *Mare, it isn't the gods. It wears the crown. Tell whoever asks: it wears the crown.*"

Outside, the gulls wheeled over Saltrow, and the lily-smell drifted in through the gap in the shutters, sweet and funereal, and Quentin Zhou sat very still with her pen hovering over the blank space where the price should be, understanding at last that she had not come to audit a widow's miracle.

She had come to witness a transaction. And the counterparty — the thing on the other side of the entry, the thing that wore the crown — had just, through a drowning-man's borrowed mouth, sent word that it already knew she was coming.
