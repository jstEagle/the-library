## 4. A King Who Forgets His Own Edicts

The summons arrived on the third morning of the frost-fair, carried by a page whose boots were too big for him and whose breath smelled of the cinnamon wine being sold in the square below.

*The crown requires the services of a reconciler of accounts. Present yourself at the Winter Court by the ninth bell. Bring your own ink.*

Master Oren read it twice, set it down on the counting table between them, and tapped it once with one blunt finger, as though it were a coin of uncertain weight.

"Palace work," he said. "You'll go."

Quentin looked up from the column she was auditing — tallow, forty barrels, winter rate — and waited, because Oren Vesper never said anything in one sentence when two would do, and never said two when three would serve him better.

"The crown's clerks have been filing nonsense since midwinter," he went on. "Duplicate requisitions. Payments signed twice for goods delivered once. Either someone is stealing from Aldric with both hands, or the king's own hand has learned to steal from itself. The steward wants an outsider. Someone with no friends at court." A thin smile. "Someone who counts what actually happened rather than what was written down."

"And if I find that someone *is* stealing?"

"Then you find it," Oren said, "and you bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. The guild is neutral, Quentin. Neutrality means we are useful to everyone and loyal to no one. Remember which side of that sentence pays your wages."

She remembered. She had been remembering for nine years, in the way one remembers a debt — not warmly, not willingly, but constantly, the way a stone remembers the river.

But as she gathered her pens and her second-best cloak and the small brass scale she used for weighing coins whose honesty she doubted, Quentin found herself thinking not about theft but about the fishwife, Maren Ostrow, and the ledger line she had entered three nights ago: *one evening returned; cost undisclosed.* And beneath it, in her own neat hand, the note she had added after the interview, the note she had told no one about:

*The widow could not remember her husband's face. She remembered that she had loved him. She remembered the shape of the loving without anything inside it.*

*A memory with the center cut out.*

The palace had a smell Quentin had forgotten it had — or perhaps had never let herself remember: beeswax and cold stone and, underneath, faintly, the green rot of the winter garden where nothing grew but things kept alive out of stubbornness. The guards at the gate took her name without reading it. The steward's clerk walked her through corridors hung with portraits of kings who all looked like they had been interrupted mid-sentence.

And everywhere, threaded through everything, so quiet she almost mistook it for the building settling, or for blood in her own ears —

a hum.

Low. Patient. Precise.

She stopped in the middle of the portrait corridor, one hand flat against the cold wall, and made herself count. It was what she did instead of praying. One, the hum held steady. Two, it did not echo, because echoes end and this did not. Three, it was not coming from any direction, which meant it was coming from all of them, which meant it was coming from below, or from inside the walls, or from somewhere that did not have directions at all.

"Reconciler?" said the clerk, ten paces ahead. "The steward hasn't got all morning."

Neither, Quentin thought, do kings. But she said nothing, and followed, and the hum followed too, keeping pace with her heartbeat like something that had learned her rhythm and found it agreeable.

* * *

Lord Cassian Vael had been a courtier for thirty-one years, which is to say he had survived thirty-one winters at a court where survival was a craft practiced more diligently than any of the arts the court patronized. He knew how to read a summons list the way sailors read weather. He knew which ministers were rising and which were being allowed to rise only so their fall would be longer. And he knew, with the certainty of long experience, that the steward's decision to bring in a guild accountant — a *guild* accountant, into the Winter Court, in the season of the answered prayers — meant that whatever was wrong with the crown's books had stopped being a problem and become a symptom.

He watched her from the gallery above the counting room while she worked. Small woman. Grey eyes that moved across a page the way a hawk moves over a field — not reading, *hunting*. She'd been at it four hours and had not once asked for wine, or warmth, or flattery, the three currencies in which the court paid its debts.

Cassian approved. He also pitied her, mildly, the way one pities a mouse brought into a house full of cats to solve a problem with the cheese.

Because Cassian knew what the ministers would not say aloud: the books were not being stolen from. He had checked. He had spent eleven sleepless nights checking, with his own hand, his own seal, his own fear. Every entry balanced. Every payment matched its requisition. The crown's accounts were *perfect* — perfect in the way a corpse is perfect, arranged and composed and utterly without life in it.

It was the king who was wrong.

Three days ago Aldric had summoned his council at the seventh bell and dictated an edict — grain prices for the frost-fair, relief for the river districts, the usual winter mercy — and Lord Chancellor Breck had read it back for approval, and the king had approved it, and the scribe had copied it fair, and the king had signed it. Standard. Ordinary. Cassian had witnessed a thousand such mornings.

Yesterday, the same edict had come back up from the chancery for countersignature — a formality, a duplicate, the kind of thing that happens in every bureaucracy in every kingdom since the invention of paper — and the king had read it, frowning, and asked, in a voice perfectly mild, perfectly kingly:

"*Who wrote this?*"

And Chancellor Breck had said, "Your Majesty dictated it yourself, the day before yesterday."

And the king had looked at the document for a long time. Long enough that the candles burned down a finger's width. Long enough that Breck began, visibly, to sweat. And then Aldric had smiled — a warm, gracious, terrible smile — and said, "Well. Whoever he was, he had good sense," and signed it again, and asked to be told when supper was, as though supper were a matter of state.

Cassian had seen men lose their minds. He had seen fevers take them, and grief, and the slow rot of the drink. This was none of those. This was a man misplacing a room he was standing in. This was forgetting with *edges*, clean edges, as though something had been removed rather than lost.

And last night, walking past the chapel he no longer used, Cassian had heard the king in there alone, talking — not praying, *talking*, in the low reasonable tone of a man negotiating — and the candles inside had been burning with flames that leaned toward the altar instead of away from it.

He did not believe in omens. He believed in ledgers. Which was why, when the grey-eyed woman from the guild finished her fourth hour and lifted her head with the particular stillness of a person who has just found something, Cassian came down the stairs to meet her himself.

* * *

"You've found it," he said. "I can tell. You have the look."

Quentin did not look up. "What look?"

"The look of a woman who has just discovered the sum is wrong and knows exactly which number lied."

That earned him a glance — brief, assessing, the glance of someone pricing him whether he liked it or not. "Your accounts aren't wrong, my lord."

"No," Cassian said. "They're dead. I know. I've spent half the winter among the corpse." He drew out a chair and sat, uninvited, because courtesy was a currency and he was spending carefully these days. "Tell me what you found instead."

Quentin turned the ledger toward him. Her finger rested on a column of requisitions. "This edict. Grain prices, river districts. Dated the fourth. Signed by the king." She slid her finger down. "Here it is again. Dated the sixth. Countersigned. Same text, word for word — and I mean word for word, my lord, including a correction in the fourth line. The first copy says 'the mills at Corren's Ford,' struck through, corrected to 'the mills at Corven's Ford.' The second copy makes the same mistake and the same correction. In the same ink pressure. As if the same hand wrote it twice, three days apart, having forgotten it entirely."

"So the scribe copied carelessly."

"The scribe's draft is attached. His draft is correct on the first pass. The error exists only in the king's hand. Both times." She looked up. "My lord, people don't make identical mistakes twice. Mistakes are like fingerprints. These matched."

Cassian was quiet for a moment. "And what does the guild make of a king who repeats himself like an echo?"

"The guild," Quentin said, "makes of it what it makes of everything. An entry." But her voice had gone strange — flat, careful, the voice of a woman carrying something fragile across ice — and Cassian, who had survived thirty-one winters by noticing voices, noticed.

"What did you see?" he asked. "Out there. Before you came here. They say prayers are being answered in the lower city. They say a fishwife got her drowned husband back for an evening."

"I interviewed her. For the guild's records."

"And?"

Quentin was silent long enough that the fire in the counting-room grate popped, and somewhere far below them — so far below that it should have been inaudible, should have been stone and bedrock and silence — something hummed.

"It cost her," Quentin said finally. "The answer. It cost her the memory of him. Not the love — the love is still there, she told me, she can feel that she loved him — but everything the love was *made of*. His face. His voice. Twenty years of mornings. She remembers loving a man whose name she has to ask strangers for." Her finger was still on the ledger, pressing hard enough to whiten the knuckle. "A shape with the center cut out."

"Yes," Cassian said quietly. "That's what the king's forgetting looks like. Edges clean. Center gone."

They looked at each other across the dead-perfect accounts, two professionals recognizing each other's trade in the worst possible market.

"Where does it come from?" Quentin asked. "The answering. Do you know?"

"Chancellor Breck believes the old gods have woken. The priests are saying it too — the ones who still have congregations. A century of silence, and now suddenly the heavens are generous." Cassian's mouth twisted. "In my experience, madam, generosity that arrives after a hundred years of silence is not generosity. It is a creditor resuming collection."

Something moved behind Quentin's eyes — some arithmetic happening too fast and too privately to follow — and then she closed the ledger with a soft, final sound, like a door.

"I need to see the king," she said.

"That," said Cassian, rising, "can be arranged. Though I warn you — everyone who sees him lately comes away with the same report, and the report is not in any language the chancery keeps files for."

"What report?"

"He looks at you," Cassian said, "as if he is trying to remember where he put you. And sometimes — only sometimes, mind, usually late, usually when the candles are low — he looks at you as if he is trying to remember where he put *himself*."

* * *

They granted her the audience at dusk, which Quentin understood was less an honor than a scheduling convenience: dusk was when the king's council thinned, when the petitioners went home, when a man who was disappearing could be observed with the fewest witnesses.

King Aldric sat by the window of the long study, wrapped in a fur that made him look smaller than his portraits. He was not an old man. Fifty, perhaps. But the light did something unkind to his face, showing the fine tremor in his hands and the way his eyes moved — not wandering, precisely. *Searching*. The eyes of a man in a room he was sure contained something he needed, if only he could think what.

"Guild accountant," he said, when Cassian presented her. His voice was pleasant. Cultivated. "You've come about the winter accounts."

"To reconcile them, Your Majesty."

"Mm." He smiled. "My father never reconciled anything. He simply spent until the spending stopped meaning anything. Perhaps that was wisdom." He gestured her closer, and there — as she crossed the carpet, close enough to see the candlelight on his face — the hum rose.

Not faint now. Not distant. It swelled around her like water closing over the ears, and it was saying her name, *Quentin, Quentin, Quentin*, in that patient precise voiceless voice, and King Aldric's searching eyes fixed on her and widened — just slightly, just for a moment — with something that was not confusion.

Recognition.

"Have we met?" the king asked softly.

"No, Your Majesty."

"Strange." He tilted his head, and for one vertiginous instant Quentin felt that she was looking not at a king but at a doorway, and that something behind the door had heard her knock nine years ago and had been waiting, politely, ever since. "You feel like a debt," Aldric said. "Forgive me. That's an odd thing to say to a stranger. You feel like a debt I've nearly finished paying."

"Your Majesty," Cassian began carefully, "the lady has found irregularities—"

"There are no irregularities," the king said, still looking at Quentin, still smiling. "My accounts are perfect. Everything balances. Doesn't it balance, accountant? Everything always balances, in the end. That's the whole secret. That's what the ledger teaches." He raised one trembling hand and touched his own temple, lightly, the way a man touches a door he can no longer open. "Everything balances. Some entries just take longer to post than others."

An attendant entered with the evening dispatches, and among them, folded and sealed, was a document Quentin recognized — she had held its twin that afternoon, had weighed its ink on her brass scale. The grain edict. Third copy. For the royal archive.

Aldric broke the seal, unfolded it, and read his own decree from that very morning with the polite bafflement of a man reading a letter from a stranger. Quentin watched his eyes move down the lines. Watched them reach the fourth line, the corrected spelling, *Corven's Ford*. Watched him frown — a small, private frown, quickly mastered — and turn the page over as if the rest of himself might be written on the back.

"Who wrote this?" he asked the room.

"Your Majesty dictated it this morning," said Chancellor Breck, from the shadows by the door, in the voice of a man reciting words he had already recited too many times.

Silence. The fire popped. Below the floor, below the foundations, below sense, the hum held its single patient note.

And Quentin Zhou stood in the candlelight of the Winter Court and felt the horror arrive not as a shock but as a *reconciliation* — two columns meeting, two entries matching, the fishwife's hollowed-out love and the king's hollowed-out morning settling against each other in her mind with a click as clean and terrible as a ledger closing.

Same handwriting. Same clean edges. Same center cut out.

The king was not ill. The king was being *paid for*.

Aldric looked up from the edict and met her eyes, and smiled his warm, gracious, terrible smile, and said, "Well. Whoever he was, he had good sense."

Quentin bowed, because nine years in the guild had taught her body to bow even when her mind was screaming. And as she straightened, backing toward the door with Cassian's hand light at her elbow, the hum surged once — brief, eager, unmistakable, a dog straining at a leash — and beneath the king's smile she saw, or thought she saw, the shadow he cast on the window-glass lean *against* the light instead of away from it.

She did not sleep that night. She sat in her narrow room above the counting floor with every lamp burning, and on a fresh sheet of paper she wrote, in her smallest and most careful hand, the only sentence her whole training had prepared her to write:

*Every answered prayer has a payer. Find the account, and you find the god.*

Below the floor, faint and patient, the hum said her name — and this time, unmistakably, it paused afterward, as if waiting for her to answer.
