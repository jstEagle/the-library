## 7. Nine Years Ago

The winter that took Wren began, as all bad winters in Corvane begin, with the smell.

Quentin had been fourteen that year, and she remembered — she still remembered, though she was not supposed to, though the remembering itself was a kind of trespass — how the city had smelled first. Not sickness. Sickness came later, riding behind the smell like a debt collector after a debtor. What came first was smoke and tallow and the particular sweetness of oranges rotting in a market barrow no one dared empty, because the orange-seller had died on a Tuesday and by Thursday his whole street had been chalked with the grey cross that meant *sealed*. The plague did not announce itself with bells or processions. It announced itself with the slow abandonment of ordinary things: milk left uncollected, doors unpainted, a child's mitten lying in a gutter for six weeks until the snow ate it.

Their mother had died two years before, of nothing dramatic — her heart simply stopped agreeing to work, one morning, between the kettle and the bread. Their father had gone further back than that, into the river, when Quentin was small enough that grief for him was less a wound than a story other people told about her. So it had been the two of them, the Zhou sisters, in the crooked house on Tanner's Lane with its leaning stair and its roof that sang in high wind like a wet finger on glass. Wren was sixteen. She kept the accounts of their small life in a book she'd bound herself from butcher's paper, because she said that if you wrote down what you owed and what you had, the world could not lie to you.

Quentin had learned everything she knew about ledgers at Wren's elbow, in the lamplight, with Wren's cold fingers guiding hers down the columns. *Numbers don't flatter,* Wren liked to say. *That's why I trust them.* It was the closest thing to a family creed they had, and Quentin would carry it into a life Wren would never see, in a building Wren had never entered, keeping books for people who understood exactly what numbers were worth because they traded in the things numbers couldn't hold.

But that came later. That came after.

* * *

The plague winter proper arrived in the week of the Long Frost, when the harbor froze hard enough that men walked out to the ships and the ships sat trapped in grey ice like flies in amber. The fish went off in the barrels. The wells iced at their mouths. And in the tenements along the river the coughing started — first one voice, then a chorus, then a silence so complete you could hear the snow landing.

Wren got sick on the ninth day of the frost.

Quentin would remember — *would have* remembered; the tense of it was the whole problem, the whole horror, the whole bargain — the exact sequence of it. How Wren had come up the stairs from the wash-house with her cheeks too bright, the fever-brightness that looks like health and is the opposite. How she'd insisted on finishing the day's accounts first, sitting propped against the headboard with the butcher-paper book balanced on her knees, her pen scratching while her breath whistled. How she'd written, in her neat upright hand, *coal: three days left,* and then set the pen down very carefully, as if it were something alive that might be startled, and said, "Quentin. I think I've caught it."

Not *I'm afraid I've caught it.* Wren was never afraid in words. She was afraid in arithmetic. She lay back against the pillow and said, "Three days of coal. If we burn it slow, five. You'll need to stretch the flour with the barley," and she was already planning Quentin's survival with the same steady hand she used for everything, pricing her own death into the household column like a cost of doing business.

"I'll fetch the herb-woman," Quentin said.

"The herb-woman is dead. The priest is dead. Half of Salt Row is dead." Wren's eyes were closed now, but her mouth still held its shape, that faint dry almost-smile. "You'll fetch water, and you'll keep the fire small, and you'll sit with me. That's the prescription. Sit with me, Quen. That's all medicine there is this winter."

So Quentin sat. For four days she sat, melting snow over a fire fed one coal at a time, spooning barley-water past her sister's cracked lips, wiping the sweat from a forehead that grew hotter and more distant by the hour. On the second day Wren began to talk to people who weren't there — their mother, mostly, and once, terribly, their father, whose face Quentin could not remember and Wren apparently could, because she argued with him, gently, the way she argued with anyone who fudged a column. On the third day she was quiet, and quiet was worse. On the fourth day, near dusk, with the light going blue in the frost-ferned window, Wren opened her eyes all the way and looked at Quentin with a clarity that cut through the fever like a blade through paper.

"Listen," she said. Her voice was small and even. "When I'm gone, you're going to want to burn the book."

"Wren—"

"You'll want to. Because the last pages will be full of me dying, and you won't be able to look at them." A breath, thin as thread. "Don't. Keep the accounts. All of them. Even the ugly ones. Especially the ugly ones. Promise me, Quen. Numbers don't flatter. If you keep them honest, I stay real. Do you understand? As long as someone writes it down true, nobody's really—" The sentence didn't end. It just ran out of air, the way a candle runs out of wick.

"I promise," Quentin said.

And Wren smiled — the full smile, the rare one, the one that made her look suddenly nine years old — and said, "Good girl," which was what their mother used to say, and then she turned her face toward the window and the blue light, and sometime in the night, between one of Quentin's dozings and the next, she stopped.

* * *

Here is what Quentin remembered of the rest of that night, in the years after — the years when she wasn't supposed to remember anything at all, and yet some sediment of it always remained, silt under clear water, which should have told her something about the nature of the trade she'd made:

She remembered that she did not cry. That was the thing that frightened her most, afterward, in whatever afterward she was permitted to have. She sat beside her sister's body through the long dark and felt nothing she could name, only a pressure behind the sternum like a hand pressing on a door. Grief, she understood much later, had been there the whole time. It was simply too large to enter through any opening she had. It stood outside her all night, the way the cold stood outside the window, patient, absolute, waiting for a crack.

She remembered deciding, at some black hour before dawn, that she would not let them take Wren to the plague-carts. The carts came every morning, and the men who drove them wore rags tied over their faces and pitched bodies into mass pits beyond the walls without names, without marks, without accounts. Wren had spent her whole life writing things down so they couldn't be erased. Quentin was not going to let her sister go into a pit as an unnumbered body.

So she wrapped her. There was no shroud; she used the good blanket, the wool one with the red border that had been their mother's. Wren was heavy the way the sleeping are heavy, heavier than the living, and Quentin was fourteen and slight and it took her an hour to get her sister down the leaning stair, resting between steps, breathing through her mouth against the sweet-rot smell that was already beginning. She meant to bury her in the churchyard of Saint Ferrel's, where their mother lay, but Saint Ferrel's was sealed, and the ground was iron with frost anyway, and the carts were coming.

She got Wren as far as the yard behind the tannery, where the ground rose against the wall and the snow lay deep and undisturbed, and there she stopped, because she could not dig. The earth would not open. She had no spade worth the name and no strength worth the task, and the sky was going grey at the edges, and somewhere down the lane she could hear the creak of cart wheels.

What she did then, she did without deciding to do it. Later — in the true memory, the buried one, the one that cost more than everything — she would understand that she had been calling without knowing the word for calling. Not praying. The old gods hadn't answered prayers in a hundred years; everyone knew that; the temples were museums and the priests kept bees. But she knelt in the snow with her sister in her arms and she said, aloud, to whoever might be listening, to the dark, to the frost, to the ledger of the world itself: *"Take something. Take anything. Take me. Only give me ground."*

And the ground — or something wearing the ground's manners — answered.

* * *

The stranger came up Tanner's Lane at the exact moment the cart rounded the corner at its far end, and Quentin would always believe — in the silt-memory, in the part of her that survived the subtraction — that the timing was not coincidence but courtesy, or theater. He was tall and narrow and dressed in grey so fine it made the snow look coarse, and he carried no lantern, though the light was still bad, and he walked on the snow without printing it.

He stopped at the wall of the tannery yard and looked at her kneeling there with her dead sister in her arms, and he did not offer pity. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing that made her trust him, insofar as she trusted anything that morning: he did not waste a single syllable on sorrow. He looked at her the way a merchant looks at goods — not cruelly, but honestly, assessing what she had and what she lacked.

"You asked for ground," he said. His voice was quiet and dry, like pages turning.

"The carts are coming," Quentin said. Her voice came out flat and strange, aged thirty years in one night.

"They are. And the pits are full, and no one will write your sister's name anywhere, ever, and in forty years no one will know she lived at all." He tilted his head. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless a record is kept." He stepped into the yard — and here was the second thing she noticed, the thing her mind skated around for nine years like a tongue finding the gap where a tooth was: he stepped *over* the wall rather than through the gate, smoothly, without effort, the way you'd step over a threshold. "There is a house in this city that keeps accounts of impossible things. I am authorized to make you an offer on its behalf."

"She needs to be buried," Quentin said. "Deep enough that the dogs can't—"

"Yes. That can be arranged. Ground, opened, soft, private, marked with her name and her dates in stone that will outlast the street." He crouched then, bringing himself level with her, and his eyes were dark and bottomless and entirely without warmth, and yet she felt — this was the seduction of it, this was the hook — *seen*. Utterly seen. No adult had looked at her that way since her mother died; they looked through her, at her poverty, at her youth, at her inconvenience. This man looked *at* her. "In exchange, the guild requires payment. We always require payment. Nothing is given in this world, Miss Zhou. Things are only moved from one ledger to another."

"We have nothing. Fourteen coppers and a bag of barley."

"You have something far more valuable." He extended one pale hand, palm up, an accountant's gesture, asking to see her figures. "You have tonight."

The snow ticked softly in the silence. Down the lane, the cart wheels creaked closer.

"What do you mean?"

"The night," he said. "This night. Your sister's death — the whole of it, entire, from the first cough to this moment, kneeling in the snow. Every detail. The sound of her breathing. The weight of her in your arms. The color of the light in the window. The words she gave you to keep." His head inclined, very slightly, toward the butcher-paper book in Quentin's pocket, and something cold walked down Quentin's spine, because she had not told him about the book. "We will take the memory of it. Cleanly, completely, kindly. You will wake tomorrow knowing your sister died of the plague in the winter of the Long Frost — the fact, the entry, the number. You will grieve her as one grieves a number. But you will not carry the night. The night is the expensive part. The night is what kills children in this city, following them under their eyelids for fifty years. We are offering to take it from you. People pay us *for* this service, Miss Zhou. You would be receiving it free, plus burial, plus — " and here he paused, and the pause was priced, she understood that even then, even at fourteen, even hollowed out with grief; the pause was the most expensive thing he said — "plus shelter. The Vesper Guild takes in certain orphans of certain winters. You would have a place. Warm. Fed. Taught a trade. You have a gift for figures; we can see it from here. It glows on you like a lamp."

"My sister's book," Quentin said. "Her accounts. I promised her I'd keep them."

"Keep them. Of course. We take only the night, not the facts. Facts are cheap. Facts are everywhere." He smiled then, and it was the smile of a man closing a favorable column. "You will remember that you loved her. You will remember that she died. You will simply not remember *this* — the weight, the cold, the carts, the begging of the ground. Is that not mercy? Tell me truly. Is that not the kindest trade anyone has offered you in your whole short life?"

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Quentin would spend nine years not remembering: it *was*. Kneeling in the snow with her sister going stiff and heavy in her arms and the death-cart rattling nearer, it was mercy, it was the only mercy on offer in the whole frozen city, and she was fourteen, and she said yes.

She said yes the way you sign anything at fourteen, at midnight, in grief: completely, blindly, forever.

"Say it plainly," the stranger said. "For the record."

"I trade you the night," Quentin said. Her breath smoked in the air between them. "All of it. In exchange for ground for my sister, and a place for me."

"Witnessed and entered," said the stranger, and drew something from his coat — she would hunt the shape of that object through her dreams for years, a pen, a stylus, a sliver of bone, she could never fix it — and touched it once, lightly, to the space just above her heart, the way you'd blot a signature.

* * *

The strangest part — the part that proved, later, that the trade had been real, that it had teeth — was the seamlessness. There was no darkness, no gap, no missing page. She simply woke the next morning in a bed that was not hers, in a warm room that smelled of ink and tallow and old paper, in the ledger-house on Vesper Square, and she knew — calmly, factually, the way she knew the harbor froze in winter — that her sister Wren was dead of the plague, and that she herself had been taken in by the guild, and that was all she knew, and the *all* of it fit inside her like a coin in a purse, small and smooth and complete.

She did not remember digging. She did not remember that there had been no digging to do — that she had woken to learn that Wren lay in Saint Ferrel's extension after all, in ground the guild had somehow caused to open, under a stone that read *WREN ZHOU, BELOVED, KEPT TRUE ACCOUNTS*, carved in letters that did not weather. She visited the grave that first spring and felt what she had been sold as a feature of the purchase: a clean, abstract sorrow, grief at one remove, mourning a number. She stood at the stone and thought, *I loved her. I must have. The shape of my life says so.* And the thought rang slightly false, like a coin bitten and found wanting, and she had filed the falseness away in the drawer where she kept everything that didn't balance, and she had not opened that drawer again for nine years.

She became, quickly and thoroughly, the guild's best apprentice. Oren Vesper himself remarked on it — how naturally the work came to her, how she seemed born to the double-entry of impossible things, how she never flinched at the entries that made the other novices sick: the trades of voices, of shadows, of first kisses and final words. *You have no attachments to trouble the arithmetic,* he told her approvingly, when she was sixteen, and she had taken it as praise.

She kept Wren's butcher-paper book, as promised. It sat in the bottom of her trunk for nine years, and she never read past the middle pages, telling herself it was grief, telling herself it was respect. It was neither. It was the instinct of a good auditor refusing to reconcile an account she could feel was wrong — because the handwriting changed, somewhere in those final pages, and she had never been able to bear looking directly at where, or why, or what the last entries said, in Wren's failing hand, about a girl named Quentin who had promised something at a window with blue light in it.

A promise about keeping accounts. About staying real. About the ugly pages.

Nine years the drawer stayed shut. Then the old gods woke, and the gate beneath the ledger-house began to hum, and Quentin found a transaction in the winter accounts in her own handwriting that she had never written — and the drawer flew open all by itself, and everything she had sold came home to collect.
