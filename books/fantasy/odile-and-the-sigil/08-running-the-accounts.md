## 8. Running the Accounts

Sable had learned to count prayers the way other people counted crows.

You did it by ear, mostly. By the quality of silence in a street. A city that prayed into nothing sounded like a city coughing into a closed fist — muffled, embarrassed, done quickly and without hope. But this winter the fist had opened. This winter you could stand on Saltcote Bridge at dawn and hear them coming off the water: fishermen murmuring into their cupped hands, fishwives pressing their foreheads to doorposts, children asking for things with the terrible seriousness of children who have just discovered someone might be listening.

And someone *was* listening. That was the whole horror of it. Sable had spent eleven years being defrocked, spat at, and occasionally stoned for saying the old gods were still there — still there, just gone quiet, just turned their faces away like a mother pretending not to hear a tantrum — and now the gods were answering, and she was the only person in Vesperhold who wished to the marrow of her bones that they would stop.

Because answers had a smell.

She kept her notes in a ledger bound in grey hide, hidden under the floorboards of her room above the tallow-chandler's shop on Candlewick Row. Not a proper ledger. Proper ledgers balanced. Hers only accumulated. She had forty-one entries since the first frost, and every single one followed the same shape, and no one else in the city seemed able to see the shape because no one else had spent eleven years reading theology the way a customs officer reads manifests, looking for what was missing from the cargo.

*Item,* she wrote that morning, in her small crabbed hand, warming the ink over the candle stub because the room was cold enough to make it drag. *Tomas Ellery, rope-maker, Bell Lane. Prayed for his son's fever to break. Fever broke before matins. Cost: cannot recall the boy's face. Knows he has a son. Cannot say what the son looks like. Wife reports he wept at supper looking at the child "as at a stranger."*

*Item. Widow Ansker, Saltcote. Prayed her husband back for one evening. Received him. Paid: all memory of him entire. Now keeps his coat on its hook and does not know whose it is.*

Forty-one items. Forty-one payments. And not one prayer granted free.

The chandler's shop smelled of tallow and wet wool and the particular staleness of a building that had been cold so long the cold had moved in permanently, taken up residence, begun arranging furniture. Sable sat at her table with her feet in a straw-filled crate, wrapped in two cloaks, and did the arithmetic again, though she had done it every night for a week and it never came out differently.

Prayers answered: hundreds, citywide, maybe thousands now.
Memories paid: one each, minimum. Some more.
Sum: something was *eating.*

She blew out the candle as the bell of the Dawnhouse rang seven, and thought, not for the first time, that if she were still a priestess — if she had not been stripped of her order, her name-in-the-rolls, and her right to wear grey — she would know what to call the thing that ate. The old liturgies had names for everything. They had names for the hunger between stars and the hunger inside grief. Somewhere in the burned libraries of her childhood there had been a page about the thing that eats the past to buy the future, and she had read it at nineteen and laughed, because she was nineteen and theology was full of monsters that never came.

She was thirty-four now, and she did not laugh anymore.

* * *

The knock came at dusk.

Three knocks, spaced precisely, the way nobody knocked unless they wanted something or feared something, and then — after a pause long enough that Sable had already decided not to answer — three more, softer, almost apologetic, and a voice through the wood, low and controlled and fraying at the edges like good rope under too much weight:

"Mistress Sable. My name is Quentin Zhou. I keep the accounts of the Vesper Guild."

Sable went very still, her hand flat on the floorboard over the grey ledger.

Everyone knew the Vesper Guild the way everyone knew the tide: always there, always necessary, never looked at directly. Traders in impossible things. A gate beneath their ledger-house that must never open twice, whatever that meant; even the phrase had the polished, deliberate opacity of guild paperwork. And the guild had survived the death of the old faith better than anyone, which told Sable what she needed to know about where the guild's loyalties lay when gods started waking up.

"I'm not buying," she called through the door. "I'm not selling. Go away."

"I'm not here to trade." A pause. When the voice came again it was quieter, and the fraying had gotten worse. "Someone wrote a transaction in my ledger that I didn't write. In my own hand. I've compared the ink against nine years of entries. It's mine. I don't remember making it." Another pause, longer. "And tonight I found a book older than the guild, and my name is in it. As payment. Nine years ago. I need somewhere the guild doesn't own, and I'm told—" the voice caught itself, corrected, went on flatter, "—you've been asking questions about answered prayers. At the fishmarket. At the Dawnhouse steps. People talk about you. They say you write things down."

"They say worse than that," Sable said.

"Yes," said the voice, and there was something almost dry in it, almost a smile with no humor in it anywhere. "They also say that."

Sable stood there in the dark with her heart going like a hammer on an anvil, and considered. Every prudent instinct she owned said: the guild's chief clerk appearing at your door at dusk is not a defection, it is a fishing expedition; they have noticed you counting and they have sent their best counter to count *you*. But underneath the prudence, in the place where she had once heard the old gods and still sometimes felt them the way you feel weather through a wall, something leaned toward that voice. Something recognized the specific loneliness in it. The loneliness of a person who has just discovered the books are lying and cannot un-know it.

She opened the door.

The woman on her landing was narrow, dark-haired, salt-and-pepper at the temples though she could not have been past thirty-five, dressed in clerk's grey gone travel-wrinkled, with ink stains worked so deep into her right hand that the fingers themselves looked written-on. Her eyes did a thing Sable had seen only twice before in her life, both times in mirrors: swept the landing, the stairwell behind, the window-light, the shadows, cataloguing exits. A woman doing sums about danger.

"You're smaller than your reputation," Sable said.

"Everyone is." Quentin Zhou stepped inside without waiting to be asked, then stopped halfway across the threshold as if her manners had caught up with her, and added, stiffly, "May I come in? I should have asked. I apologize. I haven't been — I've been running for six hours and courtesy has fallen out of me somewhere around the tanners' quarter."

"Running from wardens?"

"Running from the truth," the clerk said, "which moves slower but has better endurance."

Sable shut the door, threw the bolt, and lit the candle. In its light Quentin Zhou looked worse than her voice had suggested — pale in the way of people who have not eaten, with a bruise-like exhaustion under both eyes and a leather satchel clutched to her chest the way a soldier holds a wound.

"You're the auditor," Sable said. "Zhou. They say you can find a stolen coin in a sack of ten thousand by weight alone."

"People say a great deal about the guild. Most of it is wrong. That happens to be true." Quentin's gaze had found the floorboard — not the one hiding the ledger, a different one, but she had looked at the floor the way a reader looks down a page, and Sable made a mental note that this woman missed nothing and filed everything. "Mistress Sable. Formerly Sister Sable of the Grey Vigil, defrocked year eleven for teaching that the gods hadn't abandoned us, which the Dawnhouse called heresy and which this winter has rather vindicated, hasn't it." A beat. "I read the court records. Before I came. I wanted to know who I was running to."

"And what did you conclude?"

"That you were either a fraud or the only honest priestess in Vesperhold, and that the difference didn't matter, because either way you'd want to see what I brought." Quentin knelt, set the satchel on the table, and unbuckled it with hands that were almost steady. Almost. Sable watched the tremor the way she watched everything: closely, and without mercy, and with a great deal more sympathy than her face allowed.

What came out of the satchel was paper. Reams of it. Ledgers, loose pages, a slim black book bound in something too smooth to be any lawful hide, and — last, laid down separately, as though it were hot — a folded sheet of heavy cream stock sealed with a sigil Sable had never seen and yet knew instantly, the way you know a face in a nightmare when you wake.

"What am I looking at?" she asked, though her stomach already knew, because the smell was rising off the papers now that they were open in warm air — that answer-smell, sweet and hollow, like honey poured over a wound.

"The guild's winter accounts," Quentin said. "My nine years of entries. The forbidden archive's index. And a contract dated nine years ago, in which a party I can only describe as *the sigil* purchased a memory from a girl named Quentin Zhou, aged fourteen, through intermediaries, with delivery deferred until such time as the gate required a key." Her voice stayed level the whole way through. It was a professional voice. Sable recognized it as the voice you build when the alternative is screaming. "The intermediaries were the Vesper Guild. The gate is under our ledger-house. And the key, Mistress Sable, appears to be me."

* * *

They worked through the night.

It was not a discussion, at first. It was two people with two systems of notation discovering they spoke dialects of the same dead language. Sable had her forty-one entries; Quentin had the guild's prayer-side records, which turned out to exist, which turned out to be *vast*, which turned out — and here Quentin stopped speaking for nearly a minute, staring at a column total as if it had bitten her — to be balanced to the copper against the memory-losses in Sable's book, as though somewhere a single mind held both sides of both ledgers and reconciled them nightly.

"It's bookkeeping," Quentin said finally. She said it the way other people said *it's plague.* "All of it. Every miracle this winter is a line item. The sigil grants the wish and books the memory as payment received. Look — the widow Ansker. One evening with her husband, priced at"—her finger traced down the column, stopped—"'one life, complete, as lived.' It's not even subtle. It's not even ashamed. It prices a human past the way I'd price a bolt of cloth."

"And no one sees it," Sable said, "because no one remembers paying."

"No one remembers paying," Quentin agreed softly. "Which makes it the perfect crime. You can't audit a theft the victim can't report." She looked up, and in the candlelight her eyes had a gleam in them that Sable would learn to know well in the weeks ahead: the gleam of a woman finding, at the bottom of her despair, a problem she knew how to solve. "But it *can* be audited. If someone outside the transactions keeps the count. Which is you. Which is why I'm here."

"Why are you really here?"

Quentin was quiet for a moment. The candle fluttered. Somewhere below them the chandler's dog dreamed and whimpered.

"Nine years ago," Quentin said, "someone came to my family's hovel during the plague winter and offered my parents a bargain, and I don't remember any of it, and I think — I think whatever happened that night cost me my sister, and I don't even know how, because I sold the knowing along with everything else." Her hands had gone flat on the table, pressing down as though the table might buck. "I have run the accounts of the impossible for nine years and I never once asked what I myself had paid, because Oren told me the guild had *saved* me, and gratitude is a wonderful instrument for keeping drawers shut. And tonight I read my own name in a book older than the guild, entered as *collateral*, and I understood that I was never sheltered. I was stored." The level voice finally cracked, just one hairline fracture, and was repaired immediately. "So. Why am I really here? Because you are the only person in this city who counts the ugly pages, and I need someone to witness my figures. Because a number no one witnesses is a number that can be erased. And because—" the fracture again, wider this time, "—because I have nowhere else, and I am afraid, and I am so tired of being afraid *alone*."

Sable looked at her across the candle and the scattered ledgers, this dry, precise, terrified woman who had served a power she mistrusted for nine years and was only now learning the size of the power, and felt the strange vertigo of recognition. Eleven years ago Sable had kept the rolls of the Grey Vigil, recording who prayed, what they prayed for, what the gods gave back — until she noticed the giving had stopped and reported it, and was told that hope was doctrine and doubt was treason, and lost everything for counting honestly.

Two women. Two sets of books. Two masters who preferred the books unread.

"I'll tell you what I know," Sable said at last, "and then you'll tell me what the numbers say, and then we're going to do the one thing neither of us has ever dared."

"Balance the two sides," Quentin said.

"Worse," Sable said. "We're going to find out what the balance is *for*. Because a hoard that big isn't greed, clerk. Greed spends. This thing is saving up. Forty-one payments in my book, hundreds in yours, and the gate humming under your ledger-house — it's not feeding. It's finishing. Something is almost *paid for*, and when it is, I don't think the praying stops. I think the asking does. I think it stops waiting to be invited."

Quentin had gone white. "Unbinding," she said. "There's a word in the old contract. *Upon completion of consideration, the sigil shall be unbound from its vessel.*" Her eyes came up. "Its vessel is wearing a crown, isn't it. That's why the king—"

"You've seen it?" Sable said sharply. "You've seen the king?"

"I've reconciled his accounts. He signed an edict this morning that he wrote himself at dawn and wept over it at noon like a stranger's handwriting. I thought it was age. I know better now." Quentin began, slowly, gathering the papers back into the satchel with the automatic tenderness of a clerk handling originals. "Then it's worse than eating. It's molting. It's using the whole city as—"

She stopped. Her head came up, tilted, listening, and Sable realized the clerk was hearing something through the floor, through the walls, through the winter night itself — a sound Sable had been half-hearing for days and dismissing as wind in the chimneys.

A hum. Faint, patient, vast, like a struck bell that refused to finish decaying.

"He knows I ran," Quentin whispered. "The gate hums my name, Mistress Sable. It has hummed my name for a week. And if I'm the key—" She looked at the door, then at Sable, and Sable watched her arrive at the same conclusion arriving in her own chest: *we let the key in the house.*

Outside, far off toward the center of the city, every bell in Vesperhold began to ring at once — though it was midnight, and no feast day, and no hand had touched a rope.

Quentin stood very still among the scattered ledgers. "That will be the wardens," she said. "Or it will be the palace. Or it will be both, and I genuinely cannot tell you which frightens me more." She picked up the black book, the one bound in unlawful hide, and held it out to Sable with both hands, formally, the way you surrender a weapon or swear an oath. "You said you wanted to know what the balance is for. Then hold this. Whatever comes through that door tonight — the numbers survive. Promise me the numbers survive."

Sable took it. It was cold, colder than the room, cold like the underside of a stone in January, and it hummed faintly against her palms, and she understood that she had just become, whether she willed it or no, the keeper of a second ledger for a second hungry power.

Downstairs, the knocking began.
