## 9. The Price List

Sable's rooms sat above a candlemaker's shop in the Tanners' Reach, where the smell of tallow and old fat rose through the floorboards like something slowly being rendered. The stairs were narrow and the banister was sticky, and every landing had a door that looked as if it had been kicked at least once. Quentin climbed them with her ledger satchel clutched to her chest the way a soldier carries a shield — not because it would stop anything, but because holding it made her feel like a person who knew what she was doing.

She did not know what she was doing.

Three days since she had fled the Vesper Guild-house with her own name burning in her mind like a brand pressed into wet paper. Three days of cheap rooms and cheaper wine, of walking circuits through the city with her collar up, watching the way people moved now — the loose, dreamy gait of the recently answered. Three days of hearing the hum beneath everything, faint but constant, like a tuning fork struck against the bones of the world.

Sable opened the door before Quentin finished knocking. The priestess was smaller than Quentin remembered from the temple years — everyone's gods were smaller than they used to be — but her eyes were the same: dark, quick, and appraising, the eyes of someone who had spent a lifetime counting what others pretended not to see.

"You look terrible," Sable said.

"I've been sleeping in rented beds."

"That would do it." She stepped aside. "Come in before the neighbors decide you're worth praying about."

The room inside was nothing like Quentin expected. She had imagined a disgraced priestess living among broken icons, incense ash, the sad furniture of a fallen faith. Instead the room was an archive. Every wall was papered with notes — scraps, strips, whole pages pinned and pasted in overlapping drifts, each covered in Sable's cramped hand. Strings ran between clusters of paper like the rigging of a ship. In one corner stood a table buried under ledgers — real ones, bound in cracked leather, some so old their spines had gone the color of dried blood.

Quentin stopped in the doorway. Nine years of keeping accounts had taught her to read a room the way other people read faces, and this room was shouting.

"This is a reconciliation," she said.

"It's a wall," said Sable.

"It's a *reconciliation*," Quentin insisted. "You're cross-referencing. Dates against dates, sources against sources—" She crossed to the nearest string and followed it with her finger. "You're trying to find where the books don't balance."

Sable watched her with something that might have been amusement, or might have been hunger. "The guild teaches its clerks to see money. I'm beginning to think that's the least interesting thing you've been trained to see." She pulled a stool from under the table and set it down for Quentin. "Sit. I'll show you my columns."

---

What Sable showed her was a price list.

It took the form of a ledger — of course it did; everything in this city had become a ledger — but no guild clerk would ever have kept a book like it. Down the left-hand column ran the prayers. Not the words themselves; Sable had collected those separately, on the papers strung across the walls. Here there were only categories, written in a shorthand Quentin recognized from temple charity-rolls: *healing. bread. return of the dead. forgiveness. warmth. a child. rain.*

Down the right-hand column ran the costs.

"Here," Sable said, tapping the first line. "Marta Ellery, fishwife off the Shambles. Prayed for her drowned husband back. He came — one evening, warm and solid and smelling of brine exactly as he had in life. She fed him soup. He told her he loved her. Then he went out with the tide again." Sable's finger moved to the cost column. "By morning she couldn't remember his face. Not his name, not his voice, not thirty years of marriage. She remembers that she *had* a husband the way you remember a coat you once owned. She knows the shape of the absence. Nothing else."

"And the second?"

"A cooper on Tanner's Row prayed his lame daughter could walk. She walks. He has lost every memory of his own father — who he was, what he sounded like, the day he taught the boy the trade. Gone. He knows he had a father. He cannot tell you why the sight of a barrel stave makes him weep."

"Third."

"A widow prayed for rain after three dry summers. It rained. She forgot her wedding day. A dyer prayed for his eyesight back and lost his mother's lullabies. A girl prayed her fevered brother would live, and he lives, and she has forgotten how to read."

Quentin felt the pattern settle over her before Sable even drew the line connecting the columns — felt it the way she sometimes felt an error in a ledger before her pen found it, a wrongness in the weight of things. But she made herself say it aloud anyway, because saying it aloud was how she thought.

"Every prayer is answered," she said. "And every answer costs a memory."

"Not just any memory." Sable drew a length of charcoal from behind her ear and began marking the entries. "Look at the *kind*. Marta Ellery didn't lose her childhood. Didn't lose her mother tongue, her prayers, her griefs. She lost the thing most precious to her — the thing bound tightest to the wish itself. The cooper didn't lose his wedding day. He lost his *father*, which is to say the root of everything he became. The pattern holds across forty-one cases. Forty-one, Quentin. The sigil takes the memory closest to the heart of the prayer. The tenderest thing. Always."

"Because—"

"Because it isn't eating them." Sable set down the charcoal and looked at Quentin with the flat steadiness of a woman delivering a diagnosis. "It's keeping them. There's a difference between a fire that consumes and a hand that pockets. I've spent four months listening to the answered pray again — did you know they do? They come back to their shrines, or what's left of them, and they pray to get back what they paid. And nothing answers. Because the thing that took their memories doesn't want worship. It wants *material*."

Quentin's hands had gone still on her knees. "Material for what?"

Sable rose and crossed to the far wall, where the oldest documents hung — parchments gone amber and brittle, covered in script that predated the guild, predated the temple, predated half the streets outside. One page bore a diagram Quentin had seen only once before, nine years ago, glimpsed through a forbidden door in the depths of the guild-house: a circle bisected by lines, with a shape at its center that was less a drawing than a hole in the paper.

"The old texts call it the sigil," Sable said. "A god-shaped thing that was never a god. Bound into flesh — a king, a priest, whoever the binding caught — and bound by what it lacks. It has no past of its own, do you understand? No history. No self. Everything it is, it borrowed. And everything borrowed can be repossessed." Her finger traced the diagram's hollow center. "For a century it slept inside its host and dreamed of unbinding. Now the old bindings are thinning — the gods going quiet did more damage than anyone understood — and the sigil has found a way to buy its freedom. Every memory it takes is a thread pulled out of the weave that holds it. Take enough threads—"

"—and the weave comes apart," Quentin finished.

"Take enough threads, and there's no longer enough of the host left to hold the knot." Sable turned. "King Aldric signed away his father's funeral last week. Did you hear? He asked his chamberlain why the palace kitchens were baking mourning bread. He has forgotten his own coronation oath — the archivists found the copy and he read it like a stranger's will. The man is nearly empty, Quentin. And when he's empty entirely, whatever sits inside him steps out wearing the crown."

---

They worked through the afternoon while the light failed over the Tanners' Reach and the candle-shop below sent up its sweet burnt smell. Sable talked; Quentin wrote. It was the first time in three days that her hands had felt like her own.

They built the reconciliation together. Sable supplied the prayers and the witnesses; Quentin built the columns, tested the sums, hunted the inconsistencies the way a hound hunts blood. Forty-one answered prayers in ninety-one days. Forty-one memories taken, each one weighed and selected with a precision that made the hair rise on Quentin's arms — because she knew that precision. She had seen it in the winter accounts. She had seen it in her own handwriting, in a number that should never have existed.

"There's a rate," she said at last, staring at the totals. "Do you see it? It's not taking randomly. Look at the progression." She turned the ledger toward Sable. "In the first month, the payments were small — small prayers, small losses. A name here, an afternoon there. Second month, larger. Third month, larger again. It's escalating on a curve. And the curve—" She did the arithmetic in her head, twice, because the answer refused to be believed the first time. "The curve is convergent. It's approaching a limit."

"What limit?"

"A total." Quentin heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for delivering bad numbers to men who wanted to kill the messenger. "There's a fixed sum of memory it needs. A quantity. And it is spending the city's pasts to reach that exact figure — no more, no less. This isn't appetite, Sable. This is a budget. It knows precisely how much remains between Aldric and the open air."

Sable had gone very pale. "How much remains?"

"I can't know without knowing the starting sum. But if the escalation holds—" Quentin ran it again, hating the elegance of it, the tidiness. "Weeks. Perhaps six. Perhaps fewer. It will finish emptying the king sometime before spring thaw, and then it won't need to grant wishes anymore, because it will no longer need permission to take." She closed the ledger. "Everything after that point is theft."

The room was quiet except for the settling creak of old paper on the walls. Somewhere below, the candlemaker was banking her fires for the night.

"You understand what you're describing," Sable said quietly. "You're describing a creature that does arithmetic."

"I'm describing a creature that thinks arithmetic is all there is." Quentin stood and walked to the wall of papers, reading the fragments of prayers strung there like moths pinned in a case. *Let her live. Let me forget. Let me go home.* So many of them small. So many of them desperate. People bargaining away the best of themselves because the worst of themselves was unbearable — and she knew that transaction from the inside, knew the weight of the pen and the taste of the ink and the particular silence that follows a signature. "That's its weakness. It prices everything. It believes everything can be priced. It has never met a debt it couldn't enter in a column."

"And you have?"

Quentin didn't answer. Her eye had caught on a strip of paper near the bottom of the wall, half-hidden behind a newer note. The writing on it was Sable's, but older, hurried, the charcoal worn to a ghost. She leaned close.

*Q.Z. — guild clerk, age approx. 20 at acquisition. Watch her.*

She turned. "You've known about me longer than three days."

"I've known *of* you for two years." Sable didn't flinch. "I keep files on everyone connected to the gate. You were a footnote. A guild clerk with a nine-year-old contract and no visible past — do you have any idea how rare you are? Most people's histories are messy. Yours was *clean*. Purchased clean. I flagged you and I waited, because footnotes have a way of becoming chapters." She crossed the room and stood close, close enough that Quentin could see the exhaustion under the priestess's composure, the fear under the exhaustion. "Then three nights ago you appeared on my stair soaked in sleet, telling me your own name was written in a ledger older than your guild, asking me to teach you about the sigil. Footnote, meet chapter."

"Why does it matter?" Quentin asked. "Why watch me?"

"Because of the date." Sable reached past her and unpinned a different paper — a timeline, long and ruled, marked with the forty-one answered prayers in ascending order. At its head, nine years back, sat a single entry in a circle. "Your contract was signed nine years ago this winter, Quentin. Before any of this began. Before the first prayer was answered, before the first memory was taken, before anyone in this city knew a sigil existed. Whatever bought your memory planted you here the way a man plants a field — *in advance*. You weren't sheltered by the guild." Her voice dropped. "You were positioned."

The word went into Quentin like a splinter, small and deep, and she knew she would be working it out of herself for a long time. *Positioned.* Not saved. Not purchased even — seeded. She thought of Oren Vesper's face when he burned her report, the careful blankness of a man guarding a secret he'd been paid to keep. She thought of the gate humming her name.

But underneath the vertigo, something colder and calmer was assembling itself — the thing that happened when the numbers finally balanced. She looked at the wall of prayers, at the ascending curve of thefts, at the convergent sum, and she felt what she always felt at the moment a fraud revealed its structure: not despair. Recognition. A cheat is only a cheat until you understand its rule. After that, it's a weakness wearing a disguise.

"It escalates on a schedule," she said slowly. "It pays exact prices. It keeps perfect accounts — I'd bet my life its hoard is ordered, indexed, *ledgered*. Things that keep perfect accounts can be audited."

Sable stared at her. "You want to audit a god-shaped absence."

"I want to reconcile it," Quentin said. "Line by line. Debt by debt. And then I want to find the entry it can't balance." She touched the circled date at the head of the timeline — her own signature, nine years old, waiting at the top of everything. "Every account in this city runs through that night, Sable. Through me. Which means somewhere in its hoard is a ledger line with my sister's name on it. And I am going to read every line above and below it until I understand exactly what it paid, exactly what it owes, and exactly where the books break."

Outside, the first snow of the true winter was beginning to fall, thick and soundless, covering the city the way ash covers a banked fire. Down in the streets, Quentin could see people turning their faces up toward it — and she saw, with terrible clarity, how many of them wore the loosened, grateful look of the answered, and how few of them remembered why they had ever needed to kneel.

"We need to move tonight," she said. "Before it takes anything else."

"Why tonight?"

"Because tomorrow morning," Quentin said, "the guild closes its doors. And I intend to be standing on the right side of them when it does."
