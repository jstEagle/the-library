## 10. The Guild Closes Its Doors

The summons came at dawn, folded into the slot of my door on paper I recognized before I touched it — guild stock, cream-colored, watermarked with the Vesper key. Nine years of reading that watermark had taught me its weight in the hand, the way a physician knows fever by the heat of a brow. Official business. Summoned like any other clerk.

I dressed with care anyway. If Oren Vesper wanted to pretend this was ordinary, I would let him pretend, and I would sit across from him with my face arranged into the same careful blankness I had worn for nine years, and inside me the thing Sable had shown me — the arithmetic of it, the whole terrible sum — would keep ticking like a clock in a locked drawer.

The guild-house was quiet in the way it had never been quiet before. Not the hush of early hours, when the clerks moved through the corridors with their ledgers and their lamps and their murmured tallies of impossible things. This was the silence of a held breath. The wardens stood at every landing, and they did not look at me as I passed, which was itself an answer: you do not look at a woman who is already counted.

* * *

Oren received me in the counting room, not his study. That was the first wrongness. The counting room was where the guild did its public face — where debtors were received and settlements witnessed and the great brass scales kept polished for show. It was a room built to be seen. His study was where the real work happened, behind three locks and a door that sweated iron in summer.

He stood at the long table with the shutters open behind him, and winter light came through gray and thin, laying itself across the table like a shroud laid out for measuring. He was not alone. Maren Cole stood at his left hand, the guild's second-oldest clerk, a woman so old her handwriting had gone from copperplate to spiderwork over forty years of service. And at his right, two wardens I did not know by name, though I knew their type: broad men with soft voices and hard hands, the kind the guild hired when a transaction required witnesses who could also lift things.

"Quentin." Oren's voice was warm. It was always warm. That was the horror of him, the thing I had mistaken all these years for kindness — he had the warmth of a hearth in a house where you were not sure you were guest or kindling. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please."

I sat. I folded my hands. I waited, because waiting was a discipline and I had made a religion of disciplines.

"You'll have heard," he said, "that the city has grown... excitable."

"I've heard prayers are being answered," I said. "I've heard what they cost."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, quickly buried. "Then you'll understand why the guild must act. We have served this city for two hundred years, Quentin. Through plague and famine and the long silence of the gods, we have been the one constant. A neutral ground. A place where anything could be bought and nothing could be stolen, because everything was paid for." He laid his palm flat on the table, and I saw that beneath his hand lay a document, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. "That neutrality ends today if we do not formalize it. The crown will come to us soon — perhaps already has, in ways we don't yet see. Factions will want the gate. Armies, if it comes to armies. And so the guild closes its doors."

"Closes them how?"

"The only way they can be closed." He turned the document toward me. "By lock."

I looked at the seal and felt the floor tilt under my chair, very slightly, the way a ship tilts when you realize the water is rising.

Because I knew that seal. I had written it.

Not the wax — the entry beneath it. Even upside down, even half-hidden by his palm, I knew my own hand the way I knew my own reflection: the narrow downstrokes, the small deliberate hooks where I crossed my sevens. It was the entry I had found in the forbidden archive six nights ago, the ledger older than the guild, the bargain nine years deep with my name at the bottom of it. He had copied it. Or had it copied. And now it lay on a table in daylight, being folded into something official.

"What is this?" I asked. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it.

"A lock," Oren said. "The gate beneath us was built to be opened twice — once to let the trade begin, once to end it. Every closing requires a key, and every key requires a debt. There is exactly one debtor alive whose debt is written in the old ledger's own hand." He smiled at me, gentle as snowfall. "You."

* * *

I want to say I stood up then. I want to say I shouted, or wept, or put my hands around his throat. What I actually did was worse. I listened. Nine years of training do not dissolve in a moment; they set like plaster. I sat in the counting room of the guild that owned me and I listened to the terms of my cage being read aloud, and some cold clerk-thing in the back of my skull began checking the figures.

Maren Cole read them. Her spiderwork voice went through the clauses like a psalm:

*The Vesper Guild, in the interest of continued neutrality, hereby seals the lower gate against all traffic until such time as the present disturbance resolves. The seal shall be effected by binding the outstanding debt of Quentin Zhou, entered in the elder ledger, to the gate mechanism itself. The debtor shall remain within the bounds of the city. The debtor shall not depart the city by any road, river, gate, or craft, on pain of the debt's immediate collection. The debt shall pass to no heir, for the debtor shall have none. Upon the debtor's death, the debt shall collect itself.*

I heard that last clause twice before it finished going through me.

"Die free," I said. "You mean the debt takes whatever's left. When I die, it collects the rest of me."

"The clause is standard," Maren said, without looking up.

"It's not standard. I've read four hundred contracts in that archive. Death-clauses release the estate. They don't—" I stopped. Breathed. "There is no estate, is there. Because there's nothing left of me that isn't already owed."

Oren watched me work it out with an expression I will remember until I die, which may be sooner than I once assumed: patience. Tenderness, even. The look of a man watching a sum arrive at its total.

"You understand," he said, "why it must be you."

"I understand why it's convenient that it's me."

"Quentin—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of me louder than I intended, and both wardens shifted, and I made myself smaller, quieter, colder. "Don't say my name like that. Don't say it like you're doing me a kindness. You bought me. Nine years ago someone from this guild stood in a plague-house and bought a dying girl's worst night, and I thought — I *thought* — that the guild sheltered me afterward. That the ledgers were a refuge. That you taught me numbers because numbers were safe." My hands were flat on the table now; I didn't remember putting them there. "You taught me numbers because I *am* one. A figure in an older book. A key someone paid for and stored in a clerk's body until it was needed."

The shutters rattled in a gust off the harbor. Somewhere below us — below the floors, below the cellars, below the sealed door with its seven locks that I had inspected just days ago while it hummed my name — something turned over in its sleep.

Oren did not flinch. He never flinched. "If it eases you to hate me, hate me. But know what you're hating. I was in that plague winter too, Quentin. I signed papers while children died in the streets, and I told myself the guild's rules were mercy, and some nights I still believe it. But the sigil under this city does not care what we believe. It cares what we owe. And right now the only wall between it and everything it wants is a piece of paper with your name on it." He slid the document across the table until it touched my fingertips. Cold wax. Cold iron gall ink. My own handwriting staring up at me like a drowned face. "So I am asking you to sign."

"Asking."

"Forcing, if I must. I'd rather ask."

"And if I refuse? If I walk out of here and keep walking — north road, river barge, anywhere —"

"Then the debt collects early." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow crueler. "You'd feel it start at your fingertips. Sable Ferrow could tell you what that looks like, if she remembers enough left to describe it. By the border you'd be a woman with no name walking in the wrong direction. By the mountains, nothing at all."

Sable. So he knew about Sable. Of course he knew. The guild had eyes in every parish and pawnshop in the city, and I had spent five nights sleeping behind a defrocked priestess's altar like a child hiding under blankets, believing the dark was mine.

* * *

They gave me an hour to decide. An hour, as if the matter were divisible into units, as if rage could be metered out like lamp oil. They put me in the scriptorium — my own scriptorium, my own desk, my own lamp with its green glass shade — and posted a warden at the door, and left me alone with the document and the sound of the building breathing around me.

I sat at my desk and I did not touch the pen.

Instead I did what I have always done when the world becomes unbearable: I audited.

Let us take inventory, I told myself. Item: one life, purchased nine years ago at the price of one night. The night in question contained a sister, Wren Zhou, aged eleven, who coughed blood onto my hands and told me a joke about crows and died at some hour I can no longer name because I sold the hour. Item: nine years of service rendered to the purchaser's agent, during which the debtor was fed, housed, educated, and shaped into a functioning component of the lock she was always meant to become. Item: one escape attempt, five nights old, currently compromised. Item: one ally, a priestess, whose value to me I decline to enter into any ledger the guild will ever see.

And item — the last item, the one I turned over and over like a coin too worn to read — the gate hummed my name. Before Oren ever showed me this document, before Maren unrolled her clauses, the gate beneath the guild-house had been humming *Quentin, Quentin, Quentin* into the dark like a thing calling a dog home. Which meant the lock was not new. Which meant Oren had not decided this yesterday. Which meant the paperwork was theater, and the cage had been built years ago, and all this morning's ceremony was the guild decorating bars it had installed before I was old enough to see them.

The rage came then, finally, properly — not the hot flash I might have wanted but a slow flooding cold thing, rising through the scriptorium like water through a hull. I looked at my ledgers stacked along the wall, nine years of my neat columns recording the trade in impossible things, and I understood that every column had been a brick. Every audit, every reconciliation, every midnight tally had been me mortaring myself into place. The cage was never the wardens. The cage was the paperwork. It had always been the paperwork, and I had loved the paperwork, because loving it was the only way to survive living inside it.

I picked up the pen.

Not to sign. Not yet. I picked it up because my hands needed to do something and because — forgive me, I am what they made me — because a thought had arrived, small and sharp as a pin: *a contract is only as strong as its terms, and terms can be read closely, and I am the best reader this guild has ever produced.*

I read the document the way I had never been permitted to read my original contract: slowly, line by line, hunting the spaces between the words. Maren's clauses were elegant. Old-fashioned in places, which meant old, which meant drawn from precedents older than the guild — and old law, I knew from the archive, had gaps. Gaps like the one about debts that collect themselves upon death. Gaps like the definition of *the city*, which the document took from a boundary-stone survey sixty years out of date. Gaps like the phrase *on pain of the debt's immediate collection*, which specified collection but not completion.

I was still reading when the bell above the counting room rang — the deep bronze bell that had not sounded in my nine years, the bell reserved, per the guild charter, for the declaration of closure. Its voice rolled down through the floors and up through the ceilings, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, rising through the stone like breath through a keyhole, the gate began to hum again.

My name. Over and over. Patient as winter.

The warden at the door cleared his throat. "Time," he said.

I rose. I carried the document in both hands, the way one carries a wounded bird or a live coal, and I walked back down to the counting room, and I signed my name at the bottom of my own cage in ink I had mixed myself a hundred times — iron gall, oak apple, a drop of clove oil against rot.

But as the nib touched the paper, I did one thing more. One small thing, invisible, the kind of thing only another ledger-keeper would ever catch. In the margin, beside the death-clause, in letters so fine they might be flourishes, I wrote a single word in shorthand of my own devising — a cipher I invented at fifteen and taught to no one, not even Oren, especially not Oren.

The word was *unpaid*.

Then I blotted the page, and the guild's doors closed forever, and somewhere beneath my feet a god-shaped absence settled deeper into its king and smiled at the taste of a signature.

I walked back to my desk past the wardens, past Maren with her folded hands, past Oren Vesper and his hearth-warm eyes, and I bowed my head exactly as far as courtesy required.

And I thought: *you have locked a door with me as the key. Fine. But keys turn.*
