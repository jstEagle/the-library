## 11. Breaking into the Palace

The winter court of King Aldric kept its accounts the way a drunkard keeps his promises: generously, and never twice the same way.

Quentin learned this in three days of reading what Sable's contacts could smuggle out of the palace chancery — copies of copies, water-damaged, initialed by clerks whose hands shook. She read them by candlelight in the back room of a shuttered chandlery that smelled of old tallow and colder dust, and she read them the way she had once read the Vesper Guild's ledgers: not for what they said, but for what they were trying not to say.

"They're hiding something," Sable said, watching her work. The priestess sat cross-legged on an upturned crate, her grey order-robe replaced by a wool dress the color of wet slate, her shaved head wrapped against the cold. She looked wrong in it. She looked like a woman pretending to be a woman, which was perhaps the best disguise either of them owned.

"Everyone hides something in their accounts," Quentin said. "Most people hide their mistakes. This is different." She turned a page toward the candlelight. "Look at the grain tariffs. Third month of autumn, the crown purchased forty thousand measures at a fixed price. Here's the contract. Here's the payment record. Here's the warehouse receipt confirming delivery." She laid the three documents side by side. "All of them agree perfectly."

"That sounds like good bookkeeping."

"It sounds like *impossible* bookkeeping. No transaction that large agrees perfectly across three documents. Somewhere there's a clerk who miscounts, a merchant who rounds in his own favor, a warehouse rat who eats through a receipt. Perfection isn't accuracy. Perfection is forgery." She tapped the tariff contract with one finger. "Someone wrote all three of these from the same hand, after the fact, to make the past look orderly. And they did it well enough that only another forger would feel the itch."

"And you feel the itch."

"I've felt it since I was nineteen years old." She gathered the pages back into their oilcloth. "But here's the part that matters. Whoever forged these didn't do it to steal. There's no missing money. No skimming. The numbers balance down to the copper. They did it because the *real* records stopped making sense — because the king signed things he couldn't remember signing, ordered things he couldn't remember ordering, and some terrified chancery clerk started rewriting history so the crown's papers wouldn't contradict themselves in public."

Sable was quiet a moment. Outside, the wind came down off the mountains and worried the shutters like a dog worrying a bone. "You think the sigil keeps its own books."

"I think everything keeps books. Gods, guilds, kings. The question is always who holds the pen." Quentin tied the oilcloth closed. "And I intend to walk into that palace holding one."

* * *

They went in as debt.

It had been Sable's idea first, and then, once Quentin took hold of it, it became hers entirely — reshaped, refined, made true. The winter court, hungry and frightened and praying itself hollow, had begun summoning creditors to the palace to beg indulgence on the crown's obligations. Every merchant house, every bank, every guild with a claim on the treasury was being granted audience, one by one, in a chancery chamber beneath the great hall. The line of petitioners stretched through the outer ward each morning, breath steaming, ledgers clutched to chests like children.

"You can't forge your way into the throne room," Sable had said. "But you can forge your way into the queue."

So Quentin forged.

She worked four nights, and they were the best four nights of work she had ever done, and she knew it even as she did it, the way a singer knows a note is true while it is still leaving her throat. She built a merchant house out of nothing: *House Corvel*, dealers in northern timber and southern dye, established sixty-one years ago, with a credit history she wrote backward from the present — every loan issued, repaid, renewed, defaulted once (a small, believable default, twenty-two years back, during the salt famine), recovered. She aged the paper with tea and smoke and handled it with tallow-slicked fingers until the creases looked lived-in. She invented a dead founder with a plausible signature and practiced that signature until her hand produced it in her sleep, and then she used it exactly eleven times across eleven documents, because twelve would have been showing off.

"Forgeries fail from vanity," Oren Vesper had told her once, years ago, standing over her shoulder in the counting room while she copied bills of lading. "The amateur makes everything beautiful. The master makes everything *tired*. Remember that, Quentin. Tired documents pass. Beautiful ones get hanged."

She hated that she still heard his voice. She hated more that he had been right.

On the fifth morning she dressed in House Corvel's colors — a plain charcoal coat with a faded green lining, chosen because faded green suggested money that had once been new — and pinned the forged seal to her breast, and she and Sable joined the petitioner's queue in the outer ward as the snow came down in fat, indifferent flakes.

"What's our claim?" Sable murmured. They had rehearsed this, but rehearsal was a kind of prayer, and Quentin had learned that prayers got answered lately, and answers cost.

"Fourteen thousand crowns outstanding on dye contracts, plus interest accrued since midsummer. We are owed, we are patient, we are willing to discuss restructuring if the crown grants us favorable terms on future procurement." Quentin kept her eyes forward, her breath shallow. "We are boring. Boring is armor."

"And if they ask why a timber-and-dye house sent two women?"

"Because the founder's sons are dead and the daughters-in-law run the books now. It's in our file. Everything's in our file. That's the point of a file."

The queue moved by inches. Around them the ward filled with the particular misery of the indebted: a wool factor arguing with his son in low furious tones; a widow in mourning velvet who kept opening her ledger and closing it again without reading anything; two brothers from the river trade who had stopped speaking to each other somewhere around the third hour and now stood facing opposite directions, united only in grievance. Everyone's breath hung white. Everyone's eyes had the same look Quentin had seen on the fishwife's face — the look of people who had prayed and been answered and could no longer quite remember what they had lost to afford it.

A guard worked down the line checking seals against a manifest. He reached them near noon.

"House?" His voice was flat with cold and repetition.

"Corvel. Timber and dye. Fourteen thousand crowns, plus interest." Quentin handed over the letter of introduction and did not watch him read it. Watching a man read your forgery was how forgers died; the urge to search his face for doubt was vanity, and vanity got hanged.

The guard read. Snow accumulated on his helmet brim. Somewhere behind Quentin's sternum her heart performed a slow, deliberate act of insubordination.

"Tamberly and—" he squinted at the second name, "—Tamberly and Voss?"

"My partner and I. The house uses married names for its principals. It's in the file."

He grunted, stamped the letter with a leaden *admitted*, and moved on. Sable exhaled beside her, a long plume of white.

"*Voss*," Sable muttered under her breath. "You gave me my own name."

"Forged names should be close enough to true ones to answer to. If someone shouts 'Voss' in a crisis, you'll turn around. That's worth more than anonymity." Quentin allowed herself one breath of relief, rationed like everything else. "Besides, you've spent nine years being called Sister Sable. You should get to be something else for a day."

* * *

The chancery chamber beneath the great hall was warm, and the warmth was the first wrong thing.

Every other room in the palace, from what they saw of it — the escorted corridors, the anterooms, the stair down — was cold in the way buildings get when the people inside have stopped truly living in them. Fires lit but untended. Tapestries askew where no one had straightened them. Servants who moved through the halls with the smooth, vacant efficiency of sleepwalkers, answering questions a half-second too late, smiling a half-second too long.

But the chancery chamber was warm, and bright, and busy. A dozen clerks bent over desks beneath a ceiling painted with clouds, copying, collating, sealing. A fire burned in a hearth big enough to roast an ox. And along the far wall stood a cabinet of ledgers — dozens of them, bound in white vellum, spines unmarked — and every clerk who passed that cabinet touched it, briefly, the way superstitious sailors touch the mast.

Quentin felt the hum before she understood what she was feeling. Not sound. Something under sound. A pressure in the teeth, a tremor in the inkwell, the sensation of standing too near a struck bell.

"The gate," she breathed. "That's the gate's hum. Under the palace."

Sable's hand found her wrist under the table. "Later," the priestess murmured. "Face forward. We're being received."

The receiving clerk was a narrow man named Pellwin with ink-stained cuffs and the desperate competence of someone holding a dam together with his body. He reviewed their claim, asked three questions about dye lots and one about the salt-famine default — Quentin answered all four without hesitation, because she had written the answers herself and believed them completely, which was the whole secret of lying — and then set down his pen and rubbed his eyes.

"Restructuring terms will require the chancellor's approval," he said. "Or the king's. And I'll be honest with you, mistress, because you seem like a woman who respects honesty in matters of money." He lowered his voice. "Half the claims brought before the court these past two months don't exist. Oh, the documents are real enough — real paper, real seals, real signatures. But the debts underneath them are phantoms. Houses claiming loans that were never made. Merchants with receipts for goods that never left their warehouses." He laughed, short and unhappy. "I've taken to auditing the petitioners before I audit their claims. Which is why yours took so long. Your House Corvel checks out. Sixty-one years of clean history. Frankly, it's the cleanest file I've seen all season, and I'd very much like to know where you found a clerk good enough to keep it that way."

*Tired documents pass,* Quentin thought. *Beautiful ones get hanged.* Aloud she said, "Our founder believed a ledger should read like a life. Long, mostly dull, and honest in the places people check."

Pellwin smiled tiredly and stamped their petition. "Chancellor's review within the week. Possibly the king himself, if the matter's large enough. Fourteen thousand crowns may qualify." He glanced toward the white-vellum cabinet, and for just a moment his professional weariness cracked into something rawer — fear, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Mistress Tamberly. May I offer unsolicited advice? Whatever business brings you to this court, conclude it quickly. People here forget things. Not little things. *Themselves.* My own junior clerk signed a loyalty oath last month and asked me the next morning what a loyalty oath was. The physicians say it's a fever. The priests say it's a judgment." He straightened his cuffs with trembling precision. "I say it's arithmetic. Something is collecting. And I keep books, so I know what collecting looks like."

* * *

They were lodged that night in the petitioners' wing — a long dormitory of narrow beds where visiting creditors slept under the same roof as their hopes. Quentin lay awake in the dark listening to the building breathe, and beneath the breathing, faint but unmistakable, the hum. It had changed since the autumn. She was almost sure it had changed. Less like a bell now. More like a voice clearing its throat.

*Sable,* she thought. *Are you awake.*

But she didn't whisper it aloud, because the walls of palaces had ears, and because there was a better use for the dark hours than talk.

She rose, took out her writing kit, and by the light of a shielded candle began to draft — not a forgery this time, but something stranger. A private ledger. A single column of entries in her smallest, most careful hand:

*Nine years' wages paid to Q.Z., room and board, per original contract. Value: assessed.*
*One memory, held as collateral, per original contract. Value: unassessed.*
*One debt, outstanding, debtor: the sigil. Creditor: Q.Z. Terms: pending renegotiation.*

She stared at the last line for a long time. Then she added, beneath it, in letters so small they were nearly a secret from herself:

*A debt unpaid accrues interest. Even gods pay interest. Especially gods.*

"You're doing arithmetic," Sable whispered from the next bed. The priestess hadn't been asleep after all. "At midnight. In an enemy's house."

"I'm preparing terms of settlement. If I'm going to sit across a table from that thing, I want to arrive the way a creditor arrives — with the numbers already done." Quentin blew out the candle. In the darkness the hum seemed closer, or perhaps she was simply listening harder. "Everyone prays to it, Sable. Everyone begs. Nobody has ever once presented it with an invoice."

"And you think it will honor one?"

"I think it's a creature of contracts. Contracts have rules. Rules have seams." She pulled the blanket to her chin. "And I think it already knows I'm coming. It let us through the door. Things that hoard memories don't leave the one memory they can't price wandering around unattended unless they want something."

The hum swelled, very slightly, and settled — satisfied, or amused, or simply patient. Quentin closed her eyes and did not sleep, and in the gray hour before dawn she heard, distantly, through stone and snow and sleeping court, footsteps in the corridor outside the petitioners' wing. Slow. Unhurried. They paused outside the door — paused long enough to be noticed, which no accidental pause ever is — and then moved on.

When she finally looked, the frost on the inside of the windowpane had melted in a single patch, roughly the size of a handprint, at exactly the height of her own eye.

Below it, traced in the dripping water by some finger she never saw, was a small, neat figure: a ledger line, ruled straight, with a blank space waiting at the end of it for her signature.
