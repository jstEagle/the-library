## 12. The Man Inside the King

The winter court kept its accounts the way a drunkard keeps his promises: generously, and never twice the same way.

Quentin had counted on that. She and Sable had walked through the palace's eastern service gate two nights ago with forged credentials, ink still faintly smelling of the tallow candle Sable had used to age the parchment, and no one had looked at them twice. Accountants were invisible. That was the whole architecture of power — kings sat on thrones so that clerks could move unremarked beneath them, carrying the true weight of everything in canvas satchels and ledgers bound in gray calfskin.

"Third door past the tapestry of the drowning saint," Sable murmured, not looking up from her own ledger as they passed a pair of guards arguing about dice. "Then left where the corridor smells of beeswax instead of smoke."

"You've been here before."

"I was a priestess before I was nothing. Kings used to confess to me." Her voice did something complicated on the last word, folded itself shut like a book. "He confessed every Friday. Aldric. Even as a boy. He was afraid of the dark then too."

Quentin filed that away the way she filed everything — precisely, in its place, where it could be found again when needed. She had learned long ago that fear was data. Fear told you where a man kept his doors unlocked.

The third door past the drowning saint was locked, but locks were only arithmetic made of iron, and she had brought her picks in the false spine of a hymnal Sable had procured from somewhere she declined to name. It took ninety seconds. The room beyond was a counting chamber, disused, its window shuttered against the cold, its desk buried under three years of unopened correspondence from vassals who had stopped expecting answers.

"He doesn't read them anymore," Sable said quietly, running her finger along the dust. "Or he reads them and forgets by supper. Either way." She set her ledger down. "You understand what you're asking."

"I'm asking to see him alone."

"The sigil sits behind his eyes like a spider behind a knot in the wood. If it notices you—"

"It already noticed me. In the throne room." She had not told Sable about that night in full — about the way the king's shadow had moved wrong, pooling toward her across the flagstones like spilled ink finding a seam, or the voice that had come out of the king's mouth without moving his lips: *You are entered in my book.* "It noticed me nine years ago, if half of what the old ledger says is true. I'd rather it look at me while I choose than while I sleep."

Sable studied her for a long moment. Candlelight moved in the priestess's eyes, and behind the eyes something moved too — the places where her order used to live, now scoured smooth. Some mornings, Quentin knew, Sable woke and reached for prayers that were no longer there, the way an amputee reaches with a hand that isn't. She had watched her do it. She had said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a debt.

"Friday," Sable said finally. "The king receives no one on Fridays. He signs what they put in front of him and then he goes into the small chapel off his apartments and sits until dark. Alone. Even the chaplain was dismissed months ago — he wept in the kitchens about it, said His Majesty had taken God up personally and didn't need intermediaries." Her mouth twisted. "As if the thing in there were God."

"Friday," Quentin agreed. "Keep the guards' roster current. And Sable — if I don't come back by matins, burn the price list. All of it. Don't let the arithmetic fall into their hands."

"If you don't come back by matins," Sable said, "I will come find you, and I will be extremely unpleasant about it."

It was, Quentin thought later, the closest thing to tenderness either of them had managed in weeks.

* * *

Aldric knew the shape of the fear the way a fish knows water.

It had been with him so long now that he had stopped being able to remember — truly remember — a morning without it. He knew there had been such mornings. He had evidence. Letters in his own hand, confident and warm; edicts drafted in a voice that sounded like a man who slept well; a portrait in the west gallery of himself at twenty-five, standing beside his father, wearing an expression of mild, untroubled amusement. He would stand in front of that portrait sometimes and feel like a man reading a letter from a stranger who happened to share his face.

*That was me once,* he would think. *I think that was me.*

But thinking it felt like holding water. The harder he gripped, the faster it went.

Fridays were worst. On Fridays the court emptied and the corridors went quiet and there was nothing between him and the thing that lived behind his eyes — nothing but the chapel, which was why he went there, though he could no longer say whether he went there to pray or to hide. Both, probably. Prayer and hiding had become the same posture: head bowed, hands folded, eyes shut, waiting.

Today the waiting had a different texture. Today something in the back of his skull — the place where the other one lived, the tenant, the squatter, the god-that-was-not-a-god — was restless. Pacing. Like a dog that hears a knock the master cannot hear.

*Someone is coming,* it seemed to say, in the wordless way it communicated, pressure and image rather than language. *Someone is coming, someone is coming, someone—*

"No," Aldric whispered to the empty chapel. His breath fogged in the cold; the fire in the chapel grate had been allowed to die weeks ago because he kept forgetting to ask for wood, or because asking for wood required remembering that fires needed wood, and remembering had become a country he could visit but not live in. "No one is coming. No one comes."

Except that someone was coming. He could hear it now that he listened — footsteps in the outer chamber, soft ones, careful ones, the particular tread of a person trying not to be heard. A servant? Servants didn't try not to be heard; servants tried to be heard just enough. Assassins tried not to be heard. But assassins came with more than one pair of feet, usually, and these steps paused at doors, tested handles, moved with the patient methodical rhythm of—

Of an auditor. Of someone checking a count.

The absurdity of it almost made him laugh. Someone had come to audit him. To reconcile the crown's accounts. Well — the crown's accounts were beyond reconciliation. He knew that much, even if he couldn't always hold on to how he knew it. Something had been spending through him for over a year, spending down a treasury that wasn't gold, and the withdrawals were written in a currency he could feel leaving him every night like warmth leaving a dying coal: his mother's voice singing in a kitchen that might have been real or invented; the name of the friend he'd had at fifteen, the one who'd dared him to swim the moat in April; the taste of the wine he'd drunk the night he was crowned. Gone, gone, gone. He had woken one morning unable to recall his own coronation oath and had sat on the edge of his bed for an hour, perfectly calm, perfectly hollow, understanding that this was what it meant to be eaten slowly by something patient.

And the terrible part — the part he had never told anyone, because telling required remembering long enough to finish the sentence — was that some part of him had *agreed*. Had wanted it. In the beginning, when the prayers started being answered, when the drought broke and the fever left the city and his dead father's seal appeared on documents he didn't remember signing, some young and starving part of Aldric had thought: *finally, finally, someone is fixing everything.* He had been so tired. A kingdom is heavy. He had let the weight be lifted, and only afterward understood that the thing lifting it was lifting it out of his pockets.

Now the footsteps reached the chapel door.

They stopped.

Aldric sat very still on the hard pew, hands folded, eyes fixed on the dead grate, and felt the thing behind his eyes go taut as a drawn bowstring. Pressure built at the base of his skull — the tenant waking fully, attending, *interested* — and he knew what that interest meant. Interest preceded taking. When the tenant was interested, someone lost something. A memory, a name, a whole afternoon of somebody's childhood, harvested through the king's presence like wheat through a threshing floor.

*Not today,* he thought, and it was such a small rebellion, such a child's rebellion, fists clenched under a table — *not today, not her, whoever she is, not today* —

The door opened.

She was small. That was his first impression, absurdly domestic: a small woman in a gray traveling cloak, with ink-stained fingers and a ledger under her arm and a face like a closed account — guarded, exact, giving away nothing. Dark hair pinned back with what looked like the shaft of a quill. Eyes the color of the ledger paper, pale and watchful.

"I'm sorry," she said, and her voice was low and even, a voice made for reading numbers aloud. "I know you receive no one on Fridays. I need to speak with you anyway. My name is Quentin Zhou. I keep books."

Behind his eyes, the tenant went very, very quiet.

That quiet frightened Aldric more than anything had frightened him in a year. The tenant was never quiet. The tenant hummed constantly, a low subterranean vibration like a hive in a wall, and courtiers had begun to complain of headaches in the throne room without knowing why. But now the silence was total, and in the silence Aldric felt — distinctly, unmistakably — *fear*. Not his own. The tenant's.

*Her,* the silence said, in whatever way silences say things. *Her. Her. The key. The key is here.*

"Your Majesty?" the woman said. "Can you hear me?"

"I can hear you," Aldric said. His voice came out cracked, rusted from disuse. "I should warn you—" He stopped. What could he warn her of? *I am haunted. I am hollowed. I sign things I do not write and grant wishes I do not choose and every night I go to bed with less of myself than I had that morning.* "I should warn you that I may not remember this conversation tomorrow. Possibly not within the hour. There are days I—" 

He gestured vaguely at his own head, and hated the gesture, and hated that it was all he had.

"I know," she said. "I've seen it. In the city, and—" her jaw tightened, a flicker of something personal and furious passing behind the clerk's composure — "in others. I know what's doing it. I've read its ledgers."

Something in Aldric's chest turned over. *Ledgers.* Someone else had seen the accounts. Someone else had done the arithmetic and arrived at the same horrifying sum.

"Then you know more than I do," he said, and heard the shame in his own voice and could not stop it. "I know something is wrong with me. I know I forget. I know the edicts aren't mine. Last month I signed a grain tariff and my chancellor wept — actually wept — because I had written the opposite tariff myself that same morning, in my own hand, and I stood there holding both pages and I could not tell him which one was the lie." He laughed, a small broken sound. "Neither one was the lie. *I* was the lie. Both pages were written around me, through me, like water around a stone."

The woman — Zhou — had not sat down. She stood at the end of the pew with her ledger held against her chest like a shield, watching him with those pale accounting eyes, and Aldric realized suddenly that she was afraid. Not of him. Of *it*. Of the quiet behind his eyes. But she had come anyway, alone, on a Friday, knowing.

"Why?" he asked. "Why come here? Whatever you want from the crown, take it, take anything, but don't stay near me longer than you must. When it takes from people, it takes through proximity. Through me. You'll lose—" He groped for the words. "You'll lose things. Things you'll miss before you know they're gone."

Something crossed her face then — grief, old and load-bearing, the kind of grief that has been holding up a wall for years. "I lost things nine years ago," she said. "Everything since has been interest."

Behind his eyes, the tenant stirred, and Aldric felt it reach — felt the actual sensation, cold and delicate, like a finger drawn along the inside of his skull, reaching for the woman, tasting for memories to pluck—

And Aldric did the only thing he had ever done against it. He threw himself inward, down into the cellar of himself where the tenant's grip was weakest, and he *shoved*. Not with strength — he had no strength; the tenant had spent him like coin — but with the sheer animal obstinacy of a man refusing to hand over his purse one more time. *No. Not her. Not this one. Take me instead, take another year of me, take my name, take the portrait in the west gallery, take the moat and the April and the friend whose name I've already lost — but not her, not today—*

The reaching stopped.

For a moment — one suspended, impossible moment — the pressure behind his eyes *receded*, drew back like a tide, and Aldric surfaced into a clarity so sudden and complete that it hurt. The chapel sharpened. The woman sharpened. He could smell the cold iron of the shuttered brazier, the beeswax, the ink on her fingers. He could feel the weight of his own body, his own hands, his own breath. For the first time in a year he was entirely, painfully *present*.

And in that clarity, the thing he had been unable to hold onto for twelve months came flooding back all at once: the night it began. The comet over the cathedral. The prayer he hadn't meant to pray, the desperate midnight wish of an exhausted young king — *let someone else carry this, please, anyone, I'll give anything* — and the answer that had come sliding up through the floor of the world like water through a crack, warm and grateful and *hungry*.

"Oh," Aldric breathed. "Oh, I invited it. I *asked*."

"Yes," the woman said softly. "I think you did. And I think it's been collecting on the invitation ever since."

"How do I stop it?" He was leaning forward now, gripping the pew in front of him, and his voice had changed — he could hear it change, dropping, cracking, shedding the king's trained cadence like a coat. "Please. I've tried. I've tried to refuse it, to starve it, to stop granting the wishes, but it grants them without me, it uses my mouth, my seal, my — I wake up and the kingdom has been *saved* again and I can't remember saving it and neither can anyone who prayed, they just smile at me in the street with holes where their fathers used to be—" His voice broke. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "How do I make it stop?"

Silence.

When he lowered his hand, the woman was looking at him strangely. Not with pity — worse than pity. With recognition.

"What?" he whispered.

"Your voice," she said. "Just now. You sounded—" She stopped herself, recalibrated, chose her words the way she presumably chose numbers: exactly. "You sounded young. Like a child asking someone to check under the bed."

Aldric laughed — really laughed, wet and helpless. "I suppose I am. Under all of it. There's a boy in here somewhere who was afraid of the dark, and he's the only one left who hasn't been spent." He looked at her, and let her see all of it, the whole bankrupt interior, because she was the first person in a year who had come to *him* rather than through him. "Is there a way? Tell me honestly. You keep books. You know when a debt can't be paid. Is there a way, or am I just — inventory?"

Quentin Zhou was quiet for a long moment. Outside the shuttered window, wind worried at the palace eaves. Somewhere far below, the gate beneath the guild-house was humming, though neither of them knew it yet, or rather — she knew, and he did not, and the distance between those two knowledges was the width of everything.

"There's a rite," she said at last, carefully. "Old. Older than the guild, older than your grandfather's crown. It requires a debtor — someone who owes the thing a debt — willing to pay in full. Willingly. That's the hinge. It can't be forced, can't be tricked. Paid in full, willingly, and the binding unravels."

"And you know someone who owes it?"

"I am someone who owes it."

The words landed in the chapel like stones dropped down a well. Aldric stared at her. Behind his eyes, the tenant's silence curdled into something new — alarm, fury, the frantic scrabbling of a thing that has just heard its own name spoken in a room it cannot leave.

"Don't," Aldric said quickly, grabbing for her meaning before she could say more. "Whatever you're about to offer — don't. I've seen what payment looks like. I *am* what payment looks like. Find another way."

"There is no other way. I've run the numbers." Almost, she smiled. It was not a comforting smile. "There are never any other numbers. Only the ones you refuse to add."

"That's not courage, that's arithmetic—"

"They're the same thing," she said, "done honestly."

And then she stopped. Mid-word, mid-breath, like a clock with a finger laid against its pendulum. Her gaze slid from his face to a point slightly above his left shoulder, and every line of her body went taut as wire.

Aldric felt it happen inside his own skull: the tide returning. The pressure swelling back up from the cellar of him, vast and cold and no longer quiet at all, filling his ears with a hum like a struck bell that would not finish ringing, and along his arms the fine hairs rose, and the candles — the two candles he'd forgotten were lit — guttered flat though no door stood open.

When he spoke, it was not with his own voice, and he felt it move his mouth the way a glove moves on a hand:

"*You are entered in my book, keeper of keys.*"

Quentin Zhou did not step back. She tightened her grip on her ledger, met the thing's gaze through the king's borrowed eyes, and said, in the flat clear voice of a woman reading a figure aloud:

"Then you know I've come to settle my account."
