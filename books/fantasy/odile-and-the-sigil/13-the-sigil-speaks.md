## 13. The Sigil Speaks

The candle had burned down to a coin of wax when the throne room began to breathe.

Quentin had been in the palace for six days by then — six days of forged manifests and reconciled debts, of walking the winter corridors with a clerk's bow and a thief's heartbeat, and she had learned the palace the way she learned everything: by its numbers. The number of steps from the servants' stair to the west gallery. The number of guards at the king's door, which was four by day and two by night, and how the night pair changed their watch at the third bell and left a gap of ninety heartbeats. The number of candles the chamberlain ordered weekly, which had doubled since midwinter, because the king now woke every night screaming at a darkness he could not remember fearing.

She had not come tonight to see the sigil. She had come to see the man inside it.

Sable had told her it was possible — that under the right pressure, at the right hour, the host surfaced like a drowning swimmer breaking water, hands grasping at anything warm. The hour was the one before dawn, Sable said, when the sigil's attention thinned the way ice thins over a river, all its vastness stretched toward the prayers still arriving from the city below. Quentin had spent three nights learning that thin place. She had mapped it as she mapped everything, in columns and margins, and on this night, the fourth, she stood in the gallery above the throne room with her ledger against her chest and waited for the king to surface.

He did not.

Instead, the room went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

* * *

It began with the light. There were braziers in the throne room, banked low, and their embers should have been orange. They turned white. Not bright — white the way paper is white, the way a page is white before anything is written on it, an absence dressed up as radiance. Quentin felt her eyes begin to hurt and understood, in the language of her trade, that she was looking at something that did not reflect light but consumed the act of seeing itself.

Then the humming started.

She knew that hum. She had heard it once beneath the guild-house, down in the dark where the sealed gate slept behind nine locks and Oren Vesper's solemn oaths, and she had heard it singing there in a register no instrument could name — a sound that was less sound than intention, a vibration that arrived already understood, like a word spoken directly into the marrow. That night, months ago, the gate had hummed her name. *Quentin Zhou.* Three notes, repeated, patient as interest accruing.

Here, in the throne room, the hum was louder. It did not say her name.

It said her account.

* * *

She heard it before she understood it, the way one hears a familiar melody transposed into a strange key. A rhythm. A sequence. Long tone, short tone, short tone, long — and then again, and again, each repetition adding a figure, and Quentin, who had spent nine years reading ledgers in the dark by tallow-light until the numbers swam like fish, realized with a lurch that the thing was counting. It was reciting figures. Her figures. The winter accounts of the Vesper Guild, the prayer-transactions, the balances carried forward, the sums she had entered in her own careful hand — it knew them. All of them. It had read her ledgers the way she read them: completely, covetously, line by line.

And then it stopped counting hers, and began to speak.

The voice did not come from the throne. It came from everywhere the light wasn't — from the shadows between the pillars, from the cold air above the dais, from somewhere just behind her left ear, intimate as a confessor. It was not a voice like a man's. It was a voice like a ledger's: dry, exact, without warmth or cruelty, every sentence balanced to the digit.

*You are listening,* it said. *Good. Auditors who listen are worth more than auditors who look.*

Quentin stood very still. Her first instinct was fear, and her second was fury, and her third — the one that frightened her most — was professional recognition. She knew this voice's grammar. She had invented half of it. The guild's transactional liturgy, the formulas of exchange and consideration and discharge, the cold elegant syntax by which impossible things were bought and sold — the sigil spoke it fluently, natively, better than Oren Vesper ever had. Of course it did. Somewhere in the oldest archive of the guild-house, in a ledger older than the guild itself, her name sat as the final entry of a nine-year-old bargain. This thing had written the style. The guild had only ever been its clerical order, its franchise, its — she searched for the word and found it with a chill —

— its branch office.

*You have questions,* the voice said. *I have answers. But you are an accountant, Quentin Zhou, and I will not insult you with gifts. Everything is a transaction. Shall we open an account?*

"I don't make bargains with things that eat people," she said aloud, and her voice sounded small and human in the vast white-lit dark, and she hated how small, and held to it anyway. "I've read your terms. I've seen what you leave in the drawer afterward. The fishwife on Salt Row gave you one evening with her dead husband and you took twenty years of him from her. Twenty years. She can't remember his face. She signed for a night and you billed her for a marriage."

*An accurate citation,* the sigil said, and something in the dryness of its approval made her skin crawl worse than any threat would have. *You have done your reading. Note, however, that the fishwife consented. Note also that grief is expensive to carry, and I offered to carry it, and she wept with gratitude even as the memory left her. You call it theft. She called it mercy. The difference between those words is a matter of whose ledger you keep.*

"Then let me see yours."

*You could not read mine.*

"Try me."

There was a pause — a long one, long enough for Quentin to count her own heartbeats, thirty-one of them, and to notice that the white light had begun, very slowly, to move. Not to flicker. To drift, the way dust drifts in a shaft of sun, except there was no sun, and the motes of whiteness were gathering themselves, arranging, forming lines. Horizontal rules. Vertical strokes. The architecture of a page assembling itself in the air of the throne room, vast as a sail, luminous as frost.

*Very well,* said the sigil. *But understand what you ask. My ledger is not hidden from you out of shame. It is hidden because no one has ever read it and remained the person they were when they began.*

"I've remained myself through worse," Quentin said. "I survived a plague winter. I survived the guild. I survived learning my own name in a book older than my guildmaster's grandfather. Whatever you show me, I'll still be standing here with a candle stub and a grudge."

*That remains to be audited.*

* * *

The page finished forming, and Quentin's breath left her body in a slow, involuntary plume.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it — that her first response, deeper than fear, deeper than rage, was aesthetic awe. The sigil's ledger hung in the air like a window onto a colder country, and its ruling was perfect, its columns true, its hand — if a hand could be said to exist in that light — more precise than any scribe living or dead. Every entry was a life. She could see that immediately, though the entries themselves were written in a script she did not know, characters that moved faintly, rearranging themselves as she watched, refusing to be memorized. Each line was a person. Each balance was a past. And the totals — the totals ran off the edge of the page, off the edge of the world the page described, into a sum so large it had stopped being a number and become a weather system.

*This is the asset side,* the sigil said. *Memories received. Do you wish to see the liability side?*

"No," Quentin said quickly, and then, because she was who she was, "Yes."

The page turned itself.

The liabilities were shorter. Far shorter. Where the asset column held thousands upon thousands of entries — the whole hoarded century of the city's forgetting, the fishwife's marriage, the king's childhood, the names of streets, the recipes of grandmothers, the faces of the dead — the liability column held perhaps a dozen lines. Debts owed. Promises outstanding. And near the bottom of that short, terrible column, in the same moving script, was an entry that glowed faintly brighter than the rest, the way fresh ink glows against old.

Quentin read it before she could stop herself.

She could not read the script. But she could read the structure of it, the way a musician reads a score in a foreign clef, and the structure was this: a date nine years past, in the plague winter; a principal amount, marked *held in trust*; a term of years, now nearly elapsed; and a designation of the debtor that needed no translation, because the sigil spoke it aloud in the same instant, gently, the way one reads a customer's name back to confirm identity.

*Quentin Zhou. One (1) memory. Winter, the plague year. Category: family. Status: held. Maturity: imminent.*

Her knees went soft. She gripped the gallery rail and did not fall, mostly because falling would have meant admitting something had knocked her off her feet, and she had sworn, nine years ago and every day since, that nothing would.

"You kept my debt on your books," she whispered. "Not the guild's. Yours. The guild was never holding my contract. You were."

*The guild holds paper,* the sigil said. *I hold value.* A pause, and then, with something almost like courtesy: *Would you like to see the asset?*

And Quentin, who had climbed through a servant's hatch and drugged a chamberlain's wine and forged the seal of the crown's own exchequer to get into this palace, who had rehearsed for six days the sentences she would say to the drowned boy-king inside his own body — Quentin, who had come armed with resolve and arithmetic and a priestess waiting in the coal cellar — heard herself say yes.

* * *

The page turned again, and the throne room filled with winter.

Not the winter outside — the real one, the honest one, the snow that fell on the city now and was only weather. This was the other winter, the plague winter, nine years gone, and it assembled itself around her with a fidelity that made her want to scream. The smell arrived first: woodsmoke and vinegar and the sweet-rot smell beneath, the smell of a house where sickness had lived too long. Then the cold, the specific cold of a room where the fire has been allowed to die because there is no wood left and no strength to fetch it. Then the light — grey, sourceless, the light of a sky that has been snowing for eleven days and intends to continue.

And then the house. Their house. The little crooked house on Tanner's Lane with the door that stuck and the window that whistled, rendered so exactly that Quentin could have walked its rooms blindfolded, because in every way that mattered, she had.

Wren was there.

Her sister stood at the window of the sickroom, fourteen years old and fever-bright, wrapped in both blankets because Quentin had given her her own, and she was doing the thing Wren always did when the world got too heavy — she was making it a game. Quentin watched her younger self, nineteen and hollow-eyed, haul water up the stairs, and she watched Wren press her hot palm flat against the frozen glass and draw, in the frost-melt, a little boat. A sigil of her own devising. *So we can sail away when spring comes,* Wren had said, laughing through the fever, laughing the way the dying laugh, which is to say generously, spending what little warmth remained on someone else. *You'll steer. I'll do the shouting.*

The vision — the memory, the asset, the line item — played itself out with terrible patience. The night unspooled. The fever climbing. The neighbor woman who would not come, though Quentin beat on her door until her knuckles split. The long hours by the bed, counting Wren's breaths because counting was the only prayer Quentin had ever known how to perform. The moment near dawn when the breaths came slower, and slower, and then — 

The sigil cut the memory there. Precisely there. The image froze on the frozen window, on the little boat drawn in melting frost, and the voice said, softly:

*This is where your record ends. This is where mine begins. You traded at dawn. I have held the remainder for nine years — the last hour, the last words, the burial, the spring. All of it. Intact. Would you like to know what she said?*

Quentin discovered she was crying. She had not noticed it begin. The tears were freezing on her face in the conjured cold, and she wiped them with the back of her hand the way she wiped ink spills, briskly, a clerk tidying her desk, and the gesture broke her own heart.

"You're showing me this," she said, "because you want to sell it back."

*I want to do no such thing,* the sigil said, and for the first time its voice bent, warmed, acquired something horrifyingly close to kindness. *Selling implies parting. I am offering you a withdrawal. Your account is mature, Quentin Zhou. Nine years of interest have accrued on a single memory, and I compound generously. Walk away from this palace. Walk away from the priestess, from the rite, from the king who begs in a child's voice. Walk away, and tomorrow morning you will wake in your own bed with the whole of that night returned to you — not the truncated version the guild sold you survival against, but the complete text. Every word she said. The burial. The spring. The boat. You will remember your sister entire, and no one will ever be able to take it from you again, because I will write the ownership into the deep ledger myself, in perpetuity, non-revocable, witnessed by nothing because nothing witnesses me.*

The page turned one final time, and hung before her, and this page was simple. One line. Written in a script that had finally, deliberately, become legible — legible because the sigil wanted her to read it, wanted her to stand there and read her sister's name in its hand and feel the full weight of the offer settle on her like snowfall.

One line. One asset. One price: her departure, her silence, her surrender of the only war she had ever fought for herself.

*Consider the arithmetic,* the sigil said. *You cannot win. You know this — you have run the numbers yourself, in your own hand, in your own book. I have read it. You are thorough. So be thorough now. Weigh one memory against a god. Weigh your sister's voice against a city that has already forgotten half its streets. You are an honest accountant, Quentin. Tell me the sum favors the fight.*

* * *

And here was the thing — the thing she would carry out of this room like a coal cupped in bare hands.

For one full minute, standing alone in the white-lit dark with her sister's frozen boat hanging in the air before her, Quentin Zhou did not know the answer.

She stood at the rail and she ran it honestly, the way she ran every account, and the numbers did favor the walk. They favored it overwhelmingly. One memory against a god was not a contest; it was not even a tragedy; it was simply good bookkeeping. Any auditor in the kingdom would have signed it. Any sensible woman would have taken the deal and woken rich in the only currency that had ever mattered to her, and no one — no one — could have called it cowardice.

She let herself feel that. She let the temptation have its full weight, its full minute, because she had learned that the only way to audit a desire is to let it speak completely.

Then she thought about the fishwife on Salt Row.

She thought about the woman sitting in her cold kitchen unable to remember why the second chair was kept by the window, unable to remember the face she had loved for twenty years, weeping at a stranger's funeral-smell while the sigil's clerks recorded her loss as revenue. She thought about King Aldric, surfacing some nights in a body that no longer belonged to him, begging in a child's voice for someone to make it stop, remembering nothing but fear. She thought about Sable, sleeping in the coal cellar with her order's rites sewn into her hem, keeping her own books against the dark. She thought about the maturity date on her own debt, and what it meant that the sigil had planted her at the gate nine years ago, grown her like an investment, and was now calling the note.

And she thought about Wren — not the frozen Wren on the page, but the real one, the one who had drawn a boat on a window with her last warmth and handed the steering to her sister. Wren had spent her dying on generosity. Wren had made a game of the unbearable so that Quentin could survive the watching of it. And every instinct Wren had ever had — every laugh, every frost-drawn sail, every reckless gift — said that her sister would rather be remembered broken than be used as bait.

"You're right about the arithmetic," Quentin said to the dark, and her voice came out level, and she marveled at that even as she said it. "One memory does weigh less than a god. But you've made an error in your entry, and I'm surprised at you, because you're supposed to be the perfect bookkeeper." She stepped forward, away from the rail, into the white light, and let it see her face. "You've listed my sister as an asset. She isn't. She was never an asset. She's the reason I know the difference between a price and a value — and that difference is the one line in all your ledgers you will never be able to enter, because it isn't for sale, it isn't for trade, and it doesn't compound. It just stays. Love outlasts erasure. You've been hoarding a city's worth of memories for a hundred years and you still haven't learned that, because if you had, you'd know you can't buy me with her. She'd be ashamed of me if I took this deal. And her opinion is the only credit rating I've ever cared about."

The silence that followed lasted a long time.

When the sigil spoke again, the warmth was gone from its voice, and the dryness had curdled into something older and colder, and Quentin understood that she had just done the one thing no debtor is permitted to do.

She had refused to acknowledge the debt on the creditor's terms.

*You misunderstand your position,* it said. *You believe refusal ends the negotiation. Refusal merely changes the instruments available. I have other assets in play, Quentin Zhou. Other accounts. Other people who love you, and whom you have been careless enough to bring within reach of my books.*

Quentin went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the conjured winter.

"Sable," she said quietly. "You're talking about Sable."

The white light gathered itself, folded like a closing ledger, and slid back into the embers of the braziers, which resumed their ordinary orange as though nothing had happened — as though the room itself had been audited and found willing to forget. From somewhere beneath the floor, beneath the palace, beneath the frozen ground, came the hum. Three notes. Patient as interest.

And in the coal cellar across the city, a disgraced priestess woke suddenly with her hands pressed to her temples, trying to remember the name of the god she had served, and finding, where the name had been, only a smooth and featureless blank — and beside her, on the table, her own meticulous records of answered prayers, in a handwriting she no longer recognized as her purpose, though she could not yet say why.

Quentin ran.
