## 15. The Unbinding Rite

Sable's rooms above the tannery smelled of lamp oil and old smoke and, faintly, of incense from a faith that no longer had a name anyone could remember. That last was new. Three days ago the room had smelled only of oil and smoke. Now there was a ghost of something floral and resinous in the corners, a scent with no owner, drifting through a house that had forgotten why it kept it.

Quentin noticed it the moment she climbed the stairs, because noticing was the one discipline she had never let them take from her.

"Sable," she said, shouldering the door open. "I found it."

Sable sat at her table with her back to the window, and the winter light came through her as if she were a curtain. Not translucent — nothing so dramatic, nothing so merciful — but *thinned*. The sigil's punishment had taken the order from her: every memory of the sisterhood, of the rites, of the god she had served before the gods went quiet. What remained was a woman who knew she had been someone, the way a burned field knows it was wheat. She had spent three days writing down what survived, and the pages around her looked like the wreckage of an argument she was having with herself.

She looked up at Quentin, and for a heartbeat her eyes did not recognize her. Quentin had learned to wait out that heartbeat. It was always exactly one heartbeat long, and then Sable's face reassembled itself into something like trust, built from scratch each time, mortar mixed fresh.

"You found it." Her voice was rough from disuse. "Found what?"

"The rite. Or the bones of it." Quentin set her satchel on the table, on top of Sable's half-finished lists, and began unpacking it with the care she brought to everything: ledger first, then the loose folios, then the bundle of stolen archive pages wrapped in oilcloth, then — wrapped separately, wrapped like something that might bite — the scrap she had taken from the palace library two nights ago, at the cost of a warden's attention and nearly more. "You said your order kept the unbinding. You said it couldn't be written down whole, that it lived in pieces across a dozen houses so no one hand could carry it away." She laid the pieces out in a row. "No one hand. But I'm not a hand, Sable. I'm an accountant."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes — not recognition, exactly. Recognition has a face attached to it, a name. This was older than that. It was the look of a woman watching someone do the thing she had been made to do and then unmade from doing.

"The fragments," Sable said slowly. "You're going to reconcile them."

"Like any other set of books." Quentin pulled the stool close and sat. "Every rite is a transaction. Every transaction leaves entries. Entries can be cross-referenced even when the parties to them are dead, scattered, or eaten." She said it evenly, but her hands were not quite steady, and she saw Sable see it, and neither of them remarked on it. There was a courtesy between them now, the kind that grows between people who have both seen the inside of a bargain: they did not point at each other's wounds.

* * *

The work took the day.

It took the day because the fragments fought her, and Quentin respected anything that fought her, in the way a locksmith respects a lock. The oldest piece was a strip of vellum from Sable's own order, recovered from beneath the floor of their ruined chapel, written in a liturgical hand that used the names of the old gods as punctuation. The second was a merchant's letter, ninety years old, in which a frightened man described what his grandmother had called *the laying-down* — how, once, a devourer had been unmade from a host, and how the family that did it had paid something and gone somewhere and never spoken of it again except in this one letter, which they had apparently kept because grief keeps receipts. The third was Quentin's own theft from the palace: a page torn from a book of coronation rites, one paragraph excised by some careful censor generations ago, the knife-marks still visible at the margins where the words had been cut out — cut out so cleanly that the surrounding text remembered their shape, and Quentin, who had spent nine years reading ledgers full of redactions, could read a hole the way other people read ink.

By late afternoon the candle had burned down to its collar and the room had gone blue with dusk, and the fragments lay arranged on the table in a pattern that meant nothing to anyone but the two women bending over it.

"Read it," Sable said. "Out loud. I want to hear whether my body remembers it even if I don't."

Quentin read.

"*That which wears a soul without wearing its debts cannot be cast out by force, nor starved, nor named against its will.*" Her voice found a rhythm almost immediately, the way it always did with well-kept accounts. "*For force is a price, and it will pay any price; and hunger is a price, and it will outwait any famine; and a name is only a handle, and it will grow a new one.*"

Sable's breath had changed. Quentin glanced up. The priestess's lips were moving, silently, half a syllable behind the words — following, or being followed.

"But," Quentin continued, "*a debt is not a price. A debt is a claim older than the debtor's consent, and it binds the thing owed to the shape of what was taken. Therefore the devourer may be unbound only by its debtor, paying in full, and willingly; for what is taken under compulsion it may digest, but what is given freely it must hold entire, and there are things it cannot hold.*"

She stopped. The words sat in the cold air of the room like coins on a counter.

"There it is," Sable whispered. "There it is. Gods. My hands—" She held them up, turning them over as though expecting the memory to be visible in the skin. "My mouth knew the cadence. I've sung that. Or I would have sung it. There's a version of me that sang that every solstice, and I can't—"

Her voice cracked, not with sorrow but with the specific fury of a scholar missing a page she could feel the weight of. Quentin reached across and put her hand over Sable's knotted fingers, and left it there, because that was the only currency she had ever been good at spending: presence, exact and undramatic.

"It can't hold things given freely," Quentin said. "That's the flaw. That's why it takes instead of asks. That's why it eats memories instead of borrowing them. Everything it takes by force it can digest. But a willing payment—" She turned the sentence over, testing its weight. "A willing payment stays whole inside it. Whole and *other*. A splinter. And the rite uses the splinter to split it."

"From whom?" Sable asked. And then, because the answer was already assembling itself in the silence, she said it anyway: "Who owes it?"

Quentin withdrew her hand and looked at the fragments again, at the excised paragraph, at the merchant's trembling letter, at the vellum strip with its god-names for punctuation. She had known since the palace library. She had known, probably, since the night she'd found her name in the ledger older than the guild, but knowing arrived in installments with her, the way all large sums did, and this installment had been sitting in her chest for two days waiting for her to count it.

"I do," she said.

* * *

Sable stood up so fast her stool went over backward. "No."

"You haven't heard the arithmetic yet."

"I don't need the arithmetic. I need you to hear what you just said." She paced to the wall and back, three strides each way, a woman whose body remembered ritual movement even where her mind had lost it. "Paying in full means it takes all of it. Whatever the debt is — whatever memory you traded, whatever the sigil holds the claim on — *all of it*, freely, and then the rite burns through it and through you, possibly. The fragment says *the debtor pays in full*; it does not say the debtor survives the payment. Look at your own source, Quentin. The family in that letter 'went somewhere.' Grandmother's phrasing. They didn't go somewhere. They were *spent*."

"Then we amend the terms."

"With what authority? You can't amend a divine contract. You can't renegotiate with—"

"You can if the contract was drafted badly." Quentin's voice had gone quiet, which those who worked under her knew was worse than loud. "And this one was. Listen to me. Nine years ago something bought a memory from a dying girl. It didn't buy it from the girl's *consenting adult self*; it bought it through an intermediary — the guild, Oren Vesper, whoever signed on my behalf while I was feverish and fourteen and would have signed anything to stop hurting. That's a defective instrument. Any court in the kingdom would throw it out. But the sigil doesn't answer to courts, so we don't use a court." She tapped the vellum strip. "We use its own rule. *What is given freely it must hold entire.* Freely. Willingly. By the debtor. So the debt has to be paid by the debtor — me — and paid *freely* — which means I choose it, in full knowledge, with no intermediary, no coercion, no fever. Which means the original purchase was flawed, and a flawed debt can be discharged in a corrected form."

Sable stared at her. "You're talking about rewriting the terms of a god's claim on your soul using contract law."

"I'm talking about bookkeeping," Quentin said. "It's the same thing done honestly."

And there it was again — that flicker in Sable's face, the ghost of a smile belonging to a woman who had once argued theology and enjoyed it. "The old texts say the devourer fears a debtor who understands the debt better than it does."

"The old texts can say whatever they like. It happens to be true." Quentin gathered the fragments, squared their edges, aligned them to the table's grain — habits, only habits, but her hands needed work or they would shake. "Here's what matters. Here's what I've been counting since the fishwife told us about her husband. Every answered prayer cost a memory. The sigil hoards them — thousands of them now, maybe tens of thousands, a whole city's pasts stacked in vaults I can't audit. It needs them to finish unbinding from Aldric; you showed me that, the pattern in the losses, the escalation. When it has enough, it walks out of him and the king dies empty and the sigil goes wherever such things go with a century of harvested souls in its pockets." She looked up. "But the rite says it can be split by a willing payment it cannot hold. One splinter. One debt paid clean. And I am the only living person who owes it directly. Everyone else's debts run through the guild, through intermediaries, through paper. Mine runs straight. I'm the only clean line into the thing."

"That's not a reason to volunteer. That's a reason it will expect you."

"Yes," Quentin agreed. "It will expect me. It offered me Wren back, remember? It tried to buy my walking away. It thinks in prices. It thinks everyone thinks in prices. And it has never once, in nine years of owning a piece of me, considered that I might give rather than sell — because giving isn't in its grammar." She allowed herself, briefly, the smallest expression she owned, which was not quite a smile but shared a border with one. "It made one mistake with me, Sable. It priced the wrong thing. It took the night Wren died and thought what it was buying was grief. Grief it can hold. Grief digests fine. But that night wasn't only grief."

"What was it?"

Quentin was silent a moment. In the street below, a cart passed, and someone laughed, and the laugh hung in the cold air a long time, the way sounds did in winter, reluctant to fall.

"It was the last night my sister and I were both alive," she said finally, "and we spent it being happy. That's what it took. That's what it's been holding for nine years without knowing what it had. Happiness that outlasted the person it belonged to. Love with nowhere left to go, still going." She shook her head slightly. "It can't hold that. Nothing that eats memories can hold that, because that isn't a memory anymore. It's a proof. And proofs don't rot."

Sable had gone very still. "You're going to feed it the one thing it can't stomach."

"On purpose. Willingly. In full." Quentin began rolling the fragments back into their wrappings, each motion precise, unhurried. "Which means I have to mean it. That's the trap in the trap, and I won't pretend I'm not afraid of it. The rite requires willingness — real willingness, no crossed fingers, no hidden reservation, because the sigil will taste any reservation like a cook tastes salt. To pay it freely I have to actually want to give up the night Wren died. I have to open my hand all the way." Her jaw tightened. "Nine years I've survived by holding on. Now the winning move is letting go, completely, in front of the enemy, betting everything that what I let go of will burn it from the inside."

"And if you're wrong? If it can hold it after all?"

"Then it keeps Wren's night, and I lose the last of her twice, and the city loses everything anyway." She tied off the oilcloth. "I've run those numbers too. I don't love either column, Sable, but one column has a chance in it, and I have never once in my life bet on the column without a chance."

* * *

They worked through the evening reconstructing the rest. The rite was short — that surprised Quentin; she had expected liturgy, hours of it, architecture. Instead it was barely twenty lines: an acknowledgment of the debt, a naming of what was owed, the offering made freely, and then a single instruction repeated three times, which the excised paragraph rendered as a question the debtor asked the devourer until the devourer could no longer answer it.

Sable wrote out what she could recover from the muscle-memory of her own hands, and where her memory failed, Quentin filled gaps from context the way she filled gaps in any ledger — inference, consistency, the discipline of never writing down a number you couldn't defend. By midnight they had a working text. By midnight they also had a problem, and the problem had a name.

"We can't do it here," Quentin said. "The sigil won't come to a tannery. And I can't summon it — you don't summon that thing, you get *noticed* by it, and getting noticed before I'm ready means it offers me another bargain and I have to stand in front of it hungry for my own memory and refuse twice." She rubbed her eyes. Behind them, exhaustion had built up like frost. "It has to be on ground where the debt was struck. Contracts bind at the place of signing. Even its own rules will honor that — especially its own rules. Where was I bought, Sable? Where did the guild take me, that winter?"

Sable looked at her for a long moment, and Quentin watched the answer arrive in her friend's face before she said it, watched it land like weather.

"The gate," Sable said. "Under the ledger-house. The gate was built around the debt. You've felt it — you told me, the night you came here first. It hums your name."

"Yes," Quentin said. "It does."

She looked at the window, where the snow was beginning again, fat slow flakes falling through the lamplight of the city that was forgetting itself street by street — yesterday the tanner's boy hadn't known his own mother, and today half of Cord Street had reportedly stopped recognizing their doors. Somewhere beneath the guild-house, in the dark, the gate was humming, patient as interest accruing.

"So we go back," Quentin said. "Into the ledger-house. Past Oren and his wardens, past every lock and seal he's hung on me. I have to steal back my own contract, walk it down to the gate, and pay a god the one thing I have left — freely, in full, with open hands."

She picked up the satchel and slung it across her shoulder, and the gesture had the finality of a ledger closed and balanced.

"And I have to do it," she added quietly, "before the sigil finishes eating the king."
