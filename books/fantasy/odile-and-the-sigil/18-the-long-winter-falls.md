## 18. The Long Winter Falls

The first street to forget itself was Tanner's Row.

It happened the way a candle gutters — a flicker, a lean toward darkness, and then nothing. The tanners woke that morning and stood in their doorways looking at their own vats and beams and stretching-frames as though they had been set down in a stranger's house. Old Maren Cotter, who had cured hides for forty years and whose hands knew lime and oakgall better than they knew her own face, picked up her scraping knife and asked her apprentice what it was for. The apprentice did not know either. They stood together in the reek of the pits, two people holding tools like relics from a faith neither could name, while the snow came down soft and patient on the row.

By noon, Maren had remembered the knife but not her husband's name. By dusk she had remembered the husband — *Tomas, Tomas who whistled off-key* — and lost the word for winter, so that when her neighbors spoke of snow she only saw white falling and felt a cold she had no language for, and wept without knowing why.

The city told itself it was a sickness of the row. Bad air from the vats, perhaps. The city was very good at telling itself things.

* * *

In the fishmarket by the harbor, Ilse Vant stood at her stall with herring silvering in the cold and counted her coins three times because the numbers kept sliding.

She had sold fish here since she was nine years old. She knew every customer's face, every haggle, every lie about the freshness of the catch. But this morning a woman in a gray shawl had come to buy mackerel, and Ilse had looked at her and felt the whole architecture of recognition collapse — not into forgetting exactly, but into a terrible *unfamiliarity*, as though the woman were a stranger wearing a familiar coat. The woman had called her by name. *Ilse, love, the usual.* And Ilse had wrapped the fish and taken the coin and smiled, and inside she had been screaming at herself: *you know her, you have known her twenty years, look harder, look harder.*

The woman left. Ilse could not have said what color her eyes were, or whether she had children, or why the sight of her going made Ilse's chest ache like a bruise.

Around her the market went on, louder than usual, everyone talking too fast, laughing too easily, the way people do when silence might let something in. A boy dropped a crate and an old man shouted at him with real fury, and then the old man stopped mid-shout, blinking, as if he'd forgotten mid-breath what he was angry about or why anger lived in his mouth at all.

Ilse counted her coins again. Eleven. She was almost sure it was eleven. She was almost sure she had eleven yesterday too.

The snow kept falling. It had fallen for six days now without stopping, and no one could remember a winter that began like this one, though several people swore they had — and each of them described a different beginning, and none of them noticed that they disagreed.

* * *

Sable heard about Tanner's Row before noon and about the fishmarket before dark, and by lamplight she sat in the back room of the drowned chapel with a map of the city spread across the floor and small stones marking every place where memory had gone out like a snuffed lamp.

There were nineteen stones. There had been four two days ago.

"They're not random," Quentin said. She crouched at the map's edge, coat still damp, the stolen contract a hard rectangle against her ribs. "Look. Tanner's Row, the fishmarket, the chandlers' court on Bell Street, the granaries. They're all *supply*. Food, light, salt, cloth. The sigil isn't eating memories at random — it's eating the city's knowledge of how to feed and warm itself."

"Why?" Sable asked, though she feared she knew.

"Because wishes are voluntary. Prayers cost the pray-er, and there are only so many people willing to pay." Quentin traced the stones with one finger. "But if the city forgets how to make bread, how to mend roofs, how to keep a lighthouse lit — then next week the prayers write themselves. *Please, let someone remember the baker's craft. Please, let the ships find the harbor.* And the sigil answers, and takes more, and the hole gets deeper." Her voice stayed level, ledger-flat, and Sable understood that the flatness was costing her. "It's not hoarding anymore. It's farming."

Sable looked down at the map, at the nineteen stones sitting on her city like a rash, and thought of her order — scattered, defrocked, reduced to her and a cellar full of half-burned rite fragments — and thought: *we spent a century saying the gods had gone silent, and we never once imagined silence was mercy.*

"The king," she said. "How close is it?"

Quentin was quiet a moment. "I read the gate's accounts," she said finally. "Before I ran. The hum has a rhythm. Nine years ago it beat once a season. Last month, once a day. Yesterday—" she stopped. "Yesterday it didn't stop. It's not beating anymore. It's ringing."

Outside, the snow fell through the broken chapel roof and did not melt where it landed on the old altar stone, and both women noticed, and neither said anything, because some things do not need saying to be believed.

* * *

In the palace, King Aldric's steward, Master Corvin Hale, discovered that he had forgotten the layout of the west wing.

He had served in the palace for thirty-one years. He had walked the west wing ten thousand times, had carried trays and ledgers and secrets down its corridors, had known which doors stuck and which floors creaked. Now he stood at the junction outside the tapestry gallery and the corridors went out from him like spokes of a wheel, and he could not have said which one led to the queen's apartments, or whether there was a queen, or —

There was a queen. *Lysandra.* He clung to the name the way a drowning man clings to driftwood, and the name held, and behind it came a little of her — her laugh, low and dry; the way she bit her thumbnail when reading dispatches — and Corvin stood in the corridor with his hand pressed flat against the cold stone wall and breathed until his heart slowed.

The forgetting came in tides. That was the worst of it. You would lose a thing and grieve it, and then an hour later it would wash back, and you would rejoice, and then it would go again, and each time it went you trusted it less, until you were holding every memory you loved up to the light daily, testing them like coins, and the testing wore them thin.

He reached the king's study and found Aldric standing at the window with snowlight on his face, and for a moment — a strange, suspended moment — the king looked *present*, looked like himself, looked like a man rather than a room someone else was living in.

"Corvin," Aldric said. His voice was rough. "How long have I been king?"

"Twenty-two years, Majesty."

Aldric nodded slowly. "And before that?"

"You were duke of Marrow Vale. Your father held the vale before you."

"Marrow Vale." The king said it like a word in a language he'd studied but never spoken. Then his face changed — flickered — and for an instant something moved behind his eyes that was not Aldric at all, something vast and patient turning over in a shallow bed, and Corvin took an involuntary step backward.

When it passed, Aldric was looking at him with naked terror.

"Hale," the king whispered. "Whatever you remember — whatever is still yours — write it down. Hide it. Bury it in the garden if you must. Because I can feel it taking the rest of me, and I don't know how much longer I'll be able to tell anyone what I was." He gripped Corvin's sleeve with a strength that surprised them both. "There's a girl. A ledger-keeper. Zhou. If she comes — *let her in.* Whatever the guards say, whatever orders stand — those aren't my orders anymore. Do you understand? Let her in."

Then the flicker came again, and the terror smoothed away into a mildness worse than madness, and the king turned back to the window and watched the snow as though it were the most interesting thing in the world, and Corvin Hale walked out of the study with his king's last sane words folded into him like a letter he was now responsible for delivering.

That night he wrote everything he remembered onto strips of linen in walnut ink and buried them in three places in the palace gardens, under the rosemary, under the sundial, under the dead plum tree. His hands shook. He could no longer recall his mother's face.

* * *

The bell-ringer of Saint Ossian's tower forgot the bells.

Not their sound — he could still hear them, could still feel the ropes' bite and the bronze shudder coming up through his palms — but their names, their voices, their order. For forty years he had called them his children: big Brannigan, sweet-voiced Mira, old cracked Pell who sang a half-tone flat and whom he loved best of all. He climbed the tower on the seventh night of the snow and stood among them in the dark and felt nothing. They were instruments. Metal. He pulled the ropes because pulling them was his work, and the bells rang out over the city, and somewhere below, people heard the hour and did not know what it meant, and the ringer stood in the swaying dark and wept at metal, at mere metal, and could not say why.

Across the river, a midwife forgot which herb stopped bleeding. A ferryman forgot the sandbars of the winter channel. Three hundred souls in the Almshouse of the Good Shepherd forgot, all in the same hour, the prayer they had said every night of their lives, and sat in the refectory moving their lips around a silence shaped like words.

And in the streets, the snow kept falling, and the city kept mistaking it for weather.

* * *

Oren Vesper stood in the great counting room of the guild-house and listened to the ledgers scream.

Not literally. Ledgers did not scream. But every book open on every desk was wrong — columns crawling, sums refusing to hold, entries fading even as clerks copied them. Nine years of guild records, and the ink was going gray. Not smudged, not water-damaged. *Fading*, as though the paper were drinking it, as though the past itself had become negotiable.

"It's in the books now," said Tam Weller, senior clerk, his voice cracked and young. "Master Vesper, it's in the *accounts*. Look at the prayer-transactions from Tuesday — the consideration column, the memories paid — they're blanking. We can't prove what was traded. We can't prove anything was traded. In a week there'll be no record that any of this ever—"

"I can see it, Tam."

"And the wardens on the gate — Rennick forgot his wife's name at supper, and his wife is *alive*, Master, she's in the kitchen —"

"*I can see it.*"

The room went quiet except for pens, scratching, scratching, the whole staff writing faster as if speed could outrun erasure, as if a copy made quickly enough might hold.

Oren turned to the window. Snow filled the courtyard to the sills. Somewhere beneath his feet the gate rang like a struck bowl that would not finish its note, and he felt it in his teeth, in the hollows where his own traded years had been, in the vast smooth emptiness that had lived inside him for forty years and that he had always called peace.

He had thought he understood the arithmetic. He had made his trade when he was young and hungry and had told himself ever after that the price was fair, that memory was surplus, that a man could run an empire of impossible things on will alone. He had built the cage around the gate and called it a vault and slept well.

Now the vault was opening, and he understood — with the cold clarity of a man reading his own obituary in a rival's hand — that he had never been the keeper of the gate at all. He had been the lock's first bolt. The sigil had used him the way it had used the girl Zhou: planted, priced, positioned. Forty years of service, and he could not remember a single reason he had ever wanted anything else.

"Master?" Tam said. "What are your orders?"

Oren looked at the fading ledgers, at his clerks' frightened faces, at the snow burying the world he had helped build and helped betray.

"Copy everything," he said. "Twice. Once in ink and once in blood if the ink won't hold. And send word to the girl." The words came out of him like a splinter drawn from flesh. "Quentin Zhou. Tell her — tell her the guild releases its claim. Tell her the debt is hers to call now, not mine. Tell her..." He searched himself for something to add, some wisdom, some apology, and found the shelves bare. "Tell her she was right about everything, and I am sorry, and I don't remember enough to know what for."

Tam wrote it down. Even that, Oren thought. Even my repentance becomes an entry.

* * *

Quentin felt the tide reach Sable's chapel on the ninth night.

She woke past midnight with her heart hammering and lay in the dark doing what she always did when the world shook: she counted. Her name — Quentin Zhou. Her sister — Wren, Wren, Wren, whose laugh she had bought back at a price she was still deciding how to pay. The contract against her ribs. The rite fragments Sable had made her memorize, syllable by syllable, over three nights of lamplight: *what is owed in full and freely given cannot be held.*

Her name. Her sister. The rite. Those were the load-bearing walls. Everything else she held loosely, ready to let go.

Beside the cold hearth, Sable sat upright, awake too, her lips moving.

"What are you doing?" Quentin whispered.

"Reciting my order's names. All two hundred and eleven." Sable's voice was steady, but her hands, in the snow-light from the broken roof, were not. "I've lost four since Tuesday. I recite the list every night, and every morning there are fewer, and I can't tell which ones are gone because —" she laughed, short and terrible, "because I've forgotten that I've forgotten them. So I recite the list, and when I reach a gap, I just... step over it. Like a missing stair."

Quentin got up and sat beside her. Neither spoke for a while. Outside, the city lay silent under snow — too silent, no carts, no dogs, no drunks singing, just the bells ringing hours that fewer and fewer people could count.

"Sable. Tomorrow I go to the palace."

"I know."

"If the rite works, everything it holds comes back. Every memory it's eaten, all of it, everywhere." Quentin stared at the falling snow. "If it fails, I'm emptied, the king dies, and the city forgets itself down to the last stone. Those are the accounts. I've checked them four times. There's no third line."

"There's always a third line," Sable said. "That's what my order believed. That's what I'd forgotten until I met you." She reached over and gripped Quentin's wrist, hard, priestess-strong. "The gods went silent a century ago, and everyone said the age of miracles ended. But maybe they didn't go silent. Maybe they *withdrew* — because they saw this thing coming, this hunger dressed as generosity, and they took themselves out of reach so it couldn't learn where they kept their hands." Her grip tightened. "You're not walking into the throne room alone tomorrow, ledger-keeper. Whatever's waiting in that king, it should have to face two of us."

Quentin looked at her — the disgraced priestess with her fraying list of names, guarding other people's pasts with her fingernails — and felt something in her chest that was not arithmetic at all.

"All right," she said. "Two of us."

Far off across the sleeping city, the great bell of Saint Ossian's rang the hour, and the sound rolled over the rooftops and the snow, and in the tower the ringer who had forgotten his bells stood with his hand on the rope of old cracked Pell, feeling the bronze shudder die away into the dark, and did not know why he was crying, and did not stop.

The hour was midnight. Or possibly it was the end of something longer than midnight. The snow did not care which. It fell, and fell, and fell, covering the streets where the city's memory lay buried like coins no one would dig up, and beneath the ledger-house the gate rang on, patient as interest, gathering the whole winter into its single unbroken note —

— and in the chapel by the drowned quay, Quentin Zhou closed her eyes and began, one last time, to rehearse the terms of the only bargain she intended to keep.
