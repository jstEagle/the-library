## 21. The King, Unbound

The first thing Aldric felt was cold.

Not the cold of the throne room, which he had not truly felt in years — the sigil had seen to that, wrapping him in a warmth that was borrowed and false, the warmth of a fire someone else was burning. This was real cold. It came through the stone beneath his knees and through the wet wool of his nightshirt and it hurt, and he was grateful for the pain the way a starving man is grateful for the first bite: because it proved there was still a mouth to feed.

He opened his eyes.

Snow. Snow falling through the shattered clerestory windows of his own great hall, settling on the toppled throne, on the splintered dais steps, on the body of a woman sitting very still at the center of the wreckage with her hands open in her lap as though she had set something down and did not know what to do with empty hands.

And inside himself — inside himself there was *everything*.

It came back the way floodwater comes back to a dry riverbed, all at once and from every direction at once, and it did not ask permission. His mother's voice singing the harvest hymn off-key on purpose because she liked to hear him correct her. The smell of the stables when he was nine and hid there from his father's disappointment. The weight of a crown on a brow too young for it, the specific ache behind the eyes. His wedding day, brief and bright and ended. The taste of the wine he had drunk the night the plague reached the city walls, and the prayer he had prayed, drunk and desperate and alone in the dark of his own bedchamber: *anything. I will give anything.*

He remembered praying it.

That was the worst of it. Not the years stolen, not the edicts signed by a hand that was not entirely his, not the courtiers' faces going smooth and polite as their memories drained away one bargain at a time. The worst of it was that he remembered the moment of invitation. He had opened the door himself. He had held it open with both hands.

*Aldric,* said no one, in the place where a voice had lived for nine years. But it was only an echo now, an afterimage, the shape a presence leaves in a room it has finally left. He could feel it fading even as he listened — the vast attention of it thinning out like smoke, like a debt discharged, like a ledger line ruled closed.

Gone.

He put his hand against the frozen floor and pushed himself upright, and his legs nearly folded under him, because they were *his* legs again, forty-one years old and unaccustomed to kneeling in snow, and no god was holding them up.

* * *

The hall was full of people who did not yet understand what was happening to them.

They had come running when the light went out of the throne — Quentin's rite had torn through the palace like a bell struck too hard, and half the court had come at a run and then stopped dead in the doors, because what poured out of the broken sigil was not destruction but *return*. Aldric watched a chambermaid in the doorway suddenly press both hands over her mouth and begin to sob, and he knew without being told that she had just gotten back her mother's face. A guard captain staggered against the doorframe, mouthing a name — a son's name, perhaps, or a brother's; the war had taken so many — catching it like a man catching rain after drought. An old treasurer sat down where he stood on the frozen flags, indifferent to the cold, laughing and weeping in equal measure, reciting what could only be inventory numbers from a shop he'd owned thirty years ago, before any of this.

The memories were raining down on the city the same way. He could feel it happening beyond the walls, a pressure in the air like weather, like the moment before spring thunder. Somewhere out there a fishwife was remembering her husband whole. Somewhere streets were remembering their own names.

Aldric stood among them, king again and stranger to it, and found that his legs would carry him exactly far enough to reach the woman in the snow.

She had not moved. She sat in the ruin of the dais with her palms upturned, and the snow gathered in them and melted and gathered again, and her eyes were fixed on nothing — not the thousand-yard stare of shock, he knew that look from soldiers, but something worse. Something searched-for and absent. As if she had gone looking inside herself for a particular room and found the doorway opening onto a wall.

"You paid," he said. His voice came out rusted. He knelt, because a king kneels to no one, which meant that what knelt here was not quite a king yet, only a man. "Whatever it cost you. You paid it."

She looked at him then, and he saw that she was trying to be courteous, the way one is courteous to a weeping stranger whose grief has no name you know.

"Did I?" she said. "I don't remember."

* * *

They gave him back the palace slowly, the way one returns a thing borrowed, item by item with an accounting.

The physicians wanted to bleed him. He refused. The council wanted to convene. He refused that too, and instead spent the first evening of his returned life walking the corridors he had haunted for nine years without knowing he haunted them, laying his palm flat against the stones as though he could take their temperature. In the library he found his own handwriting in the margins of a treatise on irrigation and read his younger self's impatient objections and laughed until the laugh turned into something else, and his steward, hovering anxiously in the door, pretended very hard not to see a grown king cry over drainage ditches.

Because that was the arithmetic no one warned you about. The sigil had taken his past and he had wanted it back so badly, all those hollow years, without knowing the hollowness had a shape. And now he had it back — all of it, undamaged, restored to the hour — and restoration was not the same as return. The man who had written those marginal notes had been twenty-three and arrogant and afraid of his father. That man was a boy to him now across a gulf of nine lost years, and Aldric loved him and grieved him and could not become him. You do not step back into your own life. You stand beside it, dripping, and look at it through glass.

He thought about the fishwife. He sent for her records — the guild's ledgers had been seized, scattered, carried off in armfuls by clerks and looters alike — and could not find her name, and understood that somewhere in the city a woman was waking up whole with a dead man's evening restored to her like a coat returned from storage, and that no one, least of all him, could tell her what it had cost the woman in the snow to buy it back for everyone.

* * *

On the third morning he went down to the gate.

The guild-house had been sealed by its own Guildmaster and then unsealed by events; the wardens had drifted home to families they were only beginning to remember they had; the great doors stood open to anyone, and snow blew in across the marble floor of the entry hall and lay in drifts against the counters where fortunes had once been counted. Beneath it all, down the stairs he had never descended, the gate stood shut.

It was not humming anymore. That was the first thing. Nine years of that subsonic note beneath everything, the note the whole city had learned to ignore the way you ignore your own heartbeat — gone. The silence down there had texture. It pressed on the ears.

Aldric stood before the gate a long time.

He was not a scholar of the old gods. He had been a soldier's son and a reluctant king, and theology had always seemed to him a branch of arithmetic practiced by men who disliked arithmetic. But he understood ledgers now, better than any living soul should. He understood that debts are not erased, only transferred; that every payment must come from somewhere; that the books of heaven balance with the same pitiless elegance as the books of earth.

Someone had paid for him. Someone had walked into that throne room carrying the one thing the thing wearing his crown could not price, and had let it be taken, and the taking had unmade it.

He had asked around — carefully, gently, hating himself for asking — and this was what he had pieced together: her name was Quentin Zhou. She had kept the guild's accounts for nine years. She had a sister once. She did not speak of the sister, because she could not, because for her the sister was not dead and not alive and not anything at all — she was a hole in a page, an entry scraped away, and Quentin Zhou walked through the world with that absence in her chest the way an amputee walks through the world with a missing limb that aches in weather.

The gate did not hum. The gate did nothing. It was only a door now, and doors are innocent; it is the traffic through them that sins.

"I don't know how," Aldric said aloud, to the silence, to the woman three floors above him counting final columns by candlelight. "To owe someone a thing they can't be repaid. I was never taught that sum."

The silence declined to answer. Silence is honest that way.

He climbed back up into the winter, and gave orders — his own orders, in his own hand, each word weighed — that the guild's remaining stores be opened to the quarter, that the displaced wardens be fed, that no man in his kingdom be made homeless by the closing of a house that had sheltered him falsely. Then he took off the crown, which he had worn since the throne room without thinking, and looked at it, and set it on the table beside his unfinished supper, and ate his supper like a man instead of a symbol.

* * *

It snowed for six days.

On the sixth day he went, alone, unwrapped in ceremony, to the ledger-house, because he had learned that she was leaving it — that the last ledger was closed, that the Vesper Guild was dissolved by its final accountant's own signature, that today she walked out of those doors and into a life with no institution between her and the sky.

He almost turned back twice. Kings are not owed entrances into private griefs.

But he found her on the steps, wrapped in a grey coat, a single satchel over her shoulder, blinking in the daylight like a creature emerging from nine years underground — which, he supposed, she was. Beside her stood a priestess with white streaks in her hair and gaps in her gaze, a woman who kept touching her own wrist as if checking that she was still attached to her own reasons. Behind them both, the ledger-house stood empty, its windows dark, already beginning to look less like an institution than like a building waiting for new tenants.

They saw him and did not kneel. He was grateful. He did not want kneeling; he had had nine years of things bowing to him, and none of them had been human.

"Your Majesty," said the priestess, in the tone of a woman who had once served gods and had learned precisely how much such titles weighed. Almost nothing. Almost everything.

"Lady Zhou." He stopped on the step below hers, so that for once in the history of crowns, the king looked up. "I've come to say a thing that no chronicle will record and no council will approve. I prayed, nine years ago. Drunk, and frightened, and alone. I opened the door myself. Everything that followed — the bargains, the forgetting, the cost to you—" his voice caught and he mastered it the way he had once mastered horses, with patience and refusal, "—the first coin of all of it was mine. So whatever remains of my gratitude for the rest of my life, it begins in debt. To you."

Quentin Zhou looked at him for a long moment. There was snow on her shoulders and exhaustion carved under her eyes, and something else, something he recognized from his own mirror these past days: the expression of a person taking inventory of a self that has just changed size.

"You shouldn't owe me," she said at last. "Owing is how it started. Owing is the whole disease." She shifted her satchel higher on her shoulder. "Just — remember things, Your Majesty. Loudly. Out loud, where other people can hear. Write them down wrong if you have to. A memory hoarded is a treasure; a memory shared is just... weather."

And then she smiled — small, cracked, real — and stepped past him down into the street, and the priestess followed, and Aldric stood on the steps of the dead guild-house watching two women walk into the falling snow, one carrying nothing, one carrying almost nothing, both of them richer by a debt discharged than any treasury he would ever count.

Behind him, in the empty hall of the ledger-house, faint and impossible and unmistakable, a young woman's laughter rang out — bright, unburdened, asking someone the way to the market — and Aldric turned toward the sound with his heart hammering, because he had read every report, every ledger line, every scraped-away entry, and he knew that voice belonged to no one living.

He went inside to find out why the silence was singing.
