## 22. Spring Arithmetic

The last ledger of the Vesper Guild weighed less than a dead bird.

Quentin Zhou held it in both hands and felt that lightness the way a sailor feels a calm — with suspicion, with a kind of grief. For nine years she had carried these books up and down the stairs of the ledger-house, their spines cracking like knuckles, their pages heavy with the ink of impossible things: a wedding ring returned from the river bottom, a voice given back to a mute child, snow in high summer sold by the sackful to a man who missed his mother's garden. The ledgers had always had weight because the things they recorded had weight. Now the guild was closing, and the final book recorded only endings, and endings, it turned out, were nearly weightless.

She sat at her old desk beneath the high window where the winter light used to come down like a verdict. It was not winter anymore. The window stood open for the first time since she'd worked here, and the air that came through it smelled of wet stone and green things pushing up through the cracks in the courtyard flagstones. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past on the street, and a vendor was calling out the price of early radishes, and the sound of it was so ordinary that Quentin had to stop and listen to it twice, the way you might stare at a fire that has finally stopped burning.

The sigil was gone. The king was only a king. The gate beneath the guild-house was silent — truly silent now, not the held-breath silence of something waiting, but the honest silence of stone doing nothing at all. She had gone down to it three days after the unbinding, alone, carrying a lamp, half expecting it to hum her name again. It hadn't. It had been only a door then, an old door in an older room, its sigil-work faded to gray scratches, its purpose finished or perhaps never what they'd thought. She had put her palm flat against it anyway. Cold. Dumb. A door.

*It was built to hold me,* she had thought, standing there. *And now it doesn't need to hold anything.*

She had laughed, down there in the dark, alone with a lamp and a door. It had come out of her ragged, more like crying than laughing, and she had let it, because no one was keeping accounts anymore.

Now she dipped her pen and wrote the final entry.

*On this day, the Vesper Guild is dissolved. Its debts are paid. Its holdings are released to the city: the ledger-house to be made a school, the vaults to be opened, the gate to be bricked over with the stones facing inward, so that whatever looks at it next must look from inside.* She paused, pen hovering. *No further bargains will be honored. No further prayers will be answered. Let anyone who reads this understand: the price was never worth the wish.*

Her handwriting looked strange to her. Steadier than it had been in weeks. During the worst of it — during the long winter when the city forgot itself street by street, when whole neighborhoods woke not knowing their own names — her hand had shaken so badly she'd written numbers that looped and doubled like eels. Now it was spring, and her hand was quiet, and the numbers sat still on the page like tame animals.

She wrote the date. Then, because she was who she was, she checked it against the calendar, and against the position of the sun through the window, and against the almanac, out of nine years of habit. All three agreed. That was the thing about arithmetic, she thought. Arithmetic had never lied to her. People lied, gods lied, institutions lied most elegantly of all, in columns and totals and balanced lines. But two plus two had never once pretended to be five, and there was a strange comfort in that, a floor under the world that even the sigil hadn't managed to rot through.

The sigil had hoarded memories the way misers hoarded coin, and when it unraveled, everything it held had rained back down over the city like weather. Quentin remembered standing in the snow outside the palace, emptied of Wren, watching the memories fall — watching a baker suddenly remember his daughter's face and sit down in a drift weeping; watching two old men turn to each other in the street and recognize fifty years of friendship that had been taken and now returned, whole, like a coat handed back from a cloakroom. The city had spent weeks relearning itself. Some things had not come back. Some people had gotten back memories that didn't fit them anymore, the way a house refurnished by someone else feels wrong in the morning. But most of it had come home. Most of it.

Not hers. Not Wren.

That was the trade, and trades were trades, and Quentin Zhou had never once in her life asked to renegotiate a fair contract. She had paid the night of her sister's death in full, willingly, bound into the rite, and the sigil had taken it and broken against it and died of it, and what remained in Quentin afterward was not nothing. That was the discovery that had saved the world, if she was honest about it — and she was always honest about it, in the privacy of her own skull, which was the only place honesty had ever been required of her. What remained was not grief. Grief had been the shape of the memory, the wrapping. What remained was the knowledge underneath: that Wren had existed, that Quentin had loved her, that love had outlasted erasure once already and could do it again. She knew her sister the way you know a country you've never visited but have read about every day of your life. She knew the fact of her without the texture. She knew the sum without the working.

Some mornings that felt like a wound. Some mornings it felt like a kind of mercy. Most mornings it felt like both, which was, she suspected, simply what surviving felt like when you did it honestly.

The bell above the ledger-house door rang. Quentin looked up.

A young clerk stood there — one of the four who had stayed until the end, the loyal ones, the ones who had kept coming through the forgetting winter when half the city couldn't remember why the building mattered. This one was called Merrin, a narrow serious boy with ink permanently stained around his right middle finger, and he was holding a stack of papers tied with string.

"Final reconciliations," he said. "Everything balances."

Of course it does, Quentin thought. I trained you.

"Put them with the others," she said. "The city archivists come tomorrow. After that, the shelves stay empty forever." She said it lightly, but the words caught on something in her throat on the way out, and Merrin — who was perceptive in the way of young people who had survived something terrible together with their elders — pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

He set the papers down, hesitated. "Will you stay for the sealing? Of the gate?"

"No."

"They'll want a speech."

"Tell them the guild's last official statement." She tapped the final ledger. "*The price was never worth the wish.* Short enough to carve over a door. True enough to carve over one, too."

Merrin almost smiled. Then he bowed — actually bowed, which embarrassed them both — and left, and the bell rang again behind him, a small bright sound, and Quentin was alone with the ledgers and the light.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she had done for nine years, which was putting things in order, except that this time the order was an ending, and endings required a different discipline than continuations. A continuation could tolerate loose threads; an ending could not. She inventoried the vaults. She signed the release forms. She found, in the bottom drawer of Oren Vesper's old desk, the man's personal effects — a ring, a letter never sent to a woman thirty years dead, and a single page in his handwriting, dated decades before Quentin had ever come to this city, recording a bargain whose terms were blank. He had traded his memory of the night it happened too. Whatever night it had been. By the end he had had nothing left inside, and Quentin had pitied him, and pity had been the strangest thing she'd ever felt toward an enemy, and she had never quite gotten rid of it since.

She burned the blank page. It seemed right. Some contracts should die unsigned.

By late afternoon the ledger-house was empty of everything except furniture and dust and the particular echo that large rooms get when they are no longer listened to. Quentin walked through it one last time, room by room, touching nothing. The counting hall with its long tables. The archive where she had found her own name in a book older than the guild, the vertigo of that moment still stored somewhere in her body, in her knees, in the set of her shoulders. The stairs down to the gate, which she did not descend. She had said her piece to the door already. Doors don't need to hear things twice.

At the threshold she stopped and looked back once.

Nine years ago she had walked into this building as a girl with a hole in her memory the size of a person, and the guild had given her work and walls and a system so exacting that there had been no room in it for wondering what was missing. She had thought, for years, that it had sheltered her. Then she had learned it had purchased her. And now, standing in the doorway with the spring air moving through her hair, she understood the third truth, the one that took the longest: it had done both. Institutions were like that. They were cages and shelters at once, and the bars and the beams came from the same timber, and you did not get to choose which one you leaned on. You only got to choose, eventually, whether to walk out.

She walked out.

* * *

The city in spring was a city remembering how to be a city.

That was the phrase Sable had used, weeks ago, sitting in the temple ruins with her half-erased memory and her stubborn spine, watching the first true thaw. *A city remembering how to be a city.* The disgraced priestess had lost her order's name to the sigil's punishment — could not remember why she fought, only that she fought — and yet she had rebuilt herself from fragments faster than anyone Quentin had ever seen, the way a bone knits if you keep it straight. She was in the southern provinces now, reinstating rites, writing down what she could reconstruct, keeping new books for old gods who had learned, presumably, some manners. They corresponded. Sable's letters arrived irregularly and were always too long and always ended with some version of the same sentence: *You did the arithmetic right. Don't argue.*

Quentin walked down the hill from the ledger-house into streets that were loud in the ordinary way. Children ran past chasing a hoop. A woman beat a rug from a second-story window, and the dust came down gold in the low sun. On the corner where the fishwife had once told Quentin about her dead husband's evening visit — dread disguised as professional curiosity, that interview, the first thread she'd pulled — there was now a flower stall, and the fishwife herself was selling cress and violets, and she recognized Quentin and raised a hand, and Quentin raised one back, and neither of them said anything, because what was there to say that hadn't been entered into a record somewhere?

The king had reopened the winter court as a spring court, and rumor said Aldric slept badly and ate well and had begun, of all things, to write poetry, which his advisors despaired of and his people privately adored. Quentin had received one letter from him, formal and slightly stiff, thanking her in language that a chancery clerk had clearly drafted, and then, at the bottom, in a different hand, smaller, hurried, as if written before courage failed: *I remember my mother's kitchen. I had forgotten for eleven years. Thank you.* She had read it maybe forty times. She had not answered yet. She would. There was time now. Time was the one commodity the guild had never traded in, and it turned out to be the only one worth having.

She reached the market square as the lamps were being lit, though the sky was still pale — spring evenings stretched themselves out like cats, unwilling to commit to dark. The stalls were full of early things: radishes, sorrel, lamb, the year's first honey. People haggled. A dog stole a sausage and was chased by three boys and a butcher, all of whom appeared to be enjoying themselves. The whole scene was so aggressively unremarkable that Quentin stood at the edge of it for a moment just looking, the way she had once stood in front of a silent gate, checking, checking, making sure the miracle was real.

Miracles, she decided, were mostly this. Mostly radishes. Mostly noise. The impossible things had all been in the ledgers, and the possible things were here, unsupervised, unpriced, free.

She bought honey. She had no strong desire for honey, but the seller was an old man who weighed it out slowly and told her about his bees the way old men tell you about bees, whether you ask or not, and she found she wanted to hear it — wanted to stand in the sun and hear about bees, about something that had never been in any ledger, that had never been prayed for or bargained over or written in a hand that shook. The bees had made it through the winter. The bees did not know there had ever been anything to survive. She envied them briefly, and then decided envy was a waste of good honey money, and paid, and moved on.

She was crossing the square toward the fountain when she heard it.

A laugh. From somewhere behind the fruit stalls — bright, quick, doubled at the end the way a certain laugh had always doubled at the end, rising on the second note like a bird deciding to go back up. Quentin stopped. Her heart did something complicated and painful, the way a lock does when a key that shouldn't fit slides halfway in.

She knew — she knew with the whole of her meticulous, guarded, arithmetic mind — that it could not be Wren. Wren was nine years dead. Wren was a sum without its working, a country read about daily, a fact she carried the way you carry a stone in the pocket that has worn smooth from handling. Dead was dead. The sigil was dead, and dead things stayed dead, and she had audited the universe's books on this point personally.

But the laugh came again, closer, and a young woman stepped out between the stalls into the lamplight — seventeen, maybe eighteen, brown-haired, flour on her hands, a basket of bread on her hip — and she was laughing at something the baker's boy had said, and the laugh was not Wren's laugh. Of course it wasn't. It was similar the way two keys cut from the same blank are similar. It was her own ears playing tricks, her own hunger finding shapes in noise. Memory returns not as it was, Quentin thought. It returns as something you can carry. And sometimes, apparently, it hums.

The young woman saw her staring and smiled, unbothered, the easy smile of someone who has never had to audit anything. "Excuse me," she said. "Could you tell me the way to the market? Only I'm new here, and everyone keeps pointing me in circles."

"This is the market," Quentin said.

The girl blinked. Then she laughed again — that doubled, rising laugh — and pressed her floury hand to her forehead. "Well. Everyone was right about the circles." She shifted her basket. "I'm looking for work, actually. Rooms above the bakery, and the baker says I need references, and I've none, and—" She stopped, cocked her head, studying Quentin with sudden frank interest. "You look like someone who writes things down."

"I'm an accountant," Quentin said. "Formerly."

"Formerly of what?"

"The Vesper Guild. It's dissolved."

"Oh." The girl frowned, searching her memory, and Quentin watched her search it and find nothing, because the guild was already becoming the kind of thing cities forget on purpose, gratefully, the way a body forgets a fever once it breaks. "Was that the place that did the wishes? Before? My grandmother talks about it. She says the prices were wicked."

"They were," Quentin said. "They always were."

The girl nodded solemnly, accepting this, and then her face cleared into practicality again. "Could you write a reference anyway? Just — that you saw me, that I'm honest? I can carry flour up four flights without dropping it, and I can add in my head, and I don't steal. Well." A grin. "Bread crusts. Only crusts."

Quentin looked at her. At the flour on her hands. At the basket. At the ordinary, impossible, unpriced fact of her, standing in the lamplight asking for a reference in a city that was learning its own name again.

There was no reason. There was no ledger line for this. There was no column in which this transaction belonged, no debt, no bargain, no price. That was precisely what made it possible.

"Yes," Quentin said. "I can do that. Do you have paper?"

The girl dug in her basket and produced a stub of pencil and a folded scrap, and Quentin took them, and her hand — her steady spring hand, the one that no longer shook — began to write.

*To whom it concerns: the bearer is honest,* she wrote, and paused, and heard the laugh again in the place where memory lived now, not as it was but as something she could carry, humming like a gate that had finally learned to be only a door. *She adds well in her head. She does not steal. I recommend her without reservation, and I am known for my reservations.*

She signed it. *Q. Zhou.*

The girl took the paper, read it, and beamed. "Thank you. Truly." She tucked it away, hitched up her basket, and then turned back, walking backward a few steps into the crowd. "What was your sister's name?" she asked — apropos of nothing, out of nowhere, the way the young ask things. "Only you looked, just now, like someone hearing a name."

The lamps hissed. The market roared its ordinary roar. Somewhere a hoop rolled, and children chased it, and the evening went on being only weather.

"Wren," Quentin said. Saying it aloud cost nothing and everything, which was, she had come to believe, the correct price for anything worth saying. "Her name was Wren."

"Wren," the girl repeated, tasting it, nodding. "That's a good name. That's a spring name." And she smiled that doubled, rising smile one last time, and turned, and went into the crowd with her basket of bread, and Quentin Zhou stood in the market square with the honey warm in her bag and the whole unsupervised world in front of her, and followed her in.
