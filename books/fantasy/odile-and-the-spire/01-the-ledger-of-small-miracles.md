## 1. The Ledger of Small Miracles

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in a cellar that smelled of tallow, dust, and old rain, and Jude Joubert had come to love that smell the way other people loved the sea.

She was twenty-two years old and had been balancing the guild's books since she was nine, when Corvain found her in the counting-house doorway reciting sums off the slates faster than the journeymen could write them. *The girl has an abacus where her heart should be,* the senior clerks had joked, and Corvain had not laughed. He had simply looked at her for a long moment with his one good eye — the other was lost behind a scarred leather patch, and no one in Ashport knew or dared ask why — and said, "Bring her a stool."

Now she sat on a better stool at a better desk, in the lamplit quiet below the guildhall, and worked through the autumn ledger the way she worked through all of them: line by line, column by column, until the numbers agreed with one another and the world made sense.

That was the whole point of the ledger, as far as Jude had ever been able to tell. The free cities were full of wonder — bread that never soured in Vell's market quarter, bridges in Ashport that mended their own cracks overnight, wells that gave sweet water in drought years, midwives who could coax a breech child into the world with a touch and a whispered word. Everyone knew such things existed. What almost no one knew was that they were *paid for*. Every miracle in the seven cities had a cost entered somewhere, and that somewhere was the cellar beneath the Guild of Impossible Things, in ledgers bound in grey calfskin and written in ink mixed with iron filings so it would outlast the paper it stained.

Jude's job was to make sure the columns balanced. Miracles rendered against payments received. Debts owed to the guild by the cities, and debts owed by the guild to — well. That was the part of the ledger she had never been permitted to see past. Every entry had two sides, like every door, and the right-hand pages were always completed before they reached her desk, in a hand that was not Corvain's. A hand older than Corvain's, thin and vertical, like frost forming on glass.

*Received: one bridge-mending, Ashport, third arch.*
*Paid:* and then the frost-hand, and then a sum she was not meant to read.

She had asked about it once, at fourteen. Corvain had told her the right-hand pages were the guild's private correspondence with its creditors, and that a ledger-keeper's virtue was knowing which side of the page she stood on. She had accepted that, because she was fourteen and because Corvain had never once lied to her about anything else.

Tonight, three days before the turning of the year, the columns refused to balance.

It began small, the way all catastrophes do. The autumn figures came up short by a margin too slight to matter to anyone but her — a fraction of a fraction, the kind of discrepancy most clerks would round away without a thought. But rounding was lying, in Jude's opinion, dressed up in arithmetic, and she did not permit herself to lie even in the privacy of her own columns. So she went back to the beginning of the season and started again, checking each entry against its twin, and somewhere around the second hour she stopped checking and simply stared.

There was an entry on folio forty-one that should not have existed.

*Received: one memory, held by T. Marrow, cooper, of the fire of 1189.*

Jude read it four times. Then she turned back a page, then five, looking for the miracle it paid for, and found nothing — no bridge, no blessing, no impossible thing rendered to any city. The entry stood alone, a payment with no purchase, like a coin spent in an empty room.

Her first thought was that it was a joke. One of the journeymen testing the new girl — except she hadn't been the new girl in thirteen years, and none of them wrote in the frost-hand, and the frost-hand did not joke. Her second thought was that it was an error, and errors in this ledger were more frightening than jokes, because the ledger did not make errors. In thirteen years she had found perhaps six mistakes in ten thousand entries, and every one of them had been hers.

She turned forward. On folio forty-four:

*Received: eleven years, from the life of S. Anselm, dyer.*

And on folio fifty-one, in the same vertical frost-script, as patient as frost itself:

*Received: one name, belonging to M. of the Tanner's Row, formerly ___.*

The blank where the name had been was not an omission. It was a *removal*. The paper there felt different under her fingertip — smoother, faintly cold, as though something had been taken out of the vellum rather than merely left unwritten. Jude snatched her finger back and pressed it against her skirt, heart tapping quick and strange against her ribs.

Names. Years. Memories. Not coin, not goods, not labor. The guild's payments — the ones that balanced against the bread-that-never-soured and the self-healing bridges and the midwives' gentle magic — were being drawn from people. From specific, named, ordinary people. A cooper. A dyer. Someone from Tanner's Row who had once had a name and now, apparently, did not.

She sat very still in the lamplight and listened to the cellar breathe. Water ticked somewhere in the old stone. A rat moved behind the shelving with the confidence of a long-term tenant. Above her, muffled by floors, the guildhall's evening sounds were winding down — a door, footsteps, the scrape of a chair. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world she had trusted for twenty-two years.

Because here was the thing Jude had built her whole understanding upon, the foundation stone under everything: the guild *paid fairly*. That was what Corvain had taught her before he taught her anything else. *We are not thieves, little ledger. We are not beggars and we are not bandits. Every trade the guild makes is witnessed, consented, and recorded.* The impossible things the guild brokered were bought and sold like anything else, honestly, aboveboard — just in a currency most people never saw. A farmer might sell a season of luck for a price that let him keep his farm. A grieving woman might buy one hour's conversation with the dead and pay with a memory she chose to lose. Consent, witness, record. Three words Corvain repeated like a prayer, and Jude had repeated them after him like an echo, and she had believed them the way she believed in sums.

But these entries had no counter-signature. No witness mark. No note of consent. And they were not purchases — nothing had been bought. They were payments flowing the wrong direction, out of the city and into whatever waited on the right-hand side of the page, and they were being drawn from people who, as far as Jude knew, had never set foot in this cellar and never signed anything at all.

Footsteps on the stair. Slow, uneven, familiar — Corvain's bad knee announcing him two steps ahead of time, as it had for years. Jude's hands moved on their own, closing the ledger, smoothing her face into the neutral competence that was her professional armor. By the time the lamp at the stair-bottom swung into view she was sitting with her elbows folded and her expression arranged into polite inquiry, and only her own pulse knew otherwise.

Corvain descended into the light. He was sixty-some years old and looked carved rather than aged, all angles and dry wood, his remaining eye sharp as a ledger-line. He carried a second lamp and a covered dish, and he set both down on the corner of her desk without ceremony, the way he had done a thousand evenings.

"You've been down here since noon," he said. "Eat something."

"I will." She nudged the dish aside without looking at it. "Master Corvain. A question about the autumn accounts."

"Then it's a question you've been chewing for six hours." He lowered himself onto the bench across from her with the careful economy of a man rationing his joints. "Ask it."

"The entries on folios forty-one, forty-four, and fifty-one." She kept her voice level, clerkly, a voice asking about freight rates. "They don't balance against anything. Payments received with no goods rendered. And the payments themselves are—" she paused, choosing the word with care, "—unusual."

"Unusual," Corvain repeated.

"Memories. Years. A name." She opened the ledger again and turned it to face him, one finger resting beside the entry on folio fifty-one, careful not to touch the smooth cold patch of paper. "Paid by whom? To whom? For what? There's no counter-entry anywhere in the season. I've checked twice."

For a moment — just a moment, a hair's breadth of time, gone so quickly she might have imagined it — Corvain's face did something she had never seen it do. Not anger. Not dismissal. Something closer to grief, arriving early, like a guest who comes before the house is ready.

Then it closed, the way a ledger closes, and he was only her master again, dry and angular and immovable.

"Those entries are outside your remit."

"They're in my ledger."

"The ledger is mine. You keep it." He rose, unhurried, and reached across to shut the book himself, his knuckles brushing the calfskin with what Jude would remember later, though she didn't know it yet, was tenderness. "Some columns are not yours to balance, Jude. That is not a punishment. It is a mercy. There are things in these pages that are easier carried by one person than two."

"I was taught that every trade is witnessed, consented, and recorded." Her voice came out quieter than she intended. "These aren't witnessed. Or consented — unless the cooper and the dyer and whoever M. of Tanner's Row used to be all came down here and signed something I've never seen. And they're barely recorded. There's nothing *on the other side*, Master. Nothing balances. In thirteen years I have never given you a column that didn't balance, and tonight I'm telling you the ledger itself is lying, and you're telling me to look away from it."

The lamp between them guttered, steadied. Somewhere above, the guildhall clock began striking the hour — nine slow bells, rolling down through the stone like stones themselves.

Corvain waited out the bells. When he spoke, his voice was lower, and older, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with his knee.

"When you were nine years old, you walked into my counting-house and told me the day's takings were short by three coppers, and that the shortfall was in the till, not the takings, and you were right." A ghost of something crossed his face — not quite a smile. "I have never once caught you in an error, little ledger. Do you know why I keep you anyway? Why I've kept you thirteen years, doing work half the guild thinks is busywork?"

"Because I'm cheap," Jude said, "and I don't gossip."

"Because you're the only person in this building who looks at the numbers and asks what they *mean* instead of what they say." He picked up his lamp. "And that is exactly why you must leave this alone. Not because you'd misunderstand it. Because you'd understand it perfectly, and understanding has a price, and I have spent twenty years making sure you never receive the bill."

He was already at the stair when she found her voice.

"What bill, Master?"

He stopped. Did not turn around. The lamplight put his shadow huge and crooked on the cellar wall, hunched over its little flame like a man carrying something heavy up a long hill.

"Eat your supper, Jude," he said. "Balance what you can balance. And tomorrow, when the criers come — stay indoors. Hear me now and hear me well: whatever you hear shouted in the streets tomorrow, stay indoors, lock your door, and do not open it for anyone wearing the king's colors."

"Criers?" Jude said. "What criers? Master, what happens tomor—"

"Goodnight, little ledger."

His footsteps climbed away from her, uneven and slow, and the stair-door boomed shut at the top, and the bolt slid home with a sound like a full stop.

Jude sat alone in the cellar with her cold supper and her unbalanced ledger and the ringing of nine bells fading out of the stone. After a while she opened the book again to folio fifty-one and looked at the smooth, cold place where a name used to be, and pressed her fingertip to it, gently, the way you'd touch a bruise to learn how deep it went.

*M. of the Tanner's Row, formerly ___.*

Someone in her city had woken up this morning without a name, and the guild's books said it was fair payment for something, and her master — her honest, unbribable, twenty-years-trustworthy master — had looked her in the eye and told her not to ask.

Jude blew out the lamps, climbed the stairs, and went home through streets gone blue with evening, and if she walked a little faster past Tanner's Row than she needed to, she told herself it was only the cold.

Behind her, in the locked cellar, the ledger lay closed on the desk, and on its cover, faint as breath on glass, the frost-hand had already begun writing tomorrow's date.

* * *

She did not sleep well. She dreamed of columns that ran off the edge of the page and kept going, down into dark paper, numbers she couldn't read stacked like stones in a flooded well, and in the dream someone was calling her name from the bottom of the ledger — not *M., formerly ___*, but her own name, *Jude, Jude, Jude* — and she woke before dawn with her heart going like a courier's boots.

Ashport at first light was gray and gull-cold, gulls wheeling over the rooftops and the harbor breathing its usual fog. Everything ordinary. Everything in its place. She made tea, ate bread, dressed, and told herself firmly that she was a grown woman, a professional, a keeper of accounts, and that dreams were not entries and did not go in the books.

She was reaching for the guildhall door when the first crier's bell rang out from the harbor square.

It was a bell she'd never heard before — deeper than the watch-bells, with a sour undertone, a cracked-note quality that set her teeth on edge. Then a second bell answered it from the east gate, and a third from the river quarter, and beneath the bells a voice, amplified somehow, thrown impossibly far across the whole waking city at once, as if the air itself had been hired to shout:

*"By decree of Aldric, rightful King of the Seven Cities and the Lands Beyond: the hunt for the gifted is declared. All who bear unlawful magic shall surrender themselves to the crown's finders within three days, or be taken. Shelterers and harborers shall be taken with them. The king's mercy is great. The king's patience is not."*

Down the street, doors were opening. People stood in their nightclothes in the cold, faces tipped up toward the sky as though the voice might be visible. Somewhere a child started crying. Somewhere else, further off, a dog barked and barked and would not stop.

Jude stood with her hand still on the guildhall's iron ring, not moving, remembering a dry old voice saying *stay indoors, lock your door, do not open it for anyone wearing the king's colors* — and understanding, with the particular horror of a bookkeeper watching a column refuse to balance, that Corvain had known. Had known last night, while he handed her supper and told her to eat. Had known, maybe, for twenty years.

*The hunt for the gifted.* The gifted. The midwives with their gentle hands. The farmers who sold their luck. The whole hidden economy of small miracles she had spent her life recording from the safe side of the page — suddenly named, suddenly hunted, suddenly worth armies.

And underneath the fear, in the quiet cellar of her mind where she kept the things she wasn't ready to think, another thought was already forming, patient as frost. Because she had read those entries. *One memory. Eleven years. One name.* Payments drawn from ordinary people, without consent, without witness — and now a king declaring war on everyone who could work wonders, and a voice in her head, cold and clerical, asking the question she would spend the rest of this story trying to answer:

*If the guild pays in gifts... whose gifts have I been balancing all along?*

The crier's voice rolled over the rooftops one last time, listing the finder-captains' names and the reward for information, and then the bells fell silent, and the city exhaled, and Jude Joubert turned and fled inside, bolting the guildhall door behind her — and did not yet know that three floors below, in the grey calfskin ledger, a sealed page had already been waiting twenty-two years for someone to find her name on it.
