## 2. The Proclamation

The bells of Ashport rang wrong that morning.

Jude Joubert noticed it before she was fully awake — the way you notice a missing stair in the dark, your body knowing before your mind consents to. The dawn bells were supposed to come in threes: three for the harbor watch, three for the guild quarter, three for the temple of the Ninefold Tide. Instead they came in ones, scattered and urgent, like a heartbeat stumbling over itself.

She lay in her narrow room above the guildhall and listened, counting out of habit. Counting was what she did. She had been balancing ledgers since she was nine years old, when Master Corvain had pressed a stub of graphite into her hand and said, *Numbers don't lie, little Jude. People do. Learn which one you'd rather trust.* Ten years later, she trusted numbers the way other people trusted saints.

The bells stopped mid-stroke. Then began again, faster.

Downstairs, the guildhall smelled of cold ash and yesterday's ink. Corvain was already at his desk in the front office, a small man folded around a large ledger, his spectacles pushed up into white hair that stood like frost on a fence. He didn't look up when she came down.

"The bells," Jude said.

"I heard them."

"They're wrong."

"Everything's wrong this morning." He turned a page with unnecessary force. "There are criers in the square. King Aldric's criers. They arrived before dawn with a proclamation and an escort of forty lances."

Jude's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop. "A proclamation of what?"

Corvain finally looked up, and what she saw in his face frightened her more than the bells had. Corvain's face was a ledger too — every entry balanced, every column neat. She had never once seen him post a figure he couldn't account for. Now something in him was red-inked and bleeding through the page.

"Go home, Jude."

"This is my home."

"Then go to the back room and copy out the winter grain tallies, and don't come to the window until I tell you the square is empty." His voice was gentle, which was worse than shouting. Corvain never wasted gentleness except on things that were breaking. "Please."

She went to the back room. She copied grain tallies. She lasted eleven minutes.

* * *

The square of Ashport was full by the time Jude reached it, though full was perhaps the wrong word. Crowds have shapes, and this crowd had the shape of a held breath — packed tight around the guildhall steps where a crier in royal blue stood with a scroll in both hands, flanked by soldiers whose armor was too clean for any campaign. Fresh armor. Armor bought for this.

"—by order of Aldric, first of his name, rightful King of Cindral and the Marches—" the crier read, his voice pitched to carry off the rooftops, "—it is proclaimed throughout the free cities that all persons possessed of unnatural gifts shall present themselves to the king's assessors within thirty days—"

The word *gifts* moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. Jude felt it bend the bodies around her, a ripple of shoulders drawing inward, of mothers' hands finding children's collars.

"—those who conceal their gift, or who having concealed it are discovered, shall be taken into the king's custody for assessment and service. The king assures his loyal subjects that no harm will come to the compliant. The gifted are not criminals. The gifted are *resources*—"

*Resources.* Jude wrote the word down in her head, the way she wrote everything down, and hated herself a little for doing it. But that was the thing about being a ledger-keeper: you learned that words were entries too, and entries always told you what someone intended to do next. You didn't call a person a resource unless you intended to spend them.

"—and be it further known," the crier continued, "that the crown pays a bounty of fifty crowns silver for true report of a concealed gifted, payable upon verification."

That was when the crowd changed. It was subtle — a shifting of weight, a recalibration. Fifty crowns was half a year's wages for a dockhand. Fifty crowns was a new roof, a paid debt, a daughter's dowry. Jude watched her neighbors look at each other and understood, with the cold clarity of arithmetic, that the hunt would not need many soldiers. The city would hunt itself.

She was turning to leave when the commotion started near the fishmongers' row.

It began as shouting, then became a knot of bodies, then broke open like a boil. Two of the king's men dragged a woman through the press by her arms — Maren Ostrey, who sold eels from a blue-painted cart, who had once given Jude a whole smoked eel for free because Jude had helped her find her runaway grandson. Maren was maybe fifty. Her gray hair had come loose from its braid and swung wild as she twisted against the soldiers' grip.

"I haven't done anything!" she was saying. Not screaming — that was the terrible part. She was saying it reasonably, over and over, a woman appealing to a court that did not exist. "I've lived here thirty years, ask anyone, ask the harbormaster, I pay my taxes—"

An assessor stepped forward from beside the guildhall steps. Jude hadn't noticed him before; he wore no armor, only a gray coat, and carried a long brass instrument like a surveyor's theodolite mounted with a lens of cloudy crystal. He raised it toward Maren the way a man might raise a lantern into a dark cellar.

The lens caught the morning light and bent it wrong. Jude saw it happen — saw the light twist as it passed through the glass, saw it go the color of deep water, of storm-light under a hull. And she saw Maren stop struggling. Saw the fight go out of her like air from a punctured lung.

"There it is," the assessor said, almost kindly. "Water-calling. Modest strength. You've been keeping yourself dry-handed for years, mistress. That must have been tiring."

"I don't know what you mean," Maren whispered. But everyone in the square had seen the light bend. Everyone had seen the way the puddles at her feet had trembled, just slightly, reaching toward her like dogs hearing their names.

They put her in a wagon. Not a cage — a wagon, ordinary and wooden, which somehow made it worse. As the soldiers lifted her up, Maren found Jude's eyes across the crowd. Jude didn't know why. There were hundreds of faces. But Maren looked straight at her, and her expression wasn't pleading. It was apologetic. As if she were sorry to show Jude this. As if she were sorry to confirm something Jude had already begun to suspect about the world.

The wagon rolled east toward the king's road. The crowd dispersed the way crowds do after violence — too quickly, everyone suddenly busy, no one meeting anyone's eye. In the silence it left behind, Jude could hear the fish slapping in Maren's abandoned cart, its blue paint already looking faded, orphaned.

She walked back to the guildhall with her hands in her pockets so no one could see them shaking.

* * *

Corvain was waiting in the doorway, and whatever he saw in her face made him step aside without a word and let her pass.

"He took Maren Ostrey," Jude said. "In daylight. In front of everyone. For nothing. For *bending light*, which might have been a trick, which might have been anything—"

"It wasn't a trick."

"You knew." It wasn't a question. She heard it land in the room like a dropped coin. "You knew they were coming. This morning, with the bells — you weren't surprised. You were *afraid*. Those are different things, Master Corvain. I keep accounts. I know the difference between a loss and a liability."

He sat down heavily behind his desk. For a long moment he only looked at her, and Jude had the strange sensation of being read — of being a page in someone else's ledger, some entry twenty years old being re-audited.

"What do you know about the guild?" he asked.

"We broker impossible trades. We're the oldest chartered house in Ashport. We take payment in — " she faltered, because here was the crack she'd found last month, the crack she still hadn't shown him, " — we take payment in unusual forms. Years. Memories. Names. First songs. I've seen the entries."

Something flickered across his face. "You've read the deep ledgers."

"I balance them. That's my work. Someone has to make the columns agree, and they only agree if you count everything." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, because her legs had decided the matter for her. "Master, I found an entry last month that has no counterweight. A payment received — one name, freely given, from a client in Vell — but no trade issued against it. Nothing owed. Nothing returned. In thirty years of books, I have never seen the guild take without giving. So either our records are wrong, or—"

"Or the payment wasn't for us." Corvain removed his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into a register she'd never heard him use — low, weary, older than the rest of him. "Jude. Before I say anything else, I need you to understand that everything I have done, I have done to keep you safe. Not the guild. *You.*"

The word landed strangely. *You,* singular. Not the apprentice. Not the staff. Her.

"Master—"

"The proclamation changes things." He was talking fast now, an accountant watching a fraud unfold in real time, trying to get ahead of the losses. "Thirty days. Assessors in every city. They'll start with the obvious — the hedge-witches, the storm-callers, poor Maren Ostrey and her like — and then they'll work inward, because Aldric's people aren't fools, whatever else they are. They'll follow the money. They'll follow the *trades*. And eventually they'll follow the trades here." He opened a drawer Jude had never seen unlocked. From it he lifted a ledger — small, black-bound, its spine cracked with age, sealed with a band of dull lead stamped with a sigil she didn't recognize: a tower, and around the tower, a ring of interlocked hands.

"What is that?"

"The reason you exist," Corvain said, "and the reason I should have sent you away ten years ago, and the reason I didn't, because I am an old man and a coward and I loved having you in my house." He set the ledger on the desk between them. His hand rested on the lead seal, and it was shaking. "This is the Sealed Page. Every guildhouse keeps one. It holds the entries that can't be posted in the open books — the covenant entries. The payments that aren't for us."

"The covenant," Jude repeated. The word tasted foreign, but not entirely unfamiliar — like a word from a language she'd spoken as an infant. "You've never used that word."

"No. And I swore I never would." He drew a thin knife from his sleeve — Corvain, who sharpened quills, who owned nothing sharper than a letter-opener — and slit the lead band with a single practiced stroke. "But they took Maren Ostrey today, and in thirty days they'll be at our door, and there is no version of the coming months in which you stay safe by knowing less. So. Knowing more. God forgive me."

He opened the book.

The pages inside were not like the guild's working ledgers. The paper was heavy, cream-colored, and the entries were written in a hand so old-fashioned it was nearly calligraphy, each line ruled in faded gold. And the entries themselves —

Jude leaned close and felt the floor tilt beneath her certainties.

*Paid to the Spire, on behalf of the Free Cities of the Coast: the memory of the flood-year, held in trust by the Guild of Vell. Received against: continued passage of the river-wisdom.*

*Paid to the Spire: three generations of the Harrow family's luck, surrendered willingly. Received against: the unbreaking of the Vell aqueducts.*

*Paid to the Spire: the name of the drowned god of Ashport harbor, freely given by the last priest who bore it. Received against: the harbor's keeping.*

Every impossible trade she had ever puzzled over — every strange payment, every unbalanced column — resolved here into a single vast pattern. The guild wasn't a brokerage. It was a treasury. And the currency it collected wasn't wealth.

It was wonder. It was the raw stuff of the world's strangeness, paid out generation by generation to something called the Spire, in exchange for — for what? The entries said *received against* and listed miracles. Rivers that behaved. Walls that held. Luck, weather, harvests, the quiet cooperation of the deep places of the earth. The free cities had been buying their safety from something inhuman for longer than Ashport had had a name.

And the guild had been dying. She'd known that part already; every ledger-keeper knew the guild was smaller than it had been, poorer, its client list shrinking year over year. She'd thought it was commerce. Competition. Changing times.

Now she read the entries in the margins, the ones in newer, shakier hands, and understood it was something else entirely.

*No bearer this generation. Payment deferred. The Spire dims.*

*Deferred again. Corvain to note: the seal remains unclaimed.*

*Third deferral. The borderlands thin. We have no one. God help us, we have no one to send.*

"Corvain." Her voice came out very small. "What is a seal?"

He turned the page.

And there — alone on a leaf otherwise blank, written in ink so fresh it seemed still wet, in a hand she recognized instantly because she had spent ten years learning to imitate it — was a single entry.

*The seal-bearer of this generation:*

*Jude Joubert.*

*Brought to this house, aged six days, by her mother, who paid the guild the only coin she had left: the memory of ever having had a daughter. Entry witnessed and sealed by E. Corvain, keeper.*

*Note: the child is not a client. The child is not an asset. The child is the covenant's living seal — the bloodline appointed to renew the bargain at the Spire when she comes of age. Let no keeper treat her as anything less than the whole of the guild's purpose. Let no keeper tell her before her time, lest fear make her run from the only thing that can save us all.*

*God forgive me. She has my eyes. I have watched her grow up in my own house and called it charity.*

The room went very quiet. Somewhere outside, a gull cried over the harbor, and the sound seemed to come from very far away, from another world entirely — a world where Jude Joubert was an orphan clerk with a talent for sums, and not whatever this was.

"Read it again," Corvain said softly. "The last line. Read what I wrote."

She read it. Her lips moved without sound. *She has my eyes.*

"You," she said. The word wouldn't behave. "You're — my mother gave up the memory of me. Of *having* me. So she doesn't — somewhere there's a woman who doesn't know she—"

"She died four winters ago," Corvain said gently. "In Vell. Fever. I paid for the burial through intermediaries. It's in the expense log, if you want the entry number."

Jude laughed. It came out broken, one syllable, awful. "Of course it is. Of course there's an entry number."

"Jude—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. She was aware, distantly, that she was crying, and that the tears felt like someone else's, belonging to some other girl whose whole life had just been repossessed. "The covenant. The renewal. 'When she comes of age.' I'm twenty-two. What does — what happens at the Spire? What does renewal *mean?*"

Corvain closed the black ledger with great care, as though shutting a coffin.

"That," he said, "is what I was trying to explain when the bells rang. And now the bells have rung, and the assessors are in the city, and we have perhaps thirty days instead of the twenty years I'd hoped for." He met her eyes, and for the first time since she'd known him, Master Corvain — the most balanced man in Ashport, the keeper of impossible accounts — looked at her with his columns wide open and nothing reconciled.

"The hunt isn't for the gifted, Jude. Or rather — it is, but that's the small hunt. Aldric wants power for his armies, and the gifted are kindling. But the crown's scholars know the old stories too. They know what a seal-bearer is worth." He rose, crossed to the shutters, and peered out at the street, where the afternoon light was going long and amber and treacherous. "If they learn what you are before we can get you out of Ashport, they won't put you in a wagon. Wagons are for resources."

Outside, far off toward the king's road, a second wagon rattled past. Jude found she could hear it clearly through the walls — the wheels, the hooves, and underneath it all, faintly, a woman's voice, still saying reasonably, still asking someone to check the records.

"Come away from the window," Corvain said. "We have work to do. Tonight, while the city sleeps, you and I are going to open every sealed page in this house — because if you are going to save two worlds, little Jude, you are at least going to do it with the books balanced."

And Jude, who had nothing left in her but the habit of obedience and the shape of her own name written in a stranger's ancient hand, followed him into the dark of the archive — where, on the lowest shelf, behind the ledgers of dead clients, something was already waiting that neither of them had put there.
