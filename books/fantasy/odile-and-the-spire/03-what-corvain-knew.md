## 3. What Corvain Knew

The thing on the lowest shelf was a ledger bound in grey leather that had once been white. Jude knew this because she had catalogued every binding in the archive twice a year for six years, and there had never been a grey book. There had never been a book on that shelf at all. The lowest shelf held nothing but dust and the smell of cold stone, because Master Corvain had told her, on her first day, that it sagged in the middle and could bear no weight.

It bore the weight of the ledger easily.

"Corvain," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "There's a book."

Her master stood at the top of the archive stairs with a lamp in one hand and a pry-bar in the other, and for a long moment he did not move. The lamplight caught his face from below, hollowing his cheeks, deepening the furrows around his mouth until he looked like a man carved by weather rather than years.

"I know," he said. "I put it there."

* * *

They did not open it at the shelf. Corvain carried it up to the reading table as though it were made of eggshell and sleeping things, and Jude watched him clear the surface of everything else — the ink pots, the day-book, the little brass scale they used for weighing coin — until only the grey ledger sat in the pool of lamplight, alone, like a defendant.

"Twenty-two years I've kept this house," Corvain said. "Twenty-two years, and in all that time I have lied to you exactly twice. Once when you asked why we never take new apprentices." He drew out a chair for her, an old courtesy, automatic as breathing. "And once tonight, when I said we would open every sealed page together. Because we won't. We'll open one. And then you're going to run, and I am going to stay, and you are never going to forgive me for either half of it."

"Master—"

"Jude." He said her name the way he corrected a mis-entered figure: gently, finally, without room for appeal. "Sit down. Read. You wanted the truth of the guild. It has wanted you longer."

She sat. She opened the cover.

The first page was not paper. Or it was paper once, but something had happened to it — pressed it flat and thin and faintly luminous, like a leaf kept too long between the pages of a heavier book. Across its face ran columns in an ink that shifted color when she moved: silver, then green, then a bruised violet, then silver again. The hand was ancient and unhurried and beautiful, and it was the same hand she had seen on the sealed page bearing her name.

*In the four hundredth year of the Accord,* the entry began, *the Spire called, and the free cities answered, and the Guild of Impossible Things was founded to keep the accounts between them.*

Below that, entries. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not payments of coin.

*A payment of memory, tendered by the weaver Anselm of Ashport: the color of his mother's eyes, surrendered willingly, in exchange for rain over the eastern barley.* Signed with a sigil that looked like a knot of thread.

*A payment of years, tendered by the ferryman's daughter Ilse: eleven years of her life, surrendered willingly, in exchange for the river staying within its banks through flood season.* Signed with an oar-blade.

*A payment of a name, tendered by a man who signed himself only "the third son": his name entire, surrendered willingly, in exchange for his brother waking whole from a fever that should have killed him.* Where the signature should have been, the page was simply blank, and somehow the blankness hurt to look at, the way a pulled tooth hurts.

Jude turned pages with hands that had begun to shake. Rain bought with memories. Harvests bought with voices. A plague turned aside from Vell at the cost of every song one district had ever known — the entry noted, in that same patient hand, that the district had not sung since, and did not know why, and had stopped asking.

"This is what the impossible trades are," she whispered. "This is what we've been balancing. Not miracles. *Prices.*"

"The guild does not sell impossible things," Corvain said quietly. "The guild *pays* for them. That is the whole of our business, Jude, and it has been the whole of our business since before Ashport had walls. There is a covenant between this world and the world of the Spire. The Spire feeds the land its wonders — the rain that falls where rain is needed, the fevers that break, the rivers that keep their manners. And the land pays. Through us. In the only currency the Spire accepts, which is the currency of selves. Memories. Years. Names. Gifts."

"Gifts," Jude said. Her mouth was dry. "You mean magic."

"I mean the talent some few are born with to make small impossibilities real. The Spire takes those too. Has always taken them. Every gifted soul in the free cities who ever 'disappeared,' who ever wandered off and was mourned and forgotten — check the old parish rolls against this ledger sometime, if you want to lose sleep for a decade. They didn't vanish. They were *tendered.*"

"And the seal-bearer." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for figures that refused to add up. "My name. In that sealed page. Written in a stranger's ancient hand twenty years ago."

Corvain closed his eyes. When he opened them again, they were wet, and he did not pretend otherwise, and somehow that was worse than anything else in the room.

"Not a stranger's hand," he said. "Mine. I wrote your name into the covenant registry the night you were born, Jude Joubert, because I had no right to do it and no choice, and because your mother made me swear I would."

* * *

The lamp guttered. Outside, Ashport lay quiet under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and somewhere in that quiet a watchman called the hour — two bells, all's well — and the sound of it seemed to come from another country.

"Your mother," Corvain said, "was named Sera Joubert. And before that she was named Sera of the Line, which is a title and not a name, and which meant that she carried in her blood the living seal of the covenant. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The covenant is not ink, child. It is not paper. The ledgers are only the *record* of it, the way a marriage contract is not the marriage. The covenant itself lives in a body. One body in each generation. A seal-bearer, born with the whole accord folded up inside her blood like a letter waiting to be opened."

Jude's hands had gone very still on the grey pages. Stillness was a habit too; she had learned it young, learned to hold herself motionless while numbers settled, while clients wept, while Corvain explained compound interest to widows. She held herself motionless now.

"My mother," she said. "Who died of a winter fever when I was three. Who was a seamstress. Who mended sails."

"Who was the seal-bearer of her generation, and who renewed the covenant at the Spire twenty-three years ago, and who came back from that renewal changed in ways I will not describe to you because you will have nightmares enough soon without my help. And who understood, better than anyone, what was coming. Because the renewals were coming faster, Jude. Each generation sooner than the last. The covenant fraying. The Spire hungry. And the guild dying — look at the membership rolls, you've balanced them yourself, forty names when there should be four hundred, and half of those forty too old or too frightened or too ignorant to know what they're keeping alive with their bookkeeping. Your mother saw that the next renewal would fall to her daughter. So she did the only merciful thing left. She hid you. She gave you a common name and a common trade and a master sworn to silence, and she prayed the covenant would hold long enough for you to live and grow old and die ungifted and unfound."

"But it hasn't held."

"No." Corvain's voice dropped to almost nothing. "It hasn't. The entries have been failing for months — you saw it yourself, the imbalances, the payments that don't reconcile. The Spire is taking what it isn't given now. Taking wild. And a failing covenant doesn't just mean fewer miracles, Jude. It means the borderlands rot. It means the impossible things turn *feral*. It means—" He stopped. Rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and for a moment he looked every one of his sixty-odd years, a tired clerk in a cold room, and Jude loved him so fiercely and so helplessly that it felt like drowning.

"It means King Aldric's hunt couldn't have come at a worse time," she finished for him. "Or a better one. Depending on what you think he knows."

"He knows enough. His witch-finders aren't hunting gifted children for soldiers, whatever his criers say. Gifted blood is the key to the Spire's door, and Aldric means to walk through it. Which means the moment anyone learns the seal-bearer lives—" 

He never finished the sentence.

Later — in the weeks after, in the whole long altered life that came after — Jude would replay that instant a thousand times and find it different each time. Sometimes she heard the window. Sometimes only the lamp flame leaning sideways as if in wind, though there was no wind. Sometimes she remembered nothing at all except that Corvain's hand shot out and snuffed the lamp, and the dark came down like a lid, and his voice — calm, clipped, the voice of the counting-house — said:

"*Back stair. Now. Don't light it.*"

* * *

She went. Obedience was the deepest habit she had, deeper than stillness, deeper than arithmetic, and she was three steps down the back stair with the grey ledger clutched to her chest before she understood that obedience was exactly what he needed from her, and hated herself for how easily it had come.

Behind her, above her, the archive door opened without a sound.

She should have kept going. Down the stair, out the coal-door, into the alley, gone. That was the design of the thing; that was what Corvain was buying with whatever he was about to spend. But Jude Joubert had spent six years learning that books must balance, and a man's life going out above her was the largest imbalance she had ever been asked to carry, and her feet simply refused.

She stopped on the stair. She looked back up through the dark.

Lamplight bloomed in the archive doorway — a cold white light, wrong for any lamp she knew, the color of moonlight through ice. Three shapes stood in it. They wore no livery, but she knew them anyway, the way livestock knows the slaughterhouse smell: the witch-finders' plain grey coats, their faces wrapped to the eyes, their hands bare. Bare hands, she thought absurdly. Of course. How do you harvest a gift except skin to skin?

"Master Corvain," said the foremost, in a voice like a file drawn across iron. "The Guild of Impossible Things. We've come to audit."

"You're late," Corvain said. He sounded almost amused. He had stepped between them and the stair, an old man in a nightshirt with a pry-bar held low and easy, and Jude thought: *he has done this before. He has imagined this exact night for twenty years, and here it is, and he is not surprised.* "The guild's books closed at midnight. Come back in the morning."

"We'll see the registry."

"The registry is sealed."

"Everything seals," said the witch-finder, "and everything opens. That is rather the point of us."

What happened next happened fast, and most of it happened in the dark, and Jude's memory of it would always be less a picture than a sequence of sounds and lights. The pry-bar swinging. The white light flaring. Corvain shouting words that were not words — syllables with weight behind them, old guild-craft, the small impossibilities of the trade turned suddenly large — and the archive shelves *moving*, groaning forward like a closing jaw, ledgers toppling in avalanches of dead clients' accounts. One witch-finder went down under a cascade of shelving. Another's wrapped face split open with a sound like tearing linen, and beneath the wrap his eyes were entirely white, and he screamed in a voice that was two voices, and Jude understood with a lurch that these men were already partly harvested, partly *spent*, puppets running on borrowed fuel.

"JUDE." Corvain's roar cracked in the middle. "THE STAIR. *GO.*"

She went. Two steps, three — and then the third witch-finder came over the top of the shelving like a spider, impossibly fast, impossibly silent, and landed on the stair above her, and the white light poured down the steps toward her like water finding its level.

She felt it touch her. Just barely — a fingertip's graze across the back of her neck, cold as well-water — and inside her chest something answered. Something vast and asleep and *hers*, turning over in its sleep, opening one eye. The grey ledger grew hot against her ribs. The stairwell filled with a smell like ozone and old rain, and the witch-finder recoiled as if scalded, and his two-voiced scream said words she didn't understand but recognized anyway, because she had read them an hour ago in shifting silver-green ink:

*Seal. Seal. The seal is HERE—*

Then Corvain hit him from behind with the pry-bar, and both of them went down in a tangle of limbs and lamplight, and Jude's master looked up at her once across the length of the stair — blood at his hairline, his breath ragged, his eyes fierce and clear and absolutely certain — and mouthed, with the exaggerated clarity of a man correcting a novice's ledger line:

*Balance. The. Books.*

Meaning: run. Meaning: live. Meaning: this is the last entry I will ever make, and it balances, because I promised your mother, and a promise kept is the only profit this house has ever needed.

She ran.

Out the coal-door into the alley, into the rain that had just begun to fall — real rain, ordinary rain, the kind that fell without anyone paying for it — and she ran through Ashport's sleeping streets with the grey ledger hugged to her chest and her master's blood on the inside of her eyelids, and behind her the white light rose over the archive like a second dawn, and ahead of her the streets were empty, all empty, all empty except—

Except for the figure standing at the mouth of Cooper's Lane. Tall. Cloaked. A sword at the hip and rain beading on shoulders that did not move to shrug it off, and eyes — even at that distance, even in the dark — eyes that caught the distant fire-light and threw it back silver.

The figure saw her. Saw the ledger. Saw, perhaps, the thing answering in her blood, because it took one step forward and said, in a voice roughened by long disuse and a foreign accent:

"So the seal lives. *Run, girl — they're behind you, and I am not your enemy, but you'll have to trust me faster than that.*"

Jude, who had trusted exactly one person in her life, and who had watched him die buying her ten minutes of head start, ran straight at the stranger anyway — because behind her the white light was spreading, and ahead was the only direction left.

The cloaked figure swore in a language that made the rain hiss, drew her sword, and turned to meet what was coming out of the dark.
