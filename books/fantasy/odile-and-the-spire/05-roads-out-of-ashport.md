## 5. Roads Out of Ashport

The rain found them before the road did.

Jude had imagined flight as something swift — a horse bought with her last coins, hooves hammering through the night. Instead there was this: wading knee-deep through a drowned barley field in the grey hour before dawn, her ledger satchel held above her head like an offering, while the woman beside her moved through the water as if it were merely an inconvenience rather than an enemy.

"Stop splashing," Odile said, without turning around.

"I'm *wading*."

"You're announcing us to every hunter between here and the hills." Odile crouched suddenly, one hand flat on the water's surface, and went still in a way that made Jude's breath catch. The rain fell around her and seemed to fall *around* her, each drop bending slightly aside, as though the water itself had been asked politely to mind its manners.

"What are you doing?"

"Listening." Odile's eyes had gone pale, almost silver, the color of the sky behind storm-clouds. "The lowlands carry sound. Footsteps. Hooves. Things worse than either." She rose after a long moment. "Nothing yet. They'll have lost our trail at the weir, but Serre doesn't lose trails for long."

"You keep saying that name. Serre."

"I know." Odile started forward again, angling east toward a line of willows that marked the old towpath. "And I'd rather not discuss her while we're standing in water that comes up to your ribs if you step wrong. Walk where I walk."

It was, Jude would reflect later, the first thing Odile ever asked of her that wasn't survival: *walk where I walk.* At the time it felt like being ordered about by a jailer who happened to be saving her life. She followed anyway, matching her steps to the prints the taller woman left in the mud, and by the time the sun hauled itself up behind a curtain of cloud they had put four miles of flooded country between themselves and Ashport's burning gates.

Four miles. It didn't feel like enough. It felt like the first inch of a very long fall.

* * *

They made their first camp in the hollow trunk of a fallen elm, and Odile built a fire that produced no smoke.

Jude stared at it for a full minute before she could speak. "That's not possible."

"No," Odile agreed. She was tearing strips from a packet of dried fish with the efficiency of someone who had eaten worse in worse places. "That's rather the point of it."

"The fire is burning. Fire makes smoke. That's — that's how combustion works, I've read the treatises—"

"You've read treatises written by people who think the world runs on gears and chemistry." Odile fed a strip of fish to the flames, which accepted it with a sound like a contented sigh. "The world runs on the Spire. Everything impossible you've ever heard of — every charm against blight, every well that never ran dry, every child born breathing underwater in the marsh villages — that's the Spire's work, leaking into the world along the covenant's channels. Smokeless fire is one of the small ones. A trickle." She glanced up, and in the firelight her face looked younger and wearier than it had in daylight. "You've been living downstream of a miracle your whole life, ledger-keeper. You just weren't taught to see the current."

Jude hugged her knees. The fish was terrible. She ate it anyway. "Corvain never told me any of this. Twenty years I kept his ledgers, and he let me believe we were — what, charity brokers? Dealers in oddities?"

"He was protecting you. Badly, and too late, but genuinely." Odile said it flatly, without comfort, which somehow made it land harder. "The guild pays the covenant in gifts. That's the trade. The Spire feeds the land its impossible things; the guild finds the gifted and pays their gifts into the seal — willingly given, freely recorded. Memory. Years. Names. The things you saw in those entries that shouldn't exist." Her eyes flicked to the satchel, where Jude's ledger slept against her hip like a sleeping animal. "You saw them because you were meant to see them eventually. Seal-bearers always could read the true columns."

"And no one thought to tell me."

"Would it have changed anything? You'd have known what you were two days earlier. You'd have died two days more afraid." Odile fed the fire another strip of fish. "I don't defend Corvain. I'm telling you why men like him lie. Because the truth has teeth, and they'd rather lose the children's trust than hand them to the teeth early."

"My master is dead," Jude said quietly. "You can stop explaining him to me."

Odile was silent for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its edge. "I know. I watched him hold that door. He bought you an hour with his life, and he knew exactly what he was buying." She looked into the smokeless flame. "Men who die like that deserve better than to be remembered only as liars."

Jude didn't answer. But she didn't argue either, and somewhere in the space between those two silences, the first plank of a bridge was laid down without either of them noticing.

* * *

On the second day the hunters found the weir.

Jude felt it before she understood it — a wrongness in the rain, a pressure behind her ears like a change in altitude. Odile had already turned, already moving, when the first horn sounded across the water: three notes, high and thin, repeated twice.

"What is that?"

"Serre's pack. Horns that sound *backward* — you hear them coming from where they aren't." Odile caught Jude's arm and hauled her off the towpath, down a bank slick with clay, into a stand of reeds tall enough to swallow them both. "Down. Knees. Breathe through your mouth."

Through the reed-stems Jude watched the far bank, where moments ago there had been nothing, and now there were riders — six of them, in Aldric's black and gold, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once lost quarry. At their head rode a figure in grey, hooded, whose horse left no prints in the mud.

"That's not Serre," Odile breathed. "That's a finder. They track gift-signature, not scent." Her grip on Jude's arm tightened painfully. "Which means they're tracking *you*, not me. Your blood is loud, seal-bearer. To them you're a bonfire in a dark field."

The riders paused at the weir. The hooded figure raised one hand, and the rain — the whole grey sheet of it over the lowlands — seemed to lean toward Jude like grass toward wind.

Then Odile did something strange. She took Jude's hand, pressed it palm-down into the mud, and murmured words in a language that sounded like water running over stones. And the rain leaned away.

The hooded figure's hand dropped. The riders wheeled, cantered south, and were gone, horns fading in the wrong direction entirely.

For a long time neither of them moved. Then Odile sat back in the muck, dripping, exhausted, and laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years.

"What?" Jude said. "What just happened?"

"I lied to the rain." She wiped her face, leaving a streak of clay across one cheekbone. "Told it your signature was a mile north. Cheap trick. It'll fool finders for half a day, no more." She looked at Jude then, really looked, and something in her expression shifted — the wariness banking down a degree, like a lamp turned low instead of out. "You didn't panic. In the reeds. Most people panic."

"I catalogued," Jude admitted. "Horn calls, rider count, direction of travel. It's what I do. When the world stops making sense, I write it down until it does again."

Something flickered across Odile's face — recognition, maybe, or its cousin. "A ledger-keeper's courage."

"It's real courage."

"I know it is." Odile got up, joints cracking, and offered her hand. "My grandmother kept bees. Same temperament. You don't run from the hive when it stings; you note which hive stung you, and why, and you come back smarter." She pulled Jude to her feet. "Come on. Half a day isn't much. We should be in the hills by dark."

* * *

The second camp was a shepherd's hut with half a roof, and the third was a hay barn that smelled of mice and old summers. On the fourth night, in the lee of the first real hills, with the lowlands spread out below them like a drowned coin, Odile finally talked about the Spire.

She talked the way some people pray — haltingly at first, then all at once, as if a dam had been waiting years for a crack.

"I was born under it," she said. The fire was small tonight, ordinary, smoking. She seemed to want it that way. "Everyone in the border villages is, a little. We're the Spire's doorstep folk. My gift came late — nineteen. Water-shaping. Nothing grand. I could ask rivers to be polite." Her mouth twisted. "The Wardens found me inside a season. Took me up the Stair. Trained me. Gave me a grey cloak and a duty: watch the covenant's channels, mend the small breaches, find the seal-bearer when renewal came due."

"And you failed."

"I refused." The words came out level, but her hands had gone still in her lap, and stillness, Jude was learning, was what Odile did instead of trembling. "There was a renewal due when I was twenty-six. The bearer was a girl from the marsh country. Sixteen years old. The terms as written — the terms the guild had recorded for centuries — required everything. Not her gift. *Her*. And I stood at the threshold of the Spire with her hand in mine, and I said no. I walked her back down the Stair myself." Odile's jaw worked. "The breach that followed killed four hundred people in the lowland towns. Floods that came uphill. Wells that ran backward. The Spire doesn't rage, ledger-keeper. It simply... withdraws. And everything it was holding up comes down."

Jude was quiet for a long time. Below them, far off, a light moved through the drowned fields — a lantern, or a finder's cold star, impossible to say.

"So they exiled you."

"They pardoned the girl. Sent her home with honors. Exiled me." Odile smiled without humor. "Because the girl was the covenant's property, and I was the covenant's officer, and officers don't get to have consciences. Consciences are expensive."

"Do you regret it?"

The question hung in the dark. Odile took her time answering, and Jude realized she was watching her do it — watching the exact moment the truth assembled itself.

"No," Odile said at last. "I regret the four hundred. I will regret the four hundred until I die, and I expect to do it thoroughly. But I don't regret telling the Spire no." She turned her head, firelight catching the grey in her eyes. "Ask me again in a week. Ask me whether I'd spend four hundred strangers to save one girl I knew. Ask me what I'd do if the girl were you."

"Why would you say it like that?"

"Because it's true, and I'm tired of talking in circles, and tomorrow we cross into country where one of us may not come back." Odile lay back against her saddle, hat over her eyes, conversation visibly concluded. "Sleep, ledger-keeper. Dawn comes whether we balance it or not."

Jude stayed awake longer. She thought about Corvain holding a door. About a sixteen-year-old girl walking down a stair to honors she hadn't asked for. About the weight of a number — four hundred — against the weight of one, and which side of that arithmetic she'd have stood on, and hated herself a little for not knowing.

When she finally slept, she dreamed of ledgers whose columns ran with water, and in the dream she wrote and wrote and could not make the numbers hold still.

* * *

They crossed the hills on the sixth day, and that evening, in a hollow above a stream that ran clear and cold, Odile taught her to listen.

"Not with your ears. With whatever it is Corvain woke in you. Close your eyes. Put your hand on the ground."

"This is ridiculous."

"Probably. Do it anyway."

Jude pressed her palm flat against the earth, feeling foolish, feeling the damp soak through her sleeve — and then, faintly, unmistakably, she felt the *current*. Not sound. Something older than sound. A vast slow turning beneath the world, like the heartbeat of something asleep, and threaded through it, thin as wire, a discord. A place where the music skipped.

"There," she whispered. "Northeast. Something's — torn."

Odile had gone very still. When Jude opened her eyes, the warden was looking at her with an expression she couldn't name — awe, perhaps, or grief wearing awe's clothes.

"A breach," Odile said softly. "A real one. You felt it on your first try. Do you know how long it took me to hear the channels? Two years of training. You put your hand in the dirt and heard it like a wrong note in a song." She shook her head slowly. "Corvain hid you well. Too well. You could have been — " She stopped herself.

"Could have been what?"

"Nothing. Rest. We move at first light." But she said it too quickly, and she took the first watch alone, and Jude lay staring at the stars and wondering what other sentences Odile was in the habit of not finishing.

* * *

On the seventh day, the rain stopped altogether, and that was worse.

"Finders hate rain," Odile explained, scanning the ridgeline with narrowed eyes. "It scrambles signature-traces. Clear weather means they can read us again." She pointed to a thread of smoke rising far to the south, thin and deliberate. "Signal fire. They're coordinating. Serre's crossed the river."

"You keep saying her name like it costs you something."

"It does." Odile shouldered her pack and started up the scree. "Serre trained me. Fought beside me for nine years. When I was exiled, she was the one who escorted me to the border — and she was the one who volunteered, a year later, to hunt the covenant's enemies for whoever paid best. The Spire first. Then Aldric, when his gold outbid loyalty." Her voice was carefully, expensively neutral. "She is the finest blade the Wardens ever produced, and she has never once in her life refused a price. If she's on our trail, we are no longer fleeing finders. We're fleeing *her*."

"How long do we have?"

"Two days. Perhaps three, if the passes are kind." Odile glanced back, and for one unguarded moment the exhaustion in her face was naked, human, almost young. "They won't be kind. Passes never are."

They climbed. Behind them the lowlands glittered, drowned and quiet, hiding whatever the flood had taken. Ahead the mountains stood up white and indifferent against a hardening sky, and somewhere among them, Jude knew, waited the free city of Vell, Magistra Ilsen, and the next chapter of a bargain she was only beginning to understand she'd inherited.

That night, sharing the last of the dried fish, Jude opened her ledger by firelight and — for the first time since Ashport — wrote in it. Not accounts. Just this, in her small careful hand:

*Day seven. Odile refused a price once. I am beginning to understand what that cost her, and what it might mean for me.*

She felt Odile watching her write. Neither of them said anything about it.

But when Jude banked the fire and lay down, she found that Odile had shifted her own bedroll closer — not touching, not quite, but near enough that the warmth reached her. Near enough to count.

Near enough, Jude thought as she drifted off, to be a kind of promise.

And far to the south, past the drowned fields and the burned gates, a rider in grey crested a hill, looked at the cold ashes of their fire, and smiled.
