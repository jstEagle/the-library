## 6. The Free City of Vell

They smelled Vell before they saw it.

Not the usual city-smell of tanneries and woodsmoke and horse dung that hung over Ashport like a damp coat. This was something rawer — charcoal and wet ash and the sour tang of too many bodies pressed into too little space, carried on a wind off the salt flats so thick with it that Odile pulled her scarf up over her nose and Jude found herself breathing through her mouth until her throat went dry.

The road crested a low ridge of glacial moraine, and there it was: Vell, the Grey Pearl, built in terraces down the flank of a dead volcano, its white walls streaked with centuries of rain-stain so that from a distance it looked like a skull wearing a crown. The lake at its feet was silver-grey in the late afternoon light, crowded with barges — more barges than Jude had ever seen on any waterway, lashed three and four abreast at the quays, their decks stacked with furniture, livestock, children.

Refugees. The word arrived in her mind with the weight of a ledger entry she didn't want to post.

"Queue at the gates," Odile said. She had stopped walking, one hand shading her eyes, and Jude watched her read the road below the way she read everything — as terrain, as threat, as distance between herself and danger. "Half a mile, maybe more. They'll be searching bags. Possibly searching people."

"Searching for what?"

"Gifts." Odile's voice was flat. "Aldric's witch-finders have agents in every free city now. They don't wear his colors. They look like pilgrims, peddlers, beggars. They watch who gets waved through quickly and who gets held. Then they follow the ones who are held."

Jude looked at the gate queue — a brown river of carts and families winding up toward walls that had stood for nine hundred years — and tried to imagine a witch-finder among them. She couldn't. That was the trouble, she was beginning to understand. The impossible things had always been invisible to her until someone put them in a ledger. Now the most important thing in her life was invisible by design, running in her blood like ink through water, and anyone at all might be able to see it.

"Then we don't go through the queue," she said.

Odile turned and looked at her, and there was something in the look that wasn't quite approval but was adjacent to it. "No," she agreed. "We don't."

* * *

The fisher-tunnels were an Ashport legend that turned out to be a Vell reality: smugglers' passages bored through the volcano's roots where it met the lake, used for generations to move untaxed brandy and unstamped silk beneath the customs houses. Odile knew of them because, she said, the Spire had known of them, because the Spire knew everything that moved through the borderlands whether the borderlands liked it or not.

Jude learned two things about wading through flooded tunnels by torchlight. First, that the cold of mountain-fed water goes through leather boots in about forty steps and then begins negotiating directly with the bone. Second, that fear, given nothing else to do, will start doing arithmetic.

She counted her assets as they walked, because counting was the only prayer she knew. One: she was alive, which Corvain had bought with his life and which she therefore owed somebody. Two: she had the sealed page from the guild ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her sternum, the one marked *seal-bearer*, with her own name written in Corvain's cramped hand twenty years before she'd been old enough to hold a pen. Three: she had Odile, who was not a friend, exactly, and not trustworthy, exactly, but who had killed two assassins in the rain outside Ashport without hesitation and then taught Jude how to walk so her footprints filled with water.

Four: Magistra Ilsen. Last senior member of the Guild of Impossible Things. Keeper of the covenant's original terms. The only person left alive who might be able to tell Jude what the mark on that page actually meant — what it demanded, what it cost, what happened if you refused to pay.

"Ilsen," Odile said, echoing the thought. Her voice bounced strangely off the wet stone. "You're certain she'll help?"

"She trained under my grandmother. She kept the western ledgers for thirty years. When I wrote to her about anomalies in the accounts, she answered every letter in detail." Jude stepped over something soft and unspeakable and kept her eyes on the torchlight ahead. "She's the only senior member who never stopped answering letters."

"People who answer letters can be found by other people who ask different questions."

"That occurred to me around mile forty."

Odile made a sound that might, in a better-lit world, have been a laugh.

They came up out of the tunnels through the floor of a cooperage near the waterfront, startling an apprentice into knocking over a barrel of hoop-poles, and emerged into Vell proper as the evening bells began — six deep bronze notes rolling down the terraces, each one slightly out of tune with the last, which the locals said was deliberate, which the locals said about everything.

* * *

Ilsen's shop sat on Inkwell Row in the middle terrace, between a mapmaker's and a shop that sold nothing but buttons, and its shutters were closed.

Not shuttered-for-the-night closed. Shuttered-and-nailed closed. Boards crossed over the windows, a tax seal — Vell's grey pear, stamped in black wax — slapped across the door seam. Dust lay in the seams of the boards in a way that suggested weeks, not days.

Jude stood in front of it and felt the arithmetic stop working.

"No," she said, to the door, to the dust, to the week of walking that had ended here. She heard how young her voice sounded and hated it. "No, she was here. She answered my letter in spring. She said—" What had she said? Jude closed her eyes and the letter surfaced whole, because she had memorized it the way she memorized everything, because memory was the only vault she'd ever trusted. *Your questions are correct, child, and that is precisely why you must stop asking them by post. Come north when the plums ripen. Bring nothing written.*

"The plums," Jude said aloud. "It's past plum season."

"It's past a great many seasons." Odile was already moving along the row, reading the street the way she read roads. A knot of people had gathered at the corner near the mapmaker's, heads together around a broadsheet; further down, a woman was chalking something on a wall and rubbing it out again when a watchman passed. "The shop was seized. Tax seal means the council did it, not Aldric's men — his would be subtler. Which means there's a record. Seizures get filed."

"You want to go to the records office."

"I want to know what they took and where they took it. You want the same thing. Records offices keep copies." Odile glanced back at her. "Also, you're the one who reads ledgers for pleasure. Try to look bored."

"Bored," Jude repeated. "I'm a fugitive whose mentor was murdered, standing in front of the nailed-shut shop of my last hope, and you want me to look *bored*."

"Yes. You're doing it now, actually. Very convincing. Come along."

* * *

The records office occupied a former chapel, which explained why the clerk behind the counter had the air of a man who had once believed in something. He was elderly, half-asleep, and deeply unimpressed by Odile's forged writ of inquiry — Jude watched him examine it and understood, with a lurch, that he saw through it completely and simply did not care enough to say so.

"Seized properties, Inkwell Row," he said, and hauled a ledger toward him with the reverence of a man handling scripture or the contempt of a man handling paperwork; with clerks, Jude thought, it was often both. His finger walked down columns. "Inkwell Row... Inkwell Row... there. Number nine. Merchant premises, formerly licensed to one Ilsen, M., trade of..." He squinted. "Appraiser. Curiosities. Appraised and seized under the Public Safety Provision, forty-one days ago. Contents removed to the Undercroft of the Council House. Proprietor—" he paused, and Jude's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth— "proprietor detained for questioning and released same day. Left the city. Destination unrecorded."

"Released," Jude said.

"Released," the clerk confirmed, and yawned. "Council's not arresting people for being odd yet, girl. Just for being loud about it. Your appraiser gave a speech at the Gate Market about the king's hunt being unlawful. Half the city heard it. Then she paid her exit toll and walked out the west gate like a sensible woman." He slid the ledger back. "Anything else? I close at the bell."

Outside, dusk had come down over the terraces, and the lamps of Vell were lighting themselves in their ancient way — no lamplighters here, just the old guild-worked flames that woke at dusk and died at dawn, one of the free cities' small stockpiled miracles, and Jude looked at them burning in their hundreds and thought: *this is what runs out. This is what the books have been quietly saying for years. The flame-oil is memory and the memory is spent and nobody balanced the account.*

"She's alive," she said. "She got out."

"She got out," Odile agreed. "West. Toward the passes. Which is either very good news or very bad news, depending on whether she knows something worth walking toward the Spire for."

"She knows everything. She told me to come when the plums ripened. She knew, Odile. Twenty years ago she knew what Corvain knew, and instead of hiding me she—" Jude stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Instead of hiding me she *left*. Both of them left me in the dark and called it protection."

"Or called it the only move on the board," Odile said, not unkindly. "Come on. We need food and a bed that isn't a tunnel, and you need to sleep before you start writing speeches to dead mentors in your head."

That was when the bells changed.

* * *

Jude had heard alarm bells exactly once in her life, during a harbor fire in Ashport, and even muffled by distance and stone, this was worse — not one bell but all of them, every tower in Vell at once, a sound like the sky cracking. The street transformed around them. Doors opened and slammed. People ran — not away from the noise, which would have made sense, but toward the Gate Market square, carrying lanterns, carrying children, carrying each other.

"Council assembly," a boy shouted as he passed. "They're posting the terms! The council's going to *answer* the king!"

The Gate Market was a bowl of stone at the heart of the middle terrace, and by the time Jude and Odile reached its rim it was full — thousands of people packed shoulder to shoulder around a raised platform where the twelve members of Vell's ruling council stood in their grey robes under a canopy, and above them, freshly pasted to the great notice-board, a single enormous sheet of paper that caught the lantern light like ice.

Jude could read it from the third tier of steps, because she had spent her life reading small print at speed, and the words went into her like a blade going into water — no resistance, just depth and cold:

*By order of the Council of Vell, in consideration of the present crisis: all persons possessing uncanny gifts shall register with the Office of Public Safety within ten days. Registered persons shall be remanded to the council's protection pending negotiation with the crown of Aldric. Refusal to register shall be treated as treason against the city.*

"They're surrendering them," Jude breathed. "They're going to hand them over."

"Not surrender," said a voice beside her, an old man with a fishmonger's apron still on, his hands shaking with rage or fear or both. "Trade. Twelve elders are going to sell our neighbors to buy themselves another winter. My daughter has a gift. She lights candles with a thought. She's *nine*."

On the platform, the council's speaker — a heavy woman with a chain of office and the face of someone who had not slept since the crisis began — was shouting over the crowd, and the crowd was shouting back, and the two shouts were about different things entirely. *Order. Survival. Arithmetic,* said the council. *Betrayal. Cowardice. Shame,* said the crowd. And underneath both, everywhere, the smaller sounds: a woman weeping into her shawl; men arguing in fast whispers about whether to run tonight or register tomorrow; somewhere near the front, a child asking a question and being hushed twice.

Odile's hand closed on Jude's arm. Not gently.

"We need to leave," Odile said quietly. "Now. Crowds like this break one direction or another, and either way, someone will be watching the edges for faces that aren't afraid correctly."

But Jude was looking at the platform, at the speaker raising her hands for silence, at the notice-board with its ten-day clock already ticking, and the ledger-keeper in her — the part of her that had spent seven years learning that every entry hides another entry — was doing the thing it did.

Because the provision had a flaw. She could see it from thirty feet away, plain as a misposted column. *Registered persons remanded pending negotiation.* Negotiation required a counterparty. And the crown of Aldric did not negotiate with cities. It invoiced them. Jude had seen the trade figures; everyone in the guild had. Aldric didn't buy things. He took possession of them, and then sent the bill for the taking.

Which meant the council wasn't selling its gifted to save the city. It was selling them to delay a reckoning it could not pay, spending the only coin it had left — its own people — on a debt that would arrive anyway, larger, next spring.

"They think they're buying time," Jude said. "They're making a down payment."

"Jude."

"If I could talk to them—"

"*Jude.*" Odile turned her fully by the shoulder, and up close her eyes were the grey-green of deep lake water, and absolutely serious. "You are a nameless fugitive with a sealed page under your shirt that three separate people have died within arm's reach of. If you stand up in that crowd and say the word 'covenant,' you will not reach the platform. You will be taken by whichever side reaches you first — the council, the mob, or Aldric's watchers, and I promise you the watchers are here, because a gathering this size is a harvest festival for them." Her grip tightened. "Corvain bought you time. Don't spend it on a soapbox."

The crowd roared — the sound swelling suddenly as someone threw a bottle that burst against the platform steps, and the council's guards closed ranks, and the bowl of the market became a single animal with ten thousand hearts beating out of rhythm.

And in that roar, in that crush, in the exact moment when every eye in Vell was fixed on the platform and none on the tiers — Jude felt it.

Not heard. Not seen. *Felt*: a thread pulling taut somewhere behind her sternum, the same sensation as the night she'd found her name in the sealed page, but stronger, directional, like a compass needle waking up. It pointed west. Through the crowd, through the wall, through the mountains — west, toward the passes, toward wherever Magistra Ilsen had walked out the gate like a sensible woman.

Toward the Spire.

The needle in her blood knew the way. Of course it did. It had always known. It was the oldest entry in the oldest ledger, and it had been waiting twenty years for her to learn to read it.

"Odile," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised them both. "How fast can we be ready to leave?"

Odile studied her face for a long moment — reading her, the way she read everything, terrain and threat and distance — and then, slowly, she smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone who has decided to lose a bet with herself.

"Give me an hour," she said, "and a very good reason."

Jude put her hand flat against her sternum, over the oilcloth and the sealed page and the needle pulling west.

"I have one," she said. "She's alive. And I think she's already on the road we have to take."

Above them, the bells of Vell rang on, and the council's ten days began.
