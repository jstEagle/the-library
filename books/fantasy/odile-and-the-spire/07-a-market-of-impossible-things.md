## 7. A Market of Impossible Things

The shop of Magistra Ilsen sat on Tallowmaker's Row like a held breath.

Jude had walked past it twice before she found the courage to stop. The street was quieter than the rest of Vell — quieter than it should have been, given the season of the Harvest Fair, when this row ought to have been thick with sugar-smoke and haggling and children underfoot. Instead the shutters were drawn along half the storefronts, and the ones still open kept their wares close to the door, as if the goods themselves had learned caution.

Ilsen's shop was the most shuttered of all. No sign hung above the door; only the ghost of one, a pale rectangle on the brick where paint had shielded the stone from weather for decades. Someone had chalked a symbol on the boards across the entrance — a circle with a slash through it, the mark Vell's neighbors used to warn off debt collectors and plague-fearers both. It had been smudged by rain and then redrawn, more than once. People were still coming here. People were still being turned away.

"Charming," Odile said behind her. She stood with her back to the wall, hood up, arms crossed, watching the street the way a cat watches a room full of rocking chairs. "Nothing says *welcome* like a plague mark."

"It's not a plague mark." Jude touched the chalk. It came away faintly grey on her fingers. "It's a guild sign. Third-circle warning. It means *the keeper is absent; nothing here is for sale.*" She hesitated. "It also means the keeper expects to come back."

Odile said nothing to that. She had said very little since they'd entered Vell two days past, since they'd learned from a harried guild clerk with ink-stained cuffs that Magistra Ilsen had not been seen in eleven days. Eleven days. In a city filling with refugees, informers, and Aldric's witch-finders dressed as tax assessors, eleven days was a very long time for an old woman to be missing.

Jude pressed her palm flat against the boards over the door. Oak, good oak, older than her. And beneath her hand, so faint she might have imagined it, something hummed. Not sound, exactly. More like the memory of sound — the way a bell keeps ringing somewhere behind the world after the ear stops hearing it.

She had felt that hum once before, standing in the guildhall at Ashport with Corvain's ledger open in front of her, reading entries that should not exist.

*Years. Names. Memories.* Payments made in things no coin could count.

"I'm going in," she said.

"You're going to stand there looking tragic until I pick the lock," Odile corrected, already kneeling. "Which we're doing quickly, because that man across the street has looked at us four times, and nobody sells chestnuts at a stall they've abandoned."

The lock surrendered with a click that sounded obscenely loud. Inside, the dark smelled of dust and dried lavender and, beneath it, something older — paper, ink, candle-tallow, the particular scent of a life spent among records. Jude's heart clenched with a homesickness she had no right to feel. It smelled like the guildhall. It smelled like Corvain's study.

"Light," Odile murmured, and a small flame kindled above her palm without gesture or word — casual as breathing, wasteful as breathing, in a city where such a display could get them both caged. Jude had stopped flinching at it sometime on the salt flats outside Ashport. She wasn't sure when. That frightened her a little, how quickly the impossible became furniture.

The shuttered light through the door slats fell in thin gold bars across a counter, shelves of jars, a workbench scattered with tools mid-use. Mid-use. A mortar with powder still in it, gone hard at the edges. An apron hung on its hook. A teacup sat on the counter beside a book, and when Jude lifted the cup, a ring of dried tea marked the wood like a small brown moon.

"She left in a hurry," Jude said. "Or she meant to come straight back."

"People who mean to come straight back leave cups," Odile agreed. She moved through the shop with a soldier's economy, checking the back door, the cellar hatch, the narrow stair to the loft. "Clear. Whoever took her didn't do it violently. Or did it very tidily."

*Took her.* Jude hated how easily those words fit now. Two weeks ago, the worst thing anyone had taken from her was an afternoon.

The book beside the teacup was a ledger. Of course it was. Jude had begun to suspect that the whole world ran on ledgers — that behind every harvest and coronation and falling leaf there was some tired clerk entering figures in a book no one would ever read. This one was bound in green leather gone soft with handling, its corners worn round as river stones. The embossing on the cover had faded to a whisper: *Vell Annex — Receipts & Renewals.*

Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself with detached interest. Since Corvain had died on the floor of his own study with blood spreading under his desk, her hands had stopped shaking entirely, as if all the trembling in her body had been spent at once and none remained for ordinary fear.

She opened the ledger on the counter, in the tea-ring, because it seemed to belong there.

* * *

The early pages were mundane, and Jude read them the way she'd been trained to read everything: fast, skeptical, hungry for discrepancy. Grain tithes. Candle wax. A recurring entry for "moonwater, 3 vials, spring collection." Fees paid by farmers for rain in dry years — modest sums, honest-looking, exactly what a market of impossible things would look like if you wanted it to pass for a spice shop.

Then, forty pages in, the handwriting changed.

Not the hand itself — the same careful, upright script, unmistakably Ilsen's — but the pressure of it. The pen pressing harder. As though the woman writing had begun gripping the pen the way you grip a railing.

*Received: one (1) memory, first snowfall, donor willing. Entered against account of the Harrow family, per standing obligation.*

*Received: three (3) years, donor willing. Donor aged nine. Entered against account of the city of Vell, flood-year adjustment.*

*Received: one (1) name, donor willing. Donor requests name not be recorded. Name recorded anyway. Obligations are obligations.*

Jude read that last line three times. *Name recorded anyway.* There was something almost fierce in it, a clerk's stubborn mercy — recording what someone had lost so that the loss, at least, was witnessed. She thought of the sealed page in Corvain's ledger, the one marked *seal-bearer*, the one with her own name written in her master's hand twenty years before she'd known what the word meant.

Someone had always been writing these things down. That was the horror and the comfort of it. Nothing vanished unrecorded. Everything was counted.

"What is it?" Odile had drifted near, drawn perhaps by the change in Jude's breathing. She didn't touch the book. Spire-trained, Jude thought. You don't handle another realm's paperwork without invitation.

"The payments." Jude turned pages carefully, faster now, following the thread. "In Ashport, the impossible trades went into the ledger and out again, balanced, closed. Here—" She stopped.

Here the entries changed character entirely. The last third of the book was full not of receipts but of *renewals*. The word appeared every few pages, always capitalized, always alone on its line like a heading waiting for text that never came:

*Renewal.*

And beneath each heading, a date. Jude ran her finger down them, and her clerk's mind did what it always did with columns of numbers — it found the pattern, the rhythm, the hidden arithmetic underneath.

Every twenty-five years. Almost to the day. Twenty-five years between each *Renewal*, stretching back through the ledger's whole depth, back past the founding of Vell's current charter, back past anything Jude had a category for.

The last dated renewal was thirty-one years ago.

Six years overdue.

The shop seemed to tilt very slightly, or perhaps that was Jude. She set her hand on the counter. The teacup's brown moon stared up at her.

"Odile," she said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her, "when did the covenant last get renewed?"

Odile went very still. It was remarkable, the way stillness moved through her — not freezing but focusing, like water going glass-smooth before it drops over a ledge. "The last renewal I know of was thirty-one years ago. I was a child. The whole Spire rang for a day and a night." Her eyes narrowed. "Why?"

"Because your Warden's office told you the signal was failing. Because the borderlands are rotting. Because the stairs are breaking." Jude turned the ledger around so Odile could see the column of dates. "Because it's supposed to happen every twenty-five years, and it hasn't happened in thirty-one. Your masters didn't send you to investigate a malfunction. They sent you after a missed appointment."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere out on Tallowmaker's Row, a cart rattled past, and the shutters trembled in their frames, letting bars of light slide across the counter like slow searchlights.

"That can't be right," Odile said finally, but she said it the way people say things they already believe. "Renewals aren't missed. They're the entire point of the covenant. The Spire feeds your lands its impossible things — the rains, the harvests, the luck, the healing. In exchange—"

"In exchange, a seal-bearer renews the bargain at the Spire's heart, once a generation." Jude had pulled another book from under the counter without quite deciding to — a slim volume, private-sized, the kind of book a person keeps for thoughts too dangerous for the official record. Its clasp was broken. "It's here. Ilsen wrote it out. Maybe for a student. Maybe for herself, to keep it straight."

She read aloud, quietly, because some sentences deserve the dignity of a voice:

*"The covenant is not a wall but a bridge, and bridges must be walked to remain bridges. Once in every generation, the seal-bearer must go up to the Spire and renew the terms willingly — willingly, for a forced renewal is no renewal at all, only theft wearing ceremony's clothes. The guild exists to find the bearer, teach the bearer, and carry the bearer to the threshold. All our trades, all our small miracles, are merely the tolls collected to keep the road open for that single journey."*

Jude lowered the book. Her throat was tight.

"All of it," she said. "Every payment of memory and years and names, every impossible trade the guild ever brokered — it was all housekeeping. Maintenance. Keeping the bridge walkable for one person to cross it." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I used to think we were hoarding miracles. We were sweeping a path."

Odile had taken the ledger dates from her and was staring at them as though she could make the arithmetic lie by glaring at it. "Thirty-one years," she said. "And the Spire's been running on reserve. On dregs. That's why the signal faded slowly instead of dying all at once. That's why the borderlands are decaying outward from the stair instead of collapsing everywhere at once." She looked up, and Jude saw something in her face she hadn't seen before — not the warden's controlled calm, but a rawer thing underneath, guilt-shaped. "They must have known. My superiors must have known the interval. Why send me now? Why wait six years?"

"Maybe they didn't know who the bearer was." Jude's mouth was dry. "Maybe that's the real reason the guild has been dying. Maybe—" She stopped, because the next thought arrived all at once, fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do.

*Maybe the guild didn't lose track of the seal-bearer. Maybe it hid the seal-bearer. Deliberately. For twenty years. In plain sight, in a guildhall, teaching her bookkeeping.*

Corvain's face rose in her memory — his tired eyes, his ink-stained fingers, the way he'd dismissed her questions about the strange entries with that gentle deflection she'd mistaken for indifference. *Some accounts balance themselves, child. Ours is to keep the books, not ask where the money sleeps.* He had known. He had known and he had hidden her, and the hiding had cost the world six years of covenant decay, and the decay had cost the borderlands their stairs and the lowlands their luck, and eventually it had cost Corvain his life on the floor of the study he'd never left, buying her eleven minutes' head start with his blood.

*Destiny arrives as fine print,* Ilsen had apparently believed, if the marginalia in this little book was any indication. Jude flipped to the back pages, where the handwriting grew shakier, more recent, and found the fine print waiting for her.

* * *

The final entries were not accounting at all. They were letters, unsent, addressed to no one — or to everyone, or to whoever found the book. Ilsen's hand deteriorated down the pages like a candle burning down.

*They have stopped believing me, the council. Thirty years ago I warned them the renewal was due and they asked me to prove the covenant existed at all. Prove it! As if the rain proves nothing, as if the wheat proves nothing. Cities forget their debts the moment the creditors grow quiet.*

*I have petitioned the other annexes. Ashport does not answer. Merrowgate burned its records in the Purist riots and calls it reform. We are the last keepers and we are keeping nothing.*

*I know who she is. Corvain confirmed it, at last, at terrible cost to his peace. The Joubert girl. He begged me to let her stay hidden — argued that a bearer raised in ignorance is a bearer unburdened, that the covenant will forgive one late renewal rather than feed a child to destiny. I have not decided if I agree. I have decided that deciding is not mine to make alone, and there is no one left to decide with.*

*If the renewal passes thirty years, the borderlands will begin to fail visibly. If it passes thirty-five, the failure reaches the waking lands. Droughts that follow no weather. Luck turning. Gifts curdling in their bearers. I am old. My legs are bad. But if no one carries the terms to the Spire, then I must, and God forgive me, I am afraid of the price.*

Jude stopped.

Read it again.

*I am afraid of the price.*

Below that, the last entry, undated, the ink blotched as though water — or something else — had fallen on the page:

*There is a term in the original covenant that the guild has never recorded in any working ledger. Corvain knew. I know. We kept it from the bearers out of kindness, and kindness, I begin to understand, is how the guild died — we loved our charges too much to tell them what inheritance means. If you are reading this, bearer, I am sorry. I am sorry we wrote you into a contract before you could write yourself. The terms are in the strongbox under the floor. What renewal costs is not what we told your mothers it costs. Come find me before you open it. Please. Come find me first.*

The shop was silent. The barred light had crawled halfway across the floor. Somewhere outside, distantly, a bell began to ring — not alarm, just the hour, but it went through Jude like a blade through wet paper.

Under the floor. A strongbox. Terms that weren't what they'd told anyone.

And Ilsen — *come find me first* — eleven days gone.

"Jude." Odile's voice, low. She'd moved to the window slats and was looking out at the street with an expression Jude had learned to dread. "The chestnut seller is gone. And there are four men walking up the row in matching coats, and matching coats in this city right now means one thing."

Witch-finders. Tax-assessor gray, official satchels, the unhurried confidence of men backed by a king's warrant.

Jude looked down at the little book in her hands, at Ilsen's blotched plea, and felt the full weight of it settle onto her shoulders — not like a burden dropped at once but like sand poured grain by grain, patient, total. Every generation, a bearer walks the bridge. This generation's crossing was six years late. The borderlands were crumbling. The waking lands would follow within the year. And the one person who knew the true price of renewal had been taken — or fled, or hidden — leaving behind a strongbox and an apology.

*We wrote you into a contract before you could write yourself.*

"No," Jude heard herself say.

Odile glanced back. "No what?"

"No, we're not sneaking out the back and running." Jude was already moving, scanning the floorboards, her clerk's eye cataloguing: the counter, the workbench, the cellar hatch Ilsen had checked earlier. Old shops like this had old floors, and old floors had old secrets. "Ilsen said come find her first. She's gone, so I can't. Which means the next best thing is to know exactly what she was afraid to tell me — because I promise you, Odile, whatever's in that strongbox, Aldric's men want it too, and I am done learning the terms of my own life secondhand."

Odile stared at her for a heartbeat. Then, slowly, she smiled — a small, sharp, genuine smile, the first Jude had ever seen from her that reached her eyes. "There she is," the warden said softly. "The negotiator."

"The bookkeeper," Jude corrected, dropping to her knees. "Bookkeepers find what everyone else hides."

The floorboard under the workbench gave a hollow knock when she pressed it — and beneath the knocking, beneath the bell, beneath the footsteps now stopping outside the shop's boarded door, Jude heard it again: the hum. Fainter than ever. A bell rung so long ago it was nearly silence.

But not yet silence.

The door handle began, very quietly, to turn.
