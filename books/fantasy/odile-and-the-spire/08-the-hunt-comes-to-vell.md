## 8. The Hunt Comes to Vell

Odile had learned to read a city the way sailors read weather, and Vell was reading storm.

She saw it in the market stalls that closed by noon instead of dusk, in the way the fishwives no longer sang, in the guards on the walls who watched the roads south instead of the streets around them. Fear had a smell — wet wool and lamp oil and the sourness of men who had decided, somewhere in the last three days, exactly how much of their neighbors' safety they were willing to sell.

She stood at a window of the safehouse above the dyer's shop, watching the sun go down over the refugee quarter, and counted the fires. Not cooking fires. Signal fires. Aldric's witch-finders had a habit of lighting them at the edges of a city they meant to take, the way a surgeon marked a limb before cutting.

"You're doing the thing again," Jude said behind her. "The counting thing."

"Old habit."

"It's a grim habit."

"It's a useful one." Odile didn't turn. "There are nine of them. There were six yesterday."

Jude came to stand beside her, and Odile was aware of her the way she was always aware of her now — a warmth at the edge of her shoulder, the particular weight of her presence that Odile had stopped pretending not to notice somewhere on the salt road, in the dark, in the space between one watch and the next. Jude's hair was still dyed the flat brown of the lowlands, badly, a smear of walnut stain behind one ear. Her eyes hadn't changed. They were still ledger-keeper's eyes, cataloguing everything, and they were fixed on the fires.

"Nine fires," Jude said. "What does that mean, in your professional opinion?"

"It means the walls won't hold a week."

"The council says—"

"The council says a great many things." Odile heard the bitterness in her own voice and didn't bother to sand it down. "The council has been debating whether to hand over its gifted for eleven days. The witch-finders outside those walls are not debating anything."

Jude was quiet for a moment. Below them, in the street, a family was loading a cart by lamplight — a woman, two children, an old man folding a blanket with the terrible patience of someone who had done this before, in some other year, some other war. The cart was too small. It was always too small.

"The gate," Jude said suddenly.

"What?"

"The south gate. If they come, they'll come from the south — the roads north are flooded, we came that way. And the south gate is where the refugees would go." Jude's finger traced the line in the air, the geometry of escape, the arithmetic of a crowd. "If the gate closes, everyone in the low quarter is trapped inside with the witch-finders."

Odile looked at her. Twenty years old, a bookkeeper three months ago, and she had just read the battle in the placement of cook-fires.

"You'd have made a fair warden," Odile said.

"I'd have made a fair *anything*," Jude said, "if anyone had let me choose."

* * *

They came on the fourth night, and they came the way Odile had known they would — not with siege engines, not with horns, but with keys.

She was awake because she was always awake now, sitting on the stairs with a whetstone and a knife that had seen better decades, when the first bell began to ring. Not the alarm bell. The *gate* bell — the one rung to open the south gate for a friendly arrival. She was moving before the second toll.

The streets were chaos already, though most of Vell didn't know it yet. The witch-finders had come dressed as pilgrims, as grain merchants, as deserters from Aldric's own army begging asylum — and the gate garrison, tired and underpaid and half of them secretly hoping the war would end however it had to end, had opened the doors to them. Odile reached the gate square as the portcullis was still groaning upward, and in the torchlight she saw them come through in good order, forty of them, fifty, gray-cloaked, unhurried, carrying their instruments the way priests carry relics.

She knew those instruments. She had seen their like before, in another life, on the other side of the border. Iron frames and silver wire and glass vials that caught the light wrong. Things that measured a person the way a butcher measures a carcass.

And she saw the garrison captain shaking the hand of the witch-finder commander. Saw the purse change hands, discreetly, beneath the captain's cloak. Saw the commander's face — young, clean, terribly calm — turn toward the low quarter where ten thousand refugees slept.

Odile had spent six years being no one. She had worn a dozen names, a dozen faces, had made herself so small and so gray that even the Spire's far-seeing eyes had lost her. Hiding had kept her alive. Hiding had also, once, cost a city four hundred souls, and she had told herself every night since that the two facts were not the same fact.

Standing in the gate square of Vell, watching the portcullis rise on its chains, she understood at last that they were exactly the same fact.

The commander raised his hand, and the gray-cloaked line began to advance into the city.

"No," Odile said.

It came out loud. It came out loud enough that the nearest witch-finder turned, and she saw him see her — a woman in a dyer's apron with a knife at her belt, nothing, nobody — and dismiss her, and she felt the old familiar shrinking start, the old instinct to fold herself into the shadows of the doorway and let it happen, let it all happen, because what could one person do, what had one person ever done—

The old man folding blankets. The cart that was too small. Jude's face on the salt road, firelit, saying *you could have just kept walking, why didn't you keep walking?* and Odile not having an answer she could bear to speak aloud.

"No," she said again, and this time she said it in the old tongue, the warden's tongue, the language of the Spire's threshold, and she let the word *mean* something.

Light came out of her.

She had kept it caged for six years, and it came out the way a river comes out when the dam breaks — not gracefully, not gently, a white-gold flood that lit the gate square like noon and threw every shadow screaming against the walls. The witch-finders staggered. Their instruments shrieked, all of them at once, glass vials cracking in their frames, silver wire glowing. The garrison captain fell off his horse.

Odile walked into the square, into her own light, and raised her hand, and the portcullis — half-risen, traitor-open — slammed down like the fist of a god. The chains snapped. The iron bit into the stone of the gateway and *held*, warded now, sealed now, locked against everything that had come through it and everything that meant to follow.

"Run," she said to the city, and her voice carried the way a warden's voice carries, the way it is made to carry, across rooftops and down alleys and into ten thousand sleeping rooms. "*Run.*"

* * *

They ran. That was the thing she would remember afterward, when she had time to remember anything — not the fighting, not the light, but the running. The low quarter emptying itself into the streets, families with their too-small bundles, the old man with the blankets shepherding children who were not his, the fishwives who had stopped singing now shouting door to door. The north postern, the flooded road, the boats. Odile stood in the gate square with her hand against her own warding and her gift pouring out of her like blood, and she held the gate.

The witch-finders recovered. Of course they did. They were not brave, but they were well-paid and well-drilled, and their commander had a voice like a whip. They came at her in a crescent, iron frames first, and Odile learned in the first thirty seconds what six years of hiding had cost her: her forms were rusty, her reserves were shallow, and the warding on the gate was drinking her strength with every heartbeat.

The first one reached her with a capture-frame raised, and she broke his wrist and put him down with the edge of her hand and felt, absurdly, Corvain's ghost at her shoulder saying *elbow in, girl, you fight like you're stirring soup* — no, that wasn't Corvain, that was a memory of a training yard on the Spire's threshold, a lifetime ago, an instructor whose name she had traded away in one of her first impossible payments and could no longer recall.

The second and third came together. She took a cut along her forearm turning a capture-frame aside — the silver wire kissed her skin and *burned*, and she felt her gift stutter, felt the warding on the gate flicker like a candle in wind — and then someone screamed a word from the rooftops and a flowerpot the size of a barrel took the third witch-finder in the head.

Jude. On the roof of the dyer's shop, walnut-dyed hair wild, throwing pottery with the deadly accuracy of a woman who had spent her life calculating trajectories of a different kind.

"Go *inside*—" Odile began.

"Shut up and hold the gate!" Jude screamed back, and hurled a dye-jar that burst across two witch-finders in a sheet of violet, blinding them, and Odile — in the middle of a battle, in the middle of her own ruin — had to bite down on a laugh that was dangerously close to something else.

But the commander was not stupid. She saw him see it, the way she had seen things on the Spire's walls a hundred times: the flicker in the warding, the cost written on the warden's face. He stopped advancing. He drew his men back into a ring, out of reach, and he smiled at her across the square with terrible patience.

"Warden," he called. "A warden of the Spire, in Vell. Oh, the king will want to hear of this. The *Spire* has come down to play among mortals."

"The Spire," Odile said, and her voice was steady, and her arm was not, "sends its regards."

"Your gate will fall when you do." He gestured, and two of his men unslung something from a cart — a longer frame, a heavier wire, an instrument she recognized and had hoped never to see again on this side of the border. A harvester's frame, scaled up. Not for capturing. For *draining*. "And then we will go into your lovely city, and we will find every gifted soul in it, starting with the young woman on the roof, and we will be very, very thorough."

The frame began to sing. Odile felt it like a hook behind her ribs, dragging at her gift, dragging at the gate-warding, and she set her feet and poured more of herself into the seal and felt her vision go white at the edges.

*Six years,* she thought. *Six years of being no one, to die as myself, holding a door.*

She found, to her surprise, that she didn't regret it. That was the strangest thing. That of all the prices she had ever paid, this one — being *seen*, being known, being exactly what she was in the open light of her own making — was the first one that felt like it had bought something worth the cost.

The harvester's frame sang louder. The gate-warding cracked, a sound like ice on a pond.

And then the night went violet.

Not the dye. Light — a column of it, rising from the heart of the low quarter, violet shot through with gold, and Odile knew that light, had been raised under it, had sworn her oaths beneath it. Spire-light. Covenant-light. It poured up into the sky over Vell like a second dawn, and every witch-finder in the square turned to stare, and the harvester's frame shrieked and cracked down its length as something vast and old and *awake* rolled over the city like a wave.

Odile's knees hit the cobbles. Her gift was spent, her arm was bleeding, her gate-warding was holding by a thread — and over the rooftops, wrapped in light that did not belong to her, stood Jude Joubert, youngest ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, with her hand raised and her eyes wide with an astonishment that Odile recognized, because she had worn it herself the first time her gift had answered her.

*Seal-bearer,* Odile thought. *Oh, Jude. They can see you now. The whole city can see you now. And so can everything hunting you.*

The witch-finder commander was staring too. And then he did the thing Odile would replay in her mind for weeks afterward, the thing that turned her blood to ice: he smiled. He turned from the gate — from her, from the broken frame, from the fight he'd been losing — and looked up at the light like a man reading a map, and he said, softly, to his men:

"Forget the gate. We have what we came for."

The light guttered. Jude swayed on the rooftop, caught herself on the chimney, and Odile was already running — through the square, up the stairs, across the dyer's roof with her burned arm screaming — to catch her before the last of the covenant's borrowed fire went out of her and left her, in the dark, visible to everything.
