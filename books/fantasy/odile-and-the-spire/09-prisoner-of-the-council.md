## 9. Prisoner of the Council

They came for her at dawn, and they came with bread.

Jude knew what the bread meant before the woman carrying it said a word. She had grown up in a guild house; she had watched Corvain send loaves and salt and sweet wine to families who had suffered losses the guild preferred unspoken. Bread was an apology that cost nothing. Bread was how institutions said *we are sorry for what we are about to do to you* without dirtying their mouths.

"Seal-bearer," the woman said. She was gray-haired, gray-cloaked, one of the Council's wardens, and she stood in the doorway of the dyer's loft with her hands folded like a woman attending a funeral she had personally arranged. Behind her, in the stairwell, four more waited with pikes. Not witch-finders' iron — Vell's own steel, city-forged. "The Council requests your presence."

"Requests," Jude repeated.

The woman's gaze flicked past her, to where Odile lay propped against the wall, arm bound in scorched linen, awake and watching with the stillness of a drawn blade. "The other matter is already decided. The Spire-born will be held separately."

"She has a name," Odile said.

"And it will be recorded," the warden said, in the tone of someone recording names all day long, none of which would save anyone.

Jude looked down at her hands. They were steady. That surprised her; everything else felt like it was shaking apart beneath her skin — the burned square, the gate, the sound the witch-finders' machines made when they drank a person hollow. Last night she had stood on a rooftop while Odile tore open her own gift like a purse spilled into wind, just so strangers could run. And this was the thanks. Bread and pikes and a polite word like *requests*.

She thought about arguing. She thought about running. She was, she realized, standing three steps from a window, and she had spent twenty years learning every ledger trick there was, including the oldest one: *what looks like a door is often a trap, and what looks like a trap is sometimes the only honest exit.*

But below the window, the street was full. Refugees from the burned quarter camped under awnings. Children. Old men. People Odile had bled for.

If she ran, the Council would hunt her through streets full of exactly those people, and Aldric's finders were still inside the walls somewhere, patient as rust.

"No chains?" Jude asked.

"You are a guest of the Council," the warden said.

"I've read guest registers," Jude said. "Guests get to leave."

The warden didn't flinch. That, more than anything, told Jude the truth: the woman was ashamed. Ashamed people never flinch; they go very still, like a debtor when the auditor walks in.

Jude picked up the loaf, because refusing it would be cruelty to a woman who needed to believe in the fiction, and walked out into the gray morning between the pikes.

Behind her, she heard Odile rise, and the scrape of two pike-butts shifting to bar a doorway, and Odile's voice, low and even: "Touch me and we'll both regret it." Then, softer, almost lost under the stairs: "Jude. Don't sign anything."

It was such a *ledger-keeper's* thing to say that Jude laughed — one short, cracked note — and then the stairwell swallowed her.

* * *

They kept her in the top of the Bellfounders' Tower, which was either an honor or a joke or both. The room had once belonged to the master of bells; the great shaft dropped away through the floor behind a grate, down into darkness where the bells hung like sleeping whales. Someone had brought a chair, a table, a candle, a ewer of watered wine. Someone had barred the door from the outside.

Through the narrow windows she could see all of Vell: the terraced roofs, the canals gone green with algae because no one dared waste water on beauty anymore, the refugee camps spreading along the walls like lichen, and beyond them, far off to the south, a thin smudge on the horizon that might have been weather and might have been Aldric's army, burning what it did not eat.

A guard changed every hour. They were young. Most of them wouldn't meet her eyes. One did — a boy of perhaps seventeen with a burn scar on his knuckles, who looked at her the way people look at a shrine they've been told is now forbidden.

"You're really her," he said, on his third watch, when the corridor was empty. "The seal-bearer. The one the covenant needs."

"I'm really her," Jude said.

"My sister was taken in Ashport," the boy said. "In the first sweep. Before anyone knew to hide." He shifted his pike. "If you're what stops him—"

"I don't know what I am," Jude said. It was the most honest sentence she'd spoken in days, and it landed on the boy like a blow. He went back to not meeting her eyes.

That was the shape of it, she thought, alone in the bell-master's room with its borrowed furniture. Everyone wanted her to be something. The guild had wanted her to be nothing — a hidden page, a sealed entry, twenty years of ink spent keeping her blank. Odile wanted her to be the covenant's last chance. The Council wanted her to be currency. The boy with the burned knuckles wanted her to be a miracle with a face.

And she had wanted, only wanted, to balance the books.

Her whole life she had believed the ledgers were the truest thing in the world. Numbers didn't flatter. A column balanced or it didn't. But she understood now that a ledger is only ever a story told about value — and whoever holds the pen decides what a person is worth. Corvain had held the pen over her for twenty years. The Council meant to hold it now, and write her down as *payment rendered*, and close the account of Vell's freedom with her name in the margin.

She tried the door twice. Barred. She examined the grate over the bell-shaft; iron, old, but set deep. She measured the window with her eyes and then with a ribbon pulled from her cuff, and did not like the answer.

So instead she did the thing she was actually good at.

There was paper on the table — the Council had left her paper, perhaps out of courtesy, perhaps so she could draft whatever statement they wished her to make. She turned a sheet over, took the stub of charcoal from the candle ledge, and began to work.

Not a plea. Not a statement. An accounting.

*Aldric's forces: forty thousand by Ilsen's estimates, plus the harvested. The harvested fade — Odile says the stolen gift burns out in weeks, so his army has a hunger with a clock on it. Which means he cannot besiege Vell. Besieging wastes time he doesn't have. He will offer terms. He always offers terms first; it's cheaper.*

And if Vell offered terms back — if Vell put her, the seal-bearer, on the scale —

She stopped. Her hand had gone cold around the charcoal.

Because the arithmetic worked. That was the horror of it. It *worked*. One life, traded, bought a city months — maybe years. Every guild instinct she owned leaned toward it the way a compass leans north. Corvain would have done it. Corvain had spent his whole life making exactly these trades, small miracles purchased with small payments, and he had died for the sin of finally telling her the price list had her name on it.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, and the word fell down the bell-shaft and came back to her twice, small and strange.

She sat until the candle guttered, and then she did not sleep, and near morning she heard, distantly, the sound she had been dreading: horns at the south gate. Not attack-horns. Parley-horns.

Aldric had sent an envoy.

* * *

They came for her at midday, six guards this time and Magister Dorn himself — the First Speaker of Vell's Council, a broad, careful man with the face of someone who had spent thirty years saying no to things that needed saying yes to. He unlocked the door with his own key and stood aside to let her see the room beyond: a council chamber hastily cleared, maps weighted with cups, and along one wall, the envoy.

She knew him instantly from Odile's descriptions, though Odile had never described his face — only his function. Lord Casserly, King Aldric's Voice. He was elegant and unhurried, dressed in traveling grays, and he smiled at Jude the way a moneylender smiles at collateral.

"The famous bookkeeper," he said. "Or should I say the famous *seal*. Names are so unstable these days."

"Magistra Ilsen called me the last honest entry," Jude said. "I prefer hers."

Something flickered behind the smile — the briefest recalculation. So he knew Ilsen was dead, or suspected it, and had expected a frightened girl and found a counterparty. Good. Frightened girls got consumed; counterparties got contracts.

Dorn cleared his throat. "The Council has been in session since dawn. The envoy brings terms."

"Let me guess them for you," Jude said, and watched Dorn's eyebrows climb. "You give me up. In exchange, Aldric withdraws his army to the Salt Road, lifts the harvest edicts in Vell's territory for — a year? Two? — and swears protection of the free cities against the Spire's 'excesses.' Am I warm?"

Casserly's smile widened by a degree. "You have read our correspondence."

"I've read *people*," Jude said. "Correspondence lies. Budgets don't." She turned to Dorn, and here it was, the moment she had rehearsed against the bell-shaft's echo all night. "First Speaker, before you weigh my life against a treaty, ask His Excellency one question. Ask him what happens to the gifted of Vell after year three."

Dorn frowned. "The terms include—"

"The terms include whatever keeps this city quiet until his machines finish eating everyone they've caught," Jude said. "Ask him why his army moves like it's starving. Ask him what happens to a man who drinks stolen magic when the magic runs out. Ask him—" and she let her voice drop, let it carry the weight of everything Odile had told her on the flooded roads, everything Ilsen's ledgers implied, everything she herself had pieced together in the dark, "—ask him what King Aldric intends to do with a key to the Spire."

The silence that followed had texture. She heard it in the creak of a guard's leather, the tick of cooling candles, Casserly's breathing going very slightly less unhurried.

"That," Casserly said pleasantly, "is sedition."

"That's arithmetic," Jude said. "I'm the Guild of Impossible Things' youngest ledger-keeper. It's the only language I have left."

Dorn was looking at her differently now — not as currency, not yet as an ally, but as a variable he had failed to model. "The Council will consider," he said stiffly, "all testimony."

"The Council will consider," Casserly echoed, "or the king will consider alternatives. He is a reasonable man, First Speaker, right up until the moment he isn't. I'll want an answer by tomorrow's dusk." He bowed to Jude, shallow and mocking. "Enjoy the tower, Mistress Joubert. Do think kindly of us in your figures."

They took her back up. The door barred. The candle relit. Through the window, far below, she saw Casserly ride out through the south gate with his escort, unhurried as tide.

And she saw something else. A figure on the roofline across the canal, half-hidden behind a chimney pot, moving with a particular economy — favoring the left arm.

Odile.

Free, despite the pikes, despite the separate holding, despite everything — or perhaps because some jailer had been paid, or some lock had been old, or because Odile was simply what she was. Watching the tower. Watching for her.

Their eyes met across sixty yards of gray air. Odile raised her good hand — not a wave. Three fingers, then a flat palm. Jude's heart slammed. She knew that signal. Odile had taught it to her on the roads: *three days, then night.* Whatever Odile was planning, she would come for her at night, three nights hence.

Jude pressed her hand flat to the glass in answer, and Odile was gone, melted into the roofscape like water into cloth.

Three nights. Jude sat back down at the table and looked at her charcoal notes, at the columns of Aldric's hunger, and understood with terrible clarity what she had just done in the council chamber. She had raised the price of herself. She had shown them both what she was worth to the king and what the king feared she knew — and frightened councils with valuable prisoners do not wait politely for rescues.

They move up the schedule.

* * *

They came at dusk, not dawn, and this time it was Dorn alone, with no bread.

"The vote was close," he said, without preamble. He stood in the doorway with the key still in the lock, as if unsure whether to use it. "Nine to seven. You understand that seven voted for you. Seven men and women staked their cities on a girl they met yesterday, because she talked to them about budgets."

"I understand," Jude said quietly.

"Do you?" He stepped inside, and shut the door behind him — shutting out his own guard, which was either trust or desperation. "Then understand this too. Casserly expects delivery at dusk tomorrow. If we refuse, he rides home and the army comes, and my seven votes become seven resignations and a mob. If we accept—" His jaw worked. "I have read the old chronicles, Mistress Joubert. My grandmother saw the last breach. She said the sky over the lowlands wept black rain for a season. I do not want to sell you. I also do not want to bury a city."

"Then don't do either," Jude said. "Postpone."

"There is no postponing a king."

"There is *renegotiating* one," she said. "That's all any bargain ever is. You taught yourselves that when you joined the free cities — you bargained with Ashport, with the river lords, with the guild itself. Aldric wants me delivered whole and willing, because a seal-bearer taken by force is worth nothing — Odile told me that, and your own chronicles will confirm it if you read the margins. So he needs Vell's cooperation. Which means Vell has leverage. Real leverage, tonight, while his envoy is still sipping your wine."

Dorn stared at her. "What leverage?"

"Make him bid," Jude said. "Openly. Make him raise the terms in front of witnesses — make him put in writing protections for every gifted soul in your territory, written guarantees, enforced ones, with penalties he agrees to in advance. Make him negotiate for me the way one negotiates for anything precious. Because the moment he does, First Speaker, he confirms in front of your whole council that I'm worth more than a treaty — and every one of your nine votes starts wondering what else a living seal-bearer might buy that a dead one can't."

It was audacious and half-formed and built on assumptions stacked like cards, and it was also the best hand she had, and she laid it down anyway.

Dorn was silent a long moment. Then he said, "You sound like Ilsen."

"You knew her?"

"Everyone in this building knew her. She argued with us for forty years." Something moved through the old man's face — grief, worn smooth by use. "She used to say the Council's tragedy was that it could only count to nine." He turned the key over in his fingers. "I'll take your proposal to the floor tonight. If it fails—"

"If it fails," Jude said, "you'll have to choose between your city and your conscience, and I'm sorry, because that choice shouldn't belong to you."

He looked at her then, really looked, and nodded once, and left, and the bar fell into place behind him like a period at the end of a sentence.

Jude stood at the window as full dark came down. Somewhere out in the roofscape, a woman with a burned arm was counting hours. Below, the council lamps burned late. Tomorrow dusk, or maybe sooner, maybe the moment the vote broke badly — they would come with rope and a wagon, or Casserly's men would come with chains, and everything depended on doors and locks and the difference between three nights and two.

She tested the window latch again. Still fastened. Still iron-deep.

Then, faint, from the bell-shaft behind the grate — a sound she had taken for the settling of old metal, all night, all day. A soft, rhythmic scrape. Rope on stone.

She froze. Listened.

The scrape came again, closer now, patient as climbing.

And a voice drifted up out of the dark throat of the tower, hoarse and dry and unmistakably amused:

"Don't sign anything. I *did* warn you."
