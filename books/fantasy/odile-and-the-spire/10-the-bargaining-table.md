## 10. The Bargaining Table

They dressed her for the negotiation the way one dresses a corpse — carefully, with respect, and with no intention of asking her opinion.

The council's seamstress arrived at dawn with a gown of Vell grey, high-collared and buttoned to the throat, and two guards who stood in the doorway of Jude's cell with the particular blankness of men who had been told not to speak to the prisoner. The gown fit well enough. That was the insult of it. Someone had measured her while she slept.

"Arms out," the seamstress said, not unkindly. She was an older woman with ink-stained fingers, the kind of hands Jude had seen a thousand times in the guildhall, and it made her chest ache in a way she hadn't prepared for.

"You're dressing me to be sold," Jude said.

"I'm dressing you to be presented." The woman pinned a fold at the shoulder, her mouth a thin line. "In this city those stopped being different things about six months ago."

Jude let her work. There was no point fighting here, in a room with one window and two bored guards and a door that locked from the outside with the soft, apologetic click of a city that still thought of itself as decent. She had spent the night doing what she did best, which was reading. Not books — there were none — but the room itself. The window faced the council spire's inner court, and through its frost-feathered glass she had watched the watch change twice, counted the bells, noted which guard favored his left knee and which one kept glancing up at the window as if he too had once been someone who looked at skies instead of walls.

She had also spent the night being honest with herself, which was harder.

*You are going to stand in front of eleven frightened old people,* she told herself, *and they are going to decide your life like a line item.* And the terrible, clarifying truth beneath that truth was: they weren't wrong to try. They were wrong about the arithmetic. That was all. That was everything.

Corvain would have said something dry about ledgers just now. Something like, *Every bargain is a ledger, little wren; your only question is who holds the pen.* Her throat tightened. She made herself breathe past it.

"Done," the seamstress said. She stepped back, surveyed her work, and then — quickly, before the guards could see — pressed something small into Jude's palm. A bent pin. Sewing steel, three inches, dull. "For the collar," the woman said loudly, "in case it sits wrong." And then she was gone, and the door clicked, and Jude stood alone in a grey dress holding a weapon the length of her finger and understanding, suddenly, that the city of Vell contained more people than the ones in power, and some of them had chosen sides without ever meeting her.

She hid the pin in her sleeve and waited to be summoned.

* * *

The council chamber of Vell occupied the top floor of the salt-tower, a round room walled in pale stone that had been quarried, so the guidebooks claimed, from the same cliffs as the Spire itself. Eleven seats ringed a central table shaped like a compass rose. Morning light came through windows on every side, so that whoever sat at the table was always lit from behind, always half-shadow, always slightly difficult to read.

Someone had designed it that way on purpose, centuries ago. Jude approved. It was good architecture for liars, and she intended to make full use of it.

They seated her at the table's southern point — the guest's place, or the accused's; the geometry allowed both — and Magistrate Corvel took the seat directly opposite hers. He was the council's speaker, a broad man gone soft at the edges, with the careful, exhausted face of someone who had spent half a year signing documents he hated. To his right sat Magistra Dune, sharp-faced, silver-braided, whom Jude recognized from the wanted-notices as the council's chief voice for accommodation. To his left, a younger man, Halloway, whose eyes kept moving to the windows as if he expected Aldric's witch-finders to come up through the clouds.

Eleven faces. Nine of them afraid. Two of them ambitious. Jude catalogued them the way she'd once catalogued entries: by risk.

"Jude Joubert," Corvel began, folding his hands. "Thank you for agreeing to meet with us."

"I wasn't given a choice," Jude said. "But yes."

A flicker of discomfort moved around the table. Good. Let them feel it. Let them sit inside the fact that they had locked a twenty-two-year-old bookkeeper in a tower and called it statecraft.

"You understand our position," Corvel continued, pressing on. "Aldric's army is nineteen days out. His witch-finders are already inside our walls — you saw what happened at the gate. He has offered terms. Terms, Miss Joubert. Not siege. Not fire. He wants one thing from us." His eyes came up and met hers, and there was genuine misery in them, which somehow made it worse. "He wants the seal-bearer."

"And in exchange," Jude said, "he leaves Vell standing."

"He withdraws his finders. He guarantees the free cities' autonomy under his crown. He signs—" Dune slid a document down the table, wax-sealed, heavy with ribbon, "—a binding accord. Witnessed by three realms' worth of envoys."

Jude didn't touch it. "May I ask a question first?"

Corvel hesitated. "You may."

"How many of you have actually read Aldric's earlier accords?"

Silence. Somewhere below, a bell rang the third hour.

"The Accord of Maren's Ford," Jude said. "Two years ago, when he first took the throne. The free town of Harrow signed a similar guarantee — autonomy under his crown, withdrawal of troops, witnessed seals. I've read the transcript. It's in the Guild of Impossible Things' archive, file forty-one, cross-referenced under 'treaties, broken.'" She let that word sit. *Broken.* "Harrow surrendered its gifted. Fourteen people. Children among them. Within a year, Harrow's granaries were requisitioned 'for the war effort.' Its harbor dues tripled. Its militia was folded into the crown levies. Last spring Harrow stopped sending letters south, because last spring Harrow stopped existing as anything but a supply depot with a name."

"That is—" Halloway started.

"Aldric keeps his word exactly as written," Jude said over him, "which is why the writing matters more than anything else in the world. And I have read his writing. All of it. Reading is my gift, magistrates, if you want to call it that. I have spent four years in the deepest archive of the Guild of Impossible Things, and I am going to tell you what your accord will actually buy you, and then you can decide whether to sell me."

She reached into the grey dress and drew out the folded pages she had been carrying since the night of the gate — pages she had copied by candlelight from Odile's salvaged Spire records during the road north, in Odile's own hand, verified against fragments of guild ledger she knew better than her own heartbeat. She smoothed them flat on the compass rose.

"Tell me what you know about where magic comes from."

Dune's jaw tightened. "Fairy stories. The Spire. Old wives—"

"The Spire," Jude agreed. "Which is real, which is dying, and which is the reason your wheat comes up in poor soil and your plague-years end early and your children are occasionally born knowing things no one taught them. Every gifted person in this room's history — every hedge-witch, every storm-caller, every child who dreamed true — is a leak. A small, beautiful leak in a wall between two worlds. The covenant manages that wall. My family managed the covenant. And King Aldric has been harvesting the leaks."

She turned the page toward them. Diagrams. Odile's precise warden-hand, arrows flowing from marked figures toward a central column.

"This is what harvest does. A harvested gift doesn't vanish. It's taken *up*. Stored. Aldric isn't collecting weapons, magistrates. He's building a key. Enough stored gift, concentrated in one vessel — himself — and he won't need to negotiate with the Spire at all. He'll breach it. He'll walk through the wall your ancestors died to keep orderly, and he will hold both realms in one fist. Your accord doesn't save Vell. It delivers him the final component."

"You can't know that," Dune said. But her voice had changed register, the way voices do when they're arguing against something they've begun to believe.

"Magistrate, I balance accounts for a living. Look at the flow." Jude tapped the diagram. "His harvest quotas doubled last autumn. Doubled again after Midwinter. You don't double a quota unless the yield is falling short of a target — and no target makes sense except a threshold. Warden Odile of the Spire can confirm the mechanics; she held your gate open with her own gift two nights ago, which I'd have thought would earn her a seat at this table rather than a cell, but we'll come back to that." She saw Halloway flinch. Good. "And if you doubt me, doubt me intelligently: send riders to Harrow's ruins. Count the empty cradles. Then ask yourselves why a king who merely feared magic bothers to *store* it."

The light shifted through the western windows. No one spoke. Jude could hear the city below — carts, gulls, the ordinary machinery of a morning that believed it would continue.

Corvel rubbed his eyes. "Even granting all this. Even if the king intends what you say. What would you have us do? Refuse him? He has ten thousand men and we have walls and prayers."

"Now you're asking the right question," Jude said, "so let me answer it properly. Because I didn't come up here to refuse anything. I came up here to renegotiate."

She turned back to the first page and laid her finger on the compass rose's center, on the empty space where the table's points converged.

"Aldric wants the seal-bearer because the seal-bearer is the one thing that opens the Spire without force. Fine. Then the seal-bearer is worth more than he thinks — and worth more than *you* think, which is your real problem. If you trade me, you get nineteen days of safety and a slow death you've already seen the shape of in Harrow. But if you *don't* trade me — if you instead become the free city that protected the covenant's renewal — you get something Aldric can't offer and can't take. You get the Spire's gratitude. And the Spire's gratitude, magistrates, looks like this:" she ticked it off, ledger-fashion, her voice steadying with each item because numbers were home ground, "harvests that never reach your borders, because the covenant's wardens will know your name. First claim on the borderland trade routes when the crossings stabilize. Warning of every breach, every failing, every hungry winter the land tries to visit on you — before it arrives. The Guild of Impossible Things reborn with Vell as its seat. Do you understand? You are currently negotiating to sell me for peace. I am offering to make you the treasury of two worlds."

"That's a promise made on behalf of a realm you've never seen," Dune said coldly.

"Yes," Jude said. "It is. Welcome to my week."

A sound escaped Halloway — almost a laugh, strangled at birth. Even Corvel's mouth twitched before discipline reclaimed it. Jude felt something loosen in her chest. Not confidence, exactly. Something older and more useful: the feeling of a ledger balancing, of columns finally agreeing with each other.

"Here are my terms," she said. "One: I walk out of this tower free, today, with Warden Odile beside her, her wounds treated by your physicians and her pardon entered in your civic record. Two: Vell refuses Aldric's accord — publicly, and soon enough that he learns defiance is expensive before he learns it's safe. Three: you give me your fastest riders and your sealed authority, because I have a covenant to reforge and a deadline that has nothing to do with armies. In exchange, Vell's name goes into the new covenant's first page. Written by me. Witnessed by the Spire itself. Not a payment. A partnership." She paused, and then, because Corvain would have wanted precision: "I'll even draft the accord for you tonight. I'm very good at accords. It's the one inheritance I got that didn't cost blood."

Dune leaned forward. "And if we simply take the third option — the one you haven't said aloud. Hand you to Aldric anyway, sign anyway, and hope your fairy story is wrong."

"Then I'd ask you one question," Jude said quietly. "When the wall comes down and both realms learn what Aldric becomes — do you want to be the city that fed him, or the city that stood? Because I promise you, Magistrate, everyone gets to choose a side in this ledger eventually. The only variable is whether you choose it while you still have something to trade."

The silence that followed was long and total. Jude sat in it without moving, hands flat on the table, heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door, and understood that she had never in her life been this afraid or this awake.

It was Corvel who broke it. He rose, walked to the eastern window, and stood looking out at the smoke of the refugee camps beyond the walls. When he turned back, he looked ten years older and, for the first time, like a magistrate rather than a hostage.

"Magistra Dune. Magistrate Halloway. The rest of you." His voice was hoarse. "I came into this chamber intending to sell this girl. I want that minuted. I want it remembered that we intended it, so we never mistake ourselves for the innocent party." He looked at Jude, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with bargains — the look of one tired person recognizing another. "Miss Joubert. You'll have your riders. You'll have your warden. And you'll have Vell — such as she is, such as she has left."

"It won't be enough," Dune said flatly. "Riders and defiance. He'll still come."

"He'll come," Jude agreed. She gathered her pages, and her fingers found the bent pin in her sleeve, and she thought of a seamstress with ink-stained hands choosing a side in a locked room, and of Odile bleeding in a cell below her, and of Corvain dying in a doorway so that a ledger could keep moving north. "So we'd best make sure that when he does, he finds the door already answered."

* * *

They released Odile at dusk.

Jude was waiting in the tower yard when they brought her out — walking stiffly, left arm bound to her chest, a physician's linen wrapped high around her ribs, but upright, and scowling at the escorting guards with an expression of such offended dignity that Jude had to press her lips together to keep from laughing, or crying, or both.

"They gave me broth," Odile said, arriving. "As an apology. For shooting me."

"Did you drink it?"

"I made them taste it first." Her eyes swept Jude — the grey dress, the unbound hair, the absence of chains — cataloguing damage the way she catalogued everything, fast and thorough. "You're alive. You're dressed like a magistrate's funeral. And you're smiling. Report."

"I renegotiated," Jude said. "Vell's in. Riders at dawn. Pardon entered in the civic record — yours too, apparently. You're officially a hero of the realm as of the fourth bell." She paused. "There's a ceremony planned. There's a sash."

"There is not a sash."

"There is absolutely a sash. Green and white. The clerk showed me the pattern."

Odile closed her eyes briefly, as if petitioning whatever powers governed warden dignity, none of whom, evidently, answered. "We ride north at dawn," she said. "Past the salt flats, through the pass at Cael's Teeth. Eleven days to the border if the weather holds and Serre doesn't find us first."

"She will find us first."

"Yes," Odile said simply. "Probably."

Above them, the evening sky over Vell was the color of cooling iron, and far to the north, past the walls and the camps and the waiting dark, Jude could just make out — or imagined she could — a thin vertical brightness on the horizon, like a needle of light sewn through the world's hem. The Spire. Waiting. Overdue.

She touched her sleeve, where the bent pin lay hidden, and thought: *every bargain is a ledger, and I hold the pen now.*

"Then we'd better be worth finding," she said, and turned toward the stables, and the dawn, and the long road into the teeth of winter.
