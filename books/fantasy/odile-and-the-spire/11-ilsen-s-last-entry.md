## 11. Ilsen's Last Entry

The council released her at dusk with the grudging ceremony of people who had decided to be ashamed of themselves and wanted credit for it.

"Free passage," said the First Speaker, not meeting Jude's eyes. "And our... apologies. The city was afraid. Fear makes us do poor arithmetic."

Jude took the words without thanks. She had spent three days in a locked room above the council hall, listening to boots on stairs and the muffled arguments of men deciding whether she was a person or a payment, and she had no appetite for absolution bought cheaply. What she wanted — what had kept her pacing the floor until her slippers wore thin at the heels — was written somewhere beneath Vell, in a shuttered shop on Candlemakers' Row, in the hand of a woman who had vanished the day the witch-finders breached the walls.

Magistra Ilsen.

*Find me when they let you go,* the note had said, slipped under her door in a hand so small it might have been written by a bird. *Not before. Before is death for both of us.*

They had let her go. The sun was going down over Vell's crooked rooftops, gilding the weathervanes and the refugee tents pitched in the temple squares, and Jude Joubert walked out of the council hall with nothing but the clothes she stood in, a stolen stub of pencil behind her ear, and the particular lightness of a ledger-keeper who has just been told the books are worse than anyone admitted.

Odile was waiting in the shadow of the archway across the square, hood up, one arm bound tight against her ribs where the gate-stone's backlash had caught her. She fell into step without a word. They walked together through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and fear, past doors chalked with the white mark of households that had surrendered their gifted, past a shrine where someone had left a candle burning for a daughter who was already on a wagon north.

"You shouldn't come," Jude said finally. "You can barely stand."

"I've stood through worse." Odile's voice was flat as slate. "Besides, if Ilsen sent for you specifically, then whatever she has to say, I'm the reason half of it exists. I'd rather hear my sins from the source."

"You don't know that—"

"I know exiles," Odile said. "We recognize each other."

Candlemakers' Row had emptied. The shops were shuttered, some boarded, one burned to its rafters and still smoking faintly, as if the fire were embarrassed to have finished. Ilsen's shop sat in the middle of the row like a closed eye: *Ilsen & Daughter, Curiosities Repaired*, the paint faded to the color of old milk. The door was locked with three separate mechanisms, and Odile picked two of them before Jude found the third hidden in the lintel — a hollow stone that turned when pressed in the pattern of the guild's oldest sigil, the circle-within-the-square that appeared on every ledger cover Jude had ever balanced.

Inside, the shop smelled of dust and lamp oil and something else, something older: the dry mineral scent of the Spire borderlands, which Jude had never smelled but knew instantly, the way you know a word you've never read aloud.

"She's not here," Odile said, sweeping the room with a warden's economy. "But she was. Recently."

Jude was already at the counter. Beneath the register lay a ledger — not a guild ledger, but close kin, bound in the same grey-green leather, its spine cracked from decades of use. The first page bore an inscription in a firm, slanted hand:

*Property of M. Ilsen. If found by a Joubert, read all of it. If found by anyone else, put it down and walk away, and I will know.*

"How would she know?" Odile asked.

"That's rather the point of Magistra Ilsen, I think." Jude opened the book.

The entries ran backward through years like tree rings. Payments recorded in Corvain's neat columns — memory, years, names — appeared here too, but annotated, corrected, *argued with*. In margins gone brown with age, Ilsen had written rebuttals to the guild's own arithmetic. *This entry is theft.* *No one consented to this; the family was never told what 'a name' costs.* *Corvain, you are balancing cruelty and calling it bookkeeping.*

Page after page. Decade after decade. And threaded through them, in entries spaced years apart, a second story: the covenant itself, described not in the guild's official language of *obligations discharged* but plainly, almost tenderly, as if Ilsen had once loved it.

*The Spire feeds us its impossible things: rain where rain is needed, roads that hold, children born whole in plague years. We pay in gift. That was the bargain. That was always supposed to be the whole bargain.*

Jude turned pages faster. Odile drifted closer, reading over her shoulder, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

Then, near the end, the handwriting changed. It shrank. It slowed. And the dates stopped being years apart and started being days.

*The renewal is overdue. Everyone at the top knows and no one says it aloud, the way no one says aloud that a roof leaks until the ceiling comes down. Corvain hid the seal-bearer instead of preparing her, which was mercy or cowardice or both, and I have run out of time to decide which.*

*I am going below. There are places in this city older than the council, older than the guild, and one of them keeps what the guild lost. If you are reading this, Jude Joubert — and you will be, because you could never leave a ledger unfinished — come down. Bring your pencil. You will want to take notes.*

*Third landing of the Drowned Stair. Knock in the old rhythm. Tell no one. Not even her.*

Jude looked up. Odile was watching her with an expression Jude couldn't parse — something between warning and surrender.

"'Not even her,'" Jude said. "'Her' being you."

"Apparently." Odile's jaw tightened. "Fine. Then wait here."

"No."

"Jude—"

"You heard her. *Come down.* She wrote *come down* to me, by name, twenty years before I knew my own name meant anything." Jude closed the ledger and held it against her chest like a shield. "I have spent my whole life being managed. Corvain managed me. The council managed me. You've been managing me since the rain in Ashport, and I know — I *know* — most of it was protection. But I am done being the entry in other people's books. I'm going down those stairs, and either you come with me as my partner or you stay up here as my jailer, and you should think hard about which one you want to be, because I have seen what happens to jailers in this story."

For a long moment the shop was silent except for the tick of cooling embers in the burned house next door.

Then Odile laughed — short, startled, genuine — and pressed her good hand briefly to her own sternum as if steadying something there. "Partner," she said. "God help me. Partner."

* * *

The Drowned Stair began behind a cistern grate in the undercity, descending into dark that smelled of standing water and cold stone. Vell had been built in layers, city atop city atop city, and the lowest layers were legend: vaults from before the free cities, tunnels from before the guild, stairways that went down farther than any surveyor had bothered to map because there was nothing down there worth taxing.

They knocked in the old rhythm — Jude had practiced it four times on the way down, knuckles rapping the pattern from the ledger's margin: *two, pause, three, pause, one* — and waited in the dripping dark.

Something shifted far below. A light kindled, weak and amber, and climbed toward them at the pace of someone conserving every step.

Magistra Ilsen came up the last flight leaning on the wall, and Jude's breath caught, because the woman in Ilsen's ledgers had been iron and ink and certainty, and the woman in front of her was a candle burned to its socket. Her hair had gone fully white. Her hands shook around the lamp. When she saw Jude, she smiled, and the smile cost her visibly.

"There you are," she said. "Twenty years I wondered what they'd done to you. Corvain wrote to me twice a year — *the girl is well, the girl suspects nothing, the girl balances faster than I ever did* — and every letter I thought, *you're keeping a living seal in a counting-house like a coin in a drawer.*" She laughed, which turned into coughing, which she rode out with the grim patience of the very ill. "Well. Come down, then. Both of you. Yes, warden, you too — I said *not even her* because I wasn't sure whose side you'd landed on. Exiles are weather. But Odile Vane holding a gate open for refugees is a report I heard three times today, and reports like that settle questions."

She turned and started down. Jude followed. Behind her, Odile paused — just a heartbeat, just long enough for Jude to feel it — and then her footsteps came too.

The chamber at the bottom was round, older than anything Jude had ever touched, its walls carved with the circle-within-the-square repeated in spirals, worn nearly smooth by hands long dead. Shelves lined the curve of the room, holding objects that made Jude's skin prickle: a lantern that burned without oil; a key labeled in a language that rearranged itself when she wasn't looking directly at it; a jar of preserved light, golden and slow-swirling, dated in a hand four hundred years dead.

In the center stood a table, and on the table lay a document.

Not paper. Something older — vellum made of something that wasn't animal, pale and faintly luminous, covered in script that shifted between two alphabets the way water shifts between reflections.

"The original terms," Ilsen said. "Recovered from the Spire's borderland six hundred years ago by a guild magistra named Hessa, who paid forty years of her life for the privilege and considered it a bargain." She lowered herself into a chair with care, like someone setting down something breakable. "Every renewal since has been conducted from copies. Copies of copies. And somewhere in the copying — deliberately, I believe, though I've never proven it — a line was changed."

Jude approached the document slowly. The script resolved as she neared it, responding to something in her blood, and she read:

*Let the seal-bearer come willing to the heart of the Spire, and offer up the whole of the gift's promise — every working it might ever do, every wonder it might ever make — and the covenant shall bind anew for a generation. This is the price entire. Nothing further shall be taken. No life shall be owed. The bearer shall walk out whole, and merely less.*

"*Merely less*," Jude whispered.

"Now find the copy," Ilsen said. "The one the guild has used for nine renewals."

Jude didn't need to look. She had it by heart — every guild child did; it was recited at induction like a catechism. *The bearer gives herself, body and soul, unto the covenant, and is consumed entirely, and her name is struck from all records in honor of her sacrifice.*

"They wrote it as death," she said. The words came out strange, distant. "Nine generations of seal-bearers read that clause and walked into the Spire believing they were walking into a furnace. Believing there was no version of this where they survived. And the guild recorded them as martyrs, and—" 

"And trained everyone after to think of the covenant as a thing that eats." Ilsen nodded, slow. "Including, I suspect, one young warden who was asked to escort a bearer to her death and found she couldn't do it."

Odile had gone very still by the door. Jude didn't turn around. She couldn't, not yet, not while her own eyes were burning.

"But that's not why you called me down," Jude said. "That lie is terrible, but it's a *kind* lie — it means I live. You said the renewal demands a price the guild never recorded. Something worse than the lie."

"Yes." All the warmth went out of Ilsen's voice. "Sit down, child. Both of you. This is the part where I need you to be ledger-keepers, because sentiment will make you stupid, and stupid gets worlds killed."

Jude sat. After a moment, Odile did too, at the edge of the lamplight, arms crossed over her wounded ribs.

"The covenant is a contract between two realms," Ilsen said. "Contracts require consideration flowing both ways. The Spire gives. We give back. Fine. But ask yourself: *who receives our payment?* Not the Spire-stone. Not the wardens. There is something at the heart of the Spire that holds the covenant — call it the remnant, call it the hearth, call it whatever your theology prefers — and it does not simply store what we pay it. It *lives* on it. It has lived on nine generations of gifts, and it is old now, and hungry, and the hunger has grown faster than the payments."

"So the overdue renewal isn't paperwork," Jude said. "It's starvation."

"It's starvation wearing paperwork's clothes, yes. And here is the price the guild never recorded, because recording it would have ended the guild: the renewal doesn't draw only from the bearer's gift. It draws from everything the bearer *is*. Gift, yes — the whole of its promise, per the true terms. But a starving covenant takes interest on the rest. Memories. Years. The capacity to be loved cleanly, some say, though that may be superstition. Hessa's marginalia claims the drain extends to 'all that the bearer holds dear,' and Hessa was not a woman who wrote superstition." Ilsen's hands trembled around the lamp; she set it down. "The guild recorded bearers as dying because a clean death is a story people can honor. The truth is messier. The truth is that bearers walked out of the Spire alive and *hollowed*, and the guild struck their names anyway, because a hollowed woman asking after a family she can no longer quite remember is not a story anyone wants in the books."

Silence filled the round room like water filling a hull.

Jude thought of the entries that had started everything — payments of memory, years, names — and understood, with a lurch, that she had been reading the covenant's small change all along. The great withdrawal was coming. It had her name on it.

"And there's no alternative," she said. "If I don't go, the covenant fails, the borderlands die — I've seen the Broken Stair, I've seen what's already happening — and Aldric harvests the gifted and eventually breaches the Spire himself, and then both realms belong to a man who thinks love is a resource."

"There is no alternative within the terms as written," Ilsen agreed. "Which is why I called you down. Not to hand you your sentence." She reached across the table and, with effort, turned the luminous document so it faced Jude fully. "To hand you the *original*. Look at the bottom, child. Look at what none of the copies ever carried."

At the foot of the ancient terms, beneath the shifting script, was a single line in a different hand — older, larger, carved rather than written, as if it had been part of the document before the document was anything at all:

*Terms may be renegotiated at renewal by mutual consent of bearer and hearth. So it was at the first sealing. So it may be again.*

Jude stared at it. Read it again. Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and enormous.

"It's always been there," she breathed. "On the original. Every copyist *omitted* it. Nine generations of bearers died—or hollowed—believing the terms were fixed, and the power to rewrite them was sitting right there at the bottom of the page."

"Why would the guild cut it out?" Odile asked quietly. From her, the question had a blade's edge. She was thinking, Jude realized, of her own refusal — the failed renewal, the breach, the hundreds dead. Thinking about whether she'd been wrong.

"Because an institution built on unchangeable terms cannot survive discoverable ones," Ilsen said. "A guild that admits the covenant can be renegotiated must admit it can be *renegotiated badly* — that a frightened or ambitious bearer might demand terms that starve the Spire instead. Better, they decided, to keep the lie simple. Martyrs make better bookkeeping than diplomats." She coughed again, longer this time, and when it passed she looked smaller, dimmer, a lamp with minutes left in it. "So. Now you know everything I know. The true terms. The omitted line. The hunger. The price. And you know what I know about timing, which is that Aldric's army is eleven days out, his witch-finders are already inside my city, and the renewal window closes at midwinter whether anyone is ready or not."

"Eleven days," Jude said. "The Salt Road north takes—"

"Nine, if the passes hold. Sixteen if they don't." Ilsen smiled again, that costly smile. "Yes. I've done the arithmetic too. It's the one thing we guild folk are reliably good at."

Jude looked down at the original terms, at the carved line at the bottom, and felt something shift in her chest — the same feeling she'd had the night she found the impossible entries, the first crack in the world she trusted, except this was the opposite. This was a crack in the world she'd *feared*. Light coming through.

Renegotiation. Mutual consent. A new bargain.

*So it was at the first sealing. So it may be again.*

"Magistra," she said. "Why you? Why did *you* keep the original? Why did you spend your life arguing with the guild's margins?"

Ilsen studied her for a long moment. The lamplight caught the white of her hair, the deep lines around her mouth, and something underneath both — a fierce, banked thing that sixty years of careful bookkeeping had never managed to smother.

"Because I was a seal-bearer candidate too," she said. "Sixty-one years ago. I stood where you're standing, I read the copied terms, and I said yes. And then, three days before I was to travel north, my younger sister drowned in the Vell floods — floods the covenant was supposed to prevent, floods that happened *because* the covenant was already thinning — and I sat with her body and I thought: *I am about to give everything I am to a contract that couldn't spare one small miracle for her.* And I refused. I walked away. I let another bearer go in my place, a girl named Tamsin who deserved the choice far less than I did, and I have spent sixty-one years telling myself I was a coward, when the truth—" her voice cracked, and she mastered it, "—the truth is I was the first person in nine generations to read the fine print and say *no*, and I was too ashamed of my own courage to understand it."

The lamp flickered. Ilsen's breathing had gone shallow.

"Take the original," she said. "Take Hessa's marginalia. Take my ledger — the arguments matter more than the entries now. And Jude Joubert, listen to me, because I haven't much left and I refuse to waste the end of it on anything but this:" She reached out and gripped Jude's wrist with surprising strength, her pulse fluttering against Jude's skin like a moth. "You are not a payment. You were never a payment. You are a *party to a contract*, and parties negotiate. Whatever you give at that hearth — and you will have to give, there is no renewal without cost, I won't lie to you as the guild lied — let it be *terms you wrote*. Terms you chose. Promise me. Promise me the guild dies with me and something honest is born in its place."

"I promise," Jude said, and found she was crying, and found she didn't mind.

Ilsen smiled — the full smile this time, the expensive one, the one that lit the whole ruined socket of her — and her grip eased. "Good girl. Good *ledger-keeper*. Now help me up. I want to see the sky. I haven't been above ground since the witch-finders came, and if I'm to die tonight I'll do it under something bigger than a ceiling."

They got her up the Drowned Stair slowly, Odile carrying the lamp, Jude bearing most of the old woman's weight and all of the documents wrapped in oilcloth against her back. At the cistern grate, Ilsen insisted on climbing the last ladder herself, one rung at a time, while Jude hovered below with her heart in her throat.

They emerged into the alley behind Candlemakers' Row, into the blue hour between sunset and dark, and Ilsen leaned against the wall and looked up at the first stars and the smoke of the burning quarter and the mountains far to the north where the Spire stood invisible behind winter.

"There," she said softly. "That's where you're going. That's where Tamsin went. That's where Hessa went." She turned her head and looked at Jude, and her eyes were clear and calm and absolutely certain. "Go differently than they did. That's all. Go *differently*."

She died there against the wall, in the open air, between one star and the next, with a ledger-keeper's hand holding hers and a warden standing watch at the alley's mouth.

Jude knelt for a long time. When she finally rose, her grief had changed shape — compressed, the way coal compresses into something that burns hotter. She wrapped Ilsen's body in her own cloak, because the magistra would not have tolerated being left untidy, and she tucked the oilcloth bundle tight against her spine.

Above the rooftops, the northern sky was black and hard and full of winter.

"Eleven days," Odile said quietly beside her. "Nine if the passes hold."

"Then we walk tonight," Jude said. "While the witch-finders are still counting their prisoners. While Aldric thinks the seal-bearer is a bargaining chip sitting in a Vell cell." She looked down at Ilsen's still face, and then north, and her voice came out level and cold and completely new. "He's about to learn he was negotiating with the wrong party."

They left Vell by the north road under a rising moon, two figures and a bundle of forbidden truths, walking into the passes where the snow had already begun to fall.
