## 12. The Usurper's Court

The throne room of Ashport had been built for a different kind of king.

Aldric Vane had torn out the old stained glass within a week of taking the crown — saints and spire-motifs, all of it — and replaced the windows with clear panes that let in the honest gray light of the harbor mornings. He wanted no gilding between himself and his work. The work was why he rose before dawn every day now, why he took his meals standing at the war-table, why he slept four hours and dreamed of arithmetic.

The kingdom was dying. Everyone knew it, though only he said it aloud. The harvests thinned year over year. Children were born smaller. The old roads sank into bogs that no amount of drainage could cure, as if the land itself were forgetting how to hold its shape. And in the borderlands to the north, where the Spire's twilight pressed against the world like a bruise through cloth, whole villages had simply gone quiet — not dead, but *less*, their inhabitants moving through days with the dimness of candles burned down to the wick.

The free cities called it the slow ebbing and blamed the covenant's age. Aldric called it what it was: an account in arrears. And he had never in his life permitted an account to go unpaid.

"Bring them in," he said.

* * *

They came through the east doors in single file, escorted by guards who did not look at them directly. Forty-one of them this morning. The harvested gifted.

Aldric had ordered chairs for them at first, in the early weeks, when there had been more of them and they still had the strength to be insulted. Now they shuffled or were wheeled, and the chairs would have been a cruelty — something to fall from. He watched them arrange themselves in the loose semicircle below the dais with the terrible patience of the exhausted, and he made himself look at each face. That was the discipline. His ministers flinched away from them; his witch-finders catalogued them; his soldiers feared them even hollowed as they were. Only the king looked. Someone had to remember what the price looked like, or the spending would become careless.

The girl nearest the front — Maren, a weaver's daughter from the lowlands, taken in the third month of the hunt — sat with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes fixed on the middle distance. Once she had been able to coax color back into faded cloth, make a bolt of gray wool blush rose or deepen to wine with a touch and a hummed note. The extraction had taken the gift entire. What remained of Maren sat very straight and breathed, and sometimes her lips moved as if humming, and nothing happened at all.

"How is she eating?" Aldric asked quietly.

The physician at his elbow, Master Corvel, consulted his slate. "Poorly, majesty. She takes broth when reminded. They all do better when reminded."

"Then remind her. Every meal. By name." He paused. "Has she spoken?"

"Not since autumn."

Aldric nodded once, filing it away in the ledger behind his eyes, the one with columns no clerk ever saw. Forty-one souls on the slate this morning. Eleven lost since the equinox — not to death, precisely; the bodies persisted, breathing and blinking, but the person inside went down like a lamp running dry, and one morning the thing wearing the beloved face stopped answering to the name. The priests said those ones waited for judgment. Aldric knew better. They had been spent. Poured into the great vessels beneath the citadel, along with everything else his hunters had gathered, waiting to be forged into the one thing that could save what remained.

"You are fed," he told the semicircle, his voice carrying without effort; he had learned projection in twenty years of mustering troops. "You are warm. You are safe here, under my protection, and your families draw your wages and live in houses the crown provides. I will not lie to you. I know some of you can still understand me." A few heads lifted slightly. Maren's did not. "What was taken from you was not stolen. It was conscripted, as your brothers were conscripted to the wall regiments, as your grain was requisitioned in the famine years. The realm asks everything of everyone now. You gave the rarest thing. History will write your names in its first chapter, when the histories are written again."

Silence. Somewhere in the ranks of them, an old man began quietly weeping, the way people weep in their sleep.

Aldric held the silence a moment longer, because he owed them that, and then turned away before his face could do anything he hadn't authorized. He climbed the three steps to the throne — he rarely sat in it; it had been his father's vanity, a chair carved with the sevenfold knot of the covenant, and Aldric had kept it mostly as a reminder of what he had replaced — and stood beside it instead, hand resting on the armrest, looking out over the harvested and the gray light and the banners of the hunt hanging motionless in the still air.

It was working. That was what none of them understood, what the whisperers in the wine-shops and the council dissidents in Vell refused to see. The vessels beneath the citadel held enough raw gift now — memory-craft, weather-sense, the little domestic miracles of hedge-witches and charm-smiths — to attempt the working. Not yet refined. Not yet *directed*. But sufficient mass, and growing daily. When the harvest reached critical weight, his artificers would pour it through the lattice he'd commissioned, and the resulting power would be enough to crack open the borderland, climb the Spire, and take renewal by force rather than begging it from a guild of bookkeepers who had let two generations lapse without so much as a reminder letter.

Renewal freely given might save the land slowly, over years, the way rain saves a field. Renewal *taken* — seized whole, concentrated, compelled — would save it in a season. Aldric had done the sums a hundred times. Slow mercy meant millions dead across the decades of the ebbing. One hard payment now meant a generation saved entire. He had chosen, as kings must choose, the arithmetic of the living over the comfort of the squeamish.

That the payment fell on the few and not the many was regrettable. That it fell on him most of all — that he would be the hand, and bear the hand's history — was the part no chronicler would ever record, and the part he thought about at night.

"Majesty." The chamberlain's voice, pitched low. "The Master of Whispers requests audience. He says—" a hesitation, distaste compressed into half a breath— "he says the seal has woken."

Aldric's hand tightened on the armrest until the old wood creaked.

"Send him in."

* * *

Dain Mercator had been a spy for three kings before Aldric and had survived the transition between each, which told you most of what you needed to know about him. He was small, gray, and unremarkable in the way of expensive furniture, and he crossed the throne room floor with his hood back and his hands visible, because he liked his masters relaxed.

He stopped at the proper distance and bowed exactly deep enough.

"Well?" Aldric did not turn from the window. In the harbor below, a supply galley was being loaded for the northern stations. Salt pork, arrowheads, the iron collars the artificers used to steady the vessels during extraction. Ordinary cargo, monstrous manifest.

"The report is confirmed twice over, majesty, and paid for once at a rate I'm ashamed to disclose." Dain's voice was dry as old vellum. "The seal-bearer lives. She has been identified. Jude Joubert, youngest ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, here in Ashport until recently. Nineteen years of age. The bloodline runs through her mother, and her mother's mother before her — the guild hid it well. Corvain buried the girl's heritage under twenty years of clerical dust."

"Corvain." Aldric finally turned. "The guildmaster."

"The same. Who, I regret to report, is dead. Killed in his own counting-house nine nights ago — not by us. The wounds were Spire-work. There was a warden involved."

The word landed in Aldric's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He listened for the splash.

"A warden," he repeated. "The Spire sent someone."

"It appears the covenant's failure rang loud enough to reach them after all. Exiled warden, by our best reading — one Odile Vess, struck from the rolls some fifteen years past. She killed Corvain's assassins, took the girl, and left the city by the south road during the flood-tide. My agents picked up the trail at the Ashford mills and lost it in the marsh country." Dain's mouth twitched, the closest he came to apology. "She is good. Better than good. Whatever she was exiled for, it wasn't incompetence."

Aldric walked slowly down from the dais. Below him, the harvested sat in their patient semicircle, and Maren's lips moved silently around a song that would never color anything again. He stopped at the edge of the dais steps, where his voice would not carry to them.

"Fifteen years ago," he said. "What was Vess's failure?"

Dain hesitated — a calculated hesitation, priced and measured — then decided the information was worth giving free. "There was a renewal due, majesty. A generation ago. The guild selected a seal-bearer, a young woman of the Joubert line — the current girl's aunt, as it happens. Vess was the escorting warden. At the threshold of the Spire, the bearer refused. Whether she refused the price or was persuaded to refuse it, the accounts differ. Vess did not compel her. The renewal failed. The breach that followed killed several hundred people in the border villages — the quieting you've seen in the north, majesty, began that winter. The Spire court stripped Vess's rank and cast her out. She's been living mortal-side ever since."

The quieting. Aldric felt the shape of it click into place, one more column balancing. Fifteen years of slow death across the northern provinces, and the cause was not the covenant's age but a single refusal — one warden's scruple, one bearer's fear, and hundreds of villages dimming like lamps in the wind ever since. He thought of the maps in the war-room, the gray spreading stain of the ebbing creeping year by year down from the borderlands, and he felt the cold clean anger that was the closest thing he allowed himself to feeling.

"And now the line produces another bearer," he said, "and the guild's last master dies hiding her, and an exile runs off with her into the marshes." He laughed once, short and without humor. "They're not fleeing, Dain. They're *marching*. To the Spire. To renew it themselves, on the old terms, in secret — and leave my kingdom to crawl back to health at a bookkeeper's pace, if it crawls at all."

"That would be my reading also, majesty."

"Where does the salt road north begin?"

"At Vell. Assuming they stop there — and they'll stop there. The guild's last senior member, Magistra Ilsen, keeps a shop in the Undermarket. If the girl wants the covenant's original terms, she'll go to Ilsen for them."

"Vell." Aldric turned the name over. The council of Vell had been prevaricating for months — pledging cooperation with the hunt while sheltering half the gifted refugees in the free cities behind their walls, voting resolutions while their granaries filled with mouths. He had intended to deal with Vell eventually. Eventually had just acquired a date. "How many witch-finders can I move on Vell within the week?"

"Two full companies by forced march, plus Serre."

The name settled over the room. Even Dain, who feared nothing that drew breath, paused a fraction before speaking it.

"She is hunting already," Dain said. "I released her onto the trail the moment the confirmation came in. She'll reach Vell ahead of any army — possibly ahead of the girl herself."

Aldric nodded slowly. Serre. Once the Spire's finest blade, its warden-protector, its sworn sword — until the court's politics soured and she had read the wind and come to him offering her service, her knowledge of the border approaches, and her intimate understanding of how wardens fought. Some in his court called her a traitor to her own kind. Aldric called her a realist. She alone among all his instruments understood the truth he had built his reign upon: that the realms were not two things but one thing failing, and that failure did not negotiate.

"Serre is not to kill the girl," he said.

Dain's eyebrows rose a careful quarter-inch. "Majesty?"

"The seal-bearer comes to me alive and intact. Her blood opens the door; her gift turns the lock; I have read enough of the old terms to know the renewal requires a willing hand at the heart of the Spire." His voice dropped, and the gray light through the clear windows made his shadow long across the floor, reaching almost to the harvested, almost to Maren's folded hands. "Serre may kill whoever stands beside her. The warden especially. Warden Vess has failed this realm once at catastrophic cost, and I will not pay for a second lesson. But the girl — the girl is not prey, Dain. She is the treasury. Treat every hair on her head as worth more than the city she's hiding in. Do I make myself plain?"

"Perfectly, majesty." Dain bowed. "And if she refuses? If this warden of theirs has spent the road filling her head with the guild's version of things — the old terms, the willing surrender, all the rest of it?"

Aldric looked out the window again. The supply galley was pulling away from the quay, its wake cutting a white seam across the dark water, bound north with iron and salt toward a war against twilight itself.

"Then Serre brings me her refusal," he said quietly, "and I will explain to her, as I have explained to everyone, that refusal has a price, and the price is always paid by someone — the only question is whether the payer chooses it or has it chosen for her. Her aunt chose refusal. I have seen the northern villages her choice emptied." He turned back, and Dain, who had watched kings die and kingdoms burn without blinking, found something in the usurper's face that made him bow lower than protocol required. "The girl will not refuse. No one refuses twice. Bring me the seal-bearer, Dain, and bring me Vess's head besides — and by midsummer, this kingdom eats again."

* * *

He dismissed his court and walked alone down through the citadel, past the guard posts, past the sealed doors, down and down the spiral stair into the vaulted dark beneath the rock where the vessels stood.

They had been the citadel's cisterns once, in his grandfather's day — great lead-lined basements where the city's water was hoarded against siege. Now they held a rarer fluid. Lamplight moved across the surfaces as Aldric descended the iron stair: forty vessels in concentric rings, each brimming with a pale luminescence that had no color quite, the harvested essence of a hundred stolen gifts suspended and waiting. It murmured. That was the thing his artificers didn't like to discuss — the way the mass murmured when he entered, a sound below hearing, like a congregation praying behind a thick wall.

Aldric stood at the rail above the nearest vessel and looked down into the light, and the light, in some sense he could never prove and never disprove, looked back.

"I know," he said softly. His breath fogged in the vault cold. "I know what you are. I know what you were. Weaver-girl. Charm-man. Hedge-priest. I say your names to myself at night, all of them, all hundred and six." He gripped the rail. "And I am telling you what I told the others upstairs: you are not stolen. You are *spent*. There is a difference, and the difference is that I will spend you on something worthy. On bread in every granary. On children born whole. On a kingdom that outlives the covens and the guilds and every dusty covenant that let it starve while it balanced its books."

The murmur rose and fell. It might have been agreement. It might have been grief. In fifteen years of listening, he had never been able to tell, and he had stopped pretending the distinction mattered.

Above him, far above, the doors at the top of the stair boomed shut, and somewhere beyond them — south, in the marsh country, on the wet roads toward Vell — a nineteen-year-old bookkeeper was running toward the Spire with a disgraced warden at her side and a dead man's secrets in her satchel.

Aldric climbed back up into the gray morning light and sent for his mapmakers.

The hunt would ride within the hour.
