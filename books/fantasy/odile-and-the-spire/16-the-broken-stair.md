## 16. The Broken Stair

The stair appeared out of the fog like the spine of a drowned god.

Jude had imagined the approach to the Spire a hundred times since Ilsen pressed the covenant's original terms into her hands — she had imagined marble, perhaps, or gold, or some impossible material that didn't have a name. What she saw instead was stone gone gray as old teeth, a staircase wide enough for twenty abreast, descending from the borderland's twilight cliffs and climbing toward a light that wasn't quite light, hanging in the fog above like a wound in the sky.

And it was breaking.

Not all at once. Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. As they stood at the first landing, a section of railing simply *forgot itself* — the iron unbecoming, unraveling into rust-red motes that drifted upward instead of down, as if even gravity here had grown uncertain.

"It's worse than the maps said," Odile murmured. She stood at the edge of the landing in her patched warden's coat, one hand resting on the hilt of the sword she'd taken from Serre's body. She hadn't been the same since the shrine. Not colder, exactly. More *careful*, the way a person is careful with a cracked cup they've decided to keep using anyway.

"Maps?" Tam shifted the bundle on his back — their food, what remained of it, wrapped in oilcloth. He was nineteen, a refugee gifted from the salt villages who could make small fires bloom in his palms when he was frightened, which on this road was often. "There are maps of this place?"

"There were maps of everything once," Odile said. "The Spire kept records too. Everything keeps records." Her eyes flicked to Jude, and something almost like a smile touched her mouth. "Some of us just write them down better than others."

Wren laughed — a short, startled sound, as if laughter were a language she was relearning. She was older than Tam, mid-thirties maybe, with silver threading her dark hair and a gift for mending that had made her valuable enough to hide from Aldric's witch-finders through two winters of running. On the salt road she had repaired Tam's boots, Odile's cloak, the strap of Jude's satchel, and once, quietly, without being asked, the fraying seam of Odile's left glove — the one Odile wore to cover the warden's brand.

Jude had watched her do it. Odile had let her. Neither of them had spoken of it afterward, but Jude had thought about it for a long time that night: how trust sometimes arrived not as a declaration but as an outstretched hand and a needle and thread.

"Look at the steps," Wren said now, crouching. She ran her palm over the stone, and where she touched it, faintly, the gray warmed toward its original white — then faded again, like breath on glass. "It's not damaged. It's *starving*."

Jude felt those words land somewhere beneath her ribs. She knelt beside Wren and pressed her own hand flat against the step. Nothing happened — she couldn't mend stone — but she could feel it, or imagine she could: a thinness under the surface, a hollowness, like pressing her ear to a wall and hearing no one home.

"The covenant feeds this place," she said. "Ilsen's terms said so. The Spire pours outward; the seal-bearer renews the channel every generation. And we're—" She did the arithmetic she'd been doing in her head for three hundred miles, the arithmetic that got worse every time she did it. "We're forty years late."

"Then we climb," Odile said. "Quickly, and lightly, and nobody touches anything that hums."

They climbed.

* * *

Odile went first. It wasn't a decision she announced; it was simply where her body put her, the way it always had. Warden training died hard, and besides, someone had to test each flight before the others trusted it, and she had spent eleven years in exile learning exactly how much weight a failing thing could bear before it stopped pretending.

She counted the stairs as she climbed. It was an old habit from the Spire's service — wardens counted everything, because in a realm built on impossible things, numbers were the last honest language. Four hundred and twelve steps to the second landing. The railings there still held. The fog thinned enough to show the country below them: the borderland spreading out in colors that didn't exist on the mortal side of anything, meadows of glass-bladed grass, rivers running slow and silver as mercury, a forest of trees that grew blossoms and fruit and small blue flames all from the same branches.

It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. And it was dying, and now that Odile knew what dying looked like, she could see it everywhere — in the grass blades that had gone cloudy and brittle, in the river's edges where the silver simply *stopped*, dissolving into ordinary gray water that fell away down the mountainside and became, presumably, merely wet.

Her fault. Some part of it, anyway. The breach she'd refused to prevent, eleven years ago, had started this rot at the edges, and the rot had spread inward year by year like ink through wet paper. She had told herself for a decade that the covenant would hold without her compliance, that the price was unjust and refusal was righteousness. She had told herself a great many things while people died.

"You keep looking back," Tam said. He had come up beside her on the third flight, quiet-footed despite his size, fire-gifted hands tucked in his armpits against the cold. "At the view. You keep looking back at the view."

"Wardens look back," Odile said. "That's most of the job."

"What's the rest?"

"Remembering why you left."

He grinned at that, young enough to think it was wit rather than confession. Ahead of him, Wren had paused at a landing where a statue stood — or had stood. Half of it remained: a figure robed and faceless, holding out both hands, one of which had crumbled into gravel that glittered with what might have been gold dust. Wren touched the surviving hand gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"Who was it?" she asked.

"No one knows," Odile said. "That's the truth of the Spire. It's older than its own memory. Even the wardens—" She stopped. Even the wardens, she had been about to say, only ever learned fragments. Even the wardens were given just enough understanding to serve and never enough to refuse intelligently. She had learned that lesson too late and too well.

"It looks like it's giving something," Wren said softly. "Or asking."

"Same gesture," Odile said. "Here, it always was."

Above them, faintly, the light in the fog pulsed. Once. Like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Jude looked up at it, and Odile watched her look — watched the ledger-keeper's face do the thing it did when the numbers came out wrong, the small tightening around the eyes, the lips moving slightly as if writing in invisible ink. Jude Joubert counted everything too. It was, Odile had decided somewhere on the salt road, the thing she liked best about her: that they were both, in their different ruined ways, keepers of accounts.

"Two hundred and six steps," Odile called back. "Then the long span. Stay close to the inner wall."

"Why the inner wall?" Tam asked.

"Because the outer edge is where the world forgot to finish," Odile said, and started climbing.

* * *

The long span was a bridge as much as a stair — a single great arc of stone crossing a gorge that the fog filled to its brim, so that walking it felt like walking on the lip of a cloud. The wind up there had a sound Jude had never heard before and hoped never to stop hearing: a low, resonant tone, like a struck bell that never fully faded. The Spire's voice, Odile said. Or what remained of it.

Halfway across, Jude understood what Odile meant by *the world forgot to finish.*

A section of the bridge ahead of them — ten feet, maybe fifteen — was simply absent. Not broken. Absent. The stone ended in clean, smooth curves, as if the missing piece had been scooped away by an enormous careful hand, and beyond the gap the bridge resumed as though nothing had interrupted it. Through the gap, far below, the fog glowed faintly, lit from underneath by something patient and deep.

"That wasn't on the way in," Odile said flatly. "Nothing decays that fast."

"It's not decaying," Jude said. Her voice came out strange to her own ears, thin and clear, the voice she used when a ledger refused to balance. "It's being *called back*. Don't you see? The Spire is starving. It's pulling itself inward. It's eating its own extremities to keep the heart alive." She looked at the gap, at the fog, at the pulsing light above. "Every hour we take, there'll be more gaps. This whole borderland is the covenant's body, and the body is being spent to keep the—"

She stopped, because Wren had stepped forward onto the bridge's edge, and because Tam had reached out to steady her, and because in that instant — Jude would replay it ten thousand times in the years after, frame by frame, the way one replays a fall — the stone beneath Wren's feet quietly ceased to exist.

No crack. No warning rumble. One moment the bridge held her; the next it was fog and empty air, and Wren was dropping, arms wheeling, her mouth open on a scream that the bell-tone wind swallowed whole.

Tam lunged.

Later, Jude would understand that he must have known — that a boy who had survived two winters of hiding from witch-finders knew precisely what his gift cost and what it couldn't do, that fire does not catch on falling air. But he lunged anyway, flinging himself flat across the gap's edge, one arm plunging down into the void, and his other hand opened and *burned* — flame roaring out of him in a way Jude had never seen, not the frightened candle-flicker of the campfires but a pillar of desperate gold, and whether it was the fire or the reach or the sheer stupid magnificent arithmetic of love, his fingers closed on Wren's wrist.

The jolt nearly took him too. His body slammed against the bridge's broken edge, boots scrabbling on stone, and for one endless second he hung there half over the void with Wren's full weight swinging beneath him, and Jude heard him make a sound she had never heard a human being make — not a scream, something smaller and worse, the sound of a person discovering the exact limit of their strength.

Odile moved.

Jude barely saw it. There was a blur of patched gray coat, a flash of the branded wrist bare above its ruined glove, and then Odile was stretched along the gap's edge with her legs hooked around a railing-stub, both hands locked around Tam's arm, hauling with a strength that had nothing to do with muscle and everything to do with eleven years of refusing to fail twice.

"Together!" she snarled. "*Pull!*"

Jude threw herself down beside them and took Tam's collar. Wren, below, had stopped screaming and started climbing — hand over hand up Tam's burning arm, her mender's gift flaring silver where she gripped, knitting torn skin and scorched cloth as she rose, so that the boy who held her was healed by the very act of holding her. Jude would remember that too. She would carry it the way you carry embers in cupped hands.

They came up over the edge together, all three of them, a tangle of limbs and smoke and sobbing, and collapsed onto the solid stone of the far side in a heap that had briefly been the entire meaning of the world.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The bell-tone wind rang on. Below them, the fog glowed, indifferent.

Then Tam began to laugh — high, cracked, hysterical — with Wren's arms locked around him and his own hands still smoking, and Wren laughing too, wet-eyed, checking his fingers one by one as if counting them, and Jude found that she was crying without any memory of starting.

Odile sat apart, on her knees, breathing hard. When Jude crawled to her, she saw that the warden's hands were shaking — fine, constant, uncontrollable tremors, the hands of someone who had just held three lives over a void on fingers made of will alone.

"Hey," Jude said softly. "Hey. They're alive. You did that."

"I know what I did." Odile's voice was rough. She was staring at the gap. At the fog. At the place where the world had simply stopped finishing itself. "Jude. It took the bridge *while she was standing on it*. No warning. No decay. Do you understand what that means?"

Jude followed her gaze, and the arithmetic finished itself, cold and complete, in the space behind her eyes.

"It means it's not waiting anymore," she whispered. "The decay isn't creeping. It's accelerating. Every mile of this borderland is being consumed to feed the Spire's heart, and the heart is losing anyway, and when the heart goes—" She thought of Ashport's flooded lowlands, of Vell's powder-keg streets, of the free cities' green fields and the mortal rain and every small impossible mercy the covenant had paid for across a thousand years. "When the heart goes, it doesn't just take the Spire. It takes both worlds. Everything the covenant ever touched. Everything it ever *was*."

"Then we don't stop," Odile said. She rose. She picked up her sword. Her hands were still shaking, and she sheathed the blade anyway, and turned to face the remaining span of the bridge, and the fog-wrapped light above it, pulsing now faster — or perhaps that was only Jude's own heartbeat, climbing into her throat.

"How much farther?" Wren asked quietly. She was on her feet, steadier than any of them deserved, one hand still resting on Tam's shoulder as if to confirm he was real.

"One span," Odile said. "One more gap, maybe two, if the world has forgotten anything else since we crossed. Then the threshold." Something moved behind her eyes — something old and heavily guarded, a door with a great deal of weight behind it. "Beyond the threshold, there are things I have to tell you. Things about the renewal. About the last time someone stood where you're going to stand, Jude, and what I did when they asked me the question you're about to be asked."

Jude looked at her — at the shaking hands, the bare brand, the woman who had caught a falling world on her arms and was already standing again — and nodded slowly.

"Then tell me at the top," Jude said. "Because whatever the question is, I'm answering it either way. I'd like to answer it knowing."

The bell-tone rang. Above them, through the thinning fog, the Spire's light pulsed — once, twice — like a heart deciding whether to go on.

They crossed the last of the bridge in single file, close to the inner wall, and did not look down.
