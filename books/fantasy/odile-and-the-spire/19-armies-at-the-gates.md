## 19. Armies at the Gates

The horns began at dusk.

Jude heard them through the Spire's threshold — a sound that should not have carried this far, across the border of realms, up through the twilight air where distance was supposed to be a suggestion rather than a law. But the covenant was failing, and failing things let wrongness leak through. The horns came thin and brazen over the mountains like smoke finding cracks in a sealed room.

She stood on the last landing of the Broken Stair with her hands flat against the living stone and felt the vibration of them in her teeth.

"They're at Vell," she said.

Odile came to stand beside her. Below, the borderland spread out in its impossible geography — rivers that ran uphill into clouds, forests of glass-leaved trees chiming in a wind that touched nothing else, the whole twilight country dimmed now, its colors drained toward gray where the covenant's decay had bitten deepest. Far to the south, past the border's shimmering veil, the lights of the free cities were visible as a scatter of embers. And among those embers, new lights had kindled that did not belong. Fires. Siege-fires. A ring of them, closing.

"Vell, Ashport, maybe Marrow by now." Odile's voice was flat, the way it went flat when she was keeping something worse underneath. "He didn't wait for spring. He didn't wait for anything."

"He knows we're here."

"He's known since Serre." Odile's hand went briefly to the wound at her ribs — bound now, but deep, the price of the shrine and the duel and the long climb after. "A hunter doesn't fail quietly. She'd have sent word before she died. Or after." A muscle worked in her jaw. "Serre always did her duty. Even to the end. Especially to the end."

Below them, on the stair's shattered middle reaches, Tam and Wren were making camp in the lee of a fallen archway — the two refugee gifted who had come with them from the salt road, a boy who could unmake small fires and a woman whose songs mended broken bone. Small gifts. The kind Aldric's witch-finders had once overlooked, back when overlooking was policy. Now his harvest took everything with a spark in it, and the sparks were burning out faster than he could feed them into his war machines.

Jude watched the little campfire wink and thought about ledgers. It was what she always did when fear got too large to hold — she broke it into columns. Assets and debts. What they had: herself, the seal in her blood, a disgraced warden, two frightened children of the gift, and a stair half-collapsed between them and the Spire's heart. What Aldric had: an army, a fleet of harvested gifted hollowed out into weapons, and a king's certainty that taking was the same as having.

The arithmetic was obscene. But she had spent her whole life learning that the ledger never lies, even when you wish it would.

"The defenses will break tonight," she said. "Vell's walls were built to stop soldiers. Not what he's bringing."

Odile said nothing, which was agreement.

Far below, one of the ember-lights flared white — too white, wrong-white — and Jude felt it like a hook behind her sternum. Someone down there was being used. Someone's gift, torn out and spent like coin, hurled against stone until the stone gave or the giver did.

"We have until dawn," she said. "Maybe less."

* * *

King Aldric Vane climbed the mountain road on foot, because horses would not go past the border stones.

His generals had begged him to wait below, to let the vanguard test whatever waited in the twilight country. He had thanked them, smiled the smile that ended arguments, and ordered his guard to remain at the tree line. This part of the work was his alone. He had understood that for years, though he had only recently admitted it aloud, alone, in the dark of his campaign tent, to no one.

The harvested walked ahead of him in a silent column. Twelve of them, out of the hundreds his finders had gathered; these twelve were the strongest, the ones whose gifts had not yet guttered. They moved like sleepwalkers, eyes open and empty, their stolen power drawn up through the chains of sigils stitched into their collars and held ready, banked fire waiting for his word. He had learned to read their remaining reserves by the color of their lips, the steadiness of their hands. The girl called Maren — a weather-worker once, from the fishing villages — had perhaps three days left in her. He intended to spend her well before then.

It was necessary. That was the sentence he returned to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. His kingdom had been dying slowly for a century — the blights, the failed harvests, the slow retreat of magic from the common soil while the guild hoarded it in secret ledgers and paid it out to *them*, to the other side, to the Spire and its insatiable appetite. He had found the truth in a dead guildmaster's papers and it had explained everything: every lean winter, every plague that burned through the lowlands while the impossible things bloomed fat and wild beyond the border. The free cities had been tithed without their consent for generations. He was simply ending the tithe. Taking back what was taken.

That the taking required taking, that the harvested faded like lamps starved of oil — these were costs. He had never pretended otherwise. Kings who pretended otherwise were liars, and he had made himself one promise on the night he took the crown from a dead man's head: *I will not lie to myself.* Everything else was negotiable.

The spymaster had told him the seal-bearer lived. Had told him she was climbing toward the Spire with a warden of the other realm at her side — a traitor twice over, exiled by her own people, which made her either worthless or very dangerous. Aldric had read the reports three times and then burned them, because paper could be intercepted and the shape of the thing was already fixed in his mind.

If the girl renewed the covenant, the tithe resumed, and his kingdom kept starving politely for another hundred years while the guild counted its secret coins.

If he reached the Spire's heart first — if renewal could be seized instead of given, forced through a bearer whether she willed it or not — then the power flowed one way. Into him. Into the realm. Into the armies that would make the borders irrelevant, because there would be nothing left on the other side worth defending.

He did not know if it could be done. The old terms were clear that renewal must be willing. But terms were written by someone, and everything written could be rewritten by whoever held the pen hard enough.

Behind him, far below, the horns sounded the assault on Vell's last gate. He did not turn to look. The city would fall or it wouldn't; either way it bought him the night. He climbed.

At the border stones, the twilight took him. The air changed — thicker, sweeter, faintly wrong, like fruit just past ripening. One of the harvested stumbled, and the sigil-chains flared blue as the collars drank deeper to keep them upright. Aldric watched the flare with clinical interest and noted that Maren's lips had gone nearly white.

Three days. Perhaps two, up here, where the air itself seemed to drink.

He climbed faster.

* * *

In Vell, on the wall above the Salt Gate, a boy named Tam's older brother stood with a pike he barely knew how to hold and watched the sky catch fire.

Not fire exactly. Light. It came rolling up the siege lines in slow luminous waves, and where it touched the wall the ancient mortar glowed and crumbled, and men screamed in a way that had nothing to do with wounds. The council had surrendered its gifted two days ago — marched them out the west postern in chains, a payment offered up like the guild's old tithes, *take them and spare us* — and it had bought nothing. Aldric's army had accepted the gift and kept coming, because the army had never been the point. The point was the Spire, and the girl, and the door in the world that stood open only until someone closed it forever.

The brother did not know any of this. He knew the light was coming again, and that the man beside him had stopped screaming and started singing, high and tuneless, and that singing was worse somehow, and that his own hands on the pike were shaking so badly the spearhead chattered against the battlement like a spoon in a cup.

Then the wave hit, and the wall under his feet sighed like a tired animal, and a section of it the length of a market street folded inward with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Through the breach came no charge. Just the light, walking. Figures in it, upright and unhurried, faces slack, hands raised, doing what they were made to do now, which was spend themselves on command until there was nothing left to spend.

The brother dropped his pike. He was not ashamed of it. Later, in the refugee columns winding north through the passes, he would tell anyone who asked that he had fought, and most of them would nod, because everyone in those columns had fought, in the way that surviving was fighting, in the way that walking away with your life was the only victory the season offered.

By midnight Vell's bells rang surrender, and by dawn the ring of siege-fires had gone dark, because the army was moving north, all of it, leaving the broken cities behind like shed skin.

Everything Aldric had, he was sending up the mountain.

* * *

Jude felt the moment Vell fell.

There was no horn for it, no signal. It arrived instead as a change in the pressure of the world — a softness in the stone under her palms, a flicker in the glass-forest's chiming, as if the borderland itself had flinched. She had been attuned to the covenant's decay for weeks now; she could feel its sickness the way she had once felt errors in a ledger, that itch of wrongness no amount of checking could soothe. And when a city full of frightened people gave up its hope all at once, the decay drank it, and she felt the drinking.

"It's done," she said. "Vell's opened its gates."

Wren looked up from the campfire, her face hollowed by the climb and by grief — her sister had been in Vell's harvest carts, marched west in chains. "Then there's nothing between him and us."

"There never was," Odile said. "The cities were never the defense. We are." She rose, wincing, and Jude saw her take stock the way a soldier takes stock — the stair above, the wind, the light dying in the west. "We climb tonight. All of us. Rest is a luxury for people with time."

They climbed. The Broken Stair was worse in the dark — sections missing entirely, forcing them along ledges of fused glass where the old covenant's death-throes had melted stone into something半 alive, something that hummed underfoot. Twice the humming became shuddering, and twice they pressed themselves flat while a whole span of stair sloughed away into the glowing dark below. Tam crossed one gap on a bridge of unmade fire — his gift turned inside out, a cold absence in the shape of flame that things could walk on — and afterward he sat down abruptly and shook for a quarter hour, and Wren held his shoulders and sang low until the shaking stopped.

Jude did not shake. She wanted to. Instead she counted steps, because counting was hers, counting was the oldest thing she had, and somewhere around four hundred she realized she was no longer counting steps but heartbeats, and that each one felt borrowed.

Near the top, where the stair ended at the Spire's threshold plateau, she stopped and looked back.

The world below was laid out like a ledger page. South, the free cities, ember-scattered, smoking. Between them and the mountain, a river of torchlight moving — slow, inevitable, orderly, the way debt moves when it finally comes due. She could see the column's head already entering the borderland's outer marches, and she could see, at its center, a knot of pale light that did not flicker like torchlight. It burned steady. Banked. Harvested power, carried like coin in a strongbox, spending nothing until the moment of purchase.

And at the head of the light, a single figure on foot, climbing.

"Aldric," Odile said quietly. She had come to stand at Jude's shoulder. Her breath smoked in the cold. "He's coming himself."

"I know."

"You know how I know? Because I've seen that walk before." Odile's voice had an edge Jude hadn't heard since the shrine. "On the Spire's side. Wardens who decided the covenant belonged to them. Who decided that if the price was terrible, they'd simply take the power and pay nothing." Her hand found Jude's in the dark, grip tight, callused, warm. "Whatever happens at the heart — whatever the remnant shows you, whatever the old terms say — he cannot be allowed to touch the seal. Not to force it. Not to bargain with it. If it comes to that, you run, and I hold the door, and I don't care what it costs me. I've paid worse for less."

Jude looked at their joined hands, then south, at the climbing light.

"No," she said.

Odile turned. In the borderland's dim glow her face was all planes and shadow, and her eyes were the eyes of someone braced for a blow.

"No," Jude said again, and heard her own voice steady in a way that surprised her — the bookkeeper's voice, the voice that read fine print aloud in rooms full of people who wanted her to sign quickly. "I'm done running from things and calling it strategy. Corvain died buying me time. Ilsen died passing me the truth. Half the borderland has died of the covenant's rot, and Vell died tonight of fear, and every mile of that ledger comes due at the heart of the Spire. I'll be there when it does. Standing. Not running." She squeezed Odile's hand. "You can hold the door. But you'll be holding it with me on this side of it."

For a long moment Odile said nothing. Then, softly, almost a laugh: "Corvain trained you?"

"Ledgers train you. Everything else is commentary."

Above them, the Spire's threshold opened — a doorway of woven light at the top of the last flight, breathing slow as a sleeping animal, waiting. Below them, the torch-river climbed. And between the two, on the shattered stair, four small figures turned together and began the final ascent, while behind and beneath them the whole weight of a kingdom's hunger came on through the dark.

Jude climbed. At the doorway she paused, one hand on the frame of light, and felt it recognize her — felt the seal in her blood answer like a struck bell — and heard, very faintly, rising through the stone, through the wind, through the failing weave of both worlds:

the sound of the first harvested power being spent against the Spire's outer wall.

Dawn was still hours away. They had less than that.

She stepped through.
