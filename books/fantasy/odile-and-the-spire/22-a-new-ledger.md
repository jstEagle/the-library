## 22. A New Ledger

The guildhall smelled of ash and lye and, beneath both, faintly of the old tallow candles Corvain had refused to replace with anything newer or better. Jude stood in the doorway of the counting room for a long moment before she could make herself cross it.

They had buried him in March, under a rowan tree on the hill above Ashport's flooded terraces, because he had once said — drunk, laughing, in one of the three nights she had ever seen him unbuttoned — that rowans kept off ill-wishing. The ground there had been soft from the thaw. Odile had stood at the graveside with her hood down, which for Odile was the equivalent of weeping, and Jude had put her hand flat on the turned earth and felt nothing but cold and the enormous, ordinary weight of loss.

Now she opened the shutters.

Light came in across the long counting table, across the scarred oak where six generations of ledger-keepers had bent their backs over the impossible arithmetic of the world. The ledgers themselves were gone — carried north to the Spire, every volume, at the covenant's own request. *We are done being a vault,* the remnant consciousness had said, in its voice like wind through a bell. *A vault keeps. A bridge carries.* The shelves stood empty, dusted and waiting, and in their middle, alone, lay a single new book.

Jude had bound it herself. She was not good at it — the spine was crooked, the stitching uneven where her hands had shaken — but she had sewn it with thread pulled from the hem of Corvain's coat, and the cover was grey leather that had once been the satchel Ilsen carried through forty years of undercity dark. Nothing in this book had been bought. Everything in it had been given.

She sat down in her master's chair. It still fit her badly. She suspected it always would, and she found, testing the thought like a coin bitten between the teeth, that she did not mind. A chair you grew into was a chair someone had left you. A chair that fit perfectly was a chair no one had loved enough to wear out.

She opened the front cover.

Blank pages. She had counted them twice, then a third time, because counting was the oldest prayer she knew — but they were blank, all of them, white as the first snow on the Salt Road, white as the borderlands had been before the wild impossible things came back blooming through the thaw. No columns ruled in advance. No categories waiting. No *payments received* and no *payments owed*.

For twenty years, the guild's books had recorded what the realms took from each other: memories traded for rain, years of life for the mending of a bridge, names surrendered so that harvests would not fail. Fine print stacked upon fine print until the whole covenant was a debt collection wearing the mask of a miracle. And when the debts came due faster than the payments, the guild had quietly begun to die, and no one had thought to ask why the ledger only ever ran one direction.

Jude dipped her pen.

Her hand hovered. Old habit wanted columns. Old habit wanted the comfort of numbers, the safety of entries that balanced, debits against credits, everything accounted for and nothing left open. For two months since the Reforging she had felt the absence of her gift like the absence of a tooth — the tongue going again and again to the gap. She had poured everything the gift made possible into the seal: every glimpse of what she might have been, every door her blood could have opened, the whole unwritten country of her other life. It was not death. The remnant had told the truth about that, at least. But it was a real price, paid really, and some mornings she woke grieving it without being able to name what she grieved.

And yet.

She set the nib to paper and wrote, in her careful ledger-hand, the first entry of the Guild of Impossible Things as it would now be:

*Entry the First. Spring, first year of the New Accord.*

*Received: nothing.*

*Given: nothing taken.*

*Promised: that no gift shall be harvested, bought, or conscripted in either realm; that all exchange between the free cities and the Spire shall be made freely, witnessed, and fairly weighed; that the seal shall pass by choice and never by blood alone; and that this guild shall keep honest record of every bargain struck between worlds, so that neither may again forget what it owes the other.*

*Witnessed by J. Joubert, ledger-keeper, who writes this of her own will, owing nothing, owning nothing here but the truth of it.*

*Witnessed also by O. Vael-Serrenne, warden of the Spire, exiled, pardoned, and self-exiled once more by choice, who signs below though she says my handwriting is a scandal.*

Jude smiled at the page. Below her own signature, in tall sharp letters that looked like they were trying to escape the book entirely, Odile had already signed — days ago, impatiently, while Jude was still drafting. Beneath the name she had added, in Spire-script, a single line that translated roughly to: *Let the record show I argued for better terms and was overruled by a bookkeeper.*

*Overruled,* Jude thought. *By a bookkeeper.* Two years ago she had been the youngest ledger-keeper in a dying guild, balancing accounts no one read, asking questions no one answered. Now her handwriting sat at the head of a covenant between worlds, and kings — well. There were no kings now. That had been the first entry in the old book's final page, crossed out in Aldric's own hand before the remnant unmade him: *the crown of the free cities is dissolved, its powers returned to the councils and the commons.* Aldric himself had left nothing to bury. The covenant had simply closed around his reaching, the way water closes around a stone, and where he had stood there was a scorch mark on the Spire's stair and a silence that everyone agreed felt less like tragedy than like weather finally clearing.

She blotted the ink. Outside the window, Ashport was loud in the way it had not been in years — hammers, cartwheels, someone singing badly two streets over, the particular racket of a city rebuilding itself without permission from anyone.

* * *

Odile found her at midday, the way Odile always found her, which was to say by making enough noise on the stairs that surprise became technically impossible and therefore, by Odile's reasoning, unnecessary.

"You're still writing," Odile said from the doorway. She had her arms full — bread, a jar of honey from the lowland apiaries, and a bundle of blue cloth that Jude recognized, with a small jolt, as a warden's cloak cut down and re-dyed. "You've been writing since dawn. I could hear the pen scratching from the market. It sounded like a mouse dying in the walls."

"It sounded like governance."

"It sounded like a mouse." Odile dropped the bundle on the table and shook out the cloak. The warden's silver knot had been unpicked from the collar and resewn at the shoulder in plain brown thread — the mark, the Spire's heralds had explained, of an assigned crossing, permanent, revocable only by the wearer herself. First warden in the Spire's history to request posting to the mortal side. The council of wardens had taken the news the way mountains take weather. "Try it on. If it hangs wrong I'm taking it back to Marta, and she'll lecture me for an hour about how I measured you by memory, and my memory, she'll say, is sentimental."

"Your memory is accurate. You just refuse to admit I've stopped flinching at loud noises."

"You flinched at the honey jar."

"The honey jar was thrown."

"It was *tossed*. With love." Odile held the cloak up behind her shoulders, and Jude stood and let her settle it, feeling the light weight of it, the smell of new dye and woodsmoke. In the grey light of the counting room, with Odile's hands at her collar, the gesture was so domestic it made something in her chest go quiet and wide, like a held breath finally let out.

"There," Odile said. "Now you look like what you are, which is a person who owns exactly one good cloak and wears it to official functions."

"I own two cloaks."

"One good cloak. The other one has a burn hole shaped like Serre's sword."

"That burn hole is a historical document."

"That burn hole is why Marta laughed at me." Odile came around the table and looked down at the open ledger, and her expression did the thing it did — the small closing-off, the warden's habit of guarding whatever moved her most. But she didn't close it this time. She reached out and touched the edge of the first page, careful, the way she touched everything that mattered, as if it might startle. "Is it done? The entry?"

"Done. Witnessed. Filed." Jude tapped the last line. "Filed nowhere, actually. That's rather the point. This book doesn't live in a vault. It lives wherever someone is willing to carry it."

"So it lives with you."

"So it lives with me." Jude closed it and wrapped the strap around it, and the weight of it against her hip was lighter than any ledger she had ever carried, and heavier too, in the way that chosen things are heavier than inherited ones. "The councils want copies in Vell and Ashport and the Salt Road stations. The Spire wants a reading posted at the border shrine — the little one, where we—" She stopped. "Where the stair used to be. They're rebuilding the approach, did you know? Slowly. Stone by stone. Not a stair this time. A road. Wide enough for carts. Wide enough for anyone who wants to walk between."

"I know. I signed the work order." Odile's mouth curved. "I sign so many things now. Half of them in your terrible hand because you keep making me practice."

"My hand is legible."

"My hand is *beautiful*, and yours is a scandal, and history will remember mine." But she said it softly, looking at the ledger, and then she looked up, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all, because there are moments when the accounting is finished and only the living remain, standing in a dusty room with honey and bread and a blue cloak, and anything more said would be smaller than the silence.

It was Odile, of course, who broke it, because Odile could not bear a silence longer than four seconds. "The refugees' children are racing paper boats in the flood-channels. Marta says if we don't come judge the race, there will be a riot, and she will personally lead it."

"Then we'd better go," Jude said. "Before the riot gets written up. I'm not balancing those books."

* * *

They walked down through the guildhall together, past the empty shelves, past the room where the old ledgers had slept for centuries — now swept clean, its windows open, designated by the new accord as the Hall of Reading, where any citizen of either realm might come and study what the covenant had been and choose what it would become. Past the door of Corvain's old office, which Jude still did not open, and which she suspected she never would, and which she had decided was allowed. Grief did not have to balance. Some entries stayed open forever, and that was not a failure of the books. That was what the books were *for*.

Outside, spring had taken the city the way a tide takes a shore.

Ashport's flooded terraces had drained in March, and now the lower streets were mud and scaffolding and the green insistence of weeds coming up through everything, and nobody minded, because the weeds were growing through the cracks Aldric's war had made, and growth in a crack is a kind of answer. The rowan on the hill was budding white. Market stalls leaned out over the cobbles where the witch-finders' posts had been torn down, selling early greens and ribbon and, at one stall run by a one-eyed woman from the salt flats, small carved spires that children bought with stolen buttons and used as swords.

At the flood-channel, the race was in chaos. Six paper boats, four of them sinking. A girl of perhaps eight — a refugee from Vell, gifted with small warm magics that made her palms glow faintly when she laughed, which was often — was arguing jurisprudence with a boy twice her size, and losing on volume and winning on logic. Odile waded in immediately, took both sides in turn, declared the race void on grounds of sabotage by duck, negotiated a rematch, and within minutes had assembled a tribunal of children so ruthlessly fair that Jude had to turn away to hide her face.

She stood on the bank instead, the ledger at her hip, the sun on the back of her neck, and watched.

This, then. This was what she had bought with everything the gift might have been. Not a throne, not a place in the Spire's high galleries among the slow singing minds of the covenant — she had seen those offered to her, in the moment of the Reforging, seen the life she could have lived among them, and set it down like a heavy coat in summer. What she had bought was this: a muddy bank in April. A child's glowing hands. A disgraced warden adjudicating a paper boat dispute with the gravity of a high justice. Honey in the satchel. A road going north, wide enough for carts, that anyone could walk.

Somewhere beyond the twilight border, the Spire stood in its long evening, no longer a fortress and no longer a creditor, its doors — its actual doors, built these past weeks by mortal masons working beside warden-shapers — open onto the borderlands where impossible things grew wild and free for the first time in a thousand years, belonging to no covenant, owned by no one, miracles become simply *weather*.

The girl with glowing hands won the rematch. The duck was acquitted. Odile came splashing back up the bank, soaked to the knee, grinning in the unguarded way she grinned perhaps four times a year, and fell into step beside Jude without either of them deciding to.

"North?" Odile said. It was half question, half ritual. Every few days one of them asked it, and every few days the answer was yes, and they went a little farther along the road each time, walking the new route between the worlds as it was built, checking the milestones, carrying the ledger to be read aloud at each station so that the terms of the accord would be heard by living voices and not just ink.

"North," Jude said.

They passed the rowan tree on the way out of the city. Jude did not stop — Corvain would have disapproved of stopping, would have said sentiment was a tax levied on the inefficient — but she slowed, and put her hand briefly on the trunk, and felt the sap moving under the bark, rising, the way everything rose that spring.

Above the gate, the old royal proclamation board had been pried down and replaced with a fresh plank, and on it, in paint that was still drying, someone had copied the first line of the new ledger's first entry in letters a meter high, so that everyone leaving or entering the city would walk beneath it:

*RECEIVED: NOTHING. GIVEN: NOTHING TAKEN.*

Below it, smaller, in a different hand, someone had already added graffiti: *and the books finally balance.*

Jude laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and Odile looked up at the plank, and then at her, and did not ask what was funny, because Odile had read every word of every ledger too, and knew better than anyone alive what a miracle it was, after all those centuries of payments, to see the words *nothing taken* written anywhere at all.

They went out through the gate together, into the rebuilding spring, two figures on a road that anyone could walk, carrying between them one crooked-spined book of blank pages — and the first promise ever entered in it, which was also, if you read it rightly, an invitation:

*All exchanges between the realms shall be made freely.*

Anyone could write the next entry. That was the whole point. That was the entire inheritance, rewritten at last as choice — and somewhere ahead, past the flooded lowlands and the salt flats and the border shrine with its new wide road, the next page was already waiting, blank and patient, for whoever came to claim the pen.
