## 1. The Weight of Blank Pages

The jar arrived on a Tuesday, which Theo Thornbury considered a small mercy. Tuesdays were quiet days at the Guild of Impossible Things—no debt-collectors rattling the door, no weeping clients clutching receipts for things they'd sold and regretted, no guildmaster sweeping through the counting room with her questions tucked under her arm like knives.

Just Theo, the ledgers, and a crate that hummed when you got too close to it.

He set it on the counting table between the candlestick (which burned without heat) and the taxidermied raven (which occasionally offered unsolicited financial advice), and he looked at it the way he looked at all incoming shipments: with suspicion first, curiosity second, and only then, if the thing survived both, something like respect.

The crate was oak, iron-banded, stamped with the seal of the Cartographers' Consortium out of Greyhollow—which was odd, because the Consortium dealt in maps of places that moved, not in cargo. The stamp was fresh. The wood was old. Someone had wanted this delivery to look routine, and someone else had wanted it to look anything but.

Theo fetched his knife—not the one wrapped in oilcloth beneath the third floorboard of his room, never that one—and slit the bindings. The lid came away with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, packed in straw that had once been someone's hair (he checked; guild policy), sat a glass jar the size of a fist. It glowed faintly, the way coals glow an hour after the fire dies: amber, patient, warm-looking but not warm. A paper label was tied to its neck in red string, and the handwriting on it looped and slanted like a drunk trying to walk a chalk line.

*Borrowed courage. One measure. To be returned by the new moon or interest accrues.*

Below that, smaller, in different ink:

*Do not shake.*

Theo read the label twice, then a third time, because the third reading was where the trouble usually hid. The guild's rules were simple, carved into the lintel over the front door in letters deep enough to catch rainwater: **Never ask what a thing cost. Never ask who paid it. Never speak the old words.** Three rules, and every impossible thing that crossed his counting table tested exactly one of them.

This jar tested the first. He wanted, badly, to know whose courage this had been before it was borrowed—whether "borrowed" meant lent willingly or taken the way weather takes a roof. The label didn't say. The ledger would tell him what it *was*, but ledgers were honest about objects and liars about people, and Theo had learned, ten years ago, in a room he did not let himself remember, that some questions were doors best left painted shut.

He opened the master ledger instead—the big one, bound in green leather gone soft as ear at the spine—and dipped his pen.

"Item," he wrote, and his voice came out rusted from disuse, so he cleared his throat and tried again, whispering now, the way he did all his speaking, as though the words might be overheard by someone he owed. "One jar, borrowed courage, provenance Greyhollow, condition intact. Interest clause: new moon. Storage: shelf nine, behind the salt."

Shelf nine, behind the salt, was where the guild kept everything that might bite. The salt was there because some things couldn't cross a line of it, and because Sable Vane believed that any problem could be delayed, if not solved, by proper shelving.

He lifted the jar. Through the glass, the courage inside shifted—a slow golden turning, like honey stirred by thought rather than spoon. For half a heartbeat, holding it, Theo felt something rise in his own chest. Not the courage itself. Just the memory of having had some, once, long ago, in another life, under another name.

He put the jar down quickly.

That was the trick of working at the Guild of Impossible Things: everything on the shelves remembered being part of someone. The guilt here was a coat that fit anyone who stayed long enough. You catalogued long enough, and you started recognizing yourself in the inventory.

Theo was very good at not recognizing himself. Ten years of practice. He'd gotten so good at it that some mornings, lathering his face at the basin, he caught his reflection looking back with something like disappointment—as if even his mirror knew he was hiding, and disapproved of how well he did it.

* * *

Sable Vane found him at midday, which meant she'd been looking for him since morning.

She had a way of entering rooms that made the rooms feel briefly audited. She was small and grey and precise, with ink stains on her fingers she wore like rings, and eyes that had priced more souls than most priests had blessed. When she stopped at his table and set both hands flat on the wood, Theo did not look up from the ledger. Looking up invited conversation. Conversation invited questions. Questions were the one import the guild ran a permanent surplus of and Theo refused to stock.

"The Greyhollow shipment," she said.

"Catalogued. Shelf nine, behind the salt."

"Mm." Her fingers drummed once. "And the manifest?"

"Forged. Consortium stamp, private hand. Whoever sent it wanted it to look official and wanted the Consortium blamed if it went wrong."

"And you didn't ask who."

"No," Theo said, to the ledger. "Rule one."

"Rule one," Sable repeated, softly, in the tone of a woman filing something away in a drawer that locked. "You're very good at rule one, Theo. Better than anyone I've employed. Sometimes I wonder if you weren't built for it."

He turned a page he hadn't finished reading. "There's a discrepancy in the March entries," he said. "Two vials of bottled sleep entered as three. I'd like to do a full count of the cabinet before month's end."

It was a deflection made of genuine work, which was the only kind Sable respected enough to accept. She studied him a moment longer—he felt it the way you feel sun through a window, warmth with weight behind it—and then she straightened.

"Do the count," she said. "And Theo."

He waited.

"When you're ready to tell me where you learned to hold a pen like a sword, I'll be at my desk. I keep office hours for exactly one question, and it's yours."

"I don't have questions," Theo said.

"Everyone has one question," said Sable Vane. "Most people are just afraid it'll be answered." She left the way she'd come, taking the room's audit with her, and Theo sat in the sudden ordinary silence and hated how much of his relief was just loneliness wearing a practical coat.

Because that was the truth he kept folded small at the bottom of himself, next to the oilcloth and the blade: he was not unhappy here. That was the terrible part. He had a room, a trade, colleagues who respected his silences, a chair that had learned the shape of him. He had routine the way other men had religion, and it served the same purpose—it organized the days into something bearable and promised nothing afterward.

But routine was also a wall, and walls keep things out and in with perfect impartiality. At night, in his narrow bed above the counting room, Theo sometimes lay awake listening to the guild settle around him—the creak of shelf eight, where the homesickness was stored in stoppered bottles; the tick of the clock that ran backward on Thursdays—and he felt the silence in his mouth like a coin held under the tongue. Not the silence of a man with nothing to say. The silence of a man with a great deal he had sworn, on a blade and in blood and in a language older than either, never to say again.

The old words. Rule three. The one rule he'd broken already, once, ten years ago, and the breaking had cost him everything except the habit of counting costs.

He did not think about that. He was excellent at not thinking about that. He picked up his pen and began the count of the sleep vials, and the pen scratched, and the scratch was almost company.

* * *

Evening came down over the guildhall like a lid.

Theo worked late, as he always did, because the ledgers were better company than his own head and because the lamps in the counting room burned steady while the ones upstairs flickered at things only they could see. Around him the guild performed its nightly transformation: Ferro the locksmith clanking shut the grate over the strongroom, whistling through the gap in his teeth a tune that changed keys whenever it passed a locked door; Maren in the map room, arguing quietly with a chart of a coastline that had, since Tuesday, relocated itself forty miles north out of what Maren chose to interpret as spite.

"You should eat," Ferro said, pausing at the counting room door. He was a broad man built like something meant to hold a wall up, with hands scarred in the specific geography of a life spent persuading locks. "Maren made soup. It's either turnip or regret. With her cooking it's genuinely difficult to say."

"I'll eat later."

"You said that last night. And the night before." Ferro leaned on the doorframe. He had the patience of his trade—men who crack safes for a living learn that everything opens eventually, given time and the right pressure, and he applied that philosophy freely to his friends. "You know what I've noticed about you, Theo? Ten years, and I've noticed it maybe a hundred times, and I keep noticing it."

Theo kept writing. "Noticed what."

"That you treat 'later' like a place you're saving up to visit someday." Ferro shrugged. "Soup's on the stove. So am I, if you want talking. Or not-talking. I'm fluent in both."

Something in Theo's chest pulled tight, the way a knot pulls when you test it without meaning to. He had spent ten years making sure no one at the guild knew him, and in doing so had somehow assembled, without ever permitting it, a collection of people who knew him anyway—in the useless, sideways way that mattered more. Ferro knew he took his tea bitter and his silences long. Maren knew he stood at windows during storms. Sable knew whatever Sable knew, which was more than Theo had ever agreed to.

They were not family. He had not asked them to be. They had simply accumulated around him, the way moss accumulates on the north side of a stone, and he had never once had the courage—or perhaps had never once permitted himself the courage—to turn and face the fact of it.

"Later," Theo said again, gently, and Ferro sighed the sigh of a man adding another entry to his own private ledger, and clanked away down the hall.

The counting room settled. The lamp burned. Theo catalogued two more items—a compass that pointed at whoever loved you most (unclaimed, shelf four) and a letter that rewrote itself each time it was read (do not deliver, shelf eleven)—and the work was good, the work was always good, the scratch of the pen and the certainty of columns, a world where everything had a price and a place and a shelf, and nothing ever arrived that couldn't be written down.

Which was why, when the knock came, he knew immediately that something was wrong.

It was past midnight. The guild door was barred, warded, salted, and locked, and nobody knocked on the Guild of Impossible Things after midnight unless they were desperate, lost, or sent. Three slow knocks, spaced like a heartbeat that had forgotten what hearts were for.

Theo set down his pen. He listened. Rain had begun against the windows sometime in the last hour, soft and steady, and beneath it—beneath the knock's echo—he heard something that stopped the breath in his chest and held it there.

Humming. Faint, through the door, half-drowned in rain. A child's humming, tuneless and wandering—but threaded through it, underneath it, syllables that were not a tune at all. Syllables with weight. Syllables that made the lamp flame lean sideways and the ink in his open ledger crawl toward the margin like startled ants.

Ten years ago, in a stone room under a mountain of rules, a man with a voice like a drawn blade had taught Theo those sounds. Had made him repeat them until his gums bled. Had sworn him, last of all his students, to a silence with teeth.

*Never speak them,* the master had said. *And pray you never hear them spoken by one who doesn't know what they are.*

Theo rose from his chair. His body moved before his caution could file an objection, and that alone frightened him more than the knocking. He crossed the counting room, unbarred the inner door, and stood with his hand on the latch, listening to the rain and the humming and the old, old words woven through it like wire through wax.

On the other side of the door, something small was waiting. And every blank page in every ledger in the building seemed, all at once, to lean toward it.
