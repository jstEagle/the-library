## 2. A Knock at Odd Hours

The rain came down like a debt being collected.

Theo Thornbury heard it first as a change in the rhythm against the guild's leaded windows—a shift from the polite patter of evening to something harder, more insistent, the sky above Grayharbor deciding it was done with patience. He did not look up from his work. Looking up, he had learned in ten years of careful living, was how trouble found you. Trouble was drawn to attention the way moths were drawn to flame, and Theo had spent a decade teaching himself to be glass: present, transparent, unremarkable.

He sat in the counting room on the third floor of the Guild of Impossible Things, cataloging the week's acquisitions by candlelight. The ledger before him was bound in gray calfskin and older than the city it sat in, its pages soft as cloth from centuries of handling. Tonight's entries were modest, even by the guild's standards:

*One jar, sealed with wax and wire, containing borrowed courage (three measures, lightly used, returned by a widow who no longer required it).*

*One name, feminine, surrendered voluntarily by a woman fleeing an arranged marriage. Condition: slightly worn at the edges, as names are when they have been called in anger.*

*One memory of a summer afternoon, umber-toned, traded for passage across the Silt Sea. Seller's note appended: "It was worth it."*

Theo wrote each entry in the ledger's patient script, dipped his pen, blotted, turned the page. The work was simple and it was holy, though he would never have used that word aloud. There was a rightness to it—things given their proper weight, their proper place, nothing lost and nothing invented. In a world where memories could be bottled and names could be peeled away like bark, someone had to keep the accounts honest. That was what the guild was for, beneath all its strangeness. Not miracles. Bookkeeping.

The jar of courage sat on the corner of his desk where he'd set it three hours ago, glowing faintly amber whenever the candle guttered, as if it were nervous about the dark. Courage, he'd found, always looked anxious in storage. It wanted to be spent. It rattled faintly against the glass when thunder rolled over the harbor, and Theo found himself resting two fingers on the lid without meaning to, the way one might touch a sleeping animal.

*Three measures, lightly used.* He wondered what she had needed it for. The widow had come in on Tuesday with her shawl pinned at the throat and her chin up, and she had not explained, because the guild's first rule was *never ask*. She had placed the jar on the counter, said "I'm finished with it," and walked out into the rain before anyone could offer tea or condolences or anything else that might constitute a question.

Never ask. Never tell. And above both, carved into the lintel of every door in the building in letters worn nearly smooth: *never speak the old words.*

Theo had not spoken them in ten years. He had not spoken much of anything in ten years, if he was honest, and honesty was a luxury he permitted himself only in the privacy of his own skull, where no ledger could reach it. Silence was not his nature—it had been beaten into him, folded into him like a blade quenched too many times until it held an edge but had forgotten how to be anything else. The other members of the guild knew him as the quiet one, the precise one, Thornbury who kept the books and asked nothing and wanted nothing and was therefore safe. Safe as a locked room. Safe as a blank page.

The candle flickered again. The jar of courage glowed, dimmed, glowed.

And then, through the rain, through the wind worrying at the eaves, Theo heard humming.

It came up through the bones of the building rather than through the air—he felt it in the desk, in the floorboards, in the fillings of his own teeth. A child's hum, tuneless and wandering, the kind of sound a child makes when they are frightened and trying to convince themselves otherwise. But there was something underneath it. Something structured. Syllables rising and falling in patterns too deliberate for nonsense, too old for invention.

Theo's pen stopped moving.

A drop of ink fell from the nib onto the ledger page—and then, impossibly, it moved. Crawled. The black bead of it skittered sideways across the entry for the borrowed courage as though recoiling from something, leaving a thin trembling line like a snail's trail, before pooling at the margin and going still.

Theo stared at it. In ten years, the ink had never once misbehaved. The ledgers were warded, anchored, bound with more care than most cathedrals. Ink did not crawl. Ink obeyed.

The humming grew louder. Closer. It was coming up through the stairwell now, from the direction of the front door, and threaded through it Theo could hear old Maretta the doorkeeper's voice, cracked and querulous: "Now see here, little one, you can't just—the guild's closed after dusk, it's been closed after dusk for sixty years—"

More humming. And beneath it, so faint he might have imagined it, a syllable that made the candleflame bend sideways though there was no draft.

Theo stood up so fast his chair went over backward. He caught it out of habit—one did not make noise in this house, noise drew questions—and stood in the middle of the counting room with his heart slamming against his ribs like something caged.

He knew those syllables.

He had sworn on blood and bone that he would never know them again.

* * *

Sable Vane was in her study on the fourth floor, doing what she did best, which was losing gracefully at solitaire and winning quietly at everything else.

She was a narrow woman built like a question mark, silver-haired, with eyes the color of a storm over slate and a manner that suggested she had already read the last page of whatever book you were currently living in. Sixty years she had run the Guild of Impossible Things, and in that time she had learned that the universe was essentially a marketplace—everything for sale, everything priced, everything eventually coming due. Her genius lay not in acquiring impossible things but in knowing which ones to refuse. Refusal, she had told a hundred apprentices, was the guild's true art. Anyone could take in a haunted mirror. Knowing which haunted mirrors would burn your house down around you—that was mastery.

So when the knocking began, Sable Vane did not move at once.

She listened instead. Three knocks, spaced evenly, then a pause, then three more—not the frantic hammering of the pursued, nor the entitled rapping of the rich. Something between. Something hesitant and desperate at once, the knock of someone who had nowhere else left to try.

Below her, she heard Maretta's voice raised in protest, and beneath Maretta's voice, something else. Something that made the crystal decanter on her sideboard sing a single low, mournful note.

Sable set down her cards.

She had heard a great many strange things in sixty years. She had heard a sword recite poetry, heard a dead man's shadow beg for employment, heard the guild's own walls whisper inventory numbers in their sleep. But she had not heard *that*—those syllables, that cadence—in a very long time, and the last time she had heard it, three of her people had died and one had vanished and she had personally burned the contract that caused it all.

She descended the stairs with the unhurried tread of a woman who refused to be rushed by anything, including catastrophe.

The entrance hall of the guild was a tall, gloomy chamber hung with objects that defied inventory: a clock that ran on regret, a coat rack holding coats that had belonged to people who had never existed, a taxidermied griffin that opened one eye when it liked. Rain lashed the high windows. Lamplight pooled yellow on the flagstones. And in the middle of it all, dripping onto the stones, stood a child.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, maybe nine—half-starved, hollow-eyed, wrapped in a man's coat far too big for her, clutching a canvas satchel to her chest with both arms as though it were the last solid thing in the world. Water streamed off her in rivulets. She was shivering so hard her teeth should have been chattering, should have been, except—

Except her mouth did not open at all.

Maretta hovered nearby, wringing her hands. "She won't speak, mistress. Won't say her name, won't say where she's from. I've asked her everything short of her grandmother's maiden name. She just—" Maretta lowered her voice, uneasy. "She does that. With her mouth shut."

The girl was humming. Standing in a puddle of her own making, soaked and starving and clearly at the end of everything, she hummed those slow, deliberate syllables with her lips pressed together, the sound coming from somewhere deeper than a throat ought to reach. And with every phrase, the hall responded. The lamplight leaned toward her. The dead griffin's feathers rippled. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, Sable could hear the ledgers rustling on their shelves like leaves in wind—hundreds of volumes, stirring restlessly, pages turning themselves to no particular place.

The girl saw Sable and stopped humming. Her eyes—gray, enormous, ancient in a way no child's eyes should be—fixed on the guildmaster with an expression Sable had seen exactly twice before in six decades, and both times it had preceded either a miracle or a funeral.

Then the girl did something stranger than any humming. She opened her satchel, hands shaking, and drew out a piece of paper, thrusting it forward like a shield, like a plea, like a coin offered to a toll-keeper whose price she feared.

Sable took it. The paper was cheap stock, rain-spotted, written in a hasty merchant's hand:

*To whoever keeps the house of strange goods — our daughter cannot speak. Not will not. CANNOT. We sold the thing itself, God forgive us, we sold it to buy grain, and the buyers said it could be got back, said there was a place in Grayharbor that dealt in such things. Please. Her name is Wren. Please help her. We have nothing left to give but this note and our shame.*

No signature. No village named. Famine refugees, then—Ashwinter had scattered them across half the coast like ash indeed, families burned down to essentials, and some had burned down further. Some had sold things that should never have entered any market.

Sable read the note twice. Then she looked at the child—Wren—who watched her with the terrible patience of someone who has already been abandoned once and is braced for the second time.

"Well," Sable said, and her voice was dry as ever, but something underneath it had gone very quiet and very cold. "That explains the ledgers."

"Mistress?" Maretta said.

"Never mind. Get her dry clothes and something hot. And Maretta—" Sable paused, hearing her own caution war with something older. "Put her in the blue room. Not the annex."

Because the annex was where the guild kept things it did not yet understand, and Sable Vane had looked at this child's face and made a decision in the space of a heartbeat that sixty years of policy told her was madness: this was not a thing. This was a girl.

She turned to go back upstairs, to fetch the proper forms, to begin the process of deciding whether the Guild of Impossible Things could afford this particular impossibility—

And found Theo Thornbury standing at the foot of the stairs.

* * *

He had not meant to come down. Every instinct he owned had screamed at him to stay in the counting room, to let the guildmaster handle it, to remain glass. But his feet had carried him down two flights of stairs anyway, drawn by the humming the way iron filings are drawn, and now here he stood, exposed in the lamplight, watching water drip from a child's coat and feeling ten years of careful silence crack down the middle like river ice in spring.

Sable Vane regarded him with narrowed eyes. In sixty years she had never once asked Theo Thornbury about his past, because asking was against the rules and because she had long ago deduced that the answer was dangerous. Now she watched him look at the child, and she watched his face do something it had never done in all his years under her roof.

His face showed recognition.

"Theo," she said mildly. "Something wrong with the books?"

"No, guildmaster." His voice was steady. He had practiced steadiness the way other men practiced swordwork, and it served him now. "The ink was behaving strangely. I came to check the ward-stones."

It was a lie, and a poor one, and Sable Vane let it stand the way one lets a bad card lie on the table—watching, waiting.

The girl was looking at him. Not at Sable, not at Maretta bustling back with towels. At him. Her gray eyes had fixed on Theo with an intensity that made the air between them feel charged, and slowly—hesitantly—she began to hum again. Those same syllables. But different now. Shaped differently. Directed.

Theo's breath left him in a rush.

Because he recognized the pattern. Not merely the language—though it was that, unmistakably, the First Tongue, the old words, the speech that had been old when mountains were pebbles, the language that did not describe things but *bound* them, that made promises into chains and names into anchors. He recognized the specific phrases. The cadence of greeting-and-pleading that every sworn speaker learned before they learned to hold a blade. A formula children were taught in another age, in another order, by masters with ash-grey cloaks and voices like struck bells.

*Speaker greets speaker. Speaker asks shelter of speaker. The road is long and the night is cold.*

She was asking him for sanctuary. In the First Tongue. Hummed through sealed lips by a child who could not speak—and yet somehow spoke more truly than anyone Theo had heard in ten years.

The ink crawled again. He could feel it happening behind him, in the ledger room, hundreds of entries shifting restlessly in their bindings, and he understood suddenly why: the ledgers knew. Books of record always knew. Somewhere in those gray pages was an account of this child, an entry, a transaction, a price paid in grain during a famine year, and the ledgers were straining toward her like dogs toward a whistle.

"Wren," Theo said quietly. Her name, given in the note. Just her name, nothing more, carefully stripped of any power.

The girl's chin trembled. She nodded once, small and fierce, and thrust her hand into her satchel again, producing a stub of charcoal and a scrap of paper. She wrote in a quick, childish scrawl, and held it up:

*You know the words. I saw your face. You KNOW them.*

Theo looked at the paper for a long moment. When he raised his eyes, Sable Vane was watching him with the expression of a woman recalculating sixty years of assumptions.

"I think," Sable said slowly, "that you and I need to talk, Master Thornbury. In my study. Immediately after this child is fed." She glanced at Wren, and her voice softened by a degree so slight that only someone who knew her well would have caught it. "Both of you. It seems the guild may have taken in something impossible tonight after all."

Wren looked from the guildmaster to Theo and back again. Then, deliberately, she folded her scrap of paper, tucked it away, and hummed one final phrase—soft, almost shy, the syllables curling through the lamplit hall like smoke.

Theo translated it without wanting to, the way one translates pain.

*Please don't send me away again.*

Behind them, faintly, from the depths of the ledger room, came the sound of a single book falling open on its shelf—as if something in the guild's records had just turned, of its own accord, to a page it had been waiting ten years for someone to read.
