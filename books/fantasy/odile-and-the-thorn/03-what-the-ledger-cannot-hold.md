## 3. What the Ledger Cannot Hold

The ledger room smelled of tallow and old paper and, faintly, of the river that had never quite been persuaded to stay outside the Guild of Impossible Things. Rain came down in ropes against the high windows, and the gutters above the counting house groaned like something being flayed. Theo Thornbury stood at the long table with a lamp pulled close, a jar of borrowed courage sealed and shelved behind him, and the child's humming still caught in his ears like a burr in wool.

She sat across from him on a stool too tall for her, feet swinging, watching the ledger with the flat patience of someone who had learned that watching was all she was permitted. Wren. That was what Sable Vane had called her, tossing the name out like a coin to a beggar, and the girl had accepted it without expression, which told Theo she understood names were things given and taken by other people.

The ink was crawling again.

He had seen ink do many improper things in his years at the guild — bloom into flowers when a love letter was filed among the debts, bleed backward off the page when a lie was recorded badly enough, weep salt when a widow's grief was entered twice by mistake. But this was different. As the girl hummed her low, syllableless syllables, the entries near her end of the table shivered, letters rising half out of the paper like drowned men breaking surface, then sinking again.

*Stop*, he almost said. *Stop doing that.* And then remembered that she wasn't doing anything. The words were doing it. The words were old.

Theo turned to the shelf behind him and took down the third ledger of the Deep Catalogue, the one bound in grey hide that no one at the guild would name. He did not open it often. Opening it often was how clerks went strange.

"Let's see what you are," he murmured, and immediately regretted speaking aloud, because the girl's eyes came up and fixed on him with an attention that felt less like being watched and more like being read.

She could not answer him. Her mouth opened when he spoke — a small reflexive thing, the way a struck bell hums — but nothing came out. He had noticed it last night, when Sable had fed her stew and asked questions in the gentle roundabout way the guildmaster used on everyone: Where from? Whose child? Who sent you? The girl had answered with her hands, with sketches in spilled salt on the table, with a patience that was itself an answer: *no one sent me; I am from nowhere you can follow; I belong to whoever is holding my voice.*

And she had hummed. Just once, when the fire guttered, she had hummed two notes with shapes inside them, and Theo had felt those shapes in his back teeth, in the scar tissue along his palms where oath-marks had once burned before he seared them smooth. He knew those shapes. He had been taught them as a boy, kneeling on cold stone while a master with smoke-grey eyes made him repeat sounds until the sounds repeated themselves back.

The First Tongue. The language that binds souls. The language of oaths and their keeping, of names and their weights, of bargains struck between what a person is and what a person owes. Dead everywhere except where it mattered most.

A mute child carrying it in her throat like swallowed lightning.

Theo wet his pen. He wrote, in careful clerk's hand, on a scrap of practice parchment: **Can you write?**

She took the pen from him — took it gently, the way one takes a knife from someone who might cut themselves — and wrote beneath: **I can draw better.**

So she drew. She drew the rain first, because it was there, quick looping strokes. Then she drew a mountain, tall and narrow-shouldered, with a door at its base drawn as a small dark mouth. Then she pointed at her own throat, and at the mountain's mouth, and drew a line between them.

"You were taken there," Theo said slowly. "Or sold there."

Her jaw tightened. She crossed out the mountain. She drew instead a field — stubble rows, a dead tree, a hut with its roof caved — and beside it three small figures, stick-thin, holding hands. Two big ones. One little one. Then she drew scales. On one side of the scale she put the little figure. On the other side she drew sack after sack after sack, until the sacks outweighed the child, and the scale tipped.

Theo's hands went cold around the pen.

Grain. A famine trade. A voice for bread. It was the oldest transaction in the world and the cruelest, because no one in it was a monster — only hungry, only desperate, only parents with one child and three mouths' worth of winter and a buyer who paid in flour and took payment in soul.

He had seen such an entry before. Once. Years ago, in his first season at the guild, before he had learned the deep shelves, when he was still a clerk sorting impossible claims from fraudulent ones. A woman had come to the guild door soaked through, holding a contract written on vellum so fine it was nearly transparent, asking if the Guild of Impossible Things could buy back her daughter's laughter, which she had sold — not the whole voice, just the laughter — during the hard year, to a buyer whose agent had worn a mask of polished bone.

Sable had read the contract and gone quiet in the way that meant arithmetic was happening behind his eyes. And Theo, young and careful and already practiced at being nobody, had said the words that had lived under his skin ever since:

"We don't take retrieval cases. We're a house of records, not a house of wars."

The woman had thanked him anyway. That was the worst part. She had thanked him, and folded the transparent contract away, and walked back into the rain toward whatever the Court Beneath the Mountain did with people who came asking after their own children.

*We don't take retrieval cases.*

Theo looked up at the girl drawing sacks of grain with a steady hand, and the guilt rose in him like floodwater finding every crack in a wall he had spent ten years building.

The Deep Catalogue was organized by a logic only its first compiler had understood, and that compiler had been eaten by something in the Weeping Moors in the guild's founding century, so the logic was now a matter of faith rather than knowledge. Theo navigated it the way he navigated everything — patiently, invisibly, touching each spine and listening with his fingers.

Wren followed him between the shelves. She had appointed herself his shadow within a day, and he had not found it in himself to object. When he glanced back she was there, small and watchful, cataloguing him in turn with those grave eyes.

He pulled volumes. *Debts of Flesh and Breath, 3rd Recension. Transactions of the Hungry Years. A Register of Voices, Being an Incomplete Accounting.* The last one he handled with gloves, because voices were sticky things to record and the pages sometimes sang if you turned them too fast.

"Here," he said softly, more to himself than to her. "Famine transactions cluster. Ashwinter, the Long Grey, the Salt Famine. If your voice was traded for grain, it'll be in one of these."

He worked through the night. Wren slept at some point, curled in the great chair by the cold hearth, and woke, and slept again. The lamps burned down. Rain kept up its siege. And Theo moved through the ledgers the way he moved through everything else in his life — thoroughly, silently, and with the growing, dreadful certainty that he was going to find what he was looking for.

He found it an hour past midnight, in *Transactions of the Hungry Years*, under the district marker for the river villages south of the Ashlands.

The entry was brief. Most true things are.

**Ashwinter, day 41 of want. One (1) human voice, entire, being the speech-soul of a female child, age approx. 6 years, surrendered willingly by lawful parents in exchange for: barley, 14 sacks; salt, 2 crocks; passage rights through the toll-roads, winter season. Buyer: the Court. Seller's mark affixed. Condition of goods: unblemished. Note: the First Tongue abides in the goods. Do not teach it to speak.**

*Do not teach it to speak.*

They hadn't needed to worry. The child couldn't have spoken even the common tongue anymore; the voice they'd bought included the language it carried. Everything she might have said had gone down the mountain's dark mouth in a merchant's cart, weighed against fourteen sacks of barley.

Fourteen sacks. Theo did the arithmetic without wanting to. Fourteen sacks was perhaps a village's margin — the difference between most of the children dying and only some of them. Perhaps her parents had fed other people's children with her voice. Perhaps they had told themselves, as desperate people must, that a voice was a small thing next to a life, that she could live whole and wordless, that words were a luxury—

But she had carried the First Tongue. They might not have known that. Or worse — they might have known, and sold her precisely *because* the buyer paid extra for it.

Beside him, Wren stirred in the chair. She was looking at the page upside-down. She couldn't have read the clerk's script — or could she? Children who lose their voices learn to read everything, he thought. Faces. Doors. Ledgers.

Her finger came out and touched the entry, just below the line about the First Tongue, and her face did something terrible and quiet. Not crying. Past crying, maybe, or before it. She looked at the record of herself the way you look at a scar you don't remember earning.

Then she picked up her chalk slate and wrote, in a hand far too controlled for six years old or however old she was:

**Is it still mine?**

Theo's throat closed. Every rule of the guild rose in him like a phalanx of small polite soldiers. *Never ask. Never promise. We are a house of records, not a house of wars. Weigh everything, commit to nothing.*

"Yes," he heard himself say. "It's still yours. It was always yours. They only held it."

The soldiers scattered. The girl looked at him for a long moment, and then she did the thing that undid him more thoroughly than tears would have: she nodded, once, businesslike, and closed her eyes, and went to sleep — trusting him with it, the way you can only trust someone if you believe they've already decided.

Theo sat in the lamplight with the ledger open and his hands flat on either side of it, and let the guilt finish its work.

Because here was the truth he had kept folded away for ten years, pressed flat like a flower in a book: the guild *could* help people like her. The rules against retrieval cases weren't doctrine. They weren't law. They were Sable Vane's insurance policy, and Theo's convenience, and the collective cowardice of a house full of clever people who had learned that the safest way to survive impossible things was to file them.

And he had been their best clerk. He had refused that woman in the rain with a clear conscience and a steady voice, and she had thanked him, and somewhere beneath the mountain her daughter's laughter hung on a bone hook among hundreds of others, gathering dust that was not dust.

How many entries like Wren's were in these shelves? He looked down the dark aisle of the Deep Catalogue and imagined the registers breathing. Hundreds. Hundreds of voices, names, years, faces, sold in famines and wars and ordinary bad winters, all of it legal, all of it witnessed, all of it *filed*. The guild had never stolen anything. The guild had only ever kept the receipts.

Wren slept on. Outside, the rain eased at last, and somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked three times, the way pipes do when houses are settling — or when they're thinking.

Theo closed the ledger gently, so as not to wake her, and made his decision the way he made all his decisions: quietly, completely, and with the full knowledge that he would pay for it.

In the morning, he would go to Sable Vane and ask for the retrieval contract. He would use the word *ask*, though both of them would know what it really was. And the guildmaster would look at him over those spectacles and remind him of ten years of careful invisibility, and offer him a choice shaped exactly like a trap.

Theo banked the lamps. In the dark, the girl breathed slow and even, and the ledgers around them seemed, very faintly, to lean closer — hundreds of unclaimed voices waiting in their grey bindings, as if they too had heard what he'd said.

*It was always yours.*

Somewhere below the city, beneath stone older than stone, something with a ledger of its own marked the debt, and began, patiently, to wait for him.
