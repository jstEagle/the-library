## 4. The Guildmaster's Bargain

The rain had not stopped in three days, and Theo had begun to suspect it never intended to.

He heard it through the guildhouse walls as a constant whispering—water finding its way along the old beams, pooling in the gutters above the counting room, dripping somewhere behind the shelves where they kept the weathered things no one wanted to buy back. In his first years at the Guild of Impossible Things, that sound had kept him awake. Now it was as familiar as his own pulse, which was to say: something he noticed only when it changed.

This morning it had changed, because Wren was awake before him.

He found her in the counting room, sitting cross-legged on the floor between the ledger cabinets with a stub of charcoal in her fist and a sheet of ruined vellum spread before her. She drew the way other children breathed—constantly, urgently, without thought for how it looked. This morning's work was a mountain drawn from below: a great dark triangle looming over stick figures whose mouths were open circles. She had scratched out the mouths. All of them. Scratched them into black smudges, over and over, until the charcoal tore the vellum.

"Good morning," Theo said, because he always said it, even though she could not answer, even though every greeting was a small coin dropped down an empty well.

She looked up at him. Her eyes were the grey of the moors in winter, and they held his the way a held breath holds itself—not because it is comfortable, but because letting go would cost too much.

Then she hummed.

It was barely anything. Three notes, four—a fragment of melody with words buried inside it like seeds in frozen ground. But the ink in the open ledger on the cabinet beside her stirred. He saw it happen: the entries rippling as if a finger had been dragged beneath the page, the careful guildhand script shivering out of its columns and settling crooked. One entry—the sale of a memory of first snow, purchased by a widow in Harrowgate—bled sideways across the margin as if trying to get closer to her.

Wren stopped humming the instant she saw his face. She clapped both hands over her mouth, which was an old habit of hers, he'd realized. A mute child protecting the world from a voice she didn't understand—or perhaps protecting herself from what using it would remind her she'd lost.

"It's all right," Theo said quietly. He crouched beside her, careful of his knees, careful of everything, the way he had been careful for ten years. "The ledgers don't mind. They're just... listening."

That was true, and it was also the most dangerous sentence he had spoken aloud in a decade, because if the ledgers were listening, then so was everything the ledgers were connected to. And the First Tongue did not drift through the world unnoticed. It rang. Somewhere far beneath the Weeping Moors, under the roots of the mountain the maps refused to name twice, he suspected something had already turned its head toward the sound of a child's half-dreamed syllables.

He smoothed the ledger flat with his palm. The widow's entry crept reluctantly back into line.

*You once refused this kind of case,* said the voice in his head that sounded like his old master, dry as chalk dust. *You swore you'd never again be the man who went looking for stolen souls. Look at you now. Sitting on the floor with your heart already halfway sold.*

Theo ignored it, the way he ignored the whispering rain, the way he ignored the oath-blade wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards of his room, the way he ignored every part of himself that had ever mattered. That was the discipline. That was the whole of it, really—ten years of practiced deafness, worn smooth as a river stone.

Sable Vane chose that moment to end it.

* * *

The guildmaster's office occupied the tower's topmost room, which meant it collected all the weather the sky had to offer. Rain lashed the leaded windows. Wind worried the shutters with long patient fingers. Sable sat behind a desk built from the hull of a ship that had, according to guild legend, been impossible to sink right up until the moment it sank, and she watched Theo come up the stairs with the expression of a woman who had already written this conversation in her head and found it wanting.

"You've been feeding her," Sable said.

"I've been feeding a guest."

"She's been here six days." Sable's voice was low and even, a blade kept in felt. "Six days, Thornbury. She sleeps in a storeroom. She eats at our table. Ferro has made her a lockbox for her drawings—did you know that? A little iron box with a key. He says it's so 'the rain can't read them.' The man hasn't smiled that much since his daughter left. Maren has redrawn three maps to show her, maps of places that don't hold still, and the child follows them like scripture." Sable folded her hands. "And you have catalogued forty-one items this week while pretending none of it is happening."

"The rules say we don't ask," Theo said. "I'm not asking."

"The rules also say we don't keep strays who hum in dead languages." She rose. Behind her, rain ran down the window glass in braided ropes. "Do you know what came through the post yesterday? Not the usual nonsense—not love knots or borrowed courage or petitions to un-say a wedding vow. A writ. Official seal, grey wax, the kind they press with the Signet of Registry." She picked up a paper from her desk and held it without offering it. "An inquiry regarding an unregistered speaker, last seen entering the Guild of Impossible Things. They know she's here, Theo. They don't know her name, because she doesn't have one anyone wrote down—but they know the shape of what walked through our door on Thursday night, soaked to the bone and speaking the language that binds souls."

Theo stood very still. Stillness was his oldest skill; he had learned it before he learned letters. But beneath the stillness his blood had gone loud.

"The Wardens of Registry don't issue inquiries," he said. "They issue sentences."

"They issue inquiries when they want the paperwork to look clean," Sable agreed. "Which means someone upstairs wants this handled gently, which means someone upstairs wants her *delivered*, which means—" she let the paper fall back to the desk, "—you know exactly what happens to unregistered speakers, because you were almost one yourself, once, before I bought your silence along with your labor. So. Here is where we find ourselves."

She came around the desk. Up close, Sable Vane's age showed only in her eyes, which had the layered depth of a well—dark water over older dark water over, presumably, a bottom.

"I am going to offer you a choice," she said. "Not because the choice is real. The writ exists whether you like either option or not. But because you are the last swordspeaker sworn to silence in these provinces, and because I have watched you spend ten years being no one, and because I am, despite considerable evidence to the contrary, a sentimental woman. So."

She laid two objects on the desk between them.

The first was the writ itself, grey wax gleaming, the Registry's signet stamped deep: a closed mouth above an open hand. Hand her over, it said, in the language of bureaucracy, which is the coldest language there is. Deliver the asset. File the transfer. The Court Beneath the Mountain keeps meticulous accounts, and the Registry keeps them company.

The second object was a contract blank—one of the guild's own, heavy cream paper, the header reading RETRIEVAL OF SOLD PROPERTY in the old formal script. Blank but for one line already filled in, in Sable's sharp hand:

*One (1) human voice, belonging to Wren, age approximately eight, sold Ashwinter of the famine year to the buyer listed as THE COURT.*

"The transaction is legitimate," Sable said. "By the laws that govern such sales—and I know you know those laws better than any living soul, because you were trained in them—it is binding. A voice freely sold cannot simply be taken back. It must be *bought*, or bargained, or won by terms the seller acknowledges. Which means a retrieval contract. Which means a contractor. Which means," and here she paused, and the well-eyes fixed on him, "someone in this guild putting their name to paper and walking toward the one place in the world where names are currency and oaths are coin, carrying the single most collectible oath-name in the northern provinces."

"My name isn't worth anything," Theo said. It came out flat, automatic, a coin he'd polished so long its face had worn away.

"Don't lie to me in my own tower." Sable's voice didn't rise. It lowered, which was worse. "Your oath-name is the anchor of the old order. There are collectors under that mountain who would trade a province for it. You are not invisible, Theo Thornbury. You are *expensive*, and expensive things get found. The moment you sign that contract and cross the moor road, every debt-broker, every bone-noble, every quill-wielding horror in the Court will smell you coming like smoke."

"Then the second option," Theo said.

"Hand her to the Wardens." Sable nodded slowly. "Yes. Let me describe it, since you were kind enough to let me start. They'll take her to the Registry house in Corvess. There will be papers. There will be a hearing, very brief, conducted in a language she can't speak and can't hear, because they'll bind her jaw with a silence-bridle within the hour—they're not fools, whatever else they are. Then she'll be assessed. Value assigned. And then she'll be sold." She tilted her head. "Probably back to the Court, actually. Isn't that elegant? The same destination, either way. Except in this version, she goes in a cage with her hands tied and a bit in her mouth, and in the other version, she goes with you."

"And if I refuse both?"

"Then I choose for you, and I'll tell you honestly, Theo, I don't know which way I'd choose, and I hate not knowing." For the first time something cracked in her voice—a hairline fracture, quickly sealed. "Six days. That child has been here six days and Ferro is whittling her toys and Maren has stopped drinking alone in her room and I—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "I have run this guild for thirty years on the principle that we deal in small miracles and never in people. Someone sold that girl's voice for grain. Grain, Theo. A season of bread for a lifetime of silence. And now everyone else in the world wants to finish the transaction."

The rain filled the silence that followed, patient and grey.

Theo looked at the contract. The cream paper seemed to glow faintly against the dark wood, the way all guild contracts glowed when they were true—which was the guild's one piece of honest magic: a true contract could not lie about itself. *RETRIEVAL OF SOLD PROPERTY.* Below the header, the signature line waited, empty as an unfilled grave.

Ten years ago, he had refused a case like this. He could still see the woman—Marta, a tanner's widow from the fen-edge villages—standing in the counting room with a child's shoe in her hands, asking him to go south and buy back her son's laughter, sold to a carnival that traded in extracted joys. He had been two years silent then, newly sworn, terrified of his own shadow and the power coiled inside it. He had told her the guild didn't handle soul-work. He had watched her leave. He had gone back to cataloguing jars of borrowed courage and telling himself that survival was a virtue.

Two winters later he'd heard the boy had died—carnival accident, or so the story went, though stories about the soul-trade always ended badly because the trade itself was the accident. Theo had not slept for a month afterward. Then he had slept, because that was the terrible mercy of guilt: the body forgives what the conscience cannot.

His master's voice again, chalk-dry: *A swordspeaker's oath is not silence, fool. It is a held breath. You hold it until the moment it must be spent. Spend it on nothing, and it was never a breath at all—just a habit.*

"You're staring at the paper like it owes you money," Sable said.

"It does," Theo said. "Everyone does. That's rather the point of the place we'd be going."

He reached out and took the contract. The paper was warm. Guild magic, recognizing intent, or maybe just his own blood finally answering a question it had been asked six days ago, on a rain-lashed night, when a knock came at odd hours and a child stood in the doorway with no voice and eyes like the whole grey world.

"If I sign this," he said, "the Registry's writ becomes void. Retrieval contractors supersede civil transfer. Old law, but law."

"Older than their signet," Sable confirmed. "They'll be furious. Fury makes them thorough. You'll have maybe a week before they decide a contract signed by an anonymous guildsman is worth overriding."

"Then I'll need to not be anonymous." He said it lightly, and the lightness cost him more than the sentence. "I'll need the name I swore never to use. The oath-name. On paper, in front of witnesses, spoken aloud if the terms require it."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind gave up on the shutters and tried the chimney instead, sending a soft groan down through the tower's bones.

"Do you understand what that means?" she asked. "Every collector of the old order will feel it. Every debt that's been waiting ten years for you to become findable—it comes due. Not just theirs. *Yours.*"

"Yes."

"And you'll do it anyway. For a child who cannot say thank you."

"She said thank you," Theo said. "Once. By accident. Half-asleep, the second night. One word." He had not told anyone this. The word had rattled the windowpanes and made the lamp flame stand straight up like a soldier at attention, and Wren had woken herself with it, and pressed her hands to her mouth, and wept without any sound at all, which was somehow the worst thing he had ever witnessed. "It was the most beautiful thing I've ever heard. And they took it. They took it for *grain*."

He dipped the pen. The nib hovered over the signature line, trembling—not with fear, he realized, or not only fear, but with something adjacent to it, some emotion wearing fear's coat, the way dawn wears the night's cold. Resolve, maybe. Or grief, finally given somewhere to go.

"Thornbury," Sable said, and for once used the whole name, the dangerous name, the name that was a key. "When you sign, you stop being safe. Do you hear me? Whatever happens under that mountain—you stop being safe, forever. There is no coming back from a spoken oath-name. The old order doesn't forget a face once it's paid for."

"No," Theo agreed. "But she'll have hers back. That seems a fair exchange. It's more than anyone offered me."

He signed.

The ink caught fire—briefly, silently, a ribbon of gold flame running along the letters and sinking into the paper, the guild's truth-magic sealing the contract the way wax seals a letter. Somewhere in the counting room below, he knew, forty-one ledgers had just turned their pages at once. Somewhere far beneath the Weeping Moors, something vast and patient had just felt a bell ring that had not rung in ten years.

Sable took the contract, examined the signature, and exhaled through her nose—the closest thing to prayer she permitted herself.

"So," she said. "The last swordspeaker walks again. Gods help us all, and gods help the Court most of all." She tucked the contract into a leather sleeve and handed it back to him. "You'll need allies. You'll need supplies. You'll need—" she hesitated, and her gaze dropped meaningfully toward the floor, toward the storerooms below, toward the one thing in this building Theo had spent a decade pretending wasn't wrapped in oilcloth beneath his bed. "You'll need the blade."

Theo's throat tightened around ten years of silence.

"I know," he said.

"And Theo?" Sable turned back to the rain-lashed window, her silhouette edged in grey light, suddenly looking every year of her age and then some. "Whatever they offer you down there—and they will offer you *everything*, that's what they do, that's how they've lasted—remember that the Court doesn't take bribes. It takes trades. Everything has a price on the page. Make sure you know yours before they write it in."

He descended the tower stairs slowly, contract against his chest, the rain still whispering, the ledgers still stirring. In the counting room, Wren looked up from her drawings. Ferro stood behind her with a half-carved wooden bird forgotten in his hands. Maren leaned in the doorway, map-cylinders under her arm, watching him with the expression of someone who reads roads for a living and had just seen one open where no road had been.

Theo knelt, took the charcoal from Wren's fingers, and on her ruined sheet of vellum—beneath the mountain with its scratched-out mouths—he drew a single line. A road. Running down, and down, into the dark.

"We're going to get your voice back," he said.

Wren stared at the drawing. Then at him. And slowly, deliberately, she reached out and closed his hand around the charcoal, and held it there, and looked at him with those grey moorland eyes until he understood:

She wasn't thanking him.

She was asking how much it would cost him—and telling him she would help pay.
