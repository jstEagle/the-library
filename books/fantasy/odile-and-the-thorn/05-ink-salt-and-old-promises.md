## 5. Ink, Salt, and Old Promises

The oath-blade had not been touched in ten years, and it knew.

Theo felt its awareness before he saw it—a pressure against the back of his thoughts, like a name called from another room. It lived beneath the floor of the guild's salt cellar, wrapped in oilcloth and buried under four decades of crystallized neglect, because the Guild of Impossible Things kept its salt where no one would ever look for anything else. Salt was honest. Salt was cheap. Nobody stole salt, and nobody knelt in the cellar scraping at flagstones on the off chance that a swordspeaker's last relic lay beneath them.

He went down at the hour between third bell and fourth, when Sable Vane was asleep and Wren was—God willing—asleep too, curled in the cot they'd given her beside the map room, breathing the small breaths of a child who had learned that sleep could be taken away if you made noise while having it.

A candle stub. A pry bar disguised as a cellar tool. His own heartbeat, loud as a drum in the stone quiet.

The flagstone came up easier than he expected, as though the floor had only been waiting for him to ask. Beneath it, the oilcloth had gone stiff and amber with age, and when he peeled it back the candlelight found steel so dark it seemed to drink the flame rather than reflect it.

The blade was called Lament—not by him, never by him aloud, but by the order that had forged it, back when there was an order. Swordspeakers did not carry weapons, properly speaking. They carried *arguments*. The old words were the argument; the steel was only where the argument lived when the mouth was closed. Every oath-blade held the full litany of its speaker's vows, pressed into the metal at the moment of swearing, and every blade remembered.

He reached out and touched the flat of it with one finger.

It whispered.

Not words, not yet—it was too polite for that, or too cruel, he'd never decided which. It whispered the way wind whispers through a keyhole, the way a sleeping person murmurs a name that isn't the name of the person beside them. The sound crawled up through his fingerbone and settled behind his teeth, and ten years of silence cracked along a seam he hadn't known was there.

*You came back,* the blade said, in the voice of everything he had buried.

"I didn't," Theo said. His voice came out rusted. He almost never spoke down here; there was no one to hear. "I'm borrowing you. There's a child upstairs who can't say her own name."

*There were children before,* the blade said. *You walked past their entries in the ledger. You told yourself the ledger was not your concern.*

"Yes." He lifted it out of the earth. It was lighter than memory suggested, or he was weaker than memory insisted. "And I'm done telling myself that."

The whisper changed then—softened, or sharpened, he couldn't tell. It began to say something else, something long, rhythmic, familiar in the way a scar is familiar, and he recognized the opening syllables of his own oath before he could stop himself from recognizing them. The first line. The one that began *I, who was named—*

He clapped his palm over the flat of the blade as if silencing a mouth.

"Not here," he breathed. "Not yet. When I speak it, I'll speak all of it, and I'll speak it where it does some good. Until then, be quiet. Be quiet, old friend. We're both of us out of practice."

The blade subsided into a hum, low and continuous, like a hive deciding whether to swarm. He wrapped it—not in the oilcloth this time but in a strip of guild linen, the kind used for wrapping fragile consignments—and carried it up through the dark, past the salt bins, past the door with its iron hinge he oiled monthly so it would never sing.

At the top of the stairs, he paused. Above him, through the floorboards, he could hear the guild at rest: Ferro snoring in the workshop loft with the particular thunder of a man who slept beside his tools the way other men slept beside wives. Maren's room was silent, but Maren's room was always silent; she kept hours that belonged to no clock. And beneath it all, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, Wren's humming—the First Tongue reduced to melody, syllables without their teeth, a language hummed the way a bird sings in a cage it has stopped trying to leave.

Theo stood on the stairs with a whispering blade under his arm and understood, with the clarity that comes only at ugly hours, that he had already made his choice. Sable's bargain had been a formality. The contract had signed itself the night a mute child knocked on their door and the ink in the ledger had crawled toward her like dogs toward a whistle.

Now he only had to gather the people foolish enough to come with him.

* * *

Ferro the locksmith owed Theo a debt that neither of them discussed, which was how Ferro preferred all his debts.

Theo found him in the workshop at dawn, which was unusual—Ferro's mornings belonged to tea and grievance, in that order—but the man was already bent over his bench, working a lock mechanism no bigger than a walnut, his great scarred hands moving with a delicacy that never stopped being strange to see. Ferro had hands like shovels and the patience of a saint being flayed.

"You're up early," Theo said.

"Couldn't sleep." Ferro didn't look up. "Kept thinking about the girl. About what's under the mountain." The pick in his hand turned a quarter-inch. "Sable told me, you know. Last night, after you went to bed. Told me everything. The Court. The contract. What she offered you."

"Of course she did."

"She wanted me to talk you out of it." Now Ferro looked up. He had a face built for scowling, all brow and jaw, weathered like a dock piling. "I told her I'd think about it. That was eight hours ago and I've thought about it plenty, and here's what I've got." He set the pick down with a small precise click. "Ten years ago, I had a debt to a man in Lowmarsh who collected with fire. You know the story. Everyone thinks they know the story."

"I don't collect stories," Theo said quietly. "Guild rule."

"No. You don't." Ferro's eyes held his, and there was something underneath the gruffness, something old and carefully maintained, like paint over rot. "That's why it's you I owe. You never asked. Not once, not in ten years, not even when I came to your door with burned hands and no eyebrows and a story I wouldn't tell. You just wrote me into the guild roster and taught me which shelves creak and never once—" his voice caught, recovered, "—never once looked at my hands like they were a question."

"Ferro—"

"The vault under the mountain." Ferro stood, joints cracking like green wood. "I've heard about the Court's locks. Bone-and-oath mechanisms, they say. Locks that taste the hand that turns them. Locks that need a thief who's got nothing left to lose, because they take something from everyone who opens them." He flexed those shovel-hands, and Theo watched him decide something. "Well. I've got less than nothing. I've got a daughter-shaped hole where a daughter used to be, and a debt I've been carrying around like a stone in my boot, and a friend who never asked me a single question in ten years." He stuck out one enormous hand. "So. Where do we start?"

Theo took the hand. It swallowed his.

"There's a tollhouse on the moor road," he said. "And after that, a door that hasn't been opened honestly in three hundred years."

"Good," said Ferro. "Honest doors are boring."

But as they gathered tools—picks and tension wrenches and a vial of acid disguised as vinegar—Theo watched the locksmith work, and saw what he had always seen and never let himself see: that Ferro checked each tool twice, that he wrapped his burned hands in fresh linen with the care of ritual, that he glanced once, quickly, at the small tin box on his shelf where he kept the things he could not bring himself to throw away and could not bear to open.

Everyone in the guild was paying for something. That was, Theo suspected, the real admission price. Impossible things cost impossible amounts, and the guild filled up with people who had already paid.

* * *

Maren was harder.

Maren Ashvael, cartographer of places that move, kept an office at the top of the guildhouse in a room that was, depending on the day, larger or smaller than the room below it. She was there now, naturally, standing at her great table with its living maps—charts drawn on skin that shifted, ink that migrated overnight to record roads that had wandered, coastlines that sulked and reformed. She was a narrow woman, gray-streaked, with eyes the color of weather coming, and she did not turn around when Theo entered.

"I heard the blade," she said.

Theo stopped in the doorway. "You couldn't have. It whispered. It only whispers."

"To you, maybe." She turned. In her hand she held a compass whose needle spun slowly, aimlessly, drunk. "My instruments have been misbehaving since midnight. The needle points at the cellar. All night. So either the north pole moved into our salt stores, or you went digging." She set the compass down. "Which is it, Thornbury?"

The name landed like a blow. No one used it. No one *knew* it—he'd scrubbed it from every document, paid three separate forgers to unmake it, lived a decade as the quiet man with the ledgers. But Maren drew maps of places that moved, and perhaps names were places too, and perhaps she had simply followed his name home the way water finds a cellar crack.

"How long have you known?" he asked.

"Since the day you arrived. Names leak. Especially sworn ones—they want to be spoken, so they seep." She came around the table. "I never said anything because asking questions is against guild law and because I assumed whatever you were running from would eventually catch you, and I didn't want to be standing next to you when it did." Her mouth twisted. "Then a child knocked on our door humming the First Tongue, and Sable handed you a retrieval contract, and now the catching is scheduled. So. Standing next to you it is."

"Why?" Theo asked. It was the question he'd spent ten years training himself not to ask anyone, and here it was, aimed at him. "Maren, this isn't a mapping job. The Weeping Moors rearrange themselves for liars. The Court collects oaths like coin. If we go, we go into a place where everything you are can be priced and sold. Why would you—"

"Because I mapped the Court once." Her voice went flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice they'd fallen through before. "Twenty years ago. Before your time. A commission—I never learned who paid. I charted the corridors, the bone cathedral, the vaults. Three weeks inside." She held up her right hand, and in the lamplight Theo saw what he had always taken for a birthmark: a pale tracery across the fingers, fine as frost ferns, shaped almost like writing. "They let me leave. They always let the cartographer leave. Because a map is an invitation, and they wanted someone to come back someday, following my lines, carrying something valuable." She smiled without any humor in it at all. "Congratulations, Thornbury. You're the something valuable. And I'm the invitation."

"That's why Sable chose you for the roster," Theo said slowly. "That's why a cartographer of moving places ended up in a guild of misfits."

"Sable collects us. Broken instruments still play; you just have to learn which notes they've lost." Maren was already pulling charts from her racks—moorland surveys, deep-road fragments, a scrap of vellum that writhed gently like a captured fish. "Here's what I know that the Court doesn't know I know: there's a way in that isn't on their guest lists. An old pilgrim road, pre-Court, from when the mountain was holy instead of hungry. It moves. Of course it moves—all the old roads move—but it moves *in patterns*, and I've spent twenty years charting the patterns." She spread the vellum flat and pinned its corners, and it stilled, sulking. "I can get us to the door. Whether the door remembers me kindly—that's a different fee."

"And the tollhouse?" Theo asked. "On the moor road. The collectors there take payment in memories."

Maren was quiet a moment. Outside, rain had begun again, soft against the high windows, and somewhere below Wren's humming threaded up through the floors, patient as water.

"I'll pay what I pay," Maren said finally. "We all will. That's the arithmetic of this place, isn't it? Everything costs what it costs, and the poor pay in themselves." She rolled the charts and bound them with cord, brisk again, weather-eyed. "Be at the stable yard at dusk. Bring the girl. Bring the blade. And Theo—" she used no name at all, but he heard it anyway, "—when you speak your oath under that mountain, and you will, because men like you always do, exactly once, at the worst possible moment—make sure it's worth what it costs. I've seen what happens when it isn't. I map the aftermath."

* * *

Dusk came down over the guildhouse like a lid.

Theo stood in the stable yard with Lament wrapped in linen under his coat, whispering its low continuous whisper against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and watched his improbable company assemble. Ferro arrived first, hauling a trunk of tools, complaining about the weather with the fluency of a man praying. Then Maren, charts slung across her back, compass in hand, its needle now pointing steadily south-southeast—toward the mountain, toward the Court, toward everything.

And then Wren, in a coat two sizes too big that Sable had dug from the guild's donation chest, her small face grave above the collar, her sketchbook clutched to her chest like a shield.

She stopped in front of Theo and opened the book to a page she'd prepared. She had drawn it sometime that afternoon, he realized—drawn it with the terrible precision of a child who understood more than she could say. Four figures on a road. One large with tools. One thin with a roll of paper. One small with a book. And one tall figure whose face was left blank, holding something long and wrapped.

She looked up at him. Pointed at the blank-faced figure. Then pointed at him.

"That's me," Theo said.

Wren nodded. Then she frowned, took up her charcoal, and added something to the blank face: a single line, curved, deliberate. A smile. She held it up for his inspection, defiant, as if daring him to argue.

Behind him, Ferro snorted. "Kid's got your number already."

"The kid," Theo said, "has known me for three days."

"Long enough." Maren swung up onto the cart seat and shook the reins. "Roads won't wait. The moors especially—they get ideas after dark."

The cart rolled out through the guild gate, past the wardstones, past the boundary where the city's noise thinned into the moor's long silence. Theo sat in the back among the trunks and charts with a whispering blade against his heart and a child drawing in a book beside him, and he thought about ledgers—about how every entry balanced, how every debt found its collector, how somewhere beneath the mountain a page waited with a child's voice written on it in a hand like frost.

Lament stirred against his ribs. And this time, softly, in the voice of his dead master, it began—very quietly, very patiently—to teach him the first line of his oath again.

*I, who was named—*

"Not yet," Theo murmured. "Soon."

Ahead, the Weeping Moors rose out of the dark like a country remembering grief, and the road beneath the wheels began, very slightly, to move.
