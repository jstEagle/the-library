## 6. The Road That Remembers

The Weeping Moors began where the map ended, which Maren insisted was the only honest way to enter them.

"Every cartographer who's charted these moors has lied," she said, unrolling a sheet of vellum across the tailgate of Ferro's borrowed cart. "So the moors learned lying back. Now they rearrange themselves for anyone who speaks an untruth. Walk honestly, and the road stays put. Lie, and you'll find yourself walking in circles until your boots rot off."

Theo studied her map. It was less a map than a confession—every route marked with small annotations of shame. *Here I swore I knew the ford and did not.* *Here I told a dying man he would live.* The ink around those annotations had bled and blurred, as though the paper itself had wept.

"And Wren?" he asked quietly.

Maren's eyes went to the girl, who sat among the luggage with a stub of charcoal she'd found somewhere, drawing on the back of an old invoice. She drew the way other children breathed—constantly, compulsively, as though silence were a room too small to hold her.

"The moors don't care about charcoal," Maren said. "But if she speaks—" She didn't finish. None of them liked finishing sentences that ended in what Wren might do.

Ferro clicked his tongue at the mule, and they rolled onto the first black flagstone of the moor road. Rain came down in a fine grey gauze, not falling so much as hanging, beading on everything like the land itself was sweating out its grief. The peat hissed softly under the wheels. Somewhere to the left, water moved without wind.

Theo rode at the rear, beside Wren, and watched her draw.

She had filled three invoices already. The first was a house—a crooked little house with smoke curling from a chimney, two tall figures in the doorway, one small figure between them. The second was the same house, but the door stood open and the figures were gone, and the smoke curled wrong, sideways, like it was fleeing. The third was just a jar. A jar with something inside it that she'd shaded so heavily the paper had torn.

He recognized the shading. He'd cataloged enough jars in the guild's cellars to know desperation when he saw it rendered in charcoal.

"Wren." He kept his voice low, pitched under the rain. "When you want to tell me something, you can show me. Draw it. I'll understand. I'm good at reading things that don't say what they mean." He almost smiled. "I've been one for ten years."

She looked up at him. Her eyes were the color of shallow water over slate, and they held the particular stillness of a child who has learned that opening her mouth is the most dangerous thing she can do. Then she turned the invoice over and drew, quickly, a stick figure with a sword, standing next to a smaller stick figure. Between them she drew a line. Not a path. A thread.

He looked at it for a long time.

"Yes," he said finally. "That too."

* * *

They made camp that night on a shelf of dry stone above a tarn that reflected no stars. Ferro built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and Maren checked her map against the landmarks—which had, mercifully, stayed where she'd left them—and pronounced their honesty sufficient.

Wren did not sleep. Theo found her at the edge of the firelight, drawing again, and this time the picture stopped him cold.

It was a bridge. A long stone bridge spanning a gorge, drawn with the careful repetition of a child who has seen a thing many times and needs to be sure of it. Under the bridge, in the gorge, she had drawn shapes. Rounded shapes. Heads, maybe. Or stones that wanted to be heads.

"That's ahead of us," Theo said. "The Gallowmere Span. How do you know it?"

She shrugged—the universal gesture of children who have answered enough questions. But then she tapped the drawing, twice, and pointed east, toward the road they'd take in the morning. And then she did something else. She cupped her hands around her mouth—not blowing into them, not speaking, just holding them there, the shape of a word that never came out—and her whole body shuddered.

Theo crouched beside her. Up close he could see the fine tremor in her fingers, the charcoal worn down to a nub between them.

"You felt it," he said. "The bridge. Something under it."

She nodded once, fast, the way people nod when they want the subject closed.

He should have let it close. Ten years of practice had taught him exactly how to let things close. But he heard himself say, "When we get there, you stay behind me. And if you feel anything—if the words come up your throat—you squeeze my hand. Twice. Can you do that?"

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she took his hand, turned it palm up, and squeezed twice: firm, deliberate, unmistakable. Then she squeezed four times, rapid, and pointed at herself with a look of fierce inquiry.

"Four means...?" He searched her face. "Four means you can't hold it?"

She nodded. Then, after a moment, she held up one finger and looked at him with an expression of such naked pleading that his chest ached.

"One means you need to say it anyway."

He exhaled slowly. "Then we'll hope for two."

* * *

The Gallowmere Span appeared out of the rain near midday, exactly as she had drawn it: a long spine of grey stone arching over a gorge so deep the bottom was a rumor. The mist below moved. Theo chose not to think about what moved it.

Ferro went first, testing each flagstone with his weight before committing, the habit of a man who had spent his life trusting locks more than floors. Maren followed with the mule, murmuring to it in the steady monotone she used for nervous animals and nervous maps. Theo brought up the rear with Wren's hand locked in his.

Halfway across, she squeezed twice.

He stopped. "What is it?"

She pointed down. Down through the gaps between the flagstones, down into the moving mist. And then she pressed her free hand flat against her sternum, and he saw it happen—the swallow, the convulsive effort of a throat working around something rising, the way her shoulders climbed toward her ears.

"Hold it," he said quietly. "Whatever it is, hold it. Breathe through your nose. Count with me—one, two—"

Her mouth opened.

"No—" 

But it wasn't a scream. It wasn't even loud, really. It was a single syllable, round and resonant, in a language that predated the stone under their feet and the grief under theirs, and the bridge *heard* it.

The sound left her like a bird leaving a cage, and for half a heartbeat the world was perfectly, terribly clear: every drop of rain suspended, every breath held, the syllable hanging in the air with the weight of a verdict.

Then the bridge shattered.

Not all at once. It broke the way ice breaks on a pond—a crack racing out from beneath Wren's feet, splitting flagstones, buckling the span in a rolling wave that ran both directions toward the shores. Ferro shouted. The mule screamed. Maren grabbed its halter and hauled, and the three of them ran—or tried to run—across a road that was coming apart beneath them like a loaf breaking.

Theo caught Wren around the waist and threw them both forward as the section behind them dropped away into the gorge. They landed hard on the far shore, skidding across wet gravel, and behind them the last of the Gallowmere Span folded inward and fell, taking the mist and whatever lived in it down into the dark with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

Silence. Rain. The ragged breathing of four survivors and one mule.

Wren was shaking. Not from cold. She stared at her own hands as though they belonged to someone else, someone dangerous, and her face was doing something Theo recognized from his own mirror, years ago: the slow collapse of a person realizing what they are.

"It's all right," he said, and the lie nearly cost them another bridge, because the moors heard it and the ground beneath them shifted a full yard to the left before he corrected himself. "It's not all right," he said instead, honestly, kneeling in the gravel to face her. "But listen to me. Listen. What happened wasn't your fault. The words are strong because *you're* strong. That's not a curse. It's just a fact, and facts can be carried."

She was crying now, silently, the tears indistinguishable from the rain.

"I'm going to teach you something," he said. "My master taught me, when I was younger than you. When the words come, you don't fight them and you don't loose them. You give them somewhere to go." He took her hand and pressed it, palm flat, against his own chest, over his heart. "Feel that? Everything I've swallowed for ten years lives right here. It's heavy. But it hasn't killed me yet. You press the word down—here—and you keep it. You keep it until it's safe to spend. Do you understand? Words aren't just power. They're *currency*. And you never, ever spend your currency in front of strangers."

She pressed her hand harder against his chest, feeling the heartbeat there, steady and stubborn. Then she moved her hand to her own small chest, pressing the lesson into herself, and nodded.

Twice.

* * *

They walked the rest of the day along the gorge's rim, because the bridge was gone and the moors offered no second chances to liars or the unlucky. As dusk gathered, Wren drew by firelight again, but the drawings had changed. No more houses. No more jars. She drew the bridge, broken. She drew herself, small, with lines radiating from her open mouth. And then, in the corner of the page, she drew a hand holding another hand, and she did not tear that page out. She folded it, carefully, and tucked it into the breast of her coat, over her heart, where the words were supposed to go.

Theo lay awake long after the others slept, listening to the rain and the distant mutter of the moors rearranging themselves around some liar's campfire miles away.

One word had dropped a bridge. He thought of the Court Beneath the Mountain, where nobles wore stolen voices like jewelry, and wondered what a voice like Wren's would fetch there. He thought of the ledger entry he'd found: *one soul's voice, sold for grain.* Grain. A winter's bread for a language that could crack stone.

And he thought about the oath sleeping in the false bottom of his traveling trunk, wrapped in oilcloth, whispering to itself in the dark. He had not touched it since leaving the guild. He could feel it touching him.

Near dawn, Wren shook him awake. She held up her slate, and on it, in careful charcoal strokes, she had drawn a road—a real road, wide and paved, running between mountains. At the end of the road she had drawn a building with towers, and inside the building, drawn small and precise, were chandeliers. Bone-white. Hanging.

Below the picture, she had written nothing, because she could write nothing. Instead she had drawn one more thing: a small figure at the mouth of the road, looking back over its shoulder, with a question mark above its head like a bird.

Theo looked at the drawing for a long time. Then he looked east, where the rain was thinning and the mountains were raising their white shoulders out of the moorland, and understood that the child had known the bridge was going to break because some part of her already knew the way to the place that had bought her voice—and that the road remembered her, the way the moors remembered lies.

"She's calling us forward," Maren said quietly behind him. "Or the mountain is. With roads like these, best to assume it's both."

Ferro tightened the mule's harness and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer.

Theo folded the slate drawing into his coat, beside his heart, where the heavy things went.

"All right," he said to the mountains, to the girl, to the oath whispering in the dark of his trunk. "We hear you. Lead on."
