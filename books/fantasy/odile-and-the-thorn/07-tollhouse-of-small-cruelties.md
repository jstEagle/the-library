## 7. Tollhouse of Small Cruelties

The tollhouse sat at the throat of the border like a swallowed stone that refused to go down.

It had been a chapel once—Theo could see the bones of it in the architecture, the arched windows bricked over with mismatched stone, the bell tower sawn off at the shoulder and capped with a slate roof. Now a wooden sign hung where a saint's icon might have been, its letters painted in the flat black of bureaucratic ink: *TOLL. ALL PASSAGES ASSESSED. COIN, GOODS, OR EQUIVALENT.* The word *equivalent* had been underlined twice, and beneath it, in smaller script, someone had added: *the assessor determines equivalence.*

Rain came down in gray ropes. Beyond the tollhouse, the road forked around a ridge of black rock and vanished into the Weeping Moors proper, where the fog stood up from the ground like the dead rising to look at travelers. There was no way around. Maren had checked her maps three times, and each time the roads conspired to funnel them here, as if the landscape itself were a customs agent with a sense of humor.

"The assessor's name is Gribben," Maren said, folding her map with the particular care she reserved for maps she didn't trust. "Or it was, eleven years ago, when I mapped this crossing. Toll assessors don't tend to keep their names long. Names are currency here too."

"Cheerful," Ferro said. He was working a splinter out of his thumb with his knife, unhurried, a man who had spent his life convincing locks that nothing about him was urgent. "What's the worst they can take? My boots? I've got coin."

Maren looked at him the way a cartographer looks at a map drawn by someone who has never seen the coast. "They don't want your boots, Ferro."

Wren stood close to Theo's side, her small hand hooked into two fingers of his coat pocket—a habit she'd developed on the third day of travel, wordless, proprietary, as though she'd decided he was furniture she intended to keep. She was studying the tollhouse with the flat, unblinking attention she gave to everything, and Theo followed her gaze to the window, where a lamp burned behind horn panes and a shadow moved with the slow deliberation of something that had all the time in the world.

He knew what tolls like this took. He had known since he was nine years old, when his master had made him memorize the border protocols alongside the forms of address for kings: *Never cross a memory-toll with anything in your head you cannot lose. Never let them see which memories you flinch toward. And never, ever, let them learn your name.*

Ten years of silence had kept his name safe. Ten years of being no one worth assessing.

"We pay coin," he said. "All of it, if we have to. Coin first. If they push—" He touched the wrapped bundle at his back, where the oath-blade slept under oilcloth, whispering even now, a sound like wind through a keyhole. "We don't push back. Not here."

Ferro snorted. "You say that like I've ever pushed back at anything."

"You pushed back at a door last month because it squeaked."

"It squeaked *disrespectfully*."

Wren made a sound—not a laugh, she couldn't laugh properly, not with the hole where her voice should be, but a huff through her nose, a ghost of one—and Theo felt it land somewhere under his ribs. Three days ago she had shattered a bridge with a single spoken syllable, and still, somehow, her quiet jokes landed softer than anything he'd heard in a decade.

He crouched to her level. She was seven, maybe eight; famine years made children hard to date. Her eyes were the brown of river water over stones, and right now they were frightened in the way she never allowed them to be in front of the others—wide, fixed on the tollhouse window, on the moving shadow.

"They'll ask questions," he said, and because she read lips better than most hearing children, he said it slowly, facing her. "You don't answer any of them. Not with hands, not with drawings, not with anything. You're my daughter. Daughters are boring. Boring is safe."

Her mouth twitched. Then she lifted her slate—the one Maren had bought her in the last market town—and wrote, in careful, slanting letters:

*I am NOT boring.*

"No," Theo agreed gravely. "But we're going to lie about it."

She considered this, then wiped the slate and wrote: *I'm good at lying. I did it for two years so nobody would notice me.*

Something cold walked down Theo's spine. A child who had learned invisibility as survival—he knew that curriculum. He'd taken the same course, with the same tuition: everything.

Behind them, the tollhouse door opened.

* * *

Gribben—or whatever the assessor called himself now—was a thin man assembled entirely of angles, as though he'd been folded rather than born. His coat was gray, his skin was gray, his eyes were the color of ledger paper left too long in the sun. Only his hands had any life in them: long, delicate, restless, turning a small brass instrument over and over between his fingers. It looked like an astrolabe designed by someone who hated the stars.

"Travelers," he said. His voice was pleasant the way a well-kept cage is pleasant. "Four souls, one cart, one mule of indifferent temperament. Welcome to the Weeping Crossing. State your business beyond the border."

"Cartography commission," Maren said smoothly. "Surveying the moor paths for the Duke's office."

"Duke's dead," said Gribben.

"Then his office owes us back pay," said Maren, without missing a beat, and Theo silently revised his opinion of her nerve upward.

Gribben smiled. It did not involve the rest of his face. "Coin is accepted. The standard rate for four souls and conveyance is thirty crowns." He held out one of those restless hands, palm up, and waited.

Ferro counted it out with theatrical reluctance, complaining about the exchange rate, the weather, the moors, and the general state of the world, and Gribben absorbed the complaint the way sand absorbs rain. But when the coins stopped coming, the hand stayed open.

"That's the passage toll," Gribben said. "There is also the *inspection* toll."

"There wasn't an inspection toll on any map I've drawn," Maren said.

"Maps are drawn by people who leave." Gribben turned the brass instrument in his fingers, and its rings began to rotate on their own, slowly, with a sound like a sigh. "New tariff. Instituted after the Ashwinter, when we learned that coin burns and grain rots but a memory keeps its value forever. One memory per soul. Small ones. Nothing you'd miss." His paper-colored eyes moved across them, one by one, cataloguing. "A childhood pet. The smell of a meal you liked. The name of a street you'll never walk again. The assessor determines equivalence."

"And if we refuse?" Ferro asked.

"Then you wait here until you don't refuse. The moors are patient. So am I." Gribben's gaze slid past them all and settled, with the unerring instinct of his kind, on Wren. "Though I confess the little one interests me. Children carry such *dense* memories. Everything is the first time, with them. First snow. First loss. First lie." He crouched slightly, bringing himself nearer her height, and his voice went soft and syrupy. "What do you remember, little traveler?"

Wren's hand found Theo's pocket again and gripped.

"She's mute," Theo said. His voice came out level, which cost him more than the blade at his back ever had. "Deaf-born. She remembers nothing worth taking."

"Mute." Gribben straightened, and something flickered behind his eyes—the brief, hungry recalculation of a man who has just heard a number change. "How convenient. Mutes are exempt, then. By law." He pronounced the word *law* the way other men pronounced *heresy*. "Of course, exemptions require documentation. Registration papers. Certification from a licensed physiker." He let the silence stretch. "You have none of these things. Which means she isn't exempt. Which means she's an *unregistered speaker traveling under false pretenses*, and there is a separate schedule for that. A much more interesting schedule."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere in the fog behind them, the moors made a sound like breathing.

Theo felt the old reflexes wake—the ones trained into him before his voice was locked away, the swordspeaker's instincts that measured every room for exits and threats and the exact angle at which a man's neck becomes a problem. Gribben was alone in the tollhouse. Two guards, maybe, dozing in the back. The brass instrument was a tool, not a weapon. He could end this in six seconds and be across the border before the lamp finished guttering.

And then what? Every collector between here and the Court Beneath the Mountain would know a swordspeaker had crossed unassessed. Every debt-broker, every bounty clerk, every creature in the deep places that traded in names. Ten years of invisibility, spent in six seconds, for nothing.

*Never let them see which memories you flinch toward.*

"I'll pay hers," Theo said.

Gribben's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Generous. But memories aren't transferable, sir. They must be *voluntary*. Freely given, freely taken—that's the whole architecture of the trade. A memory given under coercion curdles. Worthless within the week." He turned the brass rings again, idly, and Theo caught a glimpse inside the instrument: tiny glass vials, dozens of them, each holding a curl of silver light that moved like a fish under ice. "So. Who volunteers?"

"I do," said Ferro.

* * *

Later, Theo would try to reconstruct exactly how it happened—whether Ferro stepped forward before anyone else could move, or whether some part of Theo had already calculated that Ferro would, and had let him. He would fail at the reconstruction. Some moments refuse to be mapped.

"Ferro," Maren said sharply. "Don't be an idiot. Give them the name of a street. Give them the taste of bad wine. There are a hundred worthless things in your head—"

"Nah." Ferro was already unbuckling his tool roll, laying it on the tollhouse counter with the same care he'd give a patient on a table. "Look at him." He nodded at Gribben, at the brass instrument, at the vials of silver light. "This bloke doesn't take junk. Junk doesn't keep. He'll poke around in there until he finds something with weight, and he'll find it, because I've got exactly one thing in my head with weight, and we both know what it is." He rolled up his sleeve. "Better I choose it than let him dig."

"You don't have to—"

"Yeah, I do." Ferro looked at Theo then, and his face did something complicated and quick, the face of a man setting down something heavy and pretending it weighed nothing. "You recorded the toll at Graywater in your ledger. Collateral, you said. When we get home, you write this one down too. All of it. Every detail." He grinned, and the grin almost worked. "I'll want it back someday. That's how collateral works, right? You hold it. I come collect."

"That's how collateral works," Theo said quietly.

"Right." Ferro turned to Gribben. "Go on, then. Take it."

"Take *what*, precisely?" Gribben asked, and his voice had changed—the pleasantness burned away, leaving only appetite. "Be specific. Vague memories spoil."

Ferro closed his eyes. "My daughter's face."

The rain seemed to stop just to listen.

"She'll be eleven next month," Ferro said, eyes still shut, talking fast now, the way men talk when they're jumping. "I haven't seen her in six years. I left because staying would've gotten her killed—long story, doesn't matter. I used to sit in the shop after closing and try to draw her from memory. Couldn't get the nose right. Always made it too much like mine." A breath. "That's what I'm paying. Her face. The whole of it. Every version I've got."

"Ferro—" Maren started.

"Done," said Gribben.

The brass instrument opened like a flower made of hinges. Inside, where the vials of light turned, there was a single empty socket, waiting, and Gribben reached into it with two long fingers and drew out something that was not quite a needle and not quite a pen. He took Ferro's hand—gently, absurdly gently, a torturer's courtesy—and pressed the point to the back of it.

"Don't fight it," Gribben murmured. "Fighting bruises the memory. Bruised memories fetch half price, and I only pay full."

Silver light climbed the needle. It entered Ferro's hand and traveled up his arm in a slow luminous thread, visible through the skin like a vein of dawn, and Ferro made a sound low in his chest—not pain, exactly. Something worse than pain. The sound of a man watching a door close on a lit room.

It lasted perhaps ten seconds.

Then the light withdrew, coiled itself into the empty vial, and settled there, swimming, alive, *hers*—and Ferro swayed, and Maren caught his elbow, and he blinked at the tollhouse ceiling with the expression of a man who has walked into a room and forgotten why.

"Huh," he said. "That's... huh." He flexed his hand. Looked at it. Looked at the rest of them, and his brow creased with a small, polite confusion, the confusion of a man searching his pockets for keys. "Why's everyone looking at me like that?"

"Ferro," Maren said carefully. "Do you know why we're traveling together?"

"Course I do. Locksmithing job. Big one. Under a mountain, apparently, which is daft, but the pay's—" He stopped. Frowned. "There's... there was a reason I said yes. Wasn't there? Something about..." He trailed off, patting his pockets absently, and Theo watched him search for a daughter and find only a shape where she'd been, a silhouette with the middle cut out. "Huh," Ferro said again, softer. "Feels like I've forgotten something important. Feels like I've forgotten that I've forgotten it. Is that normal?"

"Completely," said Gribben, already sealing the vial into the instrument with a click that sounded obscenely final. "Passage is granted. Do give my regards to the moors." He paused at the door, and his paper eyes lingered on Wren one moment too long. "And do get the child registered, whoever you are. Unregistered speakers are worth a great deal to certain parties. I'd hate for someone less *civilized* than myself to find her first."

The door closed. The lamp behind the horn panes dimmed.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the ridge, past the border stones, where the fog of the moors began but did not yet reach. Maren built a fire with the economy of someone who had camped in places that rearranged themselves. Wren sat close to Ferro and leaned against his arm, and Ferro let her, and patted her head with the vague good nature of a man who liked children in the abstract and could not, at the moment, have said why.

"He keeps asking me her name," Maren whispered to Theo across the fire. "Every hour or so. 'The girl—what's her name?' And then ten minutes later he asks again. It's not sticking. Nothing about her is sticking." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she turned it into a cough, angrily. "He gave up his own daughter to keep a stranger's daughter safe, and now he can't even remember he did it."

"I know," Theo said.

"Do you? Because I've been trying to work out how to tell him, and there's no way to tell a man he's lost something when he doesn't know what it is. You can't grieve a hole. You can't miss a face you can't—" She stopped, jaw tight, staring into the fire. "Fix it. Whatever it costs. Fix it."

Theo said nothing. He was already reaching into his coat.

The guild ledger was smaller than its reputation—a fat volume bound in green leather gone soft with handling, its pages crowded with entries in a dozen hands. Sable Vane had given it to him with one instruction: *Everything gets written down. Everything. That's the whole religion.* He turned to a fresh page, uncorked his ink, and by firelight, while Ferro hummed tunelessly and Wren pretended to sleep against his shoulder, Theo Thornbury wrote:

*Borrowed against: one (1) memory, belonging to Ferro of Copperlane, locksmith. Contents: the face of his daughter, aged approximately five years at last seeing, name withheld by debtor's request given aloud before witnesses. Creditor: the tollhouse at the Weeping Crossing, assessor unknown, instrument of extraction: brass, self-turning. Terms: held in trust by the undersigned until reclaimed in person by the debtor. Interest accrued: all of it. This debt will be collected if it takes the rest of my life.*

He signed it. Then, because the ledger deserved honesty even when its keeper had none, he added a second line in smaller script:

*Witnessed by T.T., who stood by and let it happen, and intends to answer for that.*

"What are you writing?"

He looked up. Wren was awake, watching him with her chin on Ferro's arm, her eyes catching the firelight. He turned the ledger so she could read it. She studied the entry for a long time, her lips moving silently over the words, and then she took the slate from her pack and wrote:

*Will he really come collect it?*

"Yes," Theo said. "People like Ferro always do. They just need something to collect it *from*. That's what the entry is. Proof that the debt exists. Proof that he lost something worth getting back." He tapped the page. "Memories can't be destroyed, Wren. Only moved. It's sitting in a glass vial in a tollhouse right now, waiting. And ledgers—" he allowed himself the smallest smile— "ledgers are how we remember what the world wants us to forget."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then she wiped the slate and wrote, in her careful slanting hand:

*Like my voice.*

Theo's smile died.

*Like your voice,* he wrote back, beneath her words, because he would not say it aloud where the others could hear, and because some truths deserved the dignity of ink.

She handed the slate back and lay down, pulling her blanket to her chin, and just before she closed her eyes she looked at him once more—an old look, an adult look, the look of a child who had already learned that the world ran on transactions and was deciding, deliberately, to trust the merchant.

Across the fire, Ferro laughed at something Maren said, warm and easy and hollow at the center, a bell with the clapper removed. Theo watched him and felt the entry drying on the page like a wound scabbing over.

In the morning they would enter the Weeping Moors, where the paths rearranged themselves for liars and the fog remembered everyone who walked it. In the morning, Maren would unfold her maps and find that the roads had opinions. In the morning—

—but tonight, as the fire burned low, Theo heard it: far behind them, faint under the rain, the tollhouse bell that had no tower rang once, softly, like a ledger closing. And in the fog ahead, something answered.
