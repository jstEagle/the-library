## 8. Voices in the Deeproot

The mountain began speaking three hours after they entered it.

Theo felt it first in his teeth—a vibration too low to be sound, more like the memory of sound, as though somewhere far above a bell had been struck and the whole world was still ringing with it. He stopped on the tunnel's lip and pressed his palm flat against the stone. It was warm. Stone should not be warm this deep, where no sun had ever reached and the cold came up through the soles of boots like water through cloth.

"It does that," Maren said, not turning around. She walked ahead with her lantern held low, its light pooled at her feet like something she was leading on a leash. "The talking. You'll want to get used to it or you'll want to leave, and there's no leaving. The Deeproot only goes one direction for people who mean to go through."

"What is it saying?" Ferro asked. He kept one hand on Wren's shoulder, and Wren kept one hand on the wall, trailing her fingers along the stone the way other children trailed fingers along fence posts. She had not drawn anything since they'd entered. That worried Theo more than the warmth or the voice.

"Nothing," Maren said. "Everything. It's older than meaning." She glanced back, and in the lantern light her face was all hollows and patience. "The cartographers' guild calls these the Deeproot galleries. We think the mountain grew around something that used to speak—something that died still talking—and the stone learned the habit. Like a bird that outlives its owner."

"Charming," said Ferro.

"You wanted a route the Court doesn't watch," Maren said. "This is it. Nobody watches the Deeproot. Nobody comes here at all if they can help it." A pause, and then, softer: "Mostly because of what it says to you."

Theo let his hand fall from the wall. The warmth stayed in his palm like a coin held too long.

They went on.

* * *

The tunnels widened by degrees, the way grief widens—not all at once but chamber by chamber, each larger than the last, until Theo lost his sense of how much sky could reasonably be missing overhead. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some drowned organ. Their footsteps rang off surfaces he couldn't see, and between the rings, the voice—or voices, he corrected himself; there were many, layered like sediment—rose and fell.

It was not language. Not quite. But it was shaped like language, with pauses where pauses would fall, stresses where emphasis belonged, and once—only once—he caught something that was almost a word, a rounded vowel that dissolved before it finished forming.

Wren heard it too. He saw it in the set of her shoulders, the way she turned her head like a bird catching a distant wingbeat. Then she crouched, dug the stub of charcoal from her pocket, and wrote on the flagstone in careful, looping letters:

**IT SOUNDS LIKE THE FIRST TONGUE.**

"Not the First Tongue," Theo said quietly, kneeling beside her. "Older. Or younger. I honestly don't know which." He touched the letters she'd written. "The First Tongue binds things together. Souls to bodies, oaths to souls, names to names. This—" he gestured at the caverns, at the slow rolling murmur in the walls— "this sounds like something that was unbound. Something the binding failed to hold."

Wren looked up at him. In the lantern light her eyes were very dark and very steady, and she wrote one more line:

**DID IT HURT?**

He didn't answer. He didn't know how to answer. He took her hand instead and they walked on, and behind them Ferro muttered to himself the way he always did when the world got strange—inventory, Theo realized. The man recited lock mechanisms to calm himself. Wardens and tumblers and spring-loads, an entire litany of small precise mercies.

Maren led them down a stair that hadn't been built by anyone, carved by nothing but water and time and perhaps patience, and the voice grew louder.

* * *

It was in the third gallery—the one the maps called the Choir, though Maren's map called it nothing at all because, she admitted, the galleries moved and any name she gave them would be a lie—that Theo heard the master's voice.

He knew instantly that it wasn't the master. He knew it the way you know a stranger wearing a dead man's coat: the fit was wrong, the gait wrong, everything wrong except the timbre. But knowing did not save him. The voice rolled out of a side passage, low and dry and faintly amused, the way Master Aldous Vane-of-no-relation had sounded when Theo was nine years old and had just done something clever and forbidden:

*You hold the word too tightly, boy. A word gripped is a word strangled.*

Theo stopped walking.

The others went on—Ferro's boots, Maren's soft tread, the whisper of Wren's hand along the wall—but Theo stood in the dark of the gallery with his heart going like a hammer on a nail, and the voice came again, out of the stone, out of ten years of silence:

*Again. Slower. A swordspeaker's oath is not a cage. It is a door, and you are the hinge.*

"There's no one there," Theo said aloud, and hated how his voice cracked on the second word.

There was no one there. Of course there was no one there. Aldous had been dead eleven years—dead in the purge, dead in the fire at the Cloister of the Sworn Tongue, dead with forty other speakers when the collectors came with their iron ledgers and their writs of silence. Dead while Theo hid in a wine cellar with his hands over his own mouth, biting down on the words rising in him like floodwater, surviving because he refused to be what he was.

The mountain said nothing else. The mountain never said anything else. It only echoed what you brought into it, Maren had told him, and now he understood that she had been warning him.

He found the others fifty paces on, gathered around a column of stone that rose from floor to unseen ceiling, smooth as glass. Wren stood with both palms flat against it. Her eyes were closed. And she was humming—that same low syllable-murmur she'd made the night she arrived at the guild door, the sound that made ledger ink crawl, except here it did not crawl. Here it harmonized. The column hummed back at her, two notes braided into one, and the whole gallery seemed to lean in and listen.

"Wren." Theo's voice came out rougher than he intended. "Stop."

She opened her eyes. She looked at him with an expression he'd seen once before on the face of a dying speaker: not fear, not defiance. Recognition. As if the sound were a place she remembered being happy.

"She's not doing anything wrong," Ferro said carefully. "Is she?"

"No." Theo exhaled. "No, she isn't. But this place gives things back, and she has more to give back than most." He knelt so he was level with her, and spoke slowly, shaping each word so she could read his lips even in the bad light. "When it offers you sounds, don't take them. They're not gifts. They're hooks."

She studied him for a long moment. Then she took out her charcoal and wrote, in letters smaller and more hesitant than usual:

**YOU KNOW THIS PLACE. NOT THE PLACE. THE FEELING.**

Theo looked at the words for a long time.

"Yes," he said finally.

That was all. But Wren folded the question away the way she folded all her drawings—carefully, completely, keeping every crease—and he understood that she would ask again someday, and that someday he might have to answer, and that this, too, was a debt accruing somewhere in a ledger he could not see.

* * *

They camped in the fourth gallery because Maren said the fifth was worse, and she declined to elaborate, and none of them asked, because by now they had all learned the shape of her silences and respected them the way sailors respect weather.

Ferro cooked. It was an absurd domesticity—flatbread warming on a pan over a spirit-flame, deep under a mountain that talked in dead tongues—but Ferro insisted that food was the first architecture of safety, and Theo had long since stopped arguing. Wren ate cross-legged with her back against Ferro's pack, and drew. Tonight's drawing was the four of them: stick figures rendered with startling precision of feeling. Maren tall with her lantern. Ferro round and holding bread. Herself, small, with a scribbled dark space where her mouth should be.

And Theo, drawn last, drawn largest, standing slightly apart, with a jagged line coming out of his chest like a crack.

He saw it and said nothing, and she watched him see it and say nothing, and something passed between them that needed neither voice nor ink.

Later, when the fire had burned low and Ferro snored with the contentment of a man who trusted locks, Theo sat the first watch with his oath-blade across his knees.

He had unwrapped it only once since the guild—back in chapter and cellar of his old life, in the years when it lived buried under the floorboards of a house he no longer owned. The blade whispered when touched. Everyone assumed this was a figure of speech until they touched it themselves and went pale. It was an oath-blade, forged for the swearing of the Swordspeakers, quenched in spoken words the way other blades were quenched in oil, and it remembered every promise ever made upon it. Including his. Especially his.

*I, Theo Thornbury, called the Quiet, do swear the Last Oath: that my tongue shall be a locked room, my voice a spent coin, my words given to no purpose but the protection of the sworn—*

He ran his thumb along the flat and the whisper rose to meet him, and beneath the whisper, beneath the blade, the mountain murmured on. And it was the master's voice again, or near enough to break him:

*Why did you run, boy?*

"I didn't run," Theo said to the dark, barely breathing it. "I survived. There's a difference."

*Is there?* said the stone, in the voice of a man eleven years dead. *You keep the ledger of everyone else's prices. Voices sold, names traded, courage borrowed and returned with interest. You write it all down in your neat hand. When will you enter your own account? What did your silence cost, and who paid it?*

"Theo?"

He flinched. Maren stood at the edge of the firelight, lantern unlit, her face just a pale suggestion in the dark. How long she'd been standing there he couldn't guess. With Maren it was impossible to guess anything.

"The mountain talks to everyone," she said. "But it talks longest to people who have something unsaid."

"Does it talk to you?"

"Every night." She sat down across the embers from him, folding her long limbs with a cartographer's economy. "It uses my mother's voice. My mother, who sold our family's road-rights to the Court to pay a gambling debt, and never told me until I found the entry myself, in their records, in her handwriting." She turned her hands over, palms up, as if reading lines there. "So yes. I know what it's offering you right now. And I know you're not going to take it, because I watched you walk past that side passage today without answering, and I've watched you not-answer things for two hundred miles. You're very good at it. It's the best and worst thing about you."

Theo looked at the blade across his knees. "You know what I was."

"I know what you carry. The rest is yours to tell or bury." She lay back, arms behind her head, and stared up into the black where the ceiling should have been. "For what it's worth—the cost of silence isn't paid once. It's paid daily. Small installments. Most people never notice they're spending. You notice. That's why it hurts you more."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true." She closed her eyes. "Sleep. I'll take the middle watch. Tomorrow we pass through the last galleries, and after that the air starts tasting of candle smoke and old bone, and you'll need to be rested, because the Court smells strangers the way sharks smell blood, and we are going to be the strangest strangers it has smelled in a century."

She said it lightly, but her hands, laced behind her head, were white-knuckled. Theo pretended not to notice. It was the courtesy the guild taught first: never ask, never press, let people own their own wounds.

He settled back against the stone. It was warm again, and murmuring, and he let it murmur without listening, the way you let rain fall on a roof. Just before sleep took him, he thought he heard—clearly, unmistakably, in a voice that was entirely his own—his master's final lesson, the last thing Aldous ever taught him, the night before the fires:

*A name is not a possession, boy. It is a debt owed forward. Spend it well.*

Then only the mountain, saying nothing, saying everything.

* * *

They broke camp at what Maren swore was morning, though morning was a rumor this far underground. The fifth gallery proved worse exactly as promised: the voice here was not layered but singular, vast, patient, and it pronounced—slowly, over the hour it took to cross—one word. Only one. A word in no living tongue, and yet Wren stopped dead in the middle of the gallery and stared at the far wall with her charcoal halfway to the stone, trembling.

"What is it?" Ferro asked. "What's it saying? Can you read it?"

She shook her head. Then, slowly, she wrote on the flagstone:

**IT'S MY NAME.**

Theo felt the floor tilt under certainties. "Your name? Your true name?"

She nodded once, and under the writing she added, in letters pressed so hard the charcoal snapped:

**THE MOUNTAIN REMEMBERS ME. THE COURT SOLD MY NAME WITH MY VOICE. AND NOW SOMETHING DOWN HERE IS GIVING IT BACK.**

Maren had gone very still by the mouth of the next passage, her lantern raised, and the light showed what lay beyond: the galleries ended. Ahead, carved stairs descended in wide ceremonial sweeps toward a glow that was not firelight and not daylight but something amber and organized, and on the air came the taste Maren had named—candle smoke, old bone—and beneath it, faint and sweet and terrible, the sound of singing. Dozens of voices. None of them belonging to the throats that wore them.

"The Deeproot ends here," Maren said quietly. "Everything past those stairs belongs to the Court Beneath the Mountain."

Wren stood at the top of the stairs with her broken charcoal in her fist and her stolen name still glowing in the stone behind her, and she did not hesitate.

She started down.
