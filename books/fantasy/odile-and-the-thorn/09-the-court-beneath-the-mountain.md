## 9. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not have a door. It had a throat.

Theo felt it the moment they passed beneath the overhang of black stone — a change in pressure, a hush that pressed against his eardrums like a held breath. The passage sloped downward in wide, polished curves, as though the rock itself had been swallowed and smoothed by centuries of traffic. No torches lined the walls. Instead, veins of pale light ran through the stone like marrow, pulsing faintly, dimming when they spoke and brightening when they fell silent.

The mountain was listening. Theo had known it would be. He had simply not been prepared for how much it would feel like being watched by something patient.

Wren walked close to his side, her small hand hooked into two fingers of his left hand — the hand that had once sworn oaths, the hand that had not spoken a true word in ten years. She had not drawn anything since they entered the Deeproot caverns. Her chalk stayed in her pocket. Her eyes moved constantly: up the ribbed vaults of the passage, along the glowing seams, to the shadows between them where the dark pooled like standing water.

Ferro walked behind them with his lockpicks wrapped in oilcloth and slung across his back like a soldier's sword. He had been quiet since the tollhouse. Not sullen — Ferro did not do sullen — but quiet in the way of a man who kept reaching for a memory and finding a smooth blank wall where a face used to be. Twice on the road he had started to say something about his daughter and stopped mid-breath, his mouth working around a shape that no longer had anything inside it.

Maren led them. Her maps were useless here — she had said so herself, laughing, at the mouth of the caverns, before the laughter died in her throat — but she could read the mountain's moods in other ways: the temperature of the air, the direction the light-veins flowed, the way the silence leaned. "The Court doesn't want people to find it," she'd said. "It wants people to *need* to find it. There's a difference, and the difference is a road."

Now the road opened.

They stepped out of the throat of the passage and onto a ledge of white stone, and below them — impossibly far below, and yet somehow also very near, the way things are in dreams — spread the Court Beneath the Mountain.

Theo had seen cathedrals. He had been trained in one, back when he had a name and a voice and a future shaped like a blade. He knew what human hands could build when they were trying to make God feel small. This was not that. This was what happened when something older than God decided to build for its own amusement.

The cavern stretched farther than the eye could follow, its ceiling lost in a haze of glittering dark. And hanging from that darkness, tier upon tier upon tier, were chandeliers made of bone.

Not carved bone. Not assembled bone. They grew from the ceiling like stalactites of ivory, branching and rebranching, each arm cradling a flame that burned without heat or smoke — flames the color of candle-wax, of milk, of moonlight through frost. Some of the chandeliers were small, the size of a cart wheel. One, directly above the center of the Court, was vast beyond reason, its branches spreading like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die and been too beautiful to bury.

And beneath them, filling the cathedral floor in a slow, glittering tide, moved the Court itself.

"They're dancing," Wren wrote later, when her hands remembered how. But that wasn't quite it. They were moving the way water moves — nobles in robes of dyed shadow and woven metal, gliding between columns of black marble, gathering in clusters that dissolved and reformed like schools of fish. Music drifted up from somewhere below, thin and sweet and wrong, a melody that seemed to arrive slightly after the instruments played it.

"Look at their throats," Maren whispered.

Theo looked, and his stomach turned over slowly, like a door swinging on a rusted hinge.

Every noble wore jewelry at the throat. That was the fashion here, apparently — collars and gorgets and pendant-chains, all of it exquisite, all of it gleaming. But the jewels were not stones. They were small glass vials, blown in a hundred shapes: teardrops, stars, coiled serpents, tiny bells. And inside each vial something moved. A curl of silver light. A wisp of grey breath. A thread of gold that pulsed in time with the music.

Voices. Bottled voices, worn against the skin, warm from the wearer's pulse.

One noble — a tall figure in robes the color of dried blood — wore an entire necklace of them, dozens of vials strung together, and as Theo watched, the figure lifted one to their lips and uncorked it, and a woman's laugh spilled out, bright and helpless, ringing across the cathedral floor until the noble stoppered it again and smiled behind their fan.

"That's a child's laugh," Ferro said. His voice came out flat and strange. "That's — I know what a child's laugh sounds like."

"I know," Theo said.

He had spent ten years telling himself stories about the Court Beneath the Mountain. Every swordspeaker learned them as a child, the way children learn about wolves: half warning, half fascination. The Court collected oaths. The Court traded in souls. The Court's ledger never closed. But the stories had always been abstract, distant, dressed in the fog of legend. Legends did not wear stolen laughter as jewelry. Legends did not dance.

This was worse than any story, because it was *casual*. That was the horror of it, Theo thought, descending the great stair with Wren's fingers tight in his. Not cruelty — cruelty he understood, cruelty had a shape and a logic. It was the ease. The grace. A whole civilization built on the flesh of other people's voices, wearing it beautifully, humming along.

* *

The stair down took an hour. Perhaps longer; time behaved strangely under chandeliers of bone, stretching and pooling like spilled honey. Theo used every minute of it.

"The rules," he murmured, lips barely moving, eyes forward. "Say them back to me."

"Never give your true name," Ferro recited. "Never accept a gift. Never sign anything you haven't read twice, and read it a third time anyway. Never let them hear fear — they can taste it in the air, Maren says. Never—"

"Never trade anything you can't afford to lose," Theo finished. "And everything can be lost. Everything has a price tag here. The trick is making sure you're the one who writes it."

"And you?" Maren asked. She was watching him sidelong, her map-case clutched to her chest like a shield. "What's your part?"

"I'm Master Aldous Finch, debt-broker of the surface guilds." The name sat in his mouth like a pebble. He had invented it three days ago, in the dark of a campsite, stitching it together from dead men he had known. "I've come to negotiate the purchase of certain outstanding debts owed to my clients. I'm arrogant, well-paid, and bored. I find the Court quaint."

"You find it *quaint*," Ferro repeated.

"People believe what flatters them. If I sneer at their cathedral, they'll spend the whole audience trying to impress me instead of studying me." Theo adjusted the heavy chain of office he wore — lead-gilt, bought from a fence in the Weeping Moors, heavy enough to feel like authority. "Arrogance is armor. It gives them something to look at that isn't me."

"And Wren?"

Wren looked up at the sound of her name. In the marrow-light her face was pale and sharp, older than nine years old, and Theo's chest ached looking at her.

"Wren is my ward," he said. "A foundling. Deaf-mute since birth, poor thing, which is why I keep her close." He crouched to her level, and she met his eyes with the fierce, unblinking attention she gave to everything now — as if she had decided that if the world insisted on taking things from her, she would at least watch it while it did. "Can you do that? Can you be quiet even inside your head?"

She took out her chalk and wrote on the flagstone, small careful letters:

*can they take thoughts too*

Theo held her gaze. Lying to her felt like swallowing glass. "Not yet," he said. "So we don't give them time to learn."

She considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. Then she wiped the letters away with her sleeve, took his two fingers in her hand again, and nodded once.

* *

The gates of the Court were not gates. They were mouths — two archways of ribbed bone facing each other across a plaza of white stone, and passing between them felt like passing a tongue. Guards stood there, if guards was the word: figures in lacquered armor the color of old teeth, their faces hidden behind masks of smooth porcelain. Each mask had a single seam where a mouth would be, stitched shut with silver wire.

None of them spoke. None of them needed to. As Theo's party approached, the nearest guard simply raised one gauntleted hand, palm out, and waited.

Theo stopped. Reached slowly into his coat. Produced the letter of introduction Sable Vane had forged for him — real wax, real seals, real signatures from surface guilds that either existed or had been made to exist for this purpose — and laid it on the guard's open palm.

The porcelain mask tilted. The guard read. The plaza was silent except for the distant music and, underneath it, a sound Theo hadn't noticed until now: a low, continuous murmur rising from everywhere at once, like the mountain breathing through its sleep. Thousands of whispers layered into a single tone.

The guard handed the letter back. Then it reached up, and with one clawed finger, tapped the seam of its own stitched mouth. Twice.

An invitation. Or a warning. With the Court, Theo suspected, the two had long ago become the same thing.

They passed through the mouth of the gate.

Inside, the scale of the place closed around them. The columns rose like trees, black marble veined with the same pale light as the passage walls. The floor was polished to a mirror shine, and Theo caught glimpses of himself in it as he walked — a broad man in a broker's chain, a mute child at his side, a locksmith and a cartographer trailing behind — and in the reflections, sometimes, the figures didn't quite match the walkers. His reflection's chain was a moment ahead of his real one. Wren's reflection was smiling when she wasn't.

"Don't look down," Maren breathed. "The floor remembers who looks."

The crowd of nobles parted around them the way water parts around a stone — not fleeing, not welcoming, simply acknowledging that something foreign had entered and adjusting accordingly. Theo felt their attention like heat from a forge. Behind their fans and their veils and their collars of bottled voices, they studied him, weighing him, pricing him. He could almost see the numbers turning behind their eyes.

He lifted his chin, let boredom settle over his features like a mask of its own, and walked as though the cathedral had been built for his convenience.

It nearly worked.

It was working, right up until the music stopped.

* *

It happened between one note and the next. The melody — that thin, delayed, sweet-wrong melody — simply ceased, and in the silence that followed, every noble in the cathedral turned toward the far end of the floor.

There, beneath the greatest of the bone chandeliers, a dais rose in tiers of white stone. Upon it stood a throne of interlocking ribs, and beside the throne —

Beside the throne hung a cage.

It was small, gilded, delicate as a birdcage, suspended from a silver chain that descended from darkness above. And inside it, curled up small, was a voice. Not a vial this time, not a jewel — a full voice, visible, a shape of shifting silver light the size of a cat, pressing its luminous self against the bars. As Theo watched, it opened — there was no other word for it — and sang.

The song filled the cathedral the way floodwater fills a valley. It was a woman's voice, rich and aching, singing something wordless and old, and every noble below tilted their heads back to receive it with expressions of deep, languid pleasure, the way diners savor a rare vintage.

"There she is," murmured a voice at Theo's shoulder. "Vesperine's pride. Her nightingale. She brings it out for special occasions — new debts, mostly."

Theo turned. The speaker was a slender figure in dove-grey robes, ageless in the way of the Court, wearing a collar bearing a single vial — and in the vial, a man's voice, deep and warm, asleep. The figure smiled with too many teeth.

"Master... Finch, is it?" The figure extended a hand gloved in grey silk. "The gate told us you were coming. Letters travel fast here. Everything travels fast here, if it's worth anything." The smile widened. "You've arrived on a wonderful night. Lady Vesperine is holding court, and she does so love new creditors. Tell me — what is it you brokers deal in, up there in the sun? Debts of coin?"

"Debts of every currency," Theo said. His voice came out steady, bored, perfect. Inside, he was counting heartbeats. "Coin, labor, years, names. My clients are flexible. My clients are *hungry*."

"Mmm. Hungry." The figure tasted the word. "Then you'll fit right in."

Beyond the grey-robed courtier, past the dancing nobles, beneath the singing cage, Theo saw it: at the base of the dais, half-hidden among the pillars, a door of black iron banded in silver. Unremarkable. Guarded by two of the stitched-mouth masks. And yet his eyes snagged on it and would not let go, because Maren's intelligence — paid for in blood and favors — had been specific.

*Below the dais,* she had told him, *there is a vault. Below the vault, the ledger.*

Somewhere behind that unremarkable door, in a book older than the famine, was a line written in ink and grief: a girl's voice, sold for grain, buyer listed only as *the Court*.

The cage-singer's song swelled toward its final, shattering note. The nobles sighed. Somewhere in the crowd, a vial was uncorked and a stolen sob was released, savored, and stoppered again.

"Come," said the grey courtier, gesturing toward the dais with a silk-gloved hand. "Lady Vesperine will see you now. She's in an excellent mood — the nightingale always puts her in an excellent mood." The too-many-teeth smile again, closer now, and the sleeping man's voice in the courtier's collar stirred in its vial, dreaming. "And do bring the little one. Her Ladyship is *so* fond of children."

At Theo's side, Wren's grip on his fingers went rigid.

The cage above the throne swung gently on its silver chain, its light dimming, exhausted, spent. And as the courtier led them across the mirrored floor toward the tiers of white stone, Theo felt the mountain's murmur rise around them — thousands of whispers braided into one long, patient breath — and understood, with cold clarity, that every soul in this cathedral already knew why he had come.

The only question was whether they knew it before he did.
