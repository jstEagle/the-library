## 10. An Audience of Borrowed Throats

The summons came at the hour the Court called dusk, though no sun had ever reached this deep. A herald made of pale cartilage and borrowed breath appeared at their guest-cells' threshold without footsteps, without shadow, holding a scroll that unrolled itself like a tongue.

*Lady Vesperine receives the debt-broker Thornbury. Alone.*

Theo read the name on the scroll twice. *Thornbury.* Not his oath-name—never that—but the trade-name he had worn into the cathedral of bone like a coat over armor. Someone in this court had done their arithmetic. He folded the scroll until it was a small hard square and let it dissolve between his fingers, as court-paper did when dismissed.

"You don't have to go alone," Ferro said from the doorway of his cell. The locksmith looked worse than he had a day ago; the mountain's air was thin with something older than oxygen, and it leeched color from everything living. "I could come as your clerk. Brokers travel with clerks."

"Brokers travel with clerks who can hold their faces still," Theo said. "Yours is doing something complicated right now."

"I'm offended. That's my face being professional."

"Go sit with Wren." His voice gentled despite himself. "She doesn't like the chandeliers."

Nobody liked the chandeliers. They hung through the vaulted dark in cascades, hundreds of them, each lamp a curved white bone cradling a flame that burned without heat—and each flame, if you listened too long, was singing. Different songs. Lullabies in dead dialects, work-chants, a child's counting rhyme, all braided together into a sound like a city dreaming. Theo had understood on the first night what fueled them, and he had not slept well since. Voices. The Court wore its collection openly here: voices as jewelry, as light, as currency stacked in bone vaults below.

He went alone, as summoned, down corridors lined with doors that breathed.

* * *

Lady Vesperine received him in a chamber that had once, perhaps, been a chapel to something the mountain had eaten. The ceiling arched overhead like the inside of a throat. Candles of tallow and teeth burned along the walls. And she sat at the center of it on a chair of black iron filigree, dressed in layered grays that moved like smoke deciding to be silk, and she was—there was no honest way around it—beautiful, in the way that a blade is beautiful, or a winter lake before you understand how deep it goes.

Her throat was bare. That was the first thing he noticed, because every other noble he'd passed in these halls wore their acquisitions visibly: collars of tiny glass vials, pendants that murmured, rings set with chips of amber that whistled when the wind found them. Vesperine wore nothing at her neck but skin.

"Master Thornbury." Her voice arrived a half-second after her lips moved, like thunder lagging lightning, and it was the most terrible voice Theo had ever heard—not because it was ugly, but because it was perfect. It rang with harmonics no human throat should hold. It was a stolen singing voice, worn so long it fit her like her own skeleton. "You've been very quiet since you arrived. Quiet men make me nervous. Loud men I can price. Quiet ones are hiding their value."

"My lady honors me with her attention."

"She does nothing of the kind." A smile, small and dry. "Sit. You're making my floor nervous too; it likes to know where everyone's weight is."

He sat. The chair across from hers was warm, which meant someone had left it recently, which meant he was not the first audience of the evening—or the last. In the Court Beneath the Mountain, even furniture kept accounts.

"Wine?" She gestured, and a servant assembled itself from the shadows—a person, Theo corrected himself, a person trained out of their own outline. "It's pressed from memory-grapes. Drink enough and you'll remember a vintage you never tasted. Very popular with people trying to feel they've lived more than they have."

"No, thank you."

"Mm. Discipline. Or fear. They taste the same down here." She poured for herself, and the wine came out of the bottle silently, which struck Theo as obscene; even liquid should have a voice. "Let's not perform, Master Thornbury. Debt-brokers smell of ink and anxiety, and you smell of neither. You smell of cold iron and old oaths. There are perhaps four men alive who carry that scent, and three of them are dead and don't know it yet."

Theo kept his hands loose on his knees and his breathing even, and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

"There it is," Vesperine said softly. "The silence. Do you know what we do with silence here? We bottle it. Sell it to widows. But yours—" she leaned forward, and the candlelight bent toward her like iron filings to a lodestone "—yours isn't emptiness. It's *held*. Something lives inside your quiet, swordspeaker, and it has been screaming for ten years."

The word landed in him like a hook finding flesh. *Swordspeaker.* No one had called him that aloud since the night the order died, since he had knelt in the ash of the Chapterhouse and sworn the last oath—the one that bound his true name behind his teeth and sealed the old words of power in his chest like coals wrapped in wet cloth. He had been Theo Thornbury, ledger-keeper, invisible man, for a decade. This woman had peeled that disguise off in four sentences.

"You're mistaken," he said. "I'm a broker. I've come about an asset."

"A girl." Vesperine swirled her wine. "Small. Dark-haired. Mute by contract, entry nine-and-forty thousand and some, sold during the Ashwinter for eleven sacks of barley and a mother's guilt." She said it flatly, the way a merchant reads a bill of lading, and Theo's hands curled against his knees before he could stop them. "Yes. I know why you're here. The Court knows everything that walks its halls. You think you slipped in under a forged seal and a broker's patter? Child, we *invented* forgery. We invented patter."

"Then you know the sale was coerced. Famine terms. The parents were starving."

"The parents were *stupid*, which is different and far more common." She drank. "They sold what wasn't wholly theirs to sell. A voice is half the child's, half the world's, and the Court holds title to the middle portion. All quite legal. All quite eternal. Contracts beneath the mountain do not expire, Master Thornbury. They only change hands."

"And that's why I'm here." He made his voice into a ledger-line, flat and priced. "Name your figure. The guild will meet it. Coin, memories, years of service—we have instruments for every appetite."

Vesperine laughed, and the laugh was worse than the voice, because for one clear moment underneath the stolen harmonics there was something tired and human, like a face seen through frosted glass. "Coin. He offers me *coin*." She set down the glass. "What would I do with coin? Buy bread? I own the granaries. Buy beauty? I own the singers whose throats ache with everything I'll never feel again. No, Master Thornbury. I want only one thing in this world, and by a miracle of poor timing, you've carried it into my house."

The candles guttered, all together, though there was no draft.

"Your oath-name," she said. "Give me the name that anchors your power, and the girl walks out of here with her voice in her throat before the next bell. Signed, sealed, witnessed by the mountain itself. Clean transfer. You'll never be summoned by the old words again. Never hunted by collectors. Never *anything*. Isn't that what you've wanted? Ten years of hiding in your little guild of impossible things, flinching at knocks, cataloging jars of borrowed courage—here is your door out, and all it costs is the name you never use anyway."

* * *

Theo had imagined this moment, in the way that soldiers imagine wounds: clinically, from a distance, certain it would never truly arrive. He had thought that if the Court ever offered him a bargain, he would refuse it cleanly, the way his master had taught him—*a swordspeaker's name is not his, Odile's-own-sake, it is held in trust for every soul that cannot speak for itself*—and walk away with the moral high ground intact.

He had not imagined that it would feel like this. Like a key turning in a lock he'd spent ten years pretending wasn't there.

Because she was right. That was the obscenity of it. Somewhere beneath the horror, a small starved part of him stood up and *listened*. No more oath. No more weight. The old words gone silent in his chest forever, the collectors' ledgers closed against him, the last of the order finally, truly dead—and free. Free to be only Theo Thornbury, keeper of jars and ledgers, a man who owed nothing to history.

And Wren would speak again. She would laugh. She would say her own name.

The price was a name he had already buried. Wasn't it?

"That's a generous offer," he heard himself say, and hated how easily the broker's cadence covered the shaking underneath.

"It's not generous. It's *fair*, which is rarer and worth more." Vesperine rose and drifted to the wall of candles, and as she passed them, each flame leaned toward her throat and sang a little louder, the way flowers turn to sun. "Do you know what I hear, every hour of every day?" she asked, without turning. "I hear her. The voice I wear. It sings when I sleep. It sings things I never taught it—songs from a country that stopped existing before my grandmother was born. It is the most beautiful thing in this mountain and it is not mine, and every morning I put it on like a coat that fits perfectly and keeps me *warm and hates me*." She turned. Her eyes were the gray of ledger paper. "So yes, I understand wanting to be rid of what binds you. I am offering you the only mercy this court has ever extended anyone. Take it."

"The name isn't mine to give," Theo said quietly. "That's rather the point of an oath."

"Everything is someone's to give. That's rather the point of a court."

* * *

He stalled. He heard himself doing it and let himself do it, buying seconds the way Ferro bought locks—one careful turn at a time.

"Such a transfer requires verification," he said. "The guild's instruments. An independent witness. You wouldn't want the mountain voiding the contract later on grounds of improper conveyance."

"The mountain witnesses everything."

"Then it also remembers everything, and mountains are litigious." He stood, slowly, keeping his palms open and visible, an old habit from negotiations that had nearly become duels. "I'll need three days. To draw the instrument properly. To confirm the name's legal standing—you'd be astonished how many oath-names were already pledged elsewhere before their speakers knew it. If yours carries a lien, Lady Vesperine, the transfer fails, and we've both wasted a miracle."

It was nonsense, most of it, stitched from broker's cant and hope. But Vesperine studied him with the patience of something that had watched empires default, and something in her face shifted—not belief, but interest. The interest of a collector who has found an item with a hidden provenance.

"Three days," she said. "Not because I believe you. Because I want to watch you decide. That's the best sport left, down here—watching the righteous discover their price." She extended her hand, and on her palm lay a sliver of bone, etched with characters that crawled like insects. "My token. Show it at any door. And Master Thornbury—if you're considering the vault instead, consider this: the ledger pages burn at a touch of their owner's will. Every page. Including, should I grow impatient, page forty-nine thousand and some."

She was telling him she knew. Of course she knew. The Court collected oaths like currency, and it counted everything else besides.

Theo took the token. It was cold and faintly damp, like something recently wept on.

"One question," he said, at the door, because he was a fool and always had been. "The voice you wear. Where did it come from?"

For a moment—half a heartbeat, no more—the perfect harmonics fell away from her face, and what looked out of Lady Vesperine was a woman standing in a ruined chapel listening to a song she couldn't stop hearing.

"From a family," she said, "that sold it to pay *their* debts. Generations ago. The Court keeps excellent records." The harmonics slid back into place like a visor lowering. "Three days, swordspeaker. Spend them well. Everyone does, in the end."

* * *

The corridor back to the guest-cells was long and dim and lined with doors that whispered as he passed—doors that had learned voices the way walls learn damp. Theo walked with the bone token in his fist, its edges biting his palm, and did not let himself think until he was behind his own door with the bar drawn.

Then he sat on the narrow bed and put his head in his hands and admitted it, in the privacy of his own skull, where even the mountain's accounting might not reach:

He wanted to say yes.

God help him, some hollowed-out corner of him had *sung* at the offer. Ten years of fear, ended in a signature. Ten years of flinching at every knock, of cataloging other people's miracles because he'd forfeited his own, of waking at night with his master's dying words caught in his teeth—all of it purchasable, all of it refundable, and the cost charged to an account he'd already written off as lost.

And Wren. Wren would get her voice either way. That was the poison in it—that the bargain asked nothing of *her*. He could buy her whole with a coin that was only his. What kind of man hesitated at that? What kind of man stood in a bone chapel weighing his own freedom against a child's laughter and finding the scales *tipping the wrong way*?

His hand went, without permission, to the hilt beneath his coat—to the oath-blade, hidden, whispering. It had whispered louder since they'd entered the mountain, a fine constant shiver of metal, like a dog smelling home. Now, under his fingers, it seemed almost to press against his palm, the way Wren pressed her drawings into his hands: *look. look at this. look at what you are.*

A swordspeaker's name is held in trust.

If he gave it up, the order didn't just die—it was *erased*, unclaimed, its last promise defaulted. And every soul the old words could have shielded, every mute child sold in every famine to come, would stand before courts like this one with no one left who even remembered how to bargain in their language.

But if he refused—

If he refused, he needed another way, and there was exactly one other way, and it was a vault full of burning pages guarded by a court that already knew his face and his scent and the shape of his silence. Three days. She had given him three days, which meant she expected him to fail, or to break, or to come crawling back with the name on his tongue like a penitent with coins.

Below, faint through the mountain's bones, the chandeliers sang their stolen songs. And threaded among them—so faint he might have invented it, so familiar his chest seized around it—he heard a melody he knew. A lullaby from the Chapterhouse. His master's voice, or the ghost of it, wearing bone for a lamp.

Theo stood up. He took out the oath-blade, and it rang softly in the dark, once, like a struck bell remembering its note.

Three days. One vault. Half a plan and a locksmith who owed him more than he'd ever said aloud.

He sheathed the blade and went to find Ferro—and stopped, because the door was already opening, and Ferro stood in the gap with a look on his face that Theo had learned, in ten years of reading ledgers, to recognize as the look of a man carrying news that costs.

"They've taken Maren," Ferro said. "And Theo—the guards knew exactly which cell to open."
