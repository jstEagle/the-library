## 11. The Ledger of Sold Things

The vault door was a masterpiece of cruelty, and Ferro loved it the way a man loves an old enemy.

"Three tumblers," he murmured, his cheek nearly pressed to the cold iron, one eye shut, the other squinting through the keyhole as though it might blink first. "But they're not stacked. They're *nested*. Whoever built this wanted the lock to lie about itself." His picks—slivers of whalebone and blackened steel, thin as confessions—moved inside the mechanism with the delicacy of a surgeon's fingers. "It'll take time."

"How much?" Theo kept his voice low. Sound traveled strangely down here; the corridors drank it and gave it back altered, from directions that made no sense.

"An hour. Two if the third tumbler is what I think it is." Ferro's jaw tightened around something he didn't say. Since the tollhouse, since the memory he'd fed into the collector's brass funnel like a man paying with his own blood, he had been quieter—not hollowed, exactly, but *careful*, as if he were handling the inside of his own head gently now, checking that everything still sat where he'd left it. "Don't rush me. Rushed locks bite."

Theo nodded and turned away, giving him the privacy of his work.

Maren was already three paces down the corridor, chalk in hand, sketching on the walls themselves—the only surface here that stayed put long enough to hold a mark. She drew the passage behind them as a series of nested arcs, then stood back and watched. Within a minute, two of the arcs had drifted, the corridor's geometry quietly rearranging itself like a sleeper turning over.

"It moves when we're not looking," she said. "Not fast. But it moves." She tapped the wall. "So we mark everything. Every junction, every archway. I map the way a drunk maps a city—by what doesn't change." Her eyes flicked to Theo. "And what doesn't change down here, Thorn, is mostly bone."

They had left Wren at the mouth of the service stair, tucked into an alcove behind a curtain of hanging moss that Maren swore was blind. The girl sat cross-legged with her slate across her knees, drawing—she drew constantly now, her charcoal stub worn to a nub—and beside her sat the half-page of ledger vellum they'd torn free in their first desperate pass through the archives, the entry bearing her transaction, incomplete and faintly warm, like a thing breathing.

*Two hours,* Theo told himself. *Get in, find the master ledger, get out.*

He should have known better. He had spent ten years among ledgers. He knew exactly how long it takes to read a book that doesn't want to be read.

* * *

The vault opened onto a gallery, and the gallery opened onto the truth.

Ferro's picks gave a final click like a knuckle cracking, and the door swung inward without a sound—which was somehow worse than groaning hinges. Beyond lay a chamber vast enough that their lantern light failed to find its ceiling. Shelves rose in terraces, carved from the living rock and lined not with books but with *objects*: jars of murmuring dark, bells without clappers, small glass domes beneath which things moved that might have been moths or might have been thoughts. And at the center, on a plinth of black stone, rested a book.

It was large as a paving slab, bound in leather gone the color of dried liver, its spine reinforced with bands of pale metal that Theo recognized with a lurch—he had bands like that on his oath-blade's scabbard. Oath-iron. The binding metal of promises.

"The Ledger of Sold Things," he breathed.

"You said there'd be a ledger," Ferro said, easing the door shut behind them. "You didn't say it would look like it eats people."

Maren was already circling the room's perimeter, chalking the walls. "This chamber doesn't shift," she reported after a moment, surprise flattening her voice. "Everything around it does. It's the anchor. The whole undermountain rearranges itself around this room." She looked up at the invisible ceiling. "Which means the Court can't afford to lose it either. Whatever's in that book, they've built their house around it."

Theo approached the plinth. Up close, the ledger's cover was scarred with use—thumb-worn corners, a stain near the fore-edge that might have been wax or might have been something else. A chain of oath-iron linked its spine to the stone, but the links hung slack. It wasn't chained *shut*. It was chained so no one could carry it away.

He opened it.

The pages were vellum, dense with entries in a dozen hands spanning centuries. Each entry followed the same liturgy: a description of the thing sold, the price, the seller, the buyer. Most buyers were marked simply *the Court*, which told him nothing and everything. He turned pages with increasing speed, scanning for names he knew, for patterns, for—

He stopped.

The entry was old, the ink browned. *One voice, girl-child, seven years. Sold by father and mother, jointly, in the famine year. Price: forty measures of barley, one goat, passage south.* Below it, in a different hand, a note: *Buyer: the Court. Status: unreclaimed.*

Unreclaimed.

Theo turned another page. Another voice, sold. Unreclaimed. Another—a memory, whole childhoods of them, sold by a mother who needed medicine. Unreclaimed. Another. Another. A laugh, sold by a widow's brother "for her own good." Unreclaimed. A name—someone's actual name, the kind your mother gives you and no one else may call you—sold to settle a gambling debt. Unreclaimed.

Page after page after page. The entries went back four hundred years. And threading through all of them, in that same second-hand script, ran the same terrible notation: *unreclaimed. unreclaimed. unreclaimed.*

"They never give them back," Theo said. His voice came out strange, flat, as though someone else were using his throat. "Nobody ever comes to reclaim them. Or they come, and—" He stopped, because he remembered the fine print of his own guild training, the clauses about retrieval contracts, the ones Sable Vane had made him memorize before she'd let him touch the shipping manifests. *Retrieval requires proof of claim. Proof of claim requires the seller's consent or the seller's death. In cases of sale by parents, consent lapses at majority unless formally renewed.*

Formally renewed. By parents who had traded their daughter's voice for barley and were, by now, twenty years dead or twenty years distant or simply unwilling to sign their names to the shame of it.

Wren would never be reclaimed by the rules. Not by anyone playing the rules.

"There are hundreds," Ferro said quietly. He had come to stand at Theo's shoulder, and his face, in the lantern light, had gone the color of the ledger's binding. "Hundreds of children. Look—here, this one, sold the same famine year. Same price. Same goat." His finger hovered over the entry without touching it, the way you hover over a wound. "They bought a harvest of voices, and they just—kept them."

"Not kept." Theo's hand had closed around the edge of the page, and the paper whispered against his grip. "*Wearing* them. You saw the court up there. Voices as jewelry. Laughter at the throat. They don't keep these children's voices in drawers out of sentiment. They wear them to dinner."

Something was happening in Theo's chest, a pressure he hadn't felt in ten years, since the last time he'd stood in a room full of other people's grief and been told, by his own oath, to do nothing. Back then he had obeyed. Back then he had told himself that silence was discipline, that discipline was virtue, that virtue was enough. He had built a life on that arithmetic, and the arithmetic had held until a mute child had walked through the guild door humming syllables that made ink crawl.

Now the arithmetic broke.

"We came for one voice," he said. "One entry. We tear out one page, we complete it, we go home." He heard his own words and hated how reasonable they sounded, how they were the words of a man who had spent a decade being reasonable in order to stay invisible. "That was the contract."

"Yes," Ferro said.

"That's what Sable signed."

"Yes."

Theo looked down at the ledger, at the hundreds of unreclaimed entries stretching back through centuries of famines and debts and desperate, loving, unforgivable bargains. Somewhere in those pages was a boy whose master had sold his own death to buy his student time to run. Somewhere was a laughing widow. Somewhere were children who had grown old without ever again hearing the sound of their own names spoken aloud.

Somewhere was a girl named Odile, whose parents had loved her enough to feed her and not enough to keep her whole.

"We're going to need a bigger page," Theo said.

Ferro stared at him. "Thorn—"

"I know what I'm saying." He was already moving along the shelves, pulling down jars, examining seals, looking for blank vellum, for copying tools, for anything. His hands were steady. That surprised him. Inside, everything was burning. "I'm saying we didn't climb into hell for one soul. I'm saying there are four hundred years of unreclaimed entries in this book and every single one of them is a theft that somebody, somewhere, has to answer for. I'm saying—" He stopped himself, breathed. "I'm saying the guild takes impossible things. This one's just bigger than most."

Maren appeared in the doorway, chalk dust on her fingers, her expression tight. "Corridor's shifting faster. Something knows we're here. We have minutes, not hours." Then she saw his face, and whatever she read there made her go still. "...What did you find?"

"Everything," Theo said. "We found everything."

* * *

They worked like thieves in a burning house, because that was what they were.

Maren tore strips from the hem of her coat and soaked them in lamp oil—slow-burning, acrid, the kind of smoke that clings. Ferro produced, from the depths of his coat, a folding knife and a block of soft graphite and began, with the efficiency of long practice, taking rubbings: page after page laid flat, paper pressed over the entries, the graphite whispering across the surfaces, ghost-copies accumulating in his satchel. He worked in silence, jaw set, and Theo understood that Ferro had decided something too, in his quiet locksmith's way—that a man who had already paid a daughter's face for this journey was not a man who intended to leave with less than everything.

Theo copied the oldest entries by hand, the ones where the ink had faded past what graphite could catch. His writing arm ached. He didn't stop.

It was Maren, working her way along the far shelves, who found the drawer.

It was small, iron-bound, tucked beneath a shelf of glass domes, and it was locked—but Ferro had it open in eleven seconds, and inside lay bundles of letters, tied with cord, each bundle labeled in that same second-hand script: *UNCLAIMED. AWAITING SELLER'S CONSENT.*

Letters. Letters written by the sellers—or attempted by them, or begun and abandoned. Theo untied the nearest bundle with hands that had begun, very slightly, to shake.

The top letter was from a mother, dated sixty years ago. It read, in a cramped and blotted hand:

*I have come to your door four times. Four times I have climbed the mountain and four times your stewards have turned me away, saying the debt is settled and settled debts cannot be reopened. My son is fourteen now. He has not spoken since the winter I sold his voice, and the doctors say nothing is wrong with him. Nothing is wrong with him. I have the barley still. I will return it. I will return the goat. I will return double. Please. I only want to hear him say my name once more before I die.*

Below it, in the Court's second hand, a single line: *Received. No action.*

Theo set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might detonate.

"They wrote back," he said. "People came. For centuries, people climbed this mountain and begged, and the Court *wrote back*." His voice cracked on the word. "No action. No action. No action." He fanned the letters like a hand of cards, each one a small catastrophe in someone's handwriting. "They kept the receipts of every mercy they refused."

"That's why the drawer's locked," Maren said softly. "Not to protect the letters. To protect the Court. If anyone ever read these—" She stopped. She didn't need to finish. Everyone in the room could finish it.

Theo folded the mother's letter and slid it inside his shirt, against his skin, next to where the half-page of Wren's entry rode in its oilcloth. Some documents you copy. Some you carry.

"Time?" he asked.

Maren consulted her chalk marks through the doorway, and her face answered before she did. "Less than we had."

* * *

They were sealing the satchels when the ledger spoke.

There was no other way to describe it. As Theo closed the great book's covers—as his palms pressed flat against the oath-iron bands—a sound rose from within it, low and layered, like wind through a canyon full of open throats. Hundreds of voices, faint as memories, rising all at once. Not words. Not pleas. Just *presence*, the acoustic signature of souls packed too long in one place, the sound a library would make if libraries could grieve.

Ferro stumbled back a step. Maren's chalk snapped in her fingers.

But Theo leaned closer, and in the chorus he heard—one thread among hundreds, thin and clear and unmistakably young—a hum. Three notes. The same three notes Wren had hummed in the guild doorway on the rain-lashed night, the syllables that made the ledger ink crawl.

She was in there. Her voice was in there, and it knew its own name, and it was singing toward him through four hundred years of stolen things.

"I hear you," Theo whispered—to the book, to the girl upstairs in the moss-hung alcove, to the boy in the sixty-year-old letter who had never spoken again. "I hear all of you. And I am coming back."

The chorus faded. The chamber settled into silence. And in that silence, from far above, muffled through miles of rearranging stone, came a sound that froze all three of them where they stood.

A bell. Deep, bronze, deliberate. Ringing once.

Then, after a pause long enough to hold a heartbeat, ringing again.

"They know," Maren breathed. "The vault's been breached—they're sounding the alarm—"

"No." Ferro was listening, head cocked, his face draining. "It's not an alarm. Alarms ring fast. That's—" He swallowed. "That's a summons. They're calling everyone. Guards, collectors, the Master of Accounts. Everything the Court has."

Theo slung the satchel across his chest and felt the weight of it—hundreds of rubbings, dozens of copied entries, one mother's letter warm against his ribs. Enough to prove the thefts. Not yet enough to undo them. One page short of a miracle, and somewhere above them, the mountain was waking up hungry.

"Move," he said. "Now. Before the corridors finish deciding where they want to be."

They ran. And behind them, on its plinth of black stone, the Ledger of Sold Things lay open to a page near the end—where, in fresh ink that none of them had written, a new entry was drying:

*One oath-name, swordspeaker, last of his order. Seller: pending. Buyer: the Court. Status:* —and the rest of the line was still wet, still forming, as though the book itself had begun to write him in.
