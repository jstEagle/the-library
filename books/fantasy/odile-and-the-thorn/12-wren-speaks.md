## 12. Wren Speaks

The dark was not the same as the dark at home.

Wren knew this the way she knew most things now—by feel, by weight, by the way it pressed against her. The dark under the mountain had a texture to it, like wet wool, like the inside of a closed mouth. It breathed. It listened.

She sat on the cold stone floor of a side chamber off the vault corridor, knees pulled up to her chin, and she drew in the dust with her finger because there was nothing else to draw with. Thorn—the man who did not speak, who spoke only with his hands when he thought no one was watching—had shown her how, that first week on the moors. *Draw what you cannot say,* he'd signed, and then he'd frowned at himself, because of course she couldn't read signs yet either, not then. But she had understood anyway. Understanding came easier than words ever had.

The dust-picture grew under her finger: a small square house. A crooked chimney. Two tall shapes standing in the doorway.

Mama. Papa.

She made Papa taller than Mama, because he was, though not by much. She gave him a hat with a feather, because he had worn one the day they left, the last day, the day the men with the scales came through the village weighing everything—grain against livestock, livestock against children—and calling it *salvation*.

*Ashwinter*, they had called that year. Wren remembered the name because Mama had said it over and over into her hair while holding her too tight: *It's Ashwinter, little bird. Ashwinter, and we have nothing left to burn.*

Except they had something left to burn. They had her voice.

Wren's finger stilled in the dust.

She did not remember the selling itself. That was the strange part, the hole-in-the-middle part, the part that made her chest ache in a way she had no picture for. She remembered before: sitting on Papa's shoulders, singing the goose-song from the well, the one about the foolish bird who traded its feathers for gold. She remembered after: opening her mouth on the cart out of the village and finding nothing inside it, a door painted on a wall, air where the song should be.

And between—only flashes. A table of black wood. A woman's hands, beautiful and terrible, turning her chin toward candlelight like Papa turned apples to check for rot. And the sound. Oh, the sound of it leaving her—a feeling more than a sound, like a rib being drawn gently out through her throat, like warm water poured out of her until she was an empty jug.

She had screamed. She knew she must have screamed, because afterward her throat had bled, and Mama had cried onto her hair, and the beautiful terrible woman had smiled with someone else's mouth and said—in Wren's own small voice, pitched high and sweet—*"There. Now doesn't that sound lovely?"*

Hearing your own voice come out of a stranger's face was the worst thing that had ever happened to Wren Odile.

Worse than the famine. Worse than the moors with their rearranging paths. Worse than the bridge shattering when her one word got loose, worse than the guilt of watching Thorn's face go white as bone-chalk while stones rained down around them.

Because the famine had been done *to* everyone. The word had been an accident. But the sale—that had been done to *her*, alone, specifically, deliberately. Someone had looked at her and seen a thing worth grain.

Her finger moved again in the dust. She drew the table of black wood. Above it she drew the woman with the beautiful hands. Around the woman she drew the jewelry—all the little glinting shapes that hung at ears and throats and wrists down in the cathedral place, each one a stolen sound wearing the shape of ornament. Wren had heard them humming as they passed through the halls, before Ferro was taken, before everything went wrong. Voices. Hundreds of voices, caged in metal and gemstone, murmuring to each other in the dark like birds in a hundred separate cages hung too close together.

Somewhere in that humming was her.

She could feel it, faintly, the way you could feel weather coming in your teeth. A pull behind her breastbone, toward the deep places of the court, toward the vaults where the ledger of souls lay open on its lectern like a great sleeping moth. Her voice was in there. Not the memory of it. *Her.* A piece of her, folded up small, waiting.

Thorn had torn the page free. Half of it. He carried it against his heart now, wrapped in oilcloth, and it whispered to him at night—she had heard it whispering, a dry paper rustle like a mouth trying to form words. But half a page was half a name, and half a name was dying slowly, its ink fading like breath on glass. Three days, Thorn had signed to her, his hands grim and steady. Three days, or it dissolves, and you stay voiceless forever.

Three days.

Wren looked at her dust-drawing: the house, the parents, the table, the woman, the cage-voices. And beneath them all, because her hand seemed to move without asking her, she drew a third figure. Small. Standing apart. Holding a blank space where its face should be.

That was her. That was what the selling had made: a girl with an empty place where the middle of herself used to be.

She stared at it a long time.

Then, slowly, deliberately, she dragged her palm across the whole drawing and erased it.

* * *

They found her an hour later, when Maren came looking with a strip of dried apple and a worried crease between her brows.

"Drawing again?" Maren asked, settling down beside her on the stone. Maren always talked enough for three people, maybe because maps had taught her that empty spaces were dangerous things. "Show me, then. What have we got?"

Wren hesitated. Then she smoothed the fresh patch of dust flat and began again—slower this time, deliberate, the way Thorn had taught her to draw *for* someone instead of just *out of* herself.

A house. Two figures in the doorway.

"Your home," Maren said softly. "Before."

Wren nodded. She drew the table next. Black, heavy-legged. Then a small figure seated at it, and above the figure she drew wavy lines coming out of its open mouth—the sign they'd all agreed on for *song*, for *speech*, for the thing Wren didn't have.

Then she crossed out the lines. Firmly. Twice.

Maren went very still. "They took it," she said quietly. "At that table. They took your words."

Wren nodded again, and her eyes were hot, and she hated that, hated crying without being able to sob properly, because sobs needed sound and sound was the thing she'd been robbed of. Tears could fall silently. Grief could fall silently. Everything about her now was silent, and she was so tired of silence being the shape of her whole life.

She kept drawing.

The cathedral. Bone chandeliers hanging like frozen rain. Small circles at throats and ears—the voice-jewels, humming. Then a close-up, if dust could do such a thing: one circle, larger than the rest, and beside it a tiny figure of herself reaching toward it with both hands.

Maren studied it. "You want it back," she said. "Your voice. From them."

Yes. Yes, but it wasn't only wanting, and Wren needed Maren to understand that, so she added to the picture. She drew the tiny figure of herself again, and this time she drew the circle breaking, cracking, the voice flying out of it like a bird from a smashed cage. And she drew the bird flying back to the small figure's open mouth, going home, going *in*.

"There's a word for that," Maren murmured, almost to herself. "Reclamation. Ferro would know the legal term; he knows all the terms. 'Restitution of stolen goods.' As if you were a spoon somebody pocketed." Her jaw tightened. "You're not a spoon."

Wren touched Maren's arm. When the map-woman looked at her, Wren pointed at the cracked jewel, at the returning bird—and then she drew, carefully, one more thing. A line. A jagged, splintering line running straight through the small figure's chest.

*Even if it hurts.*

Maren understood. Wren watched her understand, watched the color shift in her face, watched her look away toward the corridor where Thorn had gone to stand watch with his oath-blade that whispered.

"You know what it'll cost," Maren said. Not a question. "You felt it when you spent the word in the tunnel. Part of you went into the sealing, didn't it. Part of—" She stopped. Started again, gentler. "Words like yours aren't sounds, love. They're substance. You spend one, you're spending yourself."

Wren nodded.

She knew. Better than any of them, she knew. When she had spoken the sealing-word in the collapsing tunnel, it hadn't been like using a tool. It had been like cutting off a finger and giving it to the mountain. For three days afterward she had felt thin, translucent, like paper held up to a lamp. Something of her had gone into that stone and stayed there, and she was smaller now, and she would be smaller still with every word she spent.

And she wanted her voice back anyway.

Not despite the cost. *With* the cost. That was the thing none of them seemed to grasp, the kind ones, the ones who kept trying to shield her from decisions, from danger, from the ledger's truth. They thought she was a parcel to be protected and delivered. But she was the goods. Whatever was done to reclaim her voice would pass through her first, and she wanted—needed—to be the one who chose it.

She took Maren's charcoal stub from her belt-pouch—it was always charcoal with Maren, graphite and ink-stained fingers—and she drew one final image on the stone floor where the dust-pictures had lived.

She drew herself. Whole this time, with a face. And she drew the voice-bird returning, and she drew herself *open-mouthed to receive it*, unflinching, even though she also drew the jagged cost-line through her chest, even though she drew small tears falling from her drawn eyes.

Then, beneath it all, she wrote. Not in the First Tongue—she would never risk that, never again, not after the bridge—but in plain trade-script, the letters Thorn had been teaching her on the road, laboriously, letter by patient letter:

**I WANT IT. I PAY. MY CHOOSING.**

The charcoal snapped a little on the final stroke. Wren didn't mind. It felt right that it should cost the charcoal something too.

Maren read it twice. Then she did something unexpected: she laughed, short and wet, and pulled Wren into a fierce hug that smelled of graphite and road-dust and the particular staleness of someone who had slept badly in caves for a week.

"Sable Vane is going to adore you," Maren said into her hair. "You know that? Terrible, stubborn child. You're going to run that guild within five years."

But when she pulled back, her face had gone serious again, and she tapped the drawing with one ink-dark finger. "He has to hear this. Thorn. He carries that page like it's his sin and his penance both, and he thinks this is his debt to pay alone. He's wrong. But he won't believe it unless it comes from you." She paused. "Can you tell him? In whatever way you tell?"

Wren looked down the corridor, toward the faint grey glow where Thorn stood watch, blade across his knees, listening to it whisper.

Yes, she signed—her hands making the shape Maren had taught her, the simple one, the one that meant *yes* and *I will* and *it is mine to do*.

* * *

She found him at the changing of the watch, or rather she found his shadow first, thrown long and thin along the cavern wall by the guttering light of Ferro's salvaged lantern. He sat with his back to the stone and the oath-blade laid flat across his thighs, and even from ten paces Wren could see the tension in him—not fear exactly, but readiness worn so long it had become a kind of posture, a way of holding his skeleton.

The blade whispered. It always whispered near her now. She thought perhaps it recognized what she was, what she'd been made of—the First Tongue ran through her hollowed-out places like water through channels cut for it, and old things made for speaking that language tended to notice.

She sat down beside him. Close, but not touching. He was particular about space, Thorn. He liked to know where every edge of himself ended.

For a while they simply sat, two silent people in a loud dark, and it was the easiest quiet Wren had ever shared with anyone. With Thorn, silence wasn't absence. It was a room they both lived in.

Then she began to draw.

She used the flat dust-film on the stone between them, working quickly, surely, and she felt him turn his head to watch. First the page—the torn half-page, the fading entry, rendered in careful strokes. Then an arrow pointing from it to a drawing of her own mouth, open, with the voice-lines flowing back in.

His breathing changed. She heard it catch, restart, slow itself by force.

She kept drawing. The jagged line through her chest. The tears. And then the trade-script, large and unambiguous, pressing hard enough with her finger to score the stone:

**I WANT IT. I PAY. MY CHOOSING.**

When she finished, she sat back and waited.

Thorn stared at the drawing for a long, long moment. Then he looked at her—at *her*, not the picture—with an expression she was learning to read the way sailors read weather. There was grief in it. There was guilt, the great heavy guilt he wore like a coat that no longer fit. He had told her once, in signs, haltingly, that years ago he had refused a case like hers. A voice sold, a plea ignored, a door closed on his own cowardice. He thought this was his punishment. He thought the ledger's price was his to pay, his name to surrender, his silence to break.

She reached out and, very deliberately, erased the arrow that pointed from the page to her mouth. Then she redrew it—from *her* to the page. From her to him. Both directions. A road with traffic moving both ways, which was, Maren had told her once, the only kind of road worth mapping.

Thorn made a sound. Small, rough, involuntary—the first noise she had ever heard him make that wasn't a blade-sound or a footstep. Almost a laugh. Almost a sob. The noise of a man whose argument has just been dismantled by a seven-year-old with a dusty finger.

He signed to her then, slow and formal, the guild-signs she was only beginning to learn: *Dangerous. You understand what you're choosing?*

**MY CHOOSING**, she tapped on the stone, tapping each scored letter.

Something shifted in his face. Something settled, the way sediment settles in shaken water. He looked at the oath-blade on his knees, and the whispering of it rose slightly, eagerly, like a dog hearing its leash picked up, and Wren saw him make his peace with it—or begin to. Some peaces take longer than three days.

Then he did something he had never done before. He reached out and, with one careful fingertip, drew in the dust beside her pictures: a small figure of himself, and beside it a small figure of her, and around them both a single unbroken line. A circle. A household, drawn in the oldest shorthand there is.

Family. Or the promise of it. Or the audacity of it, down here in the dark, with Ferro in a cell and half a page dissolving and a court of voice-thieves between them and daylight.

Wren looked at the circle for a long time.

Then the whispering of the blade changed.

She felt it before she understood it—a tightening in her teeth, the same pressure that preceded the tunnel-word, the same pull behind her breastbone. The blade's murmur sharpened into something almost like speech, almost like her own stolen timbre echoing up through steel, and far off down the corridors of the Court Beneath the Mountain, something answered.

A hum. Faint, vast, choral—the sound of hundreds of caged voices stirring at once, like birds all turning their heads toward the same window.

They had heard her choosing. Or the page had. Or the mountain itself, which spoke in dead languages and remembered everything, including, Wren suspected, the exact weight of a small girl's song.

Thorn was already on his feet, blade in hand, pulling her up by the other. His eyes said what his hands barely had time to sign: *They know we're here. Move. Now.*

But as they ran, hand in hand, through the bone-lit dark toward the sound of Ferro's distant cell and the rising storm of the court, Wren found that she was not afraid.

Or rather—she was afraid, thoroughly, down to the marrow.

But for the first time since the table of black wood, the fear had somewhere to go. It had a direction. It had a purpose. It was *hers*.

Her choosing. Her voice. Her payment, if payment came due.

She squeezed Thorn's hand, and together they ran deeper into the dark, toward the half a name that was dying, toward the full name that was waiting, toward whatever terrible and necessary thing came next.
