## 14. The Half a Name

The dark beneath the mountain was not empty. It was *occupied*.

Theo felt it the way he had once felt the presence of his master's blade across a training hall—something watching, weighing, patient as debt. He ran with Wren's small hand crushed in his, Ferro's lockpicks still jingling uselessly in the satchel against his hip, and behind them, receding like a tide going out, the bone cathedral's alarm rang through stone: a sound not of bells but of voices, hundreds of them, screaming in unison.

*The ledger page.* It burned against his chest where he'd tucked it inside his coat. Half an entry, torn diagonally from the great book of sold things: Wren's name in one column, the price in grain in another, and then—nothing. The rest of the transaction, the terms of ownership, the clause of reclamation, all of it sheared away and left somewhere on the vault floor or in Ferro's captors' hands.

He did not need to read it to feel what it was doing. The paper pulsed against his ribs like a second heart, and each pulse came a little weaker than the last.

* * *

They ran until the cathedral's screaming faded to a whisper behind them, and then they ran further, because Theo did not trust whispers in this place. Maren had mapped three exits from the Court's territories—Maren, who had sold their route, whose maps might be laced with betrayal like arsenic in wine—and so Theo ignored all three and chose instead the one direction no map could help him: down, and sideways, into the unmapped warrens where the mountain's roots tangled with older things.

The tunnel narrowed. The walls changed from worked stone to something rawer, veined with mineral light that throbbed slow as a sleeping pulse. Wren's breathing came ragged beside him. He slowed, finally, at a junction where four passages met like the fingers of a buried hand, and let her lean against the wall and gulp air.

"Rest," he said. Then, remembering himself, remembered her—he crouched, dug the stub of charcoal from his pocket, and held out his palm flat, the way you offer feed to a horse you don't yet trust.

Wren looked at his hand. Then at him. There was ash on her face and a smear of blood at her hairline that he was trying very hard not to look at directly, because looking at it directly would mean admitting what the ambush had cost, what Ferro's absence cost, and he could not afford that admission yet. Grief was a luxury for people who weren't being hunted.

She drew in his palm. Quick strokes, sure even now: a stick figure running. A jagged shape behind it—the guards, the pen-armed clerks. Then she tapped his chest, twice, and pointed at the place where the page lay hidden, and made a gesture he understood without translation: the fingers of one hand closing over nothing, crumbling.

*I know,* he wrote back on his own palm, tracing invisible letters. *It's dying.*

She nodded once, grim as a guildmaster closing accounts, and he almost laughed—a cracked, disbelieving sound—at how much like Sable Vane this eight-year-old looked when she did it. Found family assembled itself in strange shapes.

He took out the page.

Even by the mineral-light it looked wrong. The ink had begun to move—not crawling the way it had the first night Wren arrived at the guild door, humming syllables that made the ledger ink writhe, but *draining*, the letters going gray and thin like water seeping out of cloth. And around the torn edge, the paper itself was fraying into fibers, into dust, into less than dust.

He understood, then, with the cold clarity that always came too late and exactly on time: the page was not paper anymore. It was a contract, and contracts were alive in the way wounds are alive. Torn from its book, severed from its witness-stone and its seal, it was dying—and when it died, whatever claim it carried would die with it. Wren's voice, her name, the whole of what they'd bled for, dissolving back into the Court's ledgers as if none of it had ever been contested. As if the sale had simply... completed.

There was only one way a half-contract survived. It had to be completed. Terms written, seal set, witnessed—within three days, or the ink forgot itself entirely.

Three days. In the undermountain, hunted, with half a name and no map.

"Right," Theo said aloud, to no one, to the dark. His voice sounded strange down here, flattened by stone, stripped of echo. "Then we don't stop."

* * *

They walked through that sleepless stretch of hours that caves make of night, and the mountain talked to them.

He'd heard it before, in the Deeproot caverns with Maren leading—dead languages layered in the walls like sediment, voices of speakers long gone soaked into the stone. But here, closer to the Court's heart, the voices weren't dead. They were *collected*. Phrases drifted past in the dark, worn smooth from handling: a woman counting bread loaves in a language no famine had left survivors to speak; a child reciting a prayer with the wrong god's name in it; someone, over and over, saying *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry* in a voice that had clearly been used for other things since.

Wren pressed close to his side. He put a hand on her shoulder and kept walking.

Once—only once—a voice said something in the First Tongue. Three syllables, low and warm, spoken the way his master had spoken them twenty years ago across a training yard dusted with snow: gently, the way you speak to power you respect and fear in equal measure.

Theo stopped. Every muscle in his body turned toward the sound. His mouth opened before his mind could intervene, the old words rising in his throat like water finding a crack—

Wren's hand closed on his coat.

He looked down at her. She shook her head, slowly, her eyes wide and wet and absolutely certain, and he understood that she knew better than anyone alive what the mountain did with offered voices. It kept them.

He closed his mouth. The words went back down like swallowed glass. They walked on.

* * *

The tunnel ended at a chasm.

Not dramatically—the undermountain didn't do drama, it did erosion—but absolutely. The passage simply ceased, sheared away into a black drop that breathed cold air up at them, wide enough that even Maren's moving-map magic would have shrugged and drawn a question mark. On the far side, maybe forty feet, maybe sixty, the passage continued: a darker slot in the far wall, and above it, faint as a scar, a thread of daylight-colored stone that suggested the surface world existed somewhere beyond.

Behind them, faint but persistent, the Court's hunters. Not voices now. Footsteps. Many, and unhurried, which was worse than running. Things that hunted in the dark did not need to hurry.

Theo stood at the edge and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the vault. One bridge-builder among them, captured. One oath-blade that whispered but did not fly. One half-page with perhaps two days left in its ink. One child.

One word.

He had known it since the junction, probably. Since the palm-drawing. He had been walking toward this the way water walks toward a crack.

"Wren." He crouched so they were eye to eye. He took her hands. "I need you to listen, and I need you to be angry with me after, but you have to listen first."

Her chin lifted. Stubborn. Good. Anger was fuel; he'd built ten years of careful invisibility out of it.

"The page is dying," he signed against her palms, letter by letter, because some things shouldn't be trusted to the dark air. "When it dies, your voice stays theirs forever. I can complete the contract—I know the old forms, I can write them, but writing takes time we don't have, and time needs distance, and distance needs a bridge."

Her eyes went to the chasm. Back to him. She already knew. Children always already knew; that was the obscenity of it, that the world made children do this arithmetic while the adults pretended otherwise.

"There is another way across," he signed. "A loud way. A way that costs."

She pulled one hand free. Drew on his palm: a single word-shape in the First Tongue—one of her precious hoarded syllables, spent like coin, and there were so few left in her. He could feel the weight of it through his skin, the way you feel weather coming in an old wound.

Then she drew something else. A stick figure with his crooked posture and his too-big coat. A house with a sign he couldn't read but recognized anyway—the Guild of Impossible Things, the misfit family that had taken her in without asking questions because asking was against the rules. Then she drew herself inside it.

*You're my reason to get there,* the drawing said. *So I'll pay.*

"No," Theo said aloud, and his voice cracked on it, the first true break in ten years of measured speech. "No, there has to be another—"

But even as he said it, he heard the footsteps round the corner behind them, unhurried, inevitable, and Wren was already turning to face the chasm, planting her feet the way he'd planted his feet before every oath he'd ever sworn, and there was no time, there had never been any time, the whole world was a tollhouse and everyone demanded payment in the only currency that mattered.

She breathed in.

What came out of her was not a shout. It was smaller than that, and vaster: a single syllable of the First Tongue, the binding-word, the one that means roughly—and only roughly—*hold*. It left her mouth and the air itself obeyed.

Stone moved. From the chasm's walls it drew itself out like thread from a spindle, weaving, knotting, bridging—a span of raw rock arcing across the black, humming with the residue of her voice, glowing faintly along its seams like a thing freshly alive. It was ugly and lopsided and it was the most beautiful thing Theo Thornbury had ever seen.

And Wren swayed.

He caught her before she fell. She was lighter than she should have been—that was the horror of it, that words weighed what they cost, and he could feel the difference in his arms, a subtraction, part of her spent into the bridge and gone wherever spent things go. Her eyes found his. Dizzy, but level. She tapped his chest, twice, over the hidden page, and then pointed across the bridge with a hand that trembled and did not lower.

*Go. Finish it.*

"I'm carrying you," he said, in the voice he imagined his master would have used, the voice of a man making a promise the world would try to break. "That's the price *I* set. Argue later."

Something flickered across her face that was almost a smile. She let him lift her.

* * *

He carried her across the humming span with the hunters' footsteps growing louder behind them, and the bridge held, and on the far side he turned and did the only honorable thing left to a man with a whispering blade and no more words to spend: he cut the bridge down behind them.

It fell into the dark still glowing, taking the syllable's strength with it, sealing them into the narrow slot of passage ahead. The footsteps stopped on the other side of the chasm. Voices rose—not angry. Worse. *Amused*. One of them called out across the gap, in trade-tongue, clear and carrying:

"Two days and some hours, swordspeaker. The ink keeps better time than you think."

Then silence, except for the page against his ribs, pulsing fainter, fainter, fainter.

Theo sank down against the wall with Wren heavy and too-light in his lap, and let himself, at last and only briefly, feel it: the guilt arriving like a debt collector, itemized and exact. The child who had paid for his hesitation in the vault. The friend sitting in a bone cell trading memories to buy them minutes. The oath he would have to speak aloud, soon, fully, with every collector in the old order listening.

Wren's fingers moved weakly against his arm. He looked down. She had drawn one last thing on his sleeve, in charcoal nearly worn to nothing:

A blank ledger page. An arrow pointing at it. And below, in the clumsy trade-script Ferro had been teaching her on the road:

*room for what comes*

He pressed his hand flat over the words, over her hand, over the dying page, and in the dark of the mountain's throat, Theo began—to the rhythm of her slowing breath, to the tick of two days' remaining ink—to write the completion of the contract in his mind, word by forbidden word, gathering the storm he would have to become.

Somewhere above them, faint as memory, the mountain spoke his master's voice again.

This time, Theo answered.
