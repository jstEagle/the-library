## 15. What Ferro Chose

The cell had no door.

That was the first thing Ferro understood about his captivity, and it was somehow worse than a door would have been. A door could be picked. A door had hinges, had tolerances, had a tongue of iron that answered to the patient persuasion of a good pick and a better hand. Ferro had opened doors that kings had sworn were unopenable. He had once let himself out of a prison in Corveth by lifting the entire lock mechanism through the wall with a bent spoon and eleven hours of whispered encouragement.

This cell had an *absence*. A doorway-shaped hole in the bone-white stone, and through it, nothing he could work with. No tumblers. No wards with seams. The threshold itself was the lock, and it was written, not built—lines of silver script floating in the air like frost on glass, rearranging themselves whenever he looked away.

*Contracted prisoner*, one line read. *Payment outstanding.*

He sat on the floor with his back to the wall and counted what he had left.

That was how locksmiths prayed, his mother had taught him. Not with words—with inventory. Fingers: ten, all present, two scarred from old mistakes. Boots: gone; they'd taken them, which meant they thought he might run, which meant they feared him running, which was a small comfort he held onto like a coal in cupped hands. Memory: he did not count those yet. He wasn't ready. Counting them would mean naming them, and naming them would mean standing at the edge of the shelf and seeing how much was already gone.

The tollhouse memory still stung. His daughter's face—that was the price he'd paid at the border, surrendered to a tax collector with soft hands and softer eyes, and he had walked out of that little office knowing the shape of the loss but not its weight. You couldn't feel the weight until you reached for it. Now, in the dark, he reached for it constantly, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. There was a hole where her face should be. He knew her name—Liesl, Liesl who was seven and afraid of thunder, Liesl who used to sit under his workbench and sort springs by size while he worked—but when he closed his eyes and tried to see her, he found only a warm blur, a voice without a face, laughter without features.

They had taken the picture and left him the love. He hadn't decided yet whether that was mercy or the cruelest arithmetic imaginable.

Footsteps. Not boots—the Court Beneath the Mountain did not seem to go in for boots, or perhaps its servants simply weighed too little to need them. A rustle, like paper learning to walk.

The thing that entered was shaped mostly like a man, if a man were drafted rather than born. Its skin had the texture of vellum, and where its face should have been there was a smooth expanse with faint lines ruled across it, as though waiting for someone to write upon it. It carried a ledger—not a book so much as a bound absence, pages that drank the light around them.

"Prisoner Ferro," it said. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way a contract speaks when it is being read aloud. "Your account has come due."

"I haven't bought anything," Ferro said.

"You were purchased," it said. "When you crossed the threshold of the vault, you became collateral against your companion's debts. Your labor, your skills—" the ruled face tilted, considering him, "—and your holdings."

"My holdings," Ferro repeated flatly. "My memories."

"The Court deals in what is real. Memories are real. Names are real. Promises are the most real things in existence." It set the dark ledger down on the floor between them with a sound like a coffin lid closing. "You have been assessed. Your remaining holdings are considerable."

Ferro looked at the book. He thought about the shelf inside himself, all the small bright jars of his life, and how many were already empty or missing.

"How much do I owe?"

"That is not the correct question."

"It's the only question I've got."

The creature regarded him. Somewhere far above—or below; direction was unreliable here—a great bell tolled once, and the sound came up through the floor into Ferro's spine like cold water.

"The question," the thing said, "is what you wish to purchase."

* * *

They gave him three days.

Not three days of freedom—three days of *audience*. The Court, it turned out, ran on negotiation the way a mill ran on water. Every prisoner was also a customer. Every debt was also an offer. The vellum-faced clerk—it called itself Ninefold, because it claimed to hold nine offices simultaneously and refused to be reduced to any one of them—came twice a day with the ledger open and terms laid out like a merchant laying out cloth.

And every time, Ferro bargained.

Not for himself. That was the part that puzzled Ninefold, he could tell. The ruled face grew more agitated each visit, more lines appearing across it like wrinkles of confusion.

"You are offering everything you have," it said on the second day, "for a delay."

"A delay in the collection of my companion's debts," Ferro corrected. "There's a difference. A delay is a window. Windows can be climbed through."

"The swordspeaker cannot reach you. He is hunted through the deep galleries. His page is dissolving even now." Ninefold said it gently, almost kindly, the way a doctor delivers a prognosis. "By the third day, the entry will be illegible, and Wren's voice will belong to the Court absolutely. Nothing you buy will change that."

"Then I'm buying cheap," Ferro said. "Everything I have for something that won't matter anyway. That's the best deal anyone's ever gotten down here, and you know it."

But it wasn't true, and they both knew it wasn't true, and the lie sat between them like a coin too large to hide.

Because it *would* matter. Everything Ferlo had was real, and the Court dealt in what was real, and real things spent in the right place at the right moment were worth more than any army.

He had made his list on the first night, scratching it into the stone with a sliver of bone from the chandelier dust that drifted down through some unseen fissure above. Not the memories he'd lost—no point mourning those—but the ones that remained. And the list was short, and getting shorter, and every item on it hurt to write:

*Liesl's laugh.* (He'd kept the sound, thank every merciful god, even after they took the face.)

*The first lock I ever opened.* His master's workshop, rain on the windows, the click like a word being spoken.

*Maren's face, the day we met.* She'd been arguing with a river. Actually arguing, shaking her fist at the water, telling it that maps were promises and it had broken one. He'd laughed so hard he'd fallen off the dock.

*Theo's oath.* Not the words—he didn't know the words—but the moment on the road when Theo had shown them the hidden blade, and it had whispered against his palm, and Ferro had watched ten years of silence crack open like ice on a spring river.

*The smell of bread in the morning.* Small. Stupid. But it was his, and it was warm, and the Court took warm things most eagerly of all.

Nine items. Ninefold found this deeply amusing.

"An elegant symmetry," it said. "Nine memories for nine offices. Shall we begin?"

"Not yet." Ferro put his hand flat on the dark ledger, feeling it pull at him faintly, the way deep water pulls. "First I want to know exactly what I'm buying. I'm a professional. I don't sell to customers who don't understand the goods, and I don't buy from them either."

"What you are purchasing," Ninefold said, "is a stay of execution on the entry of Wren. Three additional days of stability for the half-page the swordspeaker carries. Time enough, perhaps, for him to complete it. Perhaps not."

"And the catch."

"There is no catch. There is a *price*, and you have seen the price. This is the honesty of the Court, locksmith. We never cheat. We merely charge."

Ferro sat back against the wall. Above him, impossibly high, a chandelier of bone turned slowly, casting light that had no warmth in it.

Three more days for the page. Three more days for Theo to find whatever he needed—Vesperine, the vault, the words, the miracle—to finish what they'd started. Three days bought with the last warm things in a life that had already been bled twice.

It was robbery. It was salvation. It was, Ferro thought, the first honest transaction he'd ever been part of, because everyone involved knew precisely what was being stolen.

"When does the payment come due?" he asked.

"On completion of the contract. If the swordspeaker fails, your memories return to you, and the Court collects Wren instead." Ninefold's ruled face almost smiled. "We are fair, locksmith. If he succeeds, you will have spent yourself for nothing—and for everything."

"Then let's do it properly." Ferro rolled up his sleeves, an old habit, the gesture of a man about to do careful work. "Item by item. I want receipts."

* * *

The first one they took was Maren's face.

They didn't take it the way the tax collector had, with a pen and a formality. Ninefold simply opened the ledger to a blank page, and Ferro spoke the memory aloud—describing her, the dock, the furious river, the way she'd pushed her hair out of her eyes with the back of her wrist—and as he described it, it thinned, like breath fading from a mirror. By the end he was reciting facts. *A woman. Brown hair, maybe. She argued with rivers. Why did she argue with rivers?*

By nightfall he couldn't remember why he'd listed her at all.

The second was the first lock. Then the bread. Then the oath-moment, though that one fought him; he clung to it through two full attempts, describing Theo's face in the firelight, the whisper of the blade, before the ledger finally pulled it loose like a tooth coming free.

Each time, somewhere far away, a bell tolled, and the frost-script on his cell doorway rearranged itself, and a new line appeared: *Payment received. Balance reduced.*

He saved Liesl's laugh for last.

On the third morning, Ninefold came and stood in the doorway-shaped absence and waited, patient as paper. The final installment. The balance would be settled, the contract sealed, the three days purchased in full.

Ferro sat with the memory in his lap the way you'd hold a sleeping child.

He could hear it still, if he listened hard: a summer evening, a courtyard behind the workshop, Liesl chasing sparks from the forge and laughing when they died before they could touch her, laughing with her whole body, head thrown back, the sound rising up through the warm air like something that intended to keep going forever. He had stopped working to listen. He remembered—he would remember for a few more minutes—that he had thought: *this is the sound I would walk into a mountain to hear again.*

Which was, he supposed, more or less what he was doing.

"Locksmith," Ninefold said quietly. For a creature of contracts, it sounded almost reluctant. "I am required to inform you that you may refuse. Refusal voids the agreement. Your companion's page dissolves within hours. You lose nothing but him."

"And Wren loses her voice forever."

"Yes."

"And Theo loses everything he came here to do."

"Yes."

Ferro nodded. He wiped his palms on his knees—an old habit before delicate work, steadying the hands, honoring the task—and he lifted the memory up like a key.

"Liesl," he said, and said her name aloud one last time so it would be the last thing to go, anchored to the sound of her laugh. "Papa's buying you time. You'll never know it. That's the whole point of the good ones."

He let it go.

The bell tolled. The script on the doorway flared silver and then settled, satisfied. Somewhere in the deep galleries, a torn half-page of soul-ledger steadied, its edges knitting, holding—for three more days.

Ferro sat in the empty cell with his empty shelves and found, to his surprise, that he was smiling.

They had taken nearly everything. They could not take the choosing.

"Balance settled," Ninefold said. It paused at the threshold, the ruled face turning back toward him, and for the first time the clerk sounded less like a contract and more like something old and tired underneath one. "I have processed forty thousand accounts, locksmith. Yours is the first I hope defaults."

"Thank you," Ferro said. "Now get out. I've got work to do."

"Work?"

"I'm going to pick your court apart from the inside," Ferro said pleasantly, flexing his fingers. "You showed me the ledger. You let me touch it. Big mistake, paper-face."

And far above, in a tunnel lit by the last stubborn glow of a dying torch, a swordsinger with a half-page in his coat felt the ink beneath his fingers suddenly stop dissolving—and pressed on into the dark, not knowing why the mountain had just given him three more days, only that somehow, somewhere, a friend had paid for them.
