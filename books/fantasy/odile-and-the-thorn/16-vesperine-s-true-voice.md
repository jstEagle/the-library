## 16. Vesperine's True Voice

The Court Beneath the Mountain did not sleep so much as it listened.

Theo had learned this in his three days of hiding among its corridors—corridors that Maren had mapped for him in charcoal on salt-cured hide, her hand shaking only a little as she drew the routes she had once sold. The cathedral never went dark; bone chandeliers burned with a pale, cold light that came from no flame he could name, and beneath it the nobles drifted like moths who had forgotten what they were looking for, their throats glittering with other people's voices.

He moved through them now wearing the borrowed confidence of a debt-broker's seal he had forged from Ferro's tools and half of a dead man's ring. No one looked at his face. In this place, faces were the least valuable thing a person carried.

Lady Vesperine kept apartments above the Hall of Accounts, and it was there, on the second night after Ferro's capture, that Theo found himself standing before a door of black wood carved with nightingales—or what had once been nightingales. Someone had chiseled out their tongues. All of them. Every bird on every panel, beaks open, throats hollowed to smooth and silent stone.

He raised his hand to knock and stopped.

*This is madness,* said the careful voice inside him, the one that had kept him alive for ten years of blank pages and unasked questions. *You are one man with half a ledger page dissolving in your coat, a locksmith in a cell below, and an oath you swore never to break sitting in your chest like a swallowed coal. Walk away. Find another way.*

But there was no other way. He had spent two days searching the vault records Maren had copied, and every route to Wren's voice ran through Vesperine. She held the contract. She held the collateral. And she held, Theo now suspected, something else entirely—something he had glimpsed only once, in the moment before her guards dragged Ferro away, when the Lady of Borrowed Throats had turned toward the sound of Wren humming in the corridor and her face had done something no mask is built to do.

It had cracked.

He knocked.

"Enter," said a voice that was not hers. It was beautiful—that was the horror of it—a soprano like water over glass, a voice meant for opera houses and wedding mornings, and it came out of Vesperine's mouth the way a coin comes out of a stranger's purse: with weight, with value, and with nothing of the spender in it.

Theo entered.

* * *

Her apartments were the first honest room he had seen beneath the mountain.

Not honest in their contents—the shelves held jars of breath, each labeled in a fine clerk's hand; the vanity held a comb carved from a single vertebra; the bed was draped in cloth woven, if the rumors were true, from the last words of dying kings. But honest in their disorder. Here, away from the cathedral's audience, the perfect composure came apart at the seams. A wine cup sat unfinished. A letter lay torn in half, then in quarters, then gathered hastily into a drawer left open an inch. And on the writing desk, half-hidden beneath a ledger bound in grey skin, sat a small wooden bird.

A nightingale. Whole. With its tongue intact.

Vesperine stood at the window with her back to him, watching the cavern's false dark. Up close, without the cathedral light flattering her, she looked less like a noblewoman than like a woman who had been holding a very heavy thing above her head for a very long time.

"You are either very brave or very stupid, broker," she said, still not turning. "Coming here. After your locksmith was taken."

"I'm told those are often the same quality."

That earned him a quarter-turn of her head. Not quite amusement. Something adjacent to it, worn thin.

"The Master of Accounts believes you will come for the child's voice tonight. He has tripled the guard on the vault." She paused. "I told him you would come here instead. I told him you were too clever for the obvious door."

"Why?"

Now she turned fully, and Theo saw what the borrowed voice could not disguise: her eyes were tired in the specific way of someone who has not slept alone with their own thoughts in decades. "Because I want to know what you are before he decides what you are. Because—" and here her hand rose, involuntarily, to her own throat, fingers resting against it the way a person touches a bruise they keep forgetting and rediscovering, "—because you looked at me in the hall. When the child sang. Everyone else looked at the child. You looked at me."

"I saw your face break," Theo said quietly.

Something moved behind her expression—anger first, arriving fast and familiar, and then, underneath it, the thing the anger was built to protect.

"Get out," she said, but her voice—the real one, the one under the soprano, low and rough as river stones—betrayed her by staying exactly where it was.

Theo did not get out. Instead he reached into his coat and drew out the half page of the ledger, the torn edge whispering as it caught the air, and laid it on her desk beside the wooden nightingale.

Wren's entry stared up at both of them. *One voice, female child, age six. Sold for forty measures of grain, Ashwinter. Buyer: the Court. Custodian: L. Vesperine.*

"You've read it a hundred times," Vesperine said. "I can tell. The ink is worn where your thumb rests."

"I've read my own entries a hundred times too," Theo said. "In another ledger. Ones I couldn't stop. Ones I watched happen." He met her eyes. "I know what it looks like, the face of someone reading a transaction they can't undo. It looks exactly like yours."

For a long moment the only sound was the faint, ceaseless hum of the mountain itself, that deep tectonic murmur that visitors mistook for silence until they'd been beneath the stone long enough to hear it breathing.

Then Vesperine laughed—one short, bitter note—and the terrible thing happened: the soprano laughed with her, harmonizing, a perfect silver bell of laughter ringing out of her mouth while her actual throat made nothing at all. Two voices, one body, neither of them hers. Theo felt his stomach turn.

"Do you know how I got this voice?" she asked, gesturing at her own face as though the sound lived in her cheekbones. "Everyone asks, eventually. They assume I bought it. That I won it. That I did something clever and cruel, because that is the only story this place tells." She crossed to the vanity and picked up the wooden nightingale, turning it in her fingers. "My grandmother sold it."

Theo went very still.

"Not mine," Vesperine said. "Mine was still in my throat when I was born, screaming like any infant. My grandmother sold the *original*. Generations ago—before the Ashwinter, before your guild had a name, back when the Court still traded openly in the upper world. Our family were singers. Famous ones. There are operas written for throats like ours." Her thumb traced the bird's carved breast. "And my grandmother owed debts. Debts of the old kind, the kind that compound. So she sold the family voice—not her use of it, you understand, the Court doesn't work that way—she sold the *root* of it. The true voice. The one that lives under all the others, the one that makes a song yours instead of merely correct."

"And the Court gave her a replacement," Theo said slowly. Understanding rising in him like cold water. "A loan."

"A loan," Vesperine agreed, "at interest. Compounding across generations. Every daughter born to our line inherited the debt and a voice on loan—a beautiful voice, a *perfect* voice, and none of it ours. Do you know what it is, broker, to sing and feel nothing? To open your mouth and have the whole hall weep while you stand inside yourself like a tenant in a seized house?" She set the bird down with exaggerated gentleness, the way one sets down something one wants to shatter. "I have performed for emperors. I have been called the finest voice in three kingdoms. And I have never once heard myself."

The jars of breath on her shelves seemed suddenly obscene. Theo thought of the ledger of souls in the vault, the hundreds of entries, the children never reclaimed, and understood for the first time that the Court's cruelty was not a machine that someone had built. It was a machine that built more of itself. Grandmother to granddaughter. Creditor to debtor. Each generation taught to hold the whip because the alternative was feeling the lash.

"My parents fled a famine," Theo heard himself say. The words came out before he decided to let them. "They sold nothing. But I trained, as a boy, among people who spoke the First Tongue, and I watched what it cost them, and when I was grown I chose silence instead. Ten years of it. I told myself it was discipline." His mouth twisted. "It was fear wearing discipline's clothes."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked why I looked at you." He stepped closer, carefully, the way one approaches a wild thing deciding whether to bite. "And because I think you already know how this ends for you. The interest on your grandmother's debt. What happens when the loan comes due and there's nothing left of your family to collect from."

Vesperine's hand went to her throat again. This time she did not pretend it was anything but what it was.

"They'll take the loan-voice," she said. "When the line fails. That's the final term—it's written in the original contract, sealed in the deep vault, older than the Master of Accounts himself. When the last of my blood can no longer service the debt, the Court reclaims even the borrowed voice. And I will spend whatever remains of my life as I began it: mute. A woman with a hole where her self used to be." Her laugh came again, and this time she strangled the soprano into silence so that only the raw, ruined real sound escaped—ugly and human and true. "So yes, broker. I know how it ends for me. I have always known. The question is why you think knowing changes anything."

"Because it changed things for me," Theo said. "Three days ago I was a man who catalogued impossible things and asked no questions. Knowing what I'd lost didn't change anything. Choosing did."

"*Choosing.*" She spat the word. "You speak of choice to me, in this place? Where choice is the currency and we are all spent?" She advanced on him, and for a moment Theo saw the creature the Court believed her to be—cold, jeweled, terrible. "You want the child's voice. Fine. Tell me what you offer. Coin? Service? Your precious oath-name that the Master of Accounts has been salivating over since you walked in wearing that ridiculous seal? Everyone offers. Everyone always offers. That is the only prayer this court answers."

"No," Theo said.

Vesperine stopped.

"No," he said again, and reached past her—to the desk, to the small wooden bird—and set it in her hands. "No one offers. Not tonight. Tonight I am asking you a question instead, and it is this: when the guards took my friend, when the Master of Accounts smiled at you across the hall, when you signed the order sealing the vault—did any part of you feel like your grandmother?"

The silence that followed was the longest of Theo's life.

He watched it land. Watched the anger fail to catch, watch the coldness crack along lines that had been forming for years, maybe decades, maybe since a girl had first opened her mouth to sing and heard a stranger's beauty come out. Vesperine looked down at the nightingale in her palms—at the one bird in all her carved doors that had been allowed to keep its tongue—and her shoulders, those imperial shoulders that had borne emperors' applause, began to shake.

"She cried for a week afterward," Vesperine whispered. "My grandmother. I found the letters. She wrote to her sister: *I have given away the well and kept the bucket, and now every morning I draw up nothing, nothing, nothing, and call it water.*" Her fingers closed around the bird until the wood creaked. "She spent sixty years trying to buy it back. She died owing more than when she started. That is what this place does to repentance, broker. It prices it. It compounds it. It makes sure that being sorry costs more than being cruel."

"Then don't be sorry," Theo said. "Be useful."

She looked up. Her eyes were wet and furious and, beneath the fury, terrified in a way that made her look suddenly, shockingly young—the age Wren had been, perhaps, when her parents counted out grain and did not look at her face.

"If I help you," Vesperine said, "if I open the deep vault, if I give you the original contracts—including mine, including the terms the Master of Accounts would burn the mountain to protect—I am not repenting. Repentance is a luxury of people whose debts are paid. I am simply choosing which ruin I stand in." She drew a breath that shook. "His pen writes wounds, broker. You've seen what it does. If he learns what I've done before you're ready—"

"He won't learn it from me."

"He won't need to. This place hears everything. The walls are contracts; the light is collateral; the very dark down here is *owned*." She laughed again, hopeless and bright. "There is no such thing as a secret beneath the mountain. There is only information that hasn't reached the right desk yet."

"Then we move tonight," Theo said. "Before it reaches any desk at all."

Vesperine studied him for a long moment—the forged seal, the worn ledger page, the scarred hands of a man who had spent ten years pretending to be no one. Whatever she was looking for, she appeared to find it, because something in her posture shifted, some load redistributed, as though she had finally set down the heavy thing she'd been holding overhead and discovered her arms still worked.

"The deep vault opens to two keys," she said. "Mine"—she touched the signet at her throat, a black stone that pulsed faintly, horribly, like a heartbeat—"and the Master's pen. He keeps it on him always. Even sleeping. Even—" she paused, and color rose in her face, "—even in moments when a man ought to be unarmed."

"Then we take the vault while he holds audience," Theo said. "Tomorrow at dusk. The whole court attends; he'll be on display, pen in hand, signing petitions. He can't leave the dais without ending the audience early, and a creature like that never surrenders an audience."

Something flickered across Vesperine's face—genuine surprise, and under it, unmistakably, delight. "You've been listening to the rhythms of this place."

"I catalogued impossible things for ten years," Theo said. "Impossible things have rhythms."

She turned to her desk, drew out a sheet of paper, and began to write—then stopped, quill hovering, and looked at him with an expression he would carry for the rest of his unnamed life.

"One condition," she said. "When this is done—when the child has her voice and your locksmith walks free and the contracts burn—find my grandmother's entry in the ledger of souls. Before the fire takes it. Read me her name." Her voice dropped to almost nothing, the real voice, the ruined and honest one. "Sixty years in these halls and I never learned what she was called before she was a debtor. I would like to say it. Once. Out loud. With whatever throat I have left."

Theo bowed—the first full bow he had offered anyone since he'd buried his master's blade beneath a rowan tree a decade ago.

"On my life," he said. "Such as it is."

* * *

He was three corridors from her door, moving fast through the bone-lit dark with Maren's map burning against his ribs, when the mountain's hum changed pitch.

Theo froze. He knew that sound. Every speaker trained in the First Tongue knew it: the harmonic shift that meant a binding was being written somewhere nearby, an oath taken or enforced, a soul entering the books.

Then the chandeliers overhead flared white—all of them, down every corridor, the whole cathedral blazing at once like a struck bell—and a voice rolled through the stone, amplified by a thousand throats of contract, speaking in the dry, precise cadence of a man who had never once raised it:

*"All accounts staff to the Hall of Records. An irregularity has been noted in the deep vault inventory."*

A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk.

*"Lady Vesperine. Attend me. Bring your key."*

Theo pressed himself into an alcove between two jars of bottled breath and closed his eyes, doing the arithmetic he did not want to do. The vault audit was scheduled for tomorrow. The audience at dusk. Everything balanced on a timeline that had just been cut in half.

Somewhere above, boots were already moving.

He had until the Lady of Borrowed Throats reached the Hall of Records—and not one minute more—to decide whether the plan survived, or whether he went now, tonight, alone, through the front door of the deep vault with half a page in his coat and an oath like a coal in his chest, waiting to be spoken.
