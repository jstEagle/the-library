## 17. The Oath Unbound

The page lay on the altar-stone like a wounded bird.

Half of it, anyway—the torn remnant Theo had carried out of the vault, its edges browning where the ink wept. Wren's entry crawled across it in the Court's cramped script: *One voice, First Tongue, sold in the year of Ashwinter for nine measures of grain and one apology.* Below that, the transaction record dissolved into ragged absence, and where absence lived, the words were already dying. He could see it happening as he watched—the letters paling, the grain of the paper graying, as though the page were being unmade from the inside out. Three days, Maren had said. Perhaps less now. The mountain did not keep honest time.

Theo knelt before it with his oath-blade across his knees.

The blade whispered. It had whispered since Ferro first pressed it back into his hands in the tollhouse dark—*you kept this hidden in a wall, you mad quiet man*—and it had not stopped. A low, continuous murmur, like wind through a keyhole, like a name being repeated just below hearing. His master's forge had made it that way, quenching the steel in spoken vows instead of water. Every swordspeaker's blade remembered the oath that shaped it. That was the whole cruelty of the weapon: it would not let you forget what you had promised to never say.

Ten years he had kept it sheathed and silent. Ten years of ledgers and small miracles, of trading other people's memories while his own gathered dust behind a wall in a room he'd paid to have bricked up. He had told himself it was discipline. He had told himself it was penance. Kneeling here, with the dying page beneath his hands and Wren asleep against Ferro's coat in the corner of the ruined chapel they'd found in the undermountain's ribs, he could finally admit what it had actually been.

It had been fear. Nothing more ornate than that.

"Theo." Maren's voice, soft, from the chapel door. She still wouldn't quite meet his eyes—not since the betrayal, not since the ambush, not since Ferro had shoved him through the collapsing corridor and been taken in his place. But she had come back. She had mapped them a path through three miles of living stone rather than let him walk into the Court's teeth alone, and there was a kind of forgiveness in cartography that words could never manage. "The patrols have doubled. Vesperine's collectors are sweeping the deep galleries. Whatever you're going to do—"

"I know."

"You can't know. You weren't there when the old order fell. You didn't see what they do to oath-breakers who *speak*." She crossed the chapel, her boots silent on bone-pale flagstone, and crouched beside him. Up close her face was hollowed, gray-lit by the veins of luminous fungus along the walls. "They don't kill you, Theo. That's the mercy no one tells you about. They make you *useful*. They write your name into their collections and then every word you ever speak belongs to someone wearing it as jewelry."

He looked at her. For a long moment the only sound was the blade's whisper and Wren's slow breathing and the distant, tectonic groan of the mountain turning over in its sleep.

"Then I'll be useful," Theo said. "For once in my life, I'll be exactly as useful as I choose to be."

Maren exhaled—a laugh, almost, wrung out of dread. "You've been rehearsing that line for ten years, haven't you."

"Since the day I took the vow."

* * *

Wren woke an hour before he meant to wake her.

He found her sitting up in the dark, drawing again—she always drew when the silence pressed too hard, charcoal stubs Ferro had whittled for her worn down to thumbnails. The floor around her was covered in pictures: the guild's crooked doorway, Sable Vane scowling over her spectacles, the jar of borrowed courage on its shelf. And newest, still wet at the edges, a figure standing before a great dark mouth of stone, holding something that caught light.

She looked up at him. Then at the blade. Then back at him, and her eyes—her steady, unchildlike eyes—asked the question she could not.

"It's the only way," Theo said. He sat down across from her, cross-legged, the way his master had made him sit when the lessons got hard. "The page is half a contract. Half a contract isn't broken law—it's *open* law. To finish it, someone has to complete the terms aloud, in the old words, witnessed by the ledger itself. And the old words are..." He turned the blade over. Firelight from the fungus-veins ran along its fuller like a vein of captive dawn. "The old words are my oath. All of it. Spoken whole, from the first syllable to the last. There's no partial way to say it. There never was. That was the design."

Wren's charcoal hovered. Then, deliberately, she wrote on the stone between them:

**WHAT HAPPENS TO YOU?**

He smiled, and it hurt. "You've learned to ask the dangerous questions."

**FERRO ASKED THEM TOO. HE'S GONE.**

"He's not gone." Theo's voice came out harder than he intended. "He bargained. He's buying us time with the only coin he has left, and when this is done, I'm going to buy every memory back that they took from him, even if I have to sell the guild to do it. But you're right. If I speak the oath, everything changes. Every debt-collector of the old order will hear it. Every one of them bound by the same words I'm breaking. It's a bell rung in a room full of wolves, Wren. There's no unsounding it."

She considered him for a long moment. Then she set down the charcoal, took his scarred hand in both of hers—small, warm, impossibly steady—and pressed it flat against the dying page.

The paper shivered under his palm. He felt the words fading beneath his skin like a pulse weakening.

**I WANT MY VOICE,** she wrote afterward, in strokes so fierce the charcoal snapped. **EVEN IF IT HURTS ME. YOU TAUGHT ME THAT ON THE BRIDGE. HOLD THE WORDS IN UNTIL THEY'RE WORTH SAYING. THESE ARE WORTH IT.**

"And if saying mine costs everything?"

**THEN IT WAS NEVER YOURS TO KEEP BY HIDING IT.**

Theo stared at her. Somewhere in the mountain's throat, water dripped in a rhythm like a patient clock. And he understood, with the strange clarity that arrives only at the edge of ruin, that a mute child had just handed him back his own philosophy, sharpened, and asked him why he wasn't using it.

"All right," he said quietly. "All right."

* * *

They chose the cathedral itself.

It was madness—Maren said so twice, mapping the route with shaking hands—but it was also the only ground where the completion would hold. The ledger of souls was anchored there; the Court's power pooled in that bone-hung nave like light in a bowl. A contract finished anywhere else could be contested, re-litigated, drowned in procedure. Finished there, before the ledger itself, it became irrevocable. Law made flesh. Debt called due.

So they went down the moving corridors Maren had memorized, through galleries of root and salt, past alcoves where dead languages murmured in the walls—and Theo heard his master's voice among them, as he always did, and this time he did not flinch away. *You taught me to hold the words in,* he thought toward the darkness. *Watch me spend them.*

The cathedral opened above them like the inside of a lung.

Bone chandeliers hung in their hundreds, each one strung with voices—little glass vials and hollowed vertebrae, whispering, singing, muttering in stolen timbres. The nobles were absent; the hour between courts, Vesperine's note had promised, bought them an empty nave. Only the ledger remained, vast on its altar of fused spines, pages turning themselves in a wind that did not exist. And only the guards—no. Not guards.

Debt-collectors.

They stood in the shadows between chandeliers, dozens of them, tall and wrong, draped in coats stitched from contracts. Their faces were blank as unsigned paper. They had heard something coming. They were waiting to hear what.

Theo walked down the center aisle alone.

Maren waited at the doors with Wren, and the look the child gave him over Maren's shoulder was the last thing he let himself see clearly: not afraid. *Certain.* He put his hand on the hilt of the oath-blade and drew it, and the whisper became a ring, a clear struck-note that rolled down the nave and set every stolen voice in the chandeliers trembling.

The nearest collector tilted its blank head.

"Step forward, then," Theo said, and his voice sounded strange to him—loud, unguarded, *his*, in a way it hadn't been since he was nineteen years old and kneeling in a forge. "Witness me. All of you. You were made to collect oaths—so collect this one."

He raised the blade until it caught the ledger-light, and he began.

The first words came out rusted. Ten years of disuse, ten years of swallowing them, and they tore his throat on the way up like blades drawn backward from a wound. But the blade rang with each syllable, amplifying, harmonizing, and the old order had built its weapons well: an oath spoken on an oath-blade could not be mumbled, could not be softened, could not be lied. It filled the cathedral. It shook dust from the bone chandeliers. It made the vials of stolen voices rattle in their housings like teeth.

*"I swear my silence to the sword and my sword to the sworn—"*

A collector moved. Another. From the shadowed galleries above, more came—dozens, scores, pouring down the buttresses like ink poured down glass, summoned by the very words they existed to enforce. Theo did not stop. Stopping was death; stopping was worse than death, it was meaninglessness, it was ten years wasted in a single swallowed breath.

*"—my name to the order and my order to the end of things—"*

His name. Saying it was like driving a nail through his own hand. *Theo Thornbury,* spoken aloud for the first time in a decade, and the moment the syllables left him he felt them *leave*—felt the name tear loose from his chest like a tooth from the jaw, felt it fly up into the dark and hang there, visible, legible, *claimable*. The collectors surged faster. One of them was already writing, a pen scratching against the air itself, drafting the instrument of his forfeiture.

*"—and I break this oath freely, wholly, before the ledger and the law—"*

The ledger on its altar slammed open to a fresh page. The wind that turned it screamed.

*"—because a child was sold for grain, and I was silent, and silence was my sin—"*

He was weeping. He hadn't known it. The words came out of him burning, each one a coal pulled glowing from the forge where he'd been made, and the blade sang so loudly now that the chandeliers began to crack, hairline fractures spidering through bone and glass, stolen voices leaking out in thin escaping threads of song.

*"—so take my name, take my silence, take the ten years I buried—take all of it as payment, and render unto the child what the child is owed!"*

The last word left him and the cathedral went white.

Not light—*law*. The air itself became parchment; the pressure of a thousand simultaneous transactions crushed down like deep water. Theo stood in the center of it with an empty sheath and an emptier chest, hollowed, anonymous already in his own ears—he reached for his own name and found the shape of it, the place where it had been, and nothing else. The collectors froze mid-stride, their pens hovering, their blank faces flickering like pages in wind. Somewhere above, Lady Vesperine's private box shattered open, and a voice that was not yet hers and not yet anyone's rose out of the wreckage, singing.

And on the altar, the torn half-page drank the completed oath like rain on cracked earth.

Ink flooded back into the faded letters. The transaction record rewrote itself, term by term, the ledger accepting the payment Theo had offered against the debt of a child's voice—one name, fully surrendered, against one voice, fully restored. The balance sheet of his life closed with a sound like a book snapping shut.

The lead collector lowered its pen. When it spoke, its voice was every voice in the chandeliers at once.

*"Payment tendered. Claim... contested."*

Contested. Theo swayed, gripping the altar's edge, and understood a heartbeat too late: the Court Beneath the Mountain had never lost a debtor to honesty before. It did not intend to begin tonight.

From the galleries above, the nobles came flooding down in their silk and their stolen throats, and the Master of Accounts stepped into the candlelight with a pen that gleamed like a scalpel, smiling the smile of a creditor who has just discovered a loophole.

"Bold," the Master said, in a voice assembled from a hundred dead singers. "But incomplete. You surrendered a name, little oath-breaker. You did not surrender the *debt*."

Behind Theo, at the far doors, Wren opened her mouth—and for the first time in years, something answered from inside her.
