## 18. Storm of Debts Called Due

The last word of the oath still hung in the cathedral air like a struck bell that refused to stop ringing.

Theo felt it in his teeth, in the hollow of his chest where his name had lived for forty years. Speaking it had been like pulling a thread from the weave of himself, and now the whole garment trembled. Around him, the bone chandeliers shuddered on their chains, and every shadow in the Court Beneath the Mountain turned toward him with the hunger of things that had waited a very long time to collect.

*Debt-collectors of the old order.* His master's voice, dead these ten years, had described them once: *They are not people, Theo. They are what remains when a person is nothing but what they owe.*

They came out of the walls.

Not through doors—through the mortar itself, peeling free of the cathedral's bones like ink lifting off wet paper. They were tall and wrong-jointed, wrapped in ledgers' worth of parchment that whispered against their limbs, and where their faces should have been there were only tallies: rows of small carved marks, thousands upon thousands, counting something no one would ever finish counting. Each one carried an instrument. A scale. A shears. A small brass bell that rang notes of pure obligation.

And at their head, descending the dais steps with the unhurried grace of a man who has never once been late for anything, came the Master of Accounts.

He was smaller than Theo expected. Neat. Grey-robed, grey-faced, with spectacles perched on a nose like a folded receipt. In his right hand he held a pen—long, black, its nib gleaming wet—and Theo's skin crawled because he recognized the make. Oath-steel. The same metal as the blade hidden under his coat. The pen was to writing what his sword was to speech: a weapon from before the world learned to be careful.

"Thornbury," the Master said, and the name came out of his mouth like a coin spat onto a counter. "The last swordspeaker. Do you know how long your oath has sat in our vaults? Interest accrues, you understand. Ten years of it." He smiled thinly. "You have made yourself very expensive tonight."

Behind Theo, Wren pressed against his leg. She could not scream—her voice was still caged somewhere in this cathedral, in a vault or a throat or a ledger line—but her hands gripped his coat with a strength that said everything her mouth could not. *Don't give me back. Don't let them have me.*

"I'm not here to pay," Theo said. "I'm here to close the account."

The Master of Accounts sighed, the way a clerk sighs at a customer who insists on arguing arithmetic. He raised the pen.

"Then we proceed to enforcement."

* * *

The first collector reached him before the echo of the word *enforcement* died.

Theo drew the oath-blade, and it sang—not a metaphor, not poetry, but an actual ringing note that made the nearest chandelier of femurs rattle apart. The blade remembered every vow ever spoken over it, and right now it remembered his, fresh and bleeding, and it was *glad*. Swordspeakers' blades always were. That was the thing no one told you about oaths: they didn't weigh you down. They burned.

He cut the first collector from tally-crown to parchment-hem. It did not bleed. It *unraveled*, coming apart into strips of contract-paper that fluttered and dissolved, and for one heartbeat Theo allowed himself to think it might be easy.

Then the rest of them arrived all at once.

There was no room for thought after that. The cathedral floor became a churn of grey limbs and white bone-dust and the whispering of ten thousand pages. Theo fought the way his master had beaten into him—not with flourish but with economy, each cut a sentence, each parry a refusal. The collectors did not tire and did not fear, but they *hesitated* around the blade, flinching from the oath still written into its edge, and hesitation was a kind of wound, and wounds were a language Theo spoke fluently.

A shears snipped at his sleeve and took a strip of memory with it—he felt the absence like a pulled tooth, some afternoon, some warm room, gone before he could name it. He killed that one twice as hard.

Across the nave, chaos of a different order. Maren stood atop a fallen pew with her maps spread around her like wings, shouting directions in three languages, two of them dead. "Left corridor's a lie—it folds back on itself—take the arch with the cracked keystone!" Collectors swarmed toward her and slid off the edges of her cartography as though the floor itself had been redrawn to disagree with their footing. But there were too many, and even her moving places could not hold them forever.

And Ferro—

Ferro came out of the dark like a landslide given opinions.

Theo heard him before he saw him: a bellow, a crash, the particular shriek of a vault door being persuaded open by someone who considered locks a personal insult. Ferro emerged from the side gallery dragging half a chain behind him, blood sheeting down one arm from manacles he had clearly opened by breaking his own wrists' worth of skin rather than wait for a key, and his eyes found Theo across the battlefield with a ferocity that needed no words.

"You," Ferro roared, wrenching a collector's arm off at the elbow and using it as a club, "owe me," *crack*, "one daughter's face," *crack*, "and I have come to COLLECT!"

Something in Theo's chest cracked along with the collector's bones. He had no time to honor it. Three more of the creatures closed on him, and then—

Wren stepped out from behind him.

She raised both hands, palms out, and *hummed*. Not the First Tongue—not fully; she had spent so little of herself, and Theo's heart seized—but a fragment of it, a single held note that she had kept hidden even from him, saved like a coin sewn into a hem. The note hit the three collectors like a wave hits sandcastles. Their tallied faces split. Their parchment unwound. And in the sudden silence, Wren swayed, went grey, and Theo caught her before she fell, lowering her behind an overturned font with a gentleness that contradicted everything else his body was doing.

"Hold on," he told her. "Almost done."

Her eyes, fever-bright, fixed on the dais. On the vault door beyond it. On the ledger of souls that lay somewhere inside, and on the half-page in Theo's coat that was dissolving even now, its ink crawling like insects dying.

Three days. They had perhaps three minutes.

* * *

The Master of Accounts watched the battle the way a banker watches weather: with interest, but without alarm.

"The girl spends herself," he observed, as Wren collapsed. "Remarkable collateral. You understand, of course, that every word she uses diminishes the asset I am being asked to return? You are destroying value, Thornbury. There will be penalties."

"There always are," Theo said, and came at him.

The duel, if it could be called that, began in the space between two heartbeats. The Master did not dodge like a fighter; he dodged like an *edit*. Theo's first cut passed through the space where the man had been and found only air that smelled of ink. The second was deflected by the pen itself—a sound like a nail dragged across slate, a sound that made Theo's fillings ache—and then the nib flicked toward him, quick as a striking adder, and wrote.

It wrote on the air. It wrote on Theo.

He felt the stroke land across his forearm like a line of fire, and when he looked down, there it was: a thin black line of script sunk into his skin, reading *laceration*, and beneath the word, obediently, the laceration opened. Blood welled along the letters as though his flesh were paper taking its instruction.

"Words of power," the Master said mildly, circling. "Yours require the voice. Mine require only a hand. Tell me—which of us has been more economical?"

Theo answered with the blade.

What followed was the hardest fight of his life, and he had once spent a winter dueling a river spirit for the right to cross a bridge. The Master rewrote reality in strokes: a slash of the pen and the flagstones under Theo's feet read *slick* and became ice; another and the air between them read *heavy* and Theo fought through syrup. Theo gave ground, gave more, gave blood—each exchange costing him a new written wound, a *bruise* blooming purple along his ribs, a *fatigue* sinking into his shoulders like wet sand—while the oath-blade sang and sang and found nothing to cut but air and ink.

But the Master had grown old behind a desk. And Theo had grown old in the world.

He feinted high—the oldest lie in fencing, the first thing his master ever taught him, *the body is honest but the wrist can sin*—and the Master's pen darted up to intercept, and Theo dropped low and drove his shoulder into the grey robe's sternum and took him down onto the dais steps with all his weight and all his grief behind it.

The pen skittered free. Theo's hand closed around it first.

For a moment they knelt there, forehead to forehead, the swordspeaker and the accountant, breathing the same bone-dust air.

"Do you know what I keep in my vaults?" the Master whispered. His spectacles had cracked; one lens showed Theo his own eye, magnified and wild. "Every voice. Every name. Every promise anyone ever regretted. The Court does not hoard these things out of cruelty, swordspeaker. We hoard them because *someone must*. Unsecured souls wander. Unwritten debts go unpaid. Order—"

"Is just a word," Theo said, "for who gets to hold the pen."

He drove the pen's nib into the dais stone and wrote, in letters that glowed like banked coals, four words:

***ALL CONTRACTS HEREBY VOID.***

* * *

The cathedral screamed.

Not the collectors—they simply stopped, mid-motion, and came apart like puppets whose strings had been itemized and repossessed. It was the building itself that screamed, every bone in every chandelier vibrating at once, because the Court Beneath the Mountain was not a place. It was a *contract*, and Theo had just broken its spine.

Above them, the ceiling began to fold inward, stone and bone collapsing toward a center point that had suddenly lost its reason to exist. The vault door groaned, warped, and burst—not outward, but *upward*, sucked into the implosion along with everything else it contained. Ledgers, coins, jars of voices, centuries of collected souls—all of it streaming into the closing wound in the world like water down a drain.

"No—" Maren was on her feet, maps flying. "The exits! If it seals while we're inside—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Theo saw her make the choice in the set of her shoulders: she flung her arms wide over her scattered maps, and every map she owned—every moving place she had ever charted, every road home she had ever drawn—blazed up in light around her, and she *pulled*.

The Court's exit routes, the corridors that shifted and lied, the passages that had betrayed them—Maren seized them all in her cartographer's grip and collapsed them herself, deliberately, folding the ways out into nothing, sealing the implosion into a clean and finite shape rather than letting it swallow the mountain and everything above it. Her nose bled freely. Her maps burned to ash one by one, each one a place she would never find again.

"Ferro!" she shouted through the roar. "The girl! The east wall—there's still one true road—I mapped it years ago—it's the only thing I never sold them!"

Ferro didn't ask how she knew he'd reach it. He simply scooped Wren up under one arm like a sack of grain, grabbed Theo's collar with his ruined hand, and ran.

Behind them, Lady Vesperine stood alone on the melting dais, her stolen singing voice rising from her throat at last—her own voice, returned to her by the voiding of every contract—and she sang against the collapse, one long clear note that held the ceiling back for exactly as long as they needed.

Theo looked back once. He saw her singing in the ruin, free and doomed and magnificent, and he memorized her face, because someone should.

Then the east wall opened onto Maren's one true road, and daylight—actual daylight, grey and cold and impossibly sweet—spilled down the passage ahead of them, and they ran into it as the Court Beneath the Mountain finished dying at their backs.

* * *

They came out onto a hillside under a bruised morning sky, coughing, bleeding, alive.

Wren stirred in Ferro's arms and opened her mouth—and paused, frowning, touching her own throat with wonder, because the half-page in Theo's coat had gone still, its ink settled, its terms fulfilled. Something had come back to her. Not everything. But something.

She tried to speak. What came out was a croak, a rusted hinge of a sound, and then—faint, cracked, but unmistakably hers—one syllable of the First Tongue, spoken freely, belonging to no court and no ledger.

Theo's knees nearly gave. He put a hand against a boulder and breathed.

Ferro lowered the girl to the grass. Maren sat down hard beside them, wiping blood from her face, leaving red streaks like map-lines across her cheeks. For a long moment none of them said anything. The wind moved the moor grass. Somewhere below, the mountain settled into its grave with a sound like a book closing.

Then Wren tugged Theo's sleeve.

He looked down. She was pointing—at him, at his chest, with an expression of growing confusion. Her lips moved. No sound came out, but the shape of them was clear enough: she was trying to say his name. *Theo.* She tried again. Her brow furrowed deeper.

Ferro frowned too. "Oi. Thornbury. You alright?" And then Ferro's own face went strange, the words snagging somewhere behind his teeth. "...Thorn... Th..." He shook his head like a man clearing water from his ears. "That's odd."

Maren reached for her satchel, for a map, for anything with his name on it, and found only blank vellum.

Theo touched his own chest, where the pen's final stroke had landed unbidden in the last moment before the vault imploded—a stroke he had never seen, aimed at the one target he had left exposed: himself. He could feel the absence where his name used to be, smooth and hollow, like a tooth-socket healed over.

The ledger had taken its payment after all.

Wren's hand found his. She looked up at him—this child who had lost her voice to a famine and a court, who knew better than anyone in the world what it meant to have a piece of yourself sold away—and her eyes filled, and she squeezed his fingers, and she did not look away.

Below them, the road home waited, long and unmapped.

And Theo Thornbury—whoever he had been—could not remember a single reason to walk it alone.
