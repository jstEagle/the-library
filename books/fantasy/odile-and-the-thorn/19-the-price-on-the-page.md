## 19. The Price on the Page

The pen of the Master of Accounts wrote a wound across Theo's forearm, and the pain arrived a heartbeat late, like thunder after lightning—because the wound had not been made in flesh first. It had been made in the ledger, and his body was only obeying.

Theo staggered back along the bone dais, blood sheeting warm over his knuckles, and the Master of Accounts followed with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once been surprised. Around them, the cathedral of the Court Beneath the Mountain rang with war. Chandeliers of femur and rib swung overhead, casting wheeling shadows that turned the marble floor into a sea of drowning figures. Debt-collectors poured through the shattered doors in their grey vestments, ledgers chained to their belts, quills drawn like stilettos. Ferro's roar came from somewhere near the vault stairs—he had torn free of his bindings with nothing but stubbornness and a stolen key, and now he fought like a man reclaiming every memory the tollhouse had ever taxed from him. And at the far end of the hall, Maren stood atop a collapsed archway with her maps burning in her hands, feeding the great doors page by page so that no route out would answer to the Court again.

But none of it mattered if Theo fell here.

"You cannot win," said the Master of Accounts. His voice was dry as a closing book. "You are a debt, swordspeaker. A debt walking upright. Every word you have ever spoken under oath is an entry in my ledger, and entries do not negotiate."

Theo spat blood onto the marble. "I've been told I'm bad at negotiating."

The pen moved. Theo threw himself sideways and the stroke missed him by a finger's width, splitting the dais behind him into two clean halves, as though the stone itself had been found delinquent. He rolled, came up with his oath-blade in hand—and the blade *sang*. Not the whisper it had given him for ten years, hidden in its wrappings beneath the guild floor. This was the full voice, the one he had unbound when he spoke his oath aloud not an hour past, and it filled the cathedral like a struck bell, making the bone chandeliers tremble and the collectors flinch and cover their ears.

Because they were creatures of contract, and the blade was pure oath, and oaths were the one currency the Court could not debase.

"Interesting," said the Master of Accounts. For the first time, something moved behind his eyes—not fear, but the flicker of an accountant finding an error in a column that had balanced for centuries. "You have made yourself loud, swordspeaker. Loud things can be priced."

"I know." Theo circled left, keeping the dais between them. His arm burned; the wound had begun to write words along its edges, tiny characters surfacing in the cut like fish rising in a pond. *Delinquent. Sworn. Forfeit.* The pen was trying to finish the entry. "That's rather the point."

He had understood it in the moment Vesperine had told him the truth of her own stolen voice—that everything in this place was bought and sold and *recorded*, and that recording was the Court's true power. Not the collectors. Not the vaults. The ink. As long as Wren's voice sat in the ledger of souls, it belonged to the Court, no matter how many locks Ferro picked or how many corridors Maren unmapped. He held half a page in his coat, the torn record of her sale, dissolving even now at its ragged edge, three days bleeding down to three hours. To complete it he needed the whole truth written and witnessed and *paid for*.

And there was only one payment large enough to settle a soul's ransom against a court that collected oaths like coin.

A name. His name. The oath-name his master had burned into him at fourteen, kneeling in the snow, the anchor of every word of power he could speak. *Theo Thornbury, last swordspeaker sworn to silence.* If he wrote that name out of existence—if he erased himself from every ledger that held him, including this one, including the Court's claim—

Then there would be no Theo Thornbury to owe anything. And the transaction balancing against Wren's voice would have no counterweight. The books would scream. The books would break.

And so would he.

"Vesperine tried to warn me," Theo said, moving, always moving, giving the pen nothing still to write upon. "She said you'd offer me anything I wanted for my name. She was right about what it costs. She just didn't know I'd already decided to pay it."

"The name is all you are," said the Master of Accounts. "Erase it and you will not remember your own reflection. Your friends will look at you and see a stranger. Every kindness done to you will have been done to no one."

"Yes," Theo said. "I've read the terms."

He let the blade sing louder. Let it call every oath he had ever kept to the surface of the air—the silence of ten years, the vow never to speak the old words, the promise he'd broken to save a mute child who hummed syllables that made ink crawl. The cathedral shuddered. Somewhere above, a chandelier of ribs cracked along its length.

The Master of Accounts did the one thing accountants do when the numbers turn against them: he reached for his ledger to make a final entry.

Theo was faster.

He closed the distance in three strides, caught the Master's wrist with his wounded hand—blood smearing across pale skin, his own name half-written in the wound—and drove the oath-blade not through flesh but through the great ledger itself, pinning it open to the final page. Then, with his free hand, he tore the pen from the Master's grip.

It struggled. Oh, it struggled—it squirmed like a living thing, like a throat being held, and it wrote a screaming line of pain up Theo's palm as he seized it. But the blade's song held it still, oath against instrument, and Theo dipped the stolen pen in his own welling blood, and he wrote.

*Theo Thornbury.*

His own name, in his own hand, on the ledger of sold things.

Then he drew a single line through it.

The sound the cathedral made was not a sound. It was the absence of one—a vast inhale, as though the whole mountain had been holding its breath since the first bargain was ever struck. Every collector froze mid-stroke. Every chandelier dimmed. In the vault below, Theo felt rather than heard hundreds of pages flutter at once, like birds startled from a tree, as his name burned itself out of record after record after record. The guild's ledger, leagues away, would show a blank where his entry had been. The old order's rolls, the debt-brokers' registries, the Court's claim on him—all of it unwriting, all of it coming loose like thread pulled from a hem.

The Master of Accounts stared at the struck-through name, and for the first time in his long dry existence, he looked afraid.

"What are you?" he whispered.

"No one," Theo said. And the strange thing, the terrible thing, was how much of the words remained true even as he spoke them—how much of *him* was already thinning, already going soft at the edges like wet paper. He could feel his master's face going. He could feel the snow of that long-ago kneel going. He gripped the half-page in his coat—the record of Wren—and completed it with the last strength the pen allowed: the buyer, the price, the grain that had bought a child's voice during the Ashwinter famine, and beneath it, in letters that burned:

*Paid in full. Claim void. Witnessed by no one, because no one remains.*

The page sealed itself with a sound like a kiss.

And Wren screamed.

She had been hiding behind Ferro's abandoned manacles, small and silent as she had been since the night she arrived at the guild door—but the moment the claim voided, her voice came home to her all at once, ten years of it, every word she had never spoken arriving in a single note. The scream went up through the cathedral and the chandeliers *cracked*, bone splitting along ancient seams, raining white shards across the marble. Collectors scattered. The Master of Accounts covered his ears and sank to his knees before his ruined ledger. The sound was grief and joy and rage braided together, a child's voice stretched to the size of a mountain's grief, and Theo stood in the middle of it and felt it pass through him like weather, like forgiveness, like loss.

When it faded, the silence that followed was an ordinary silence, human-sized, and in it Theo heard the most ordinary and extraordinary thing in the world: a little girl, sobbing, saying words.

"—want my mama—" crack, sob, breath, "*—want my mama, want my papa, why did they sell me, why—*" and then smaller, wrecked, disbelieving: "*I can talk. I can talk. I can talk.*"

Ferro reached her first, dropping to his knees despite the blood on him, and she crashed into his arms still saying it, over and over, *I can talk, I can talk*, and Ferro—who had given away his daughter's face at a tollhouse and would never get it back—held her and wept without any shame at all.

Maren came down from the archway slowly, her hands still smoking with burnt map, and looked at Theo across the ruined hall. Her expression was the worst thing in the room: not horror, not gratitude, but recognition. She knew what he had done. She knew what it cost. And she could not tell him so, because—

Because she opened her mouth and said, "Thank you," and Theo watched the words hit him like arrows striking water. He felt gratitude land on him. He felt it belong to someone. He could not say who.

"Who—" he began, and stopped, because the question had no shape. Who was asking? Who was being thanked?

He looked down at his hands. They were steady hands, scarred, familiar in the way a tool is familiar. He knew how to hold the blade. He could not remember who had given it to him.

The Master of Accounts was crawling away into the dark beneath the dais, dragging his broken ledger, and Theo supposed someone should stop him, and supposed also that it no longer mattered. Without names, the Court could collect nothing. Already the walls were beginning to soften, contracts voiding by the hundred, the cathedral's bone architecture groaning as the mountain considered swallowing its own tenant. Maren shouted something urgent about exits and daylight and *now*, and her voice was kind and meant for him, he was almost sure it was meant for him, and he followed because following was a thing hands and feet remembered even when hearts forgot.

Wren broke away from Ferro and ran to him—ran to the stranger with the sword—and took his wounded hand in both of hers, and looked up at him with red-rimmed eyes that were, impossibly, laughing.

"You did it," she said. Her voice was hoarse and new and cracked on every second word, and it was the most beautiful sound Theo had ever heard, though some quiet, hollowed room inside him noted that he could not remember having heard beautiful things before, or rather, that he had heard them but could not remember *who they had happened to*. "You gave it back. You gave *me* back."

"I—" Theo started, and found nothing there. No speech prepared. No clever broker's deflection. Just a blank page where a man used to be.

Wren studied his face the way she had once studied the ledger ink that crawled at her humming. Then she squeezed his hand harder.

"Don't be scared," she said. "I remember you. Even if you don't. I'll remember for both of us. That's a deal, okay? That's a bargain. And bargains are *binding*."

Somewhere far above, faint as a rumor, daylight touched the mouth of the road home.

* * *

They climbed for hours through corridors that Maren mapped aloud as they went, her voice steady, naming turns and stairwells into the empty air as though writing on the world itself. The Court collapsed inward behind them with a long, grinding sigh, contracts voiding floor by floor, until the dark simply gave up and became ordinary stone. Ferro carried Wren on his shoulders when her legs gave out. Theo walked at the rear and tried, at each turning, to catch his own reflection in the wet rock, and each time found only a stranger looking back—someone tall, someone tired, someone with a swordspeaker's calluses and a hole where a life had been.

At the last threshold, where the tunnel opened onto rain-washed moorland and a sky gone grey-gold with dawn, Wren slid down from Ferro's shoulders and stood in the wet grass breathing like someone tasting air for the first time. Then she turned around and took Theo's hand again, firmly, the way one takes hold of something valuable that might otherwise be misplaced.

"We need to call you something," she said. "Everyone needs a name. That's how the ledgers find you, Ferro says. But maybe that's also how people find you." She frowned, thinking hard, her new voice roughening with use. "Thornbury is gone. I heard it go. So—" She looked up at him, and dawn caught her face, and she smiled with the whole reckless courage of a child who had once traded drawings for words and was done trading. "So we'll start smaller. *Thorn.* Just Thorn. Sharp and prickly and hard to pull out once it gets in you." She nodded, satisfied, sealing it. "That's your name now. I gave it to you. Which means it's a gift, and gifts don't go in ledgers."

Behind them, the mountain settled into silence. Ahead, the moors ran green and endless toward a road that Maren swore led home—to a guild full of misfits, to a guildmaster waiting with questions she must never ask, to a fresh page in a ledger that had a blank space where a man used to be.

Thorn stood at the threshold between the dark and the morning, nameless in every book in the world except one, held in the grip of a small girl who had decided he belonged to her.

"Come on," Wren said, tugging his hand. "Home's this way. I'll say it out loud the whole way, so you learn it. *Home's this way. Home's this way.*"

And Thorn, who could not remember his own reflection, followed the sound of a voice that had been worth everything, toward a door he could not remember opening, where someone waited who would write his name down—not as an asset, not as a debt, but as kin.
