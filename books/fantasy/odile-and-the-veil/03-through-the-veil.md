## 3. Through the Veil

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in the margins of everything — that I did not run to the border like a madman. I packed a bag first. That is the difference between a scholar and a lunatic: lunatics don't bring spare ink.

The bag took me an hour to pack and most of that hour was spent arguing with myself about what a rational man brings to investigate a flicker of light on a salt boundary. Rations, obviously. A second cloak, because the valley's nights turn cruel even in high summer. My good pens, the goose-quill ones that hold a line without spitting. The prophecy book went into the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, because whatever else it was, it was evidence, and evidence gets ruined by weather.

And then, because I am apparently the kind of man who does this now, I brought a third cloak, folded flat, for whoever or whatever was standing on the other side of that border looking back at me.

"You're not going," I told myself in the mirror, in the firm voice I use on apprentices who've been drinking the survey brandy.

"I'm going at dawn," I answered, "with a full survey kit, and I'm taking measurements, and if anything moves toward me I'm walking backward very fast."

We shook on it. Neither of us believed us.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and ordinary, which I took as a good omen. Ordinary dawn meant an ordinary world, and ordinary worlds do not eat cartographers before breakfast. I walked the south road out of Hollowmere with my staff clicking against the stones and nodded to Old Brammett, who was dragging his nets down to the mill pond for reasons he has never once explained to my satisfaction.

"Off mapping, Brightwater?" he called.

"Always mapping," I said.

"Mind the salt line. My grandfather said his grandfather saw a man step over it once and come back wrong."

"Your grandfather's grandfather also claimed he'd wrestled a river spirit," I said. "In a valley where nothing magical can cross the border."

"Aye," Brammett said comfortably. "That's how you know it was true. Nothing here to wrestle *with*."

I left him to his nets and his impossible ancestors and walked on, telling myself the whole way that this was exactly the kind of folklore I had spent twenty years collecting and cataloguing and gently deflating. The Veiled Valley is famous — insofar as anywhere is famous when its neighbors are more rumor than geography — for one thing only: magic cannot cross its borders. Not won't. *Can't.* Spells fizzle at the salt line like sparks hitting wet stone. Enchanted things go dull. Once, according to Pellam Oxley's heretical little histories, a sorcerer tried to fly over the boundary and simply... stopped being able to fly, mid-air, at altitude. The valley keeps no record of what happened next, because the valley keeps no records of anything interesting, by design.

I had mapped that boundary three times in my life. Every stone, every salt-post, every marker my predecessors had driven into the earth going back two hundred years. It was the most boring map I owned. Borders are usually the exciting part of a map; mine was a long gray line annotated with things like SALT POST 44, LEANING and HERE THE GROUND IS WET FOR NO REASON.

I reached Post 44 just past midmorning. It leaned, as advertised. The ground was wet for no reason, as advertised. And the air above the border —

Well. In daylight, with the sun up and Brammett's nets visible half a mile behind me, there was nothing. Just the shimmer that always hung over the salt line, the heat-haze effect that isn't heat haze because it doesn't move with the temperature, the faint wrongness that every child in the valley learns to see past the way you learn to see past your own nose.

I sat down on a rock, ate bread and hard cheese, and waited.

This is the part of the story where, in any honest account, the narrator admits how long he sat there doing nothing. Three hours. I measured the shimmer four times. I sketched Post 44 from six angles. I caught myself narrating my own boredom in the voice I use for field notes — *subject remains quiescent; observer's sanity holding, barely* — and made myself stop, because talking to yourself at a haunted border is how you end up as someone else's folklore.

It was late afternoon when the light changed.

Not sunset-light. Sunset comes slantwise and gold. This came from underneath, from the ground itself, as though someone had lit a lamp beneath the skin of the world. The shimmer over the salt line brightened, steadied, and — there is no other word for it — *breathed*. In. Out. In.

I stood up so fast I knocked over my ink bottle and didn't care.

Last night, at dusk, something on the other side had flickered back at me. Now, in the long amber light, the whole border was awake. And I could see — I pressed my knuckles against my eyes and looked again and I could still see — shapes in the shimmer. Depth. Distance. As if the border weren't a wall at all but a window, and someone had finally cleaned the glass.

My hands were shaking. I noted that too, because noting things is how I keep from screaming.

"Right," I said aloud. "Measurements."

I got out my instruments. I actually did. I measured the luminance of the shimmer against my standard grayscales, and the numbers were nonsense — values off the top of every scale I owned, values that would have gotten my charts laughed out of the Guildhall. I measured the temperature: unchanged. The humidity: unchanged. The distance across the border, which I had measured a hundred times and which had always been simply *the width of the salt line*, eleven feet of consecrated ground:

The instrument said forty feet. Then sixty. Then it said a number I declined to write down, because writing it down would make it real, and I was not ready for it to be real.

The light breathed again. Closer, this time. Or larger. Or both.

Here is what I should have done. I should have packed my instruments, walked back down the south road, found Pellam Oxley, put the prophecy book in his heretical hands, and let the two of us be frightened together in a room with locks on the door. That was the plan. That had always been the plan. Dawn, measurements, backward very fast.

But the light breathed a third time, and in the space between one breath and the next I saw — clearly, unmistakably, in colors I had no names for — snow. Falling snow, endless and slow, through a sky the color of a bruise, and beneath it a country of white hills going on forever, and a figure standing in it, far away, facing me.

Waiting.

Every rational bone in my body said *no*. But I have spent my whole life drawing maps of places that exist only in rumor, and there is a particular sickness in that work, a hunger, and I will tell you honestly: when the rumor stands up and looks back at you, the hunger wins. It won. I felt it win. I watched myself take one step forward, then another, my boots crunching on the salt-crusted ground, my hand out like a fool greeting a stranger.

The Veil took me mid-step.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I had left. Not at the border. Not when my foot crossed the salt line. Mid-step — between one stride and the next, in the moment when neither foot is on the ground, in the little interval of flight that every walker knows and no walker thinks about. The world closed around me like a door in a house I hadn't known I was standing in.

And I fell.

* * *

Falling is the wrong word, maybe, but it's the only one my body agreed to. There was a sensation of enormous distance compressed into no time at all — of passing through something vast without touching it — of being, for one endless instant, *annotated*, which is a feeling I cannot describe and will spend the rest of my life failing to describe. Then cold hit my face like a slap, and my boots hit snow, and I went down onto my knees in it with the breath knocked out of me.

Snow. Real snow, falling in fat unhurried flakes, catching in my hair and melting down my collar. I looked up.

The sky was the color of a bruise. Deep violet shading to black at the zenith, though the light said afternoon — a low amber glow along the horizon that never strengthened and never faded, the light of a sun that had given up halfway through rising. Snow-covered hills rolled away in every direction, white and white and white, broken only by dark stands of trees that wore their frost like armor. The air tasted clean and metallic, like water from a deep well.

It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I say that as a man who once mapped the Mirrorfen at dawn.

"Oh," I said, to nobody. "Oh, it's *real*."

I stayed on my knees longer than dignity allows, because my legs had opinions and none of them involved standing. When I finally got up, I did it slowly, cataloguing as I went — the snow depth, perhaps eight inches, unbroken by tracks except mine; the wind direction, from the north, carrying a smell of ice and something faintly sweet, like flowers frozen mid-bloom; the visibility, excellent, which meant the absence of any human structure within miles. No smoke. No roads. No sound at all except the whisper of snowfall, which was not silence exactly but a kind of hush, as if the whole country were holding its breath.

I dug out the prophecy book. My hands were steady enough, which surprised me. Unwrapped the oilcloth. Found the passage — I had marked it with a ribbon, because even in extremis I am a professional — and read it standing there in the falling snow.

*When the date that should not be arrives, the Veil will open for the one who draws its lines. He will come mid-stride, between one world and the next, and fall upon the snows of Odile in the season of the dying light.*

I looked at my boots, planted in the snow. I looked at the bruise-colored sky. Dying light, check. Mid-stride — well, I'd been taken mid-step, close enough that whoever wrote this had either remarkable sources or a direct line to my afternoon.

*He will carry the book that carries him,* the next line read, *and the frost will know his name before he speaks it.*

"That last bit seems unlikely," I said, because talking to yourself at a haunted border is a habit and apparently it travels.

The book's pages stirred, though there was no wind strong enough to move them. I closed it quickly and held it against my chest like a shield, which, given what happened next, is roughly what it was.

They came out of the north within the hour.

I heard them before I saw them — a creak of harness, a rhythm of boots on snow, disciplined, many. I had time to consider hiding and reject it, partly because there was nowhere to hide in eight inches of fresh powder, and partly because a small, doomed scholarly part of me wanted to see them properly. So I stood in the open, in the falling snow, holding a centuries-old book, and watched twelve soldiers crest the hill.

They were armored in frost. I mean that literally. Their plate was rimed white, ice grown along every edge and joint in patterns too regular to be weather — deliberate, grown, *worn*, the way another army might wear plumes. Their faces were hidden behind visors glazed with clear ice, and through the glaze I could see eyes, pale as winter water. They carried spears tipped with blades of black glass, and they moved with the terrible economy of people who have done this march ten thousand times and expect to do it ten thousand more.

A patrol. An actual patrol, in an actual other world, wearing actual impossible armor. Some distant, giddy corner of my brain started composing the map legend already — *frost-armored infantry, standard issue, note the glacis pattern* — before the rest of me remembered to be afraid.

The patrol halted thirty feet away. Twelve spears angled toward me in perfect unison. The lead soldier — taller than the rest, with a rank-mark of blue ice worked into the shoulder — stepped forward and looked at me through the icy visor, and I had the distinct impression of being weighed, measured, and entered into a ledger.

"Name yourself," the soldier said. Her voice was a woman's, flat with command, fogging the inside of her visor.

"Sebastian," I said. My voice cracked on the second syllable, which was mortifying. "Sebastian Brightwater. Cartographer. Of — elsewhere." I gestured vaguely at the entire sky.

Silence. The snow fell. Somewhere behind the hills, something made a low groaning sound, like a glacier shifting in its sleep, and every soldier's head turned toward it and turned back, practiced, unbothered, which frightened me more than the spears did.

Then the lead soldier's gaze dropped to the book in my arms.

I watched recognition happen. It moved through the patrol like wind through wheat — a shifting, a stillness, twelve frost-glazed visors tilting down toward the oilcloth-wrapped shape against my chest. The lead soldier went rigid. Then she did something I will never forget as long as I live, which may not be as long as I'd like, given current circumstances.

She knelt.

One knee in the snow, spear grounded, head bowed. And behind her, in a ripple like the crack of ice on a spring lake, eleven more soldiers knelt, until I stood alone and upright in a circle of kneeling frost-armored strangers, snow gathering on their shoulders, the bruise-dark sky pressing down, and the prophecy book warm — actually warm, I could feel it through the oilcloth, warm as a living thing — against my heart.

"Stand up," I said. "Please. You've made a mistake. I'm a mapmaker. I draw coastlines. I once spent a summer charting a river that turned out to be a very large newt."

No one moved. The lead soldier lifted her head, and through the ice-glaze her pale eyes fixed on mine, and there was such certainty in them — such absolute, immovable, terrifying certainty — that my protest died somewhere in my throat and lay there.

"The Cartographer of Ends," she said, and the words went through the kneeling ranks in a murmur, passed mouth to mouth like a flame down a row of candles. "Come through the Veil, as it was written. Come at the dying of the light, as it was written." She rose, snow sliding from her knee, and extended one gauntleted hand — not to shake, I realized, but to escort, palm up, formal as a courtier. "The Queen commands your protection, Cartographer. You will come to Vharen."

"And if I'd rather not?"

The twelve spears did not move, but somehow they all became slightly more present.

"Then the Queen commands your protection *more firmly*," she said.

I looked at the offered hand. I looked at the endless snow, the dying light, the book warm against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Somewhere behind those white hills was a door I had fallen through, and nothing in my instruments, my training, or my considerable experience of being lied to by old books told me how to find it again.

Wonder, I discovered, collapses into terror remarkably easily. All it takes is kneeling.

"Vharen," I repeated, buying time, tasting the word. "That's the citadel. On the old charts. The ones my — " I stopped. Careful, Sebastian. "The ones I've seen."

Something flickered in the soldier's eyes at the catch in my voice. She noticed. Of course she noticed; noticing things is her profession, same as mine.

"Yes," she said mildly. "The ones you've *seen*. Come, Cartographer. The winter is worsening, and it has been waiting for you."

She turned and led off north, and the patrol closed around me — escort or cage, the architecture was identical — and I walked into Odile carrying a book that was warmer than my own blood, while behind us, soft and patient, the snow began to fill in our footprints as though the country wanted no record kept.

As a cartographer, I took that personally.
