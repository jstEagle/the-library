## 4. The Cartographer of Ends

They did not drag me, exactly. That was the strange courtesy of it. Two frost-armored soldiers walked on either side of me with the patient grip of men herding livestock they'd been told was valuable, and the third carried the prophecy book in a canvas sling like an infant he'd been ordered not to drop. My ankles were bound with cord that bit through my trousers every time the snow deepened, but nobody shoved me, nobody spat, and when I stumbled on a buried fence-post one of them actually slowed.

I decided this meant I was either very important or about to be very dead, and that in Vharen these were possibly the same thing.

The citadel rose out of the white like something the mountain had grown rather than built — black basalt terraces stacked in tiers, each rimmed with ice that caught the grey light and threw it back in colors that had no business existing in a sky this colorless. Banners hung from its walls, stiff with frost, snapping only at their frayed edges. They bore a device I didn't recognize: a silver crown above a closed eye.

*Closed,* I noted. Not blindfolded. Closed. As if it might open later, and everyone preferred to know when.

"You're limping," said the soldier on my left. His voice was muffled by his helm but I could hear the frown in it.

"I'm walking on frozen ground tied at the ankles," I said. "Limping is ambition."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. The one on my right elbowed him. Discipline, apparently, was also cold in Odile.

Inside, the citadel swallowed sound. Corridors of dark stone lit by lanterns burning a blue-white flame that gave off no smoke and, I noticed with a cartographer's unease, no heat. My breath fogged anyway. Everything here was warm *about* being warm without ever committing to it.

They took my pack first. Then my instruments — the brass calipers, the folding rule, the ink case my mother gave me. Each item was catalogued by a clerk with a ledger and a face like a locked drawer.

"Name?" the clerk asked.

"Sebastian Brightwater."

His pen stopped. It was a small thing, that stop, but I've spent my life reading maps and maps are nothing but small things arranged meaningfully, so I saw it the way you see a fault line in otherwise solid rock.

"And your house?"

"It's a cottage," I said. "On Lantern Row. In the Veiled Valley."

"That is not what I asked."

"Then we're going to be stuck here a while," I said, "because I don't understand the question."

The clerk looked up at me then, properly, for the first time. Whatever he saw made him close the ledger with unusual gentleness, as though the book itself might bruise.

"The Queen will want him clean," he said to the soldiers. "And fed. And afraid."

"He already looks afraid," said the left-hand soldier.

"I'm not afraid," I said. "I'm cold, confused, and deeply underqualified. Fear would require me to believe any of this is real."

Nobody laughed at that one. I was beginning to suspect humor traveled poorly across the Veil.

* * *

They cleaned me in a room where water ran hot from pipes in the walls — actual heated pipes, which told me Vharen's wealth went somewhere interesting before it went to the poor. They dressed me in wool and fur that fit suspiciously well, as if someone had guessed my measurements before I arrived, and that frightened me more than the arrest had. Prophecy, apparently, was thorough.

Then they fed me. Bread, salted fish, a soup of some root vegetable I couldn't name. I ate like a man who has realized that refusing food is a statement, and statements get you killed faster than compliance does.

And then they brought me to the Queen.

I had imagined a throne room. Everyone imagines a throne room. What I got instead was a map room.

Understand what that did to me. After days of vertigo and snow and kneeling strangers, I walked into a circular chamber walled floor-to-ceiling with charts — hand-drawn, inked, annotated, corrected — covering every surface like ivy. Shelves of rolled vellum. A great round table in the center bearing a relief map of the entire kingdom in painted plaster, mountains no taller than my thumb, rivers of blue glass.

It was the most beautiful room I had ever stood in, and I resented how much it felt like home.

Queen Isolde sat at that table, alone, moving tiny carved figures across the glass rivers with one finger. She did not look up when I entered. She was perhaps fifty, grey-haired, plain-faced in the way powerful people are permitted to be plain — no crown, just a circlet of iron worn like a tool rather than a jewel. Her hands were a working woman's hands. Ink-stained at the edges.

I liked her immediately, which is exactly why I distrusted myself.

"Do you know what this is?" she asked, still not looking up.

"A map room."

"This," she said, tapping the plaster kingdom, "is the only honest thing in Vharen. Men lie to me daily. Documents lie. Priests lie, and they do it beautifully. But the map—" she moved a figure along a river, "—the map only shows what is true, or it shows nothing at all." Now she looked up. Her eyes were pale grey, almost colorless, and they went through me like a surveyor's line. "So I am going to ask you a question, cartographer, and I want the kind of answer a map would give. No ornament. No flattery. Do you know why you are here?"

"No," I said.

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Approval, maybe. Or disappointment. With queens the two probably share plumbing.

"Good. A liar would have said yes." She set down the figure — a little wooden soldier — and folded her hands. "Three hundred years ago, a book was written. We keep it in the Vault beneath this room. Most of it is the usual prophetic sludge — births, deaths, floods, a comet, the standard inventory of dread. But one passage concerns a figure called the Cartographer of Ends. It says he will come from beyond the Veil, carrying the Book itself. It says he will *map the ending of all things.*"

"That's a terrible job title," I said.

"It is a terrible job," she agreed. "Last night, my watch-captains reported a tear in the Veil over the southern wastes. This morning, my soldiers found a man lying in the snow holding that exact book." She rose. Circled the table toward me. "You will forgive me if I decline to believe in coincidence."

"I don't believe in it either," I said. "But belief isn't the problem. The problem is that I'm not him. Your Cartographer. I'm a mapmaker of places that don't exist — rumors, legends, valley-folk tales. I draw borders for a living, madam, and the border I lived behind for thirty years had a rule: *magic cannot cross.* I have never cast a spell. I can't light a candle with my mind. If your prophecy wants someone who'll 'map the ending of all things,' it grabbed the wrong man off the wrong side of the wrong wall."

She listened to all of it. That unnerved me most — she let me finish, watched my hands while I talked the way my old tutors used to, checking for tremors.

"Show me your palms," she said.

"What?"

"Your palms."

I held them out. She took my wrists — her grip stronger than her frame suggested — turned my hands over, and studied the lines like a fortune-teller, except I knew she wasn't reading fate. She was looking at calluses. At ink. At the specific wear-patterns of a life spent holding pens instead of swords.

"Hm," she said. "You are what you claim. A draftsman." She released me. "That changes nothing."

"It changes everything! It means your prophecy is wrong, or your soldiers found the wrong man, or—"

"Or," she said, "the prophecy is right, and the universe chose a draftsman deliberately, because it wanted someone who understood that a map is not a territory." She returned to her chair and sat, arranging herself with the economy of a woman who wasted no motion she could spend elsewhere. "Sit down, Master Brightwater. You look like a heron standing there."

I sat.

"Here is what happens now," she said. "You will be given quarters. Comfortable ones. You will be given access to my archives, my scholars, and every chart my family has collected in three hundred years — including charts of the Veil that no outsider has ever seen. You will study the prophecy. You will study the winter, which is worsening, in case you hadn't noticed, and has been worsening for eleven years in a pattern my astronomers cannot explain."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, you will be *protected.*" She said the word the way other monarchs say *imprisoned*, with the same flat administrative care. "There are people in this city who would kill you tonight if I allowed it. There are people in the north who would kill you slowly. And there is a faction at court — I will not name them yet — who believe that the arrival of the Cartographer of Ends is the best news Odile has had in a century, because it means the ending of all things may finally be scheduled." She smiled, thinly. "Queens learn to hear enthusiasm like that and reach for their guards."

"So I'm a guest."

"You are a *resource*," she said. "Guests may leave. Resources stay until depleted." She must have seen my face, because she added, without softening: "I promised you honesty. Here is the rest: if you fail — if midwinter comes and the winter does not break — then I will have no further use for protecting you, and the factions will have every use for killing you, and I will permit it. That is the shape of the bargain. Take it or refuse it; refusal merely moves the date of your death earlier on the calendar."

I thought about the valley. About my cottage on Lantern Row with its crooked shelves and my kettle with the chipped spout. About the archive where a date that shouldn't exist had been waiting a century for me to read it. About the border of salt, flickering at dusk, something on the other side flickering *back.*

Every road out of this conversation led through the same country: forward.

"And if I succeed?" I asked. "If I 'map the ending of all things' and the winter breaks? What then?"

"Then you will be the most celebrated man in Odile's history," she said. "For approximately one week. After which you will be the most dangerous man in Odile's history, because you will know things about the Veil that kingdoms have gone to war over, and everyone in this room—" she gestured at the beautiful, honest maps, "—will want to own that knowledge. Including me. I am not exempt from my own warning, Master Brightwater. Remember that I told you."

"I will," I said. "You say that like it's a kindness."

"It is the only kindness I have left that costs me nothing."

She rang a small bell. The doors opened. A guard entered — and beside him, a woman in armor that had clearly seen better decades, sword at her hip with the easy familiarity of an old scar, her hair cropped short and going grey at the temples, her posture suggesting a person perpetually braced for bad news and rarely disappointed.

"Captain Hale," the Queen said. "This is Sebastian Brightwater. He is to be escorted, accommodated, and kept alive. In that order. He answers to me, and until further notice, he answers to you."

The woman looked at me. I looked at her. Something passed between us — I couldn't have named it then, but I recognized it later: two people taking each other's measure and finding the same crack running through the same foundation.

"Does he answer to me," Captain Hale asked, "if answering to me requires disobeying you?"

Isolde smiled again, and this one reached her eyes, and somehow that was worse.

"Yes," she said. "That is precisely why I chose you. Dismissed."

* * *

My quarters were three rooms in the east tower: a bedchamber, a washroom, and a study with a desk facing a window overlooking the city. On the desk, waiting for me, were paper, ink, calipers — my calipers, returned from the clerk's catalogue — and a single sheet bearing the seal of the royal archive, granting me access.

Someone had also left a plate of candied ginger. I have no idea why that detail undid me more than everything else, but I sat on the edge of the unfamiliar bed and ate all of it, and halfway through the second piece I realized my hands were shaking.

Not from cold. From the specific vertigo of a man who has just been handed a role too large for him by people who can kill him for declining it.

Because here's what I hadn't said to the Queen, couldn't say, wouldn't say: part of me *wanted* it. Some small, shameful, starving corner of me had spent thirty years drawing maps of places that don't exist and dreaming that one day I'd find a place that did — and now the universe had answered, and the answer came with a body count and a deadline and a queen who measured kindness in what it cost her nothing.

The prophecy called me the Cartographer of Ends. I kept turning the phrase over like a coin I suspected was counterfeit. *Map the ending of all things.* I was a man who labeled coastlines. Who argued with himself about whether a mountain range deserved two hachures or three. The largest decision in my professional life had been whether to shade a disputed border in red or leave it dashed and let the reader decide.

And now entire nations would live or die by whatever I drew next.

A knock at the door. Captain Hale entered without waiting — a habit, I'd later learn, of women who'd once commanded fortresses and had been reduced to escorting civilians.

"Tour of your accommodations," she said. "Window there. Door there. Both guarded. Don't bother with the window; it's four hundred feet to the rocks and the rocks are having a worse winter than you are."

"Noted."

"There's a bell-pull. Kitchen sends food. If anyone knocks claiming to be from the kitchen when you haven't rung, don't answer."

"Why?"

"Because kitchen staff knock *after* you ring," she said, "and assassins always introduce themselves as kitchen staff. Every assassin I've ever hanged introduced himself as kitchen staff, a priest, or a lost relative. Be suspicious of all three."

"Are you the fourth option?"

"I'm your jailer," she said, without inflection. "Get some sleep, Master Brightwater. Tomorrow you start work."

She turned to go. I should have let her. Instead I heard myself ask:

"Captain. Do you believe I'm him? The Cartographer of Ends?"

She paused at the door. For a moment her shoulders did something complicated — a whole speech delivered entirely in trapezius muscles.

"I believe," she said finally, "that the last time this kingdom put its faith in a prophecy, forty thousand people died in the Burnt Reach. So no, Master Brightwater. I don't believe you're anything." She looked back at me, and her eyes were tired in the ancient way of eyes that have seen the thing they were trained for. "Which is why I'm the right person to guard you. Believers protect what they worship. I protect what I'm paid to, until it earns better."

She left. The door shut. The wind found the tower windows and worried at them like a dog with a bone.

I lay down on the bed without undressing and stared at the ceiling, and did what I always do when the world stops making sense: I started mapping. Mentally. The citadel — seven tiers, I'd climbed four. The city below — harbor to the south, so trade comes by sea until the strait freezes. The Queen's map room — directly above us, which meant the Vault beneath the map room was directly beneath my feet, and somewhere down there sat a book with my arrival written in it before I was born.

A resource, she'd called me. Stay until depleted.

Sleep came eventually, the thin, armed kind. And it brought a dream — no, not a dream. A memory that wasn't mine. Snow, and a woman's voice saying *draw it shut, draw it shut, whatever it costs* — and a hand holding a pen, and the pen bleeding darkness onto vellum, and—

I woke before dawn with ink on my fingers.

Actual ink. Fresh. Black. My hands had been clean when I fell asleep; I'd checked, the way you check such things, twice.

On the desk, the blank sheet the archive had provided — the one I hadn't touched — now bore a single line of writing in a hand that was not mine, and yet was, in the way a father's face is echoed in his son's:

*The map remembers what the maker forgets. Come to the north shelf. Bring your blood, Brightwater. It opens faster than keys.*

Below it, unsigned, in the margin, drawn small and precise as a fingerprint: a compass rose.

With nine points.

I sat there until the sun came up over Vharen, staring at it, and then I did the only sensible thing available to a man in my position.

I knocked on my jailer's door and said, "Captain Hale. I think I've just received mail from a dead man."
