## 5. A Knight With No Order

They gave me the foreigner because nobody else wanted him.

I want to be clear about that. It wasn't an honor. It wasn't a test of my loyalty, whatever the captain said when he pressed the assignment into my hands like a coin he was embarrassed to be seen spending. It was a job nobody in the citadel of Vharen would take, and I had spent three years learning to be the person who took those jobs, because the alternative was being the person who stood around waiting for one worth taking, and that person starved.

"Jailer-escort," the captain said, as if the words belonged together naturally, like *bread-and-water* or *gallows-and-grin*. "You keep him alive, you keep him contained, and you keep your mouth shut about all three."

"And if he runs?"

"He won't." The captain almost smiled. "Where would he go? He's a mapmaker from beyond the Veil. The man's never seen a winter like ours. He'd be an icicle by the second mile."

I took the assignment because it came with a room that had a door, and doors are a kind of wealth most people never think about until they've slept in barracks with forty other disgraced somebodies.

The prisoner — though *prisoner* was doing a lot of work, since technically he was also the prophesied savior of the realm, and Vharen had never quite decided which chain went on which wrist — was in the east tower, third floor, under guard of two soldiers who looked relieved to see me the way sailors look relieved to see a pilot boat.

He was sitting on the edge of the bed when I came in, holding the prophecy book against his chest like a shield, and the first thing I noticed about Sebastian Brightwater was not his strange valley accent or his ink-stained fingers or the way the frost-armored patrol had described him, half-awed and half-afraid.

It was his eyes. They were doing a thing I recognized.

They were moving fast, cataloguing exits. Window: too narrow, four stories down, snowbank below — survivable, actually, I noted, before catching myself. Door: me. Corridor: two guards, both bored, both armed. And underneath all that rapid arithmetic, behind the calculation, the thing the calculation was there to hide:

Terror. Clean, animal, well-managed terror.

I knew that look. I'd worn it for six years, from the day my order stripped my spurs until the day I stopped expecting anyone to give them back. It's a specific fear, the fear of being found out — not of dying, exactly, but of being *seen*, of having the gap between what people believe you are and what you know yourself to be suddenly illuminated for everyone at once.

Most people can't spot it in others. They're too busy managing their own.

But I'd had practice.

"Don't get up," I said, which was unnecessary, because he clearly had no intention of getting up anywhere near me.

"I wasn't going to," he said. His voice cracked on the second word and he covered it with a cough. Badly.

"Maren Hale." I didn't offer a hand. Offering a hand to a frightened man is a kindness; offering one to a frightened man who thinks you're his executioner is just a way to watch him flinch. "I'm to keep you alive and contained. In that order. Nobody's told me what happens if the two come into conflict, so let's hope they don't."

"They sent a knight?" He said it carefully, the way you handle something you suspect might be insulting without knowing why.

"They sent me." I leaned against the wall by the door, arms crossed, where he could see all of me and none of me could loom. "The knight part is negotiable. There's a dispute among certain authorities about whether I still qualify."

"What did you do?"

I blinked. Most people don't ask that. Most people hear *disgraced* and start inventing their own story, usually something suitably dramatic involving wine or another man's wife, because it's easier than imagining that institutions fall apart over things like truth.

"You ask direct questions for a man whose head depends on the queen's mood."

"You're not one of the authorities." He shifted the book on his lap. "You're standing guard over someone the whole citadel is afraid of, and you look more tired than scared. That means you've been afraid already and it didn't kill you. People like that tend to answer honestly if you ask them something real."

I should have been annoyed. Instead I felt the strange, unwelcome sensation of being *read* — accurately, by a stranger, in under a minute — and I remembered that this was apparently a man who read maps for a living. Of course he could find the shape of a thing quickly. It was probably the only skill he had that worked here.

"I told the truth," I said, "at a time when everyone important had agreed to lie. That's the whole story. The long version has more snow in it."

Something happened to his face then. Not a smile. More like the muscles around his mouth forgot, briefly, that they were supposed to be performing calm, and for half a second I saw the actual man underneath: young, exhausted, so far from home that home might as well be a story he'd invented to feel better about the cold.

"That's how I got here too," he said quietly. "More or less. I read a date out loud that I shouldn't have known existed."

"The date in the book."

"You've heard."

"The whole citadel's heard. The whole citadel's heard everything. That's the problem with citadels — the walls are thick but the tongues aren't." I pushed off the wall. "Dinner's coming. Eat it. The stew here is a war crime, but it's a hot war crime, and in Odile we've learned not to be picky about heat."

* * *

The stew arrived, and it was worse than I'd promised, which I took a small professional pride in.

We ate sitting on opposite ends of the bed, because the room contained a bed, a stool, a chamber pot, and a window, and only one of those was suitable for sitting on with any dignity. Brightwater — Sebastian, he'd corrected me once and I'd ignored it, mostly to see what he'd do, which was nothing, which told me he was used to being managed by people who declined to use his name — ate the way frightened men eat: fast, mechanically, watching me between spoonfuls.

I ate slow. Partly strategy. Mostly because the stew deserved to be approached with caution.

"So," I said, on the fourth or fifth spoonful. "The Cartographer of Ends."

His spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. "I'm not—"

"I know what you're going to say. You're going to say you're not him, it's a mistake, the book must be wrong, you're just a mapmaker, you draw coastlines, you've never ended anything bigger than a pot of tea." I shrugged. "Am I close?"

He set the bowl down. His hands were shaking again — fine tremors, the kind you learn to hide by gripping things. "How did you know all of that?"

"Because I've given that exact speech. Word for word, nearly. Different subject matter." I looked into the stew, which looked back, grayly. "Mine was: I'm not a traitor, the evidence must be wrong, I'm just a soldier, I follow orders, I've never betrayed anything bigger than a card game. I said it to a tribunal. They didn't believe me either."

"You were tried?"

"Convicted. Stripped. Reassigned to the sort of duties that involve guarding frightened mapmakers in towers." I gestured at the room with my spoon. "Welcome to my career."

He was quiet for a while. Outside, the wind was doing its evening work on the tower stones, that low grinding moan that visitors always think is the building settling and locals know is the building *complaining*. Snow ticked against the window glass like something asking politely to be let in.

"Why do you tell me this?" he asked finally. "If I repeat it, you lose—"

"Lose what?" I laughed, and it came out harsher than I intended. "Look around, Brightwater. What exactly do you imagine I have left to lose? My honor's already been auctioned off. My order doesn't write to me. My family—" I stopped, because that was further than I meant to go, and I watched him notice that I'd stopped, and I hated how good he was at noticing.

"Your family," he repeated softly. Not a question. A door held open, which I could walk through or not.

"Not tonight," I said.

He nodded, and — this is the part I keep returning to, the part that matters — he *let it go*. No probing, no wounded look designed to extract more, no clever follow-up. He just nodded and picked up his spoon again, and I understood that this, too, was a tell. That was how liars survived among liars: you learned which silences to respect, because you needed yours respected too.

Two liars, recognizing each other's tells. That was us, from the first night. Neither of us said it out loud. Saying it would have ruined it.

"Can I ask you something real?" he said instead. "Since we're trading."

"Trade away."

"The prophecy says I'll 'map the ending of all things.' Everyone here treats that like I'm going to *cause* it. Isolde looks at me like I'm a weapon she hasn't figured out how to aim. The soldiers kneel and then they won't meet my eyes. And I keep thinking—" He turned the bowl in his hands. "I keep thinking maybe they're right. Maybe the book knows something I don't. Maybe I walked through that Veil because I'm supposed to end everything, and all this"—he gestured at the tower, the citadel, the endless white pressing at the windows—"is just the world politely clearing the table."

I put my spoon down.

"There it is," I said.

"There what is?"

"The speech. The second speech. The one after the first one fails." I'd given that one too, in the weeks after the tribunal, alone, to ceilings. *Maybe they were right about me. Maybe the evidence saw something I couldn't.* It's the most dangerous lie a condemned person tells, because it feels like humility. It tastes like honesty. And it does the prosecution's work for free.

"Let me tell you what I know about books and tribunals and powerful people deciding what you are," I said. "They are never reading you. They're reading what they need. The queen needs a Cartographer of Ends, so the book says you're him. If the queen needed a scapegoat, the book would say that instead, and it would be equally convincing, because conviction isn't the book's job. Convincing is."

"But the date. The date was real. It matched today exactly, a hundred years before today."

"So did every horoscope ever cast, if you squint hard enough." I held up a hand before he could argue. "I'm not saying it's false. I'm saying I've stood exactly where you're standing, holding exactly that kind of proof, and I can tell you from experience: the proof is real and the conclusion is still wrong. Both things. At once. That's how they get you."

He stared at me. And then — the first honest thing I'd seen him do — he laughed. Short, startled, half-hysterical, the laugh of a man who has been holding his breath for days and just got permission to exhale.

"They warned me about you," he said. "'The Hale woman. Disgraced. Bitter. Do not engage her in conversation.'"

"Good advice."

"It's terrible advice. This is the first conversation I've had in this entire kingdom where I understood what was happening."

We sat with that. The wind ground its teeth outside. Somewhere below, the changing of the watch clanked and muttered, boots on stone, the citadel breathing its slow nightly breath.

"Here's the truce," I said, because I'd decided somewhere in the last hour, without announcing it to myself, that I was going to make one. "I don't report our conversations. The captain asks, I tell him you eat, you sleep, you stare out the window like a man memorizing escape routes — which, by the way, the window's narrower than your shoulders, I checked, don't waste the hope. In exchange, you stop performing brave for me. It's exhausting to watch and you're bad at it."

"I'm not—"

"You gripped that book like a breastplate for the first hour I was here. Your hands shake when you think I'm not looking. You laugh like it's contraband." I stood, collected my bowl. "I wore that armor for six years, Brightwater. I know every buckle."

He looked up at me then, really looked, and I watched him decide something — whether to trust me, or whether trusting me was simply less exhausting than not, which in my experience is how most trust actually gets built.

"Sebastian," he said. "My name. You keep calling me Brightwater."

"I know what I'm calling you."

A pause. Then, quieter: "Why?"

Because the name was a death sentence in this kingdom — Pellam Oxley hadn't told him yet, but I'd heard the archivists whispering, and I knew what the surname meant here, what war it was stitched into. Because the moment he learned it, he'd flinch every time someone said it, and I wasn't ready to be the one who taught him that.

"Because names are for friends," I said, "and we're not there yet."

Which was, of course, a lie. We were closer than I'd let myself admit. But I was out of practice telling the truth to anyone who mattered, and he was out of practice hearing it, and between the two of us we had enough dishonesty to build a bridge — which, looking back, is exactly what we did.

At the door I paused. Habit. Also, honesty, in the small doses I could still afford.

"Eat the rest of the stew," I said. "Tomorrow it'll be worse. Everything here gets worse before midwinter. That's not prophecy. That's just Odile."

And that was the night the truce was struck — unspoken, unsigned, sealed in bad stew and recognized fear. I walked back down the tower stairs with the captain's questions queuing up in my throat and answers ready that were true in shape and hollow in center, the specialty of my house.

What I didn't know — what nobody in that citadel knew yet — was that the book on the prisoner's knees had already started writing again. New lines, fresh ink, lines that named a traitor in the queen's own circle.

And lines, further down, in smaller letters, describing in careful detail how the Cartographer of Ends would die at midwinter.
