## 7. What the Winter Remembers

I have been cold in a great many ways in my life. I have been cold the way soldiers are cold, standing watch at some godforsaken pass while the wind files its teeth on the rocks. I have been cold the way disgraced people are cold, which is to say indoors, among warm fires and warm company, feeling it anyway. But I had never been cold the way Odile was cold that week — cold with *intent*, cold like something leaning closer to look at you.

The winter was supposed to be deepening. That's what everyone kept saying, in the markets, in the barracks, in the queen's corridors where they said things more quietly. Deepening. Natural. The long winter had lasted three hundred years; another hard month meant nothing.

Except I'd stood enough watches in enough passes to know what weather looks like when it's just weather, and what it looks like when it's *going somewhere*.

This was going somewhere.

* * *

We left Vharen before dawn, four days after Pellam put my name in Sebastian Brightwater's mouth like a coin he expected me to spend carefully.

"Supply run," I'd told the gate sergeant, and he'd waved us through without looking up from his tea, because supply runs north were beneath notice. Nobody watched the northern road. Nobody watched anything north of the last villages anymore, because there was less and less to see.

Sebastian rode behind me on a borrowed mule, which felt appropriate. He was not a rider. He was not a soldier. He was, as far as I could tell, a man made entirely of ink-stained fingers and questions, wrapped in a coat that had been expensive in whatever world he came from and now looked like something a beggar would turn down.

"You're staring at me," he said.

"I'm checking the road."

"The road is behind you."

"I'm checking it thoroughly."

He was quiet for a while. Then: "You don't trust me."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because lying to him seemed pointless and because — though I'd never have admitted this — I was tired of lying by default. "I don't know you. Trust is a thing you build. Like a wall. You don't trust a wall until you've leaned on it."

"And you've leaned on a lot of walls that fell down."

I didn't answer that one. He noticed me not answering it. He was observant, this cartographer. Annoyingly so. It occurred to me that a man who spent his life reading terrain probably read people the same way — noting the slopes, the places where the ground gave way.

The first silent village was Hallowmere, or what the maps called Hallowmere. We found the well, the shrine, the longhouse with its door swinging. No bodies. No blood. Just absence, arranged neatly, like a room after the furniture's been sold.

"Maybe they went south," Sebastian said, in the voice of a man trying very hard to believe his own map.

"In midwinter? With no tracks?" I dismounted and pressed my palm flat against the longhouse doorframe. The wood was cold, but not dead-cold. Not frozen-through. "They left recently. They left *quickly*. And they left—" I stopped.

"Left what?"

"Bowls." There were bowls on the table inside, still crusted with food gone grey. "You don't leave your supper, Cartographer. Not unless something told you supper doesn't matter anymore."

He came to the doorway and looked in, and I watched his face do the thing faces did around him lately — the thing where wonder and horror fought for the same real estate and both lost. "Something called them out," he said. "North."

"Or something called them *in*." I nodded toward the hills beyond the village, where the snow lay strange — too smooth, too even, like a sheet pulled tight over something underneath. "Either way, we're riding into its mouth. So keep your book dry and your mouth shut if anyone asks who you are."

"Who am I, if anyone asks?"

"A clerk. My clerk. Clerks are boring. Boring men die of old age."

"I've been in this kingdom nine days," he said, "and I've already been arrested, worshipped, threatened by a queen, and declared an enemy of someone's family line. I'd like to be boring. Boring sounds like paradise."

For the first time since the Veil spat him into our world, I laughed. It surprised us both. He looked at me like I'd done a trick.

"Don't get used to it," I said, and swung back into the saddle.

* * *

The blizzard caught us two days out of Hallowmere, in the teeth of the old pass the maps named Coldharrows and the locals, when there had still been locals, had named nothing at all, because naming a place like that felt like inviting it to remember you.

We holed up in a waystation — a stone hut built by soldiers three hundred years ago, back when my order still had honor and roads worth guarding. I got a fire going while Sebastian saw to the animals, and then we sat in the orange light and listened to the wind try to take the roof apart, and I thought about how many times I'd sat exactly like this, in exactly these huts, with exactly this silence between me and another person.

Silence was safe. Silence was professional. Silence was what you gave people when you couldn't give them the truth.

"So," Sebastian said, into the silence, because of course he did. "Maren Hale. Knight of—"

"Don't."

"—of no order," he finished, gently, the way you finish a sentence when you already know it'll cost you. "Pellam said 'disgraced.' Wren Ashvale will say worse, apparently. Everyone in this kingdom has an opinion about me, and somehow I've ended up traveling with the only person nobody tells me anything about."

"That's not true. They tell you I'm a jailer. A drunk. A woman who lost her sword and her name in the same afternoon."

"They didn't tell me any of those things, actually. They just let me assume them." He fed a stick to the fire. "Assumptions are lazy cartography. I prefer surveying."

I looked at him across the fire. Snow-light came through the arrow slit behind him and turned his breath to ghosts. He was afraid — he'd been afraid since the day he arrived, and he carried it honestly, the way some men carry wounds they can't hide — but under the fear was something stubborn. Something that wanted to *know*, even knowing hurt.

I knew that thing. I used to be that thing, before I learned better.

"You want the story," I said.

"I want whatever you're willing to give. If it's nothing, it's nothing. But I'd rather hear it from you than find it in one of Pellam's banned books. His version will have footnotes."

And that — the sheer absurdity of him, this terrified foreigner offering me the courtesy of letting me keep my own secrets — that's what broke it open. Not the storm. Not the wine, because we had none. Him.

"All right," I said. "Survey away."

* * *

"My father was a farrier," I began, because every story of mine started there whether it needed to or not. "Horses. Shoes. Honest work. When I was twelve, the Order of the Pale Standard came through our town escorting a tax convoy, and their captain let me hold his sword. Just hold it. Ten seconds. It weighed nothing and everything, and I decided in those ten seconds that I would be one of them or die trying, and for eleven years those were genuinely the only two options I considered."

Sebastian said nothing. Good. Most people interrupted here, with something kind, and kindness at the wrong moment makes people stop talking.

"I was good. That's not vanity, it's just fact — I tested high, I served clean, I made corporal at nineteen, knight-banneret at twenty-six. The Pale Standard doesn't knight many women. I was going to be the argument that settled the question forever." I turned my hands toward the fire. Old burn scars from forge-work, newer ones from other work. "And then came the garrison at Vael's Crossing."

"What happened at Vael's Crossing?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's the whole story. Nothing happened, and I let it."

The wind screamed against the stones. I let it scream a while.

"There was a village on the border of the Burnt Reach. Ashfall, it was called. And in the third year of my posting, orders came down — sealed orders, from the Lord Chancellor's own office — to evacuate Ashfall and burn it behind us. Plague, the orders said. Quarantine protocol." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it went flat whenever I recited this part, like a report I'd given so many times to imaginary tribunals that it had worn smooth. "Two hundred people. We marched them out in the snow, and we burned their homes, and I stood at the head of my troop and I did not ask why."

"Why not?"

"Because asking why isn't the job. Because the man who gave the orders wore the same white sash I did, and the whole architecture of my life was built on the idea that the sash meant something. Because I was twenty-eight years old and I had spent half my life becoming a tool, and tools don't ask why." I looked at him. "You want to know the disgrace? It isn't that I burned the village. It's that I burned it *well*. Efficiently. Commendably. They put a commendation in my file."

Sebastian's face had gone very still. Not horrified — or not only. There was something else in it, something recognizing.

"What changed?" he asked.

"A girl." The word came out rougher than I intended. "One of the evacuated. Nine years old. She came back to the burning village three days later, alone, on foot, twelve miles through the snow, because her cat was still in there. And I found her standing in the ashes of her own house, holding this scorched, furious, half-dead animal, looking at me like—" 

I stopped. Some images don't survive being described. They lose weight in the telling.

"She looked at me like I was the plague. And she was right. And I went back to the garrison and I opened the sealed orders again and I read them properly this time, all the fine print, and there was no plague. There was no quarantine authority. There was a signature — Dreville's office — and a sum of money paid to a shipping concern in Vharen that didn't deal in grain or timber or anything a shipping concern should deal in." I shrugged, because the shrug was easier than the rest. "So I reported it. Up the chain. Properly, in writing, with seals. And the chain ate it. Every link. And then the chain turned around and ate me instead — falsified logs, missing funds, a tribunal that took one afternoon. They struck my name from the rolls. They let me keep the scars and the horse and nothing else."

"Dreville," Sebastian said slowly. "Lord Chancellor Dreville."

"You see why I flinched when Pellam said your prophecy book mentioned traitors."

"He burned a village to hide a shipment. What shipment?"

"If I knew, I'd be dangerous instead of merely disgraced." I poked the fire harder than it deserved. "But I've had eight years to think about it, and here's what I think: whatever came out of Ashfall, it wasn't goods. Goods get sold. Whatever it was, Dreville needed it *gone* and needed the ground salted after, and he used my order to do it because my order obeys without asking. We were the perfect tool. Clean, loyal, blind." I met his eyes across the fire. "So when I say I don't trust easily, Cartographer, understand it's not cynicism. It's scar tissue. I trusted once, wholesale, and the interest came due on two hundred lives."

The wind dropped for a moment — one of those sudden, unnatural calms that storms sometimes grant, like a held breath — and in the quiet, Sebastian spoke.

"At Vharen," he said, "when Isolde offered me protection with one hand and a cell with the other, everyone in that hall knelt to me. Strangers. Soldiers. They knelt, and I wanted to run, because I knew — I *knew* — I wasn't what they thought I was. I'm a mapmaker. I draw coastlines for people who will never sail them. I'm nobody's prophesied anything." He turned the prophecy book over in his hands, not opening it. "But you want to know the worst part? The worst part is that some small voice in me keeps whispering: *what if you could be?* What if you just performed it well enough, believed it hard enough — wouldn't that be easier than being the fraud who lets everyone down?"

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly what it's like."

We looked at each other across the fire, and something settled between us — not trust, not yet, trust was a wall and walls took time — but the *groundwork* of it. Two liars, sitting in the ruins of a fort my order built back when it deserved the name, each having just handed the other the blueprint of their own disgrace.

"Your order built this hut," he said, nodding at the stones.

"We built most of the passes. Three hundred years ago, when the Order guarded the borders of things." I glanced at the arrow slit, at the dark beyond it. "Before the winter. Before there was nothing left worth guarding except the memory of guarding it."

"Do you miss it? The order?"

"Every day. That's the trap of it. They cut me out and I still wake up reaching for the sash." I lay back against my pack. "Get some sleep, Cartographer. Tomorrow we make the strait, and the woman waiting on the other side of it charges by the mile and the secret both."

* * *

I woke in the black hour before dawn to a silence so complete it rang.

Blizzards don't stop. They falter, they lull, they cheat — but they don't stop dead, all at once, like a door closing. I was on my feet with my hand on my sword before my eyes finished opening, and Sebastian was sitting up too, wide-eyed, listening to the same nothing.

Outside, the snow had stopped falling. It hung in the air instead — suspended, millions of flakes motionless in the windless dark, catching the light of the stars like a held breath. And far off to the north, past the pass, past the silent villages, past everything, the sky above the Burnt Reach glowed faintly green, the way it does over ice that's about to give.

"Is that normal?" Sebastian whispered.

"No," I said. "None of this is normal. It hasn't been normal since Hallowmere." I strapped on my sword and woke the mule with a hand on its trembling neck. "The winter's remembering something, Cartographer. And I think it's remembering *you*."

Behind us, in the waystation, forgotten on the table where he'd left it, the prophecy book fell open on its own — to a page neither of us had marked.
