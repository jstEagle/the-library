## 8. The Smuggler's Toll

There are three rules to running cargo across the Greyfrost Strait, and I learned all of them the way you learn most things worth knowing: expensively.

One. Never name your price before you've seen what's being carried.

Two. Never carry anything that breathes if you can avoid it, because breathing things have histories, and histories have teeth.

Three. Never, under any circumstances, take a passenger who looks at your ship like she's a map instead of a boat.

I broke rule three within ten seconds of meeting him. The tall one — Sebastian, Maren called him, in a tone that suggested I'd better remember it — stood on my dock at Saltmere with snow in his hair and a leather satchel clutched to his chest like a firstborn child, and he looked past me, past my ship, out across the frozen strait, and I watched his eyes do the thing. The measuring thing. The *I could draw that* thing.

Cartographers. Every one of them a menace. Give a man a map and he starts believing the world owes him edges.

"Passage to the far shore," Maren said. She'd come down the coast road looking like a woman who'd been exiled twice and was considering a third time for variety. "Two passengers, one crate of supplies, discretion non-negotiable."

"Everything's negotiable," I said. "That's the whole point of me."

She put a purse on the bollard. It made the heavy, honest sound of real coin, which in Odile these days was rarer than sunlight. I weighed it in my palm, did some arithmetic involving risk, weather, and my own towering greed, and named a number that would have made my mother proud and my father weep into whatever fae wine he'd been drinking that week.

The tall one didn't flinch. That should have been my second warning. Passengers who don't flinch at my prices either have more money than sense or less to lose than sense, and both kinds end up being my problem.

* * *

My ship is called the *Kestrel's Regret*, which is a long story involving a bird, a bet, and a man I still owe an apology, and she is currently not so much sailing as lying on top of forty feet of sea ice like a very expensive sled. In winter the strait freezes solid enough to walk on, mostly, and every smuggler with a hull and no shame waits for it, because the Crown's patrols don't bother watching a road they think God himself has closed.

So there we were, three humans and one quarter-fae with excellent hearing and worse patience, hauling the *Regret* north over the ice with runners greased and sails furled, the sky the color of old pewter, the wind coming off the glacier fields with opinions about us personally.

I put the tall one on the bow line because he had the longest arms and the most apologetic expression, which is the correct combination for bow work. Maren took the stern. I steered, meaning I walked behind everything holding a rope and shouting, which is what passes for command among people like us.

For two hours it went fine. Cold, miserable, fine. The ice groaned beneath us in long whale-sounds, and somewhere out in the white a wind-wight sang its three-note song, and I threw it a copper without breaking stride because you do not let a wight sing the fourth note near your ship unless you enjoy repairs.

Then we stopped for the midday ration, huddled in the lee of the hull, and the tall one unwrapped his satchel to check on something inside it, and I saw the book.

Now. Let me tell you something about half-fae eyes. We see the world at a slight angle to everyone else. Mortal things look a little worn to us, a little faded, like cloth washed too many times. Fae things look too sharp, too bright, like cut glass left out in sun. And books that have been *touched by something older than either* — those look like neither. Those look like a door painted on a wall, and every instinct I have screams *do not open the painted door.*

His book looked like a painted door. Worse. It looked like a painted door someone had recently knocked on from the other side.

"What is that?" I asked, too casually, which anyone who knows me would tell you is the only way I ask questions that matter.

He hugged it closer. "A family heirloom."

"Funny," I said. "It looks like a prophecy."

Maren's hand drifted toward her sword, which for her is basically a nervous tic. "Wren."

"I'm making conversation." I leaned forward. "Because here's my problem, cartographer. My problem is that I know what Brightwater charts look like. My grandmother ran contraband up and down this coast for sixty years, and half her manifests were maps, and the ones she wouldn't touch — the ones she burned, the ones she made *me* burn when I was nine years old and crying about it — those were Brightwater charts. So when a man boards my ship with a book that hums and calls it an heirloom, I get curious. And then I get unfriendly."

He looked at me the way people do when they're deciding whether to lie to a person who can hear their heartbeat. Because I can, incidentally. Half-fae. It's not as useful as it sounds; most hearts are boring.

"The name's Brightwater," he said quietly.

And there it was. Rule two, broken. Breathing cargo with history.

* * *

Here's what the name means to people like me, in case you were raised somewhere warm and loved.

Before the Veil, before the winter, the fae courts and the mortal kingdoms traded openly, and the trade routes ran through the straits and the fens and the deep forests, and the smugglers — we weren't criminals then, we were *carriers*, a guild, a proud and larcenous institution. Then came the war, and after the war came the Veil, and after the Veil came the winter that has lasted longer than any winter has a right to, and the carriers lost everything. Our routes died. Our guildhall burned. Our kin on the other side of the Veil became stories, then rumors, then nothing at all.

And the story we were told — the story everyone was told — was that a mortal mapmaker drew the Veil shut. Drew it like a border on a chart, salt and ink and intention, and closed the door between the worlds, and never wrote down how to open it again.

The mapmaker's name was Brightwater.

You want to know why the fae half of me runs cold at that name? Because somewhere beyond the Veil I have cousins. Maybe. Had. There's a girl on the other side, or there was, a hundred years ago, who used to braid my hair wrong on purpose because she knew it made me laugh. Her name was Sessily. When the Veil closed, she was on the far side visiting our grandmother's grave.

I was six. I waited at the shore for four winters, because children don't understand borders, they only understand that someone didn't come back.

So no, I don't have warm feelings about Brightwaters. I have a scar where warm feelings used to be.

"You're joking," I said. "Tell me you're joking."

"He's not," Maren said.

"He could be. He could be a liar. Liars are my favorite kind of person." I stood up. The wind took my hair sideways. "Get off my ship."

"Wren—"

"Both of you, off. Take your coin, take your crate, take your haunted book, and walk back the way you came, because I will drag this hull to the far shore alone before I ferry a Brightwater anywhere."

The tall one — Sebastian — didn't move. That was the infuriating part. Most people, when I say *get off my ship*, get off my ship. It's practically my only superpower. But he just sat there in the lee of the hull with the wind trying to steal his hood and his stupid honest face doing something complicated, and finally he said:

"You knew Sessily."

The world went very quiet, the way it does over ice, like even the cold was leaning in to listen.

"My cousin," I said, "knew a Brightwater?"

"She was my grandmother's apprentice." His voice cracked on *apprentice*, just slightly, just enough that my traitor ears believed him. "In the valley. Before. She talked about a little sister on the coast who braided hair badly on purpose."

"That was a *compliment*, you absolute—" I stopped. Breathed. Hated myself. "She remembered me."

"She kept a drawing of you. Two girls on a dock. You were yelling at a gull."

"I was *negotiating* with a gull. There's a difference." I turned away because my face was doing something I didn't authorize. "That proves nothing. Names get borrowed. Stories get sold. For all I know you found her journal in a ditch and built yourself a personality out of it."

"Then make me prove it," he said. "Ask me anything a Brightwater would know. Ask me what she called the valley's border stones. Ask me—" he hesitated, and I heard his heart stumble, actually stumble, "—ask me why she stopped writing letters home."

"Why did she?" The question came out smaller than I wanted.

"Because the last letter she sent got answered by a magistrate asking how she knew the shape of the coastline beyond the Veil." He swallowed. "They nearly arrested her. After that she was afraid anything she wrote would be read as treason. Or magic. She thought silence was safer." His jaw tightened. "She was wrong. Silence is just a slower way of losing people."

Well. Damn him. Damn him for having an answer, and damn him for having the right one, and damn him most of all for saying it like it cost him something, because liars spend truth like counterfeiters spend gold — convincingly, and only when it profits them.

But his heart hadn't lied once. Not through the whole exchange. I'd have heard it.

* * *

Maren, to her credit, stayed out of it. She'd settled back against the crate with her arms crossed and the expression of a woman watching a fire decide whether to catch, and when I finally sat back down — heavily, gracelessly, the way you sit when you've lost an argument with yourself — she passed me the tea flask without comment.

We drank in silence for a while. The wind-wight sang its three notes somewhere east. I threw it another copper. Sentimental, yes, but it had witnessed that conversation and I don't need wights gossiping about me in the between-places.

"Here's what happens now," I said. "You'll tell me everything. On the ice, tonight, while we walk. All of it — the prophecy, the queen, the book, why a knight with no order and a cartographer with a death-name are running north instead of south. And if I believe you, I take you to the far shore, and maybe further, depending on how stupid I'm feeling. And if I don't believe you—" I let the pause stretch, because pauses are free and I'm frugal, "—well. The strait is very large and you two are very findable."

"That's fair," Sebastian said.

"It's not fair. Fairness is for markets. This is a toll." I stood, shouldered my rope, and nodded north, toward the pale line where the ice met a sky that couldn't decide what color to be. "Walk and talk, Brightwater. You've got until the light goes."

And he did. He told me about the date in the century-old book, and the border flickering at dusk, and falling through the Veil mid-step into snow that matched the prophecy word for word. He told me about Queen Isolde's protection offered with one hand and a cell held ready with the other. He told me about Pellam Oxley and the banned shelf, about the first Brightwater who drew the Veil shut — deliberately, protectively, a wall raised by someone trying to save both sides and damning them both instead. He told me about villages going silent on the northern roads, and a winter deepening too fast to be natural, and a book that had started rewriting itself.

Maren filled in the gaps he was too careful to say aloud: the executions Isolde was preparing, the chancellor Dreville whose smile reminded her of every officer who ever sold her out, the plan to reach the Veil's anchor before midwinter or die in the attempt.

By the time the light began to fail — that bruised violet that passes for dusk in Odile — I had walked perhaps five miles and traveled considerably further than that.

Because here's the thing I hadn't expected. Somewhere between *get off my ship* and *she kept a drawing of you*, the arithmetic changed. I'd spent thirty years treating the Brightwater name as a debt owed to me, to Sessily, to every carrier who froze waiting for a door that never opened. And now here was the last Brightwater, standing on my ice, telling me the door wasn't locked out of malice but out of fear — that his ancestor had slammed it shut to keep something *in*, something both worlds had made together, something that was now, apparently, waking up hungry.

That's not a villain. That's a warning.

And warnings, unlike debts, you can act on.

"So," I said, when he ran out of words. The stars were coming out hard and cold, the way they do. "The thing under the Veil. The thing your family imprisoned. Does it have a name?"

"Not that we've found. Pellam thinks the name might be the lock."

"Mm." I chewed on that. Names as locks. My grandmother would have had opinions; she used to say the fae gave their true names away the way drunk men give away keys. "And you mean to reach this anchor and do what, exactly? Open it? Close it harder? Draw a nicer border?"

"I don't know yet." He said it plainly, no performance, no false confidence, and somehow that was the most convincing thing he'd said all day. "I know I'm the only one who can read the ancestor's charts properly. Beyond that, I'm making it up as I go, and I'd appreciate it if nobody told the queen."

I laughed despite myself. First time in weeks, if I'm honest. The winter doesn't leave much room for laughing.

"Fine," I said. "Here's my toll, revised. Passage to the far shore, yes. And passage after that, wherever this idiot pilgrimage goes, because I've decided I want to see it. Not for you." I jabbed a finger at his chest, and he flinched, and I enjoyed that enormously. "Not even for Sessily, though she'd have liked you, which annoys me. I'm coming because if there's a door that opens twice in a hundred years, I intend to be standing next to it when it does. With my boots on. And my knife. And possibly a list of names on the other side I'd very much like to check."

Sebastian blinked. "That's... more than I hoped for."

"Hoped-for things are cheap. Negotiated things hold value." I turned back to the rope lines. "Rule four, Brightwater, since you clearly need it: never trust a smuggler's generosity. Trust her self-interest. Mine says the world ends if you fail, and I've grown attached to the world. Parts of it, anyway. The parts with tea."

Maren snorted. "Welcome aboard, cartographer. You've been conscripted."

"Story of my life," he said, and took up the bow line.

We walked on into the dark, three small figures dragging a ship across a frozen sea, headed toward an anchor beneath the ice and a war that wanted waking. Behind us the wind-wight finally sang its fourth note, far off, harmless now, and I chose to take it as applause.

Ahead, far ahead, at the edge of sight where the ice shone faintly blue — wrong-blue, lit-from-below-blue — something glimmered that hadn't glimmered yesterday.

I'd sailed this strait twenty winters. I knew every reflection it owned.

That light was new.

And new lights on old ice, in my experience, mean somebody downstairs has started knocking.
