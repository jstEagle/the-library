## 9. Maps of Places That Never Were

Wren's hold turned out to be a cave.

Not a ship, as I'd assumed when she said "my place" — a cave in the sea-cliff below a dead fishing village called Skerrow, its mouth hidden behind a curtain of icicles so thick they rang like organ pipes when the wind found them. Inside, the ice gave way to black stone, and the black stone gave way to something that made me stop walking entirely.

Bookshelves.

A cave full of bookshelves, carved into the living rock, rising three stories into the dark, connected by rope ladders and iron walkways that had no business existing under a smuggler's hideout. Lanterns burned in niches with the flat amber light of oil that never guttered. And everywhere — everywhere — maps.

Rolled maps in clay tubes. Flat maps pinned under river stones. Maps hanging from the ceiling like banners, some of them taller than Maren, who was not a short woman even before the armor.

"You live in a library," I said.

"I live in a smuggling den," Wren corrected, shaking snow out of her braids. "The library is camouflage."

"Camouflage for what?"

"For the fact that I'm a thief." She said it cheerfully, tossing her coat over a chair carved from a whale's vertebra. "Everything on these shelves was stolen from someone who didn't deserve it. Chancellors. Inquisitors. One archbishop who screamed like a kettle." She looked back at me, and her mismatched eyes — one dark, one the pale green of sea glass — caught the lantern light. "Including your family's things, Brightwater. That's why you're here. Try to act surprised."

I wasn't surprised. I'd stopped being surprised somewhere around the moment a patrol of frost-armored soldiers knelt to me in the snow because I happened to be holding a book. Surprise, I'd learned, was a luxury for people whose lives still had edges.

"Where do we start?" Maren asked. She'd taken up a position by the entrance out of habit — jailer's instinct, or bodyguard's, or just a woman who'd spent too many years sleeping with her back to walls. Her sword lay across her knees as she settled onto a bench, which was how she relaxed. I'd noticed this. It worried me slightly.

"With the chest," Wren said. "Bottom shelf, third gallery. The one sealed with wax and bad intentions. Pellam Oxley wrote to me about it years ago — the heretic wanted it, but he couldn't pay my rates, and I don't extend credit to men who've been excommunicated twice." She grinned. "You, though — you're the heir. Heirloom retrieval. No charge. Consider it an apology for threatening to leave you on the ice."

"You did leave us on the ice. For an hour."

"I left you *near* the ice. There's a difference, and it's measured in my conscience, which is very small." She was already climbing a ladder two rungs at a time, half-fae sure-footedness making the ropes look like solid ground. "Bring a lantern. And don't touch anything with your bare hands until I say. Some of these charts bite."

I believed her. By then, I believed almost anything about Odile.

* * *

The chest was iron-banded oak, small enough to carry, heavy enough to mean something. The seal on it had cracked with age, but the wax held the impression of a compass rose — and beneath the rose, worked into the design like a watermark, a name I knew better than my own handwriting.

*Brightwater.*

My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because in every other account of this night I am described as trembling, and while that may have been true later, in this first moment there was only a strange, ringing calm. A cartographer opening a cartouche. Routine work. The oldest routine I had.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were eleven charts.

I unwrapped the first one the way you'd unwrap a wound, expecting to hurt someone. What I saw instead made me sit down on the floor without deciding to.

It was the Veiled Valley.

Not the valley I'd mapped a hundred times — the borders, the salt lines, the passes where the fog pooled like milk — but the valley *before*. Before the Veil. Rendered in ink the color of dried blood on vellum gone ivory with age, drawn in a hand I recognized down to my bones, because it was nearly my own. The same slant. The same way of letting mountain ridges taper into feathered strokes. My father drew like that. His father, presumably. All the way back to whoever stood at this desk with this pen and decided, deliberately, to draw a wall around a world.

"This is pre-Veil," I said. My voice came out wrong. Thin. "This is — Wren, this is a map of the valley with *doors in it.*"

"Doors?"

"Passages. Marked crossings." My finger traced them: seven points along the valley's rim, each marked not with the conventional symbol for a border station but with something else — a circle bisected by a line, drawn again and again, each time more heavily, as if the draftsman had pressed harder each time he drew it. As if he was trying to make the mark *hold*. "These aren't records of crossings. They're —"

I stopped. Because that wasn't right either, and the wrongness of it was crawling up my spine like cold water.

Maps record. That's the first thing they teach you, or the first thing I ever taught myself, alone in the archive with borrowed instruments and no teacher who could see what I saw. A map says *here is the world; I have been here; here is where the water runs.* A map looks backward. A map is memory made navigable.

I laid the second chart beside the first, and then the third, and spread them across the whale-vertebra table until they overlapped in a fan of yellowed centuries, and the pattern stepped out of them the way patterns do when you finally stand far enough away — all at once, embarrassingly obvious, the kind of truth you feel stupid for not seeing sooner.

The charts weren't recording the Veil's construction.

They were *drafting* it.

* * *

"Look at the margins," I said. I'd pulled four charts into alignment, the earliest ones, and the sequence told a story no historian would believe. In the first, the valley sat open, its borders soft-edged, dotted with those seven door-marks. In the second, faint hatching appeared along the rim — the cartographic shorthand for uncertainty, for *something is here that I cannot fully render.* In the third, the hatching thickened into a continuous line. In the fourth, the line had weight. Shading. A wall, drawn in ink and intention.

And in the margin of the fourth, in that familiar hand, a single sentence in Old Valley script — the script I'd spent my childhood deciphering off tombstones:

*I am not closing the road. I am hiding the hinge.*

"He wasn't describing what he'd done," I heard myself say. "He was writing instructions. To himself. Or —" and here the ringing calm finally broke, and something much larger moved through my chest, awe and terror sharing a body the way they always do — "or to whoever came after him. To *me*. Every chart is a stage. Look — the line gets stronger each time because he's testing the notation. Figuring out how to draw a thing into being. These aren't records of places that exist. They're plans for places that *don't yet.*"

The lantern light seemed to pull inward. Even the wind outside the icicles went quiet, as if the cave itself were listening.

Maren spoke first, carefully, the way soldiers speak when they've realized the ground has changed under them. "You're saying your ancestor didn't find the Veil. He built it."

"I'm saying someone with my blood and my hand learned that a map, drawn rightly enough, doesn't describe the world. It *argues* with it." I touched the fourth chart's border-line, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "And he argued one into existence around an entire valley."

Wren had gone very still, which for Wren was its own kind of alarm. Half-fae stillness — the stillness of a creature whose people remembered the world before walls. "Why," she said. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes. "Nobody builds a wall that big over nothing. Your pretty valley sits between fae country and mortal country like a cork in a bottle. Somebody paid for that cork. Somebody *bled* for it — my grandmother's songs are all about the year the roads closed, and none of them end well for anyone. So why would a mortal mapmaker shut the door on both our kinds at once?"

"I don't know yet." I gathered the remaining charts, unwrapping them faster now. "But he wrote more than one line. Cartographers annotate. We can't help ourselves."

The fifth chart through the eleventh were progressively stranger. The valley appeared in each, but smaller, more abstracted, less a place than a diagram — until the last chart, where the valley wasn't a drawing at all anymore. It was a *keyhole.*

Drawn in plan view, geometric, precise: a shape of nested curves that any locksmith would recognize, rendered at the scale of a landscape. And around the keyhole, in a careful ring, the same words repeated in three scripts — Old Valley, and two others I didn't know, though Wren made a sound low in her throat when she saw the second one, a sound like grief remembering itself.

"What does it say?" Maren asked.

"It says the same thing in all three tongues," Wren said quietly. "It says *both sides agreed.*" She looked up at me, and the sea-glass eye seemed to look through me, at ancestors I'd never met. "Your man didn't build the Veil alone, Brightwater. Someone on my side of the water helped him. That's the part the histories burned. That's the part they killed archivists for keeping."

"And the keyhole?" I asked. "What's it a keyhole *to?*"

"That," Wren said, "is the wrong question, and the fact that you're asking it means Pellam Oxley was right about your blood." She tapped the empty space inside the keyhole's curve — the space the valley occupied, the space the whole diagram orbited. "The right question isn't what the lock opens. The right question is what the lock *holds shut.* Because nobody draws a door that elaborate to keep people out."

She let that land.

"People build doors," she said, "to keep something *in.*"

* * *

We worked until the lanterns burned low, which Wren swore they never did, which should have frightened me more than it did. Maren slept in shifts against the wall with her hand on her sword. I barely slept at all. There will be time to catalog everything I found that night — the marginalia in cipher, the measurements that matched the prophecy book's dimensions exactly, the sketch of a compass whose needle pointed not north but *downward*, toward whatever lies beneath both worlds — but what matters now, what mattered at 3 a.m. with frost ferns creeping up the cave walls, is the realization I wrote in my own journal in my own shaky hand, the first honest sentence I'd produced since falling through the sky:

*The Veil was not an accident of geography or a punishment from heaven. It was an engineering project. Undertaken jointly. By mortals and fae. To imprison something they had made together — and my family has spent ten generations standing guard over the blueprints without knowing what they guarded.*

I remember setting the pen down after that and laughing, once, quietly, so as not to wake Maren. Because the alternative to laughing was screaming, and I'd already done enough screaming for one lifetime, and besides —

Besides, there was a worse thought underneath, and it surfaced just before dawn, when Wren climbed down from the upper galleries carrying a twelfth chart I hadn't seen, one she'd kept apart, in a case of black glass, and set in front of me without a word.

It was unfinished.

The keyhole diagram again — but this version was newer, the ink brighter, the vellum barely aged. Dated, in that ancestral hand, to a year only sixty years past. Long after my family's records claimed the last Brightwater mapmaker died.

And beneath the unfinished diagram, one final line of annotation, the handwriting degrading now, hurried, frightened:

*It has learned the shape of the lock. Someone on the inside is teaching it. If the keyhole turns, do not seek the heir of Brightwater —*

The sentence stopped there. Cut off mid-word, the pen stroke trailing into a blot, as if the writer's hand had been interrupted.

Or taken.

Wren watched me read it twice. Then she said, very softly, for a smuggler confessing to a client: "I stole this one six months ago. From Lord Chancellor Dreville's private study. Under his floorboards. In a locked box."

Outside the cave, through the singing curtain of icicles, far out across the frozen strait, the new light — the wrong-blue, lit-from-below light we'd seen from the ice — pulsed once, twice, slow and patient, like something knocking.

Like something that had learned, at long last, the shape of the lock.
