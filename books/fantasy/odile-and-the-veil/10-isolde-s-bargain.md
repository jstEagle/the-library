## 10. Isolde's Bargain

The summons came on a raven's wing and a dead man's horse.

I'll explain the second part. The courier who reached us at the crossroads shrine outside Thornmere rode a gray gelding with army tack and an army brand, and when I asked him whose horse it was, he looked at me the way soldiers look at each other when they've both seen too much, and said, "It was Captain Voss's."

Voss had commanded the northern garrison at Coldharbor. I'd drunk with him twice. He'd been a decent man in a profession that eats them.

"And where is Captain Voss now?"

"Snow took him," the courier said, which was the official phrase for everything that winter. Snow took him. Snow took the village of Ellswater — three hundred souls, silent since the last moon. Snow took the road to the coast, the granaries at Dunmark, the bell-ringer at Saint Aldric's who rang the hours for forty years until one morning nobody heard anything but wind.

Snow was taking the whole kingdom, one silence at a time, and Queen Isolde wanted her Cartographer back in Vharen.

Sebastian read the summons over my shoulder. I felt him go still the way a rabbit goes still — not calm, just listening for the hawk.

"'By order of Her Majesty,'" he read aloud, "'the Cartographer of Ends shall return to the citadel to fulfill his charge before midwinter.' It says fulfill his charge. It doesn't say how."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't."

"Pellam," Sebastian called back toward the shrine, where the old archivist was arguing with the shrine's one surviving votive candle, "does the prophecy say *how*?"

Pellam emerged from the dark of the doorway with the book hugged to his chest like a squalling infant. "The prophecy says many things, most of them recently, all of them alarming. The most recent addition describes your death at midwinter in some detail." He adjusted his spectacles. "I would not characterize any of it as instructions."

"So we're being summoned to a deadline with no method attached," Sebastian said. "That's not a summons. That's a noose with good penmanship."

He wasn't wrong. That was the trouble with Sebastian Brightwater: he kept being right about things that would have been kinder to be wrong about. Three weeks ago he'd been a lost valley-boy clutching a book in the snow, and I'd taken one look at the fear on him and thought, *there it is, that's my own face from ten years ago.* Now he stood straighter. He'd learned to hold the prophecy book without flinching. The kingdom had gotten its teeth into him, and kingdoms don't let go politely.

"We could run," Wren said. She'd appeared out of nowhere, as she did — one moment absent, the next leaning against the shrine's lichened wall with her arms crossed and her half-fae eyes catching what little light there was. "South, past the Reach, down to the coves. I know routes the queen's own scouts couldn't find with a bloodhound and a bribe."

"You'd abandon your cargo?" Pellam asked mildly. He meant Sebastian. Wren had started calling him cargo somewhere over the strait, and none of us had stopped her, because it was funnier than the truth.

"I'd relocate my cargo," Wren said. "There's a difference. One involves paperwork."

"The queen summoned me by name," Sebastian said quietly. "If I run, she doesn't stop looking. And every village that goes silent while she's busy hunting me — that's on my ledger too." He looked at me. That was the worst part, lately. He'd started looking at me like I was the one who knew things. "Maren?"

I looked north, where the road ran white and empty toward Vharen, and I thought about Captain Voss's horse standing patient in the cold, and I said the thing I already knew I'd say.

"We ride for Vharen."

* * *

Vharen had grown teeth since I'd last seen it.

The citadel always perched on its crag like something waiting, but now the walls bristled with new timber stakes hung with lanterns that burned blue — fae-fire, bought at whatever price Isolde was paying these days. Soldiers lined the causeway in double file, and their armor was wrong: frost-plate, the old pattern, the kind that hadn't been forged since the First War. Somebody had opened the armory vaults. Somebody was preparing for a war they expected to lose.

"They're dressing for history," Pellam murmured beside me as we walked the causeway. "Kings and queens do that when they've stopped believing in tomorrow."

At the gates, a chamberlain with a face like folded paper separated us. "Her Majesty will receive the Cartographer. His companions will be quartered."

"His companions," I said, "stay with him."

Something flickered behind the chamberlain's composure. "Sir Maren Hale is relieved of escort duty by royal decree. You will report to the Marshal of the Household." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Your reinstatement hearing has been scheduled. For tonight."

And there it was. Ten years I'd carried my disgrace like a stone sewn into my coat, and now the crown offered to cut it out — the night before everything, the exact night a disgraced knight might prove inconveniently loyal to a prisoner instead of a queen.

They weren't even subtle. That was the thing about Isolde's court everyone forgot: it didn't need to be subtle. It had never once needed to lie to you when it could simply hand you exactly what you wanted and watch what you did with it.

I handed over my sword because protocol demanded it, and because refusing in front of forty soldiers would have solved nothing except the question of whether I could die here today. They let Pellam and Wren go to the guest quarters — watched, I had no doubt. They took Sebastian down a different corridor, toward the royal wing, and the last I saw of him was his face turning back to me, asking the same question he'd asked on the road.

*Maren?*

I gave him the only answer I had left, which was a nod I didn't believe in, and went to find out what my honor cost these days.

* * *

The reinstatement hearing lasted eleven minutes and consisted of Lord Chancellor Dreville reading charges I barely remembered into a record nobody would ever consult, then declaring them expunged by royal mercy, then shaking my hand with fingers cool as a banker's.

"Ser Maren Hale," Dreville said, and the title landed on me like snow off a roof. "The Order of the Veil rises again, and it begins with you. Her Majesty requires knights she can trust. Completely." His smile was a masterwork of nothing. "You understand trust, I hope. You understand what it costs to lose it."

"I understand it better than most, my lord."

"Yes," he said softly. "You do. That's rather the point."

I should have understood him right then. Later — after everything, after the Burnt Reach and the traitor's face and the long fall of it all — I would replay this conversation a hundred times and curse myself for a fool. But that night, all I saw was a chancellor doing his job, and all I felt was the stone falling out of my coat after ten years, and I was so light with it that I walked to my assigned quarters like a man walking on the deck of a ship that had finally stopped pitching.

Then a maid brought me supper I hadn't ordered, and under the bread was a note in a hand I recognized from a hundred dispatches:

*West gallery. Midnight. Come alone. — I.*

* * *

The west gallery of Vharen citadel was a corridor of windows facing the mountains, hung with portraits of dead monarchs who all wore the same expression: the look of people who had learned that ruling was mostly watching things end. At midnight the gallery was lit by a single brazier, and Queen Isolde stood before the middle window with her crown off, which I had never seen, holding a cup of wine she wasn't drinking, which I had.

"Maren Hale," she said, without turning. "Ten years ago you were the finest sword in my father's guard. Then you burned a village."

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "Your Majesty—"

"Don't." She turned. Up close, without the crown, she looked less like a queen than like a woman who had been awake for a very long time. "I've read the full account. Every page. You were ordered to burn Ashfell because the court believed plague had taken it. You rode there and found the plague was a lie — a story told to justify burning a village that sheltered deserters. And you refused. You stood in front of that village with your sword drawn against your own brothers-in-arms, and you held them off until dawn, and when the order came again you broke ranks and let the deserters scatter into the hills." She took a slow sip of wine. "The court called it disgrace. I've always called it the only honorable act committed by anyone in that entire miserable affair."

The gallery was very quiet. Somewhere below, a sentry's boots rang on stone.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I'm about to ask you to do it again." She set the cup on the window ledge. "Come. There's something you need to see before you decide anything."

She led me up a spiral stair to a small room I'd have sworn the citadel didn't have — a room with one window, heavily shuttered, and a peephole bored through the shutter's seam. She gestured me to it.

Through the seam I looked down into the great hall, and my breath stopped.

The hall blazed with light that wasn't fire. Chandeliers of blue flame burned without heat, and beneath them, at the long table, sat the queen's council on one side — Dreville, the Marshal, three lords whose names I knew from proclamations — and on the other side, the envoys.

They were beautiful the way ice is beautiful, which is to say they were beautiful and they would kill you. Tall, silver-haired, dressed in colors I couldn't fix in memory; every time I looked away and back, their garments had changed hue. The Winter Court of the fae, seated at a negotiating table in the heart of Vharen, and the negotiation did not look like it was going well.

"What are they offering?" I whispered.

"An end to the winter," Isolde said behind me. "Their terms are simple. They claim the thing beneath the Veil — the prisoner, the source of all this cold — belongs to them by ancient law. They want it returned to their keeping. In exchange, spring."

"And the catch?"

"The door opens both ways, Ser Maren. If the Veil opens enough to hand over their prisoner, it opens enough for everything else to come through. Their courts, their wars, their hungers. My ancestors sealed the Veil precisely to prevent that. Now their envoys sit in my hall telling me that sealing it was theft, and that the theft is why my kingdom is dying." Her voice stayed level, but I heard the crack running under it, the way you hear ice groan before it gives. "Dreville counsels acceptance. He says a bargain honored is better than a world frozen. The Marshal counsels refusal. And I—" For one unguarded moment she closed her eyes, and she looked every year of her reign. "I counsel nothing, because whichever way I move, I move my people into the dark."

I stepped back from the peephole. "Where does Sebastian fit in this?"

"Ah." She smiled, and it was the coldest thing in the room, colder than the envoys below. "Now we arrive at the honest part of the evening."

She crossed to a writing desk in the corner and drew out a paper. I knew the format before she turned it around. Every soldier knows it. It was short. It was signed in advance. It bore the royal seal and a date line left blank.

"A warrant of execution," she said. "'For the impostor styling himself Cartographer of Ends, whose failure has doomed the realm.'" She held it out to me. "If he ends the winter by midwinter, this burns. If he fails — if he runs, or falters, or turns out to be exactly what he fears he is, a mapmaker with a famous name and no magic at all — then this is signed, dated, and carried out, and the realm gets a corpse to blame instead of a queen to depose."

"You'd kill him." My voice came out flat. Good. Flat was better than what was underneath it. "He's not a weapon, Your Majesty. He's a frightened man who got pulled through a wall by a prophecy he never asked for."

"He is the only hope my kingdom has, and hope that cannot fail is called faith, and faith does not feed anyone in spring." She set the warrant on the desk between us, deliberately, like a card played face-up. "Here is my bargain, Ser Hale. Your honor is restored. Your Order is reborn with you at its head — a new Order, clean, unforsworn, the thing you've dreamed of in the ten years since Ashfell. Command it. Rebuild it. All I require is this: keep the Cartographer close. Watch him. And if midwinter comes and the winter hasn't broken — you will be the one who carries out this sentence."

The room tilted. I put my hand on the desk to steady it, and my hand stopped an inch from the warrant, because touching it felt like touching a blade I'd be expected to use.

"You're asking me to guard him and grave him."

"I'm asking you to be the one person in this citadel whose word means something. If it must be done, let it be done by someone who will hate it properly. Not Dreville's men, who would enjoy it. Not the frost-guard, who feel nothing." Her eyes held mine, and I realized with a strange, distant clarity that she wasn't cruel. That was the horror of it. She was kind, in the way that surgeons are kind, and she had reasoned herself all the way to this desk and this warrant and this offer, and she genuinely believed she was sparing everyone a worse death. "You held a village against your own order once, Maren. I am asking: can you hold a kingdom against your own heart?"

For a long moment I said nothing. Through the shutter's seam, faintly, I could still see the blue light of the fae chandeliers, and beyond them the dark window at the far end of the great hall, and beyond that, somewhere north, the mountains where the Veil stood, and beneath the Veil, the thing that was eating the winter into us one silent village at a time.

Ashfell. Ten years ago, I'd stood in front of a burning order with my sword drawn, and I'd lost everything, and I'd told myself for a decade that it had been worth it, and some nights I'd almost believed it.

"I need until morning," I said.

Isolde studied me. Whatever she saw, she nodded once, the nod of a woman adding a figure to a ledger she already knew would balance.

"Morning, then. The warrant stays on this desk." As I reached the stair, her voice followed me, quiet and terrible: "Ser Hale. Whichever way you choose — choose it knowing that I chose this crown over my own sister, when the succession split us, and I have slept every night since. It can be done. That is all I promised myself. That it can be done."

I climbed down into the dark of the citadel with my honor freshly minted and already spending itself, and I went to find the others.

* * *

Wren was awake, of course. Half-fae don't sleep like us; they hover somewhere between dreaming and listening, and she'd heard my boots coming down the guest corridor before I reached the door. She opened it before I knocked.

"Well?" she said. "You've got the face. The face you get when someone's offered you something you want and something you hate in the same box."

"Isolde restored my name. New Order, my command. In exchange, I execute Sebastian if he fails."

Wren whistled low. "Crowns really do all shop at the same store." She glanced back into the room, where Pellam sat surrounded by open books by candlelight, and past him, on the narrow cot, Sebastian lay asleep — actually asleep, for the first time since I'd known him, one arm flung over his eyes, the prophecy book locked in the chest at the foot of the bed because he couldn't bear to look at it and couldn't bear to let it out of reach.

"He trusts you," Wren said quietly. All the mockery gone out of her voice, which from Wren was like a drawn blade being sheathed. "Out of everyone in this frozen madhouse, he trusts you. So." Her mismatched eyes came up to mine. "What did you tell her?"

"Nothing yet. I have until morning."

"Then you've got until morning to work out the only question that matters." She leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. "Not crown or conscience. That's the pretty version they give you in songs. The real question is: when the moment comes — and it'll come fast, they always do — which do you obey? The woman who gave you back your name, or the man who gave you back your reason to have one?"

Behind her, in his sleep, Sebastian murmured something. I caught only a fragment — *the margins, look at the margins* — and Pellam looked up sharply from his books, because the archivist had spent all evening with the ancestor's charts, and I saw in his face that the sleeping man had said something the waking books agreed with.

"Wren," Pellam said slowly, setting down his pen. "Wake him. Gently. I think our cartographer has just remembered where his grandfather hid the truth — and I suspect, given the company this court keeps, that we may not have until morning after all."

Down the corridor, faint but unmistakable, boots were already coming. More than one pair. Moving with purpose.

I put my hand to the empty scabbard where my sword used to be, and made my choice in the space of a heartbeat, the way choices always come — not weighed, but thrown.

"Not morning, then," I said.
