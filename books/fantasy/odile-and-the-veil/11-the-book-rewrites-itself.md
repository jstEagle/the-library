## 11. The Book Rewrites Itself

I had always believed that books were the most honest things in the world. A book, once written, does not flinch. It does not revise itself to spare your feelings or to suit the politics of whoever happens to be reading over your shoulder. Ink is a kind of promise. That was the first thing Pellam Oxley ever taught me, back when I still thought of him as a harmless old man with ink-stained fingers and a heretic's smile — though in fairness, he taught it to me by accident, in passing, while arguing with a shelf.

So you can imagine how I felt when I watched a book break its own promise in front of my eyes.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes in Vharen do, with an invitation I couldn't refuse and probably should have.

Queen Isolde had granted me "the freedom of the citadel," which is royal-speak for *you may walk anywhere we can see you*. Maren shadowed me through the corridors like a guilt-ridden ghost in half-armor, and I had learned to stop resenting it. There are worse fates than being guarded by someone who has already decided you're not worth assassinating but hasn't yet decided whether you're worth liking. It keeps a man humble.

Pellam's archive lived in the undercroft beneath the east wing, down a stair that smelled of cold stone and older paper. The queen called it a repository of state records. Pellam called it "the part of the library where truth goes to be punished." He kept the banned histories there — chronicles the crown had suppressed, genealogies scrubbed of inconvenient names, maps of places the kingdom preferred to forget existed. He showed them to me anyway. That should have been my first warning about Pellam: he treats prohibitions as filing suggestions.

That evening, he had the prophecy book open on a lectern between two candles, and he was looking at it the way a physician looks at a patient who has stopped breathing politely and started breathing rudely.

"You wanted to see it again," he said, without turning around. He always knew it was me; I never did learn how. "Sit down, Brightwater. And put your hands in your lap. You have the touch of a man who drops things when he's nervous."

"I'm not nervous."

"You're holding your breath. Sit."

I sat. The book lay open to the passage that had ruined my life — the one that named tonight's date a century before tonight existed, the one that described a cartographer falling out of nowhere into Odile's snow with a book in his hands. My arrival, word for word, down to the snowfall. I had read it so many times I could recite it in my sleep, which I did, frequently, usually around the hour when sleep gave up on me entirely.

"Read it to me," Pellam said. "Aloud. Slowly."

"Why?"

"Because I want to hear whether the words sound different to you than they did yesterday." He finally turned, and his eyes behind their smudged spectacles were sharper than any blade Maren carried. "Texts like this one are dead things, Sebastian. Beautiful dead things. They say what they said a thousand years ago and nothing else. If this one has started saying something new..."

He didn't finish. Scholars never finish sentences like that. They leave the terrible implication hanging in the air like a sword of Damocles made of punctuation.

I read. The old lines came out steady enough: the Cartographer of Ends, the ending of all things, midwinter, the Veil. Then I reached the bottom of the page, where the vellum should have been blank except for a century of dust and one wine ring left by some long-dead archivist with poor discipline —

— and there was writing there.

I want to be precise about this moment, because precision is all I have left. There had been no writing on that portion of the page yesterday. I would stake my life on it, and in fact several people in this kingdom were actively trying to make that arrangement permanent, so the stakes were not theoretical. The page had been blank. Now it was not. The ink was black and fine and sat *on top of* the vellum rather than sinking into it, the way ink sits on a surface that hasn't decided yet whether to accept it.

The letters read:

*When the frost remembers its name, a traitor walks crowned in borrowed light. Look to the hand that feeds the dark beneath. He shall sup with the queen and smile.*

I read it twice. Three times. My mouth went dry somewhere around the second pass.

"Pellam."

"Mm."

"There's writing here that wasn't here before."

"Yes," he said, very calmly, in the voice of a man who has been waiting three days for someone else to notice. "There is."

"That's impossible."

"Wonderful word, impossible. It means 'I have not personally seen it happen.' Nothing more." He pulled his spectacles off and polished them on his sleeve, which was what he did instead of screaming. I was beginning to catalog these habits. "Watch the page, boy. Don't read. Watch."

I watched.

For a long moment, nothing happened. The candles guttered. Somewhere above us, the citadel creaked and settled like an old ship. I was just beginning to compose the rational explanation I would offer Maren later — damp, mineral seepage, a very committed forgery — when the surface of the vellum shivered.

Not moved. *Shivered.* The way water shivers when something passes beneath it.

Then, stroke by stroke, as if drawn by a pen held by a hand I couldn't see, new letters surfaced. The ink rose up out of the page itself, or perhaps condensed out of the cold air above it — I genuinely could not tell which, and neither explanation made me feel better. Line after line, in the same antique script as the rest of the book:

*And the Cartographer of Ends shall stand at the anchor beneath the ice, and the choice shall be set before him as a mirror is set before a drowning man. What he chooses, all worlds shall wear. But mark this well: the door opens both ways, and midwinter takes its toll. The Cartographer shall die at midwinter, unmourned by crowns, and his blood shall feed the frost forever.*

The pen-strokes finished. The ink dried. The candle flames stood perfectly straight, as if they too were afraid to move.

Neither of us spoke for what felt like the length of a small war.

"Well," Pellam said at last, setting his spectacles back on his nose with hands that were almost steady. "In four hundred years of keeping banned texts, I have seen books forged, books burned, books doctored by ambitious clerks, and one memorable volume that screamed when opened. I have never seen a book write itself."

"Maybe someone wrote it ahead of time. Invisible ink. Heat reveals —"

"Invisible ink sits *in* the page, Sebastian. This rose *out* of it. And unless someone has developed ink that anticipates the future, then this text is not a record." He looked at me over the rims of his glasses, and for the first time since I'd met him, the heretical archivist looked afraid. "It's a draft."

"A draft?"

"Prophecies get revised, boy. Everyone assumes the revision happens in heaven and the book merely reports it. But what if the book *is* the drafting table?" He tapped the fresh ink with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "What if somewhere, something is writing the future — and editing as it goes?"

* * *

Maren found us there an hour later, because Maren always found us, because I was fairly sure by now that she'd memorized my walking patterns the way I'd memorized coastlines. She took one look at our faces and unslung her sword and set it on the table, which was her version of asking what was wrong.

"Tell me," she said.

Pellam told her. I watched her face go through the stages: disbelief, professional assessment of the forgery angle, dismissal of the forgery angle, and then the particular gray stillness she got when something touched the war she'd been exiled from.

"The traitor line," she said finally. "*He shall sup with the queen and smile.* When?"

"It doesn't say," Pellam admitted. "Prophecy is generous with doom and stingy with schedules."

"And the other part." Her eyes came to me, and I saw her decide, deliberately, not to soften it. She'd told me once that softening bad news was a form of lying, and liars could recognize each other. "The death line. Midwinter."

"I heard it," I said. "I can read. Thank you."

"Sebastian —"

"I'm fine."

"You're doing the thing where you joke."

"I'm aware I'm doing the thing where I joke. It's load-bearing. Take it away and the whole structure comes down."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and died there. "Midwinter is eleven days away. Isolde expects you to end the winter by then. Dreville expects you to fail by then. And now a book that has been silent for a century says you die on the same day." She braced her hands on the lectern. "Either the book is telling the truth, or someone wants us to believe it is. Both options mean we're out of time."

"There's a third option," I said.

"Oh?"

"Option three: I'm hallucinating all of this, and honestly, I'd like to lodge a formal complaint with whoever manages that department."

Maren looked at Pellam. Pellam looked at me. Neither of them laughed, which I chose to interpret as respect for my material.

* * *

We did not sleep that night. We sat in the undercroft with the book between us like a fourth person at the table, taking turns watching it, because none of us trusted it alone with the darkness.

Around the coldest hour, it happened again.

This time I saw the whole thing clearly, because I was watching when the vellum shivered and the ink came up like frost forming on glass — slowly, beautifully, terribly. New lines, in that same patient antique hand:

*A traitor among the queen's circle, old in service, deeper in debt. He has fed the dark beneath the Veil with years and names, and the dark has fed him in turn. His bargain outlives his conscience. Seek the chancellor's seal; seek the ledger written in two hands.*

Maren read it standing, very still, the way she stood when she was converting fear into plans. "Dreville," she said quietly.

"The Lord Chancellor," Pellam confirmed grimly. "Old in service. Forty years in the queen's circle, if he's served a day. And I have heard rumors — rumors I dismissed as rumor — of ledgers kept in two hands. One for the crown. One for... other parties."

"What other parties?" I asked.

"No one knows. That's rather the point of a second ledger."

I stared at the fresh ink. *Fed the dark beneath the Veil.* In the Burnt Reach, I had seen my ancestor standing on both sides of the war's final battle, and I had come away with questions I hadn't dared ask aloud. Now the book was answering them in advance, and every answer was worse than the question.

"It's describing him like it knows him," I said. "Personally. Like it's *angry* at him."

"Books don't get angry," Maren said.

"Then what's writing this?"

Pellam took off his spectacles again. Third time that night. At this rate he was going to polish them to powder. "I've asked myself that since the first line appeared," he said. "And I keep arriving at the same answer, and I dislike the answer immensely." He laid the spectacles down. "Whoever wrote the original prophecy — whoever drew the Veil shut — left more than a warning, Sebastian. They left a *correspondence*. The book isn't predicting. It's reporting. Something on the far side of the Veil is sending dispatches."

"Through the book."

"Through anything thin enough. Paper is thin. So are the walls between worlds, at midwinter." He closed the book — carefully, the way you close a door on a room where something is crying — and rested his hand flat upon the cover. "Which brings us to the question I've been avoiding all night. The death line."

"Don't," Maren said.

"Somebody has to." Pellam looked at me, and his voice gentled into something I hadn't heard from him before — not the archivist, not the heretic, but the teacher. "The text says you die at midwinter, unmourned by crowns. Texts like this describe, they don't command. But here is what keeps an old man awake: if the book can add lines, the book can *change* lines. Which means the death at midwinter is not a wall. It's a sentence in a draft. And drafts—" his eyes glinted, fierce and tired and hopeful all at once, "—drafts can be edited."

I sat with that. Around us the undercroft breathed its cold stone breath, and above us the citadel slept, and somewhere in it a lord chancellor who fed the dark beneath the world was presumably sleeping too, smiling, having supped with his queen.

Eleven days. Eleven days until midwinter, until the date the book had promised me a century ago came due with interest.

"Then we edit," I said.

Maren frowned. "Edit what? The book won't take orders —"

"Not the book." I stood, and my legs were steadier than I expected them to be. Maybe fear and resolve use the same muscles. "Everything else. The traitor. The ledger. The thing beneath the Veil. If the future is a draft, then it's being written by whatever happens next. And what happens next—" I looked at the two of them, my jailer and my heretic, the strangest family I'd ever been drafted into, "—is up to us. Starting with finding out what Dreville keeps in his second ledger."

Pellam smiled the smile of a man whose heresies had finally been vindicated. Maren was already reaching for her sword.

And on the lectern between us, unopened, untouched, the prophecy book shivered once — softly, like a laugh, or like a warning — and I would have sworn on every map I'd ever drawn that the ink inside was moving, adding one more line to a story that had just remembered it wasn't finished with me yet.
