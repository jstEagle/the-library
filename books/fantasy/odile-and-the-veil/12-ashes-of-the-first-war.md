## 12. Ashes of the First War

I've smuggled a lot of things across a lot of borders in my time — stolen relics, fugitive archivists, one truly memorable shipment of cursed honey that I still have nightmares about — but I have never once smuggled anyone *into* the Burnt Reach on purpose. People fall into it, the way people fall into rivers: by accident, by grief, or by being spectacularly stupid. Nobody sails toward it with a smile.

And yet here we were, three days out from the coves, pointing my ship's prow at the one stretch of coast where even the ice refuses to commit.

Let me explain something about the Reach for those of you lucky enough never to have seen it. The war that ended everything — the First War, they call it, though "first" implies there was a second, and there wasn't, because nobody survived long enough to start one — was fought along this coastline two hundred years ago, give or take a decade nobody can agree on. When it ended, it didn't end so much as *stop*, mid-scream, mid-charge, mid-prayer. The snow came down afterward like an apology and then, somewhere along this particular stretch of grey shingle, it simply stopped landing.

That's the thing about the Burnt Reach that no ballad gets right. It doesn't snow *there*. Snow falls above it, all right — you can watch it falling, fat and slow and ordinary — and then it hits some invisible ceiling about ten feet up and just... hovers. Thousands of flakes, suspended, like the sky forgot to finish its sentence. Like the whole battlefield is held under glass.

Pellam says it's residual magic, frozen mid-dissipation. Maren says it's a curse. Sebastian, our resident rationalist, said it was probably an unusual thermal inversion caused by — and I quote — "localized heat differentials in the underlying permafrost," which is exactly the kind of sentence a man says when he's terrified and reaching for anything that sounds like a map legend.

Me? I say the Reach is a wound that never scabbed, and wounds don't care about thermodynamics.

* * *

We made landfall at what passed for dawn, which in deep winter this far north means the sky went from black to a bruised purple for about forty minutes before giving up and going back to black. I know these waters the way other sailors know their mothers' faces. My mother was fae; my father was a man who owed money to everyone in three ports; between the two of them I learned early that belonging anywhere is just a rumor you agree to keep telling. But even I don't belong here. Even *here* knows better than to claim me.

The four of us came ashore in the grey half-light, and I'll be honest — I nearly turned us around twice. The first time was when Maren, who has the survival instincts of a much stupider woman, suggested we walk straight up the middle of the old battle line "to get a proper view." The second time was when Sebastian asked me, in a voice pitched suspiciously casual, whether I'd ever heard ghost-lights described as *interactive.*

"Interactive how?" I said.

"As in, they respond to you."

"Everything responds to you, Brightwater. That's your whole problem."

He didn't laugh. Neither did Pellam, who had gone quiet in the way he goes quiet when a book in his head has started disagreeing with itself. The archivist had been muttering over his notes since the strait crossing, cross-referencing the ancestor's charts against three banned histories and one heretical pamphlet he keeps sewn into his coat lining, and somewhere around midnight he'd stopped taking notes and started taking *notes about his notes*, which in Pellam-language means: I have found something and I am not ready to say what.

So we walked. Four of us, roped together like climbers, because I insisted — I've pulled too many bodies out of crevasses hidden under hovering snow to trust anyone's footing, including my own. Maren led. She always leads when there's a chance of trouble; it's the last piece of the knight she still wears like a habit she can't quit. Pellam stumbled along behind her, clutching his satchel of forbidden paper like a man carrying a sleeping infant through a war zone. Sebastian walked beside me, and I noticed — because noticing things is my trade — that his hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

"You knew," he said quietly, after an hour. Not asking. Telling.

"Knew what?"

"That this place was real. That the stories weren't stories."

"Wren Ashvale," I said, "has known a great many things she wished she didn't. It comes with the bloodline. Half of mine sees doors where other people see walls." I paused, and let him sit with that for a second, because I'm generous like that. "The other half sees exits."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he'd been looking at everything in Odile lately, like a man trying to memorize a room he expected to be thrown out of. And I thought, not for the first time: this idiot actually believes he might die out here, and he's walking anyway. There's a word for that. I've heard knights use it. I've never once used it myself, because the moment you name a thing brave you're halfway to naming it stupid, and I refuse to do his eulogy.

But I walked with him. That's the arrangement. That's what we are now, the four of us — not friends, gods forbid, I've seen what friendship costs in this country and the exchange rate is appalling — but something adjacent. A crew. A conspiracy of the unwilling, bound together by a prophecy none of us asked for and a winter none of us can outrun.

* * *

By midday we reached the heart of it.

You feel the Reach before you see it. The cold changes quality — stops being weather and becomes *attention*. The wind dies completely, which sounds like mercy until you realize the silence isn't empty. It's full. Packed. Two hundred years of last words, all of them still waiting their turn to be finished.

Then the ground changed under our boots. Char. Not ash — ash is soft, apologetic, blows away. This was char, fused, glassy in places, the earth burned down to bone and then frozen mid-crisp. Here and there, shapes. A spearhead. A helm split like a walnut. A shield boss with a sigil I recognized from Pellam's histories and hated on sight: the double-star of the old Vhareni crown, back when the crown still pretended the fae were partners instead of problems.

And above it all, ten feet up, maybe twelve: the snow. Hanging. Millions of flakes, motionless, catching what little light bled through the cloud cover and throwing it back down like a ceiling of dirty lace. We walked beneath it the way you'd walk through a cathedral — carefully, loudly aware of being small.

Maren stopped us at the old line. You could still read it, if you knew how to look: a seam in the char where the burning stopped, running north-south as far as the eye could follow. On one side, the wreckage of men. On the other side, the wreckage of... something else. The fae dead hadn't left bodies, exactly. They'd left *impressions* — hollows in the fused ground shaped like whatever they'd been wearing when they fell, outlines without substance, like the world had kept the shape of them and let the rest go.

Sebastian stood at the seam and stared south. Then north. Then south again.

"That's him," he said. His voice did something strange — went flat, careful, the voice of a man reading coordinates off a chart he doesn't want to be true. "Both sides. He's on both sides."

"Who's on both sides?" Maren asked, because she's direct to the point of rudeness and I love her for it.

"The lights," Sebastian said. "My ancestor. He's standing on both sides of the line."

And then the lights came.

* * *

I want to describe the ghost-lights properly, because everything you've heard is wrong. They aren't wisps. They aren't will-o'-the-wisps leading travelers astray — the Reach doesn't bother luring anyone; it already has everything it wants. What rises out of that char when the light goes low is a *replay*. The final hour of the final battle, worn into the world like a groove in a record, playing on loop for two centuries for an audience of nobody.

They come up out of the ground as points of cold gold, dozens of them, hundreds, each one the size of a candle flame, and each one unfolds into a figure. Soldiers. Riders. Banners of light that ripple with no wind. They don't glow so much as *insist* — and the sound, gods, the sound. Not voices. Echoes of voices, the way a shell holds the sea. Cries, orders, prayers, all of it flattened and layered until it stops being language and becomes pressure. You feel it in your teeth. In the backs of your eyes.

Pellam dropped to his knees immediately, which surprised nobody; the man treats every primary source like scripture and every scripture like a trap. Maren drew her sword, which surprised everybody, because a sword is precisely the wrong tool for a memory, and she knew it, and drew it anyway. Habit. Grief. Take your pick.

I stood still. Standing still is my whole art. Smugglers who move when the danger moves die; smugglers who wait to see what the danger *wants* retire wealthy and paranoid, and I intend to do both.

And Sebastian — Sebastian walked forward. Off the rope. Straight into the replay, like a man stepping into a river he'd only ever seen painted.

"Brightwater—" Maren started after him and I caught her arm, because I'd seen something she hadn't.

The figures were turning. All of them. Every soldier of light on both sides of that ancient seam, every rider, every banner-bearer, rotating slowly to face one point in the present: a half-frozen cartographer with ink-stained fingers and the worst luck in two worlds.

Not attacking. Facing. The way a courtroom faces a witness.

"They know him," I said. "Or they know what he is. Or they know what he *carries*." Because he was carrying the book — of course he was, the man sleeps with the thing like a teddy bear with a body count — and the book was doing something I'd never seen it do. Its pages were glowing faintly through the leather, pulsing, in time with the lights. Call and response. Question and answer.

Sebastian stood in the middle of the field, arms slightly out from his sides like a man balancing on ice, and the battle played through him. I watched a rider of light pass clean through his chest and keep riding. I watched him flinch anyway. Memory doesn't need flesh to hurt you; it just needs you standing in the wrong place at the wrong angle to eternity.

And then the figures resolved enough — for maybe ten heartbeats — for me to see what he saw.

There was a man on the southern line. Tall, dark-haired, dressed in a surveyor's coat rather than armor, holding something flat and rectangular that caught the light wrong — a chart, a board, a frame of some kind. And there was a man on the northern line, among the fae, tall, dark-haired, dressed in a coat woven of something that wasn't cloth, holding something flat and rectangular that caught the light wrong in exactly the same way.

Same face. Same hands. Same terrible, deliberate stillness — while armies broke around him, he stood at both seams and *worked*, drawing, measuring, closing something, opening something, and the soldiers died around him like weather around a stone.

"He was trying to end it," Sebastian said. I could barely hear him. The pressure of all those unfinished voices had a way of flattening your own. "Both sides. He was — he's drawing on both sides of the line. He's not fighting for either of them. He's fighting *the fight itself*."

"Or," Pellam said, from his knees, in the voice of a man who has spent thirty years learning to distrust his own conclusions, "he was sealing something *in*, and both armies were in the way."

Nobody wanted to hear that. Everybody heard it.

Because here's the arithmetic that had been sitting in my gut since the strait, since Pellam went quiet over his notes, since the book started glowing: if the first Brightwater sealed the Veil to imprison something — and chapter thirteen of this miserable expedition is going to confirm it, I'd bet my ship on it, and I have never lost a bet I couldn't cheat — then the thing he sealed it *around* is still down there. Under the char. Under the hovering snow. Under two hundred years of everyone agreeing not to look.

And the Veil is tearing. We've all felt it — the winter coming early, the sky going wrong at noon, the prophecies updating themselves like a ledger someone's cooking. Whatever the first Brightwater locked away has been patient for two centuries, and patience, in my professional experience, is just appetite wearing a nicer coat.

History wasn't repeating. History was *resuming*. It had only ever been paused.

* * *

The replay ran its course — it always runs the same course, Pellam told me later, he timed it, eleven minutes and change, the poor bastard had sat through it more times than any sane archivist should — and the lights sank back into the char one by one, like coins returning to a pocket. The last figure to fade was the southern one. The surveyor. He faded facing north, mid-gesture, pen lifted, the line unfinished.

Sebastian came back to us shaking. Not cold-shaking. Something deeper, structural, the tremor of a man whose family tree just grew roots on both sides of a graveyard.

"My blood started this," he said. Or tried to say. What actually came out was quieter and worse: "My blood is *on* this. Both sides of it. Everyone who died here died around a Brightwater doing what I'm about to do."

Maren put her hand on his shoulder — awkwardly, the way she does everything tender, like she's armoring it first. "Then you're the only one alive who can undo it properly," she said. "You inherit the mistake. You also inherit the pen."

I liked that. I liked it so much I almost forgave her for the sword-drawing.

Pellam, meanwhile, had gone very still, staring at a patch of char near the seam, and when I followed his gaze I understood why, and wished I hadn't. There were marks in the burn-glass. Deliberate marks. Lines, radiating outward in a pattern no explosion makes and no erosion keeps — a pattern, drawn into the ground itself, the way you'd draw a ward. Or a lock. Or, if you were feeling honest, a *cage door*, with hinges on the inside.

And at the center of the pattern, fresh — hours old, maybe less, the edges still sharp where everything else in this place was worn smooth — someone had scratched a new mark. Small. Almost polite. A single symbol I didn't recognize, pressed into the glass like a signature.

Pellam did recognize it. I watched his face do the thing faces do when a man realizes the answer was worse than the question.

"That's Dreville's seal," he said. "The Lord Chancellor's private mark. It's on every warrant he's signed for twenty years." He looked up, and his voice cracked down the middle. "Someone from the citadel has been *here*. Recently. Feeding it, or measuring it, or waking it — I don't know which. But the Reach isn't abandoned anymore, Wren. It's *attended*."

The hovering snow above us shifted. Just a little. Just enough that a few flakes finally, after two hundred years, began to fall.

I've sailed past omens my whole life. I've ignored curses, laughed at portents, once told a storm to its face that it wasn't worth my cargo. But when that snow started coming down — finishing its sentence at last, after two centuries of hanging unfinished — I turned to my crew and said the only honest thing left in me:

"We leave. Now. And when we get back to the ship, somebody tell me exactly what's under our feet — because I am not sailing another league blind, and neither are any of you."

Sebastian looked down at the char, at the cage-door pattern, at the fresh scratch of Dreville's seal sitting in the middle of it like a hand resting on a latch.

"I think," he said slowly, "it's time I read the margins."

The snow kept falling. For the first time in two hundred years, the Reach was letting go of something.

I intended to be far out on the water before it finished.
