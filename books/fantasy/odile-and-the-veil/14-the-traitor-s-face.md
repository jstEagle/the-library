## 14. The Traitor's Face

I have been betrayed by better men than Lord Chancellor Dreville. That is not a compliment to Dreville — the competition was thin — but it does mean I know what betrayal smells like before I can name it: ink and beeswax and someone being very careful to be kind.

He was kind to us in the map room of Vharen's citadel. That should have been my first warning. The second should have been that his kindness came with tea.

"Ser Hale." He poured for me himself, which no lord had done since my order dissolved. "You look well. Prison agrees with some people."

"I wasn't imprisoned," I said. "Detained. There's a difference. One has paperwork."

"And yet here you are, walking free beside the Cartographer of Ends, when by every law still on the books you should be sweeping snow off a garrison step in Kellsmoor." He smiled the way a man smiles when he's counting exits. "The queen forgives easily. It's one of her more expensive virtues."

Sebastian sat across from him looking like a scholar who'd wandered into the wrong lecture and was too polite to leave. Pellam Oxley stood by the window doing what Pellam always did — pretending not to listen while memorizing everything. And Wren, thank every god I don't believe in, was not in the citadel at all. She'd refused the summons flatly, saying citadels were where smart smugglers went to become dead smugglers. At the time I thought it was cowardice dressed as cunning.

It was the only reason any of us survived the week.

Dreville asked gentle questions. Where had we found the book. What had the ghost-lights shown us in the Burnt Reach. Had Sebastian experienced any — he searched for the word — *communion* with the thing beneath the Veil. Each question landed soft as a feather and stuck like a hook. And each time, Sebastian answered honestly, because honesty was the one weapon he owned and didn't know how dangerous it was.

"The marginalia mentions feeding it," Sebastian said at last, carefully. "Someone kept it fed so it would sleep. Someone with access. Someone whose family—"

"Yes," Dreville said pleasantly, "the Brightwaters. Terrible business. Your ancestor nearly unmade two worlds, young man. It's fortunate the crown has such a firm hand on your leash."

And there it was. The pivot. He wanted the guilt pinned to Sebastian's name alone, wanted the story to end at *a Brightwater did this*, because any story that went deeper would eventually reach a family called Dreville.

Pellam caught my eye across the room. Pellam, who had spent thirty years reading banned histories and could smell an edited record from across a library. His expression didn't change. His thumb moved, once, against his forefinger. We'd developed signals over bad stew and worse roads. That one meant: *he's lying about something specific.*

I thanked the Chancellor for the tea. My hands were steady. I'd learned long ago that steadiness costs nothing if you practice it while terrified.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep in the guest quarters they'd given us, which were comfortable, which was wrong. The citadel had put us in rooms with thick doors and thinner walls and guards who changed shift at odd hours. I'd been a soldier twenty years. I knew what a cell looked like even when it had fresh linens and a view of the harbor.

So I did what I always do with a sleepless night. I walked it.

The citadel of Vharen at midnight is a cathedral to its own paranoia. Frost-guard patrol in pairs, lanterns hooded, boots wrapped in felt so they make no sound — a courtesy extended to everyone except whoever they're sneaking up on. I counted the patrols twice and both times the count came back wrong: one pair too many near the east stair, one pair missing from the armory corridor. Somebody had rearranged the guard rotation, and they hadn't done it to protect us.

I found Pellam awake in the archive annex, of course, sitting in a nest of candle stubs with three books open around him like a man building a fort out of paper.

"Tell me you're finding something useful," I said.

"Tell me why you're whispering in your own quarters' hallway first."

"Because I think we're guests the way rats are guests in a granary. Present until inconvenient."

He pushed a book toward me without ceremony. "House records. Genealogies. Boring beyond endurance, which is why nobody burns them." His finger stopped on a page. "Dreville's line goes back further than the crown does. Before Isolde's grandmother. Before the war, even. Do you know how rare that is? Almost nothing in Odile predates the Veil. Except—" he tapped the page, "—families who were already old when the Veil was drawn. Families who helped draw it."

"Families who helped build a prison," I said slowly, "usually want keys."

"Precisely. And look." He turned the page. In the margin, in faded brown ink, someone had written a single line, over and over down the years like a prayer or an accounting ledger: *the tithe is paid.* Dates followed. Every generation. The most recent date was eleven days ago.

"Tithe of what?" I asked.

Pellam looked up at me with the particular misery of a man who has read enough history to know exactly what people feed to prisons. "Not what," he said. "Who."

* * *

We woke Sebastian. He took it badly, then took it well, which is his pattern — horror first, cartography second. By the time the candles burned low he'd spread the ancestor's charts across Pellam's table and was tracing the anchor points with a fingertip.

"If Dreville's family held a key to the Veil," he said, "and they've been paying a tithe to keep the thing asleep, then the winter deepening isn't the Veil failing. It's the Veil being *worked*. Deliberately. From this side." He looked up. "He doesn't want the winter to end. He wants it deep enough that the queen has no choice but to use me — and whatever I do, he wins. If I open the Veil, the thing comes out. If I fail, the winter kills Odile and the fae realms blame the crown, and in the chaos, a very old family with a very old key walks through a hole in the world and takes everything."

"He's not a traitor to Odile," Pellam said quietly. "He's a traitor to *both sides*. That's rarer. Takes real commitment."

I was thinking less like a historian. "Then he can't let us leave Vharen knowing this. And he can't let us stay, either, once we've read his own family records. Which means—"

"Which means," Pellam finished, "we've been having our midnight conversations on borrowed time."

The knock came at dawn.

Three knocks. Even spacing. A frost-guard officer's knock — I'd heard it ten thousand times — but the guard rotations had changed, and the officer who entered wore a captain's insignia I didn't recognize and a face I'd never seen on any wall of the citadel. Behind him, four more filled the doorway, and their breath fogged strangely, too cold for the corridor, cold like something leaking.

"Ser Hale. Master Oxley. Master Brightwater." The captain's voice was pleasant. Everything in Vharen was pleasant lately. "The Chancellor requests your presence. Immediately."

"Now?" Sebastian asked. "It's barely light—"

"*Immediately,*" the captain repeated, and the frost on the window behind him grew a finger's width toward the glass center, like frost reaching for warmth.

I've fought in two border wars and one civil unpleasantness, and I will tell you the truth about ambushes: you almost never get certainty. You get a taste in your mouth and a count in your head. Five soldiers for three unarmed civilians was not an escort. It was arithmetic.

"We'll come," I said brightly. "Let me just fetch my sword."

"You won't need—"

"Humor me. I'm a disgraced knight. The sword is basically jewelry at this point."

He hesitated half a heartbeat, and half a heartbeat is a country mile when you know what you're listening for. "Leave it," he said, and the pleasantness dropped away like a mask slipping its peg, and behind him the four soldiers drew steel in perfect silence.

I threw the brazier.

Not at anyone — at the floor between us, scattering coals across the rushes, and the room filled with smoke and shouting, and I got Sebastian by the collar and Pellam by the sleeve and we went through the servant door behind the tapestry, which Pellam had mapped days ago because Pellam maps everything, God bless the heretical little magpie.

* * *

What follows, I remember in pieces, the way you remember any fight you survive: sound first, then blood, then the strange calm arithmetic of motion.

Servant corridors. Narrow stone throats in the citadel's belly. Boots behind us — six sets now, they'd called more — and ahead of us the dark, and Sebastian gasping questions and me answering with my hand on his neck, pushing him lower, faster.

"They're killing us," he kept saying, not as a question, just stating it to hear how it sounded. "They're actually—"

"Yes," I said. "Breathe later."

We came up through a laundry chute into a courtyard where the snow lay untouched, and for one beautiful stupid moment I thought we'd made it. Then the frost-guard stepped out from behind the woodshed, two of them, and one raised his blade, and I had no sword, so I gave him the only inheritance my order ever left me: I hit him the way Ser Maren Hale was trained to hit people, once, precisely, in the temple, and he folded like wet parchment. The other one I took his sword from, mid-swing, the old lesson, *the blade is never his until he's used it* — and then I had steel in my hand for the first time since my disgrace, and I want to tell you I felt nothing. I want to tell you it was duty.

It wasn't. It was joy, ugly and hot, and I hated myself for it even as I used it.

More boots in the corridor behind. A shout: *"Alive! The Chancellor wants the Cartographer alive!"* — and that saved Sebastian's life and doomed the rest of the plan, because it meant they'd herd rather than cut, and herding leaves gaps.

"The wall," I said. "Northeast corner. There's a drainage grate; Wren showed me—"

"Wren's not here."

"Wren showed *me*, then. Move."

We went over the wall the way smugglers go over walls, gracelessly, with much swearing and one torn palm, and dropped into the frozen canal road below. Behind us the citadel's bells began to ring — not the alarm pattern, something older, three slow tolls, over and over. Pellam, wheezing against the ice, looked up at the sound and went white.

"That's not the alarm," he said. "That's the *summons*. They're calling the full guard out. They're telling the whole city the Cartographer has fled." He laughed, hollow. "By morning there'll be a bounty. By noon, a religion. Fleeing was the confession, Maren. He wrote our guilt before we committed the crime."

"Then we make sure the crime's worth the sentence," I said, and hauled them both up, and we ran.

* * *

We ran through Vharen's sleeping streets with bells tolling behind us, and the city, which had seemed merely cold all week, now felt complicit — every shuttered window a closed eye, every snow-covered lane a page waiting for our names. Twice we heard boots. Once we saw a patrol cross an intersection ahead of us and pressed ourselves into a shrine alcove, shoulder to shoulder in the dark, Sebastian shaking, Pellam murmuring citations under his breath like a rosary, me holding my stolen sword and feeling the whole rotten shape of my life finally make sense.

This was what I'd been training for all along, apparently. Not honor. Not the Order of the Winter Vigil, with its banners and its vows and its beautiful, fatal certainty. This: getting two frightened scholars out of a burning house while the house pretends nothing's wrong.

I'd spent five years ashamed of the night I broke my oath. Standing in that shrine with a price going up on my head, I understood it differently. Oaths are maps, and maps are drawn by someone, and I had spent my whole life forgetting to ask *who drew this, and what did they leave out?*

Dreville had left out a tithe. The Order had left out the war's true cause. Isolde had left out her execution contingency. Everyone draws lines and calls them borders and forgets that lines are choices.

Well. I was done living inside other people's lines.

When the patrol passed, we moved again, down to the river quays where the fishing smacks sat locked in ice like flies in amber, and there — moored in the shadow of the breakwater, riding low and dark, flying no colors at all — waited a boat that absolutely should not have been there.

A figure leaned on the rail, half-fae ears catching the bell-light, arms crossed, expression furious.

"You absolute idiots," Wren Ashvale called softly. "I told you citadels were death. I told you. I *charged* you fair passage to avoid exactly this."

"How did you—" Sebastian started.

"I bribed a scullery maid, same as I do everything. Now get aboard before the bells finish waking the harbor, because in about four minutes every gate out of this city closes, and I am *not* spending midwinter frozen into a quay listening to you two explain genealogy." She looked past him, to me, and her fury shifted into something sharper. Something almost like respect. "Hale. You got them out alive."

"Barely."

"Barely counts." She tossed me a rope. "Welcome to the crew. Try not to get us killed twice in one week."

As the boat slid out onto the black water, the citadel's bells still tolling their lying summons, I watched Vharen shrink behind us — its towers, its frost, its pleasant chancellor with his pleasant tea — and I felt the fear in my chest change shape. Fear is a liquid. Given heat, given purpose, it boils.

Dreville had made one mistake, and it wasn't the assassins. It was letting me see his face while he lied, so that I'd know it forever, the way you know the face of the man who killed your brothers.

By midwinter, I promised the shrinking city, he would know mine.
