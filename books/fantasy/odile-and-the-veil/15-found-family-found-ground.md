## 15. Found Family, Found Ground

There are three rules to hiding in the smugglers' coves of Odile's northern coast: don't light a fire anyone can see from the water, don't sing above a whisper after dark, and never, under any circumstances, trust a man who brings prophecy into your home.

I broke all three that night.

The first because it was midwinter-come-early and cold enough to crack teeth; the second because Pellam Oxley had a voice like a landslide and no sense whatsoever about when to use it; and the third because Sebastian Brightwater sat at my driftwood fire looking like a drowned librarian who'd been told he was the hinge of history, and I couldn't find it in me to throw him back out onto the ice.

Which, for the record, I had tried. Twice. Maren had caught me by the collar both times.

"Your cave smells like fish," she'd said, which was true, and unfair, since her knightly armor smelled like old blood and worse decisions, but that's found family for you — nobody gets to be clean.

The cove itself was an old secret, older than me, which is saying something for a woman whose mother was fae and whose father was a liar. A fold in the cliffs south of the Burnt Reach, hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall that only opened for people who knew to knock twice and insult the ice on the way through. My mother taught me that much before she went back across the strait and left me with a father who thought smuggling was a moral calling. The cove held three boats under canvas, a cache of brandy I pretended didn't exist, and enough dried cod to feed a garrison through a siege nobody wanted to fight.

It also held four exiles, one map, and the last warm night any of us would get for a long time.

* * *

The planning started the way all my plans start: with an argument.

"You want to sail," Maren said. She said it the way she says everything now, flat and careful, like a woman handling broken glass she used to own. "Across the frozen strait. In midwinter."

"Not sail." I tapped the chart spread over a crate of salted herring. "Sail is what we'd do if I were stupid. We *ice-run*. The strait freezes solid this time of year — well. It used to freeze predictably. Now it freezes whenever it feels like it, which is its own problem, but the point stands. There's a road on the ice. Old smugglers' road. Marked by stakes every half mile, black iron, topped with glass."

"Why glass?" Sebastian asked.

He asked that about everything. Why glass, why stakes, why did Wren just put away the knife. I was starting to suspect it wasn't naivety. I was starting to suspect it was a cartographer's disease — the need to know what everything was made of, in case you had to draw it later.

"Because the fae put them there," I said. "Back before the war, when the strait was neutral water. Glass holds a light without burning. You tap a stake with something iron, the light wakes up, shows you the next stake. Follow the lights, stay off the thin patches where the currents run underneath, and you cross in two days instead of dying in twenty minutes."

"And if the lights lead you wrong?" Pellam asked. He was sharpening a letter knife on a whetstone while he talked, which is the sort of thing archivists do when they've been banned from universities and have to make their own entertainment.

"They won't. The fae keep their roads honest. It's everything *off* the road you have to worry about." I pulled the chart closer and drew a line with charcoal, from the cove north and east, out along the strait to a place I marked with a circle and hated marking. "Here. Under the ice shelf below the Veil's anchor. That's where Dreville's been feeding it, if Sebastian's margins are right. That's where we're going."

Silence, the good kind and the bad kind mixed together, like brandy in snowmelt.

Sebastian stared at the circle. He'd been staring at things a lot lately — his ancestor's charts, the prophecy book with its new lines, his own hands, as though they might start drawing on their own. Maybe they would. I'd seen stranger in the coves.

"The anchor," he said slowly, "is beneath the Veil itself. Which means the ice above it is—" He stopped.

"Cursed? Haunted? Riddled with holes into somewhere worse?" I shrugged. "Probably all three. But here's the arithmetic, Brightwater, and I want you to follow it, because everyone else in this room already has and I'm tired of being the only pessimist. Midwinter came early. The sky went black at noon. The Veil is tearing, Dreville has the citadel, and every day we sit in this lovely cave eating my cod is a day that thing under the ice eats better. We go now, or we don't go at all."

"We could still not go," Pellam offered. "Statistically speaking, hiding has an excellent survival rate."

"Hiding has an excellent survival rate right up until the world ends," Maren said. "Then it's just dying slower."

Pellam considered this. "You've clearly given the matter more thought than most philosophers."

"I was a soldier. Philosophy is what soldiers do instead of sleeping."

And there it was again, the thing I kept noticing about the three of them and refusing to name: the way their edges fit. Maren's grimness and Pellam's levity, grinding against each other like flint and steel. Sebastian in the middle, quiet, taking it all in, occasionally producing one sentence that reorganized the entire conversation. I'd spent thirty years running crews where the only bond was profit and the only loyalty was fear, and I knew exactly what those were worth. This was neither. This was something I didn't have a chart for.

I hated it. I was keeping it.

* * *

We argued the route until the candles burned down to puddles. Two days on the ice road, then a climb down through a sea cave Dreville's frost-guard didn't watch because they didn't know it existed — I'd found it running contraband years ago, and the knowledge had cost me a partner and most of a leg's worth of frostbite, so I felt entitled to it. Then the anchor chamber, whatever it turned out to be, with whatever Sebastian intended to do once we got him there.

"What do you intend to do once we get you there?" I finally asked, because nobody else had the nerve.

He looked up from the old charts, his great-great-whatever-grandmother's charts, covered in marginalia in handwriting that matched his own close enough to be unsettling. "I don't know yet."

"Wonderful. Inspiring. I'll carve it on our headstones: *He Didn't Know Yet.*"

"Wren." Maren's voice had the edge she used when she was serious, which I'd learned to distinguish from the edge she used when she was annoyed mostly by how tired it sounded. "Let him work."

"He's not working. He's staring."

"Staring *is* how cartographers work," Sebastian said, and for the first time in three days there was something almost like humor in it. "You should see how long it takes me to draw a coastline. First I stare at it for a week."

"A week," I repeated. "The winter ends in six days."

"Then I'll stare faster."

Pellam laughed — a real laugh, sudden and loud, the kind that echoed off the cave walls and probably alerted every seal within a mile. And Sebastian smiled, small and startled, like a man surprised to find the muscle still worked. And Maren shook her head, but she was fighting the corner of her mouth, and losing.

I fed the fire another piece of driftwood and decided that if we were all going to die on the ice, at least we'd die having laughed once. That was more than most crews got.

* * *

Later, when Pellam had fallen asleep sitting up against the boat hull with an open book sliding off his knee — archivists sleep the way they read, anywhere, instantly, and wake up offended — and Maren stood the first watch at the cave mouth with her sword across her knees, it was just me and Sebastian and the fire.

He was copying the marginalia. Translating it, really — some of it was in a script even Pellam needed a reference for — onto fresh paper, line by line, his pen moving quick and certain. Watching him work was like watching someone pray, if prayer involved more ink.

"Ask," he said, without looking up.

"I wasn't going to."

"You've been circling it all evening. You do a thing with your jaw when you're deciding whether to say something. Like you're chewing the sentence first."

"That's slander."

"It's observation. I'm a cartographer. Noticing where things are is the entire profession." He set the pen down. "So ask."

I threw a splinter of driftwood into the flames and watched it catch. "Fine. Your grandmother — great-great, whichever — she drew the Veil shut. On purpose. To protect both sides from the thing they built together. She gave up her whole life to do it, sealed herself into the work, whatever the exact shape of it was. And now you're going to stand in front of that thing and try to do *something*, and you don't know what, and you might die, or worse, you might succeed and die anyway, because the prophecy book apparently writes deaths now." I looked at him. "Doesn't any part of you want to run?"

"Yes," he said immediately. "All of me wants to run. Constantly. Running is my primary plan and has been since the night the border flickered at me." He turned the page. "But my grandmother ran the other direction, and I keep thinking — someone in my blood chose this once. Chose to hold a door closed with her whole self. And I've spent my entire life mapping places that don't exist because the place that did exist never quite fit me. So maybe this is the first real map I've ever been handed. And maybe you don't run from the one job you were actually drawn for."

I let that sit in the fire-smoke for a while.

"My mother went back across the strait when I was nine," I said. I hadn't planned to say it. It came out anyway, the way truth does around a fire — too warm to hold. "Fae side. Said she'd come back when the seasons sorted themselves out. Fae don't lie, exactly, but they promise in tenses that don't exist, and 'when' turned out to mean never. My father drank himself to death telling everyone she'd return. So I learned the coves, learned the roads, learned to charge everyone for everything and trust nothing that breathes." I nodded at the other two, asleep and watching, exile and exile. "And then these three idiots fell into my boat, and now I'm planning a suicide run for free, and the worst part is it doesn't even feel strange anymore."

"No," Sebastian said quietly. "It doesn't, does it."

"Don't make it a moment, Brightwater. I'll charge you."

"Too late. It's already a moment. I'm putting it in a book."

"There's no book."

"There will be. Someone should write all this down. That's rather the family disease." He picked the pen back up, then paused. "Wren. Whatever happens under the ice — if it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong. I don't allow it."

"—if it goes wrong, I want you to know that the toll you charged me for passage over the strait was the fairest price anyone's ever asked of me."

I snorted, because my throat had gone tight and laughing was cheaper than whatever the alternative was. "That's because everyone else overpays. Merchant habit. Don't read depth into it."

But we both knew I was lying, and we both knew the other knew, and neither of us said anything, because that's the truce that holds a crew together better than oaths do: the agreement not to name the thing that's holding you.

Maren relieved herself of the watch an hour past midnight and woke Pellam for his shift by dropping a piece of dried cod on his face. He woke up mid-sentence, which apparently was normal for him. The fire burned low and gold. Outside the frozen waterfall, the wind screamed down off the glacier like something grieving, and inside, for one hour, one pocket of stolen warmth, four people who had lost everything sat close together and were, briefly, unlost.

I named it then, privately, in the ledger I keep behind my ribs where I track debts. Not allies. Allies are hired. Crew is bought with coin and kept with fear. This was the third thing, the one I'd watched other people have through tavern windows my whole life and told myself I didn't want.

Warmth, in Odile, is defiance. Everyone knows that. The winter takes everything soft eventually — that's its whole nature, its whole argument, the reason the kingdom had knelt under it for a century. Light a fire and you're arguing with the end of the world. Sit around it with people you'd bleed for, and you're winning the argument, at least for tonight.

Midwinter was coming. Let it come. It would find us lit.

* * *

It was near dawn when the trouble came, because trouble keeps honest hours if nothing else.

Pellam's shout brought us all up out of sleep — mine shallow, Maren's instant, Sebastian's slow and confused, hair sticking up like a startled hedgehog. The archivist was standing at the mouth of the cave with the prophecy book open in both hands, and the look on his face was one I'd seen once before, on a man reading his own death warrant.

"It's changed again," he said. "New lines. Written while we slept." His voice shook. "It names the route. Our route. The ice road, the sea cave — it knows where we're going, Wren. Someone's written us into it."

Maren was already buckling her sword. Sebastian had gone white as the glacier outside. And I stood there with the fire at my back doing the arithmetic I always do, odds and exits and prices, and came up with the same answer three times:

Somebody was feeding the book. Somebody who knew our plan.

And there was exactly one person alive who knew it whole.
