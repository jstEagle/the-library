## 16. Midwinter Falls

I had a theory about midwinter. Most of my theories were wrong — that's practically the job description of a cartographer working from rumor instead of survey — but this one I'd defended in front of Pellam Oxley himself: that midwinter would arrive on midwinter, because even prophecies kept office hours.

It arrived nine days early, at noon, while I was eating soup.

I want it noted for the record that the last ordinary thing I ever did was eat soup. Lentil. Slightly over-salted. Wren had smuggled us into a waystation inn three miles south of Vharen, and Maren was complaining about the bread, and Pellam was annotating a banned history with comments so heretical he kept them written backward, like Leonardo, or a man who expected his notebooks to be confiscated.

The light went out of the sky the way a candle goes out when someone pinches it — not gradually, not like dusk, but all at once, with a little smoke-wisp of afterimage hanging over the snowfields. The soup in my bowl went black. Not shadowed. Black, as if the lentils had been replaced by holes.

"Sebastian," Maren said quietly. She'd stopped chewing. That was how I knew it was bad; Maren considered stopping mid-meal a moral failing.

"I see it," I said.

"Noon," Pellam said, and there was something in his voice I'd never heard before, which frightened me more than the dark did, because Pellam was the sort of man who found wonder in plague ledgers. "It isn't noon yet by any reckoning I know. It's noon by *the book's* reckoning."

The prophecy book sat in my pack under the bench. I hadn't opened it since the traitor's name had appeared in fresh ink two weeks ago — Dreville, Lord Chancellor, feeder-of-the-thing-beneath — and I'd been sleeping badly ever since, which for me is a competitive sport. Now the pack was warm. I could feel it through the canvas and through the bench, warm the way a feverish child is warm, and I knew without looking that the pages were glowing.

"Don't," Maren said.

"It's going to open itself if I don't," I said, and hauled the pack up, and I was right, which is the worst kind of right to be.

The book fell open across my knees. The ink was moving — not smearing, not bleeding, but *traveling*, lines rearranging themselves like ants whose hill has been kicked over. And where the old text had described my death at midwinter in language elegant enough to be almost flattering, new lines were pushing up between them, growing the way frost grows on glass:

*When the sun abandons the noon hour,*
*the Veil shall remember it is a wound.*
*What was bound beneath shall press upon the seam.*
*And the Cartographer of Ends shall stand*
*where the first map was drawn, or all maps end.*

"Well," I said. "That's ominous."

"Read it again," Pellam said.

"*Where the first map was drawn.*" My mouth had gone dry. "Pellam, where was the first map drawn?"

He looked at me with an expression I can only describe as scholarly apology. "Vharen. The citadel stands on it. They built the throne room directly above the anchor stone. Every coronation for six hundred years has been a king or queen standing on the lock of the world's cage, swearing oaths they didn't know they were swearing."

Of course they had. Of course. Power always builds on top of the thing it's afraid of and calls the fear tradition.

Outside, the snow had stopped falling. That was the detail that undid me — not the dark, not the cold pressing against the shutters like a palm laid flat against glass, but the snow hanging motionless in the air, ten thousand flakes suspended at every height, waiting. Odile's snow had been the one constant since I fell through the Veil. It had fallen the day I arrived, matching the prophecy word for word, and I had hated it and leaned on it in equal measure, the way you lean on a wall you also resent.

Now it hung there, undecided.

And then, far off to the north — so far I shouldn't have been able to see anything at all, except that everything was lit now by a glow coming *up out of the ground*, a bruise-colored light seeping along the seams of the world — the sky tore.

I don't mean clouds parted. I mean the sky, the actual fabric of it, split along a line running north to south over Vharen, and through the tear came a color that has no name in any language I speak, though I suspect the fae have four names for it and none of them are polite. The tear wasn't large. From three miles away it looked like a rip in a letter.

It was the largest thing I have ever seen.

"The Veil," Pellam whispered, "is beginning to come apart."

* * *

We rode for Vharen because we had no other choice, and because Wren said, in the tone she reserved for things she intended to do regardless of our opinions, "If that thing gets loose, every smuggler route on the strait dies, and I am not starting my ledger over." Sentiment, from Wren Ashvale, always arrived disguised as accounting.

The roads were wrong. Villages we passed stood with their doors open and their hearths cold, and not abandoned — *paused*. A man halfway through chopping wood stood with the axe raised, snowflakes unmoving around him like flies in amber. A girl chasing a dog had one foot in the air. Everything inside the shadow of the tear had stopped, and everything outside it — us, our horses, the glow — moved too fast, too loud, as if we'd been wound up tight to compensate.

"They're not dead," Pellam said, riding close beside me. "I think they're being held. Saved, maybe. Or stored."

"That's worse."

"Yes," Pellam agreed cheerfully. "Most true things are."

Maren rode point with her sword loosened in its sheath, which for Maren was the equivalent of screaming. She'd been quiet since Dreville's assassins nearly took her head off in the archive stairwell — quieter than usual, and Maren's usual was already a silence you could store grain in. But when we crested the last hill before Vharen and saw what the citadel had become, she spoke.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, no."

The citadel of Vharen was burning with cold. Frost climbed its towers in patterns too regular to be weather — spirals, sigils, the same geometry I'd spent weeks finding in my ancestor's charts, the geometry of the Veil itself. And on the walls, where the queen's banners should have flown, hung banners I didn't recognize: grey silk worked with a design like a closed eye.

"Dreville's men?" I asked.

"Dreville's *house*," Maren said. "That's the arms of the Veyl family. Old money. Older bargains. His grandmother signed something under that eye, back when the crown still pretended the Veil was natural." Her jaw tightened. "He's taken the citadel. Where's Isolde?"

No one answered, because none of us knew, and because the gates of Vharen opened ahead of us — opened for us, smoothly, welcomingly, which told me everything I needed to know about whose invitation we were walking into.

"He wants us here," I said.

"Obviously," said Wren.

"He knows the prophecy says I have to be at the anchor. He doesn't want to stop me. He wants to *use* me. I'm the key. Why break down a door when you can wait for it to open itself?"

Maren turned in her saddle and looked at me, and for once there was nothing guarded in her face at all. "Then we go in knowing it's a trap," she said, "and we make the trap expensive."

I've thought about that sentence a great deal since. If I ever write a history of these days instead of just surviving them, that's the line I'll carve over the gate: *we make the trap expensive.*

* * *

Inside the walls, Vharen had become a place I didn't recognize, which is a strange thing to say about a city I'd only seen twice, but cities have manners, and this one had lost all of its. The streets were patrolled by soldiers in grey, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who believe the fighting is over. Civilians watched from windows with expressions I knew too well — the expression of people doing arithmetic about whether obedience might keep them alive.

At the citadel doors, a herald in the closed-eye livery read our names from a scroll. All four of us. Our *true* names — including Wren's, both halves of it, the fae half she never used, and I watched something flicker behind her eyes at hearing it spoken aloud by a stranger.

"The Lord Chancellor bids you welcome," the herald said, "and reminds the Cartographer that midwinter has come early. The throne room awaits."

"Doesn't he mean 'dungeon'?" Wren asked sweetly.

"The Chancellor wishes to avoid unpleasantness."

"Then he's picked the wrong century," Maren said, and walked in first, because that was Maren all over: she would walk into hell, but she'd insist on going through the door before anyone could shut it behind her.

The throne room of Vharen sits atop the anchor stone, and I felt it the moment I crossed the threshold — a hum through the floor, through my boot-soles, through my teeth, like standing on the body of something enormous and asleep that was no longer entirely asleep. The bruise-light came up between the flagstones in thin veins, mapping the floor in the same spirals as the frost on the towers, and at the center, where the throne should have been—

There was no throne. There was a hole. A perfect circle cut out of the floor, perhaps ten feet across, ringed in carved script older than the kingdom, and down in the dark of it something moved with the slow patience of deep water. The Veil's anchor. The lock. The place where the first Brightwater had knelt with pen and blood and drawn the door shut.

Dreville stood beside it.

He was not what I expected, though I couldn't have told you what I'd expected — monsters usually aren't. He was a tidy man. Grey-haired, grey-eyed, dressed in grey that matched his banners, with the mild, patient face of a librarian or a poisoner, and he greeted us the way a host greets guests who have arrived late to dinner.

"Sebastian Brightwater," he said. My surname in his mouth sounded like a verdict being read. "Descendant of Aldous Brightwater, who sealed the debt of both worlds into the dark and called it peace. Do you know what your ancestor stole?"

"A lot of people have asked me questions lately," I said. "Yours is the first I actually want to answer."

"Good." He smiled. It was the worst thing I'd seen all day, and I had seen the sky rip open. "Because the answer is *everything*. Your family's map didn't imprison a monster, boy. It imprisoned a bargain. Both sides made it together — men and fae, crown and court — and both sides have been starving ever since, feeding the winter instead of each other. My family has kept it fed. Quietly. Faithfully. For generations. And now the book says you're the one who ends it." He spread his hands, reasonable as a magistrate. "So let's not pretend you have choices. You will stand at the anchor tonight, and you will unmake the seal, and what your family buried will rise, and the war your bloodline started will finally finish — *won* this time, properly, on both fronts — and the long winter will break, because winter is only the seal's shadow. Open the cage, and the seasons return."

"And if I refuse?" I asked, though the floor's hum was rising through my bones and I suspected refusal had already been removed from the menu.

Dreville gestured, gently, toward the tall windows.

Beyond the glass, the frozen city lay under the torn sky. And along the streets, in the motionless air, the paused people had begun to move — not freely, not alive, but dragged, puppet-smooth, converging on the citadel by the hundreds, faces tilted up toward the hole in the sky like flowers toward a sun that hated them.

"The seal is failing either way," Dreville said. "You can open it deliberately, with a Brightwater's hand, in the order the bargain requires — or you can watch it tear on its own and take everything with it. I'm offering you the merciful version. History will call you the enemy of both worlds regardless. Men always need a name for the hand that opens the cage." He inclined his head, courteous to the last. "Midwinter is here, Cartographer. The book is waiting. So is the dark."

Behind me, very quietly, Maren drew her sword.

Wren's hand found the knife at her belt. Pellam murmured something in a dead language that I'm fairly sure was a swear.

And under my feet, through the anchor stone, through six hundred years of silence, the thing beneath the Veil turned over in its sleep — and I felt it notice me, the last Brightwater, the living key, standing finally within reach.

It was not angry.

That was the worst part. It sounded *grateful*.

The clock above the doors, which had stopped when the sky went black, shuddered, ticked once, and began striking midnight — hours early, hours late, or exactly on time, depending on which book you believed.

Mine was open in my hands, and the ink was moving again, and the next line was writing itself faster than I could bear to read it.
