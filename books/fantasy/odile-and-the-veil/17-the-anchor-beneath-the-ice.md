## 17. The Anchor Beneath the Ice

The citadel of Vharen had always been cold, but tonight it was *honest* about it. Frost climbed the walls in patterns that no mason had cut, and the torches burned blue-white, the color of a flame that has forgotten what it's supposed to be warming. I stood in the shadow of the outer gate with a sword I'd stolen from my own former order and a plan that consisted mostly of the word *distract*.

Somewhere below us — beneath the ice, beneath the foundations, beneath three hundred years of careful royal architecture — the Veil's anchor was waking up. And somewhere above it, Sebastian Brightwater was running through corridors he'd only ever seen on a map he'd drawn himself, which is either very reassuring or very not, depending on how much you trust maps.

I trust maps. It's people I have doubts about.

"Two guards at the ice stair," Wren murmured beside me. Her breath came out in little silver ghosts. "Four more in the undercroft. And Dreville's frost-guard — the ones in the white lacquer — they don't patrol so much as *haunt*."

"You've been inside?"

"I've smuggled things *out* of inside. Different skill set." She adjusted the strap of her pack and gave me a look that on anyone else would have been called concern. On Wren it was more like professional assessment of a bad investment. "Maren. If this goes wrong, I want you to know I plan to run very fast and feel terrible about it later."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's said to me all week."

"Pellam says the anchor chamber opens off the old cisterns. He also said, and I quote, 'the frost-guard answer to Dreville alone, and they do not sleep.'" She paused. "He was very cheerful about it. Archivists are morbid."

Below us, Vharen burned in patches. Not fire — light. The sky had gone black at noon and stayed black, and the city answered with every lamp and lantern it owned, a thousand desperate candles against a dark that had teeth. Somewhere in that dark, Dreville was declaring Sebastian an enemy of both worlds, which was impressive, since Sebastian had barely managed to become an enemy of one.

I drew my sword. The blade sang faintly against the cold, a high thin note like a wineglass about to give up.

"Go get your cartographer," Wren said. "I'll hold the gate."

"Hold it with what?"

"Charm, mostly. And if charm fails, I have a knife and no scruples whatsoever."

I left her to it.

* * *

The ice stair was exactly where Pellam's banned histories said it would be, which is the thing about banned histories: they're banned because they're true, and they're true because somebody powerful found them embarrassing. Two guards stood at the top, frost-armored, faces hidden behind visors carved to look like calm. That's the trick of Dreville's elite — they don't wear skulls or demons or any of the usual theater. They wear serenity. It's far more unsettling.

I could have gone around. There was a servant's passage, narrow and black, that would have cost me ten minutes I didn't have. Sebastian was racing a tearing Veil and a chancellor with a god complex, and every minute I spent being clever was a minute he spent being dead.

So I didn't go around.

The first guard heard me coming, which was fair; I'd stopped trying to be quiet after the third stair. He turned, serene as a chapel statue, and got my pommel across his jaw for his trouble. The second was better — she caught my blade on her vambrace and drove me back two steps, and for a moment we were just two soldiers doing the oldest arithmetic there is, distance and angle and who tires first.

She tired first. Everyone does, eventually. I put her down hard enough to keep her down and not hard enough to make it permanent, which is a line I've spent seven years learning to walk.

"Sorry," I told her, because I was raised polite before I was raised disgraced.

The undercroft was worse. Four of them, and they'd heard the noise, and they'd arranged themselves in the corridor with the kind of patient geometry that says *we have done this many times and you are not the first person to try*. White lacquer. Serene masks. Blades drawn in perfect unison, like a single creature wearing four bodies.

There was a time when I'd have fought alongside men like these. There was a time when I *was* a man like these, or close enough — armor polished, orders memorized, conscience filed neatly away in a drawer marked *later*. Looking at them now was like looking into a mirror that had been left out in the cold too long. Everything familiar. Everything frosted over.

They came at me together, and I stopped thinking entirely, which is the closest thing to prayer I've managed in years.

Corridor fighting favors the patient and punishes the brave, so I made myself small and mean. I gave ground until the walls gave it back. I broke a wrist, took a gash along the ribs that I'd worry about when I had the luxury of worrying, and used a fallen shield as a sled down the frozen steps, which was undignified and effective, my two favorite qualities in a maneuver.

When it was done, I was breathing like a bellows and three of the four were groaning, which meant I'd won without killing, which meant I was still, in some stubborn corner of myself, the knight I'd been told I could never be again.

The fourth one wasn't groaning. He was standing at the end of the corridor, mask tilted, watching me with what I can only describe as professional interest.

"Maren Hale," he said. His voice was muffled by the visor and familiar underneath it. Familiar the way a scar is familiar.

I knew that voice. I'd followed that voice into three campaigns and out of one disaster.

"Commander Aldric," I said.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about Commander Aldric Voss, and forgive me if my hands aren't steady while I tell it.

Seven years ago, I was Hale of the Silver Vigil, third sword of the eastern march, and my honor was not yet a story other people told about me. We were posted on the Veil road the winter the famine came, and a supply caravan arrived carrying grain marked for a village that had already gone silent. Orders said burn the spoiled stores. I read the manifests instead of burning them, and what I found was that nothing was spoiled at all — the grain was good, and it was being diverted, on written instruction from the Chancellor's office, to feed Dreville's private garrisons while the villages starved.

I brought it to Aldric. I brought him the manifests, the seals, the proof, everything a loyal soldier is supposed to bring her commander. And Aldric looked at it for a long time, and then he looked at me, and he said the words that ended my life as it was:

*"You will bury this, Hale. Or I will bury you with it."*

I didn't bury it. I reported it up the chain, and the chain was made of Dreville, and I learned that day that the chain has no other links. They stripped my spurs in front of the whole garrison. They called it insubordination and theft of documents. The village starved anyway. And Aldric — Aldric was promoted.

That was the disgrace. That's the whole of it. People assume worse, when they hear *disgraced knight*. They assume cowardice or treachery or some sin worth the name. The truth was smaller and colder: I told the truth, and the truth had no armor, and I lost.

Now here he was at the bottom of a frozen stairway, standing between me and the anchor of the world, still wearing the same serene mask, still carrying out the same orders from the same office, seven years older and no wiser, unless you count knowing better how to serve a monster as wisdom.

"Aldric," I said again, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Step aside."

"The Cartographer passes this way over my body."

"He's not passing this way. I am. He's already past you — did you really think four of your lacquered statues were going to stop anything tonight?" I gestured vaguely upward, toward the sound of distant thunder that wasn't thunder. The Veil, tearing. The whole sky groaning like a ship in a storm. "Look around you, Commander. Look *up*. Whatever bargain you think you're protecting, it's dying. Tonight. Everything Dreville promised you is coming apart, and you're standing in a hallway guarding the door to the room where it ends."

"The realm needs order—"

"The realm needs *grain*, Aldric." My ribs were screaming and I made myself stand straight anyway. "You buried those manifests. You buried them and they gave you my spurs and a promotion, and the village still starved, and now you're here, at the end of everything, still telling yourself it was order. It was never order. It was fear wearing armor. I know. I wore the same suit for years after. It doesn't fit. It never fit."

He drew his sword. Of course he did. What else does a man like Aldric have left? Words had failed him decades ago; the steel was all that remained, and steel is honest even when its master isn't.

"Maren," he said quietly, and for one moment the serenity cracked and I saw the man who had once taught me the footwork I was about to use against him. "You were the best of them. That's why it had to be you who fell."

"No," I said. "It had to be someone who wouldn't kneel. You made sure it was me. Thank you for that, by the way. Falling was the most honest thing I've ever done."

We fought.

I want to tell you it was epic. I want to tell you there was music in it, some grand redemption scoring our blades like a festival play. But fights are never like the songs. Fights are cold air burning in your lungs and a ribcage full of broken glass and the terrible intimacy of knowing another human being's habits so well that killing him becomes almost a conversation. He favored the high line, always had; I dropped under it and made him pay for the habit he'd taught me to punish. I favored the feint-left, always had; he read it and laughed — actually laughed, behind that serene mask — and drove me back against the wall.

"You taught me that feint," he said.

"You taught me to punish it. One of us learned the lesson."

The ice beneath us was slick, and the blue torchlight turned our shadows into dancing things, and above us the Veil screamed like a kettle coming to boil. And somewhere in the middle of it — somewhere between his blade opening my sleeve and mine opening his cheek — I understood something I'd been circling for seven years.

Redemption isn't given back. Nobody hands you your spurs across a table with an apology. You take them. You take them bleeding, in a frozen hallway, from the man who took them from you, and even then they aren't really spurs — they're just permission. Permission to stop apologizing. Permission to be the thing you already were.

I beat his blade wide. I could have killed him — he knew it, I knew it, the moment hung there like a held breath. His mask had cracked fully now, and underneath it he looked tired. So tired. A man who had been guarding doors for thirty years and had finally, finally heard what was on the other side of them.

"Do it," he said. "Finish it. It's what the Order would—"

"The Order is ash, Aldric. Dreville burned it years ago and called the smoke loyalty." I leveled my point at his throat and held it there, trembling slightly, from cold or rage or mercy, I honestly couldn't tell you. "But I'm going to do something worse than kill you. I'm going to let you live. You're going to sit in this hallway and listen to the world end or be saved — you don't get to know which — and you're going to spend whatever's left of your life knowing you chose the wrong side of a grain manifest. That's your sentence. Serve it."

I stepped back. Lowered the sword.

And Aldric Voss, Commander of the Silver Vigil, last honest liar of a rotten institution, sank down against the frozen wall and — I swear this on whatever's left of my honor — he began to laugh. Quietly. Like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"The manifests," he said. "God help me. I still have them. I kept them."

"I know," I said. "That's why I let you live. Bring them to whoever's standing when this is over. It's the only pardon anyone's going to offer you, and it isn't mine to give."

Then I ran, because somewhere below me, the ice was singing, and somewhere below that, Sebastian Brightwater was about to meet the thing his family had built a prison around, and he was going to need every second I could buy him.

* * *

The cisterns beneath Vharen were older than the citadel, older than the kingdom, maybe older than the winter. The ice down there wasn't white — it was deep green-black, glassy, and lit from *beneath*, a slow pulse of light like a heartbeat seen through a frozen chest. With every pulse, hairline cracks crawled a little further across the ceiling, and little snowfalls of ancient frost sifted down around me like the world shedding skin.

At the center of the great chamber, the anchor rose out of the ice: a pillar of black stone carved all over with maps — real maps, maps of places that no longer existed, places that had been *unmade* — and wrapped around it, chains of a metal I had no name for, glowing where the Veil pressed against them from the other side.

Sebastian stood before it with the prophecy book open in his hands, and the book was blazing, pages riffling on their own, ink crawling like living things. Pellam was beside him, shouting something scholarly and useless. Wren had made it down too, knife drawn, watching the dark water ringed with ice as though expecting it to notice her. It probably would.

And beyond them, at the edge of the chamber, Lord Chancellor Dreville stood with his hand pressed flat against the black stone, feeding it, whispering to it, an old man pouring seventy years of other people's suffering into a crack in the world.

Sebastian looked up and saw me, bloodied and limping and grinning like an idiot.

"Maren!" he called. "You're alive!"

"For the moment," I shouted back, and raised my sword at the Chancellor of Odile, the man who had ruined me before I'd ever met him, the reason for every cold night of the last seven years. "Get your map drawn, cartographer. Nothing gets past me."

Dreville turned. And the thing beneath the ice turned with him.

Behind his eyes, something vast opened one eye of its own.
