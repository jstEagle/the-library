## 18. The Choice of Ends

The anchor beneath the ice was not what I expected, which by that point in my life should have stopped surprising me and never did.

I had imagined — because I am a cartographer, and cartographers imagine in lines — a chamber. A vaulted thing, maybe, with pillars and a dais and the sort of dramatic architecture that tyrants build when they want you to know a place is important. What I got instead was a wound.

We had come down through Dreville's own passages, the ones Wren had smuggled brandy through for six years and smuggled secrets through for four, and the deeper we went the less the citadel above us mattered. The stone changed first. Then the cold changed — not the honest cold of Odile's winter, which at least had the decency to be weather, but a cold that felt *aimed*. And then the walls simply gave up pretending to be walls, and we were walking through ice that was not blue or white but the color of an old bruise, and inside the ice, things moved.

Not creatures. Not quite. Shapes. The way ink moves under water if you drop it from too high — slow, spreading, purposeful without direction. Pellam would have called them something Latin and precise. I just knew they were watching us pass, the way a scar watches the knife that made it.

"Sebastian." Maren's voice came low behind me, her sword already drawn, frost crawling up the blade in patterns I recognized now as wrong — the frost-guard's work, Dreville's work, magic leaking through cracks it was never meant to find. "Whatever's down there, remember: you don't have to touch it."

"That's very comforting," I said. "Say something else equally useless."

"Don't die."

"Better. Still working on it."

Wren said nothing at all, which from Wren was its own kind of sermon. She walked point with her hood back and her half-blood eyes catching light that wasn't there, and every few steps she'd press her palm flat against the bruised ice like she was taking its pulse. At one point she muttered, in what I'd learned was Old Fae, *"still hungry,"* and then, quieter, in Common, so only I could hear: "It knows your name, Brightwater. It's been saying it since the second stair."

"Wonderful," I said. "I've always wanted to be famous underground."

But my hands were shaking, and not from the cold, and the book — the prophecy book, blank since midwinter began, blank since I burned its certainty off every page — hung against my chest where I'd strapped it, and it was *warm*. Warm as a sleeping animal. Warm as something waiting to be fed.

* * *

The anchor itself, when we reached it, was a map.

Of course it was. Of course it was. I stood at the edge of a cavern the size of Vharen's great hall and laughed until Maren grabbed my arm, because there, suspended in the center of the dark over a lake of black ice, hung a chart — vast, hand-drawn, rendered in silver and salt and something that pulsed faintly red along its borders — and I knew the hand that had drawn it. I had spent three weeks in Wren's hold learning that hand. The flourished compass roses. The coastlines drawn with a slight tremor on the left side, as though the cartographer had been crying. The marginalia in two scripts, human and fae, arguing with each other down every border.

My ancestor had not built a prison here. He had *drawn* one. He had sat in this dark with his pens and his guilt and he had mapped the Veil into being, anchor by anchor, and this — this hanging, breathing thing of silver line and salt — was the master copy. The original. The page every other page was traced from.

And beneath it, beneath the black ice of the lake, the thing it held.

I felt it before I saw it. A pressure, like standing at the bottom of a well. Like the moment on the valley's salt border, a lifetime ago, when the dusk flickered and something flickered back. Except this was no flicker. This was a presence the size of a season, coiled under the ice, and when it became aware of me — truly aware, the way a storm becomes aware of a lit window — every hair on my body stood up and my teeth ached and somewhere far above us, snow began to fall upward.

"There you are," said a voice, and it took me a shameful moment to realize it wasn't the thing speaking. It was Dreville.

He stepped out of the dark on the far shore of the lake as though he'd been waiting for us all evening, which, knowing him, he had. No frost-guard. No soldiers. Just the Lord Chancellor of Odile in his grey fur, hands folded, wearing the patient expression of a man who has spent forty years feeding a monster and considers himself very good at it.

"Maren Hale," he said pleasantly. "Still alive. How tedious. And the smuggler — Ashvale, isn't it? Your mother sold my grandfather contraband lullabies. She'd be proud." His eyes found mine and stayed there, and I understood suddenly why courtiers went quiet around him. There was nothing behind his gaze doing anything else. All of him was looking at me. "And the Cartographer of Ends. Come to choose."

"I haven't been offered a menu," I said.

Dreville smiled. It did not reach anywhere near the rest of him. "Then allow me."

* * *

He gestured, and the ice beneath our feet lit up — not with light exactly, but with *memory*, the way the Burnt Reach had shown us ghosts. Only these weren't ghosts of battles. They were older.

I saw the world before the Veil. Or pieces of it, stitched fast and rough: a green country where men and fae traded across open borders, argued in the same courts, married, quarreled, built. And then I saw what they built *together*, in the last bright century before everything — a working, a making, a thing with no name in either tongue because both tongues had agreed not to name it. Human ambition given fae shape. Fae hunger given human hands. They had made it as a wonder. As a bridge, maybe. As a god, possibly. The memory didn't say, and the memory's silence was worse than any answer.

And then I saw what it had done when it woke up hungry.

The war hadn't started over territory. Every history — even Pellam's banned ones, even the ones I'd believed — had the shape of the lie still pressed into it. The war had started because both peoples had made something together that neither could control and neither could kill, and both had blamed the other, and while they bled each other white across a hundred battlefields, a handful of cartographers on both sides had done the only wise thing anyone did in that entire century.

They had drawn a wall through the world and shut the thing inside it.

"The Veil is not a border between your kind and mine," Dreville said, walking slowly along the shore, his voice almost gentle, which from him was the most frightening register available. "It is a lid. And lids, Master Brightwater, require holding down. My family has held it. Fed it. Kept it dreaming with small sacrifices — a village's warmth here, a summer there, the long winter you've all been so sentimental about. And now the queen sends *you*." He spread his hands. "So. Choose."

"Choose what?"

"Choose an end." He said it like a man offering tea. "Open the Veil, as your bleeding heart is no doubt already composing speeches about. Free the thing. Let it feed as it was meant to feed, on both worlds equally, until there is nothing left to feed on — but oh, the winter breaks first, the sun comes out, everyone dances. A beautiful last summer. Or close it forever. Seal the anchor with your blood, as the prophecy's darker verses suggest, and the winter never ends — but the thing starves, and dies, and the worlds are safe and gray and frozen until the sun itself gives up. Those are your ends, Cartographer. Map them."

The lake was silent. The shapes in the bruised ice had gone still, listening. Even the book against my chest seemed to hold its breath.

"And you?" I managed. "What do you get out of either?"

Dreville looked at me for a long moment, and then, astonishingly, he told the truth.

"My family's bargain keeps us alive when it wakes," he said. "Whichever way it goes. We fed it; it remembers who fed it. When the Veil opens, my line survives the feeding. When it closes forever, my line rules the frozen remnant. That is what forty years of treason buys, boy. Safety. Either way." He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "You think I'm a monster. I'm an accountant."

Behind me, I heard Maren's breath go ragged, and Wren's hand move toward whatever she kept hidden in her coat, and I raised one finger — *wait* — because a thought had arrived, and it was the most dangerous kind of thought, the kind that feels obvious.

"You're wrong about the menu," I said.

"Am I."

"You said open it or seal it. Feed it or starve it. But you're describing a cage, Dreville, and cages only have those two futures." I turned to face the lake, to face the pressure and the hunger and the vast slow attention rising under the ice to meet me. "My ancestor drew this place. I've read his margins. I've read his arguments with himself in two languages. And do you know what he wrote in the last one? The one nobody's ever deciphered, because it wasn't written for scholars or queens or chancellors?"

Dreville's patience thinned visibly. "Enlighten me."

"He wrote: *'A wall was my error. I drew it in fear, and fear has only two outcomes — breach or siege. If any hand of mine ever stands here again, let it draw better than I did.'*" My voice cracked on the last word, and I let it. I was done performing steadiness. "He knew. A hundred years ago, he knew the choice you're offering me was a false one. He just couldn't fix it from inside his own fear. So he left the door open — literally — for someone to come after him and draw better."

The lake shuddered. Under the black ice, something enormous turned over, and for the first time it didn't feel like hunger looking at me.

It felt like loneliness.

* * *

Here is what I have not said yet, what I could not say to Maren on the road or Wren on the ice or even to myself in the dark hours of Wren's hold, and it is this: I understood the thing under the lake.

Not its nature. Not its power. Its *position*. It had been made by two peoples who then spent a century blaming each other for its existence, and it had been shut away in the dark by the only people who ever understood it, and it had been fed just enough to stay alive and never enough to live, for a hundred years, by a family that considered it livestock. Every path anyone had ever offered it ended in a cage.

I had spent my whole life in a valley with no magic in it, mapping places that existed only in rumor, telling myself I preferred it that way. I knew exactly what a comfortable prison looked like from the inside. I had lived in one and called it peace.

"Sebastian." Maren's hand on my shoulder, steady, warm, human. "Whatever you're thinking — the cost—"

"Every path costs something irreplaceable," I said. "That's what a choice *is*, Maren. Dreville just wants me to believe his two prices are the only ones on the market." I looked at her, and then at Wren, who was watching me with an expression I'd learned to read as hope wearing armor. "Open the Veil and the world ends. Seal it and the world freezes. Fine. Then I won't do either."

"And what," said Dreville softly, "do you propose?"

I unstrapped the book from my chest. Blank pages. Burning-warm. Waiting the way paper waits, which is to say: completely, for whoever comes with ink.

"I propose," I said, kneeling at the edge of the black ice with the book open on my knees and my pen case in my hand, "to redraw the map."

The lake went utterly still. Above us, impossibly far above us, the torn sky of midwinter paused in its ripping, as if the whole wounded world had leaned in to watch a man with a pen.

Dreville moved first — of course he did; accountants panic at unbalanced ledgers — and shouted for his guard, and steel rang on the stair behind us, and Maren stepped past me into the dark with her sword up and her honor finally, finally straightened on her shoulders like a cloak. Wren's knives caught the strange light. The bruised ice began to scream in a language older than either of my tongues.

And I put pen to the first blank page, and began to draw.

Not a wall. Never again a wall.

A door.
