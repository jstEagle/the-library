## 20. The Long Winter Breaks

I want it on the record that I did not cry when the sun came up.

There were witnesses — gulls, a few smugglers' brats on the seawall, one very old fisherman who'd forgotten more winters than I'd survived — and none of them can prove anything about my face, because I was facing the water at the time, and the water was doing something far more interesting than I was.

It was moving.

Not the way the sea always moves, in that grudging, half-frozen shuffle Odile's coast had performed for as long as anyone could remember. Moving like it remembered what it was for. The ice that had locked the cove since before midwinter — thick enough to walk a cart across, and we had, several times, because Wren Ashvale does not let a free road go to waste — groaned once, deep, like an old man getting out of a chair he'd been sitting in for three hundred years.

Then it cracked.

The sound went through my boots, through my ribs, through whatever it is fae-blooded folk keep where other people keep their hearts. A seam of black water opened out past the point, thin as a knife cut, and then another, and then the whole sheet was talking to itself in long, splintering syllables, and the first honest dawn light in living memory came over the headland and set every one of those cracks burning gold.

"Ha," I said, to nobody. "Ha."

That's all I managed. It's not much of a prayer, but I've never been much of a praying sort. Smugglers pray to luck, to tide tables, to whichever saint handles cargo manifests, and mostly they get silence back. This was not silence. This was the whole frozen world clearing its throat after a very long nap.

Behind me, up the cliff path, the coves were already waking. Somebody was shouting. Somebody else was laughing, which is rarer and better. Old Sefa stood on her porch with her arms spread wide like she meant to catch the sunlight personally, and maybe she did; you earn the right at her age.

I stayed on the seawall and watched the ice break, and I will tell you honestly: it scared me worse than the dark ever had.

Because hope, for people like me, is not a comfort. Hope is a debt. Every time I'd let myself want something in this kingdom — a warm berth, a full hold, a friend who didn't cost me — the winter took its cut. So I'd learned to live small and cold and certain, and certainty is just suffocation with good posture. And now the sky was doing something impossible, and the sea was doing something impossible, and somewhere north of here a cartographer who mapped places that never were was either dead or had done the most impossible thing of all, and I had no idea which, and I couldn't stand not knowing, and I couldn't stop smiling anyway.

My face hurt from it. That's how out of practice I was.

* * *

Let me back up, because dawn doesn't explain itself, and neither do I.

The night before, the sky had gone black at noon — proper black, ink-black, the kind of dark that makes even the gulls shut up — and every soul in the coves had come out of their huts to stare at it, because when the world ends you might as well have company. The Veil was tearing. We could feel it even down on the coast, a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the teeth, like standing too close to a struck bell. The old-timers said it felt like the last war. Sefa said it felt like her first husband. Nobody asked follow-up questions.

And then word came down the coast road, riding with a half-dead courier and a lot of rumor: Dreville had taken the citadel. Declared the Cartographer of Ends an enemy of both worlds. Sealed the gates. Called up the frost-guard.

Maren Hale was somewhere inside those walls with a sword and a grudge and, if there was any justice left in Odile, a very clear plan. Pellam Oxley had gone to ground in Vharen's undercity with his banned books and his insufferable calm. And Sebastian Brightwater —

Sebastian was walking into the ice beneath the citadel to face the thing his family had helped bury, armed with nothing but ink, nerve, and the single most stubborn streak of decency I've ever had the misfortune to care about.

I'd offered to go with them. Maren had said no. Sebastian had said thank you, and meant it, and that was somehow worse. "Someone has to be outside," he'd said, in that reasonable voice of his, "in case there's an outside afterward."

So I'd sat in the coves with the smugglers and the fishermen and the brats, and I'd watched the sky go black, and I'd done what I do best, which is wait with excellent posture while everything I care about happens somewhere else.

I hate waiting. Fae-blood ages slow; we get very good at it and never learn to like it.

* * *

The black lasted nine hours. I counted. What else was I going to count?

Around what should have been midnight, the pressure behind the eyes changed. Anyone who's sailed the strait knows the feeling of weather turning — the air goes glassy, the gulls go quiet, your fillings ache. This was that, but underneath the skin of the world. Something vast had been straining against a door, and now the door was opening, or being opened, or being *redrawn*, and the difference mattered more than any of us knew.

Sefa found me on the seawall around then. She brought two cups of something hot and alcoholic and sat down without asking, which is the local custom and one of the reasons I love these people.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I always look like this."

"Worse than usual." She handed me a cup. "You've got the face of a woman watching a door."

"That's literally what I'm doing."

"Not the sea, girl. The door." She sipped. She'd been drinking things like this since before my grandmother's grandmother was a bad idea, and she held it like a queen holds a scepter. "You're waiting to see who walks through."

"And if nobody does?"

"Then you'll build a boat and go find out why." She said it like a fact, because it was one. "Ashvales don't wait well. Never have. Your mother once rowed out into a whiteout after a man everyone said was dead. Found him. Married him. Ruined him. Lovely woman."

"She was," I said, and then, because the dark was pressing close and Sefa was old enough to hear anything: "He wasn't my father by blood, you know. My real father was fae. Court side. He sent a letter when I turned thirty. Took him thirty years to work up to it, and the letter was four lines long, and one of the lines was about money."

"What did you write back?"

"Nothing. I kept the letter in a box for a decade and then I burned it, and then I felt bad, so I wrote him a new one in my head every year for twenty years, and never sent any of them." I shrugged. "Winter's a good teacher. It teaches you to keep things shut."

Sefa looked at me for a long moment with those milky old eyes that see entirely too much.

"And now?" she said.

"Now the door's opening whether I like it or not."

"Mm." She finished her drink. "Doors are only terrible when they open onto nothing. Wait and see what's on the other side of this one before you decide how to feel about it."

I decided to feel terrified, but I kept it off my face, because I'm a professional.

* * *

The dawn broke at what the bells said was six and what my bones said was roughly a century late.

I've described the ice cracking. What I haven't described is the light. You grow up in the long winter, you forget that sunrise has a color. Ours had been a gray smudge behind gray cloud for as long as the histories ran, a candle behind a curtain, and everyone had made their peace with it the way you make peace with a limp. But this light came up red-gold and shameless, and it hit the frost on the cliffs and set the whole coastline glittering like a dragon's hoard, and the sea beyond the breaking ice steamed faintly where the warm air touched it, and the gulls lost their minds entirely.

And along the shore, the snow began to go.

Not melt exactly — *withdraw*. Like a tide going out. The drifts sagged, settled, darkened, and showed the ground beneath: black soil, wet stone, the green stubble of something that had been waiting under the white for three hundred years and had absolutely not died. Grass. Actual grass, in Odile, in what the calendar insisted was still winter. The brats were rolling in it within the hour. One of them ate some. Children are feral everywhere; it's the one thing all worlds share.

Word came up the coast road by midmorning, and it came in pieces, the way truth always does.

Piece one: the citadel still stood, mostly. Piece two: the frost-guard had broken — not been defeated, *broken*, men dropping their weapons and weeping when the sky cleared, because they'd spent their whole lives serving the cold and hadn't known until that moment that they'd been conscripts. Piece three: Lord Chancellor Dreville was dead.

I'll give the rumors their due; they got the shape of it right, if not the details. He hadn't fallen to a blade. He'd gone down into the dark beneath the citadel to bargain with the thing his family had fed for generations — the thing both sides had made together, back when the war was young, the thing the Veil didn't separate but *imprisoned* — and when Sebastian redrew the Veil, when he turned the wall into a door and bound himself as its key, the thing's prison changed shape. And bargains, as any smuggler knows, are contracts, and contracts are maps, and when you redraw the map, the old roads stop existing.

Dreville had spent forty years feeding something through a crack in the wall. Sebastian turned the wall into a door. And the thing he'd been feeding walked out through it, unhurried, and looked at him, and whatever passed between them, Dreville didn't survive the accounting. They say he was found frozen solid in a room with no frost in it. They say his face was surprised. Both of those can be true.

I felt, examining myself, approximately nothing about it. Maybe a little satisfaction, the way you feel when a debt collector drowns. Mostly I felt the absence of fear — that constant low hum I'd carried since childhood, the hum that says the winter is patient and the winter is everywhere and the winter keeps accounts. It stopped. Just stopped. I kept reaching for it like a tongue finding the gap where a tooth was.

Piece four arrived at dusk, and this one was delivered in person, because the world has a sense of drama.

A horse came down the coast road — a real horse, lathered, half-dead, ridden by a woman in armor that had seen the inside of a citadel and the outside of a war in the same night. Maren Hale slid off it before it fully stopped, staggered, caught herself on the seawall two feet from me, and looked at me with blood on her cheekbone and the dawn still in her eyes.

"He did it," she said. Her voice was wrecked. "The idiot actually did it."

"Which idiot? Be specific. There are several."

"The cartographer." She laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound, like a hinge that hadn't moved in years. "He rewrote it. The prophecy. The book's blank, Wren. Pellam checked it three times. Three hundred years of fate and it's *blank pages* now, like the world finally got a fresh sheet." She gripped my arm hard enough to bruise. "He's alive. He's at the anchor. He says—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—he says he can't come home. Not permanently. But the door opens twice a year, and he wants—"

She stopped. Looked at me. Looked at the sea, where the last sheets of ice were drifting out on the tide like a fleet in retreat, black water shining between them.

"He wants us there," she said. "When it opens. He wants his family there."

* * *

Here's the thing about me, and I've hidden it behind a great deal of swagger and a truly excellent coat: I don't trust good news. Good news is the lure on the trap. Good news is the harbor pilot who knows where the reefs are and charges accordingly. Every gift I've ever gotten had a toll attached, and I learned early to check the price before I smiled.

So when Maren said it, my first instinct was to look for the catch. My second instinct was to list the catches: he's bound to the Veil forever; we get two days a year; the fae courts will have opinions; the new order Maren's founding will have opinions; the whole political architecture of two worlds just shifted and someone, somewhere, is already drawing up plans about a living door.

All true. All real tolls.

But Sefa's voice was in my ear: *wait and see what's on the other side of the door before you decide how to feel about it.*

And my third instinct — the one I've spent thirty years pretending doesn't exist, the one my fae father's four-line letter cracked open and Sebastian's stupid decent face pried the rest of the way — my third instinct said: he asked for us. By name. All of us. The disgraced knight, the heretic, the smuggler. Not the prophecy's chosen circle. Not the queen's instruments. Us. The found thing. The made thing. The family that isn't blood, which turns out to be the only kind that survives the winter.

I looked out at the cove. The ice was nearly gone now; the fishing boats were already putting out, crews half-laughing, half-crying, hauling nets into water that hadn't been open in living memory. On the headland, somebody had lit a bonfire in broad daylight, which is the most gloriously wasteful thing I've ever seen, and I include my own life in that assessment.

"All right," I said to Maren. "One condition."

"Name it."

"My boat. We sail to the anchor, not ride. If I'm going to watch a miracle, I'm doing it from the deck of my own ship, with my own crew, and a cargo manifest that officially lists 'miscellaneous.'"

Maren stared at me. Then she laughed again, properly this time, and it transformed her whole ruined face into something young. "You're smuggling hope now?"

"Somebody has to. It's not going to transport itself." I pushed off the seawall and headed for the harbor, and the thaw-wet stones squished under my boots, and I swear the ground smelled alive. "Besides, I know the waters. And if the door opens onto trouble instead of a homecoming, you'll want someone aboard who's good at leaving quickly."

"And if it opens onto him?"

I didn't answer right away. Out past the point, the last floe spun slowly in the swell, catching the low gold light, and broke apart with a sound like applause.

"Then I suppose," I said, "we'll finally learn what I look like when I'm not bracing for the worst."

Maren grinned. "Terrified."

"Undoubtedly. But it'll be a new flavor of it." I whistled up my crew, and the harbor answered, and for the first time in three hundred years, Odile's coast smelled like spring.

We sail at dawn. Which, these days, is a promise instead of a threat — and somewhere north, behind a door that opens twice a year, a cartographer is keeping our place.

I intend to arrive loudly.
