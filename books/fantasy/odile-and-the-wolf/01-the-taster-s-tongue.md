## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The morning the world began to end, Yara Delacroix was counting drops of henbane into a bowl of honeyed figs.

Not because she suspected the figs. Because she counted everything, every morning, the way other people said their prayers. Twelve drops of henbane tincture, measured with a glass pipette older than the king himself, stirred clockwise nine times, then left to sit while she drank a measure of it herself and waited for her heart to slow its panicked gallop back to something like a walk. The numbness would bloom across her tongue, spread down her throat, and fade within a quarter hour, leaving behind the familiar chalky taste that meant her body had won again. Her body always won. That was the whole point of her.

"Third bell," called Tam from the doorway of the tasting chamber, his flour-white apron already dusted with the morning's first casualties — a spilled sack of barley, an egg he'd dropped out of pure nerves. Tam dropped things when he was nervous, and he was nervous every time Yara tasted, which was every time Yara tasted. "Cook wants to know if you're alive yet."

"Tell Cook I'm never alive before second bell."

"You say that every day." He grinned at her, gap-toothed, twenty years old and built like a bread oven. "And every day you're alive anyway. It's almost like you're lying."

"It's almost like I'm professional." She swirled the last of the henbane in the pipette, watching it catch the grey light from the arrow-slit window. The Valley of Stillness kept its own hours — no true dawn ever reached the castle kitchens, just a pale diffusion through the wards that made every morning look like the inside of a pearl. Magic couldn't cross the valley's borders. Neither could anything else, in theory. In practice, poison traveled by road like everyone else.

Yara set down the pipette and flexed her fingers. Nineteen years old, and her hands were steadier than the surgeon's who had trained her, steadier than the spymaster's who employed her, steady as a dead woman's pulse. She'd earned that steadiness the way she'd earned everything: drop by drop, year by year, a childhood spent learning to die gracefully and then not die at all.

* * *

The great hall smelled of roast pheasant and beeswax and the particular sourness of a court that hadn't slept well. King Aldric sat at the high table beneath the stag-skull banner of House Veyran, and even from the servants' corridor Yara could see the tremor in his left hand where it rested on the armrest. The madness, they called it. It came and went like weather. Lately it had been coming more than going.

She took her place at the sideboard, where the dishes meant for the royal table passed through her hands one by one. This was the ritual, unchanged in three hundred years: the taster tastes, the court waits, the king eats. A simple covenant between one girl's stomach and one man's life.

Pheasant. She cut a sliver from the breast, chewed, swallowed, and stood very still while the hall pretended not to watch her. Salt, fat, rosemary, the faint gamey iron underneath. Nothing. She moved to the next dish.

It went on like that for the better part of an hour. Soup — nothing. Bread — nothing. A compote of valley plums so sweet it made her teeth ache — nothing. The kitchen staff watched from the service door, a row of anxious faces: Tam with his flour, old Marda the undercook with her arms crossed like she was personally offended by the possibility of poison, little Sef, the scullery boy, standing on tiptoe to see over Marda's elbow. They were her family, though no blood bound them. They had fed her since she was six years old and pulled half-dead from a snowdrift with no name anyone knew and a birthmark on her collarbone shaped like a coiled serpent. They had taught her to laugh in a castle full of fear. When she finished each dish unharmed, Marda would nod once, sharp and satisfied, like a general accepting a surrender.

Then came the honeyed figs.

They arrived on a silver dish Yara didn't recognize, carried by a servant she also didn't recognize — a thin man with a cough, new this season, assigned from somewhere in the west wing. She noted both facts the way she noted everything: automatically, without alarm. New dishes happened. New servants happened. The king liked variety; variety was how poisoners got their chances, and variety was also why the king employed a girl whose body was a graveyard of every toxin known to the alchemists' guild.

Twelve drops of henbane, she remembered. She had prepared this dish herself.

She took a spoonful.

For a moment, nothing. Then the taste bloomed wrong — not bitter, not numbing, but *cold*, a cold that started behind her teeth and poured down her spine like meltwater off a glacier. The hall tilted. The candles guttered though there was no draft. Somewhere very far away, Marda was shouting her name.

Yara gripped the edge of the sideboard. *Breathe.* She had been poisoned four hundred and eleven times in her nineteen years. She had catalogued every one of them in a leather journal hidden beneath her mattress — symptoms, onset, duration, antidote, aftermath. None of them felt like this. Henbane didn't feel like this. Henbane felt like warm wool and slow blood. This felt like being *unmade*, like something reaching past her body's hard-won armor and touching the girl underneath.

Her knees hit the floor. The hall erupted. Through the roaring in her ears she heard the king rise, heard Corvane's voice — the spymaster's voice, dry as old paper — cutting through the panic with orders. And then Marda was there, and Tam, hauling her upright between them, and someone forced something bitter between her lips — willow bark and charcoal, the standard draught, useless against whatever this was but comforting in its familiarity.

The cold peaked. Held. And then, slowly, like a tide going out, it receded — leaving Yara kneeling on the flagstones, shaking, alive, with a taste in her mouth like frost and old metal.

Silence. Every eye in the hall fixed on her.

"I'm fine," she managed. Her voice sounded scraped. "I'm fine. It passed."

Corvane appeared at her elbow the way he always did, soundless, a grey man in grey clothes with eyes like ledger entries. "You're certain."

"I'm certain I'm not dead. Beyond that—" She stopped. Swallowed. "That wasn't henbane. Or rather, it was, and it wasn't. Something was *in* it. Under it. Older."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — too fast to name, gone before she could read it. "Older," he repeated.

"Chemistry has a flavor," Yara said. "Everything the guild makes, I've tasted. This was..." She searched for the word and hated the one she found. "*Ritual.*"

The word landed in the hall like a dropped knife. In the Valley of Stillness, magic was a foreign country. Saying *ritual* aloud was like saying *plague*.

Corvane studied her for a long moment. Then he turned to the trembling servant with the cough. "You. Where did this dish come from?"

"The — the west pantry, my lord, as instructed—"

"Who instructed?"

"A note, my lord. Signed by the undercook—"

Marda's head snapped up. "I signed no such thing."

The servant's face went white. And then, before anyone could move, he bolted — shoving through the stunned courtiers, out the great doors, gone. Two guards gave chase. From the sounds echoing down the corridor, they wouldn't catch him.

Corvane looked back at Yara. For just a moment, the ledger-eyes seemed to be weighing something far heavier than a poisoning attempt. "Rest," he said. "We'll speak later. There are questions I have for you, taster, and I suspect you have questions for me."

He swept away. The hall slowly emptied, the king withdrawn under guard, the meal abandoned. Tam helped Yara to her feet properly, and she leaned on him more than she wanted to.

"That's the first time I've ever seen you go down," he said quietly.

"I know." She looked at the silver dish of figs, sitting abandoned on the sideboard. Cold radiated off it still, faintly, like a hearth gone out an hour ago. "Tam. Seal that dish. Don't let anyone touch it. And don't let Corvane's people take it before I've looked at it again."

"You think there's something left in it?"

"I think," Yara said, "that for the first time in my life, I tasted something my body didn't know how to survive. It survived anyway. I want to know why."

* * *

The tasting chamber, after dark, was hers alone. She'd dismissed the duty page, barred the door, and lit only one candle — enough to work by, not enough to be seen working.

The figs sat in their silver dish under a cloth. She'd taken a single fruit, macerated it in clean water, and drawn off the liquid into three vessels. To the first she added the standard reagents: silver nitrate, then vinegar of antimony, then the alchemists' universal indicator that turned green for arsenic, blue for nightshade kin, black for mineral salts. All three stayed clear. Whatever had been in the figs, it wasn't chemistry — or not chemistry alone.

The second vessel she saved. The third she dipped a fingertip into, raised it to her tongue, and touched it there, lightly, the way a jeweler tests a stone.

Cold. Faint now, faded, like a scent lingering in an empty room. But underneath the cold, something else — a texture, a *grain*, as if the liquid held particles too fine to see. She held the vessel up to the candlelight.

Nothing. Clear water. Except—

Except when she tilted it just so, at the exact angle where the candleflame caught the meniscus, there was a shimmer. Not reflection. *Suspension.* Something in the water, finer than dust, finer than flour, hanging there without settling.

Yara's mouth went dry. She set down the vessel, crossed to her journal, and flipped through four hundred and eleven entries looking for anything like this. She found nothing. Of course she found nothing. Everything in the journal fit inside the world she knew.

She returned to the vessel. And because she was who she was — because her whole life had been built on the principle that the only way to understand a poison is to take it into your own body — she lifted it and drank.

The cold came back, fainter, a ghost of a ghost. It washed through her and ebbed. And then her chest seized, and she coughed — a deep, wrenching cough that bent her double over the table.

When she straightened, wiping her mouth with the back of her hand, her palm was dusted with something that glittered.

Silver. Fine as flour, bright as frost, scattered across the lines of her hand like a constellation. She stared at it. It did not smudge when she rubbed two fingers together. It did not dissolve when she touched it to her tongue — it simply vanished, leaving that grain-of-cold taste behind.

Slowly, carefully, Yara tipped the rest of the vessel's contents onto the table and watched the light catch it: a suspension of silver dust, settling now, at last, into a thin shining film.

Poison laced with something older than chemistry. Something her body fought off and *coughed out*, the way a healthy lung coughs out ash.

She sat back in the dark, her heart hammering, and thought about the servant with the cough, and the forged note, and the word *ritual* hanging in the air of the great hall. She thought about the king's worsening madness, and the blighted harvest reports Marda grumbled about, and the way the wards at the valley's edge had hummed strangely all autumn — a sound only the kitchen staff noticed, because they lived closest to the bones of things.

Someone had sent death into the Valley of Stillness wearing a mask it shouldn't have been able to wear. Someone had used a poison that operated by rules her teachers had never mentioned — rules from outside the valley, or beneath it, or before it.

And her body, her faithful, poisoned, immortal body, had recognized it. Had rejected it. Had *known* it, the way a dog knows a stranger's scent.

Yara closed her fist around the silver dust and held it against her sternum, where her heartbeat was finally slowing.

Outside, far off toward the valley gate, a bell rang once — the night watch, marking the hour. Ordinary. Routine. Safe.

But as she blew out the candle, Yara could have sworn the silver dust in her palm glowed for a moment longer than the darkness should have allowed — faintly, briefly, like an ember remembering fire.

Like something answering a name.
