## 2. A Stranger at the Gate

The bell above the gate rang wrong.

Yara had lived beneath the sound of that bell for nineteen years, and she knew its moods the way a cook knows a kettle. It rang for merchants, for pilgrims, for the twice-yearly caravan from the lowland farms. It rang bright and businesslike on those days, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause. This was different. It was ragged, arrhythmic, someone hauling the rope with whatever strength remained in their arms, and it went on far longer than courtesy required.

She was in the gatehouse kitchen rendering fat for the winter stores when it started, and she had her apron off before the second stroke finished.

"Yara." Old Sef, the under-cook, didn't look up from his onions. "Not your table today."

"The bell's wrong."

"The bell's always wrong when it's cold out. Rope swells." He waved his knife at her, a small gesture of dismissal worn smooth by decades of use. "Let the guards earn their keep for once."

But Yara was already through the door, because she had learned long ago that wrongness announced itself in small ways first — an off note in the smell of a broth, a grain of rice that sat strangely on the tongue — and the people who ignored small wrongnesses eventually ate something that killed them. She crossed the inner courtyard at a jog, breath steaming, the morning so still that her footsteps sounded like trespass. The Valley of Stillness held its silence the way a cupped hand holds water. No wind reached the floor of it. No birds sang that hadn't been hatched inside the wards. Even the smoke from the baker's chimney rose straight as a plumb line and simply stopped, somewhere above the rooftops, as if the sky itself declined to stir.

The guards at the outer gate were clustered around something at the threshold, and their body language told her everything before she saw it: weight shifted back onto heels, hands resting easy near but not on weapons, the particular stiffness of men who wanted very much to be elsewhere.

"She came up the pass," one of them was saying as Yara pushed through. "Alone. In this cold. On foot."

"On foot from where?" asked another. "There's nothing on the pass road but rock."

"There's nothing *past* the pass," said the first, in the tone of a man stating a law of nature, which in the valley it essentially was.

Then they moved aside — half because Yara elbowed, half because everyone knew her, the king's taster, small and dark-eyed and perpetually unimpressed — and Yara saw the stranger.

A woman lay just inside the gate, on her side, knees drawn up as though she'd been trying to crawl and had simply run out of the will to continue. Her clothes had been fine once; Yara could read the ghost of embroidery at the hem, the good leather of her boots now split and black with frost. And her skin —

Yara had seen burns. Kitchens taught you burns early. Scalds, spatters, the glossy red of a hand caught on a hot iron. This was not that. The woman's left arm and the whole left side of her face looked *eaten*, the flesh gone the color of old candle wax, cracked in places like drying mud, and along the worst of it ran lines that glowed faintly, faintly orange, as if embers slept beneath the skin and breathed.

"That's not fire," Yara heard herself say.

"It's fire enough," said the gate sergeant, a broad, kind-faced man named Doss. "Keep back, taster. She might be contagious."

"She might be dying." Yara crouched. Up close the woman smelled of char and, beneath it, something stranger — a smell Yara had no name for, sharp and mineral, like a struck flint left out in rain. It raised the hair on her arms, and Yara could not remember the last time anything had raised the hair on her arms. Nineteen years of tasting death in small doses had sanded the flinch out of her. "Someone get Maren's girl from the healers' row. And water. Not from the well — the snowmelt cistern, it's cleaner."

"You don't give orders here—"

"Doss." She said it quietly, without looking at him. "I have tasted poison for your king every day since I was seven years old. I know what dying looks like better than any healer in this valley, because I've rehearsed it daily. Get me the water."

He got the water.

* * *

The stranger drank in small, disciplined sips, the way soldiers drink, the way people drink who have learned that gulping kills. Her eyes found Yara's face and fixed there with an intensity that had nothing feverish in it. They were grey eyes, pale as the valley's winter sky, and they were *searching*.

"You're the taster," the woman rasped. Her voice sounded like paper burning slowly. "They call you — what do they call you. Little spoon. Little spoon of the king."

"They call me that behind my back," Yara said. "To my face they say 'miss.'"

Something that might have been a laugh moved the ruined side of the woman's mouth and clearly cost her dearly. "Good. Good. You're meant to be — " A cough took her, deep and wet, and when she pulled her hand away from her lips there was blood on it, and threaded through the blood, catching the flat white light, something that glittered. Silver. Fine as flour. Yara stared at it and felt a cold finger trace down her spine, because she had coughed silver dust into her own palm eleven days ago, after the poisoned dish, and had told no one, and had spent eleven nights telling herself she had imagined it.

"What is your name?" the woman asked.

"Yara."

"No." The grey eyes bored into hers. "Your true name. The one before the valley."

"I don't understand."

"I know." The woman's burned hand moved — slowly, with enormous effort, as though lifting it against a current — toward the bundle strapped across her back. It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, and even before it was opened Yara could feel it. Not see, not hear. *Feel.* A pressure behind her sternum, a hum in her teeth, the sensation of standing too near a struck bell. "I know you don't. That was the point of you. That was the mercy of you."

"Who are you?" Yara kept her voice level. Taster's training: never let them see the tremor. "What happened to you?"

"Fire," the woman said simply. "Fire that remembers being worshipped." Her fingers found the cord and fumbled. Yara reached out and cut it with the small knife she carried for apples and for emergencies, and unwrapped the oilcloth, and stopped breathing.

It was a map. Drawn on a hide she did not recognize — not calf, not goat, not any skin she'd seen in the castle larders or the tannery yards. It was the color of storm clouds over copper, and it was warm. Warm in the frozen gateway, warm as a living thing, and the lines inked upon it were not ink. They sat slightly *above* the surface, raised like scars, and they moved. Slowly, languidly, the way a sleeper shifts — a river line flexing, a mountain range settling its shoulders.

"Dragon-hide," the woman whispered. "Last of the last. Don't let them burn it. Don't let them *read* it carelessly. It reads you back."

The guards had drifted closer despite themselves. Doss stood with his mouth slightly open. Somewhere behind Yara someone whispered a word she hadn't heard since childhood — an old nurse's curse, *drake's leavings* — and someone else shushed them hard.

The woman's hand closed around Yara's wrist.

Her grip should have been weak. She was half-dead; Yara had catalogued the signs already, the blue lips, the shallow racing pulse, the blood in every breath. But her grip was iron, and it was *hot*, hot as a kettle left on the fire, and Yara felt the heat sink through her skin into the bones of her forearm and settle there, coiling, like something taking up residence.

"Listen." Each word cost the woman visibly now. Blood frosted at the corner of her mouth. "I carried it from the Ash Coast. Eleven months. Everyone who helped me is dead. Everyone who *saw* me is dead. There is a man — " her eyes flicked to the guards, and she lowered her voice to a thread, " — a man with too many names, and he has hounds that aren't hounds, and he knows the map passed the wards. He knows, because the wards *bent* for it, and he watches the wards. Do you understand? Your valley is not hidden anymore. It was never hidden. It was *owed*."

"You need the healers," Yara said. "You need — "

"I need you to listen." The grip tightened. The ember-lines in her burned skin brightened, and Yara realized with distant, clinical horror that they were pulsing in time with the woman's failing heart. "The map goes to the Ember Altar. East, past the Thornwood, past the mountain that smokes without smoke. There is a covenant older than your king's grandfather's grandfather, and it is breaking, and when it breaks fully the fire dies and everything the fire held up comes down. The harvests. The rivers. The *minds* of kings." Her mouth twisted. "You've seen it. Don't tell me you haven't. Your king talks to empty chairs. Your wheat comes up grey at the roots. The Stillness isn't peace, little spoon. It's a bandage on a wound that's killing the whole body."

"You're delirious."

"I'm *dying*. It's different. Delirium lies. Dying doesn't have time to." The woman pulled Yara closer, close enough that Yara could see her own reflection doubled in those grey eyes. "I was told to find the child of the covenant. Smuggled in. Raised outside magic so the choice would be clean. I was given a name to look for, and the name was hidden inside another name, and I have walked eleven months to stand in front of you and I am going to die here on this stone and I need you to hear the last thing I have to say."

The world had gone very quiet. Even the guards had stopped shifting. The valley's stillness pressed in like deep water.

"It knows your name," the woman said.

And then the light went out of her.

Not dramatically. The ember-lines dimmed like banked coals, the iron grip slackened to nothing, and the grey eyes lost their searching quality and became simply eyes, looking at a middle distance that had no middle and no distance. The cold came up off the stones and took her, and Yara knelt there holding a dead woman's wrist and a living map, and discovered that she was shaking.

* * *

They took the body to the mortuary house and the map to the garrison strongbox, and both decisions were made by other people, and both felt profoundly wrong to Yara.

"It's contraband," said the captain of the gate, a tidy man named Vessic who believed the world was best understood as a list of regulations. "Unregistered goods entering the valley. It'll be logged, examined by the king's assessors, and returned or destroyed according to statute."

"Destroyed," Yara repeated. "You want to destroy dragon-hide."

"I want to destroy *unregistered* dragon-hide." Vessic's mustache twitched. "Honestly, Delacroix, I expected better sense from you. It's a trick. Some lowland con-artist's prop. There are no dragons. There have never been dragons. That's the entire point of the valley."

"Then why does it move?"

Silence, around the guardroom table. The map lay between them, rewrapped in its oilcloth, and every person present had seen the lines shift, and every person present was pretending otherwise with the desperate commitment of people whose worldview had been contradicted before breakfast.

"Ink settles," Vessic said finally. "Temperature changes. You're dismissed, all of you. Delacroix, a word."

When the others had gone he leaned across the table, and his voice dropped into a register she'd never heard him use — not the regulation voice, not the friendly voice, but something older and warier.

"The woman talked to you. Before she died."

"A little."

"What did she say?"

"Why?"

"Because," Vessic said, "a courier arrived from the capital two days ago asking after strangers on the roads. Asking specifically about a woman traveling alone, burned, carrying documents. The inquiry bears the seal of the king's spymaster. A Lord Corvane." He watched Yara's face carefully. "You know the name?"

Everyone knew the name. Corvane was a story mothers used — *behave, or Corvane will know* — except that unlike most such stories, this one was true, and the knowing was real, and people who attracted Corvane's attention tended to become topics of quiet conversation rather than living guests.

"I've heard it," Yara said.

"He'll have agents here within the week. Whatever that woman was running from, it has long arms." Vessic straightened, regulation again, armor of procedure sliding back into place. "Stay out of it, Delacroix. Taste the king's soup. Marry a baker. Grow old. Some doors are locked for a reason."

That night Yara lay in her narrow bed above the kitchens and listened to the valley be silent, and thought about locked doors.

She thought about how she had spent her whole life on the inside of one, and how she had never once, until today, wondered what was on the other side. She thought about the taste of the outside world that had come through the gate this morning: char and flint-rain and blood full of silver. She thought about the way the dying woman had said *your valley is not hidden anymore, it was never hidden, it was owed* — and how the words had lodged under her ribs like a fishbone, working deeper every time she swallowed.

She thought, most of all, about the map.

* * *

She told herself she was only going to look at it.

The garrison strongbox room was guarded at night by exactly one man, and that man was Tam Weller, nineteen years old, in love with the baker's daughter, and constitutionally incapable of refusing either a bribe or a pretty argument. Yara brought neither. She brought a jar of the honey-brandy the cooks made secretly each autumn, and the simple truth: "I need to see the map, Tam. Ten minutes. If anyone asks, you checked on it and it was sealed."

Tam looked at the brandy, and at her face, and sighed the sigh of a young man recognizing his own doom. "Ten minutes."

The lock was old and honest and opened for her picks in under a minute, because Yara had learned locks the same way she'd learned poisons — patiently, professionally, on the theory that the person who protects a king should understand everything that can get past a door.

Inside the strongbox the air was warm.

That was the first thing. The strongbox was iron, in a stone room, in a valley where winter owned nine months of the year, and the air around the map was warm as a hand. The second thing was the sound — very faint, below hearing, more vibration than noise, like a purr the size of the horizon. Yara lifted the map out and unwrapped it on the guard table, and the moment the oilcloth fell away the purr deepened, and the raised lines began to move.

Not languidly now. Purposefully.

Rivers flowed. Mountain ranges shrugged and resettled. And across the whole western edge of the hide, where the valley should have been, a shape drew itself: a ring, a ward-circle, rendered in lines that pulsed like slow heartbeats — and then, at the ring's easternmost point, a thin new line began to scratch itself outward, eastward, like a crack spreading through glass.

A path. Out.

Yara's mouth went dry. She was a professional observer of dangerous things; it was the core of her trade. So she observed: the path ran east, as the dead woman had said. It marked the pass, and beyond the pass a ford, and beyond the ford a watchtower drawn in ruins, and beyond that a borderland rendered in dense stippling that her eye somehow understood, without being told, as *poison*. Farther east, symbols accumulated like weather: a wood marked with thorn-sigils, a road drawn *underground*, a smoking mountain. And at the far eastern margin, where the hide darkened almost to black, a single mark burned brighter than all the rest — a circle of flame around an empty center.

The Ember Altar. It looked less like a place on a map than like a wound in one.

"Eleven months," Yara murmured to the empty room. "Everyone who saw you is dead." She touched the hide's edge, lightly, with one fingertip — taster's caution, never take a full dose of the unknown — and the warmth of it climbed her finger like a climbing vine, gentle and insistent, and the map *shivered* under her touch, the whole hide rippling the way a horse ripples its skin to displace a fly.

And then, in the space of a heartbeat, every line on the map went still.

All of them. Rivers, roads, mountains, the ward-ring, the burning eastern mark — everything froze, mid-motion, the way a household freezes when the master's footsteps sound unexpectedly in the hall. The silence in the strongbox room became total, became pressure, became the specific silence of a held breath.

Slowly, deliberately, a new line began to draw itself.

It started at the valley's ring and curled inward, inward, tracing streets Yara knew, the processional way, the market square, the lane of the kitchens — and stopped. Beneath the gatehouse. Beneath the guardroom. Beneath the strongbox.

Beneath her fingertip.

The line thickened. Brightened. And beside it, rising to the surface of the hide like a bruise coming up through skin, letters assembled themselves out of the moving ink — not in any script Yara knew, and yet legible anyway, the meaning arriving directly behind her eyes rather than through them:

**IT KNOWS YOUR NAME.**

The words hung there. Then, gently, horribly, the ink beneath them rearranged one final time, and formed a single word more:

**YARA.**

She did not remember stepping back. She was suddenly against the far wall with the guard table between her and the map, her heart slamming, her breath coming fast and white in the cold air — and the map lay there, warm and patient and alive, its lines resuming their slow motion as though nothing had happened, a river flowing, a mountain settling, the path east waiting, open, patient, like a door held politely ajar.

Outside, the valley lay silent as it had lain silent for nineteen years. Above the rooftops the smoke rose straight and stopped. Somewhere in the capital, a man with too many names was already turning his gaze toward the wards.

Yara looked at the map for a long time.

Then she wrapped it back in its oilcloth with steady hands — taster's hands, hands trained not to shake — returned it to the strongbox, relocked the lock, thanked Tam, and walked back through the sleeping kitchens to her narrow bed.

She did not sleep. She lay listening to the terrible, beloved silence of the only home she had ever known, and understood, with the clarity that comes at the bottom of the night, that the silence had never been peace. It had been a held breath. And held breaths end.

By dawn she had packed a bag, told no one, and stood at the window watching the eastern pass, where the sky was beginning — very faintly, for the first time in living memory — to move.
