## 3. What the Valley Keeps Out

The Royal Archive of the Valley of Stillness occupied the oldest wing of the castle, a place of cold stone and colder silences where the sun was permitted only through slits of leaded glass, as if even daylight had to be searched before entry. Yara had been inside it perhaps four times in nineteen years — once to deliver a tray of honeyed pears to the archivist, once to hide from a thunderstorm she'd been ashamed of fearing, and twice by accident.

She came now on purpose, with her bag hidden in the laundry chute and a story prepared about fetching a recipe for the king's name-day feast.

The archivist was a dried-apple of a man named Ossen who had served three generations of royalty and remembered none of them fondly. He looked up from his ledger when she entered, and his eyes did the thing all eyes in the valley did when they landed on her: flicked to her hands, then away, as though tasting were contagious.

"Food-taster," he said. "We don't keep recipes here."

"You keep everything," Yara said. "That's what Tam says. He says there are folios down here older than the wards themselves."

"Tam," said Ossen, "flatters the collection." But he said it the way proud men deny things, and Yara smiled the smile she used on suspicious cooks — open, guileless, slightly hungry — and asked to see whatever he'd allow her to see.

What he allowed her was a reading table near the window, a lamp, and access to the open shelves: genealogies, harvest ledgers, the collected toasts of dead ambassadors. She took them all and read them fast, the way she read everything, cataloguing. It was what her mind did. Nineteen years of tasting had trained her to hold a hundred flavors at once and know which one didn't belong, and within two hours she knew exactly which flavor was missing from the valley's history.

Everything began *after*.

The founding of the Valley of Stillness was recorded — she found it in six separate volumes, each agreeing with the others so perfectly it felt rehearsed. The old realm had been full of magic, the histories said, and magic was dangerous, and so the wise king of that age — no name given, just *the wise king*, as though kings came pre-labeled like jars — had raised the wards and gathered his people inside, and the valley had known peace ever since.

Peace ever since. Two hundred years of peace ever since, and not one volume said what happened *before* the wards went up. Not one named the wise king. Not one mentioned what the people had been saved *from* — or what they'd been saved *at*.

Yara set down the sixth volume and sat very still, listening to dust settle.

In the kitchens she had learned that the most dangerous dish on any table was never the strange one. It was the familiar one, the one everyone ate without looking, because familiarity was where you hid the poison. Someone had seasoned these histories the same way. Someone had wanted the valley's children to grow up swallowing *peace ever since* without ever tasting what lay underneath.

She thought of the burned woman at the gate. *It knows your name.* She thought of the map hidden in her strongbox, its ink crawling like something alive, tracing roads through country Yara had never seen except in dreams she'd always assumed were ordinary dreams.

"I need the restricted shelves," she said to Ossen.

The archivist's pen stopped moving.

"Do you," he said.

"The king's name-day feast," Yara said smoothly. "He wants the menu from the first year of his grandfather's reign. A tradition restored." It was a good lie. She'd had practice; tasters lied constantly, cheerfully, to cooks and stewards and panicking underlings — *no, the soup is fine, I only made a face because of the pepper.*

Ossen studied her for a long moment. Then he rose, keys whispering, and led her past the open shelves into a corridor where the air changed — cooler, drier, tasting faintly of iron and old smoke. The door at its end was black oak banded in dull metal, and carved above the lintel, so worn it was nearly smooth, was a symbol Yara had seen somewhere before and couldn't place: a circle broken by a flame.

Her birthmark prickled. The stain on her shoulder blade, the one the bath-house women called a scald, the one shaped like nothing and therefore like anything — it warmed against her shift as though remembering fire.

"No lamps past this point," Ossen said. "No copies. Nothing leaves." He unlocked the door and stood aside, and there was something in his face she hadn't expected: fear. Not of her. Of the room.

She went in alone.

* * *

The restricted archive was smaller than she'd imagined — one vaulted chamber, shelves half-empty, as though the room had been robbed slowly and officially for generations. Gaps in the rows wore little brass plates with catalogue numbers, and beneath many of the numbers, small cards in a precise antique hand: *Removed by order of the Crown. Reason: redundant.* Redundant. Dozens of folios, all redundant, all removed.

Whoever had scrubbed the valley's memory had done it thoroughly and recently enough to leave receipts.

Yara worked the shelves the way she worked a banquet — systematically, patiently, tasting everything. Most of what remained was harmless dross: tax rolls, weather records, a treatise on beekeeping. But bees, she noted, appeared again and again, in text after text, always in the same odd phrase — *the hives went quiet when the wards went up* — repeated across unrelated documents like a refrain someone had forgotten to remove. As if the silence of the valley had once been worth recording, and the recorders had missed one thread while pulling out all the others.

On the lowest shelf, wedged behind a false back that some long-dead hand had built and some longer-dead hand had forgotten, she found a folio bound in grey leather gone soft as skin.

It had no brass plate. No catalogue number. It had simply been walled in, hidden rather than removed — which meant whoever hid it hadn't wanted it destroyed. They'd wanted it *kept*. Waiting.

Yara carried it to the center of the floor, where a shaft of leaded light fell like a blade, and opened it.

The handwriting was old but urgent, the letters leaning forward as if running. It was a personal account, a chronicle kept by someone who signed themselves only *S., Warden of the Eastern Gate*. And it told the story the six clean volumes had agreed so perfectly to omit.

There had been a covenant. Not a war the wise king won — a *pact*, sealed between humankind and the dragons in fire, at an altar called the Ember Altar, in the age when dragons still flew openly over the eastern realms. The covenant had terms. S. wrote them plainly, without decoration, the way people write things they're afraid of forgetting: the dragons would lend their fire to keep the land alive — the crops, the rivers, the seasons themselves — and in exchange, humanity would pay. S. did not say what the payment was. That page had been cut out, long ago, with a sharp blade and a steady hand. But the shape of the missing page remained, a thin dark slot in the binding like a wound, and around it the surviving text circled and circled, a tongue probing a broken tooth:

*—the queens have misunderstood it for three generations, and each misunderstanding costs us—*

*—the fire dims where the terms go unpaid. The eastern orchards first. Then the river—*

*—she refuses. Gods forgive me, I cannot make her understand that the covenant does not want cruelty, it wants—*

And then, on the last written page, the ink going hurried, smeared, desperate:

*The pact has shattered. The wards are our last work — not to keep magic out, but to keep ONE CHILD safe from what comes next. We raise her inside the Stillness, where no power can find her or shape her, so that when she is grown she may CHOOSE freely, unforced by oaths laid on her blood before her birth. If you read this, guard her. Guard her without telling her why, for a choice made in ignorance of its weight is no choice at all, and yet she must not be burdened too soon. Let her be ordinary. Let her be free. When the time comes, the fire will know her.*

Below that, in different ink, added later by a shakier hand:

*The child lives. She does not know. May she never need to.*

Yara read the last line four times. The lamp flame — the one flame allowed in the restricted room, small and blue-hearted — seemed to lean toward her, and the birthmark on her shoulder blade burned like a brand held close, and she understood, with the flat calm that had carried her through every poisoning, that the burned woman at the gate had died carrying a map to a destiny Yara had been dressed in swaddling clothes to escape.

*Let her be ordinary. Let her be free.*

Someone had decided her ordinariness for her. Someone had built a cage of quiet years and called it mercy, and the cage had worked so well that Yara had lived nineteen years inside it calling it home.

She copied the surviving pages onto scraps of recipe paper she'd brought for the purpose, her taster's hand steady even now, and slid the folio back behind the false wall. Some instincts ran deeper than revelation. You didn't leave poison sitting out on the counter.

When she emerged, blinking, into the main archive, Ossen was not alone.

* * *

The man standing at the reading table wore grey the way other men wore armor. He was neither tall nor short, young nor old; he was the kind of man whose features seemed chosen to be forgettable, which was itself a kind of beauty, the way perfect stillness was a kind of motion. A single silver pin shaped like a closed eye sat at his collar.

Corvane. Spymaster to the king. Keeper of the Closed Eye.

Yara had seen him perhaps ten times in her life, always at a distance, always at the edges of throne-room ceremonies, and every time she'd felt the peculiar sensation of being *read* — the way she read dishes, quick and total and without sentiment. Now that sensation arrived in person, wearing a mild smile.

"Lady Delacroix," he said. She wasn't a lady. He knew she wasn't a lady. That was the greeting's whole message.

"Taster Delacroix," she corrected pleasantly. "Lord Corvane. Do you take honey in your tea? I ask professionally."

Something flickered behind the forgettable face — surprise, quickly filed away. "I don't drink tea."

"Smart man." She let her eyes travel meaningfully toward the restricted corridor, then back. "Forgive my curiosity. I'm researching the name-day feast. The king wants authenticity."

"The king," Corvane said, "wants many things lately." He turned a page of the ledger on the table without looking at it. "Tell me about the woman at the gate."

Here it was. She'd known it would come; the whole castle had known since the stranger died, the way a kitchen knows when a dish has gone off — by smell, by silence, by the way the guards stopped joking. "Burned half to death," Yara said. "Delirious. Died by morning. The physiker's report will tell you more than I can."

"The physiker's report tells me she died of burns," Corvane said. "It doesn't tell me why, in her last hour, she fought past four guardsmen to reach *you*. Of all the people in that courtyard. Why you, Taster?"

"Because I was closest."

"Were you?" He said it lightly, almost kindly, and that was the worst part — the kindness. Corvane's questions came coated like pills, easy to swallow, working on you after. "A stranger crawls out of the wastes, half-cremated, having crossed wards that nothing crosses, and spends her final strength reaching for the food-taster. And then, curiously, that same night, the food-taster visits the archive for the first time in years. Asking after old reigns. Old menus." He smiled. "You'll forgive me. Suspicion is my profession. Yours is more honest — you taste what might kill you. I merely taste what might kill the king."

"And what do I taste like, Lord Corvane?" Yara asked, and heard her own voice go quiet and level, the voice she used over a suspect dish. "Poison?"

"I haven't decided." He closed the ledger. "You should know that the gate guards' testimony has been collected. The physiker's notes as well. And the strongbox in the kitchens—" he paused, delicately, "—has an interesting lock. Interesting locks interest me."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree, the way it did when the poison hit. Yara kept her face pleasant, her breathing even, her hands loose at her sides. *Taster's hands. Taster's face.* Inside, calculations ran like fever: how long since she'd relocked the box; whether Tam had noticed the oilcloth; whether the map could be moved tonight, now, before—

"Of course," she said, "if the crown wishes to inspect the kitchens, I'll arrange it personally. I inspect everything personally. It's rather the point of me."

"Mm," said Corvane. He bowed — correctly, shallowly, a bow calibrated to end the conversation while implying it had never ended at all. "Enjoy your research, Taster. The king values... tradition. I'm sure your feast will be very authentic." At the door he stopped, without turning. "One more thing. The symbol over the restricted room. The broken circle and the flame. Did it look familiar to you?"

Every hair on Yara's arms rose.

"No," she said.

"Yes," said Corvain — no, Corvane, she corrected herself, watching him go, *Corvane* — "I imagine it wouldn't. Good day."

* * *

She did not go back to the kitchens. She went the long way, through the herb garden where the beds grew in obedient rows and nothing had thorns or toxins or teeth, because the wards kept such things out along with everything else. Safe plants. Safe soil. Safe, silent, suffocating valley.

*Let her be ordinary. Let her be free.*

She stood among the tame herbs and finally let herself feel it, all of it, the way she'd been taught never to feel anything on duty: the fury first, white and total — *they chose for me, they built my whole life around a lie of kindness* — and then, underneath the fury, worse, the grief. Because the lie had been *good*. The kitchens were good. Tam's terrible singing and Marta's honey-cakes and the great warm beast of the ovens on winter mornings — all of it real, all of it loved, and all of it, she understood now, a wall as much as a home. The wards didn't just keep magic out. They'd kept *her* in, gently, for nineteen years, the way you keep a candle in a lantern so the wind can't reach it.

Or so the wind can't see it.

She pressed her palm flat against her shoulder blade, over the burning mark, and thought about the last line of the warden's chronicle. *May she never need to know.* Well. She needed to now. The dying woman had spent her final strength making sure of it, and Corvane was already pulling threads, and the sky over the eastern pass was moving in ways the sky had never moved.

The comfort of home had curdled. She could taste it — that was the curse of her gift, she could taste everything, even this: the valley's sweetness gone thick and wrong on her tongue, milk left out too long, safety spoiling into captivity.

By full dark she had retrieved her bag from the laundry chute, moved the map from the strongbox into the lining of her coat — Corvane could pick any lock he liked and find only salt, which would at least confuse him beautifully — and stood in the doorway of the sleeping kitchens, memorizing it. The hanging copper pots. The banked ovens breathing their last warmth. Marta's shawl over her chair. Tam's stool pushed in neat, waiting for morning.

They'd set a place for her tomorrow. They always did. She was suddenly, unbearably certain of it, and equally certain she couldn't be there when they did.

"Forgive me," she whispered to the dark, to all of them, to the wise dead king and the frightened warden and the mother she'd never met who had smuggled her behind walls so she could choose freely — *this is me choosing* — "but you don't get to decide what my freedom looks like anymore. Any of you."

Then the bell in the gate tower rang — once, twice, a third time, the alarm pattern, ragged and wrong — and torchlight bloomed in the courtyard below, and she heard boots on stone, many boots, moving with purpose toward the kitchens.

Toward her.

Yara pulled her hood up, slipped out through the smoke-door that every kitchen child knew and no guard ever remembered, and ran for the eastern wall, where the wards ended and the world — vast, poisoned, burning, awake — began.
