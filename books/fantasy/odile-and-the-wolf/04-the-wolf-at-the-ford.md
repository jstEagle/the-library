## 4. The Wolf at the Ford

The bell rang wrong.

Yara had lived her whole life by the sound of the castle bells — the low bronze toll of the hour, the bright triple chime that called the kitchens to the morning bake. This was none of those. This was the alarm bell in the gate tower, rung hard and fast, the way it was only ever rung when someone important wanted a door closed.

She was elbow-deep in flour when she heard it.

"Yara." Cook's voice came from the doorway of the scullery, tight as a drawn wire. "Man from the keep. Asking after you. Not asking, neither — *demanding*."

She wiped her hands on her apron slowly, the way she did everything in the kitchen: deliberately, without waste. Through the window over the wash basin she could see the courtyard below, and crossing it with two guards flanking him, a tall figure in dove-grey with a silver pin at his collar shaped like an open eye.

Corvane. The king's spymaster had come to the kitchens himself. That was not how questions were asked of servants. That was how arrests were dressed up as conversations.

"The back stair," Cook said quietly. She didn't ask what Yara had done. Twenty years of feeding a castle taught you which storms to shelter people from. "Marta's got your bundle already. Go through the smoke-house, out past the tannery ditch. Don't stop till you're past the boundary stones."

"The boundary—" Yara's throat closed. The valley's edge wasn't a place you went. It was a wall made of law and fear, older than anyone living. Nothing crossed it. Nothing came back across it either. "Cook, I haven't done anything."

"I know it." The old woman's eyes were wet and furious at once. "That's what makes it worse, girl. Men like that don't come down for what you've *done*."

Marta was waiting at the smoke-house door with a canvas roll — bread, hard cheese, a skin of water, and pressed into the fold, the small knife Yara used for trimming roots, its handle worn smooth by four years of her grip. Marta was fifteen and cried easily, but she didn't cry now. She just held the bundle out and said, "You come back and taste my wedding pie. Promise."

"I promise," Yara said, and lied, because the truth was she had no idea what waited past the stones, and promises made into fog were still promises if you meant them.

Then she ran.

* * *

The valley kept its secrets well, but it also kept its paths, and Yara knew them the way she knew the grain stores — by years of quiet attention no one had thought to forbid. She went up through the terraces where the vineyard workers wouldn't look twice at a kitchen girl moving fast, cut along the irrigation channel where the water noise covered her breathing, and came down at last into the birch wood that climbed toward the western ridge.

Behind her, faint and getting less faint, dogs.

Not hounds on a scent — Corvane would be more careful than that, at least at first. These were the valley's own shepherd dogs, alarmed by strangers, and every bark told her exactly where the searchers were. She catalogued them the way she catalogued flavors: three voices, near the mill; one deeper, on the orchard road; silence to the west, which meant the west was where they weren't. Yet.

The map rode against her chest, wrapped in oilcloth beneath her shirt, drawn on dragon-hide that moved when she wasn't looking. She'd felt it shift against her ribs all night, ink crawling like something alive under her palm. Three days since the stranger died at the gate with her wrist in a grip gone cold, saying *it knows your name*, and Yara had spent those three days doing what she always did with dangerous things: learning them.

What she'd learned had frightened her worse than Corvane.

The folio in the sealed archive. The covenant sealed in fire. A child smuggled inside the wards. And the birthmark on her shoulder blade that Maren-the-herbwoman's grandmother had once squinted at and called *an old stain, nothing*. It wasn't a stain. She'd known it wasn't a stain since the ink on the hide had traced itself into the same shape.

The ridge path narrowed. Below her, the river ran shallow and loud over white stones — the Stillwater, which gave the valley its name, which turned back at the boundary line as if it too had been told to stay. She could see the ford now, and beyond it the standing stones, and beyond the stones—

Nothing. Fog. Not weather-fog. A wall of pale grey that began precisely where the last boundary stone stood, soft-edged and absolute, like the world simply declining to continue.

Her legs stopped working before her mind decided anything.

She stood on the bank with the water roaring over her boots' reflection and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she was about to do the one thing nineteen years of training had never covered. She could survive poison. She could not survive the unknown. No one had ever taught her how, because no one in the valley was ever supposed to face it.

The dogs sounded again — closer now, and different. Deeper voices. Real hounds. Corvane had stopped being careful.

Yara looked at the fog wall once, the way you look at a cup you've been handed and know is poisoned, and stepped into the river.

The water was cold enough to hurt. The stones rolled under her feet. Halfway across, the current grabbed her shins and tried to argue, and she leaned into it, teeth gritted, the bundle strapped high on her back, until her boot found the far gravel and hauled her up onto the far bank —

— and the world changed.

It was not dramatic. That was the terrible part. There was no flash, no thunderclap. One breath she stood in the Valley of Stillness, where magic could not follow and nothing had surprised her since childhood. The next breath she stood somewhere else, and the somewhere else was *loud*. Not sound-loud. Presence-loud. The grass under her hand was green in a way that seemed opinionated. The air tasted of things she had no names for — iron and honey and old rain and something underneath it all like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. Her birthmark burned cold against her shoulder blade.

She had crossed. And the first thing magic did, having finally gotten its hands on her, was try to kill her.

They came out of the treeline without a sound — three shapes in grey-green leathers, faces masked, crossbows already levelled. Not Corvane's men. Wrong build, wrong gear, bows of dark wood she didn't recognize. Professionals from some other country's nightmare.

"Give us the hide," the nearest one said. Voice flat as slate. "Walk away, taster-girl. We know what you are. We don't care. We just want the map."

Yara's body did what nineteen years of poison-training had built it to do: it went very calm and very still while her mind raced through options and found the shelf bare. She had a root-trimming knife. They had three crossbows. The arithmetic was insulting.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, because stalling cost nothing.

"You bled on it," the man said. "When the courier died. It tasted you. Now it only answers to you, and we can't have that." He raised his crossbow a fraction. "Last kindness. Walk away."

She believed he meant it as kindness. That was somehow the worst detail yet.

The bolt left the string with a sound like a snapped branch.

Yara threw herself sideways — too slow, always going to be too slow, she was a food-taster not a soldier — and the bolt never reached her, because something enormous hit the space between them like a landslide given intent.

A wolf.

Not a wolf. The word was too small. It was the size of a plow horse, grey as storm-cloud over slate, and it moved wrong for its size — fluid, silent, *fast* — and it took the first hunter off his feet with a shoulder-check that Yara heard crack. The second man loosed wildly and the bolt vanished into fur without effect, and then the wolf was on him, and the third man did the single sensible thing available to anyone in this situation, which was to drop his weapon and run.

The wolf let him go. That frightened Yara more than the killing would have. It watched the man crash away through the undergrowth with pale amber eyes full of something almost like boredom, then turned its great head toward her.

Yara stood very still in the shallows of terror, knife out, point shaking slightly.

"Don't," said the wolf, in a voice like a man's — deep, dry, and profoundly unimpressed. "You'll put someone's eye out. Yours, probably."

"You talk," Yara said. Her voice came out steadier than her hands, which she counted as a professional victory.

"I talk. You scream. I've seen the pattern before." The wolf sat back on its haunches, and even sitting it stood taller than she did. Up close its coat was shot through with silver, and old scars laced one foreleg, and there was an expression on its face that Yara recognized from every head cook who'd ever caught her substituting an ingredient: disappointment, worn smooth by long practice. "Let me guess. You're the bearer."

"I'm—"

"The map. Dragon-hide. Moves when you're not looking. Says yes or no, we can skip the part where you lie badly."

Yara's chin came up despite everything. "I don't lie badly."

"No?" The wolf tilted its head. "Your heartbeat's audible from here. You're holding that knife like it's a spoon. And when I killed that man's friend, you looked at me like I was the poison in the dish." He sighed, a vast, rumbling sound. "Nineteen years they hid you, and nobody taught you anything useful."

Something in Yara went hot and stubborn, the same thing that had carried her through every tasting, every swallowed death, every night she'd coughed into her palm alone. "I taught myself plenty."

"Mm." The wolf rose and shook himself, silver dust scattering. "Fen," he said, as if the word cost him. "Bound to guard the bearer of the map. Which makes you my charge, gods help us both. Can you walk?"

"Can you *not* be enormous?"

"Funny," Fen said, without any indication whatsoever that he'd found it funny. He turned toward the treeline, then paused, looking back at her over one massive shoulder. His amber eyes narrowed.

"What?"

"You crossed the ford," he said slowly. "Alone. With hunters behind you and no idea what was ahead. Into a wall of fog that swallows most people's courage before their feet touch the water."

"I didn't have a choice."

"There's always a choice. Most people make the other one." Something shifted in his expression — not warmth, exactly. Recalibration. "Huh. Maybe there's hope for you after all."

"Maybe there's hope for *you*," Yara said, "if you learn to say thank-you."

"For what?"

"I don't know. Existing. Being rude at me instead of eating me."

Fen stared at her for a long moment. Then, incredibly, the great grey wolf laughed — a low, rough sound like distant rockfall, entirely at odds with his mournful dignity.

"Oh, this is going to be miserable," he said, and started walking. "Keep up, taster. The ones who ran will bring more, and the next lot won't be kind enough to announce themselves. We have ground to cover before dark, and you look like you haven't eaten properly in a week."

"I eat constantly. It's my *job*."

"Your job," said Fen, "was dying politely so rich men could live. From here on, your job is staying alive. Try to be brave about it. Or failing that," he added, glancing back, amber eyes catching the strange loud light of the world beyond the valley, "try to be interesting."

Yara tightened her grip on the bundle, touched the hidden shape of the map against her ribs — warm now, almost purring — and followed the talking wolf into a forest that hummed with a power she could feel watching her back.

Somewhere behind her, the Valley of Stillness closed its gates. Somewhere ahead, the first waypoint waited, though neither of them knew yet what it would cost to reach it.

She was mortal, hunted, and utterly out of her depth.

For the first time in nineteen years, Yara Delacroix felt completely, terrifyingly awake.
