## 5. Ash and Rations

The girl walked like a soldier who had never been taught she was one.

Fen had watched a thousand travelers cross the boundary stones in his long, cursed years — pilgrims and poachers, knights and thieves — and every one of them announced themselves within the first mile. The proud walked with their chins up and their eyes on the horizon. The frightened hunched, hands hovering near knives they didn't know how to use. The clever moved like water, always seeking the path of least resistance.

Yara Delacroix did none of these things.

She walked the way a person walks when they have spent their whole life being watched for signs of weakness. Spine straight but shoulders loose. Steps measured, never hurried, even when the forest darkened around them and the trees began to whisper in voices that had no business belonging to trees. She did not look back at the valley more than three times — Fen counted — and each time her jaw tightened a little more, as if she were swallowing something large and jagged and refusing to let it show.

*Stubborn*, he thought. *Underfed. Alone. And lying to herself about all three.*

He padded ahead of her through the undergrowth, keeping his grey bulk between her and the treeline, and catalogued her the way she was so carefully not cataloguing him. It was a habit of his kind of vigilance: know the charge's strengths before you learn her weaknesses, because strengths are what you build a defense upon.

Her strengths, as far as he could see in the first day, were these: she did not complain. Not once. Her boots were thin-soled castle issue and the forest floor was all roots and stone, and she said nothing. When a thornbush raked her forearm she hissed once, quietly, wrapped the scratch with a strip torn from her sleeve, and kept walking. When the sun sank and the temperature dropped and she visibly began to shiver, she adjusted her pace instead of asking for rest, because asking would have been admitting need, and admitting need would have meant owing him something.

Ah. There it was. Fen had seen that particular pride before, worn by another bearer, another lifetime ago, and it had gotten her killed.

"Stop," he said.

She stopped. Of course she stopped — she'd learned in half a day that when the wolf spoke in that voice, there was a reason. But her chin came up, defiant, ready to argue on principle.

"You're limping."

"I am not."

"Your left foot has favored your right for the last two miles. You've been rationing your steps the way you'd ration bread." He sat back on his haunches, tail curling around his paws, and let the amber weight of his gaze settle on her. "We camp here. There's water forty paces east. I'll hunt."

"I can—"

"You can't hunt. You can't fight. You can't navigate a forest that rearranges itself out of spite." He turned away before she could see anything in his face that might be mistaken for kindness; kindness made people argue harder. "What you *can* do is eat, sleep, and stay alive until we reach the first waypoint. Do those three things and you'll have done enough."

She stood there a moment, arms crossed over the bundle at her chest, and Fen braced himself for the argument.

Instead she said, "Forty paces east?"

"East. Follow the sound."

And she went — without thanks, without complaint, without a single wasted word — and Fen watched her go and felt the first cold thread of something he refused to name wind through his chest.

*Don't*, he told himself. *You know how this ends. You've seen how this ends.*

But his paws were already carrying him into the dark after a rabbit, because a starving bearer was a dead bearer, and this one, whatever else she was, intended to live.

* * *

It was the plants that gave her away.

Fen returned near full dark with a brace of rabbits and found Yara kneeling at the edge of the campsite, firewood gathered in a neat stack beside her, holding a leaf up to the last grey light. She turned it over. Sniffed it. Touched its stem to her wrist and waited, watching the skin.

Then she picked a second leaf from a different plant, tore a sliver from its edge, and — before Fen could get a single word out — put it on her tongue.

"Don't—" he started.

She chewed. Swallowed. Waited, counting under her breath, lips moving. Then she nodded to herself, plucked three more leaves of the same plant, and tucked them into a fold of her cloak like a woman storing coins.

Fen dropped the rabbits. "Are you *insane?*"

"It's wood sorrel." She didn't even look up. "Oxalic acid, mild. Safe in small quantities, and it has vitamin C, which matters if we're out here longer than a month." She held up the first leaf, the one she'd only tested against her wrist. "This one I don't know. No reaction on my skin, but dermal contact proves nothing. I'll try a fragment tomorrow if nothing better turns up."

"You put a *strange plant in your mouth.*"

"I put a strange plant in my mouth after checking it four ways." Now she looked up, and in the dying light her eyes had a flat, professional calm that made the fur along his spine lift. "I was the king's food-taster for six years, wolf. I have eaten eleven hundred dishes designed to kill someone else. My body is a ledger of every poison that ever failed to kill me, and I keep meticulous accounts." She went back to sorting leaves. "One wild sorrel leaf is the least dangerous thing I've swallowed since Tuesday."

Fen stared at her.

In nineteen years of guarding, guiding, and failing the bearers of the map, he had worked with warriors and scholars, saints and fanatics. He had never once worked with a professional. This girl — this half-starved, stubborn, nineteen-year-old kitchen servant — was doing field survival triage with the methodical calm of a battlefield surgeon, and she'd apparently been doing it since the moment they crossed the wards, while he'd been busy patrolling the perimeter and feeling sorry for himself.

She wasn't out of her depth at all, he realized. She was drowning quietly, competently, and taking notes on the water.

"...There are mushrooms by the stream," he said finally, grudgingly. "Brown caps, white gills. They're safe. I've eaten them."

"Eaten them as a wolf or as—" She caught herself. "You've been human."

"A long time ago." He nudged the rabbits toward her with his nose. "Eat. All of it. You're underfed, and I won't have you fainting on a mountainside because you were too proud to finish a meal."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, quickly buried, then a wariness that had nothing to do with him and everything to do with whoever had taught her that kindness came with an invoice. She took the smallest rabbit. Left the larger one sitting there. Fen sighed, picked it up, and set it directly in front of her.

"The larger one," he said. "That wasn't a suggestion."

They ate in silence while the fire caught and the forest hummed its tuneless song. And Fen watched her eat — really watched — and saw what he should have seen at the gate: the way she ate slowly, deliberately, making each bite count, the way of someone who had grown up hungry among plenty. The way she automatically saved a portion of the sorrel leaves, dried against her body heat. The way her eyes kept moving, even now, mapping exits, cataloguing shadows, noting which way the wind carried scent.

*She's been surviving her whole life,* he thought. *She just didn't know she was training for this.*

The map, wrapped against her ribs, gave off a faint warmth he could feel from where he lay — a low, living pulse, like a heart slowed almost to stillness. It liked her. That was either very good news or very bad, and Fen had learned, painfully, that with this map it was usually both.

* * *

They reached the watchtower on the third morning.

Fen smelled it before they saw it — old stone, older moss, and underneath, sharp and wrong: iron. Blood gone to rust. Smoke long dead but not long enough.

He tried to steer her wide of it. "The waypoint is beyond the ridge. We don't need to—"

"The map says the tower." Yara had the hide unrolled across a flat rock, and the ink was moving again, crawling toward a mark that hadn't been there yesterday: a small, patient spiral, drawn in lines of gold that pulsed like embers. "It's the first waypoint. It knows we're close." She rolled it up before the ink could finish whatever it was drawing. "Besides, you said others might be hunting this. I'd rather see them coming from high ground than meet them in a hollow."

Which was, annoyingly, sound tactical reasoning from a girl whose only weapon was a paring knife.

The tower stood on the ridge like a broken tooth — a circle of blackened stone three stories tall, its top sheared away as if something enormous had bitten it. Ivy had taken the walls. Birds nested in the arrow-slits. It should have been merely sad, the way all ruins are sad, a monument to some forgotten border war.

It was not merely sad.

Fen stopped at the tree line and would not go closer, and Yara, to her credit, noticed immediately.

"What is it?"

"Burned," he said. "Not weather-burned. *Fire*-burned. Dragon-fire burned, a long time ago, and the stone remembers." His hackles were up and he couldn't make them lie down. "Places the fire touched hold its echo. To me, standing here is like standing next to a struck bell."

"You could wait outside."

"I could." He didn't move.

She looked at him for a long moment — this girl who owed him nothing and trusted him less — and then she said, quietly, "Come inside or don't. But decide. Standing at the edge gets us neither safety nor answers."

So he went inside, ears flat, teeth aching, into a ruin that hummed against his bones like an old wound, because she was right, and because leaving her alone in a place that smelled of rusted blood was unthinkable.

The evidence was everywhere, once they knew to look.

A campsite in the tower's shadow, cold for perhaps two weeks. Bedrolls — two sets. A cookfire built with the neat efficiency of trained soldiers. And scattered through the ash: boot prints, too many, overlapping, some days old and some fresher. A broken crossbow bolt, fletched in black and silver.

Corvane's colors.

"He sent hunters across the boundary already." Yara crouched over the bolt, turning it in her fingers. "Fast. Faster than I thought."

"Corvane is always faster than you think." Fen circled the campsite, nose down. "These aren't his best. His best travel lighter and leave nothing." He paused at the edge of the ash pit. Buried shallowly beneath it, half-scraped clear by animals, was something small and pale.

He dug it free with a paw.

A hand. Just a hand, severed cleanly at the wrist, browned by time, the fingers curled around nothing.

Yara went very still. Fen watched her take it in — watched her do the thing she must have learned in a castle full of poisoners and courtiers, the thing that separated survivors from victims: she let herself feel the horror for exactly three seconds, and then she folded it away and started thinking.

"One of the hunters," she said. "Or one of theirs. Someone got here first, found something, and the others..." She looked at the overlapping boot prints again, reading them now with new eyes. "No. Look. These prints converge from the north — a group arriving. These scatter south, running. And these—" she pointed to a single line of prints, deliberate, unhurried, leading up the ruined stair, "—one person stayed calm."

"The one who took the hand's owner."

"The one who took whatever the hand was holding." Yara rose, and her voice had gone flat and careful. "The map has waypoints. If the map has waypoints, other maps might too. Or keys. Or—" She stopped. Her hand drifted to her ribs, to the hidden warmth there. "Or instructions about me."

The tower's interior was a throat of broken stairs and bird filth, and at the top, in the open ring where the roof had been sheared away, they found what the calm man had left behind.

A message, carved into the stone in letters burned rather than cut — seared into the rock by heat no torch could produce:

**THE BEARER WALKS TO US. LET HER COME. WE ARE PATIENT.**

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, in ordinary scratched letters:

*She is the seventh. Six are ash.*

Fen read it twice. The second time, his legs nearly gave.

Yara read it once. "Six," she said. "Six bearers before me." She turned, and her eyes found his, and they were not afraid, which was somehow worse. "You knew."

"I knew."

"How many did you guard?"

The wind moved through the sheared roof. Somewhere below, a bird complained at the intrusion.

"All of them," Fen said. "I have guarded every bearer since the covenant broke. I found the first in a burning village and carried her to the border myself. I taught the third to fight. The fifth — the fifth I loved like a daughter." His voice came out rougher than he intended, a man's grief wearing a wolf's throat. "The sixth was a boy of sixteen who reminded me of myself. I was scouting ahead when they took him. Half a day. Half a day, Yara, and I came back to ash and this—" he flung his head toward the carved words, "—and I have been walking the boundaries ever since, waiting for the map to choose again, telling myself that this time—"

"This time you'd fail slower?"

The words landed like a slap. Fen flinched — actually flinched, a wolf baring his belly to a blade — and Yara stood there with her arms crossed and her chin up and her terrible, unsparing clarity.

"No," he said. "This time I'd succeed. Or die trying. Those are the only outcomes left to me."

"That's not an outcome. That's a wish." She crouched, picked up a handful of ash from the sixth bearer's pyre, and let it run through her fingers. "You want to know why I'm not afraid of those words?" She nodded at the burned message. "Because whoever wrote them wants me scared. Fear makes people run, and running people get caught. I spent six years eating death one bite at a time, wolf. I know exactly how fear tastes, and I refuse to swallow it on command." She stood, brushing ash from her palms. "Six bearers died. Fine. That means six sets of mistakes I can learn from, if you'll tell me about them. Every one. What they did wrong, what you did wrong, what the hunters did right."

Fen stared at her.

Somewhere in his long, gray, guilt-clogged centuries, something shifted — some load-bearing grief giving way, not breaking, but *moving*, making room.

"They'll want to know," he said at last, "that the hunters always strike at water. Water crossings. Remember that."

"Water crossings. Noted." She was already moving toward the stair, already scanning the treeline, already alive. "Come on, then. Daylight's wasting, and I'd like to be well past this tower before whoever wrote that message comes back to check on their handwriting."

He followed her down the broken stairs, past the cold campsite, past the shallow grave he'd kicked earth back over, out into the light. And as he walked, Fen realized that the thing he'd refused to name since the first day had named itself somewhere between the ash and the girl's unsparing eyes.

It wasn't pity. It wasn't duty. It wasn't even the debt he owed the dead.

It was the oldest vow of his order, spoken before the covenant broke, spoken by every knight who ever swore to the dragon-fire: *what I guard, I guard with my life, gladly given.*

He had failed six times to mean it.

Walking behind Yara Delacroix as she marched toward the ridge with ash still dusting her sleeves, cataloguing plants and exits and the taste of her own fear, Fen found — to his considerable surprise — that meaning it had become the easiest thing in the world.

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, the carved letters on the tower wall were already fading — the burned words cooling, dimming, sinking back into plain stone.

As if someone, somewhere, had received confirmation that the seventh bearer was coming.

And was pleased.
