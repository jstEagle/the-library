## 6. The Poison Garden

The borderland announced itself a full day before they reached it.

Yara smelled it first — a sweetness riding the wind like honey left too long in the sun, the kind of sweetness that in her old life had meant one thing and one thing only: *something in this room wants to kill you.* She had spent nineteen years learning to read that particular note, to distinguish it from the honest sweetness of bread or ripe plums, and it had never once lied to her.

She stopped at the crest of the ridge, and Fen stopped beside her, his grey bulk settling into stillness.

"What is it?" His voice came out of the wolf's throat low and rough, a man's voice wearing an animal's shape. He still startled her, even after four days of travel together — the way those amber eyes held hers with something older than patience.

"Flowers," Yara said. "A lot of them."

Below them, the valley floor spread out in a carpet of color that would have been beautiful if she hadn't known better. Purple and white and a red so deep it looked bruised, acres of blossoms nodding in the wind, and threaded through them a green so lush it seemed painted on. No birds crossed it. No deer grazed at its edges. The road they needed — the old trade road Fen insisted would take them south toward the mountains — ran straight through the middle of it like a spine through a body.

"The Sorrowfields," Fen said. "I'd forgotten how far they've spread."

"You've been here before?"

"I've been most places." He didn't elaborate. He rarely did. "The flowers are called weeping bells. The pollen puts you to sleep within twenty paces of the first stand. Deeper in, there's something the locals call widow's lace — touches your skin and your heart forgets its rhythm. And the ground itself is laced with rotroot; dig anywhere and you'll blister to the bone."

Yara crouched, pulling a stalk of grass from the ridge-top, and chewed the end of it thoughtfully. "And the road goes straight through."

"There's no around. Not for three hundred miles." Fen's ears flattened. "Caravans used to hire flame-wardens to burn a path each season. There are no caravans anymore, and no flame-wardens either. The fire went out of the world, little taster. Things like this grow where it used to be."

That was the phrase he kept using — *where the fire used to be* — as though dragon-fire were a season, a crop, something that could fail like a harvest. She was beginning to understand that it was exactly that. The map on her back, wrapped in oilcloth against her sweat, traced a path through this dead garden because whoever drew it had known the garden was coming.

"So we go through," she said.

"We go through," Fen agreed, "and I will carry you over my back and run the whole way, and pray the pollen doesn't take me mid-stride."

"No." She stood, brushing off her knees. "You can't smell which plants are dying and which are thriving. You can't tell a healthy stand from a poisoned one by looking. But I can."

Fen turned his great head toward her. "You're going to *taste* your way across?"

"I'm going to taste my way across."

For a long moment the wolf simply looked at her, and Yara braced herself for the argument — the dismissal, the *you're a half-starved kitchen girl*, the protective fury she'd seen flicker in him since the ford. Instead, something shifted behind those amber eyes. Something almost like wonder, quickly buried.

"You're insane," he said.

"Probably." She shouldered her pack. "But I'm also the only person alive who can do this. So let's not waste daylight."

* * *

They entered the garden at midmorning, when the dew had burned off but before the heat pressed the pollen down.

Fen walked at her shoulder, close enough that his fur brushed her arm, and Yara worked. It was, she realized with a strange lurch, the first time in her life anyone had watched her work and understood what they were seeing. In the castle kitchens she had been a fixture, half-servant, half-superstition — *the girl whose tongue knows death* — useful and slightly feared, fed well and loved gruffly but never quite asked what she actually did. Now a wolf the size of a pony paced beside her, watching her snap a stem of purple bell-flower, crush it between her fingers, touch a thread of sap to her tongue-tip, and wait.

"Bitter," she said. "Almond underneath. This stand is young — the poison's concentrated. We skirt left, thirty paces, and cut through the white ones."

"Why the white ones?" Fen asked. Not doubting. Asking. Actually asking, as though her answer mattered.

"They're older. Older plants mellow — they've spent their venom attracting pollinators for decades, and the soil around them is saturated. The danger's in the edges, where new growth crowds the old." She wiped her fingers on her sash. "It's the same with mushrooms. The young ones are proud. The old ones are tired."

"The old ones are tired," Fen repeated, and made a sound that might have been a laugh. "You make poisons sound like courtiers."

"I grew up in a castle. Everything sounds like courtiers."

They moved through the white stands in single file, Yara testing every thirty paces, calling the path left or right, forward or hold. The weeping bells hung their heavy heads around them, and the air grew thick enough to chew. Twice she felt the drowsiness brush the edge of her mind — a softness at the borders of thought, a wish to sit down, just for a moment, just here among the flowers — and twice she bit the inside of her cheek hard enough to taste blood and pushed through. Her body knew these poisons. Or rather, her body had been taught, drop by drop over nineteen years, to refuse them entry. The pollen touched her blood and found the doors already barred.

But the doors, she noticed, were not as solid as they used to be.

The first time she truly felt it was past noon, at the edge of a field of widow's lace. The plant grew in pale cascades like frozen waterfalls, and its danger wasn't in eating it — it was in touching it, breathing near it, letting the fine spores settle on skin. Yara knelt at its border and did what she had done ten thousand times in the tasting chamber: she took a spore-dusted fingertip and pressed it to her tongue.

The world tilted.

Not pain, exactly. A flutter, deep in her chest, like a bird trapped under cloth. Her heartbeat stumbled — skipped — caught itself — and for three long seconds Yara knelt in the flowers with her own pulse misbehaving like a frightened animal while Fen watched her with growing alarm.

"Yara."

"I'm fine." She sat back on her heels. Her hands were shaking. "I'm fine. It just — surprised me."

"It stopped your heart."

"It *slowed* my heart. Briefly." She flexed her fingers until the trembling eased. "In the castle, that dose wouldn't have raised my pulse at all. I've built up against widow's lace compounds before — they use a cousin of it in the southern provinces, in fishing nets." She was talking too fast and knew it. "My immunity isn't infinite. I always knew that. Master Odell told me when I was twelve: 'Every tolerance has a floor, girl, and every floor has a depth.' I just never thought I'd hit it at nineteen."

Fen lowered himself onto his belly beside her, bringing his eyes level with hers. Up close, the wolf-shape couldn't quite hide the man — the way he frowned, the way his gaze moved over her face cataloguing damage the way she catalogued poison.

"How much have you taken today?" he asked quietly.

"A dozen tastes. Maybe fifteen."

"And yesterday? And the day before? Every day since the valley?" His voice dropped further. "You've been dosing yourself since we crossed the boundary, haven't you. Every strange leaf, every unfamiliar berry. Building maps in your mouth."

"I have to know what's safe," she said. "It's what I'm *for*."

"That is not an answer to my question."

Yara looked away, out across the lace-field, pale and endless as a shroud. "No," she admitted. "It isn't."

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf of stone the flowers hadn't claimed — some quirk of the soil, or perhaps a memory of an old hearth beneath it, because the ground there was bare and clean and smelled faintly of ash. Fen hunted at the perimeter and brought back a hare, and Yara cooked it over a small fire with herbs she'd tested one by one along the road, and they ate in the blue dusk while the weeping bells closed their heads all around them like a congregation bowing to prayer.

"You should stop," Fen said.

"I can't stop. That's rather the point of me."

"You could stop *unnecessarily*. Taste only when the path demands it. Rest the rest." He pawed at the dirt, restless. "You felt it today. Your armor has holes in it now. Another week of this and—"

"And what? I die in a flowerbed?" She poked at the fire. "Fen, listen to me. I spent my whole life being the thing that stood between other people and death. Nineteen years of putting things in my mouth so that someone else wouldn't have to. Do you know what happens to a food-taster who refuses a dish? Nothing good. Do you know what happens to a kingdom whose taster dies? They find another one. A child, probably, trained up fast, and the king keeps eating, and nobody thinks about the girl who—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Nobody thinks about the girl."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a bell-flower chimed softly against its neighbor.

"But you," she said, "keep thinking about the girl. Why?"

Fen was quiet for a long time. When he answered, his voice had gone somewhere distant, somewhere years away.

"Because I once guarded someone who gave everything she had, piece by piece, because she believed it was what she was for. And I stood beside her and let her believe it, right up until there was nothing left of her to give." His amber eyes caught the firelight. "I will not watch it happen twice."

Yara's throat tightened. "Who was she?"

"A story for another night." He rose, shook out his fur, and settled himself between her and the flowers, a wall of grey muscle facing the dark. "Sleep, little taster. Tomorrow we cross the worst of it."

* * *

The worst of it took two days.

On the second morning they came to the heart of the Sorrowfields, where the road dipped into a hollow and the flowers grew chest-high and dense as wheat, and there was no skirting, no edging, no clever path around. They would have to walk the hollow's length — half a mile of the densest poison Yara had ever seen gathered in one place — and the only way through was to know, precisely, which threads of color were safe to touch.

So she did what she was for.

She walked ahead of Fen with her sleeves rolled and her hands bare, and she read the field like a page. Red bloom, crushed, tasted: *rotten cherry, then copper — deadly, avoid.* White bloom, two rows over: *green tea and winter — passable, but the roots below are rotroot, step wide.* She mapped the hollow in her mouth, plot by plot, calling directions back over her shoulder, and Fen followed her voice through the killing garden with a precision that would have seemed impossible for something so large. By midday they were halfway across, and Yara had tasted forty-one distinct poisons, and she was beginning to feel every one of them.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the terrible part. It was a slow dimming, like a lamp running low on oil. Her fingertips had gone numb an hour ago. There was a metallic taste in her mouth that no amount of water washed away. When she stumbled on a root, her leg responded a half-beat late, as though the signal had to travel farther than it used to. And beneath it all, her heartbeat had developed a stutter she could feel when she lay her hand flat on her sternum — *skip, catch, skip* — the rhythm of a body keeping accounts it could no longer balance.

"Fen." She stopped in the middle of the hollow. The flowers nodded around her waist. "How far to the far edge?"

"Two hundred paces. Why?"

"Because I need you to count them for me." She turned to face him, and saw her own unsteadiness reflected in his eyes. "Out loud. Because I think if I lose track of the number, I'm going to sit down, and I don't think I'll get up."

The wolf went very still. Then he moved to her side, pressing his warm flank against her legs — steadying her, anchoring her — and began to walk, slowly, deliberately, counting in that rough man's voice.

"One. Two. Three."

Yara put her hand in his ruff and matched him stride for stride. At fifty, her vision blurred at the edges. At ninety, the numbness climbed past her wrists. At one hundred and forty, her knees buckled, and Fen simply adjusted, shifting his weight so that she leaned on him without breaking stride, and kept counting.

"One hundred and sixty-one. One hundred and sixty-two."

At one hundred and eighty-eight, the flowers ended. The stony slope of the far ridge rose before them, brown and honest and blessedly dead, and Yara's legs folded beneath her, and Fen caught her before she hit the ground.

She woke at dusk with a blanket of shed wolf-fur over her and the taste of something bitter and medicinal coating her tongue.

"Chew it," Fen said, watching her from a pace away. "Bark from the ridge trees. Bittercress root. I watched you test both yesterday and mark them safe."

"You've been paying attention," she rasped.

"To everything." His eyes were unreadable. "You nearly died in there."

"I nearly died doing the only thing I know how to do." She struggled upright, chewing the bark. It was foul. It was wonderful. "But I didn't. And we're across. And now you know — really know — what I am. Not a curiosity. Not a superstition. A woman who walks through gardens that kill everyone else."

"Yes," Fen said quietly. "Now I know what you are." And there was something in his voice that hadn't been there before — not pity, not fear, but respect, hard-won and freely given, and Yara found it warmed her more than the fire.

She looked down at her own hand in the firelight, turning it over. Steady again, mostly. The numbness retreating like a tide. But she could feel it now, unmistakably, the way you feel a coin purse grown lighter after a long journey: the finite weight of everything she had survived, everything she had spent, everything she might yet need.

Her immunity was not a wall. It was a storehouse. And she had just emptied half its shelves crossing a field of flowers.

"Get some sleep," Fen said. "Tomorrow we reach the river country. There's a village there — old people, old ways. They'll have food, and news, and maybe a healer who can look at that stutter in your pulse."

Yara nodded and lay back, watching the stars wheel above the dead garden. And it was there, on the threshold of sleep, that she heard it — carried on the wind from the direction they'd come, thin and wrong and unmistakable:

Dogs. Many dogs. And behind them, a horn blown in the pattern she'd last heard from the towers of the Valley of Stillness.

Corvane's hounds had found the trail.

"*Fen,*" she whispered, and the wolf was already on his feet, ears pricked toward the dark, hackles rising like a silver crown.
