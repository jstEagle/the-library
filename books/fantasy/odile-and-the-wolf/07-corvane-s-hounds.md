## 7. Corvane's Hounds

The rain found them two days out of the Thornwood's eaves, and the hounds found them a day after that.

Yara smelled them first — not the animals but the men, or rather what the men carried. She had spent nineteen years learning the signature of poison in all its disguises: the almond ghost of bitter cassia beneath a stew's fat, the green-metal tang of hemlock masked with honey, the sweet rot of something distilled from grave-soil. The riders on their trail carried a smell she knew from her worst memory — the silver-dust poison that had nearly killed her in the king's own hall, the poison older than chemistry, older than the valley walls.

They were lacing their arrows with it.

"Fen." She kept her voice low, though the wind was against them anyway, carrying her scent east toward whoever hunted. "Behind us. Half a mile, maybe less. Six riders. Maybe seven."

The wolf's ears swiveled back without his head turning. He had grown larger since the Poison Garden, or she had simply stopped expecting him to be anything less than enormous — a grey shape like a moving hillside beside her, mud to the hocks, rain beading on fur that never seemed to get properly wet. *Seven,* he said. His voice lived inside her skull, a man's voice in a wolf's mouth, dry as old paper. *I counted eight. The one lagging behind is either clever or dead by morning.*

"Corvane's?"

*Who else sends hounds into the borderlands in autumn? Your spymaster does not waste good men on bad weather.* Fen cut off the road they'd been following — a merchant track gone soft with rain, winding down through hills of gorse and blackthorn — and took them north instead, up a slope where the gorse grew chest-high and vicious. *We lose them in the high ground, then double back for the ford.*

"We can't keep running forever." Yara said it between breaths, because it needed saying and because saying it made it true. Her pack thumped against her spine. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, the dragon-hide map lay still and quiet, its ink-path pointing always northeast, toward mountains she could not yet see. Three days since the ink had last moved. She had begun talking to it at night, quietly, the way one talks to a sick animal. *Not yet,* she'd tell it. *Not yet.* And it would lie there, patient as a held breath.

*Forever is not required,* Fen said. *Only until the mountain.*

"And then?"

He didn't answer. He never answered that question, and she had stopped pretending she didn't notice.

The slope steepened. Gorse tore at her sleeves; her boots, castle-made and never meant for this country, were coming apart at the seams, and she had stuffed them with wool against the wet. Below, faintly, she heard the hounds' true voices now — actual dogs, lean black things bred in the king's kennels, giving tongue on a scent line. They had not lost the trail after all. Fen's ears flattened.

*They are not tracking you,* he said, and there was something new in the voice, something she had learned to read as close to fear as he ever came. *They are tracking the map. Dragon-hide holds scent the way iron holds cold. I should have thought of it. I failed to think of it once before, and the last bearer—*

"You didn't fail," Yara said, for perhaps the hundredth time, and for perhaps the hundredth time he ignored her completely.

*Down.* His body hit hers like a landslide, gentle and total, pressing her flat into the wet gorse as the first arrow went over.

She heard it before she understood it: a sound like a wasp the size of a goose, and then the blackthorn tree ten feet to their left sprouted a shaft that smoked where it struck, and every leaf within arm's reach of the impact curled brown and dropped, in autumn rain, in a single breath.

Silver dust. On an arrowhead. Fired at a range no archer should attempt in this weather, which meant the archer did not care about the weather, which meant the archer was very good, which meant—

*Vessin's company,* Fen breathed. *Corvane's own. Not hired knives. His personal hounds.* He rolled off her, low to the ground, lips peeled back from teeth longer than her fingers. *Yara. Listen carefully. These are not men who take prisoners back to a spymaster for polite questioning. Corvane sent his best because he wants the map intact and everyone who has seen it silenced. That includes you. Especially you.*

"Then we run."

*No.* For the first time since the ford, since the beginning, Fen turned his great head and looked at her directly, rain running off his muzzle, eyes the color of storm-water over slate. *Running is done. There is a ravine ahead, a cut in the hills where the ground drops thirty feet into scree. We let them come to us. You stay low. I do what I am for.*

"And what are you for, exactly?" It came out sharper than she intended, edged with everything she'd been swallowing since the gate, since the dying woman's grip on her wrist. *It knows your name.* Since learning that her whole life — the valley, the stillness, the careful magicless years of tasting other people's deaths — might have been someone else's design.

Fen's gaze held hers a moment too long.

*For standing between,* he said finally. *Now get behind me, taster, and whatever happens — however it sounds, however it looks — do not come out until I call you by name. My true name. You'll know it when you hear it.*

* * *

The ravine was exactly what Fen had promised: a raw scar in the hillside where some ancient flood had torn the earth open, walls of wet stone and hanging roots, the floor a riverbed of loose scree that shifted underfoot like living gravel. Yara wedged herself into a hollow beneath an overhang, drew her knife — a kitchen knife, absurd, its edge honed to a taster's precision — and listened.

The hounds came at dusk, when the rain thickened and the light went the grey of dishwater.

She heard the dogs first, their voices gone ragged with excitement, and then the horses picking their way down the slope, and then voices — six, seven, eight, just as Fen had counted. A man's voice, clipped and educated, giving orders in the cadence of someone accustomed to being obeyed quickly. Vessin, presumably. Corvane's hand and sword.

"Split. Two on each rim. The rest down the center. The wolf first — do not engage it alone, do not corner it, put three shafts in it before it closes. The girl is to be taken alive if clean, dead if not. Either way the hide comes home."

Alive if clean. Dead if not. Yara turned the words over while her hands, steady as they had been over a hundred poisoned dishes, checked her knife-hold. So Corvane wanted her breathing — wanted her *clean*, unpoisoned, untainted by the world outside the valley. As if she were a vessel whose contents mattered. As if she were evidence.

The first shaft came down the ravine and Fen met it in the air.

She never saw how he moved — only heard the wet percussion of a body hitting the scree, a man's short scream cut in half, the clatter of a crossbow skittering down stone. Then the dark came alive. The wolf was everywhere at once, a grey blur between the torchlight below and the rim above, and the ravine filled with noise: shouts, the shrieking of horses, the flat cracks of crossbows loosed at nothing, and under all of it the sound she would remember longest — the hounds themselves going suddenly, terribly silent, as if they knew better than their handlers what moved among the rocks.

Yara pressed into her hollow and did what she was for. She listened.

Two men had gone down on the near rim — she could hear one moaning, one not. Three had committed to the ravine floor and were retreating back up it, dragging a fourth. The educated voice — Vessin — was still giving orders, but the orders had changed shape, tightened, and that was when Yara's ear, trained on nine years of detecting intent in the smallest sounds of a kitchen, caught the wrongness.

"Loose the dust," Vessin said. "Wide pattern. The wolf can survive it, maybe, but not fast enough to matter. Do it now."

A moment later she heard it — the soft hiss of something burning that was not fire, and then the smell arrived, rolling down the ravine like fog, and Yara's whole body remembered the king's hall, the goblet, the silver dust blooming on her tongue like frost.

It wasn't aimed at her. But air moves downhill, and she was downhill.

She got her scarf up over her mouth and nose and held her breath and ran — not away from the fight but along the ravine wall, toward where Fen had last been visible, because the arithmetic was simple and terrible: he had taken three crossbow bolts meant to be survived, and now the air itself was turning to poison around him, and he did not know that she was downstream of it.

The silver haze hung in the ravine like moonlight made solid. Through it she saw him — vast, wavering, standing over two fallen men with a third scrambling away — and saw, with a taster's terrible clarity, the way his movements had begun to slow. Not wounded. Poisoned. The same poison, the same *wrongness* that had nearly stopped her heart in a room full of courtiers who never knew how close their king had come to dying.

Her body knew what to do before her mind finished objecting.

There was a stream trickling down the far wall of the ravine — she'd marked it on the way in, the professional habit that Fen mocked and relied upon. Water wouldn't neutralize silver dust; nothing she knew would neutralize silver dust, that was rather the point of it. But water carried it, diluted it, pulled it out of the air. She wrenched her flask free, emptied it, and began hurling stream-water up the rock face in great arcing handfuls, wetting the stone above and around Fen, dragging the falling haze down into runoff, screaming through her soaked scarf — "Fen! Down-wind! MOVE!"

And the wolf, half-blinded, bolt-staggered, moved. Because she told him to. Because her voice, apparently, outranked his curse, his guilt, and eight of Corvane's finest.

They went up the ravine wall together, her fists in his ruff, his shoulder breaking her fall twice, and came out on the northern rim gasping into clean rain, and behind them Vessin's company stood in their own poisoned fog with their own dead, and no one followed.

* * *

They made three miles before Fen collapsed.

It happened between one step and the next — the huge grey body simply folded, the way a building folds, all at once and forever, and Yara barely got clear of being crushed. He lay in the lee of a granite outcrop, sides heaving, and where the crossbow bolts had struck him she found no wounds at all. Only marks. Silver-grey blooms in the fur, spreading like frost across a window, like the stains on the dead courier's map.

"No, no, no." She knelt in the mud, hands hovering, useless. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to save my life and then die of it, that's not — there are rules—"

*There are no rules,* Fen said. The voice in her skull had gone thin, distant, a man speaking through a closed door. *That is rather the theme of your life, taster. Has no one told you?*

"Don't joke. Think. What breaks it — the poison, the silver dust. What do you know?"

*I know what killed the last bearer of the map.* His eyes had drifted half-shut. *I know it was not meant for dragons' servants. It was made for something older. Made to unmake covenant-flesh. Which means somewhere in this realm, someone remembers how to make it, which means someone remembers the covenant, which means—*

"Which means Corvane knows." The realization landed cold. "Fen. Corvane isn't hunting the map for the king. If he has poisons that work on covenant-blood, he already knows more than any spymaster should. He knows what the map is. He knows what I am." She sat back on her heels in the rain, and the truth assembled itself piece by piece, the way she used to assemble the anatomy of an unknown toxin, one symptom at a time. "The madness. The king's madness started four years ago. And the blighted harvests — the valley's fine, the valley's always fine, but outside — Maren said the hedgerows were dying. The Thornwood lady said the borders of her court are fraying. Everything outside the wards is slowly being poisoned, isn't it? The whole realm. The covenant didn't just break. It's *leaking*."

Fen's breath hitched — a laugh, or the ruin of one.

*Four years,* he murmured. *Yes. The fire began guttering four years ago. The land began forgetting how to grow. The king began hearing voices, because a realm's crown sits closest to the wound.* His eye opened, fixed on her. *You have asked me since the ford why a spymaster would chase one girl across the world. Now answer it yourself.*

"Because the map leads to the Ember Altar." Yara heard her own voice go quiet. "Because whoever reforges the covenant controls what comes next — the terms, the price, the power. Corvane doesn't want to destroy it. He wants to *own* it. He wants to bind the dragons to human law before the fire dies, so that no queen can ever again refuse to pay, so that no realm ever again depends on whether one person keeps faith—" She stopped. Something else surfaced, something small and cold and old. "Or so he says. People who talk about protecting realms usually mean protecting their place in them."

*Cynical,* Fen said, *for someone raised in a valley of stillness.*

"I tasted poison for nine years. Cynicism is a job skill."

But even as she said it, doubt gnawed at her, because there had been something else in Vessin's orders, something she'd catalogued and not yet understood. *The girl is to be taken alive if clean.* Clean. Why would the cleanliness of her blood matter to a man retrieving a stolen map?

She got her answer an hour later, and it came walking out of the rain with a white flag on a spear.

* * *

He came alone, as heralds do — a tall man in oiled leather, hood back, hands empty and raised, stopping forty feet from the outcrop where Yara sat with her kitchen knife across her knees and Fen's labored breathing behind her. Rain streamed off him. He made no move to wipe his face.

"Lady Yara Delacroix," he said. Not shouted — pitched, precisely, to carry. "My name is Captain Vessin. I serve Lord Corvane, spymaster to the crown. I'm instructed to deliver a message and a choice, and then to withdraw. May I approach?"

"You may stand there and drown," Yara said. "That seems fair."

Something flickered across his face that might have been respect. "As you wish. The message: my lord knows where you are and where you're going. He has known since the watchtower. The hounds you escaped tonight were not his full strength; they were a courtesy, meant to test the wolf's condition and yours. The report he will receive is that both of you are formidable." He paused. "The report he hopes to receive, someday, is that you reached the mountain alive."

Yara's grip shifted on the knife. Behind her, Fen stirred, a low sound building in his chest.

"Why?" she called. "If he wants the Altar controlled, why send me toward it?"

"Because, my lady, Lord Corvane cannot reach it." Vessin's voice did not waver, but something under it did — a hairline fracture, carefully patched. "The Altar answers only to covenant-blood. Fire recognizes its own. He has spent eleven years trying to force doors that were never built for him, and eleven years watching the realm he swore to protect rot from the edges inward. The harvests. The rivers. The king." Here the fracture widened, briefly, before he sealed it again. "His majesty's madness is not sickness, my lady. It is the covenant's death working upward through the crown. Some nights the king remembers everything — the old pacts, the fire, the names of dragons — and weeps. Other nights he tries to set the throne room alight because he can hear it calling. Lord Corvane holds those nights together with his bare hands. He has done so for four years. He is tired beyond what I can say."

The rain filled the silence. Yara found she had stopped gripping the knife.

"He knew my mother," she said. It was not a guess. It rose in her like a taste recognized — the particular quality of grief in Vessin's voice when he said *eleven years*. Eleven years. She was nineteen. Eleven years ago she had been eight, still in the valley, still ordinary, still believing the world ended at the gates.

Vessin was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"He loved her," the captain said at last. "Before the covenant broke. Before the queen refused the price and everything burned. He was a young officer then, and she was — my lady, I only know what I've pieced together from the nights when he drinks and talks to people who aren't there. He calls her Seraiva. He says she sang to the fires. He says she was the bravest traitor the realm ever produced, because she stole a child out of a burning pact and hid it where magic couldn't find it, and he helped her do it, and it is the only thing in his life he has never regretted." Vessin's jaw tightened. "Until the child grew up and walked out of the valley carrying the one thing that could finish what her mother started — or end the realm trying."

*Seraiva.* The name went through Yara like a blade finding the gap in armor. She had no mother in her memory, only a hole shaped like one, and now the hole had a name and a song and a reputation for treachery, and every story she had told herself about her own life — orphan, accident, nobody — rearranged itself around this new center of gravity. She had not fled the valley. She had been *placed* there, like a seed in a warded pot, by a singing traitor and a lovesick officer, and everyone who had ever fed her or guarded her or set her to tasting death had known.

Even the king. Even mad, even poisoned by a dying covenant, some part of him had known — and had kept a food-taster with no family in his household, fed from his kitchens, protected by his walls.

Betrayal, she thought, tasted different depending on what you believed it preserved.

"The choice," Vessin said. "Lord Corvane offers terms. Come to him before the mountain — or permit him to come to you. He asks only to negotiate the covenant's new terms *with* you, not over your corpse. He swears by Seraiva's name that the girl who carries dragon-hide will reach the Altar breathing." A pause, and the fracture showed one last time. "He has never sworn by that name lightly. Break it, and I think it would kill something in him that the last eleven years haven't managed to."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the hounds stop being courteous." Vessin inclined his head, rain sheeting from his hood. "I'll be honest with you, my lady, since you're owed honesty from someone: I hope you refuse to come to him and live to hate him anyway. I hope you climb your mountain and pay your price and fix what we broke. But I am a captain, he is my lord, and the king weeps in the throne room. Good night."

He turned and walked back into the rain, and the dark swallowed him, and somewhere downslope the surviving hounds whined once and fell silent.

Yara sat a long time without moving. Behind her, Fen's breathing had steadied somewhat — the poison receding, or merely pausing, she couldn't tell. The map, when she finally pulled it from her pack, had begun to move again: the ink-line crawling northeast, urgent now, the path bending toward a route she hadn't seen before — higher, colder, through country marked with a sigil she now recognized from Maren's stories.

The sigil of dedication. Her mother's mark. Drawn on the map in ink that gleamed like fire, as if the hide had known all along whose daughter would carry it.

*Well?* Fen's voice was faint but dry as ever. *Do we go kneel at the spymaster's feet and negotiate our chains in advance? Or do we climb?*

Yara looked at the path her mother had drawn, at the name Vessin had spoken, at the rain washing the world outside the valley — the poisoned, blighted, magnificent world she had been born to and stolen from and was only now, at nineteen, beginning to actually see.

"We climb," she said. "But Fen — when we get to the mountain, Corvane will be there. And I'm going to want to look him in the eye and ask him why my mother sang to fires." She folded the map against her heart. "And then I'm going to decide whose side betrayal is really on."

Above the eastern hills, through a tear in the clouds, the sky glowed a faint, failing red — like embers under ash, like something vast refusing, just barely, to go out.

Three days, the glow seemed to say. Perhaps four.

The mountain was waiting, and it was not patient.
