## 9. Fire in the Deep Road

The dwarven deep road began where a mountain had been hollowed out and never finished, and it smelled of old stone and older dark.

Yara had imagined tunnels as narrow things — crawlspaces, mine shafts. This was nothing of the kind. The road ran wide enough for six wagons abreast, its ceiling lost in shadow far above, its walls carved with friezes that marched into the distance like frozen processions: bearded kings, hammering smiths, and above them all, wings. Dragons, rendered in bas-relief so old their edges had gone soft as worn coin.

"They built for grandeur," Fen said, his voice dropping to that hushed register he only used underground. In wolf-shape he padded at her left, grey fur drinking the lantern-light. "Then the mountain took it back."

"Cheerful," said Maren, walking at Yara's right with her healer's satchel clacking against her hip. The hedge-healer had insisted on coming this far — through the Thornwood's edge and into the foothills — before turning back to her village. "You'd think folk who lived under stone would learn some humility."

"The dwarves weren't humble." Fen's ears swiveled toward a side gallery, black as a mouth. "They were proud, and then they were dead, and now they're quiet. There's a lesson in it."

Yara touched the wall as she walked. The stone was cold, but not dead-cold; there was a faint tremor in it, deep below hearing, like a heartbeat heard through a door. She had felt it since they entered an hour ago and hadn't mentioned it, because mentioning it would make it real, and if it was real then something down here had a pulse.

*It knows your name.* The dying courier's words again, always again, whenever the world grew strange.

She catalogued instead. It was what she did, what nineteen years of tasting had carved into her: *bitter air, wet stone, no sulfur, no rot.* Safe, or safe enough. Her tongue was her map, and the deep road tasted of nothing worse than dust.

The pilgrim found them at dusk — or what passed for dusk, three hundred feet of rock between them and any sky.

He came around a bend ahead, staggering, and Fen's growl rolled down the corridor like a landslide starting. Yara threw out an arm on instinct, though against what she couldn't have said.

The man was old past guessing, wrapped in travel-stained robes the color of ash, and he carried a lantern.

Not iron. Not glass. The lantern was a cage of blackened bronze shaped like a dragon curled around its own egg, and inside it burned a flame that did not flicker. That was the first wrongness. Every flame Yara had ever known danced, leaned, begged at its fuel. This one stood perfectly still, a shard of orange-gold light burning without smoke, and looking at it made her chest ache in a way she had no name for — homesickness, she thought absurdly, for a home she'd never had.

"Bearer," the old man rasped, and fell to his knees.

Fen flowed forward, hackles high. "Who sent you? Who told you we were on this road?"

"No one." The pilgrim laughed, and the laugh became coughing, and the coughing brought up blood that shone too bright in the still flame's light. "No one sends me anymore. I *feel* the bearer. I've felt her since the Thornwood. Since—" His clouded eyes found Yara and widened, and whatever he saw made him bow his head to the floor. "Oh. Oh, you're *hers*. You carry her mark."

Yara's hand went to her collarbone, where the birthmark sat — the sigil Maren had named, the covenant-brand she'd spent nineteen years thinking was a wine stain. "I don't understand."

"You will. I'm nearly done, and there isn't time for understanding." He pushed himself up on shaking arms. "Take it."

"Take *what*?"

"The fire." He lifted the lantern with both hands, and Yara saw then how wasted he was, how the flame's light passed through his skin like paper. "The last of it. Vessarath's own, carried from the roost when she went to sleep, kept alive by bearers who fed it years of their lives, one after another, down a chain longer than your kingdom has existed. I am the last link." He smiled, gap-toothed and terrible. "And my hands are full of dying."

"I can't—" Yara stepped back. "I'm nobody. I'm a food-taster."

"You are the child of the covenant," the pilgrim said simply, "and the fire knows its own. Take it, or let it gutter here in the dark, and everything it warmed goes with it."

Behind her, Fen had gone very still. When he spoke, his voice came strange and low. "Yara. The flame in that lantern is why the eastern sky went grey. It's the seed of what Vessarath sleeps around. If it dies—"

"I know what happens if it dies. Everyone knows. The whole world is learning what happens." She looked at the lantern. The still flame looked back, or seemed to, and beneath her fear Yara felt that ache again, that terrible recognition, like hearing a lullaby you didn't know you remembered.

She reached out and took the handle.

The bronze was warm. Not hot — warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and the warmth climbed her arm and settled behind her sternum like a second heart. The pilgrim sagged with relief, and Maren caught him, easing him down onto a bedroll with practiced hands.

"How long?" Maren asked quietly.

"Hours. Perhaps less." He gripped Yara's wrist, the same desperate grip as the dying courier at the gate, and Yara thought bitterly that strangers kept dying into her hands these days, each one heavier than the last. "Listen. The deep road forks ahead, at the Hall of Nine Pillars. Take the left passage. Do not stop. Do not sleep near the flame, and do not—" He broke off, coughing. "Do not let it gutter. A guttering flame wakes things. Things the dwarves sealed when they still had strength to seal."

"What things?"

His eyes were going distant. "Older than us. Older than the dragons. Hungry in the way stone is hungry." His grip tightened. "Left passage, bearer. And when you reach the mountain — tell Vessarath the chain held. Tell her the last link held."

He died an hour later, mid-sentence, mid-prayer, Maren's hand in his and Yara's promise in his ear. They buried him in a niche in the wall, stacked stones over him, and Maren wept for a man she'd known two hours, because that was the kind of woman she was.

Then Maren turned back toward the daylight and the long walk home, and Yara stood alone in the dark with a wolf and a flame older than kingdoms.

* * *

They walked through the night, because the pilgrim had said *do not stop*, and because stopping meant thinking about the weight behind Yara's sternum, which had not faded. If anything it had grown more distinct — a warmth with texture, almost a presence, shifting when she shifted. She caught herself talking to it in a low voice, nonsense words, the way you'd soothe a spooked horse, and stopped only when she realized Fen was watching.

"It's not a pet," he said.

"It's not a thing at all, is it." She lifted the lantern. The flame stood motionless, but she could swear it inclined toward her, the way a flower leans sunward. "It's someone."

"It's a piece of the dragon-mother. Don't get sentimental. Sentimental bearers die." But his voice lacked its usual bite.

The Hall of Nine Pillars announced itself by echo first — their footsteps suddenly multiplying, bouncing back from vast unseen distances — and then by light. Nine columns rose out of the darkness, each thicker than a castle tower, carved top to bottom with scenes Yara's eye couldn't parse in passing: battles, bargains, figures with crowns and figures with wings, their hands meeting over something small and blazing.

At the hall's center, the road forked exactly as promised. Left passage and right, and between them, a third opening that appeared on no map and no pilgrim's warning: a stairway descending, breathing cold air that smelled — Yara's tongue catalogued before her mind caught up — of *iron and rot and something sweet underneath, like fruit going bad.*

"Fen."

"I smell it." He had planted himself between her and the stairway, lips peeled back. "That's the seal. That's what he meant. We go left, we go fast, we don't look at it."

They were forty paces into the left passage when the flame guttered.

There was no wind. There was no reason. But the still flame — the flame that had not moved once since the pilgrim handed it over — bent sideways, shrank to a blue whisper, and Yara felt the second heart behind her sternum *scream*, a silent shriek of terror that wasn't hers and was entirely hers, and every hair on her body stood up.

From the stairway came a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

"Run," Fen said.

They ran. The left passage was straight as a spear for a mile and then it wasn't, bending, narrowing, the dwarven perfection giving way to rawer cuts, and behind them the sound came again — not footsteps, nothing so kind as footsteps, but a grinding, a slithering of mass, stone moving that had agreed for ten thousand years to stay still. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Somewhere close, in the walls themselves, something began to breathe in long, satisfied draughts.

A collapse cut off the passage behind them — deliberate, Yara would think later, the thing herding them — and forced them down a side gallery that narrowed until Fen had to shift forms to fit, and then narrowed further, and then they burst into a chamber and the floor ended.

A chasm, black and bottomless, spanned by a bridge of dwarf-cut stone barely wider than a coffin. On the far side, the passage continued upward. Behind them, the grinding grew louder.

"Go," Fen said, human-shaped now, drawing the short sword he carried slung across his back. "Cross. I'll hold the mouth of the gallery."

"With *that*? Against something older than dragons?" But she was already backing onto the bridge, because arguing cost seconds, and seconds were bleeding away.

The thing came into the chamber.

Yara saw it in fragments, in the lantern's straining light: too many joints, a body like a landslide given intent, a head that was mostly mouth and partly memory — because she knew that shape, gods help her, she'd seen it carved on the nine pillars, the figure beneath the kings and dragons, the one with no face and reaching hands. The dwarves' oldest prisoner. The thing the covenant's fire had helped keep asleep.

It saw the lantern. It made a sound that was not a roar but a *want*, vast and infantile, and lunged.

Fen met it with steel and fury and got thrown the length of the chamber for his trouble, crashing against the far wall. The creature turned. Its attention — its whole gravity — swung toward the flame, toward Yara, and it came on, and the bridge shook, and the flame guttered again, lower, bluer, dying—

And Yara did the thing that made no sense and was the only sense there was.

She dropped to her knees on the swaying bridge, pulled the lantern against her chest, and wrapped herself around it. Wings of her own body, arms crossed over the bronze, cheek pressed to the cage where the last living fire shrank and shivered. *Here,* she thought, with no logic at all, the way you'd think at a frightened child. *Here. I've got you. Nothing gets past me. I've survived everything the world ever put in my cup — you will not go out while I'm holding you.*

The creature's claw came down.

Fire exploded outward.

Not from the lantern — from *her*, from everywhere she touched, a bloom of orange-gold that filled the chamber like a sunrise filling a valley. The claw stopped a hand's breadth from her spine, smoking, recoiling. The creature screamed — a real scream this time, pain and terror — and Yara felt the heat pour through her and *out* of her and understood, in the wordless way of dreams, that the flame was passing through her body as if she were made of the same substance, that it recognized her, that the sigil on her collarbone was burning like a brand and she could feel it writing itself deeper—

The fire roared. The creature fled, grinding back down its stairway, sealing its own dark behind it, and the silence that followed rang like a struck bell.

Yara knelt on the bridge, unburnt.

Her sleeves were scorched. Her hair smelled of smoke. Her palms, where she'd gripped the lantern cage, showed skin pink and new as a child's — and where the heat had licked her arms, her neck, her face, there was nothing. No blister. No scar. As if the fire had touched her and found nothing to burn, or everything to recognize.

Fen limped to the bridge's edge, blood on his temple, staring at her.

"The fire knows her," he said softly, to himself, to the dark, to whoever had set this chain in motion nineteen years before she was born. "Gods forgive us all. The fire *knows* her."

Yara looked down at the lantern. The flame stood upright again, steady, still — and brighter than before, fed somehow, and behind her sternum the second heart beat warm and calm, like something finally home.

She should have been afraid. Some part of her noted, distantly, that a good taster would be afraid — that anything which passed through your body unchanged was either harmless or already yours, and she no longer knew which this was.

But the warmth behind her ribs pulsed once, gently, like a hand squeezing hers, and Yara found she wasn't afraid at all.

She stood, lifted the lantern, and started across the bridge.

Ahead, the passage climbed toward air and sky and the dragon-mother's mountain. Behind, in the deep dark, the old hunger licked its wounds and waited.

And somewhere on the slopes above — though none of them knew it yet — watchfires were being lit in ranks and rows, an army assembling at the mountain's foot, carrying Corvane's banners and Corvane's terms.

The chain had held. The last link was still walking.

But the mountain was running out of time.
