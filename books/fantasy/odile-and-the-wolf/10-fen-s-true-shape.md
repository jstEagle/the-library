## 10. Fen's True Shape

The road's mouth was a wound in the hillside, and Fen smelled the ambush before he saw it.

He had been smelling wrongness for an hour — old iron and lamp-oil and the sour sweat of men who had waited too long in one place — but he had said nothing, because saying it aloud would make it true, and because Yara needed sleep more than she needed his dread. She walked beside him with her hood up and her hand resting lightly on the map-satchel at her hip, the way she always did now, as if the dragon-hide might bolt like a horse if she let go of it.

"Stop," he said.

She stopped. That was the thing about Yara Delacroix that no one who hadn't traveled with her could understand: she argued with everything, fought every instruction on principle, catalogued every kindness for the price hidden inside it — but when it mattered, when his voice dropped into the register that meant *now*, she obeyed as cleanly as a soldier. The kitchens of a paranoid king trained people well. He only wished they hadn't trained her quite so well. He wished she'd never needed training at all.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"Hunters. Six, maybe seven. Ahead and above." He turned his head slowly, reading the wind off the rocks. "They've been following us since the ford. Corvane's hounds don't tire."

Yara's face did what it always did when fear came: nothing. It was the most frightening thing about her, Fen thought — not the tasting, not the immunity, not the moving ink on the dragon-hide, but the way she folded terror into a small square and put it away somewhere behind her eyes, and then went on walking.

"There's no other way through," she said. "Maren said the deep road opens here or nowhere."

"I know."

"So we go through."

"We go through," he agreed, "but not together. Not all at once. If I draw them—"

"No." The word came out flat and immediate, before thought. Then she heard herself say it, and something flickered across her face — surprise at her own voice, at what it had claimed. "No," she said again, softer. "You don't get to do that. You already failed one bearer. You told me. You don't get to trade yourself to feel better about it."

The words landed where she'd aimed them, which was the trouble with traveling beside someone observant enough to taste poison in a king's soup. Fen felt the old guilt shift in its grave, the way it always did when she invoked it — half wound, half weapon, and both edges hers to hold.

"That was two hundred years ago," he said.

"And you've been carrying it ever since, so it might as well have been yesterday." She crouched, pulling a strip of dried venison from her pack, tearing it with her teeth, offering him half without looking. It was how she said things she couldn't say. He took it. "So we think. We don't sacrifice. What do hunters want?"

"The map."

"The map, not me. So I give them something that looks like the map." Her eyes had gone bright and quick, running the arithmetic of survival the way she ran it over a plate of food — this safe, this not, this will kill you in three breaths. "I make noise on the ridge to the east. You take the road's mouth. When they chase me, you go through, and I double back along the stream bed Maren showed us."

"They'll catch you."

"They won't. I've been outrunning cooks' knives and spymasters my whole life." A ghost of a grin. "Besides, I'm faster than I look. Underfed my whole childhood. Nothing on my bones but spite."

It was a bad plan. He told her it was a bad plan. She agreed that it was a bad plan, and then they executed it anyway, because it was the only plan there was, and because — though neither of them said this part aloud — they had begun, somewhere in the last hundred miles, to trust each other's bad plans.

* * *

It went wrong in the first thirty seconds.

Fen went low and fast through the bracken toward the road's mouth, and the ambush sprang not from the rocks ahead but from the gully behind — seven became nine, and nine became twelve, and they had split their force, which meant Corvane's spymaster had finally stopped underestimating the girl with the taster's tongue. Crossbow bolts hissed out of the dusk. Fen heard Yara shout — not a scream, a warning, thrown back over her shoulder even as she ran her decoy line east — and then the hunters were between them, a wall of grey cloaks and drawn steel, and the road's mouth yawned dark and empty ten yards past the nearest man.

Ten yards. He had crossed greater distances in less time. But between him and the dark stood a hunter with a hooked blade and no fear in him at all, and behind Fen, further down the slope, he could hear Yara's decoy crashing through brush with three pursuers behind her, and the mathematics of the moment assembled themselves with terrible clarity.

He could reach the road. He could be through it before they cut him down — probably. And Yara would be alone on the far side of the mountain with three killers and no wolf.

Or.

There was an *or*. There had always been an *or*, sleeping coiled at the base of his spine for two centuries, wrapped in oath and curse and the long gray habit of being something other than what he was. Taking human shape cost him. It cost him the way blood costs, the way burning a ship's own timbers costs — it bought speed and hands and a sword-arm, and it left him weak afterward, mortal-weak, for hours. In two hundred years he had taken the shape four times. Three of those times had been mistakes.

He took it a fifth time.

The change tore through him like a river reversing — fur to skin, fang to tongue, four legs folding into two — and he came up out of it standing, a man in ragged grey, drawing the dead knight's sword off his back where it hung even in wolf-shape, because some things a curse cannot make you put down. The nearest hunter had time to widen his eyes. That was all the time the world gave him.

Fen killed the first man before the man finished inhaling. The second died badly and loudly. The third, fourth, and fifth came at him together, and they were good — Corvane's hounds were always good — and Fen was fast but his body was new-minted and clumsy, two hundred years out of practice at having knees, and a blade opened his side like a letter being slit. He barely felt it. That was the danger of the shape; pain arrived late, like a messenger from a distant war. He killed the fifth and turned to find the sixth and seventh circling, patient, and understood with cold clarity that he was going to die here, ten yards from the road's mouth, having bought exactly nothing—

Except he heard her.

Not footsteps. Not shouting. A whistle — three notes, high and wrong, the call Maren had taught them for *danger, come to me* — and Yara came out of the dark at the hunters' backs with a rock in each hand and no sense whatsoever, and threw the first one into the temple of the sixth man, who dropped like a sack of grain, and the world rearranged itself around her audacity.

What happened next happened quickly and ugly. Fen got his sword up. Yara got her second rock into someone's throat. A crossbow spoke from the ridge and the bolt took Fen high in the chest, punching through muscle near the shoulder, spinning him half around, and the last thing he saw clearly before the dark came down was Yara's face — white, furious, terrified — as she dragged him by the collar toward the black opening of the deep road, screaming words he couldn't parse, and the darkness swallowed them both.

* * *

He woke to firelight on stone and the worst pain of his very long life.

That was saying something. Two centuries gave a body a deep archive of agony to draw from, and Fen had contributed generously: the breaking of the covenant, the curse itself, the first terrible year of learning to walk on four legs and think with a nose. None of it compared to the white-hot wire drawn through his chest and side, none of it compared to the fever sitting on him like a hot stone, and none of it — none of it — compared to the sight that greeted his cracking eyes.

Yara sat beside him, cross-legged, sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hands red to the wrist with his blood. She had a needle in one hand and thread pulled taut between her fingers, and she was sewing him closed with the same unhurried precision he'd watched her use to test a suspect custard, and she was crying without any sound at all, tears sliding steadily down a face that refused to change expression, because apparently even grief had to pass her inspection before it was allowed out.

"You're awake," she said. Her voice was scraped raw. "Good. Hold still. If you tear these stitches I'm letting you die out of spite."

"You—" His throat was ash. "You came back. Your decoy—"

"My decoy worked beautifully until I abandoned it to save an idiot." She tied off a stitch, bit the thread, reached for the next. "Three of them chased me. I lost them in the stream. Then I heard fighting and I came back, which Maren would tell me was stupid, and I would tell Maren that I've spent nineteen years watching other people decide what's worth dying for, and I'm done with it."

"You should have gone through the road alone."

"Yes," she agreed placidly, threading her needle. "And you should have stayed a wolf and run, instead of doing — whatever that was." Her eyes lifted to his face for the first time, and they were terrible and clear. "You took human shape. You've never done that in front of me. You told me it nearly kills you every time."

"It doesn't nearly kill me." He coughed, tasted copper. "It just feels that way."

"Fen." The needle stopped. "You're bleeding from places I didn't know had names. I've sewn kitchen wounds my whole life and I have never seen anything like what that bolt did to you. A wolf heals by morning. Tell me a wolf heals by morning."

Silence stretched between them, filled by the drip of water somewhere deeper in the mountain and the crackle of the small fire she'd built against the road's mouth, keeping the dark at bay. He looked at her — this stubborn, underfed, impossible girl whom fate had handed to him like a debt coming due — and understood that the moment had arrived that he had been dreading since the ford, since the tower, since the first night she'd fallen asleep against his flank in the rain and he had lain rigid until dawn rather than wake her.

Loyalty said keep the secret. Truth said she had earned it twice over, once with the poison garden and once with the rocks and the blood on her hands, and that loyalty without truth was just another leash.

"The fever will tell you anyway," he rasped. "Better you hear it from me."

* * *

He told her all of it, because the fever made lying expensive and because she deserved the whole ledger.

His name had been Fennric, once. He'd been born in a kingdom that no longer appeared on maps, sworn at nineteen to the Order of the Ember — knights bound to the covenant between humankind and dragonkind, guardians of the terms, escorts of the tribute, witnesses at the Altar. He'd been young and certain and very good with a sword, and he had believed, the way the young believe, that oaths were armor.

"When the queen refused to pay," he said, and his voice went strange and distant, the voice of a man reciting words worn smooth by two hundred years of repetition, "the court called it wisdom. Mercy. *Why should a life be spent to warm lizards?* They didn't understand that the covenant wasn't a tax. It was a heartbeat shared between two bodies. Refuse it, and both bodies sicken." He stared at the fire. "We knew. The Order knew. Twelve of us rode to the Altar to seal it ourselves, to pay the price in the queen's place, and we were too slow. The breaking caught us on the mountain. Dragon-fire went out of the world like a snuffed candle, and the backlash—" His hand moved unconsciously to his chest, to the scar beneath the fresh wound. "It unmade what we were. The others died. I didn't get the mercy of dying. The old magic bound me into the shape of the thing I'd failed to be. A guardian. A guard dog. Cursed to protect the covenant's heirs until the covenant stands again — and unable to stand among humans while I do it. Unable to warn them properly. Unable to do anything but watch, and fail, and watch, and fail."

"How many bearers?" Yara asked quietly.

"Seven before you." He named them, because they deserved naming, and his voice cracked on the fourth name and held steady after that through sheer discipline. "Some died of hunters. Some turned back and lived small frightened lives, and the map went dormant in their hands until they passed it on. One almost reached the Altar." He closed his eyes. "Sera. She got within a day's climb. Corvane's predecessor found her. I was fifty miles away, chasing a false trail they'd laid for me, and by the time I understood, it was over, and the curse tightened like a fist, and I swore I would never leave a bearer's side again. Which is why your little trick at the road's mouth made me so angry. Which is why I did something stupid instead."

"You did something stupid," Yara confirmed, "because loyalty is the only language you've been allowed to speak for two hundred years, and it has no word in it for *let me help you*."

He turned his head to look at her. Fever made everything swim, but her face was steady, and there was no pity in it — only that fierce, forensic attention she brought to everything, the gaze that weighed and measured and refused to flinch.

"You're not angry," he said. "I lied to you. About what I am. About all of it."

"You didn't lie. You just didn't translate." She set down the needle and wiped her hands on a cloth, methodically, finger by finger. "A wolf who speaks with a man's grief and carries a dead knight's sword and flinches every time someone says the word *oath* — that's not a secret, Fen. That's just a truth waiting for me to be ready for it. I grew up in a valley that scrubs its histories clean. I know exactly what it looks like when someone can't say a thing. It looks like silence, not deceit." She paused. "I'm furious about Sera, though. Not at you. At everyone who made you carry her alone."

The fever chose that moment to crest, and the world went white at the edges. He felt her cool hand on his brow, felt her move with brisk competence — water, willow bark from her endless pockets, a wet cloth folded with kitchen precision — and the last thing he registered before the dark took him again was her voice, low and steady, talking to him the way he'd talked to her through the poison garden:

"Stay with me. I've survived worse than you. Both of us have."

* * *

The fever dreamed him badly.

He dreamed of the mountain, of the twelve riders, of dragon-fire going out like a lamp in a window. He dreamed of Sera's face. He dreamed, worst of all, of the queen — not cruel, just tired, just human, choosing her son's life over the world's, and he woke from that one gasping, with Yara's hand pinning his shoulder and her voice cutting through the dark: "You're safe. You're stitched. Lie down before you open my work."

But it was the dream after that one that mattered — a shallow, feverish half-dream in which he lay in a kitchen he had never seen, in a valley that kept magic out, and a girl of eight years stood on a stool to reach the tasting bench, and an old cook said *spit it out, love, spit it out, that's the whole art of it* — and Fen understood, in the way dreams understand, that Yara had been raised inside a fortress built of someone else's decisions, and that every wall of it had been mortared with love, and that love and cages are not mutually exclusive, and that someday soon she was going to ask him whose decision it had been, and he was going to have to answer.

Because he knew. He had known since the valley's edge, since the day the wards parted for her like a door recognizing its key. Someone had carried a newborn through wards that repel magic, and wards do not part for smugglers.

They part for invitations.

When the fever broke, near dawn, he found her awake beside him, sharpening her knife against a whetstone with slow, even strokes, watching the entrance of the deep road where the darkness still pooled.

"Fen," she said, without turning. "In the story you told me. The child smuggled into the valley. The one raised outside magic so she could choose freely." The whetstone paused. "You said the queens misread the covenant for generations. You said her whole protected life was someone else's plan." Now she turned, and the firelight caught the birthmark at her wrist — the sigil Maren had named, the mark she'd spent nineteen years calling a stain. "Someone planned *me*. Someone carried me through wards that don't open for strangers." Her eyes were calm and absolutely terrifying. "Who carried me?"

And Fen, who had failed seven bearers and one covenant and had sworn never again to fail her, discovered that the hardest truth was the one he'd been carrying longest — and that she was not going to let him leave the mountain without it.
