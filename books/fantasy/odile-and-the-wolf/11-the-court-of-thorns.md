## 11. The Court of Thorns

The Thornwood did not begin so much as it *arrived* — one stride the road was ordinary pine and loam, and the next the trees stood too tall and too close, their trunks black as iron, their branches laced together overhead like fingers conspiring. The light that filtered down was green-gold and slow, moving through the canopy the way honey moves through water.

Yara stopped at the edge and made herself look at it properly, the way she looked at an unfamiliar dish before tasting.

"Rule one," she said quietly. "Never eat anything you haven't watched someone else survive."

Fen's ears flicked back. *You're not going to eat anything here.*

"I'm going to be offered things," she said. "That's worse."

The wolf paced a half-circle in front of her, hackles lifting at nothing she could see. *I've told you what I know of the thorn-court, which is almost nothing, and none of it good. They bargain like rivers run — always downhill, always toward themselves. And they have not forgotten the covenant. Some of them may have loved it.*

"Then they'll want to help."

*Some of them will want to help so badly they'll help you to death.* He sat, tail curling around his paws, grey fur drinking the green light. *Say nothing you don't mean. Accept nothing freely given. And if a lady of thorns offers you her name, do not give yours in return.*

"Noted." Yara adjusted the strap of her pack — lighter now than it had been two weeks ago, heavier in ways weight couldn't measure. The map rode against her ribs where the dragon-hide had warmed to her skin, or perhaps she'd warmed to it. She could no longer tell. "One more question."

*Ask.*

"When we get in there — when I'm standing in front of whatever passes for a throne — how do I keep from being afraid?"

Fen regarded her for a long moment with eyes the color of storm-water. Then, unexpectedly, he huffed something that might have been laughter. *You don't. You taste the fear, note its flavor, and swallow anyway. You've been doing it since the ford.*

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she stepped into the wood.

* * *

The path knew where it was going even if she didn't. It wound between roots the size of cottages, past flowers that turned to track them — not lazily, not like sunflowers following sun, but with the deliberate attention of faces. Once, something small and pale darted across the trail ahead, and Yara caught only a glimpse: a child-shaped thing with too many joints, watching from behind a trunk with eyes like wet river stones.

"Don't look at it directly," Fen murmured, low. "Looking is an invitation."

She fixed her gaze on the middle distance and kept walking. Behind her, she heard the small thing follow for a while, its footsteps making no sound at all, until some boundary it could not cross stopped it and its attention slid away like water off wax.

They walked for hours, or what felt like hours; in the Thornwood, Yara suspected time was a courtesy extended rather than a law obeyed. Her legs ached in a way that felt honest, at least. Hunger came and went. Twice she catalogued plants along the path out of pure habit — moonwort, she noted, and something like foxglove but wrong, its bells hanging inverted, dripping a nectar that smelled of rot-sweetness. *Deadly,* her tongue reported without being asked. *Deadly in a way that knows it's deadly.*

Then the trees opened, all at once, like a curtain drawn by a hand.

The court of thorns filled a hollow where no hollow should have been. Bramble-palaces rose in spirals, walls of living blackthorn woven tight as masonry, hung with blossoms white as bone. Lanterns floated among the upper branches — no flame in them, only a soft imprisoned glow, like fireflies pressed into glass. And everywhere, everywhere, the people: tall figures in garments of leaf and bark and spider-silk, faces beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, all edges and shine. Some had antlers. Some had crowns of thorn worn so long the wood had grown into the flesh beneath. None of them blinked often enough.

At the hollow's heart stood a dais of woven roots, and on it a throne that had once, Yara thought, been a tree — grown, not built, its seat polished smooth by centuries of sitting.

And on the steps below the throne, waiting as though she had known the exact moment of their arrival, stood a woman made of winter and roses.

She was tall, robed in grey-green that moved like smoke, and from her shoulders and sleeves and the crown of her head grew thorns — real ones, black and curved, some budding, some in bloom with small red flowers that Yara's trained eye identified immediately as *not roses at all*. Her face was ageless the way the wood was ageless. Her smile was perfect and had never once, Yara was certain, reached her eyes.

"The wolf returns," the lady said, and her voice was the sound wind makes through brambles. "And he brings a morsel."

"She brings herself," Fen said, stepping forward, "and asks audience of the Thorn Court."

"Morsels don't ask." The lady descended one step. Two. Her bare feet left no prints on the moss. "They are tasted. Tell me, little valley-child — do you know what you're standing in?"

"A court," Yara said.

"A garden," the lady corrected softly. "Everything here grows. Everything here is *growing*. Even now." Her gaze traveled over Yara like a hand. "Even you."

Around the hollow, the court had gone still in the way of predators deciding. Yara felt their attention settle on her skin like frost. Her heart was hammering — she let it, acknowledged it, named its flavor: fear, yes, but under it something older and steadier, the flat calm of a girl who had swallowed death a thousand times and learned its manners.

"I've come to petition passage," she said. "Through the Thornwood to the deep road's western mouth. We can't go around; around is Corvane's country now."

"And why should a garden open its gate for a weed?"

"Because I'm not asking for free passage." Yara held up her hands, empty. "I'm offering trade."

Something rippled through the assembled fae — interest, hunger, amusement, hard to say. The lady's smile widened by a degree that made Fen's growl start low in his chest.

"Trade," she repeated, tasting the word. "What has a food-taster to sell that a lady of thorns could want? Your obedience? Your years? Your firstborn, someday, if you ever dare to love?" She drifted closer, close enough that Yara could smell her — cold earth and crushed petals and underneath, faintly, something chemical and wrong, like almonds gone bitter. "Everyone offers those. Everyone offers the same dull coin. What else is in your purse, child of the valley?"

Yara thought of rule one. Never eat anything you haven't watched someone else survive.

But there was a second rule, older, taught to her by Mistress Odile in the castle kitchens with flour on her hands: *before you serve a dish, know your guest.*

"You're poisoned," Yara said.

The hollow went silent enough to hear a petal fall.

The lady stopped. For the first time, something moved behind her eyes — not surprise exactly. Recognition. The look of a card-player whose hidden card has just been named aloud.

"There," Yara went on, steady, pointing — carefully, respectfully, at the lady's left sleeve where the thorns grew densest. "In the growth at your wrist. It's in the sap. You've been carrying it a long time — decades, maybe longer. It's why the blooms on that arm come in red while the rest come white. The plant is fighting itself. Whatever rooted in you that year, it never left. It's eating you slowly from the inside of your own crown."

"How—" The lady's voice cracked, very slightly, like ice underfoot. Around the court, whispers rose and died.

"I taste things," Yara said simply. "It's what I am. I've spent my whole life learning what kills and what cures and what merely hurts, and I'm telling you that what's growing in you is killing you, and that I know three things that might slow it, and one thing that might stop it — and I can't make any of them here, because they need materials from outside your wood. Which is another reason," she added, "you want me to leave alive. I'm more useful coming back."

For a long moment the lady of thorns simply looked at her. The floating lanterns dimmed and brightened as though the whole court were holding a breath it didn't need.

"You would cure me," the lady said at last, "the enemy at your petition."

"I'd trade with you. Cure for passage, and safe-conduct, and one answer honestly given." Yara folded her hands to keep them from shaking. "That's my offer. Take it or refuse it, but know that I saw. Nobody else in this garden saw. That's worth something, and you know it is."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, a lantern guttered.

Then the lady laughed — a single note, clear and cold and genuinely delighted, and every thorn on her body bloomed at once, a sudden riot of impossible flowers, red and white together.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, the *nerve* of you. A mortal weed walks into my garden and tells the rose she's dying." She swept down the remaining steps until she stood an arm's length away, close enough that Yara could see the tiny movements of the thorns, breathing, growing, *feeding*. "Very well, taster. Passage granted — the western gate opens at moonrise, and no thorn of mine shall touch you on the road. Safe-conduct, sworn. And your answer, when you return with your cure, honestly given." Her head tilted. "But first, the custom. All bargains in this court are sealed at table. Will you dine with me, taster? Or does the poison-expert fear a cup of wine?"

There it was. Rule one, arriving right on schedule.

Yara met her eyes. "I'll dine," she said. "And I'll drink from your cup — after you drink from mine."

The lady's eyebrows arched. "You carry wine?"

"I carry tea." She unslung her pack and drew out the small tin Odile had pressed into her hands the night she fled — willowbark, chamomile, a pinch of dried nettle, the humble inventory of a kitchen, brewed in a dented copper pot over Fen's careful flame. "Nothing enchanted. Nothing clever. Just what a taster drinks when she wants to know she'll wake up tomorrow."

"And if I refuse to drink it?"

"Then no bargain," Yara said. "You taught your court the rules. I'm only playing by them."

Another silence — but this one had a different texture. Around the hollow, Yara realized, the fae had drawn closer, forming a ring, and their expressions had changed. Not hunger anymore. Something hungrier than hunger: *delight*. A story was happening, and stories were the rarest vintage of all.

The lady of thorns extended one thorned hand. "Pour, then."

* * *

They drank at a table the roots made for them, rising from the moss like a thing remembering it had once been furniture. The lady took the tin cup Yara offered, turned it in her fingers, examined the dents, the old burns in the copper, the tea inside — pale gold, steaming, smelling of nothing but chamomile and honesty.

"To the valley-child," she said, and drank.

Not a sip. The whole cup, watching Yara over the rim the entire time, making certain she witnessed it. When she set it down, her lips curved. "Your turn."

A goblet appeared in her hands — crystal grown, not cut, holding wine the color of garnets. Yara took it. Held it. Let the smell rise: dark fruit, oaksmoke, and beneath it, so faint a lesser nose would have missed it entirely, a thread of something green and sharp, like sap from a freshly cut stem.

*Dreamless sleep,* her tongue reported. *Not lethal. Twelve hours, maybe more. In this court, twelve hours is a weapon.*

She drank it down.

The court made a sound — a soft collective exhale, hundreds of voices at once. The lady stared at her. "You knew."

"I knew."

"And you drank anyway."

"You needed to know if I trusted you," Yara said. "Now you do. And I needed to know if you'd waste a sleeping-draught on a guest you've already decided to bargain with." She set down the empty goblet. "You wouldn't. It would insult us both. So it wasn't really a test at all — it was a courtesy. And I accepted it."

For a heartbeat the lady's composure slipped — truly slipped — and what showed underneath was ancient and tired and, briefly, grateful. Then the mask returned, smoother than before.

"Moonrise," she said. "The western gate. Go, before I decide I like you too much to let you leave." She rose, thorns whispering against silk, and paused. "One thing freely given, taster, because you gave me truth unpurchased. The Ember Altar you walk toward — you've heard what it demands?"

"A life," Yara said. "Freely given."

"Yes. That is what everyone hears." The lady's voice dropped, and the whole court leaned in to listen. "But the fae were old when the covenant was young, and I will tell you what *we* heard, at the first sealing, from the dragon-mother's own mouth. 'A life freely given' does not mean a life ended. Fire does not want death. Fire wants *fuel*. It wants everything a life is made of — the years, the gifts, the loves, the self you have built and defended and survived by." Her thorned hand closed slowly. "The queens who paid before paid wrongly, and their payment curdled, and the covenant broke. Do not pay as they paid. Find out what the fire actually eats before you feed it your throat."

The words landed in Yara's chest like stones dropped into a well — falling, falling, the splash still far below.

"Why tell me this?" she managed.

"Because you told me I was dying," the lady said, "and meant it kindly." She turned away, robes smoking, and the court parted before her like water. At the edge of the firelight she stopped, half-turned, red blooms glowing at her wrist. "Come back with my cure, taster. I find I wish to live long enough to see what you become."

* * *

The western gate was a gap in the bramble-wall shaped exactly like a doorway, and it stood open under a rising moon, spilling silver light across the moss.

Fen walked beside Yara in silence for a long time before he spoke. *You drank a sleeping draught to seal a bargain.*

"It was only twelve hours."

*Twelve hours in a fae court.* His shoulder pressed warm against her leg. *You could have woken as a songbird. Or a rumor.*

"But I didn't." She glanced down at him. "Because she didn't put it there to use. And because I read the whole cup before I drank — the wine, the host, the room. That's the job, Fen. That's all the job has ever been. Tonight it finally mattered."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh. *Odile would be proud.*

The name hit her sideways, the way it always did. She kept walking.

They passed through the gate, and the Thornwood's green gloom fell away behind them into open hill-country under a wide cold sky — the deep road's marker-stones visible far ahead, running down into the dark like a staircase into the world's cellar. Yara's legs shook now that they were allowed to. She sat down hard on a warm rock and let them.

But the lady's words would not sit still.

*A life freely given does not mean a life ended. Fire wants fuel. Find out what the fire actually eats before you feed it your throat.*

She turned the phrase over and over, the way she'd turn an unknown seed on her tongue, testing, cataloguing, refusing to swallow it whole. Fuel. Years, gifts, loves, the self you survived by.

Her hand drifted, without her permission, to the birthmark on her forearm — the stain she'd carried all her life, the sigil Maren had knelt over in the lamplight with tears in her eyes, saying *dedicated before birth, dedicated before breath.*

Every poison she had ever survived lived in her blood. Every dish, every draught, every slow lesson her body had learned in the Valley of Stillness — nineteen years of death, swallowed daily, mastered, *kept*. It was the only thing she had ever been. The taster's tongue. The survivor's blood.

If fire wanted fuel—

"Yara." Fen's voice, gentle, human-voiced, worried. *You've gone somewhere. Where?*

She looked at him — at the great grey shape of him, the storm-water eyes, the knight buried under the curse — and found she couldn't say it yet. Saying it would make it a plan, and plans had a way of becoming prices.

"Nowhere," she lied, and stood, and brushed off her skirts. "Come on. The deep road won't get any warmer waiting."

But as they walked down toward the dark, the lady's warning followed her like a second shadow, and in the pit of her stomach, quiet as a seed, a terrible understanding continued to grow:

that she already knew what the fire would ask for,

and that she had been carrying it, all along, in her veins.
