## 12. Halfway Burned

Fen dreamed of the old road again — the one that ran from the queen's hall to the mountain, the one he had walked a hundred times with a sword at his hip and a vow in his mouth like a second heartbeat.

In the dream it was night, and the sky was full of wings, and someone was screaming his name in a voice he had spent two lifetimes trying not to remember.

He woke because his ribs hurt.

That was new. For sixty years pain had been an animal's thing: thorns in the pad of a paw, the white flash of a trap, the dull ache of old wounds weather turned and re-turned. It had never been *this* — this deep human ache that started under his breastbone and radiated outward, as if someone had cracked him open along a seam and forgotten to close him again.

He was lying on a bed of bracken in the hollow behind the waterfall, where Yara had dragged him — dragged him, all hundred and ninety pounds of him, with a torn shoulder and a broken arm and no magic but stubbornness — after the ambush at the road's mouth. The wound in his side had been bound with strips of his own ruined shirt, knotted with the quick, economical competence of someone who had dressed a great many injuries that were not her own. Someone had built a fire small enough to hide its smoke. Someone had set a skin of water within reach of his hand without waking him.

He knew whose work that was before he opened his eyes.

Yara sat on the far side of the fire, cross-legged, mending one of his bandages with thread she'd pulled from the hem of her coat. The lantern — the pilgrim's lantern, the last living flame in the world — hung from a root above her head, and its light moved on her face like water. She did not look up when he stirred. Of course she didn't. She'd had three days to study every way a wounded man could shift in his sleep.

"You're supposed to be resting," she said.

"I'm supposed to be dead," Fen rasped. His voice came out wrong — too human, still strange to his own ears. "You've saved me twice now from something I earned."

"Then we'll call it even for the ford." She bit the thread, tied it off, and finally looked at him. Her eyes were red-rimmed. She would hate knowing that. "Your fever broke this morning. You talked."

Ah. There it was.

"What did I say?"

"A lot." She set down the bandage with exaggerated care, the way people do when they are buying time to keep their hands from shaking. "Mostly names. A woman called Sera. Something about a gate that wouldn't open. And you said—" Her jaw tightened. "You said, *I should have made her pay.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, water fell endlessly into the pool, patient as time.

"That's why you took human form," Yara said. "Not just to buy me time. You knew what it would cost. The curse — taking this shape cracks it, doesn't it? Every hour you spend as a man is an hour closer to dying as one." She leaned forward. "I want the truth, Fen. All of it. Not the wolf's version. Not the guard-dog version. You were there when the covenant broke. I've known that since the deep road, when you talked in your sleep like a man being tried by a court that never adjourns. So tell me. Who was Sera? What gate? And what did the queen refuse to pay?"

He could have lied. He had lied well for six decades; a wolf's silences are deeper than any man's.

But she had carried him through freezing water with a broken arm. She had shielded a stranger's lantern with her own body and been burned without scarring, and had said nothing about it, as if miracles were simply another item on her list of things to survive. And she was looking at him now with the expression of a woman who had spent nineteen years tasting other people's poison and had finally decided she deserved to know what was in her own cup.

So he told her.

* * *

"Her name was Sera of the Ember Line," Fen said. "The last queen of the old covenant. And she was the bravest coward I ever served."

Yara flinched at the phrasing, but he didn't soften it. Softness would have been a lie, and lies were how the whole ruin had begun.

"The covenant was older than either kingdom, older than the valley, older than the wards that raised you. In the beginning it was simple: dragon-fire keeps the land alive — the harvests, the rivers, the magic in the roots of things — and in return, the humans give the dragons what fire cannot make for itself." He held up his hand, flexing fingers that still felt borrowed. "A life freely given. Not taken. Never taken. Every generation, one soul came to the Ember Altar and gave themselves — willingly, gladly, some of them singing — and the fire took them into itself, and the land lived, and the dragons remembered us as kin instead of cattle.

"It was never meant to be a slaughter. That's the thing your histories got wrong, if they recorded anything at all. The Altar doesn't feed on death. It feeds on *gift*. A sacrifice dragged there screaming is worth nothing. Less than nothing. The fire turns away from coercion like a stomach turning from rot."

"And the queens?" Yara's voice was very quiet. "You said the last queen refused to pay."

"Sera was chosen young. Dedicated, like you — marked before birth, raised knowing. And she was magnificent, for a while. She trained at the Altar. She learned the old rites. And then she fell in love." He laughed, and the laugh hurt his ribs. "A common soldier. Mine, as it happens. I was her sworn shield, and I loved her the way dogs love — completely, stupidly, without any sense of my place. And she looked at me one winter night and asked me the question that broke the world.

"*Why must it be me?*"

The waterfall filled the silence for a moment.

"She wasn't wrong to ask," Fen went on. "That's the bitter joke of it. The question was honest. The question was *human*. But she didn't stop at asking. She began to look for a way around. A substitute. A loophole. And there are always men willing to sell a queen a loophole. She found a sorcerer who claimed the covenant could be satisfied with a life given *by proxy* — that a devoted knight's willing death might stand in a queen's stead. And I—" His jaw worked. "I said yes. God help me, I lay down on that Altar glad to do it, because I loved her more than I loved the world, and the world was going to burn either way.

"And the fire rejected me."

Yara had gone very still. "Because you weren't giving it freely. You were giving it to *her*."

"Yes." The word came out of him like a splinter. "It read the truth in me. My gift had a chain attached. And in the moment the flames turned away, Sera understood what she had done — sent a man she loved to die for a bargain she refused to honor — and instead of climbing onto the Altar herself, instead of paying the price she had been born to pay—"

He closed his eyes.

"—she ran. She broke the rite in the middle. And a covenant interrupted is worse than a covenant refused. The fire recoiled. The pact shattered like hot glass. And everything the flame had been holding back came through the crack at once — the blight, the wildings, the unraveling. The dragons withdrew to the mother's mountain to keep the last ember alive. The realms began to die slowly instead of living quickly. And Sera—"

"Sera was pregnant," Yara said.

He opened his eyes. She had gone pale in the lantern light, but her voice didn't waver. Nineteen years of swallowing poison taught a person to keep their face.

"You knew," Fen said.

"I suspected. Since Maren. Since the sigil." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Keep going."

"Sera fled east with the child in her belly. The king's spymaster — Corvane, though he was a younger and gentler man then — covered her flight, bought her time, lied to the council for her. Because he loved her too, in his way, though he never said it, and she never let him. And when the child was born, Sera made one final choice. The valley of Stillness — the one place in the world where magic cannot follow, where the covenant has no reach, where no fire can mark a child or bind her to a fate. She smuggled her daughter past the wards and left her at the gates with nothing but a name and a birthmark."

"And a plan," Yara said. Her voice had gone flat and strange. "She had a *plan* for me. Raise me outside magic so the covenant couldn't claim me before I could choose. So that when I came to the Altar — if I came — it would be my decision. Freely given." She laughed, one short, ugly note. "Freely. Do you hear yourself? Do you hear what you're saying? *Every part of my life was arranged.* The valley. The wards. Even the taster's post — did she arrange that too? Did someone decide that the girl who must one day choose death should first learn to survive it?"

"No one chose that for you," Fen said quietly. "You chose the kitchens yourself. At eleven. You walked out of the foundling house and asked the master cook for work because you wanted to be where the food was. Your mother hoped you'd grow up ordinary and happy. You grew up extraordinary instead. That was yours."

"But the cage was hers." Yara was on her feet now, moving, unable to be still — pacing the small hollow like something trapped, which, he thought, was exactly what she was discovering she had always been. "The walls were hers. Nineteen years, Fen. Nineteen years I thought the valley was home, and it was a *cradle with a lock on it*. Every friend I loved, every meal I tasted, every morning I woke up safe — all of it was designed. Manufactured innocence. I wasn't raised. I was *kept*."

"Yara—"

"Don't." She spun on him, and the lantern light caught her eyes and they were blazing, and for one absurd moment he thought: *there is dragon in her after all.* "Don't defend her. Don't explain her to me. I am so tired of everyone in this story loving me in ways that require me to be someone else's answer. My mother needed a loophole and I was it. The covenant needs a gift and I'm it. You need to repay a failure and apparently I'm it too—"

"That is not fair," Fen said, and his voice came out harder than he intended, hard enough that she stopped pacing. He pushed himself upright against the stone, ignoring the scream of his ribs, and met her eyes. "You want the truth? Here is more of it. I have followed your map for two hundred miles. I have bled for you twice and lain in the dirt listening to you count which berries won't kill us, cataloguing poisons in your head like coins in a purse, because survival is the only language you trust. And in all that time you have never once asked whether *anyone else* in this story gets to choose anything.

"I didn't take human form to repay a debt. I took it because the hunters were coming and a wolf can't hold a sword and I would rather die as the man I failed to be than live another century as the animal my failure made me. That was my choice. Ugly, and proud, and mine. Your mother's plan was flawed and desperate and yes, it used you — she was a frightened woman trying to leave her child one true freedom in a world that offers none. But don't you dare stand there and tell me that everything you are was manufactured. The valley kept magic out. It couldn't keep *you* out of the kitchens, or off the wall at night counting stars, or onto this road. Nobody planned your stubbornness, Yara. Believe me. I've met plans. Plans are neater than you."

The hollow rang with silence.

Her chest was heaving. Her fists were shaking at her sides. And then, horribly, her face crumpled — not into tears, worse, into grief, the deep kind, the kind that has nineteen years of sediment underneath it — and she sat down abruptly on the ground as if her legs had simply resigned.

"She named me after a swan," Yara said, to the fire. "Did you know that? Odile. The black swan. The one that dances a lie so beautifully she almost destroys everything." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, fast and angry, hating every second of it. "Was that a joke to her? Was I her penance?"

"I think," Fen said slowly, "that she wanted you to know that names can be survived. That you could dance someone else's story and still walk out of it alive."

Yara was quiet for a long time.

When she spoke again, the rage had burned down to coals, and coals last longer than flame.

"All right," she said. "Here's what I know, and here's what I'm going to do about it. The queen ran. Fine. I'm not her. I'm not running and I'm not paying anyone else's price either — not hers, not yours, not the covenant's out of guilt. When I get to that Altar, if I get there, whatever happens will happen because *I decided*, with open eyes, today, on this road. Not because a dead woman planned it. Not because a curse needs breaking. Me." She looked at him, and her eyes were wet and absolutely level. "Can you live with that? Because if you're following me hoping I'll be your redemption, Fen, you should turn around now."

Something in his chest — something older than the curse, older than the wolf — unclenched.

"I'm following you," Fen said, "because you counted the berries."

She blinked. "What?"

"Sixty years, I've watched humans face the end of the world. They pray, or they drink, or they freeze. You counted which plants were safe to eat. You prepared for tomorrow like tomorrow was worth preparing for. That's the freest thing I've ever seen anyone do." He managed something that had once, long ago, been a smile. "I'm not following a dead queen's plan. I'm following that."

Yara held his gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, briskly — the nod of a woman adding an ally to her inventory — and picked up the bandage she'd been mending, and the terrible moment passed the way her moments always did: survived, catalogued, folded away.

But Fen saw her hand tremble as she threaded the needle. And he saw her glance, just once, at the birthmark on her inner wrist — the covenant sigil, the stain, the brand of a promise made before she was born — and then deliberately turn her sleeve down over it, as if to say: *you don't own me yet.*

* * *

They traveled north through the next two days, slow, his wound forcing a pace that made Yara mutter calculations under her breath — food, distance, season — and somewhere past the second dawn the land itself began to whisper what was coming.

Fen smelled it before anything: a wrongness in the wind, thin and grey, like air sieved through ash.

By midday the birds had gone quiet. By evening they crested a ridge above a farming valley, and Yara stopped so suddenly he nearly walked into her.

Below, the fields should have been gold with early barley. They stood grey. Not dead — worse than dead — *unmade*, stalks faded like cloth left too long in the sun, color bleeding out of the whole landscape as if the world were a drawing someone was erasing from the edges inward. On the road below, a column of refugees moved westward, carts and children and hollow-eyed oxen, fleeing toward lands that Fen knew were fading too.

"The fire," Yara whispered. Her hand had gone to the lantern slung at her hip, and through its glass the last living flame flickered — guttered — flickered again, like a heartbeat skipping. "It's faster than we thought. Fen, it's *failing*."

And far to the east, over the mountains they still had to cross, the sky had gone the color of old scars.
