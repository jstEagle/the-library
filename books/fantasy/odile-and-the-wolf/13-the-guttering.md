## 13. The Guttering

The sky over Thornwood had been blue when they entered it. Three days later, it was the color of unwashed wool.

Yara noticed it first in the way Fen stopped eating. He'd been tearing at a haunch of rabbit with more enthusiasm than manners — she'd learned that wolves, even speaking ones, did not have table manners — when his head lifted mid-chew and his ears went flat.

"What is it?"

He didn't answer right away. His nostrils flared, working the wind that came down out of the north, and whatever he found in it made him set the rabbit down as though it had personally offended him.

"Smoke," he said at last. "Not woodsmoke. Not cookfire." A pause, and then, quieter, the way men say things they don't want to be true: "Ash."

"Ash from where? There's no fire."

"There doesn't need to be one." He rose, shaking out his fur, and looked east, toward the grey smear along the horizon. "When dragon-fire gutters, Yara, it doesn't go out like a candle. It burns backward. It eats its own light on the way down. Everything it ever warmed feels the cold of it."

She stood beside him and squinted into that bruised distance. She thought of the lantern in the deep road, the pilgrim who had carried the last living flame cupped against his chest like a sick child, and how it had flickered even then, even wrapped in prayer and oilcloth.

"How long?"

"I don't know." And she understood, from the flatness of his voice, that this was the worst answer a guardian could give.

* * *

They came down out of the hills onto the King's Road on the fourth morning, and the road was full of people.

Yara had seen refugees before — in stories, mostly, and once through an arrow-slit in the castle wall, a ragged line of border folk fleeing a flood years ago, distant as painted figures. This was not distant. The road was choked for as far as she could see in both directions: carts piled with children and chickens and grandmother-clock cases that would never tick again; families walking with everything they owned strapped to their backs; a man pulling a plow-horse that was too weak now to pull anything but itself. No one shouted. That was the thing that frightened her most. Ten thousand people on a road, and almost no one was shouting. They had passed beyond shouting into something quieter and heavier.

A woman sat by the roadside nursing a baby, and Yara's eyes went to her the way a taster's eyes go to the dish that smells wrong. The woman's lips were cracked. The baby was quiet — too quiet — and there was a grey dusting, faint as flour, on the blanket's edge.

Fen saw it too. "Fallout," he said. "From the east. When the fire fails, everything it made comes apart. The old growths, the charmed fields, the wells dug under blessing. The land forgets how to be generous."

"The baby—"

"Is not mine to heal, and I am a wolf." He said it harshly, but she heard what lived underneath the harshness: helplessness, wearing armor. "There are hedge-healers ahead. Maren's kind. The villages remember the old rites; they'll do what can be done."

"And if what can be done isn't enough?"

He didn't answer, which was an answer.

They walked among the refugees because there was no other way — the road was the road — and Yara did what nineteen years in a kitchen had trained her body to do without asking her mind's permission. She read people. She read the carts. A farmer with blisters on his hands who hadn't held a plow since spring, meaning he'd been a soldier or meant to become one. A merchant whose strongbox rode under the grain sacks, meaning he expected robbery. Children who flinched at shadows moving east-to-west, meaning something in the east had taught them to fear movement.

Halfway through the morning she found herself crouched beside a cart, turning a sack of turnips in her hands, sniffing them, pressing her thumbnail into the flesh.

"They're rotten," she said, puzzled. "All of them. Rotten from the inside. The outside looks—" She stopped.

"Looks fine," Fen said grimly. "That's what the guttering does. Things keep their shape after the life goes out of them." He looked at her a moment too long, and she knew he wasn't thinking about turnips anymore.

She put the sack down gently, the way you put down something dead.

* * *

They reached the village of Harrow's Bend at dusk, or what should have been dusk. The light was wrong all day — the sun a pale coin behind gauze — and the village lamps came on while the sky still held a ghost of daylight, which made the whole place look lit from below by some submerged disaster.

Harrow's Bend had been a market town once. You could see its bones: the wide square, the guild-marks carved above doorways, the fountain with a fish-lion spouting nothing. Now the square was a camp. Fires burned in iron rings dragged out of houses, and around each fire sat clusters of people performing the ancient choreography of displacement: mending, portioning, watching the road.

A queue had formed at the well. Yara joined it without deciding to, carrying their waterskin, and it was in the queue that the road finally spoke to her.

"She won't drink," said the woman ahead of her, to no one, to everyone. Her daughter — six, maybe seven — stood pressed against her skirt, staring at the well bucket with an expression Yara recognized from mirrors she'd never owned. Terror. Not of the water. Of what might be *in* it.

"It's good water," the bucket-man said wearily. He'd said it a hundred times today; Yara could hear the hundred repetitions worn into his voice like a groove. "I've blessed it myself. Old rite, my grandmother's rite. It's clean."

"You blessed it with *what*, though?" Another voice, from the back. A man with a miller's forearms and a miller's dust ground permanent into his knuckles. "With words? Words are just words now. My father's field grew under a blessing for sixty years. Last month it grew ash. You want to tell me words are enough?"

The queue rippled. Somewhere a child started crying and was hushed with more force than comfort.

Yara heard herself speak before she decided to. "May I?"

Every face turned. She held up the waterskin like a small banner of surrender. "Let me taste it first. If it's poisoned, I'll know. It's — it's what I do. I'm a taster."

It wasn't entirely true anymore. But it was true enough, and it was the only coin she carried that these people could spend.

The bucket-man filled her cup without argument — hope makes people obedient — and Yara drank, letting her tongue do its old work, cataloguing mineral and moss and the clean stone-taste of deep water. Nothing else. Nothing metallic, nothing bitter, nothing sweet where sweetness shouldn't live.

"It's good," she said. "Clean. Cold from a deep vein — see how it tastes of iron and dark? Water that's gone bad tastes of rot or of nothing at all. This tastes of the mountain it came from." She drank again, ostentatiously, and handed the cup to the frightened girl with a smile she manufactured from spare parts. "Your turn."

The girl drank. The mother wept quietly into her own hands, and the line moved, and word traveled down the queue faster than water ever could: *there's a taster here, there's a taster, bring her your grain, bring her your well-water.*

By full dark Yara had tasted forty things. Well-water and rainwater caught in a barrel. Barley that smelled of dust and barley that smelled of home. A jar of honey a grandfather refused to eat until someone trustworthy told him it hadn't soured. Dried apples, hard cheese, a suspicious stew from a communal pot that three hundred people were too hungry to distrust on their own.

She tasted them all, and every taste cost a little more than the last, and she said nothing about that, because what would she even say? *My immunity is finite, my survival has a bottom, please form an orderly queue.* Fen watched her from the edge of the firelight with an expression she couldn't read and didn't try to, because reading it might mean stopping.

It was near midnight when the old priestess found her.

Yara smelled her first — incense and camphor and age — and looked up from a cup of goat's milk to find a tiny woman wrapped in grey wool regarding her with eyes gone pale as frost.

"You're tasting for them," the priestess said. Not a question.

"It needs doing."

"Mm." The old woman lowered herself onto the bench with the economy of someone who has done it ten thousand times. "You know what I did tonight, before you came? I walked the rows. Every family. Counted them." She held up her hands, and they trembled slightly, whether from age or something else Yara couldn't tell. "Four hundred and eleven souls at last count. Sixty-one arrived today. Nine died on the road behind them — we buried three at the crossroads already. The eastern realms are emptying, child. The fire that kept the blights off their fields, the fire that sealed the plague-pits, the fire under the forges that never went cold — it's going out, and everything it promised is being called due."

Yara set down the milk. Her hands felt strange, detached, as though they belonged to someone in a story.

"I'm one person," she said. "I've tasted forty cups. What is that? Four hundred and eleven people and I've tasted forty cups."

"You think that's what matters?" The priestess's voice was dry as seed husks. "Child, I've watched them all evening. Do you know what they see when they watch you drink first? They see someone willing to stand between them and the poison. That's worth more than the tasting. That's worth everything. Fear is the thing killing this road faster than ash is. Fear makes fathers hoard. Fear makes neighbors thieves. And you—" she tapped Yara's wrist, right over the covenant sigil hidden beneath her sleeve, and Yara flinched, "—you walk up and down this square drinking first, and the fear walks away from you like a dog called home."

Yara stared at her. "I'm not brave. I'm a food-taster. I taste because it's my job and because my tongue doesn't die. That's not courage, that's a condition."

"Isn't it, though." The priestess smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile, but it was an honest one. "Then why are your eyes wet?"

Yara discovered she had no answer, and that her eyes were, in fact, wet.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around the third hour she found Fen at the square's edge, sitting in wolf-shape at the boundary between firelight and dark, watching the eastern sky the way sailors watch weather.

"Tell me," she said, sitting beside him close enough that his warmth reached her. Wolves ran hot. It was one of the few gentle facts about him.

He was silent so long she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "You want to know what the world was like when the covenant held."

"I want to know what we lose if it doesn't. Not thrones. Not dragons. Them." She nodded back at the sleeping square, the mounded blankets, the iron ring fires ticking as they cooled. "Them."

Fen's ears turned toward the camp. "There was a village," he said, "not unlike this one, eight hundred years ago. I know because I ate dinner in it." A dry sound that might once have been a laugh. "In my first life. The harvest had come in heavy and the village had promised its tithe to the dragon-roost, as villages did, and Vessarath's fire came down the mountain once a year — a single breath across the fields at sowing, a single spark carried to the hearth of the temple. One breath. That's all the covenant asked of her. And in exchange: no blight, no rot, no famine, no plague crossing the ward-stones. The fire remembered the fields the way a mother remembers her children's names."

"And now she's forgetting."

"Now she's dying. And a dying mind lets go of names one at a time." He looked east, at the grey. "That ash on the wind isn't destruction, Yara. It's forgetting. The land is losing the memory of how to feed people. And it will get worse. The fae courts will close like fists. The dwarven roads will go dark. Magic won't unravel everywhere at once — it will unravel *locally*, randomly, like lights going out house by house in a city at night. And no one will know which house goes next."

Yara pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them. In the square, a mother shifted a sleeping child from one shoulder to the other, automatically, tenderly, asleep herself almost. Someone laughed softly at a fire across the way — laughter, here, tonight, with the world going grey at the edges. Humans were astonishing. She'd always known it abstractly, from the kitchens, from the scullery girls singing while they gutted fish. But she felt it now in her ribs.

"When I left the valley," she said slowly, "it was because the map knew my name. Because someone died saying it. Because Corvane wanted me and I wanted — I don't know what I wanted. To matter, maybe. To be more than a mouth that survives." She turned to look at Fen's profile, silver-edged in the failing light. "But sitting in that well-queue today, watching that little girl afraid of water — Fen, I wasn't thinking about my name. I was thinking *somebody has to drink first.*"

"That's the job," Fen said quietly. "That was always the job. The queens forgot it. The kings never learned it. The covenant was never a contract between crowns, Yara. It was a promise between the fire and everyone who needed feeding." His voice roughened. "I swore to guard the bearers of that promise, and I failed, and I've had eight hundred years to learn exactly what failure costs. It costs *this*. Roads like rivers of grief. Wells nobody trusts. Babies breathing ash."

"So we go faster."

His head turned. Grey eye met hers, and in it she saw the question he wouldn't ask: *do you understand what you're volunteering for?*

"We go faster," she repeated, "and when we reach the Altar, we ask the fire what it actually wants. Not what queens assumed. Not what Corvane fears. What it *wants*." She stood, brushing straw from her skirts, and looked east one last time. The grey had crept visibly closer than yesterday. By tomorrow it would eat another horizon. "Because those people back there aren't going to make it to any Altar. They're going to make it to winter, if they're lucky. And winter is coming regardless. So whatever the price turns out to be—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"—I'd rather pay it standing up than spend the rest of my life tasting other people's fear and calling it survival."

Fen rose too, huge against the stars, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Then he dipped his great head — the closest a wolf comes to a bow.

"Dawn, then," he said. "We take the pilgrim road east. It's harder ground, but it's days shorter, and there's a shrine at the pass where the fire's memory runs deepest." He paused, and his voice dropped low, almost gentle. "Sleep, Yara. Even bridges need rest."

But sleep didn't come, and near morning she dreamed — not of the Altar, not of dragons, but of the castle kitchens, of Odile the head cook setting a bowl in front of her as she had every morning of nineteen years, and saying, in the dream as in life:

"Eat first, little bird. Everyone else waits for you."

She woke with tears on her face and ash on the wind, and far to the east, where the mountains met the ruined sky, she saw it: a single thread of red, thin as a heartbeat, pulsing once against the grey.

Still burning. Barely.

"Fen," she whispered. "I can see it."

He was already awake. He had been for hours. "Then we run."
