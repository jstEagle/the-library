## 14. Corvane's Bargain

The trap was set with a table.

That was the first thing Yara noticed when the riders closed the road ahead of them at dusk — not swords, not crossbows, though there were plenty of both among the thirty men in Corvane's livery — but a folding table, two chairs, and a single lantern burning with ordinary, honest oil. Someone had even laid out bread and a wedge of pale cheese. It was the most menacing thing she had ever seen.

"He's mocking us," Fen growled low in his throat. He stood at her shoulder in wolf-shape, hackles lifted, grey fur silvered by the last light. "A parley. As if we were equals."

"We are on a road," Yara said quietly. "He could have taken us on a road anywhere in three hundred miles. Instead he wants to talk." She looked at the table, the bread, the chairs facing each other across it like two halves of a scale. "Which means he needs something from me."

"Or he wants you to *think* he does."

"That too." She pulled her cloak tighter. The mountain was close now — she could feel it the way you feel a fire in the next room, a warmth behind the walls of the world. Three days' climb beyond this valley pass, and Vessarath. Three days, and everything. She would not be turned aside by thirty soldiers and a cheese plate.

Fen shifted beside her, and then he was a man — tall, grey-eyed, wearing the road-worn leathers she'd mended twice since the Thornwood. He hated taking human shape where enemies could see; it made him feel naked, he said, which struck Yara as an odd complaint from someone who'd spent years without trousers. But he took it now, because Corvane knew him as a man, and some old knightly pride demanded they meet him standing.

"Stay behind my shoulder," Fen said.

"I've been behind your shoulder for eleven chapters of disaster," Yara said. "I'll stand where I can see the exits."

They walked down into the ring of soldiers together, side by side.

Corvane rose from his chair when they approached. Up close, the spymaster looked worse than she remembered — thinner, greyer, his black coat dusty from hard riding, his eyes rimmed red as if he'd slept badly for weeks. Or not at all. There were new lines around his mouth that hadn't been there in the king's castle, back when she'd been nothing but a girl with a dangerous tongue and a place at the tasting table.

"Taster," he said. "Wolf."

"Spymaster," Fen returned coldly.

"Sit." Corvane gestured at the chair opposite his own. "Please. I've ridden nine days to catch you before the pass, and I'd rather not do this shouting."

Yara didn't sit. Old habit: never take a seat until you know who chose it for you. "You burned the inn at Marrowford," she said instead. "Two days behind us the whole way. The innkeeper's daughter told us. You burned it looking for us."

"I paid to rebuild it before I left," Corvane said. "Triple what it was worth. And no one was inside." Something flickered across his face — not quite guilt, but its cousin. "I don't burn people, Taster. I burn *trails*. There's a difference, and I suspect you're one of perhaps four people alive who understands it."

"The other three being?"

"Dead, or me." He sat down himself, unhurried, and poured water from a clay jug into two cups. He did not offer her wine. That, more than anything, told her he'd done his homework. A taster respected nothing so much as a host who understood what she would refuse.

Behind her, she heard Fen settle onto his haunches — half-crouch, weight forward, ready. The soldiers watched in silence. Somewhere up-slope, a nightjar called once and went quiet, as if even the birds had been bought off.

"You said you'd catch us before the pass," Yara said. "So talk. And then get out of our way."

"No," Corvane said. "I won't get out of your way, because your way leads somewhere I cannot allow you to go alone. Sit down, Yara Delacroix. I'm not here to arrest you. I'm here to make you a partner."

Her name in his mouth did what it always did — landed like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling toward something deep. He'd known her mother. He'd said as much on the road below the Thornwood, bleeding, furious, before the hounds separated them. She hadn't had time to ask what he meant. She suspected he'd timed it exactly that way.

She sat.

* * *

The cheese, she noted, was cut from a wheel whose rind showed no mold bloom. The bread was dense barley, baked that morning. Everything about the tableau said: *I know what you will check first.* It was almost flattering. It was absolutely a message.

"You want the Altar," she said. "You want to control whatever covenant comes out of it."

"I want the realm to survive what comes out of it." Corvane leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for a moment the mask of the courtier slipped and she saw the exhausted man underneath, the one who had carried a kingdom's secrets so long they'd grown into his spine. "Let me tell you what I know, and you tell me if I'm wrong. The harvests east of the Thornwood have failed two seasons running. Not drought-failure — the wells are full, the rains came. The grain simply *stops*, mid-ear, as if something decided growing was no longer permitted. The king—" he paused, and his jaw worked, "—the king talks to empty rooms. He has conversations with his dead father that go on for hours. Last month he ordered the execution of a gardener for letting roses die, and I talked him down from it, and I celebrated afterward like a man who'd won a war. That is what a realm looks like when its magic dies slowly, Taster. It doesn't explode. It *rots*. From the top down."

"And you think I can fix that."

"I think you can reforge the covenant, because a dying woman bled a map into your hands and a dragon-fire recognized you in the deep road. I was there for the aftermath of that, if you recall. My hounds found the tunnel mouth still warm." His eyes narrowed slightly. "Burned without scarring. Do you know how many old texts describe that? Two. Both about covenant-bearers."

"So help us," Yara said. "Openly. Why the theater? Why hunt us across half the map?"

"Because I've read those texts too." Corvane's voice dropped, and now she heard it — the thing under the exhaustion, the engine driving all of it. Fear. "Every covenant in every age has terms, Taster. Terms written by whoever stood at the Altar when the fire was lit. The first covenant bound dragons to shield humanity. The second, after the Sundering, bound them to answer human need. Each generation, the terms got rewritten by whoever held the pen, and each time, humans wrote themselves a little higher and dragons a little lower. Until a queen decided the price was too high and refused to pay at all, and everything since has been the interest coming due." He spread his hands. "You are nineteen years old. Brave, clever, immune to poison, and utterly innocent of statecraft. If you stand at that Altar alone, you will write terms out of *feeling*. Feeling makes magnificent poetry and catastrophic law. I am offering you the one thing you lack: someone who has spent thirty years learning how power actually moves, standing beside you when the ink dries."

"Not beside," Fen said sharply. "*Above.* You want control of the terms. Binding dragons to human law forever — you said it yourself in the Thornwood, to your captain, where our friend Maren's cousin overheard every word."

"I said it because it's true, and I don't lie to allies about my aims." Corvane didn't flinch. "Yes. I want the new covenant bound to human law. Permanently. Enforceably. With teeth and courts and consequences, not vague ancient promises that depend on the goodwill of whatever queen happens to be crowned. Is that so monstrous? Dragons are fire given thought, Taster. Fire should not be asked politely to please consider sparing the village. Fire should be *bound*."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached out, broke a corner from the barley loaf, and ate it, watching him watch her do it. His expression didn't change, but his shoulders came down half an inch. He'd passed the test and knew it.

"Fen," she said quietly. "Give us a moment."

"He's—"

"Give us a moment anyway."

Fen's jaw tightened, but he withdrew ten paces and stood with his arms folded, radiating the particular fury of a bodyguard being made to honor a technicality.

* * *

Alone at the table with Corvane, Yara found the conversation changed shape. Smaller. Truer.

"Tell me about my mother," she said.

Something moved behind his eyes — a door opening onto a room he kept locked. "Why now?"

"Because you've been circling it since Marrowford. Because you ride nine days and burn trails and set tables with cheese plates, and none of that is about the king's madness or the harvests. People don't exhaust themselves that thoroughly for policy." She leaned forward. "You loved her. My mother. Didn't you."

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. A soldier coughed somewhere in the dark. Then the spymaster laughed — short, bitter, genuinely surprised. "Maren told you that too? That hedge-witch sees too much for her own good."

"Maren told me I was dedicated to the dragons before I was born. The rest I worked out myself. You knew her name before I ever gave it to you. You said 'Delacroix' like it was a wound."

"It was." He looked down at his hands — elegant hands, ink-stained, a spymaster's hands, and yet for a moment they looked like a boy's. "Sera Delacroix was the queen's shadow-sworn. Her protector, her courier, her closest friend. And mine." He exhaled slowly. "I was young then. Younger than you are now. I fed the queen's fears because feeding a ruler's fears is how a spymaster stays valuable, and Sera saw me doing it, and she told me — she *told* me, plain as sunrise — that fear was going to cost the realm everything someday. That the covenant's price was real, and honorable, and that the moment we started treating it as a negotiable debt, we'd already lost." His mouth twisted. "I reported her words to the queen as sedition. Framed. Carefully. Enough doubt planted that when Sera smuggled a child through the wards rather than let her be raised as a sacrifice-in-waiting, the queen believed the worst of her. Believed what I'd taught her to believe."

Yara went very still. "You betrayed her."

"I betrayed her," Corvane agreed, "and she died for it — hunted, in the borderlands, alone except for a cursed knight who also failed her — and the child she saved grew up tasting poison in a valley, and the realm rotted for nineteen years, and the king I love went mad in rooms full of ghosts." He finally looked up, and Yara saw that his eyes were wet and that he would have executed any man who mentioned it. "So yes, Taster. Guilt. You wanted the word. There it is. I have spent nineteen years building a machine to prevent the catastrophe I helped cause, and the machine requires that the new covenant be *controlled*, because the last time people I trusted acted from feeling and honor, I buried the results."

The wind moved through the pass above them, carrying the faint mineral smell of high stone. Of the mountain. Of Vessarath, waiting.

"And the king?" Yara asked softly. "This isn't only guilt, is it. You love him. The mad king in his ghost-filled rooms. You love him, and you can't say so, so you've turned it into a kingdom-saving project instead."

Corvane's composure cracked — just a hairline, just for a breath. "Careful," he said hoarsely. "That's a blade you shouldn't have."

"I'm a taster. Blades are the one thing I was never trained for. Truths, though." She sat back. "I understand you, Corvane. That's what frightens you right now — you can see it on your face. I spent years reading faces across a tasting table, deciding whether the man opposite me would rather poison the soup or the king. You'd rather poison yourself. You've been doing it for nineteen years, one report at a time."

"And your alternative?" His voice steadied as he rebuilt the wall. "Feelings? Trust? Walk into the Altar with a wolf and a hedge-witch's blessings and write the fate of two realms on instinct?"

"My alternative," Yara said, "is remembering what you just taught me without meaning to. Every failure in your story began the same way. Not with evil. With *fear*. The queen feared the price. You feared losing your value, then losing the king, then losing control. Fear wrote every bad term in every broken covenant, and now you come to me asking to write the next one — and your opening argument is that we should be afraid." She shook her head slowly. "No. I won't carry fear to the Altar, Corvane. Not yours, not mine. Whatever terms rise from that fire, they'll be written by someone who came there freely."

"Freely." He laughed again, softer this time, and there was something almost like respect in it. "Do you know what the fae told you? What the Altar demands?" 

"A life freely given. Yes."

"And you intend to give yours." It wasn't a question. He'd read her the way she read dishes — one glance, whole truth. "Nineteen years keeping you alive, and you'll hand the fire your heart the moment it asks."

"I intend," Yara said carefully, "to find out what it actually asks. Everyone assumes. The queens assumed. You assume. Maybe everyone's been misreading the bill for a thousand years." She rose from the chair. "Here is my bargain, spymaster, and it's the only one on this table. Come with us to the mountain. Not as my master — as my witness. When the terms are written, you may speak. You may argue. You may bring every ounce of your thirty years of statecraft. And then the fire decides, and you abide by it. Bind nothing in advance. Control nothing in advance. Can you do that? Can the man who controls everything agree, for three days, to control nothing?"

Corvane stared at her. Behind him, thirty soldiers waited on his word; ahead of him, a mountain waited on hers; and between those two weights, Yara watched him weigh himself.

"You're your mother's daughter," he said at last, roughly. "She used that exact tone. Right before she was right about something."

"Is that a yes?"

He was quiet long enough that Fen began to drift closer, wary. Then Corvane stood, straightened his dusty black coat with a precise, courtly motion, and inclined his head — not quite a bow, not quite surrender, something older than both.

"It's a yes," he said. "Witness, not master. Gods help us both." He turned to his soldiers and snapped two fingers. "Break camp. We climb at first light." Then, without looking back at her: "There's a second matter, Taster. A reason I rode nine days and not seven. Word came down from the eastern realms two nights ago, fast as fear travels." His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man delivering numbers he wished weren't true. "The guttering has accelerated. The last dragon-fire is failing faster than any text predicted. By every reckoning I have — and I have the best reckonings in the realm — you don't have three days."

Yara felt the words land like ice water down her spine.

"How long?"

Corvane looked up at the dark mass of the mountain, where somewhere above, an ancient mother slept around a dying flame.

"If the pace holds," he said, "one."
