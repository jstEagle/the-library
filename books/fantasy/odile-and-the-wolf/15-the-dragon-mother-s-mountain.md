## 15. The Dragon-Mother's Mountain

The mountain did not want us to climb it.

I had told Yara this at the foot of the switchback trail, when the stone still looked like ordinary stone — grey granite laced with quartz that caught the morning light like scattered coins. I had told her again at the first ledge, where the wind came up the face in long slow breaths, in and out, in and out, and I finally understood what my own nose had been trying to tell me for an hour.

The mountain was breathing.

"Dragons don't breathe," Yara said. She said it the way she tasted a suspect dish: flat, factual, already cataloguing. "They *are* breath. Vessarath especially. The old songs say she slept so long her body became geography."

"The old songs undersell her." I put my nose to the rock. Beneath the mineral cold, beneath even the faint sweet rot of guttering flame, there was something vast and slow moving under the world's skin. "She isn't sleeping the way we sleep, little taster. She is holding herself together by will alone. Every breath you feel is her deciding, again, not to die."

Yara was quiet for a while after that. She was good at quiet. It was one of the things I had come to — not admire, exactly. Respect grudgingly. Most humans filled silence with their own noise; she filled it with watching. Even now, two thousand feet up a mountain that breathed, she was crouched at the edge of the trail, turning over a scrap of lichen with one finger, testing its texture against some private ledger she kept behind her eyes.

"You're doing it again," I said.

"Doing what?"

"Cataloguing. What grows here. What doesn't." I nodded at the slope above us, where the green things grew sparser with every hundred feet, until the stone itself seemed to have been licked bare by some enormous tongue. "Nothing up here will poison you, Yara. Nothing up here will hurt you except the altitude and the truth."

"Everything hurts," she said mildly, and kept climbing.

* * *

We made the high shoulder by late afternoon, and that was where the fire began.

Not flame — not yet. But light. It seeped up through cracks in the mountainside like dawn through shuttered windows, gold going thin at the edges, gold with a grey sickness in it, the color of a hearth left untended too long. The air warmed. The breathing of the mountain deepened until I felt it in my ribs, in my teeth, in the old scar-tissue of a curse that never quite stopped aching.

And Yara — Yara went toward it.

I should have expected this. I had watched her walk into a garden that killed everything else that entered it. I had watched her hold a lantern of dying dragon-fire against her bare chest in the dark under the earth, burned without scarring, without flinching, as if her body remembered something her mind had never been told. But watching her turn her face to that leaking golden light with an expression I could only call *homesickness* — that did something to me that no battle ever had.

It frightened me.

"Fen." Her voice came from ahead, hushed but steady. "The rock here is warm. Fen, the rock is warm all the way down."

"I know what's down there." I caught up to her at the mouth of a crevice wide enough for a man, barely wide enough for a wolf my size, glowing faintly at its throat. "The roost. What's left of it."

"What do you mean, what's *left*—"

"She has been dying for three hundred years," I said. "Since the pact broke. Since your queen refused the price and the covenant snapped like a bowstring. Everything since then — the blighted harvests, the king's rotting mind, the grey sky over the east — that is not the world ending, Yara. That is the world *bleeding*. And she—" I touched my muzzle to the warm stone, and the warmth pulsed back against me, faint as a pulse found at a wrist, "—she has been holding the wound closed with her own body."

Yara looked at the crevice for a long time. Wind moved her hair. Somewhere far below, a hawk cried and got no answer.

"My whole life," she said quietly, "I thought the Valley of Stillness was a refuge. A gift. Then I thought it was a cage someone built around me without asking. Now—" She laughed, short and bitter, the laugh of a woman who had run out of easy angers on a long road. "Now I find out the cage was a bandage. And I'm the wound."

"No," I said. "You're the *stitch*. There's a difference. Come and see."

* * *

The way down into the roost was not a tunnel. Tunnels are dug. This had been *sung* — the walls flowed in spirals and scales, in curves too large for human hands, widening and widening until our footsteps made no sound at all because the space had swallowed them. The light thickened around us, gold on gold, and the heat rose gentle as a bath, and I watched Yara walk through it unafraid and understood, finally, why the fire knew her.

She had been dedicated before she drew breath. Maren had told us that in the village of remembering; the sigil on Yara's skin had confirmed it. But knowing a thing is different from seeing it walk into a dragon's heart with its chin up.

The roost opened like the inside of a bell.

And there she was.

I had seen Vessarath once before, three centuries ago, when I was a man with a sword and a sworn oath and both of my eyes still brown. She had filled the sky then. She had been storm-colored and star-veined, and when she spoke the mountains leaned in to listen.

Now she filled the cavern, and it was almost worse.

She lay coiled in the deepest bowl of the mountain like a river that had forgotten how to move. Her scales — each one the size of a tower door — had gone the grey of old ash, shot through with veins of ember-light that pulsed slow, slower, slowest, matching the breath of the mountain, matching the guttering of the last living flame that burned in a shallow basin before her snout. Her wings were folded tight against her flanks, and they were thin. I could see the architecture of them through the membrane, bone and shadow, the way you can see a hand through a shroud.

One eye opened.

It opened the way dawn opens — gradually, inevitably, filling half the cavern with light the color of deep amber and older grief.

*A wolf,* said a voice that was not sound. It moved through the stone and the marrow and the small wet animal of my heart alike. *Still a wolf. Three hundred years, and you still wear your failure like fur.*

"Vessarath." I bowed my head to the stone. Old habit. True habit. "Mother of Ember. Keeper of the Covenant. I bring the bearer."

*I know what you bring.* The great eye slid from me to Yara, and every hair on my body rose, because I had seen dragons look at knights and kings and armies, and none of them had ever been looked at the way Vessarath looked at Yara Delacroix.

She was looked at *hungrily*. And tenderly. Both at once, the way a starving mother looks at bread.

*Little stitch,* the dragon-mother said. *Little smuggled child. Little taster of deaths. Come closer. I have carried something for nineteen years, and my arms are tired.*

Yara did not come closer. Not because she was afraid — I had learned the set of her shoulders when fear took her, and this was not it. She stood at the edge of the ember-light with her fists at her sides and said, in a voice that only shook a little:

"You'll show me. Won't you. Everyone keeps giving me pieces — Corvane, Maren, Fen — everyone gives me pieces and watches to see what I do with them. You're going to give me the whole thing."

*Yes.*

"And it will hurt."

*Yes.*

Yara closed her eyes. Breathed the way she breathed before tasting something that might kill her — deliberate, braced, professional. When she opened them again she walked forward into the light, and I pressed myself against the cavern wall, because I knew what came next, and I knew I could not stand inside it with her.

No one can. Memory is the one country a guardian cannot enter.

* * *

The dragon did not speak the memories. She poured them.

I saw them take Yara — saw her gasp, stagger, go rigid — saw the ember-light rise up around her like water closing over a dropped coin. And though I could not follow her in, the mountain showed me the edges of it, the way a window shows a storm: flickers on the stone, shadows in the gold, the whole wound of the broken pact playing itself out across the walls of the roost in light and grief.

I saw a queen.

I saw her young and crowned with mountain-flowers, standing at the Ember Altar with her hands open, and I saw the price written in fire before her: *a life freely given.* Not a life taken. Never taken. Given — freely, gladly, the way rain gives itself to the sea. And I saw the queen's face change as she understood, truly understood, that the covenant asked her to love the world more than she loved her own continuing — and I saw her close her hands.

I saw her walk away from the Altar. I saw the priests who had misread the rite for nine generations — who had taught that the fire demanded slaughter, sacrifice, blood on stone — I saw them seize her heir instead, because if the fire wanted a life, surely any life would do, surely fire could be cheated—

Fire cannot be cheated.

I saw the covenant snap. I saw the sky go grey in a single afternoon. I saw dragons fall from it like burnt leaves, one by one by one, until the last living flame was a coal cupped in Vessarath's claws and the dragon-mother herself was crawling into a mountain to die slowly enough to buy the world time.

And I saw a mother.

Younger than Yara was now. Round-bellied, marked with the same sigil Yara wore, standing in a burning birchwood with a newborn wrapped against her chest while winged shapes wheeled overhead and men with the spymaster's colors hunted the treeline. I heard her speak the words of dedication over the child — words I had not heard spoken aloud in three hundred years — *born under the covenant, promised to the fire, free to choose or refuse* — and I saw her carry that child through wards that should have killed her, bleeding magic out of herself with every step, paying passage in her own power because the Valley of Stillness took a toll and she meant her daughter to arrive with nothing owed.

I saw the moment she set the baby down in the reeds outside the valley gate.

I saw her kiss the sigil on the tiny arm. I heard her say, *Live long and ordinary, my love. Live so safely that you never once need to know what you are.* And I saw her walk back out of the wards to face the hunters, empty-handed, so they would believe the child was dead.

I saw her die for the lie. I saw her die smiling.

The light collapsed.

Yara came out of it the way a diver comes up from deep water — hard, gasping, on her knees on the warm stone, and I was moving before I decided to move, crossing the roost at a dead run to reach her, and Vessarath's eye watched me come and did not stop me, which was its own kind of permission.

Yara was crying. Not loudly. Not beautifully. She cried the way she did everything — economically, angrily, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like tears were a mess to be cleaned, a spill to be managed.

"She didn't abandon me," Yara said. Her voice was wrecked and wondering. "Everyone let me believe — the histories, the silence, the whole valley — she *died* for me. She gave up her magic to carry me in and then she—" The anger ran out mid-sentence, and what was underneath it was so raw I had to look away. "Fen. The queen didn't fail because she was evil. She failed because she was *asked*. Nobody asked anyone anything after that. They just started taking. And my mother—"

*Your mother paid gladly,* Vessarath said, and her voice, for the first time, was not vast. It was tired, and gentle, and very old. *As the covenant asks. Not slaughter, little stitch. The queens who misread it built altars of blood and called it duty. But the fire has never wanted death. The fire wants what sustains life — given freely, held nothing back. Your mother gave her magic. The stranger at your gate gave her life carrying word of you. And you—*

The great head lowered, slowly, until one ancient eye hung before Yara at the height of her own face, close enough that she could see herself reflected in it, small and tear-streaked and lit gold.

*You have spent nineteen years giving,* the dragon-mother said. *Every poison, swallowed so another might eat. Every death, tasted so another might live. Do you understand yet what you are?*

Yara stared into that eye. And I watched something move across her face that I had never seen there in all our road-worn miles — not fear, not stubbornness, not the careful competence she wore like armor. Understanding. Terrible, total, and quiet as falling snow.

"Yes," she said.

Then she straightened, wiped the last of the tears from her face, and turned to look at the basin where the last living flame guttered — small now, no bigger than a cooking fire, grey-edged, dying — and her voice came out steady as a taster naming the poison in a king's dish.

"Then show me the Altar. And show me the price. All of it this time — no riddles, no mercy, no pieces. Because I think I already know what it costs." She looked back at me, just once, just briefly — and there was something in that look, some farewell folded up small and hidden, that I would spend the rest of the night trying to unfold. "And I think I've been paying it my whole life."

Behind us, far below the mountain, faint as a heartbeat through stone, horns began to sound.
