## 16. Terms of Fire

The dragon-mother's hall was not a cave, as Yara had imagined caves all her life in the Valley of Stillness. It was a cathedral that had grown rather than been built — ribs of black stone arching up into darkness where the roof should have been, and through the gaps in that darkness, stars she did not recognize. The floor was warm under her boots. Everything here was warm. The mountain breathed.

Vessarath's head filled the far end of the hall the way a moon fills a window: too large to be fully seen, impossible to look away from. Her scales were the grey of banked coals, and beneath them light moved in slow tides, brightening and dimming with her breath. One eye was open. It was the color of the lantern-flame they had carried up the deep road, and it was fixed on Yara with an attention that felt less like being watched and more like being read.

*You climbed well,* Vessarath said. Her voice did not come through the air so much as through the stone, through the soles of Yara's feet, through the bones of her chest. *The last bearer fell at the third waypoint. You have come farther than any.*

"The last bearer," Yara said, and heard Fen shift behind her — a small sound, a man's weight settling on a wounded leg, but she knew without turning that his hand had gone to the old scar at his collarbone. "The courier. The woman who died at our gate. She carried this map for you."

*I sent no map.* Something moved in the vast dark above — wings folding, or grief. *She took it upon herself. They always do. That is what I cannot forgive myself for: that I let them.*

Yara looked down at the dragon-hide map in her hands. In the warm dark of the hall, the ink had stopped moving; the path it traced ended here, at the roost, and beyond it a single mark burned steady and patient. The Ember Altar.

"Then tell me what it asks," Yara said. "Maren told me I was dedicated before birth. The fae told me it wants a life freely given. Corvane wants to write its terms himself. Everyone has an answer, and none of them agree. So I'm asking the only one who was there when it was made."

The eye regarded her for a long moment. Then Vessarath laughed — a sound like a landslide deciding to be gentle, rolling through the hall until dust sifted down from the ribbed dark.

*You ask plainly. Your mother asked plainly too.* The tide of light beneath the scales dimmed. *Very well, little taster. Hear the terms, and hear them whole, because half-truths are what broke us the first time.*

*

*The covenant is simple,* Vessarath said. *It has always been simple. That is why your kind kept misunderstanding it.*

*Fire does not burn for free. Every flame must be fed. When your ancestors came to me, starving and freezing at the end of the Long Winter, I gave them fire — real fire, living fire, the fire that wakes seeds and heals land and holds back the things that live in the cold between realms. And in exchange they gave me a keeper. Not a slave. A bridge. One of their own blood, bound by choice, standing between the human world and mine, carrying warmth in both directions.*

*A life freely given,* Yara said slowly. "That's what the fae said."

*Yes. And every queen who heard those words heard only the first part — a life. They built altars and sharpened knives and told their daughters it was holy to die for the realm. Some went willingly. Most were pushed.* The great head turned, and the lamplight of the eye swept across Fen like a searchlight. *Your knight knows. He stood at the shoulder of the last queen who refused — refused because she finally understood that the covenant had become a slaughter dressed in ceremony, and she would not feed her child to it. But she also would not give anything else. So the covenant starved. And I have been starving for three hundred years.*

The warmth of the floor seemed suddenly obscene. Yara thought of the grey sky over the eastern realms, the refugees clogging the roads, the blighted fields she had walked through for weeks. A god dying of hunger while everyone argued about the price of bread.

"So what does it actually want?" she asked. "Not a death."

*No. Never a death. Death feeds nothing.* Vessarath's voice softened into something almost tender. *A life freely given means a life given over — handed across the threshold, poured out like wine from one cup to another. What sustains you. What defines you. The thing you would call yourself by if you could keep only one thing.*

"And if no one gives it?"

*Then the fire gutters out within the year. The wards fail. The deep things wake. Your realms unravel from the edges inward, gently at first — a bad harvest, a mad king — and then not gently at all.* The eye closed briefly, wearily. *I am tired, Covenant-Bearer. I have been dying politely for three centuries so that no one would have to make an honest choice. But politeness is finished.*

The silence after that was enormous. Yara became aware of small sounds in it: Fen's breathing, rougher than it should have been; the tick of cooling stone somewhere far above; her own heart, steady as a metronome, counting out the seconds of the world.

"I know what my thing is," she said quietly.

Behind her, Fen said, "Don't."

She turned. He had come forward despite his leg, leaning on a spear he'd taken from a dead hunter outside the Thornwood, and in the coal-light his face was drawn and fierce and terribly easy to read. Nineteen days on the road together. She could read him like a menu now — every dish, every danger.

"You don't get to say don't," she said. "This isn't yours."

"It was mine first." His voice cracked on it. "Three hundred years ago I stood in a room like this one and swore to guard the covenant, and I failed, and the curse put me in a wolf's body to think about it. Do you know what three centuries of thinking looks like, Yara? It looks like watching bearers die. It looks like carrying their maps to their graves. It looks like—" He stopped, jaw working. "I owe a debt. This is how debts are paid. Let me pay it."

"With your life? Vessarath just said—"

"Not my life." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing she had ever seen him do. "My service. My strength. Whatever remains of the knight I used to be. I'll pour it all across the threshold. Three hundred years of guilt makes a very full cup, Yara. Let me empty it."

"No."

"Yara—"

"No." The word came out harder than she intended, and it echoed off the black ribs of the hall, and she realized her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "You want to talk about cups? Fine. I spent nineteen years drinking poison so other people wouldn't have to. I know exactly what it costs to be someone's shield, Fen. I know what it takes out of you, year after year, taste after taste, until there's so little left that you can't remember what you liked before you started surviving for a living." She stepped closer. "And I will not stand here and watch you do to yourself what the valley did to me. Not to settle a debt. Debts are numbers. You're not a number."

For a moment he said nothing. Then, very quietly: "And what are you?"

The question landed like a knife between ribs. Because she knew what she was. She had known since the poison garden, since the lantern in the deep road, since Maren's thumb had traced the sigil on her shoulder blade and called it a dedication. She was the immunity. She was nineteen years of survived deaths stacked inside her like cordwood — arsenic and nightshade and the silver-dust poison that should have killed her in chapter one of her own life, in a castle kitchen, over a bowl of soup. That was the thing that sustained her. That was the thing that defined her. That was the cup she had to pour.

"Myself," she said. "All of it. Even the parts I hate."

*She understands,* Vessarath rumbled, and there was sorrow in it, vast as weather. *Do you see now, knight? Why I could not accept your offer? A debt is not a gift. A payment made from guilt is still a payment — it buys nothing. The covenant runs on love freely given, or it runs on nothing at all.*

Fen's face went white. "Then take something else from me. Anything—"

*There is nothing else.* The dragon-mother's eye gentled. *You already gave me everything you had, long ago. Your obedience. Your shape. Your name. I will not strip a beggar for his last coin, Fen of the Old Guard. Stand aside and let her choose, as she was always meant to choose. That is the only repayment I will take from you: that you let her.*

Yara saw it hit him — saw the words go in like a blade and twist. Three hundred years of penance, dismissed in a sentence. And underneath the devastation on his face, something else flickered, quick as a fish under ice: relief. Horrible, guilty relief. She loved him for it and grieved for him in the same breath.

"All right," she said, before anyone could speak again. "Terms accepted. At the Altar, I give my immunity — every poison I ever survived, everything that made me untouchable. Freely given. No one pushes me. No one—" she looked hard at Fen, "—trades themselves in my place. Agreed?"

*Agreed,* said Vessarath, and the word settled over the hall like snowfall.

"When?"

*Tomorrow. At dawn the Ember Altar opens for one hour — the last hour the old covenant can hold. After that, the terms change, and not in anyone's favor.* The great head lowered until the eye was level with Yara, close enough that she could see herself reflected in it, small and mortal and lit from behind by dying fire. *Rest tonight, Covenant-Bearer. Eat. Sleep beside someone you trust. Tomorrow you will need your whole self — even the parts you are giving away.*

*

They made camp in a side chamber where a hot spring steamed against the rock and the ceiling opened onto a slice of sky. Fen built a fire from dead scrub they'd carried up the mountain — ordinary fire, human fire, pitiful next to the dragon-mother's glow — and they ate hardtack and dried plums and the last of the Thornwood honey, and neither of them talked for a long time.

It was Fen who finally spoke. "If you do this, you'll be able to be hurt. Poisoned. Killed by a careless cook or a dropped vial or a bad winter." His voice was low, careful, the voice he used for hard truths. "Everything you built yourself out of — gone. You'd be ordinary."

"I've been extraordinary for nineteen years," Yara said. "It's overrated."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke." She pulled her knees up and watched the steam curl. "Fen, do you know what I tasted the day the stranger died at the gate? Nothing. She was dying ten feet away from me and I felt nothing, because I'd trained the feeling out of myself along with everything else. Tasters can't afford to flinch. You flinch, you hesitate, you die — or worse, the king dies and it's your fault. So I stopped flinching at anything. Poison, corpses, my own blood in my palm." She turned to look at him. "You want to know what terrifies me about tomorrow? Not the pain. The pain I can imagine. It's that I might feel everything afterward, all at once, after nineteen years of nothing — and find out I don't know how."

He crossed the space between them — awkwardly, favoring the leg — and sat down beside her, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd slept on watch for nineteen nights.

"Then I'll teach you," he said. "Flinching. I'm very good at it. Three hundred years of practice."

She laughed — a wet, broken sound, but a laugh — and leaned her head against his shoulder, and for a while the mountain breathed around them and the future stayed politely outside.

It didn't stay outside long.

The horn came up through the stone first — a deep, wrong note, felt in the teeth before it was heard. Then Vessarath's voice rolled through the whole mountain, waking every echo: *They come. Torches on the north road. Many.*

Yara was on her feet before she finished standing, map forgotten, knife already in her hand. She ran for the mouth of the chamber and looked out across the night side of the mountain — and her stomach dropped.

Torches. Hundreds of them, winding up the switchback road like a burning serpent. Banners she recognized from the castle: the king's grey tower, and above it, black on white, the personal standard of the spymaster. At the column's head rode a figure on a pale horse, unhurried, upright, unmistakable even at this distance.

Corvane hadn't come to negotiate.

"He said he'd seize the Altar by force if we failed," Fen said grimly, coming up beside her. "We failed."

"He doesn't understand what he's marching toward." Yara's mind was already working, cold and fast, the old kitchen competence rising like a reflex. "Vessarath is barely alive. If his soldiers storm the Altar during the opening hour — if they spill blood on it, if they try to bind the new covenant by conquest —"

"Then the fire dies anyway," Fen finished. "And everything with it."

Below them, the torch-serpent climbed. Above them, the stars wheeled, indifferent. And in her chest, Yara felt the strangest sensation — the nineteen years of survived poisons stirring inside her like a held breath, like an army waiting for dawn, like everything she was about to lose already grieving for the world it had one more day to save.

"We have until sunrise," she said. "Get me to Corvane. There may still be one bargain left that nobody's tried."

Fen looked at her. "Which is?"

"The truth," Yara said, and started down the mountain.
