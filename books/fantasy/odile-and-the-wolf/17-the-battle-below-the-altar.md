## 17. The Battle Below the Altar

Fen smelled the army before he saw it.

Iron and leather and the sour sweat of men who had marched three days without rest — that was the human stink of it, familiar as an old wound. But beneath it ran something else, something that made the hackles along his spine rise even now, even in this borrowed shape of fur and fang: cold iron, forged in quantity, and behind it the thin bitter reek of warding-charms cut from yew and blackthorn. Corvane had not come to negotiate. He had come armed against everything that was not human, and he had brought four hundred soldiers to prove it.

They were bivouacked in the valley below the dragon-mother's mountain, torches strung through their camp like a necklace of stolen fireflies. From the ridge where Fen stood, they looked almost orderly — ranks of pikes catching the pre-dawn grey, wagons drawn up in a square, a banner of the king's white stag drooping on a windless air. Almost orderly. Almost reasonable. That was the trap of armies; at distance they always looked like sense.

At his shoulder, Maren the hedge-healer shifted her weight and spat into the bracken. "Four hundred," she said. "And us with what — sixty? Seventy?"

"Sixty-two." Fen counted them again out of habit, though the number hadn't changed since moonset: the village levies in their quilted jacks and grandfather's swords, the fae of the Thornwood arrayed among them like autumn leaves that had decided to hold knives. Lady Vess's thorn-wrought honor guard stood apart, beautiful and terrible, their armor grown rather than made. Sixty-two, against four hundred, on ground that favored neither.

"And her?" Maren asked quietly.

Fen did not answer. He didn't have to. They both knew where Yara was — already climbing, already gone, slipped out of the camp before first light with the lantern of last flame cradled against her chest and Sef the dwarven guide leading her up the goat-paths toward the Ember Altar. Toward the price. Toward the thing Fen had sworn, in another life, in another shape, never to let anyone pay alone.

*Guard the bearer.* The oath sat in him like a swallowed stone. It had been laid on him eight hundred years ago by a queen with a dying kingdom and no other hands to trust, and it had outlived her, outlived the covenant itself, outlived his name and his body and his humanity. *Guard the bearer,* the curse whispered every dawn when he woke in wolf-flesh, *and you will be released.* Eight hundred years of guarding bearers who died anyway — of watching the map pass hand to hand to hand while the world went greyer and greyer, each failure tightening the curse around him like a fist closing.

Yara Delacroix was the ninth bearer. He had promised himself, somewhere in the poison gardens, that she would be the last.

"She shouldn't be climbing alone," Maren said.

"She isn't alone. She has Sef."

"Sef is ninety years old and half-blind in one eye."

"He's also a dwarf of the deep roads, and the paths above the treeline are his mother's mother's country." Fen said it more firmly than he felt. The truth was that Yara had refused him. Had looked at him across the banked coals of their fire — he in wolf-shape still, because the curse allowed him two-legged form only for moments, only for wounds, only for dying — and had said, in that flat taster's voice she used when she'd already decided something: *You stay. If Corvane comes, someone has to keep them off the mountain, or there won't be a covenant left to save. You're the only one they'll fear.*

She had been right. She was usually right, which was one of the things about her that most annoyed him and that he had long since stopped pretending not to admire.

But rightness was cold comfort at the bottom of a mountain, waiting for an army to move.

* * *

The army moved at midmorning.

No herald came. No parley, no summons, none of the old forms that even war had once observed back when the world had magic enough to bother with ceremony. Corvane simply formed his lines in the grey light — pikes forward, crossbows behind, a wedge of dismounted knights in the center where the banner flew — and began to climb.

Fen watched from the second ridge with Maren beside him and the fae lord Aelwynn crouched in the gorse like a cat made of shadows and spite. The levy line waited below, sixty-two souls on a saddle of scree between two rock spines, exactly where Fen had put them, because it was the only ground where sixty-two could look like more.

"They'll test us first," Aelwynn murmured. His voice had the rustle of dry leaves. "A company, no more. To see if we break."

"We won't break," Maren said. "We'll die, maybe. But we won't break."

"That is not the comforting distinction you believe it to be," Aelwynn said.

The first company came up the path in good order, shields locked, and the fighting when it came was short and ugly and nothing like the battles of Fen's memory. There was no cavalry sweep, no archery duel, no maneuver worthy of the name. It was a funnel and a choke-point and men shoving men, and the scree drank blood the way scree does, fast and silently. The levies held because Fen had placed the fae among them — thorn-knights stepping out of nowhere to unmake shield walls, hedge-healers dragging the wounded back behind the rocks, Maren herself moving through the press with her sleeves rolled up and her hands red to the wrist, doing the work that kept sixty-two from becoming forty.

Fen fought as a wolf fights, low and terrible, and hated every moment of it for reasons that had nothing to do with danger. This was the shape the curse had left him. Every kill he made in this body was a kill the curse permitted, sanctioned, required — *guard the bearer* — and each one fed the thing that held him. He could feel it, faintly, under the battle-joy: the leash humming. Obedience as a chain, and every act done within its terms making the links thicker.

By noon the first company had broken and the second was forming, and Fen stood on the saddle among the dead counting costs. Eleven levies down, two of them children of the village that remembered, boys who had followed Maren because she told them it would matter. Three fae, folded back into leaf-mold. The second company would come within the hour, and after it a third, and Corvane had four hundred men and no need to win quickly — only to win eventually, while Yara climbed.

He looked up the mountain. The goat-path stitched its way through grey scree and patches of stubborn pine, and somewhere above the clouds, invisible, the Altar waited with its ring of ancient stones and its hunger. He could not see her. He thought about her anyway — the way you think about a wound — and the thinking hurt worse than the fighting had.

That was when the third thing happened. Not the second company. Something else.

A horn sounded from the valley floor, deep and wrong, a note no human throat made. And out of the mist below the ridges came shapes that made even the fae go still: hounds. Not wolves — Fen knew wolves, had run with wolves, had *been* mistaken for wolves — but hunting-beasts the size of calves, grey-black, hairless at the muzzle, with eyes like chips of slate. Ward-hounds. Beasts bred and bound by the spymaster's own craft, warded against magic, proof against glamour, sent ahead of the second company to do what soldiers could not: to find the fae wherever they hid, and to drag the truth of them into the open.

Against the levies they would be murder. Against the thorn-knights they would be annihilation. Glamour slid off them like rain off slate; Aelwynn's people, who had held the saddle all morning by being everywhere and nowhere, suddenly had a *somewhere*, and it was bleeding.

"Hold the line," Fen heard himself say, and then he was moving — not toward the hounds, not away from them, but up.

Up the goat-path. Toward Yara.

Because the leash had spoken. In the smoke and the screaming, beneath the horn-calls and the slate-eyed beasts, Fen felt the curse stir and *instruct*: *the bearer is threatened. The bearer must be guarded. Go to her. Leave the field. Guard the bearer.* And it was true — the hounds were casting uphill already, noses down, following the lantern-smell of old flame and the trail of a girl who had walked through a poison garden to get here. Some part of the pack would reach her. Some part of Corvane's plan had always been exactly this: force the defenders to choose between the field and the girl, knowing the curse would make the choice for the wolf.

Corvane knew about the curse. Of course he did. He had known Yara's mother; he had spent twenty years assembling the shape of the broken covenant piece by piece, and a cursed knight bound to guard the map's bearer was precisely the kind of piece he collected. He had built this battle to spring that one lock.

And Fen felt the lock turning, and felt, with a clarity that arrived like pain, that he did not want to obey.

Not because obedience was wrong — guarding Yara was the whole architecture of his heart, curse or no curse. But because obedience *as such* was the enemy. Eight hundred years of it. Eight hundred years of doing the appointed thing in the appointed shape, and the appointed thing had failed nine times, and the appointed shape had eaten his name. The queen who bound him had meant well; they all meant well; the road to the Ember Altar was paved with the well-meant bindings of other people's lives. Corvane wanted to bind dragons to human law forever *for their own good*. The old queens had bound the covenant's price into blood *because they misread it*. Everyone who had ever broken this world had done it by deciding, kindly, firmly, with the best information available, what someone else must be forced to be.

Yara had climbed the mountain alone because she chose to. That was the entire point of her. That was why the fire knew her, why the poison garden had paid her toll grudgingly and let her pass, why the dragon-mother had shown her mercy instead of riddles. Everything that might save the realms was walking up that path on legs that had chosen it.

And here was Fen, wolf-shaped and leashed, being *ordered by his own chains* to follow her — and understanding, in the space of one breath on a battlefield, that if he went because the curse drove him, he would arrive as the curse's instrument and not as her guardian. He would arrive as one more thing in her life that had been decided without her.

The hounds were coming up the path. The second company was fixing bayonets — no, not bayonets, pike-heads — and the levies were going to break, and Maren was going to die on this scree among the children of her village, and Aelwynn's beautiful doomed people were going to be dragged into the light and unmade, and all of it, all of it, was engineered so that the wolf would have no choice.

So Fen did the only thing left that was truly his.

He disobeyed.

Not the way he had disobeyed before — small disobediences, delays, the sullen evasions of a chained creature conserving itself. He turned his back on the goat-path, on the hounds, on the bearer he had sworn eight hundred years ago to guard, and he faced downhill, into the second company, into the place where the line was thinnest and the slaughter was thickest, and he did the thing the curse had never once commanded and never once permitted:

He fought for someone who was not the bearer.

He fought for Maren. For the village boys. For the fae whose names he had learned only yesterday. For sixty-two strangers on a saddle of scree who had come to stand between an army and a mountain because a hedge-healer told them it would matter. He waded into the breach where the levy line was buckling and he threw himself against the second company's shields with his whole ancient weight, and he heard the curse scream inside him like a kettle left on the fire — *WRONG, WRONG, THE BEARER, THE BEARER* — and he kept going.

Something cracked.

Not loudly. Not like thunder. It was smaller than that and vaster: a sound like ice giving way on a spring river, a sound felt in the teeth more than the ears. Fen felt it travel through him from skull to tail-tip, a fault-line opening in the architecture of his bones, and in the gap of it — for one impossible half-second — he smelled the world as a man smells it. Woodsmoke. Blood. Pine resin crushed underfoot. The green breath of the Thornwood, miles away, arriving on the wind like a letter from home.

Then the moment closed, and he was wolf again, and the second company was breaking around him like water around a stone, and the hounds on the upper path were howling in frustration because their quarry-scent had just been confounded by something no handler had ever taught them to expect: the guardian abandoning the guarded.

* * *

The battle lasted until midafternoon, and then it stopped lasting.

It did not end the way battles are supposed to end. There was no decisive charge, no surrender. What ended it was arithmetic and stubbornness and the particular quality of the ground. Corvane's companies took the lower slopes at terrible cost — the fae saw to that, fighting now without restraint now that the hounds' warding had been disrupted by the general chaos, thorn-blades blooming out of the scree itself — and then found that taking ground is not the same as holding it, and that sixty-two defenders fighting on their own mountain bleed an invader fivefold. By the time the sun slanted west, the second and third companies had merged into a single exhausted line halfway up the first ridge, and the fourth — Corvane's reserve, his knights — sat motionless at the valley floor, unfed, unwatered, and visibly unwilling.

And Corvane himself came up through the smoke to see it with his own eyes.

Fen found him at the saddle where the morning's fighting had begun, standing among the dead with his helmet under his arm. Up close, without the armor of office, the spymaster looked older than his years and tired past them — a lean man going grey, with the eyes of someone who has read too many letters never meant for daylight. He had a sword, and Fen noted idly that he held it like a man who had been trained and had not practiced.

"You're the knight," Corvane said. Not a question. "The one from the old stories. The queen's man."

Fen said nothing. Wolves don't answer spymasters. But he circled, slow, keeping the bodies between them, and Corvane watched him circle with something that was not quite fear and not quite pity.

"I knew her mother," Corvane said. "Yara's mother. I was there when they carried her out of the burning court. I was the one who lost her." His voice did something complicated on the word *lost*. "I've spent twenty years trying to fix what I broke. Do you understand? I am not the villain of this story. I'm the man who read every dispatch, opened every seal, followed every thread — and still arrived too late, every single time, and decided I would never be late again." He looked up the mountain, toward the cloud-line, toward the Altar. "Whatever she's doing up there, whatever bargain she thinks she's striking — it should have been negotiated. Terms written. Laws. Something that lasts longer than one brave girl's good intentions."

"She chose it," Fen said.

The voice surprised them both. It came out of his wolf-throat rough and human, half-strangled, the words shaped clumsily by a mouth not built for speech — but it came, and it was his, and it was the first sentence he had spoken aloud in Corvane's hearing that the curse had not dictated.

Corvane stared at him. "What did you say?"

"She *chose* it." Each word cost him blood and effort, and he paid gladly. "That's the term. That was always the term. Freely given. Your laws can't write it. Your armies can't seize it. You've spent your whole life learning that everything can be taken and held if you're clever enough — and the one thing that saves the world is the one thing that can't."

For a long moment the spymaster said nothing. Then he looked down at the dead on the scree — the village boys, the fae, his own soldiers lying among them in the awful democracy of battlefields — and something in his face came apart, quietly, the way ice comes apart.

"Stand down," he said, to his officers, in a voice like a door closing. "Stand down. All of you."

* * *

Evening came up the mountain in bands of copper and violet, and Fen climbed.

He climbed slowly, because his body was failing him — the crack in the curse had not freed him, not yet, not wholly, but it had *wounded* the thing that held him, and wounded things lash out. Every hundred yards the leash convulsed around him, furious, punitive, and every hundred yards he climbed through it, one paw and then the next, up the goat-path past the places where the hounds had turned back, past Sef the dwarf sitting calmly at a switchback with his lantern and his knife and the expression of a man who has seen empires fall and found it merely inconvenient.

"How is she?" Fen managed, when he could manage words.

"Climbing," Sef said. "Stubborn as her mother, may the stone forgive us both. She stopped once, to be sick from the altitude, and then she kept going." The old dwarf looked at the wolf limping up the path toward him, at the blood in his fur that was not all his own, at the strange loosened set of his shoulders, and nodded slowly, as if confirming a calculation. "You broke something today."

"Yes."

"It shows." Sef stood, gathered his lantern, and gestured upward with his chin. "Then climb faster. The Altar doesn't wait, and neither does she."

Above the clouds the air went thin and clean and cold, and the stars came out with a hard brilliance the lowlands never saw anymore — stars that had dimmed, these last years, as the magic guttered, but here near the last flame they burned almost as they had in the world's morning. And at the top of the final rise, ringed by standing stones older than any kingdom, Fen saw the Ember Altar.

He saw Yara kneeling before it.

He saw the lantern of last flame set in the Altar's heart, its light steadier than it had been in years, drinking something in — and he understood, with a lurch that had nothing to do with the climb, that the drinking had begun. That she was paying. That the freely given thing was being given, right now, in the silence above the world, while he was still a hundred yards away and too slow and too late, again, always too late—

He ran.

The curse tore at him with everything it had left, howling through his marrow, and he ran through the howling, and with every stride the crack widened, ice-sheet by ice-sheet, until on the last stretch of path he felt his shape begin to slip — fur receding, jaw reshaping, the long spine of the wolf folding into the upright frame of a man — and he crossed the Altar's ring of stones on two feet, barefoot, human, for the first time in eight hundred years, arriving—

Arriving in time to see the fire take what she offered.

To see it roar back to life, high and gold and terrible, filling the sky over the eastern realms with light that had not existed in a generation. To see Yara Delacroix kneel in that light, mortal and magicless and shining, having paid with the only thing that had ever been hers.

To see her turn, and find him standing there — a man, at last, with a man's face and a man's hands and tears he had no practice shedding — and hear her say, hoarse and wondering, in the voice of someone newly free:

"Fen. Look at you."
