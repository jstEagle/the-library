## 18. The Ember Altar

The last stretch of the mountain was not a path so much as a wound.

Yara climbed it on hands and knees in places, the black glass of the rock still warm from some ancient burning, her palms smoking faintly where they pressed down. Below her — far below, a world away — the battle for the slopes raged on, a scatter of torches and screams and the bright, terrible bloom of fae-fire where Maren's levies held the switchbacks against Corvane's soldiers. She could not let herself look. Looking would be a kind of prayer for them, and prayers were weight, and she had to be weightless now.

Fen had tried to come with her. Of course he had. He had stood at the mouth of the altar road with blood sheeting down his side from where a spear had found him below, human-shaped and stubborn as ever, and said, *I swore an oath at this mountain before your grandmother was born.*

And she had kissed his brow — salt and ash and fever-warmth — and said, *Then keep a new one. Live.*

He had howled when she crossed the threshold alone. Not a man's howl. The other sound, the old one, torn out of him whether the curse held him or not. She carried it up the mountain with her, tucked beneath her ribs like a coal.

* * *

The Ember Altar was not what she had expected.

She had imagined something grand — a temple, a throne of basalt, a brazier the size of a cathedral. Instead there was only a hollow in the summit, a bowl of stone perhaps thirty paces across, open to a sky gone the color of dirty wool. At its center lay a ring of fused glass, ancient beyond reckoning, and within that ring a single flame no larger than a candle's.

It was dying. She could see it the way she had learned to see poison in a dish — not with her eyes but with something older, something in the marrow. The flame guttered and leaned like a starving animal. Its light did not reach the rim of the hollow. Around it, the fused glass was clouding over, frost creeping inward from the edges, and Yara understood with a lurch that the frost was not cold at all. It was *absence*. It was the world forgetting what warmth was.

"You came."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the stone itself, from the grey sky, from the small dying fire. Vessarath. The dragon-mother. Yara turned in a slow circle but saw nothing — only, at the edge of her vision, a vast suggestion of shape, a darkness folded into the mountain's shadow like a hand closed around an ember.

"I came," Yara said. Her voice sounded very small in the hollow.

"Alone." Something moved in the shadows — not disappointment; dragons did not disappoint, she thought, they *weighed*. "The knight offered himself. I felt his oath crack the curse like ice on a pond. And the spymaster offers armies, and the fae offer bargains, and every one of them comes to me with a life to spend." A long exhalation, warm enough to stir her hair even from the shadows. "But you climbed alone. Tell me why."

Yara looked at the little flame. Her knees were shaking. Nineteen years of training kept her upright.

"Because everyone keeps trying to pay for me," she said. "My mother paid by losing me. Fen paid with his whole life, cursed for a failure that wasn't even his. Corvane wants to pay with other people's freedom because he can't bear what it cost him. Even the queens—" She stopped. Breathed. "I've been reading the wrong histories my whole life. So have all of you. I want to read the right one. Before the fire goes out and there's nothing left to read."

Silence. Then, softly, terribly gently: "Come closer, child of two worlds. Let me show you what the covenant is."

* * *

The memory took her the way water takes a dropped stone.

She stood in a hall she had never seen, in a body that was not hers — or rather, in a body that was hers, centuries downstream, watching through the eyes of the first queen. Torches. A circle of dragons coiled in the smoke like living mountains, their eyes banked fires. And at the center, a dragon so old her scales had gone the grey of weathered iron, lowering her head until her breath stirred the queen's hair.

*The pact is simple,* the dragon-mother had said, in that same voice worn smooth by ages. *Fire for life. We give you our flame to warm your fields, light your winters, wake your sick back into their bodies. In return you give us what sustains you. Not your blood. Not your children.* A pause, and Yara felt, through the first queen's ears, the weight of the words as they were spoken for the first time. *Give us what you cannot live without. Give us gladly, or do not give at all. That is the whole of the law.*

And the first queen — Yara watched her face, young and terrified and radiant — had reached out and pressed her palm flat against the dragon's nose, and given—

The memory blurred. Twisted. Centuries compressed into a single terrible heartbeat: queen after queen standing at this same altar, each one hearing *a life freely given* and reaching for a knife, a cup, a cord, a child. Each one misreading. Each one paying in death, and each payment souring a little more, because death was not what the fire wanted, and a gift given wrongly is worse than no gift at all. The dragons grew fewer. The flame grew smaller. The realms grew colder at the edges, mad kings and blighted harvests, magic unraveling like a fraying rope —

— and then a queen who refused to pay anything at all, who broke and ran and hid her daughter behind wards that magic could not cross, and the covenant shattered outright, and a knight was cursed for failing to stop what could never have been stopped, and a child grew up in a valley of stillness learning to survive every poison in the world without knowing why.

Yara came back to herself on her knees in the frost-rimed glass, gasping, tears freezing on her cheeks.

"They didn't understand," she whispered. "Five hundred years of queens, and none of them understood."

"The words were true," Vessarath said, and now the great shape shifted, and Yara saw one eye opening in the dark, amber and exhausted and older than grief. "*A life freely given.* But your kind heard *take a life* and built altars of knives. The covenant asks not for death, child. Death ends a life. The covenant asks for what makes a life worth living — surrendered, gladly, into the fire, so the fire can give it back to the world multiplied. The first queen gave her fear. Do you know what it is to live without fear? Her reign was golden. Her granddaughter gave her grief, and sang for joy at her own wedding. But somewhere in the long generations, someone gave blood instead, and the fire drank it because fire drinks what it is offered, and it learned to hunger wrongly, and your queens learned to dread rightly, and everything curdled from there."

The eye regarded her. "You are the first in five hundred years to climb this mountain asking the right question."

Yara's heart was slamming. Because she already knew. She had known since the deep road, since the lantern-flame had licked her skin and found nothing to burn — known and refused to say it, even inside her own skull.

"What sustains my life," she said. Her voice cracked. She made herself finish. "It's the immunity, isn't it. Every poison. Every taste. Nineteen years of surviving things that should have killed me. That's — that's what I *am*. That's the only thing I've ever been."

"It is the wall between you and the world," Vessarath said. "You built it drop by drop, year by year, the way a pearl builds around a wound. It has kept you alive. It has also kept you *alone* — able to touch nothing without testing it, to trust nothing, to eat no meal unexamined, to love no gift unproven. You are the finest taster your valley ever bred. And you are the loneliest creature I have seen in an age of lonely creatures."

"That's not fair," Yara said, and hated how young her voice sounded. "It kept me alive. It saved the king a dozen times. It got me across the Poison Garden alive. It's the reason I'm standing here at all."

"Yes," said the dragon-mother simply. "That is what makes it a worthy gift. If it cost you nothing, it would be worthless. The covenant does not want your scraps, child. It wants your treasure."

The frost crept another inch toward the flame. Somewhere far below, faint as memory, the battle sounds rose and fell. Yara thought of Fen bleeding at the foot of the road. Of Maren's calloused hands drawing the sigil on her skin. Of Corvane's ruined face when he spoke her mother's name. Of the kitchens — Tam with his burnt knuckles, old Sefa humming over the bread ovens, the place they still set for her at the long table.

Every meal she had ever eaten alone, tested first, tasted second, trusted never.

"And if I give it?" she asked. "What's left?"

"A woman," Vessarath said. "Who can be poisoned. Who can be burned. Who can be hurt, and healed, and surprised, and killed someday, far off, after a long and ordinary and miraculous life. Who will eat bread without weighing it and drink wine without fearing it and take a friend's hand without cataloguing what might be on the skin. Everything you survived made you strong enough to stand here. Nothing you survived taught you how to *live*. You said it yourself once, I think, to a wolf at a ford — though you didn't know I heard." The amber eye glinted, and there was, impossibly, humor in it, ancient and dry as tinder. "*Surviving isn't the same as being alive.*"

Yara laughed — a broken, wet sound — because she had said that, on the worst night of the journey, and meant it about him, and never once turned it on herself.

The flame guttered. Down to almost nothing now, a blue ghost of itself, and the frost was close enough that she could see her own reflection in it, warped and pale.

She stood up.

Her legs held. Nineteen years of trained immunity, of a body tempered like a blade in poison after poison, and it held her steady now for the last thing it would ever protect her from.

"I want conditions," she said.

Vessarath's rumble might have been laughter. "Spoken like a taster. Name them."

"One — nobody pays for me. Not Fen, not Corvane, not any queen ever again. The covenant reads *gladly or not at all*, and anyone who tries to buy it with a knife gets told no. Write it into the fire itself if you can." 

"It shall be the first law of the new pact."

"Two — the valley opens. My people spent nineteen years safe behind wards while the world starved outside. That's over. They're part of this whether they like it or not, and they'll be better than they think."

"Already breaking," Vessarath murmured. "You felt it at the ford. Wards are only walls while everyone agrees to be walled."

"And three." Yara walked forward, one step, then another, into the ring of frosted glass, until she stood over the dying flame and its heat — its poor, starving, flickering heat — touched her boots. "When it's done. When the fire's back. Someone teaches me what the world tastes like. All of it. The sweet and the bitter and the strange. I've spent my whole life tasting death for other people. I'd like to taste living for myself."

The great eye closed, and opened, and Yara saw in it something she had never seen in any dragon's gaze, in any history, in any of Vessarath's memories: hope, fragile as the flame itself.

"Child of two worlds," the dragon-mother said. "Daughter of the smuggled child and the steadfast line. Last bearer of the map, first keeper of the new covenant. What do you freely give?"

Yara knelt. She cupped her hands around the little flame the way she had once cupped them around a dying courier's map, around a wounded wolf's fever, around every small precious doomed thing she had ever refused to let go of.

"Everything I survived," she said. "Gladly."

She opened her hands.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the first impossible thing.

It felt like exhaling after nineteen years of holding her breath. It rose out of her — not blood, not pain, but *light*, silver-white threaded with every poison she had ever conquered, hemlock and heartsbane and the old dark thing from the king's dish, a hundred deaths survived rising out of her marrow like sparks off a struck flint. She felt them go. Each one. She knew them the way a mother knows her children leaving home — the numbness of nightshade, the burn of the garden border, the silver dust she had coughed into her palm in the king's hall on the day her old life ended. They streamed into the flame and the flame *drank* them, and oh, it drank gladly, it leapt, it roared up from candle-height to bonfire to pillar in three heartbeats, blue going gold going white, and the frost screamed backward off the glass and the whole summit of the mountain lit like dawn arriving all at once from underground.

Yara stayed kneeling in the heart of it, and the fire washed over her and through her, and where it passed it left — nothing. No immunity. No armor. Just her. Just skin, ordinary and breakable and warm, and a heartbeat going like a festival drum, and tears that steamed off her cheeks and rose into the light.

She was crying and laughing at once and she could not have said which was which, because it felt like both a funeral and a birth. Something was dying in her — the thing that had kept her alive, the thing that had defined her, the taster's tongue, the survivor's wall — and something else was standing up in the space it left, blinking, new, unnamed.

*Who am I,* she thought, *if I can be killed?*

And the answer came, not in Vessarath's voice but in her own, in the voice she had used at the ford and in the thorn court and at the foot of this road when she told a cursed knight to live:

*Someone who chooses anyway.*

The pillar of fire bent, curled, gathered itself — and poured outward, off the mountain, north and south and east and west, rivers of light running along the ley-lines of the world like blood returning to a pale limb. Yara felt it go, felt it pass through her on its way, and in the distance, faint but unmistakable, the grey sky over the eastern realms began — began — to remember its color.

The flame settled at last into a steady burn, tall and calm and self-sustaining, and the ring of glass around it blazed clear as spring ice. Warmth soaked into the stone. Into her. For the first time since the gate, since the map, since the ford, Yara was not cold.

"You gave treasure," Vessarath said quietly, "and the fire gives back tenfold. Look at your hands, Covenant-Bearer."

Yara looked. The sigil on her wrist — the birthmark she had thought was a stain, the mark Maren had kissed and wept over — was no longer a faint brown shadow. It shone. A delicate tracery of gold, fine as filigree, winding from her wrist to her elbow like a vine of written light, and when she breathed, it breathed with her.

"It will never fade," Vessarath said. "Nor will what it means. You are the bridge now, child. Between fire and flesh, dragon and human, the valley and the world. When either realm forgets the other, they will look at you and remember." A pause, and the vast head lowered at last out of the shadows into the firelight, and Yara saw her fully for the first time — iron-grey scales scarred by centuries, wings folded like a cathedral at rest, eyes half-lidded with an exhaustion five hundred years deep and finally, finally easing. "Rest now, little mother. You have earned a long life. Go and see what it tastes like."

Yara got to her feet. Her whole body ached. Her palms were blistered from the climb, actually blistered, actually *hers*, and she stared at them and laughed again, helplessly, because she had not been capable of a simple blister since she was six years old.

Then she turned toward the downward road, and her breath caught.

Far below, the battle had stopped. Not ended — *stopped*, soldiers and levies alike standing frozen on the switchbacks, faces turned upward, every torch on the mountainside suddenly unnecessary. The whole slope was lit gold. And coming up the altar road, limping, leaning on Maren's shoulder, refusing to lean on Maren's shoulder, was a grey-eyed man with bandages soaking through at his side and an expression on his face that Yara had never seen him wear in wolf-shape or in any other:

Awe.

"Fen!" she called, and her voice carried down the mountain like a bell, and he stopped dead, and even at this distance she saw him mouth her name.

She started down toward him — mortal, magicless, marked in gold, more alive than she had ever been — and the fire at her back roared its approval against the healing sky.

Behind her, soft as settling snow, Vessarath spoke one last time:

"Tell him the curse is cracking, Covenant-Bearer. Tell him the fire remembers what he chose today. And tell him—" the great eye gleamed, ancient humor kindling anew, "—tell him he had better catch you before you fall. You are breakable now."

Yara laughed, and ran down the mountain to meet him.
