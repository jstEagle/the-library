## 20. Homecoming

The valley had a smell now.

Yara noticed it from the last ridge, before she saw anything else — before the grey walls of Stillwater Keep, before the terraced orchards going gold with autumn, before the gate itself. Nineteen years of her life she had lived inside that smell and never known it was there, the way a fish does not know water: a faint, flat nothing, the scent of air that magic could not touch. She had thought it was simply the smell of home.

Now she stood on the ridge with the wind in her face and smelled the world pouring in.

Woodsmoke and wet wool from the caravans camped along the old boundary. Horse and ox and the green bite of cut hay. Underneath it, faint as a memory, something that made the fine hairs on her arms rise — dragon-fire, carried on the wind from the east, from the mountain, from the Altar where she had given away the only thing she had ever been sure of. The wards were down. The Valley of Stillness was still no longer, and the air of the whole world had come rushing through the gap like a held breath finally let go.

"You're stalling," Fen said beside her.

He was a man now. It still startled her, some mornings, to look down and find hands instead of paws, a jaw shadowed with dark stubble instead of grey fur, a voice that came from a throat rather than rising out of the air around her like weather. He wore the knight's travel-leathers Maren had cut down for him, and a sword he was still learning to carry like it belonged to him, and he was looking at her with the particular patience of someone who had spent a decade and a curse learning to wait.

"I'm not stalling," Yara said. "I'm assessing."

"Assessing."

"Reading the ground. It's what I do." She nodded down the slope, at the road winding toward the gate. "There's a merchant train from the Thornwood — see the green lacquer on the wagons? And soldiers, but not in livery, which means they're either deserters or Corvane's, and Corvane's would be in livery by now. He's done hiding. And there—" she squinted, "—someone's hung laundry on the boundary stones. Someone has decided the boundary stones are a fence."

"Scandalous."

"It is, actually. Those stones are older than the kingdom." She heard her own voice go prim and heard him laugh, low, and realized she was doing it — the thing she had done her whole life, cataloguing danger, sorting the world into safe and unsafe, one detail at a time. Old habits. Except that now, when she looked at the road, she wasn't asking *what here could kill me*. She was asking *who is coming home, and what do they need*.

The difference sat in her chest like a swallowed coal, warm and strange.

"You could wait for the escort," Fen said. "Half the realm wants to see the Covenant-Bearer walk through that gate. Corvane offered a hundred riders."

"I know what Corvane offered. I told him no."

"You told him no *politely*. He's still not sure it was a no."

"It was a no." She started down the slope. "I walked into that valley at nineteen years old with nothing but a dead woman's map and a wolf who insulted me. I'm walking back in the same way. It's only fair."

"You had a wolf who insulted you *and* a sword."

"You gave me the sword back at the ford."

"And I'd like it noted," Fen said, falling into step beside her, "that you threw it at a hound and missed."

"I was nineteen. I'd like it noted that I've improved." She glanced at him sidelong. "Besides. If I come home with a hundred riders, they'll see a procession. If I come home alone, they'll see *me*."

"And what will they see, when they see you?"

Yara considered the question honestly, the way she considered everything now, because she had learned on a mountain that the honest answer was the only one worth carrying.

"A woman," she said at last. "Who used to be a taster. Who used to be immune. Who is, as of three weeks ago, capable of dying from a bad oyster." She turned her palm up, studied the lifeline Maren said was deeper now, the hands of someone who had actually lived. "I've spent my whole life being the thing that stood between other people and death. I'd like to find out who I am when I'm just standing *with* them instead."

Fen said nothing for a long moment. When she looked over, he was watching her with an expression she had only seen once before — on the mountain, in the dragon-mother's memory-smoke, when Vessarath had shown them the queen who refused to pay, and the mother who gave her daughter away, and the whole long wound of the broken years.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. It's only—" He shook his head. "The last bearer of the covenant I guarded was a queen who could not walk into a room without an army. You walked out of one with a map and bad boots. I think the fire chose better this time."

The fire had chosen nothing, Yara wanted to say. *I* chose. That was the whole point. That was the price and the prize of it both.

But the gate was close now, and there was no time, and some things a person could only say once the road had ended. She put the words away in the place she kept them — next to the memory of her mother's face in the smoke, next to the taste of the poison garden, next to the weight of Fen's human hand on hers in the dark of the deep road — and walked down into the valley that had made her.

* * *

The gate guards did not recognize her.

She supposed she couldn't blame them. The last time Tam and the young one — Breck, his name was Breck — had seen her, she had been a fugitive in a stolen cloak, sprinting for the treeline with Corvane's hounds a hundred yards behind. That girl had been sharp-faced and underfed and moving like a rabbit. The woman who stopped in front of them now had a sword at her hip and a scar on her forearm where the deep road's fire had kissed her and a way of standing that made both guards straighten without quite knowing why.

"Name and business," Tam said, and then his eyes actually focused, and his face did something complicated. "Saints' teeth. *Yara?*"

"Hello, Tam."

"You're dead," Breck said. Then, with the speed of a man trying to unsay a thing: "We thought — there were rumors — Corvane put out word you'd—"

"I was nearly dead a few times. It's different." She smiled. "Is the kitchen—"

She didn't finish. Tam had already turned and was shouting down the gate-tunnel in a voice that carried the way only a man who had spent thirty years shouting across a courtyard could make it carry: "*Betta! BETTA! Get out here, girl, and bring the whole—*"

Yara stood in the gate's shadow and felt the valley close around her, and it was not the valley she had left.

The road beyond the gate was crowded — not with the thin, frightened traffic of refugees she had seen clogging the eastern roads in the last weeks of the guttering, but with people *arriving*. A dwarven caravan unloading ingots under the suspicious eye of the quartermaster. Two fae in thorn-grey arguing cheerfully with a fruit-seller about whether a bargain was a bargain if both parties laughed. Children — human children, valley children — chasing a dog that was not a wolf, though it was the right color, and Yara laughed out loud before she could stop herself.

Magic was in the valley. She couldn't see it, couldn't taste it — couldn't taste anything the way she used to, that was gone, that was *gone* — but she could see it in the way the light lay on the walls, in the way the old stones of the gate seemed to have stopped merely enduring and started, faintly, to *listen*.

"Yara."

Betta. Head cook, tyrant of the scullery, the woman who had taught an orphan taster to hold a spoon like a blade. She came through the gate-tunnel at a pace that was absolutely not a run, because Betta did not run, Betta *advanced*, and behind her came half the kitchen — Cook's boys, the pastry sisters, old Hollen the pot-scrubber, little Wren with flour to her elbows — and they stopped in a ragged line and looked at her the way people look at a ghost they have been setting a place for at every meal.

"You're thin," Betta said.

"I've been traveling."

"You're thin and you're scarred and your boots are a disgrace." Betta's chin was trembling, which Betta's chin did not do. "There's a place set for you. There's been a place set for you every single night since you ran, and every single night I told them to take it away, and every single night I put it back, and if you ever leave this valley again without telling me, I will follow you, and I am *seventy-one years old*, and I will make it your problem."

"I know," Yara said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "I know you would."

And then the whole ragged line broke and came at her at once, and Yara Delacroix, Covenant-Bearer, the woman who had stood unburned before the last fire in the world, was engulfed by flour-dusted arms and onion-smelling aprons and Wren crying into her side and Hollen pounding her back hard enough to bruise — and she let it happen, let it knock her off balance, because she was mortal now and mortal was another word for *touchable*, and she had missed being touchable more than she had ever let herself know.

Over their heads, she caught Fen's eye. He had stopped at the edge of the crowd, the way he always did — the wolf in him still unsure he was allowed indoors. She reached out without looking, found his sleeve, and pulled.

"You too," she said. "You're set a place as well. Betta, this is Fen. Fen, this is Betta, and if you value your life you'll compliment the bread and never the salt."

"The bread," Fen said gravely, "is the finest I have eaten in two hundred years."

Betta looked him up and down, taking in the sword, the scar at his throat, the old-old eyes in the young man's face, and something passed over her expression that said she understood exactly what she was looking at and would be asking Yara about it later, at length, over wine.

"Sit him down," she said. "Both of you. Wren, fetch the good bowls. Tonight we eat like the world didn't end." A pause. Her voice went quieter, rougher. "Because it nearly did. And I, for one, intend to be rude about the fact that it didn't."

* * *

The king received her the next morning, in the small hall, with no throne.

She had wondered, walking up through the keep — past the tasting chamber with its little stone alcove and its row of silver spoons, past the corridor where she had coughed silver dust into her palm on an ordinary morning that had turned out to be the last ordinary morning of her old life — what she would feel, facing the man whose food she had nearly died for, whose spymaster had hunted her across half the broken realms. Fear, maybe. Anger. The valley had been a cage; the cage had been his.

What she felt, standing in the small hall, was grief for him.

King Aldric sat by the window in a plain chair, wrapped in a blanket against the autumn chill, and he was thin, and his hands shook, and his eyes — his eyes were *clear*. That was the terrible thing. She had served this man for years and never once seen his eyes clear; the covenant's slow poisoning had been in him the longest, madness creeping in like damp through stone, and everyone had called it age, and everyone had been wrong. Now the blight was lifting from the land, and the rot was lifting from him, and he was awake inside the wreckage of everything the sickness had made him say and do.

"Covenant-Bearer," he said, and tried to rise.

"Sit down, Your Majesty." She crossed the floor and took his hands — they were cold, they were so cold, the hands of a man whose fire had nearly gone out — and eased him back into the chair. "You don't kneel to me. I spent six years kneeling to *your* dinner."

A ghost of a smile. "Corvane tells me you refused a hundred riders and an escort of honor."

"Corvane tells everyone everything. It's his one flaw."

"He tells me also that I owe you an apology so large he doesn't know where I'd begin." The king's clear eyes held hers, and there was no flinching in them, no hiding. "I signed the order to arrest you. I don't remember it — that is not an excuse, only the truth — but my hand signed it, and my seal sealed it, and the men who hunted you did it in my name. If the realm has healed, it healed *despite* its king, not because of him."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. She thought of the memory-smoke on the mountain, Vessarath showing her the whole wound: the queen who refused to pay, the mother who smuggled her child behind the wards, the slow grey rot of a promise breaking, year by year, in the marrow of the world. The king had been a victim of it before she was born.

"The land is healing because the covenant is whole," she said. "And the covenant is whole because a lot of people chose to pay things they didn't owe. You included, in the end. Corvane told me what you ordered him to do when the guttering started — open the granaries, spend the treasury, tell the people the truth. A mad king doesn't do that. Whatever was left of you in there, it was the good part."

His breath went out of him slowly, like a man setting down a weight carried too far. "You are kinder than the realm deserves."

"The realm deserves exactly what it gives," Yara said. "I learned that from a dragon." She sat down on the bench across from him, and let the silence sit a moment, and then asked the question she had carried up the whole stairs. "What will you do now?"

"Listen, mostly. I've had years of not listening." He turned his shaking hands over in his lap. "The valley is open. That cannot be undone, and I find I don't wish to undo it. But open gates need tending, and old laws need rewriting, and there are those in my court who already speak of new wards, new walls, new ways to keep the magic *out* again." His jaw tightened — the first flash of the king he must have been before the sickness. "I will need people the walls cannot buy. People who have stood on both sides of a boundary and lived." He looked at her. "I am told you intend to stay."

"I intend to stay."

"Then I have work for you, if you'll take it. Not a title." A dry note entered his voice. "You've had enough of being *the* something. But the tasters' guild answers to the crown, and the crown, frankly, knows nothing about poison. I'm told you do."

Yara laughed — a short, surprised sound. "I knew a little."

"I'm told you walked through a garden that kills in a heartbeat and came out the far side teaching as you went." The king's eyes were steady. "Teach them. Not your old way. Your new one."

* * *

She began in the tasting chamber, because it was the room she knew best and the room that had the right ghosts.

There were eleven of them at the start — the tasters who had kept the king's table through the mad years, and a handful of kitchen younglings Betta had shoved through the door with the words *learn something, you're underfoot*. Wren came, flour and all, and sat in the front row with her chin on her fists like the whole world was a story being told just to her.

"I can't teach you what I was taught," Yara told them, standing where the silver spoons hung in their row, gleaming and useless. "I could taste a poison in a drop of wine from across the room. I could tell you which mushroom, which root, which powder, how much, how long, what antidote. That's gone. The fire took it, and I don't regret it, and if you're waiting for me to say I do, you'll wait a long time." She let that land. "What I have instead is this: I survived every poison in this kingdom, one taste at a time, for nineteen years. And I learned something the immunity never let me see, because immunity is its own kind of blindness. Poison is not a substance. Poison is a *decision*."

She picked up a spoon and turned it in the light.

"Anyone can memorize nightshade. Corvane's hounds memorized nightshade, and I walked past them in the Thornwood because I wasn't carrying nightshade. I was carrying a tincture of a plant that doesn't grow in this realm, in a wax that melts at body heat, dosed for someone my exact weight. The poison wasn't the weapon. The *plan* was the weapon. So we're going to stop learning poisons and start learning people." She set the spoon down. "Who benefits. Who flinched. Which dish arrived late, and which cook suddenly has new boots. You are not going to be tongues for this court. You're going to be the people who notice. That is a rarer thing, and a better one, and no one — no dragon, no spymaster, no king — can take it away from you."

Wren's hand shot up. "Did it hurt? When the fire took it?"

The room went quiet. Eleven faces, and Wren's flour-white one in front, waiting with the blunt courage of the very young.

"Yes," Yara said. "It hurt like giving something away always hurts. And then—" she searched for it, the true shape of it, the thing she had carried down the mountain in her chest like an ember, "—and then I ate an apple. A regular apple, off a regular tree, and it was the best thing I'd ever tasted. Because for the first time in my life I wasn't tasting it for *danger*. I was just tasting the apple." She smiled at them. "That's the lesson under the lesson, if you want it. Survival is not the same as living. Learn the difference early. It'll save you years."

* * *

Autumn deepened. She stayed.

She took the small room off the kitchen she'd had before, because Betta refused on grounds of ancient principle to let her have a better one — "you left it, you come back to *it*, that's how rooms work" — and because the truth was she wanted it, wanted the stone wall she'd mapped with her fingertips on sleepless nights, wanted the rafter where she'd hidden a stolen honey-cake at fourteen like a squirrel. The room was smaller than she remembered. She fit in it better than she ever had.

The tasters grew to twenty-three. Corvane came once, in plain clothes, stood at the back of the chamber for a full lesson without a word, and left a purse of coin on the table on his way out with the closest thing to an apology his face knew how to make. He was rebuilding his web of watchers, he told her, but this time the web would watch the borders, not the servants. "Someone should notice things," he said. "I'm told it's a rare skill." She let him have the last word. He'd earned one, maybe. Love had made him cruel; grief was making him something better, slowly, the way water makes a stone.

The valley filled. The boundary stones wore laundry and children and the initials of traveling couples with more enthusiasm than respect. The fae of the Thornwood sent an ambassador who turned out to be the lady of thorns herself, incognito and terrible, who took one look at Yara and said, "You still owe me a conversation about hemlock," and stayed a week, and was, by the end of it, almost pleasant.

And every evening, the kitchen.

That was the true homecoming, if she was honest — not the king's hall, not the gate, not even the ridge where she'd first smelled the world coming in. It was the long table in the kitchen's warmth, every night, the whole loud family of them, and the place set at the end that had been set every night for a year by a woman who refused to stop believing. Hollen's terrible jokes. The pastry sisters' ongoing feud. Wren moving her seat, night after night, one chair closer to Yara's, until one evening she simply arrived there and no one remarked on it, and that was how a family grows: by inches, by dinners, by nobody making a fuss.

Fen was there every night too. He had a room in the gatehouse — he said he liked the sightlines, and everyone pretended to believe him — and he was learning, slowly, the thousand small skills of being a man among people: how to lose at cards without growling, how to accept a compliment, how to sit with his back to a door. Some evenings he caught her watching him and something passed between them that needed no words, that was older than words, that had been forged in the dark of a deep road and the smoke of a mountain and was in no hurry to become anything other than what it was.

One evening in the first frost, Wren appeared at Yara's elbow as the kitchen was clearing, tugging her sleeve.

"There's someone at the gate," the girl said. "Tam sent me. He says there's a traveler — says she's half-frozen, and she won't come in, and she won't leave either. Says she's waiting for someone." Wren's eyes were wide. "She's got a map, Yara. A map that *moves*."

Yara went still.

The kitchen noise went on around her — Hollen stacking bowls, the pastry sisters bickering, Betta shouting about tomorrow's bread — all of it the warm, ordinary music of the life she had chosen. And underneath it, faint as a memory, she felt the old thing wake in her: the taster's stillness, the reader-of-rooms, the woman who noticed.

A moving map. A girl at the gate who wouldn't come in.

She looked across the table at Fen, and he was already rising, and neither of them said a word, because some stories, they both knew, do not end. They only wait at gates for the right person to come home and open them.

"Get your cloak, Wren," Yara said, standing. "You're going to want to see this."
