## 1. The Boy Who Sang Before Names

The grief of a wealthy man weighs more than the grief of a poor one. Marguerite had learned that early, back when he was still learning the trade's small brutal arithmetic: a widow's sorrow sold for coppers by the ounce, but a merchant lord's mourning — thick, aged, marbled with guilt like fat through good meat — could fetch enough to eat for a season.

So it was that Marguerite Dravenmoor sat in the amber-lit study of Master Corvel Hask, wine merchant of Thornfell's southern terraces, and lifted the man's grief out of him as gently as a surgeon lifting a stone from a wound.

Hask wept while it happened. They always did, though most didn't understand why. The extraction felt like relief and loss at once, like setting down a pack you'd carried so long your spine had grown around it. Marguerite held the memory carefully in his mind's hands: a daughter, drowned in a flooded cellar three winters past, her small hand slipping from his as the water rose. Marguerite wrapped it in the practiced folds of his craft, sealed it, and felt its weight settle into him alongside the others — the hoard he carried everywhere because there was no vault in the mountains he trusted more than his own skull.

"You'll forget you ever had a daughter," he told Hask, as he always told them. "You'll remember the funeral. You'll remember paying me. But the middle will be gone — the nights at her bedside, the last words. Gone."

Hask stared at the fire, his face already smoothing over, grief draining out of it like color from old cloth. "Will I miss her?"

"No," Marguerite said. It was never a kindness to lie about this part. "That's rather the point."

He took his payment in letters of credit, drank one glass of Hask's excellent red without being offered it — a thief's tax, taken on principle — and walked out into the blue hour of evening feeling the new memory shift against the old ones inside him, heavy and cold as river stones.

The road home climbed through the pines above the town of Greyhollow, where smugglers' paths braided together like roots. Marguerite's house sat at the end of a goat track no honest map would admit existed: a squat stone cottage with a slate roof furred with moss, a door reinforced twice over, and windows set high and narrow like a fortress deciding not to be friendly. He had bought it with stolen memories of a dead king's face. He liked it because no one visited, and no one visited because he encouraged them not to.

Which is why, when he crested the final rise and saw the shape huddled against his doorstep, his first instinct was annoyance and his second was murder.

Then he saw it was a child, and the murder settled into something colder and more confused.

* * *

The boy couldn't have been older than eight or nine. He sat wedged into the angle between Marguerite's door and the wall, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, wearing what had once been a coat of good wool and was now mostly mud, ash, and holes. His hair was dark and matted. His feet were bare. His feet were bare, in the Thornfells, where autumn came down off the peaks like a knife sliding between the ribs of the year.

Marguerite stopped ten paces away and did what he always did when confronted with something he couldn't price: he watched.

"Boy," he said.

No answer. The child's eyes were open, fixed on nothing, fixed perhaps on some middle distance where only he could see. His lips moved faintly, shaping sounds that weren't quite words.

"I know you're alive. Your breath's fogging." Marguerite came closer, circling slightly, the way one circles a snare to check for tripwires. This was, he knew, a classic opening move of every con ever run on a lonely man: put a child on the doorstep, wait for the soft-hearted fool to open his door, and then come through the window behind him with knives. Marguerite had run variants of that con himself. He checked the treeline. He checked the roofline. Nothing moved but wind and one indignant crow.

"Who sent you?"

The boy's eyes slid to him then, and Marguerite stopped breathing for a moment.

They were grey eyes. Not the grey of weather or iron but the grey of deep water, of places light had never bothered to visit. And they looked at Marguerite with an attention that made his skin prickle — the look of someone reading a document written in a language they hadn't spoken since childhood.

The boy opened his mouth. What came out was not crying, not begging, none of the repertoire of beggar children Marguerite had heard performed on a hundred street corners with varying degrees of talent. It was humming. A melody, low and slow, rising and falling in patterns that refused to repeat exactly — a tune that kept almost resolving and then turning aside, like a path that keeps promising the village and delivering the wilderness.

And Marguerite's hoard *stirred.*

He had carried stolen memories inside himself for twenty years. He knew their behavior the way a shepherd knows his flock: which ones were quiet, which ones restless, which ones tried to surface unbidden on bad nights. They did not *do* things. Memories were goods. Goods sat still.

But when the boy hummed, the stones in the riverbed of Marguerite's mind began to roll.

It started as pressure behind his eyes. Then a smell arrived from nowhere: woodsmoke and juniper and wet wool. Then a sound — bells? No. Voices. Dozens of voices singing in unison somewhere far below the ground, below even the roots of the mountains, a chord so vast and patient it wasn't music so much as geology given voice. Marguerite staggered and caught himself against his own doorpost, and the memory pressed up against the underside of his thoughts like a fish under ice — *he had heard this before, when, where, WHOSE MEMORY WAS THIS—*

The humming stopped.

The pressure stopped. The voices sank away, and Marguerite was left gripping his own door in the cold, heart slamming, staring at a starving child who stared back at him with those grey deep-water eyes.

"What," Marguerite said slowly, "was that."

The boy said nothing. Of course he said nothing. His lips were cracked and dark with cold, and when Marguerite crouched — cursing himself even as he did it — he could see the fine tremor running through the small body, the way the boy's jaw shivered though his gaze never wavered.

"You're mute," Marguerite said. "Or you're clever. Those are usually the same thing in my experience."

He should leave him. That was the correct move. Take the boy to the wayhouse down in Greyhollow, pay the keeper a silver to feed him and ask no questions, and let the problem become someone else's problem. Marguerite Dravenmoor did not keep things. He acquired, he priced, he sold. The whole architecture of his life was built on the principle that anything you kept could be taken from you, and Marguerite had been taken from enough times as a child to build his entire adult self on pilings of never-again.

But the boy swayed, just slightly, as he crouched there. And his hand — filthy, bluish at the fingertips — crept out and closed around the hem of Marguerite's coat. Not pulling. Just holding. As if letting go required more strength than he had left.

Marguerite looked at that small fist in his wool and felt something crack in a wall he'd believed load-bearing.

"Oh, damn it all," he said, and scooped the boy up.

* * *

He was lighter than he should have been. That was the thought that kept circling as Marguerite fed him broth by the fire, the child sitting wrapped in Marguerite's own spare blanket, looking smaller and smaller as the filth dried and flaked away. Lighter than a boy his size ought to be, as if he'd been walking for a very long time on very little. There were scars on his wrists — old, deliberate, evenly spaced, the kind that come from rope. Someone had bound this child and someone had lost him, and both facts were problems Marguerite did not want delivered to his door.

The boy ate the way starving children eat: with terrible control, small measured spoonfuls, clearly fighting his own body's demand to gorge. Discipline like that in a child that young was either training or trauma, and Marguerite suspected both.

"Don't expect this to become a habit," Marguerite informed him. "One night. Tomorrow I take you down to Greyhollow and we find out whose trouble you are."

The boy looked at him over the rim of the bowl. Then, deliberately, he set the bowl down, folded his blanket-wrapped hands in his lap, and began to hum again.

Marguerite's whole body went rigid. "Don't—"

Too late. The melody rose, that patient vast turning thing, and the hoard stirred again — but differently this time. Not the deep voices from before. Something smaller surfaced instead: a kitchen. A woman's hands kneading dough, flour to the wrists, a song being sung under her breath — *this* song, or a fragment of it — and Marguerite was standing in a doorway four years old with dirt on his knees watching his mother work, except he had never owned this memory, had never stolen this memory, this was not in his ledger, this was—

The boy stopped. The kitchen vanished. Marguerite found himself on his feet with no memory of standing, one hand pressed flat against his chest as if holding a door shut.

"That's not mine," he whispered. "Whatever that is, that is *not mine.*"

The boy tilted his head. And then — slowly, deliberately, with the gravity of a ritual — he raised one small hand and touched two fingers to his own chest, then pointed them at Marguerite. Held them there. As if returning something. As if saying: *yours. It was always yours.*

Outside, the wind came down off the peaks and worried at the eaves, and somewhere far below the roots of the world, Marguerite could almost swear the mountains were holding their breath.

He looked at the boy a long time. The boy looked back, unblinking, patient as bedrock.

"Right," Marguerite said finally, dragging a chair to the door and wedging it under the handle out of pure habit before remembering there was nothing left outside worth fearing more than what had already gotten in. "You sleep by the fire. I sleep armed. In the morning, we're going to see a man named Ferro who knows every tongue that ever got anyone killed — and if yours is one of them, boy, then we're both in more trouble than either of us can afford."

The boy lay down obediently. Within moments his breathing slowed into sleep — too easily, too completely, the sleep of total exhaustion or total trust, and Marguerite genuinely could not decide which frightened him more.

He sat up half the night with his knife across his knees, listening to the fire tick, watching the child's face go slack and young in the firelight. And once — just once, in the deepest hour, when the flames burned lowest — the boy smiled in his sleep, and hummed a single note in his dreams, and every stolen memory in Marguerite's skull turned over at once like a flock of birds startled into flight.

Marguerite did not sleep after that.
