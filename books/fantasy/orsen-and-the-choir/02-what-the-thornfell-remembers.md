## 2. What the Thornfell Remembers

The boy had not spoken a word in three days, but he had not stopped singing.

Marguerite noticed it most at night, when the wind came down off the Thornfell and pressed against the shutters of his house like a creditor's knuckles. The melody would rise from the pallet by the hearth — thin, wordless, patient — and his stolen memories would stir in their vaults behind his eyes like moths against glass. He had learned to ignore that stirring. A man who made his living carrying other people's pasts could not afford to let any of them rattle loose. But this song did not knock politely. It slid its fingers into the seams of him and *lifted*, and for three nights running Marguerite had woken with tears on his face and no idea whose grief they belonged to.

That was the trouble. That was precisely the trouble.

He stood at his worktable on the fourth morning, grinding ashroot into a paste to feed the boy — who ate like a starved wolf and watched Marguerite with eyes the color of river ice, ancient and unbothered — and turned the problem over the way he turned all problems: as inventory. The boy was mute. The boy was strange. The boy spoke, when he deigned to speak at all, in a language that had made old Hesper Vane at the wayhouse cross herself twice and refuse payment for their bread. And languages, Marguerite knew, were worth more than gold in certain markets. Worth more than memories, even. There were collectors in the lowland cities who paid fortunes for a single intact dialect of a dead people, the way other men paid for jewels or relics of saints.

A dead tongue in a living mouth. That was merchandise.

He told himself that was all it was, as he wrapped the ashroot paste in cloth and set it before the boy, who took it with both hands and a small formal bow that no starving child should have known how to make. He told himself that as he counted out coin for the road and tucked his theft-kit — needles, salves, the little silver extractor shaped like a tuning fork — into the lining of his coat. He told himself nothing at all about the way the boy had hummed last night and Marguerite had briefly, vividly, remembered his mother's hands, though he had sold that memory eleven years ago to a grieving widow in Cael Harrow who wanted to know what mother-love felt like.

"Stay," Marguerite said, and then, because the boy only tilted his head, "Here. Stay here. Don't open the door for anyone."

The boy looked at the door, then at Marguerite, and made a sound — one clear note, high and resonant, that made the windowpanes buzz faintly in their frames. It was not agreement. It was acknowledgment, the way a bell acknowledges being struck. Marguerite decided to take it as yes, because the alternative was admitting he had no idea what he was dealing with, and Marguerite Dravenmoor had built an entire life out of never admitting that.

* * *

Old Sable kept her shop where the smuggler roads braided together below the tree line, in a hollow of black pine that the sun visited perhaps four hours a day. She had been blind since birth, they said, or since a deal gone wrong — the stories disagreed — and she fenced everything: stolen heirlooms, forged charters, memories too hot for the open market. Her shop smelled of tallow, dried yarrow, and old paper, and it was lit by lamps she could not see, because her customers liked light even if she didn't need it.

She was seated behind her counter when Marguerite entered, sorting coins by weight alone, her long gray fingers moving over them like a spider reading web-vibrations.

"Dravenmoor." Her voice was gravel steeped in honey. "You smell like mountain rain and bad decisions. Sit."

"I have something rare."

"You always say that."

"This time it's true." He sat on the customer's stool, keeping his voice level, casual, the voice of a man offering wool rather than wonder. "A living speaker of a dead language. Pre-kingdom, I'd wager. Maybe older."

Sable's fingers stopped moving over the coins.

For a long moment the only sound was the tick of the stove and the wind worrying the eaves. Then she laughed — short, hard, without any humor in it at all.

"Oh, you *fool*." She said it almost fondly. "You beautiful, greedy fool. Do you know what you've got under your roof?"

"A curiosity. A marketable one."

"A *carrier*." She turned her blind face toward him fully now, and though her eyes were milk-pale and saw nothing, Marguerite felt seen down to the marrow. "I was a girl when the last of them passed through these mountains, before your king's grandfather's grandfather wore the usurper's crown. My grandmother heard them sing once, at a funeral in the high passes. She said the dead got up and walked home afterward. Not alive — don't mistake me — but *remembered*. Carried. The Choir, she called them. Speech older than any kingdom, older than the blood-oaths the usurper hides behind. One mind wearing many mouths." Her fingers resumed their slow circuit of the coins, but there was tension in them now, a tremor. "They held the old dynasties honest, my grandmother said. Kings feared them, because a Choir-keeper standing at your shoulder could hear whether your oaths rang true. The usurper's line has spent two hundred years making certain no one could ever check their arithmetic again."

Marguerite kept his face still. It was a professional skill. Inside, calculations were already running — price, buyer, risk, distance — and beneath them, unwelcome, the memory of the boy's note buzzing the windowpanes.

"And the market?" he asked. "There are collectors—"

"The market is *one* buyer, and you do not want it." Sable leaned forward. "The usurper's inquisitors pay in gold for speakers of that tongue. Dead or alive. Alive is worth more, but dead is easier to transport, so they're rarely particular. They call it a choral anomaly in their ledgers. They have been hunting the remnants for years, quietly, village by village, and lately not quietly at all." She reached beneath the counter and drew out a folded broadsheet, sliding it across to him. "Read that. Read it aloud, since apparently I must live vicariously."

Marguerite unfolded it. An edict, stamped with the double-crown seal of Queen Seraphine the Usurper, though the edict predated her reign — her father's hand, then, or her grandfather's, renewed each generation like a debt that never came due:

*By order of the Crown: the possession, harboring, teaching, or speaking of Choral speech is treason against the blood-oath of the rightful dynasty. Reward offered for carriers of the anomaly, living or dead. Those who shelter them share their sentence.*

His mouth had gone dry somewhere around the second line.

"There was a massacre last spring," Sable said quietly. "A sanctuary in the far peaks. The inquisitors burned it and called it quarantine. Every keeper dead, they thought. They've been sweeping the passes ever since, looking for what crawled out of the ashes." She tilted her head. "And a few weeks ago, a mute, starving child walks up to *your* doorstep — yours, Dravenmoor, the memory-thief whose loyalty is a ledger entry — and starts humming songs that make stolen ghosts sit up in their graves. Tell me again how much he's worth."

"I came to sell information," Marguerite said. His voice sounded distant to himself. "Not to be lectured."

"You came to sell a child to the people who burn children." Sable spread her hands. "So sell me instead. What will you take for walking away? Because I'm telling you plainly: whatever buyer you find for that tongue, the finding will be reported. Inquisitors have ears in every fence's shop from here to the coast. Mine included — I won't pretend otherwise; I'm blind, not stupid, and they leave me breathing because I'm useful. The moment you ask the wrong person the right question, the Ledger finds you. And it does not negotiate, whatever its agents promise you."

Marguerite stood. His legs felt strange, as though the floor had shifted a degree off true. "You've wasted my morning, old woman."

"And you've wasted a life, maybe two, if you walk back up that mountain thinking like a merchant." She turned back to her coins, dismissing him, and then paused. "One thing more, free of charge, because my grandmother would rise from her grave and box my ears. The Choir doesn't choose carriers at random. It sings through the willing, and it sings *to* the kin of those who kept it. Ask yourself, thief, why a boy who trusts no one walked through every door in the Thornfell and lay down at *yours*."

* * *

The climb back was silent and cold, and Marguerite did most of his arguing with himself on the switchbacks above the treeline, where the wind could carry the pieces away.

Sell the information — not the boy, never the boy, just the *fact* of him, the location, the anomaly — to Odile Corvane's people in Cael Harrow. The free cities' spymasters paid well for leverage against the usurper, and a living Choir-speaker was leverage beyond price. Let the great game play itself out with someone else's conscience holding the stakes.

Or take the boy south himself, past the passes, past the checkpoints, and sell him whole to Corvane — protection, patronage, a place in the free cities where the edicts didn't reach. Clean. Sensible. Profitable.

Or —

There was no *or*. There was no third column in Marguerite's ledger. He had built his life on the certainty that everything had a price and everyone had a sale, and he had never once gone hungry believing it.

But the boy had bowed over the bread. The boy had sung a note that buzzed the glass. And last night, half-asleep, Marguerite could have sworn the melody had taken shape — words in that old tongue, soft and repeated, like a lesson, like a lullaby, like a name being taught.

He crested the ridge as the light went copper, and stopped.

Smoke. Thin and wrong, rising from the valley below his house — not hearth-smoke, not woodsmoke from his own chimney, but the greasy black plume of something larger burning. And beneath the wind, faint but unmistakable, carried up the mountainside with terrible clarity: voices. Many voices, disciplined and cold, calling out in cadence — a search pattern, a sweep, the sound of soldiers working methodically through a settlement.

At the center of it, cutting through everything, one voice raised in crisp bureaucratic command. A woman's voice, reading aloud, as if from a list.

Marguerite stood on the ridge with the edict crumpled in his fist, and understood that the Ledger had already found him — that somewhere between his doorstep and Sable's counter, the question had already been asked, and answered, and written down.

He ran.
