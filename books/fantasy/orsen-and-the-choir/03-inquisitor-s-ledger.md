## 3. Inquisitor's Ledger

The ledger of Sister Veyra of the Ninth Chair contained four hundred and eleven entries, and she had written every one of them in her own hand, in ink she ground herself from gallnut and iron salts, because a record that could be read by another's light was a record that could be altered by another's will.

Entry four hundred and eleven began, as all her entries did, with the date reckoned in the Regnant's Years — the ninth year of Queen Isolde the Restorer — and continued:

*Anomaly designated CHORAL-9 detected at the tollhouse of Grey Ford crossing. Two witnesses interrogated. One confirmed resonance exposure; subject remanded for purification. One false witness; subject remanded for purification. Anomaly proceeded south by southwest into the Thornfell massif, unaccompanied, on foot, approximate age eight to ten years.*

She blotted the line, set the ledger aside, and looked out the window of the requisitioned farmhouse that served as her field office. Below, in the yard, her purifiers were loading the morning's remandments into the wagon. They worked quietly. She had trained them to work quietly. Screaming attracted attention, and attention was disorder, and disorder was the one thing the Restoration could not afford while it was still young enough to be killed in its sleep.

Veyra did not hate her work. Hatred was inefficient; it clouded the judgment, made inquisitors cruel when they should have been thorough, thorough when they should have been merciful. What she felt toward her work was closer to the feeling a mason has toward a wall — an awareness of every stone, every fault line, every place where the mortar had been laid thin. The old kingdoms had fallen because their walls were sentimental. Queen Isolde's would not be.

And yet.

She turned back to the ledger and read the entry again, because there was something in it that troubled her, and Sister Veyra had learned long ago to trust the small irritations of her mind the way sailors trusted a change in the wind. *Unaccompanied. On foot.* A child of eight or ten years, walking alone through the Thornfell passes in the last month of autumn.

Children did not walk alone through the Thornfell. Children who walked alone through the Thornfell were either dead within three days or — and this was the possibility she had entered into the margin in smaller script, the way one enters a debt one hopes never comes due — *guided.*

*Guided by what?* she had written. And beneath it: *Or by whom?*

* * *

The village was called Hallowmere, though by the time Veyra rode into it at dusk two days later, the name mattered only as a heading for the next entry in her ledger. It sat in a fold of the mountains where the smuggler roads braided together like the roots of an old tree — forty-one households, a shrine to some pre-Restoration saint whose face had been chiseled off during the first purifications, a wayhouse, a mill. Smoke rose from forty-one chimneys. Somewhere a dog was barking at the sight of soldiers, and somewhere else a mother was calling children in to supper, and the ordinary sounds of ordinary lives went on in perfect ignorance of the fact that Sister Veyra had already decided how this evening would end.

She had not decided out of cruelty. That was the thing her subordinates never understood, the thing that made the younger purifiers whisper that the Ninth Chair had no heart at all. She had decided out of arithmetic.

The anomaly — CHORAL-9, though she was beginning to suspect the designation was too small for what moved through these mountains — had passed through Hallowmere. She knew this the way she knew most things: from the tollhouse records, from a peddler's testimony extracted at the crossroads shrine, from the particular quality of silence that fell over a village when soldiers asked after a child and forty-one households answered *no, we haven't seen him* in voices pitched just slightly too carefully.

A village that lied well was worse than a village that lied badly. A village that lied well had practiced.

"Brother Cassian," she said, dismounting before the wayhouse. Her voice carried without effort; she had never needed to raise it. "How many souls in this parish?"

"Forty-one households," said Brother Cassian, a round, sweating man who had been a moneylender before the Restoration found better use for him. "Two hundred and six souls, Sister, counting—" He stopped, because she had turned to look at him, and men always stopped when she turned to look at them.

"Counting?"

"Counting the miller's family. They keep to themselves."

"The miller's family." She said it the way one repeats a phrase in a contract to make certain both parties have heard it correctly. "And has the miller's family seen a child? Eight, perhaps ten years. Dark-haired. Alone."

Cassian's face performed a small theater of confusion — brows drawn, mouth turned down, eyes flicking briefly toward the mill road and back. He was not good at it. Very few people were. "No child, Sister. We'd have taken him in. We're loyal folk here. Loyal to the Queen."

"Yes," Veyra said. "I can see that you are."

She gave him a receipt. This was important; everything in her work was receipts. *One (1) interrogation conducted, Hallowmere wayhouse, no anomalies disclosed.* Cassian signed it with the flourish of a man relieved to have survived something he did not understand, and Veyra folded her copy into the ledger and walked out into the blue evening and stood in the road looking up at the mill on its hillock, where a light burned in an upper window, and beneath that window, faint on the wind, came something that made the hair on her forearms rise beneath her sleeves.

Singing. A child's singing, wordless, in a scale that did not belong to any hymnbook in the Nine Kingdoms — intervals that slid between notes like water finding cracks in stone.

*There you are,* she thought, and felt nothing whatsoever about it, and wrote the moment down in her memory exactly as she wrote everything down, so that someday, if it was ever demanded of her, she could produce it whole.

* * *

The miller's family had hidden the boy for six days. This emerged over the course of ninety minutes, in the miller's kitchen, with the door barred and four purifiers standing along the walls, and it emerged not because Veyra threatened anyone but because she simply waited, hands folded on the table, while the miller's wife wept and the miller's jaw set and un-set and finally broke like a dam that had been holding back not fear but something older and more stubborn than fear.

"He was starving," the miller said at last. His name was Aldous; she had already entered it. "Starving and mute and half-frozen on our doorstep, and my wife fed him, and I'll answer for it, but I won't pretend we didn't. You can write me down however you like."

"I intend to," Veyra said, and did: *Aldous Fenner, miller, harbored anomaly six days. Wife, Maren Fenner, harbored anomaly six days. Daughters, Tace (11) and Bree (7), played with anomaly; Tace reports learning 'three songs.'*

It was the third daughter who interested her. The elder girl, Tace, sat with her chin up and her fists clenched, glaring at the inquisitor with the magnificent courage of children who do not yet know what courage costs. But when Veyra asked her, gently, to sing the songs the boy had taught her, the girl's defiance collapsed into something far more dangerous: eagerness.

"They're pretty," Tace said. "He can't talk but he can sing, and when he sings I can see things."

"What sorts of things?"

"Memories that aren't mine." The girl said it simply, the way children say impossible things. "My grandmother's kitchen. I never saw her kitchen, she died before I was born, but I know it now. There was a blue jug with a chip in the handle. Why do I know about the jug?" Her voice climbed, frightened now, but not of Veyra — frightened of the knowing itself. "Why do I know about the jug?"

Veyra closed her eyes for the space of one breath.

In nine years of service she had purified two hundred and thirty-seven confirmed resonance exposures, and in every case the pattern held: fragments, echoes, noise. The Choir-speech leaked into exposed minds like dye into water, staining them with secondhand recollections, and the stains faded, and the minds were scoured clean, and the ledgers balanced. That was the doctrine. That was what the seminaries taught. *Exposure is contamination, and contamination is treatable.*

But this girl was not stained. This girl was *instructed*. Whole memories, coherent, specific, transferred across a gap of decades by a mute child's humming. Which meant the boy was not merely a speaker of the forbidden tongue.

Which meant the boy might be a vessel. Perhaps *the* vessel. The last verse of the Choir, walking around in a starving child's body, sleeping in a miller's loft, teaching songs to little girls.

Queen Isolde's legitimacy rested on a blood-oath sworn by her grandfather, witnessed and sealed by the last surviving Choir-keepers — the same keepers whose sanctuary in the high Thornfell had burned eleven months ago, per the official account, in an accident of divine judgment upon heretics. Veyra had read the official account. Veyra had also read the unofficial account, which existed in exactly one copy, in exactly one ledger, in exactly one hand, and which stated plainly that there had been no accident, and that the fire had been set on the orders of the Crown, and that the purpose of the fire had been to ensure that no living voice remained capable of contesting the blood-oath.

If even one carrier had escaped that fire —

"Stand up," Veyra said to the girl, kindly. "You've done nothing wrong. None of you have done anything wrong. That is the tragedy of it, and I want you to understand that I know it is a tragedy."

Then she walked outside into the dark and gave the order, and her purifiers, who had done this nineteen times before and were very good at it, went about the village with lanterns and oil and the terrible gentleness of men who have learned to move quickly so that the suffering is brief.

* * *

Entry four hundred and twelve, written by lamplight in the saddle, in a hand that did not shake:

*Hallowmere, 41 households, 206 souls, purified in full pursuant to Doctrine of Containment, Section IV: no community exposed to choral resonance may persist as a vector. Anomaly CHORAL-9 not recovered; fled south prior to cordon. Casualties include Aldous Fenner, Maren Fenner, Tace Fenner, Bree Fenner. Note: Tace Fenner exhibited instructed-memory transfer of unprecedented coherence. Recommend revision of seminary doctrine re: exposure taxonomy.*

She blotted it. In the yard below, the fires were settling into the steady roar that burns longest and hottest, and against that light she could see Brother Cassian kneeling in the road where they had found him trying to run, and the purifiers standing over him with the patience of men waiting for a kettle to boil.

Veyra watched for a moment longer than she needed to. Then she looked down at the open ledger, at the neat columns of names and numbers, at the arithmetic of a kingdom kept safe one burning village at a time, and she permitted herself the question she never permitted herself aloud:

*What kind of song is worth this?*

Because that was the true ledger, wasn't it — the one she kept behind her eyes, unwritten, unblotted. Four hundred and twelve entries. Eleven thousand, give or take, souls. And somewhere ahead of her, south by southwest, a mute child carrying the last voice of a murdered god-old consciousness, running toward the free cities with nothing but borrowed songs and whatever stranger had taken him in.

The Choir had held the old kings in check for nine hundred years. The Choir had *witnessed* oaths, and a witness that cannot be killed cannot be bribed, and an oath witnessed by such a witness was iron. Isolde's grandfather had understood this. That was why the sanctuary burned.

And if the Choir lived —

Veyra closed the ledger, tied it shut, and gave the order to ride.

"South," she said. "We are behind schedule, and I dislike being behind schedule."

Behind them, Hallowmere burned against the mountains like a signal fire lit for no one, and ahead of them the smuggler roads ran dark and winding toward Cael Harrow, and somewhere on those roads a memory-thief named Marguerite Dravenmoor — a name she did not yet know, entered in no ledger of hers — was about to learn what his stolen goods were worth, and to whom, and at what price.

Sister Veyra rode through the smoke with her ledger strapped to her saddle and her heart locked in its box of arithmetic, and if anything followed her out of that burning valley — some small, cold finger of doubt tracing itself along her spine like a note held past its proper end — she did not write it down.

Not yet.
