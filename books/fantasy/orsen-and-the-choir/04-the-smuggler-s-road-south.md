## 4. The Smuggler's Road South

The Thornfell did not forgive, and it did not forget. Marguerite had built his life in its creases precisely because the mountains kept secrets better than any vault — but now they were keeping someone else's secret too, and every switchback trail south felt like a corridor with inquisitors at both ends.

He traveled by the old smuggler's arithmetic: sleep at dusk, move at first light, never walk a road twice, never trust a bridge you didn't pay for. The boy learned fast. That was the first thing that unsettled Marguerite — not the language that predated kingdoms, not the melody that lived under his skin like a second pulse, but how quickly he absorbed the craft of survival, as though he'd been waiting all along for someone to teach him.

"Again," Marguerite said, crouching beside a stream on their third morning out. He held up two fingers. "Two things before you drink anywhere. First, look upstream. Dead animals, old camps, mine-tailings — anything fouls the water above you, you find another source. Second—" he scooped a handful of pebbles from the bank and scattered them across the surface "—watch what floats downstream. If it's not yours, someone's upriver."

The boy watched the pebbles spin away with an intensity most men reserved for counting coin. Then he looked up, and his eyes asked the question his mouth wouldn't: *why are you teaching me this?*

"Because dead boys can't be sold," Marguerite said, and immediately regretted the shape of it. The boy flinched — small, quick, the flinch of someone who understood commerce applied to flesh — and Marguerite felt something twist behind his ribs that he refused to name. "Because," he amended, quieter, "I said I'd get you to Cael Harrow. I keep my bargains."

It wasn't entirely true. He'd made no bargain yet. But the lie sat easier than the truth, which was that he hadn't been able to leave the boy behind, and couldn't explain why, and hated himself a little for it.

* * *

They named him at a wayhouse three days south, a low-beamed place called the Drowned Bell where smugglers drank with shepherds and nobody asked anyone's business. The keeper was a broad woman named Hesk with forearms like ship rope and a laugh that rattled the mugs on her shelves. She took one look at the silent, hollow-eyed child trailing Marguerite and set down a bowl of stew without being asked.

"What's his name?" she demanded.

"He doesn't have one."

"Everything has a name." She crouched to the boy's level, studying him the way she might study a horse she intended to buy — not unkindly, but thoroughly. The boy studied her back. Somewhere in that exchange, something passed between them that Marguerite would never be privy to. "Orsen," Hesk declared finally, as if she'd read it somewhere in him. "My grandmother's word for the first light after a long dark. Means *the morning that keeps its promise.* Good enough."

The boy — Orsen now — touched his own chest, once, as if confirming the name had landed somewhere real. Then he ate his stew like a boy who had learned hunger as a native tongue.

Marguerite paid for the room with a stolen memory of a wedding — some merchant's joy, sold cheap — and lay awake half the night listening to Orsen breathe in the bed beside his, wondering when exactly he had become responsible for another heartbeat.

* * *

The singing started on the sixth day.

They were crossing a high shoulder of the mountains where the wind came off the icefields clean and merciless, and Marguerite was thinking about nothing — deliberately about nothing, which was his way of thinking about everything — when Orsen began to hum.

It was the melody again, the one from the doorstep, the one that made his stolen memories stir like leaves in a draft. But this time it didn't stop at humming. Thin, cracked, barely more than breath, the boy sang a fragment of words in that old tongue, and—

Marguerite stopped walking.

Because he could smell woodsmoke. Not the woodsmoke around them; there was no fire lit. A different smoke, years gone, from a hearth in a house he had not thought of since he was nine years old. His mother's kitchen. The iron pot. The way she'd hummed while she stirred, off-key, always off-key, and how he'd pretended to hate it.

He had sold that memory. Sold it eleven years ago to a collector in Vail's Crossing who paid handsomely for childhoods, and Marguerite had walked out of that transaction lighter and richer and certain he felt nothing about it. The memory was gone. It lived in someone else's head now, in some glass jar or gilded locket, and the hole where it had been was just — a hole. Smooth-edged. Painless.

Except the hole was full. Full of steam and iron and his mother's off-key humming, so vivid he could taste the barley porridge, could feel the cold floor under bare feet, could see the exact chipped blue glaze on the rim of the bowl she'd given him.

"Stop," he said hoarsely. "Boy. Stop singing."

Orsen fell silent instantly, watching him with those grave eyes.

Marguerite stood in the wind with his heart hammering and his hands shaking, holding a memory he had sold, that could not exist, that was sitting inside his skull warm and whole as bread. "What did you do?"

The boy tilted his head. Then, slowly, he pointed one finger at Marguerite's chest, then drew the finger back toward himself, and pressed it over his own heart. *Gave,* the gesture said. Or perhaps *returned.* The old tongue had no word Marguerite knew for it, but the meaning arrived anyway, wordless, the way the melody did.

"That's not possible," Marguerite said. "Memories don't come back. I've taken hundreds. I've *sold* hundreds. They don't—"

Orsen hummed three notes.

And Marguerite was seventeen again, standing in the rain outside a fence's stall in Vail's Crossing, selling the memory of his mother's face because winter was coming and he had nothing else. He remembered the exact weight of the coins. He remembered deciding, with the cold clarity of the young and desperate, that a face was a fair price for survival. He remembered walking away feeling *freed.*

He had told himself for eleven years that he felt freed. Now he knew what he actually felt, because the feeling had come home with the memory: grief. Enormous, patient grief, the kind that waits.

He sat down hard on a rock and put his head in his hands, and the wind screamed past them both, and Orsen — after a long moment — sat down beside him, close enough that their shoulders almost touched.

* * *

By the ninth day, Marguerite had stopped pretending it wasn't happening.

The boy sang fragments the way other children skipped stones — casually, experimentally, watching where they landed. And each fragment brought something home. A memory of his first theft, aged twelve, lifting an apple with such terror and triumph that he'd run six streets before biting it. A memory of snow on the pass at Veylmoor, the year he'd nearly frozen and been pulled from a drift by a stranger who asked nothing. A memory of laughter — actual laughter, his own, from some evening he'd erased so thoroughly he'd forgotten he'd ever been capable of it.

Each one cost Orsen something. Marguerite saw it: the boy would sing, and afterward he'd be grayer, wearier, as if the song were drawn up through him from some deep well, and the well was not infinite. So Marguerite rationed him, the way he'd ration food.

"No more today," he'd say, and Orsen would press his lips shut, obedient, frustrated, the melody visibly still moving under his skin.

"You understand you're giving me back pieces of myself I sold fair and square," Marguerite said one evening, over a fire no larger than a cupped hand. "There are people who'd call that theft. Reversed theft. I'd know. I'm the authority."

Orsen's mouth twitched. It was almost a smile. In nine days Marguerite had catalogued exactly four of them, and treasured each one with a secrecy that embarrassed him.

"I spent my whole life believing memories were only worth what someone would pay," Marguerite went on, poking the fire. It was easier to talk to the fire than to the boy. "That's the whole trade. Grief, joy, first loves, last words — all of it's inventory. You take it, you sell it, you don't feel it anymore, and everyone's happier. The seller's lighter. The buyer's fuller. Nobody bleeds." He glanced up. "But you're telling me that's a lie, aren't you. You're handing them back and they *hurt*, and somehow the hurting is — better than the hole."

Orsen considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then he reached into the dirt beside the fire, traced a shape with one finger: a circle, closed. Then he broke it open with a stroke through the middle, and looked at Marguerite.

*Whole. Broken.* And his eyes said the rest: *which one is living?*

Marguerite laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty as an unused hinge. "You're a cheeky little prophet, you know that?"

The smile again. The fifth one. Marguerite turned back to the fire and let himself feel the memory of his mother's kitchen, all of it, the steam and the off-key humming and the chipped blue bowl, and it hurt like a healing wound hurts, and he let it.

* * *

On the eleventh day they reached the shepherd's track above Greywater Vale, and Marguerite allowed them a fire large enough to cook over, because the vale was free-city territory and even inquisitors moved carefully here. Orsen sat wrapped in Marguerite's spare cloak, looking less like a starving stray and more like a boy, which frightened Marguerite more than anything the mountains had thrown at them.

Because things that became precious could be taken. He knew the market value of precious things better than anyone alive.

"We're two days from Cael Harrow," he said. "There's a woman there named Odile Corvane. Spymaster. She deals in secrets the way I deal in memories — except she never gives anything back." He watched the fire. "She'll want you. Your voice, your language, whatever lives in your head. She'll offer protection, and the protection will have teeth in it somewhere, and I'll need to find the teeth before we shake hands."

Orsen looked at him for a long moment. Then he did something new: he spoke. Not the old tongue — he'd never once used the trade-tongue, not in eleven days — but he opened his mouth, and what came out was a single word, rough and unpracticed, shaped with enormous care:

"*Family.*"

The word hung in the smoke between them.

Marguerite's whole body went still. "Where did you learn that word?"

Orsen pointed at Hesk's wayhouse direction, then at his own chest, then at Marguerite's. The morning that keeps its promise. The circle, closed.

"I'm not—" Marguerite started, and heard his own voice crack on the denial, and hated it. "I steal things, boy. That's all I am. Everything I've ever held, I've held so I could sell it. You don't want to be held by—"

Orsen hummed.

One fragment. Small, deliberate, defiant — and Marguerite's own memory rose to meet it: the doorstep. Three nights ago. Opening his door to find a starving child standing in the snow, humming a melody older than kingdoms, and feeling — beneath the wariness, beneath the merchant's calculus already running — the smallest, most dangerous warmth. The memory he'd never sold, because he'd never known it was worth anything.

The boy gave it back to him like a mirror. *This is what you felt. This is what I am keeping.*

Marguerite sat very still, firelight moving on his face, a man who had monetized every human feeling discovering one that had no price he dared calculate.

"All right," he said finally, roughly. "All right. Two days to Cael Harrow. We find Corvane's teeth before we shake her hand. And if it goes wrong—" he met the boy's eyes "—we run. Together. Understood?"

Orsen nodded once, solemn as an oath.

And far to the north, down the black throat of the pass they'd left behind them, Marguerite did not see — could not see — the thin line of torchlight descending the mountain, led by a woman in grey carrying a ledger, crossing off villages one by one as she came.
