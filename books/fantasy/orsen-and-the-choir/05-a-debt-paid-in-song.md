## 5. A Debt Paid in Song

Orsen did not have many words yet. The road was teaching him them, one at a time, the way rain teaches stone to be soft.

*Road.* That was the first one he learned that was not a name. Marguerite said it every morning, pointing ahead with his chin, and Orsen repeated it until the word sat in his mouth like a smooth pebble. *Road. Road.* Then *bread*, when they shared the dark loaf at the wayhouse. Then *fire*, which was easy, because fire made its own sound and Orsen only had to copy it. Then *cold*, which he already knew in his bones from before, from the mountain where everyone had died, though back then cold had not been a word. It had been a thing that happened to you, like sleeping or being afraid.

The old language — the Choir-speech, the blind fence had called it, though Orsen did not know fences or blindness as words — the old language did not work like this. In the old language, things were not named. They were *sung*, and when they were sung they were true. You did not say *cold*. You sang the shape of cold, and anyone who heard it felt winter settle into their chest, and there was no arguing about whether it was cold or not. Words could lie. Songs could not. That was the first law of the Choir, and Orsen carried it inside him the way other children carried hunger.

He was learning that the new words could lie. He was learning this slowly, and it frightened him more than the soldiers had.

* * *

They walked south through the Thornfell passes, and the world got wider and stranger. There were trees whose leaves turned gold and let go of themselves. There were birds that sang wrong — little sharp songs with no truth in them, songs that meant nothing, songs birds made just because they were alive and wanted to make noise. Orsen had never heard meaningless singing before. On the mountain, everything sang true or did not sing at all. He decided the small birds were brave, singing lies all day, and he liked them very much.

Marguerite walked fast. Marguerite always walked fast, as if the road behind them were a hand reaching up out of the ground. Sometimes at night Orsen heard him counting under his breath — coins, maybe, or names, or years. Orsen did not know what a year was yet. But he knew counting. Counting was how a man held on to things when he was afraid of losing them.

Orsen counted too. He counted the verses of the song inside him. There were six. He knew this because each verse had a color that lived behind his eyes when he hummed it: red like hearth-coal, white like snowlight, green like moss on the sanctuary stones, blue like deep water, black like the gap between stars, and the last one — the sixth — was silver, and it was broken. It was the verse he carried. It was the verse that had no ending, because the people who knew the ending were dead.

He had watched them die.

He did not want to tell Marguerite this. Telling was hard. His new words were small and clumsy, like trying to carry water in a net. But some nights, by the fire, when Marguerite thought Orsen was asleep, Orsen would feel the words pushing at him from inside, the way a bird pushes at an eggshell. And one night — the night they sheltered in the hollow beneath a fallen pine, with snow coming down soft as ash — the eggshell broke.

* * *

"Marguerite." He said the name carefully. It was a good name. Heavy, like a door. "I tell you thing now."

Marguerite looked up from whittling a stick into nothing. His eyes were gray-green, like the mountain lakes, and they went still when they looked at Orsen. Still eyes. Orsen had learned that still eyes meant listening, and listening meant safe.

"I come from mountain," Orsen said. "Sanctuary. High place. Many keepers there. Keepers are — " he searched, hands moving, shaping air — "people who hold song. Not own. Hold. Like cup holds water but water is not cup's."

"Yes," Marguerite said quietly. "I think I understand."

"There was fire," Orsen said. "Men in — " he made claws of his fingers, remembering the shapes — "black clothes. Red marks. They came in night. They killed everyone. Everyone who slept. Everyone who prayed. Everyone who sang. I run because — " here his voice went strange, wobbled, because the memory was not a word-memory, it was a song-memory, and songs do not know how to be quiet — "because my teacher put me in hole under floor. She said: *carry it*. She said: *the song is not yours, you are the song's*. Then she closed the stone over me and I heard her voice stop."

He pressed his face against his knees. The words ran out. There were no more words for what came after; there was only the sound the mountain had made, which was silence, which was the worst sound there was.

Marguerite did not speak for a long time. When Orsen finally lifted his head, he saw that the man's hands had stopped whittling, and that the knife was lying forgotten in the dirt, and that something behind the man's still eyes had cracked open like river ice in spring.

"The song chooses carriers," Orsen managed. "Not owners. My teacher say. Song goes where it goes. No one tells it. It tells you."

"And it chose you," Marguerite said. His voice was rough, like a stone dragged over stone.

"It chose me before born," Orsen said. "I think. Teacher say some are sung before they are. Mothers sing the child into carrying. Mother sings, and the song hears, and says *yes, this one*."

He stopped. Something stirred in him then, something older than his few weeks of new words — a knowing without language, a warmth behind the eyes, in the place where the sixth verse lived. He looked at Marguerite properly, really looked, the way you look at a song to find its truth.

"You," Orsen said. "Your mother. She sing?"

* * *

Marguerite went very still. Stillness again — but a different kind than listening-stillness. This was the stillness of a man standing on ice and hearing it begin to sing underneath him.

"My mother," Marguerite said at last, and his voice came out flat and careful, the way men speak when they are holding something heavy above a long drop. "My mother died when I was young. She used to sing. Lullabies, she called them. I never thought — "

He stopped. Started again. Stopped again. Orsen waited. Waiting was a thing he was good at; he had waited under a stone floor while the world above him ended.

"She sang in a language nobody knew," Marguerite said finally. "I asked her once what it was. She laughed and said it was nothing, just old words from her grandmother, just nonsense she'd never unlearned. But sometimes — sometimes when she sang, the room changed. I remember that. I'd forgotten I remembered it. The candle flames would stand up straighter. The water in the basin would go perfectly still, like glass. And I would feel — " his hand rose to his chest, pressed there — "I would feel *held*. Like something enormous had put its arms around our whole house."

Orsen felt the silver verse turn over inside him, slow, like a great fish rising toward light.

"That is Choir-speech," Orsen said. "I know sound of it. Even small piece. Even lullaby-piece. Candle straighten. Water still. That is song touching world and world saying yes."

"No," Marguerite said. But it was not a *no* that meant no. It was the *no* a man says when the ground has moved under him and he needs a moment to find his feet. "No. She was a weaver's daughter from a village that isn't even on maps anymore. She wasn't — she couldn't have been — "

"Keeper?" Orsen offered the word gently, like setting down something fragile.

"A Keeper." Marguerite said it the way a man reads his own name on a list of the dead. "You're telling me my mother was a Keeper."

"Not keeper maybe," Orsen said, because honesty mattered more than comfort, and the Choir taught honesty before it taught anything. "Maybe carrier. Maybe half. Maybe she carry one verse and never know. Song hide in people like seed in winter-ground. Some grow. Some sleep hundred years. Some sleep forever and never know what they held."

"But she sang it to me," Marguerite said. "She sang it into me. Before I was born. Whether she knew or not — "

"Song does not need knowing," Orsen said. "Song only needs giving."

And then he saw it happen — saw it with his own eyes, the way you see lightning and cannot afterward pretend you didn't. Marguerite began to hum. Low, barely a breath, a scrap of melody Orsen recognized the way you recognize your own reflection in dark water: it was a fragment of the second verse, the white one, the snowlight one. Marguerite didn't know what he was humming. His body knew. His mother's voice had planted it in him twenty years ago and it had lain there dormant as a seed, and now the boy on the mountainside of his life had come along and rained on it, and it was growing.

Orsen listened. And as he listened, something impossible happened, something that had happened to him only twice before, both times on the mountain, both times in the sanctuary's great hall with all the keepers singing together:

The fragment gave something back.

Marguerite hummed, and Orsen felt memories move — not his own. He could feel the edges of them, the shape of them, the way you can feel what's wrapped in cloth without unwrapping it. Memories going *into* Marguerite, or perhaps *out* of him, or perhaps simply turning over, waking, remembering themselves. Orsen did not fully understand it. But he understood enough: the song was a door, and doors opened both ways, and Marguerite had sold pieces of himself once — Orsen had heard the man talk in his sleep about sold things, sold griefs, sold years — and the song was finding those empty rooms and filling them again.

"Marguerite," Orsen whispered. "Song give back. What you sell. Some of it. If you sing more."

Marguerite's humming stopped. He stared at the boy as if the boy had spoken in tongues.

"What did you say?"

"Song is not taking-thing," Orsen said, growing bolder, the words coming easier now that the dam had broken. "Everyone think song is treasure, is weapon, is thing to steal. Wrong. Wrong wrong wrong. Song is debt. Song owes. Song pays. My teacher say: *a debt paid in song is the only debt the world forgives*. You give breath, song gives back what breath lost."

Marguerite put his face in his hands. Orsen watched the man's shoulders shake — not crying, exactly, or not only crying. Something bigger than crying. A man discovering that the thing he had spent his whole life selling — memory, other people's memory, his own memory, the whole trade of him — was not merchandise at all. Was music. Had always been music. That he had been a thief of songs and never known it, and that the song had chosen him anyway, had been waiting in him since before his birth, patient as winter, certain as spring.

"Orsen," Marguerite said, and the name came out broken and remade, "your teacher. The one who hid you. Did she ever say — did she ever say what happens when the last verse is finished? What happens to the ones who finish it?"

Orsen knew the answer. He had always known the answer, the way he had always known the sixth verse was silver and broken. He had been hoping, these weeks on the road, that he had misremembered.

"Song must be whole to work," Orsen said. "Whole song unmake false king. Whole song free cities. But whole song need — " his throat closed. He forced it open. "Need carrier give everything. All memory. All self. Song take what freely given and make it verse. Carrier wake after with nothing. Empty cup. New person almost."

The fire crackled. Snow fell past the pine branches. Somewhere far off, a wolf sang a meaningless song, and Orsen envied it.

"So if I help you," Marguerite said slowly, "if I help you finish it — you forget everything. Your teacher. The mountain. Me."

Orsen nodded. He could not make the words for *yes, and I am afraid, and I will do it anyway*, so he did the only thing the old language allowed him. He leaned across the fire, and he took Marguerite's scarred hand, and he sang — three notes only, a tiny silver shard of the broken verse, the smallest true thing he owned.

And Marguerite, thief of memories, seller of griefs, son of a hidden Keeper, squeezed the boy's hand and did not let go.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, as they always did, walking fast, always fast. But something had changed between them, and Orsen could feel it in the way Marguerite checked the road behind them — not like a man fleeing anymore, but like a man guarding. Guarding was different from fleeing. Fleeing was fear. Guarding was love wearing armor.

Orsen practiced his new words as they walked. *Guard. Love. Stay.* He rolled them around like the pebbles he used to collect at the sanctuary stream, back when the stream was still there, back when there was still a there.

Near midday, when they paused at a high saddle and looked back the way they had come, Orsen saw it at the same moment Marguerite did: a thread of smoke rising from the valley behind them, thin and black and wrong. Not chimney-smoke. Chimney-smoke was gray and lazy. This smoke stood up straight, disciplined, deliberate — the smoke of burning done on purpose, by people who kept ledgers of it.

And below the smoke, glinting on the switchback road like a line of black ants carrying fire, riders.

"Veyra," Marguerite breathed, and the name tasted of iron even to Orsen, who had never heard it before but somehow knew it, the way he knew the sixth verse was broken. The song had told him. The song always told him first.

Marguerite took his hand. "We're not stopping," he said. "We're not hiding. We're going to Cael Harrow, and we're going to finish this — you and me, whatever it costs. Do you understand me?"

Orsen understood. Understanding was the one word he had never needed to learn.

But as they turned south and began to run, the silver verse inside him stirred and sang a warning he could not yet translate into any language, old or new — a warning that Cael Harrow was not a refuge, that someone in the free city was already counting them like coins, and that the hardest choice was still waiting for them, patient as winter, at the end of the road.
