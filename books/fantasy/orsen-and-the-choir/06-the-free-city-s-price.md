## 6. The Free City's Price

Cael Harrow announced itself the way a knife announces itself: not with beauty, but with intent.

Marguerite had seen the free city from the smuggler roads a dozen times in his life — from ridgelines at dusk, when its thousand lamps spilled down the terraces like molten brass poured over black stone — but he had never seen it as he saw it now, walking through the Salt Gate with a boy's hand in his and a stolen song sleeping against his ribs. The gate guards barely glanced at them. That was the first thing wrong. In every other city of the Thornfell marches, a ragged man leading a silent child drew eyes like flies to a wound. Here, the guards looked *through* them, and Marguerite understood that someone had already told them what to look for instead.

Orsen felt it too. His small fingers tightened around Marguerite's, and beneath his breath — so faint that only a memory-thief's trained ear could have caught it — he hummed three notes of warning.

"I know," Marguerite murmured. "I know."

The city had changed since his last visit, two years gone. Then, Cael Harrow had been a sprawl of honest vice: fish-reeking quays along the Harrow Water, gambling dens stacked atop silk warehouses, the great Council Spire rising white and arrogant above it all. Now there were soldiers on the corners wearing no livery he recognized, and notices nailed to every post in three languages, offering rewards for "anomalies" — the word written plainly, without shame, as though the usurper Queen Sarelle Vane had decided that terror worked better when it was administrative. Marguerite read one notice twice. *Persons exhibiting unexplained speech, unexplained song, or unexplained knowledge are to be reported to the Office of Harmonic Inquiry.* Below the words was a woodcut of an ear.

"They're listening," Orsen whispered, in his careful, half-built Common Tongue. "With more than ears."

"Don't sing," Marguerite said. "Not here. Not even inside your head if you can help it."

The boy looked up at him with those grey eyes that were older than any child's eyes had a right to be, and nodded once. It was the nod of a soldier. It frightened Marguerite more than the notices did.

* * *

They found lodging the only way Marguerite knew how: by being nobody. A room above a dyer's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, where the air stank of ammonia and lye and no one asked questions because everyone had something to hide. Marguerite paid for a week in clipped silver and told the dyer's widow they were brothers. She believed him, or pretended to, which amounted to the same thing in Cael Harrow.

For three days, nothing happened. And that, too, was wrong.

On the fourth morning, Marguerite went out for bread and came back to find a woman sitting in their room, drinking their tea, her boots crossed at the ankle on the windowsill, watching the door with the patience of a cat that has already eaten.

She was perhaps fifty, dressed in the plain grey wool of a merchant's widow, with iron-grey hair pinned severely and hands folded in her lap like a woman at prayer. There was nothing remarkable about her at all except her stillness. Everything else in Cael Harrow moved — barges, beggars, gulls, smoke — but this woman sat as though motion were a tax she refused to pay.

"Marguerite Dravenmoor," she said. Her voice was dry and pleasant, like paper turning. "Memory-thief. Third-generation Thornfell smuggler. Banned from the Salt Gate in '41 for selling a customs officer his own wedding night back to him at interest." She smiled thinly. "I've been reading your file. It's very entertaining. You should be proud of it."

Marguerite did not move from the doorway. Behind him, in the narrow stairwell, he heard Orsen go quiet — that particular silence, the one that meant the boy was listening with the part of himself that predated language.

"You're in my room," Marguerite said.

"I'm in the Crown's room, technically. Everything in Cael Harrow is the Crown's room; we just let people borrow it." She set down the tea. "My name is Odile Corvane. I run the things in this city that don't officially exist. Sit down, thief. We're going to discuss your future, and I'd rather you weren't hovering in the doorway like a man deciding whether to jump."

Marguerite sat. It cost him something to do it, and he made sure she saw the cost. With people like Odile Corvane, you showed them your fear early and honestly, because they would find it anyway, and finding it themselves would make them curious, and curiosity in such creatures always ended in dissection.

"Where is the boy?" Odile asked.

"Safe."

"Meaning where?"

"Meaning safe."

Her smile didn't waver, but something behind it recalibrated. "You love him," she said, testing the word like a coin bitten between teeth. "That wasn't in the file. Files don't update fast enough for love. It's the one commodity in this city I can't buy at market rate." She leaned forward slightly. "So I won't try to buy it. I'll try to save it. Listen carefully, Marguerite, because I will only say this once, and then I will say worse things."

"Say them."

"The Queen's armies crossed the Ashford ford nine days ago. Forty thousand, maybe fifty. They'll be at the Thornfell passes before the first snows, and after the passes, here. Cael Harrow has walls, but walls are a promise the past makes to people who aren't paying attention. The last city that trusted its walls against Sarelle Vane is now a province with a new name." She folded her hands again. "We cannot beat her armies. We can, however, break her claim. And her claim is a piece of paper."

Marguerite waited. He had spent twenty years learning that the most dangerous words were the ones that came after a pause, and Odile Corvane paused like a professional.

"The Charter of the Blood-Oath," she said. "Sealed ninety years ago, when Vane's grandmother took the throne. It's a resonance document — blood-ink on choir-parchment, bound into the old sanctuary-stone the usurpers looted when they burned the Choir's sanctuaries. Every oath of legitimacy since has been sworn upon it. Every lord who kneels to Sarelle Vane kneels because that charter says her line holds the right of harmony over the land. It sits in the vault beneath her fortress at Greywatch, three days north of here, behind wards that were built by the same masons who built the vault itself." Her eyes, pale and flat as winter water, fixed on him. "You are going to steal it for me."

"No," Marguerite said.

"Yes," Odile said pleasantly. "You are. Because the alternative is that the Office of Harmonic Inquiry finds your boy first. They have a description circulating — a child, eight or nine years, dark-haired, mute by report, seen traveling south with a man matching yours. Two of my own agents sold me that description, Marguerite, which tells you how porous my city has become. I can protect him. No one else can. Not the Council — half of them are already drafting surrender terms in their heads. Not the temples. Me. Only me." She rose, unhurried, and went to the window. Below, the Harrow Water glittered, indifferent. "In exchange: protection for the boy under my personal seal, passage for both of you wherever you wish when this is done, and enough money that you never have to sell another stranger's grief to eat. All of it, for one theft."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I arrest you tonight for harboring an anomaly, hand you both to the Inquiry, and sleep perfectly well, because I will have done my duty by a city that doesn't deserve it." She turned back. "But you won't refuse. You've been refusing since you walked in, and your hands haven't stopped shaking once. Look at them."

He looked. They were shaking. He hated her for making him look.

* * *

He found Orsen on the roof of the dyer's shop, sitting cross-legged among the drying frames, watching the gulls wheel over the harbor. The boy didn't turn when Marguerite climbed up beside him. He simply said, in that voice still learning its own edges:

"You smell like a bargain."

"You smell like eavesdropping."

"I listen well." Orsen picked at a splinter of frame-wood. "You will say yes."

Marguerite looked at him. Three days in this city, and the boy had learned to read its silences the way Marguerite read ledgers. "Why do you say that?"

"Because you already said yes inside yourself, on the stairs, before she finished talking. Your heart did it." He touched his own chest. "Mine heard it. Hearts are loud, Papa-Marguerite. Louder than songs."

The name still caught in Marguerite's throat sometimes — *Papa-Marguerite*, the boy's own invention, half title and half talisman, as though calling him father required hedging the bet. He cleared his throat. "It's a trap, Orsen. Maybe not hers — maybe she even means what she says. But the charter isn't just paper. You told me what the song wants. What it's been wanting since the sanctuary burned."

Orsen was quiet a long moment. A gull screamed somewhere below them, and the harbor bells rang the hour, and neither of them moved.

"The song needs a vessel," the boy said finally. "Something that held oaths. Something that remembers being sworn upon. The charter is soaked in ninety years of false harmony. If the Choir can reach it..." His grey eyes lifted. "It could unmake the lie at its root. But that is not what the lady wants. The lady wants a weapon. The song wants a key. And you want—" he stopped.

"Say it."

"You want us safe. And those are three different doors, and you can only walk through one, and you are going to pretend they are the same door because pretending is what you do best." The boy said it without cruelty, the way children say devastating things, and then he leaned his head against Marguerite's shoulder, and the whole speech dissolved into the simple weight of him. "I don't mind," he murmured. "Pretend well. Pretending kept us alive this far."

Marguerite put his arm around the boy and watched the gulls and thought about how many times in his life he had sold things — grief, weddings, first kisses, dying words, the taste of a mother's bread from a dead baker's memory — and how none of those sales had ever felt like this one, where for the first time he was selling a truth he actually believed in, to a buyer who would use it as a blade.

* * *

Odile Corvane received his answer that evening in a counting-house above the fish market, surrounded by maps weighted with coins, and she accepted it with the smallest possible smile, the smile of a woman crossing an item off a list.

"Wise," she said. "You leave tomorrow, before dawn, with papers I'll provide. A carter named Brannic will take you as far as the mill road; from there you know the country better than my people do. The vault at Greywatch opens for the quarterly tithe-inspection in eleven days. You'll be inside as an inspector's clerk. After that, you're on your own until you come out — and come out you must, because everything I've promised your boy depends on you coming out."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I keep my promises anyway." For one unguarded instant, something almost human moved behind the winter-water eyes. "I'm a monster, Dravenmoor, but I'm a monster with a ledger, and debts entered in it get paid. Go home. Sleep. Kiss the boy goodnight, if you do such things."

Marguerite turned to go, and her voice stopped him at the door, light and dry and terrible:

"Oh — one thing. The Inquiry's agents reported an anomaly singing on the south road four nights ago. A fragment of Choir-song, they said, strong enough to wake a wayhouse's dead hearth-fire. They lost the trail at the river." She was looking at her maps, not at him. "Strange, isn't it, how these anomalies keep appearing exactly where you've been. Almost as if something is following you. Or as if you're carrying it." She tapped a coin flat against the map of Greywatch. "Eleven days, thief. Don't make me send someone to find out which it is."

Marguerite walked home through the lamplit streets with the bargain sealed in his mouth like a stone, and behind him, in the counting-house window, Odile Corvane watched him go and turned to a waiting clerk and said, quietly, "Prepare the second team. If he takes the charter, we take the boy. And if he dies in there — we take the boy anyway."

And three streets away, walking through the dark, Orsen stopped suddenly in the middle of the empty square, tilted his head as if hearing a bell no one else could hear, and whispered: "Papa-Marguerite. Someone just promised to take me."

The lamps guttered. Somewhere far to the north, beyond the passes, the fortress of Greywatch slept with its stolen charter beating beneath it like a second heart — and the song inside the boy stirred, patient as winter, and began, very softly, to learn the shape of the vault.
