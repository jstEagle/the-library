## 7. Vault of Hollow Kings

The fortress of Vhal Doreth had been built to be looked at. Its black spires clawed at the clouds like accusations, its banners hung stiff with the usurper's crest — a crowned hand crushing a serpent — and every window blazed with wardlight so bright that the mountain snow below it never seemed to darken into true night.

Marguerite hated it immediately, and loved it a little, the way he loved all beautiful locked things.

He crouched in the shadow of the aqueduct that fed the citadel's baths, three hundred feet of slick stone arching over the outer bailey, and let his stolen eyes do their work. He was wearing tonight a courier's memory from a man named Tessel — dead now two winters, though Tessel didn't know it, wherever the Choir had set what remained of him — and through Tessel's recollection Marguerite knew the guard rotations as intimately as his own heartbeat. Knew which captains took bribes and which took confessions. Knew that the third bell after midnight belonged to a watch-captain who drank himself blind on plum brandy and wept about his mother.

Everyone's mother, eventually. That was the whole trade.

*You come back out,* Orsen had said that morning, in the waystation loft where Odile Corvane's people had quartered them. The boy had gripped Marguerite's sleeve with both hands, small fingers surprisingly strong. *You come back. Promise.*

*I don't promise,* Marguerite had told him, because lying to the boy felt suddenly like a sin he hadn't budgeted for. *I deliver.*

Orsen had stared at him with those too-old eyes, the ones that saw through skin to whatever ledger a man kept behind his ribs, and said nothing. Which was worse than any argument.

Now, in the cold, Marguerite pressed two fingers to the aqueduct's stone and began to climb.

* * *

The vault of Vhal Doreth lay beneath the throne hall, in a chamber the builders had carved in the shape of an inverted crown. Marguerite descended into it through a ventilation shaft Tessel's memory had shown him — one narrow throat of stone, unwatched because no one believed anything human could fold itself that small. Marguerite had spent twenty years learning exactly how small a person could be when they wanted something badly enough.

He dropped the last four feet into silence.

The chamber breathed cold. Not the honest cold of stone and winter but a curated cold, a preserved cold, like the inside of a reliquary. Along the curved walls, recessed alcoves held the trophies of the usurper's legitimacy: the sword her grandfather had carried across the Sundered Plain. The marriage crown of a queen whose name had been struck from every chronicle except this one. And there, at the apex of the inverted crown, on a pedestal of white marble veined with gold —

The Charter.

It did not look like much. A scroll case of black iron, banded with silver script that predated the kingdom's alphabet. Marguerite had expected grandeur; instead he felt a low, subterranean hum in his teeth, faint as a hive heard through a wall, and the stolen memories inside him stirred like rooks before a storm.

*There you are,* something in him whispered back. Not in his voice.

He crossed the floor in eleven steps, counting them the way he counted everything, and lifted the Charter from its pedestal.

Every thief knows the moment. The half-second between touching and not-being-caught-yet, when the whole world hangs balanced on your fingertips. Marguerite had lived in that half-second more than he'd lived anywhere else. But this time the balance tipped strangely: the iron case was warm. Warm as a sleeping child's brow. And beneath his palms he felt — or imagined he felt — a rhythm. Patient. Expectant.

A vessel waiting to be filled.

He slid the Charter into the false lining of his courier's satchel, beside the forged manifests and the vial of Odile's expensive poison, and turned for the shaft.

Sister Veyra was standing at the bottom of it.

* * *

She was smaller than her reputation. That was Marguerite's first absurd thought — this grey-clad woman with ink-stained fingers and a face like a closed account, this was the terror of the Thornfell? Then she smiled, politely, the way a clerk smiles at a debtor, and the reputation assembled itself around her all at once.

"Marguerite Dravenmoor," she said. Her voice was dry paper. "Thief of memories. Seller of other people's griefs." She tilted her head. "Do you know what I admire about you? Nothing is wasted on you. You have never once kept something you could sell."

"The compliment's wasted," Marguerite said, easing backward along the wall, putting a display case between them. "I'm keeping this one."

"The Charter." She glanced at the empty pedestal without alarm, as if objects simply wandered off sometimes and clerks made note of it. "You cannot imagine why the free cities want it. You've told yourself it's leverage, ransom, coin." She stepped forward. One step only, unhurried. "But you've been carrying that boy for weeks, Master Dravenmoor. Haven't you wondered why your hoard stirs when he sings? Why old sold things come home to you?"

The hum in his teeth deepened. In the walls. In the floor.

"You're not a thief," Veyra continued softly. "You're a carrier who never learned what he carries. And I am here, by the Regent's grace, to close the accounts." She raised her right hand, and the wardlight along the ceiling gathered toward her palm like moths. "Kneel, and I will make the entry painless. It will say only: *anomaly resolved.*"

Marguerite had faced death before — in a burning warehouse in Cael Harrow, on a frozen pass with a knife at his kidney, once in a dream so vivid he'd woken weeping. He had always talked. Talked his way past blades, past borders, past the particular gravity of other people's convictions.

"I'll tell you what I know about ledgers," he said, backing toward the far arch. "Everything entered gets balanced. Every debt called in. Yours is coming, Sister. It's already singing."

Veyra frowned — genuinely puzzled, a bookkeeper confronted with an unfamiliar column — and that frown bought him three steps, four, five. Then her hand closed and the wards came down.

Light fell in bars. Alarms woke in the stone itself, a soundless shrieking Marguerite felt rather than heard, and somewhere above, boots began to drum the flagstones in disciplined thunder. Veyna — Veyra walked after him, unhurried still, certain as arithmetic.

He reached the arch. Beyond it lay a corridor, then stairs, then nothing he could outrun.

And from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls themselves, came singing.

* * *

Later, Marguerite would try to describe it and fail. It was not a melody so much as a remembering — hundreds of voices braided into one, wordless, ancient, rising up through the fortress foundations like water finding its level. The wards of Vhal Doreth had been built on old stones, stones quarried from places the usurper's histories preferred forgotten, and Orsen — defying every instruction, every promise extracted, every sensible plan — had put his hands against the outer wall of the throne hall and sung the fortress awake.

The light-bars shattered. The wardlight died in the corridor like blown candles. Above, the drumming of boots stumbled, broke rhythm, became shouting, became screaming, became — Marguerite would swear to his grave — weeping, dozens of soldiers weeping in the dark as something vast and gentle moved through their memories and refused to take anything, only gave.

Veyra stood very still in the ruined vault. Her ledger-hand hung open at her side, ink-stained fingers curled loosely, as if she were listening to a column of numbers rearrange itself.

"That song," she said quietly. "I know it. I—" She stopped. Closed her mouth. For the first time since the aqueduct, Sister Veyra looked less like a clerk and more like a woman hearing a hymn from a childhood she'd been told never happened.

"Come find out," Marguerite said, and ran.

* * *

They went out through the chaos, two shadows among many. The fortress had become a wound: guards wandering the halls with tears on their faces, officers shouting orders no one obeyed, and everywhere that strange singing threading through the stone, patient, immense. Marguerite found Orsen where he'd left him — no. Not where he'd left him. Where the boy had chosen to stand, at the base of the great outer stair, hands flat against the black rock, head bowed, pouring himself into the walls while guards knelt around him in a loose, bewildered ring, some reaching toward him, none touching.

"Orsen." Marguerite caught his shoulder. "Time to go."

The boy opened his eyes. They were luminous, unfocused, full of something that wasn't entirely Orsen anymore. "They remember," he whispered. "The stones remember being free."

"They can remember on the road. Move."

They went down through the bailey under a sky gone wrong — the auroral shimmer of the wards collapsing, green fire crawling across the clouds. Behind them, in the highest tower, a window blazed alight, and Marguerite felt rather than saw a figure standing there: tall, crowned, motionless. Watching them go. The usurper herself, learning in one terrible instant that the thing her grandmother's blood-oath had been sworn to strangle was not only alive but singing in her own walls.

The look that followed them down the mountain was not rage.

It was fear.

* * *

They ran until the fortress was a smear of dying green light behind them, until the smuggler paths swallowed them, until Orsen's legs gave out and Marguerite carried him on his back through knee-deep snow, the Charter warm against his spine like a second heartbeat.

Near dawn they collapsed in a shepherd's hut with a hole in its roof, and Orsen, half-asleep against his chest, murmured: "You came back."

"I delivered," Marguerite said.

"No." The boy's hand found his, small and burning hot. "You *came back*. There's a difference. Now you know it too."

Marguerite looked at the iron case in his lap, humming softly, patient as a seed. Somewhere south, Odile Corvane waited to take it from him and call it protection. Somewhere north, a woman with ink-stained fingers stood in a silent vault, holding an open ledger and, perhaps, beginning to doubt its arithmetic.

Triumph, Marguerite thought, was just exposure with better lighting. Everyone in the world now knew what he carried — and worse, they knew he mattered.

He pulled his cloak around the boy and watched the snow fill the roof-hole, and understood, with a thief's clarity, that the hardest part of the job had not even started yet.
