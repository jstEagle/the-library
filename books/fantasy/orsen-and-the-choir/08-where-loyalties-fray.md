## 8. Where Loyalties Fray

Odile Corvane had learned long ago that the truth of a city lived not in its temples or its councils but in its ledgers — the small ones, kept in back rooms by people who counted what others preferred to ignore. She kept such a ledger herself, bound in grey calfskin, and she was reading it by lamplight when the boy began to sing three floors below.

The sound came up through the stone of Cael Harrow like water through chalk. It was not loud. That was the terrible thing about it. It did not need to be loud. It simply *was*, the way dawn is, and every servant in the Spire stopped what they were doing to listen, and every guard forgot his post for exactly as long as the verse lasted, and Odile sat very still with her pen hovering above the page and felt something old and half-buried turn over in her chest — a memory of her mother's voice, though her mother had died singing nothing but lullabies in a tongue no one had spoken since.

*There it is again,* she thought. *The anomaly.*

She had named it that in her reports because names were instruments, and an instrument had to be shaped before it could be used. "Choral anomaly." Cold enough for the Council, precise enough for her own purposes. What it actually was — what she had known since the night Marguerite Dravenmoor came stumbling into her office smelling of mountain rain and desperation, with a starving child asleep against his shoulder — was the last living verse of the Choir, wrapped in skin.

Odile closed the ledger and went down to see him.

They kept Orsen in the east tower now, in comfortable rooms with comfortable bars on the windows that everyone pretended were decorative. He sat at the sill when she entered, small hands folded, watching the harbor lights with the patience of something far older than eight years old. He turned when she came in and gave her the look he gave everyone: open, guileless, and utterly unreadable.

"You sang tonight," she said.

"I sing every night." His accent had smoothed over the weeks; the wayhouse keeper's name had stuck to him like burrs. "It helps them sleep."

"It helps them sleep," Odile agreed. "And it helps them forget which doors they guard."

The boy tilted his head. He knew. She had never quite decided whether that frightened her more than if he hadn't.

"Orsen," she said, drawing the chair close and sitting so their eyes were level, because children trusted faces at their own height, and trust was a door she had opened ten thousand times. "Do you know why you're here?"

"To be safe," he said. "Marguerite said."

"Marguerite said," she echoed gently. "And Marguerite is a good man, in his crooked way. But good men are not always wise men, and wise men are rarely free. The usurper is coming, Orsen. Her armies crossed the Serevan ford nine days ago. In a month, maybe six weeks, her inquisitors will stand beneath these walls, and Sister Veyra does not negotiate. She burns. You have seen what she leaves behind."

The boy's eyes flickered — the first crack in the serenity. He had seen. They all had, in the refugees' faces, in the wagons of the burned coming down from the high valleys.

"I can protect you," Odile said. "Cael Harrow has walls older than the usurper's bloodline and deeper than her vaults. But walls alone won't hold her. Not this time. What will hold her is *you*. Your song unmakes her claim before her soldiers ever reach us. It unmakes the lie she built her crown on. One verse, sung from the highest tower, carried on the wind to her whole army — and her generals wake up one morning unable to remember why they follow her." She smiled, warm and reasonable. "You would end a war, Orsen. You would save a hundred thousand people who cannot save themselves. Isn't that what the Choir was *for*?"

The boy looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, in that pre-kingdom tongue that made the lamplight shiver, something short and soft, and Odile — who had spent three months paying scholars to translate fragments of Choir-speech — caught only the shape of it, and the shape was a refusal.

"You don't understand me yet," he added, in trade-tongue. "The song isn't a sword. It doesn't point where you aim it."

"Everything points where you aim it," Odile said, "if you build the aiming well."

She left him then, and climbed back up through the Spire's cold spiral stairs, and in her office she unlocked the second drawer of her desk — the one without a lock anyone else knew about — and took out the plans.

They were beautiful. She permitted herself that vanity. A resonance lattice of silver and glass, designed by the best artificer gold could buy, to be raised atop the great tower: a cage of harmonics that would take whatever the boy sang and throw it outward, amplified, directed, *aimed* — north, toward the usurper's host, and nowhere else. And when the war was won, the lattice would remain. And the boy would remain inside it. Because a weapon that could unmake a blood-oath could unmake a council charter too, and a senate, and a treaty, and Odile Corvane had read enough history to know that the free cities would never voluntarily set down an instrument like that once they'd held it.

So the lattice would remain, and the song would remain, and Orsen would remain — safe, honored, celebrated, and caged until the day he died or the day someone pried the bars apart.

She wrote the order for the lattice's construction in her neat, merciless hand, sealed it, and felt nothing she would have called guilt. Guilt was a luxury of people who could afford clean hands. The free cities survived on women willing to dirty theirs.

Still — and this she noted with clinical interest, filing it away beside the memory of her mother's lullabies — when she thought of the boy's face at the window, open and guileless and utterly unreadable, some small unledgered part of her whispered that she had just become everything she had spent thirty years fighting.

She blew out the lamp and let the whisper starve in the dark.

* * *

Marguerite found out the way he found out most things: by listening in rooms he wasn't supposed to be in.

He'd been uneasy for days — a thief's unease, the itch between the shoulder blades that meant a mark had marked him back. Odile had been too gracious since the vault. Too generous with the wine, too quick with the assurances. And Orsen had gone quiet in a way the boy was never quiet, humming less, asking — twice, carefully, in his careful new words — whether they would leave soon.

So Marguerite did what he did. He picked a lock, folded himself into the dark behind the wine racks off the Spire's council antechamber, and waited.

They came at midnight: Odile, the artificer Vess with her rolled schematics, and two senators whose voices he knew from a hundred intercepted conversations. He crouched in the dark among the bottles and listened to them plan his boy's cage.

"— resonance lattice, silver core, mounted on the great tower—"

"— amplification without consent? The Choir-keepers chose their carriers, Corvane. There are records—"

"There are *ruins*," Odile said, "and ruins don't argue. The boy sings, we aim the song, the usurper's oath breaks, the war ends. Afterward we'll discuss the ethics."

"Afterward," Vess repeated dryly. "How convenient that afterward never arrives."

"And the thief?" one of the senators asked. "Dravenmoor knows the vault, knows the lattice's purpose if he sees the schematics. Loose threads—"

"Marguerite Dravenmoor," Odile said, and there was something almost fond in it, which was worse than hatred, "is a man who has sold everything he ever loved. We'll offer him enough and he'll sell the boy himself. Failing that—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk, "—the passes are dangerous this time of year. Accidents happen to men who travel with valuable cargo."

Marguerite stayed crouched in the dark long after they left, among the wine and the dust, and felt the strange double motion of his own heart: the old Marguerite, the ledger-hearted one, calculating that Odile was right, that he *had* sold everything, that a man didn't change his nature just because a child hummed at his doorstep — and beneath that, rising like floodwater, something that was not calculation at all.

*Accidents happen.* To men who traveled with valuable cargo.

He thought of Orsen asleep in the east tower, hands folded, patient as something older than kingdoms. He thought of the wayhouse keeper naming him, of the firelit nights on the smuggler's road teaching him which mushrooms killed and which stars meant south, of the memories the boy's singing had given back to him — his mother's kitchen, his mother's voice, his mother's Keeper's hands — memories he had sold and mourned and never expected to hold again.

*I already sold my mother once,* he thought. *I'll be damned in every hell the Thornfell remembers before I sell him.*

He moved. Out through the racks, along the servants' stair, past guards whose attention he stole as lightly as coin — a touch here, a suggestion there, their gazes sliding off him like rain off oilcloth. The east tower's lock yielded in four breaths.

Orsen was awake, sitting at the barred window, and when Marguerite slipped inside the boy simply looked at him and said, "I know. I heard them building it in my head. Silver sings badly."

"We're leaving. Now. Tonight." Marguerite was already at the bars, testing them, finding the mortar soft with age. "High passes, north and west, to the sanctuary. The Choir's old seat. If the song needs finishing, it finishes there — not in their tower, not aimed like an arrow."

"The armies—"

"Are nine days past the ford and a month from these walls. We have time if we don't waste it talking." The bar groaned; stone dust sifted down. "Orsen. Look at me." The boy turned, lamplight from the harbor catching his face. "What I'm about to do costs me everything I've built here. Every contact, every buyer, every road I know. Odile will hunt us, and Veyra will hunt us, and half the free cities will call me a traitor by morning. I need you to understand that I'm doing it anyway."

The boy studied him with those ancient, guileless eyes. Then he reached out and touched Marguerite's wrist — the same gesture, Marguerite realized with a jolt, that his mother had made in a memory returned to him on a mountainside, the Keeper's greeting, palm over pulse.

"I know," Orsen said simply. "The song told me about you before I ever saw your door."

The bar came free. Below them, across the black harbor, the beacon-fires of the watchtowers were lighting one by one, north to south, like a wound opening along the coast — signal-fire, alarm-fire, the visual grammar of a nation waking to war drums. Somewhere beyond the hills the usurper's columns were marching, and somewhere closer Odile Corvane was already reaching for her pen to write the word *accident*.

Marguerite lifted the boy onto his back, took the servants' stair two steps at a time, and ran for the north gate and the dark wall of the Thornfell beyond it — toward the ruined sanctuary, toward the last verse, toward whatever the mountains demanded next.

Behind them, Cael Harrow's bells began to ring.
