## 9. The Last Verse

The sanctuary had been a cathedral once, or something older than cathedrals — Marguerite could not decide which. Its walls rose in ribbed arcs of grey stone, split by centuries of frost, and through the shattered roof the sky poured down like cold water, pooling silver on the broken flagstones. Snow drifted through the open crown of the ruin and settled on the pews, which were carved not for sitting but for *listening* — long troughs of stone angled toward a dais where nothing stood but a basin of black glass, wide as a grave.

Orsen walked ahead of him, small boots crunching frost, and the boy's humming filled the hollow place the way water fills a cup. The song did not echo. It *gathered*. It came back to them from every wall at once, layered, multiplied, as though the ruin itself had been waiting years to remember how to sing.

Marguerite stopped at the threshold and felt his stolen hoard stir.

He had carried other people's memories inside him for twenty years — a merchant's grief over a drowned daughter, a soldier's last morning with his brother, a bride's terror on her wedding night, a hundred small joys bought cheap and sold dear. They lived behind his ribs like birds in a cage, restless when he was calm, screaming when he was afraid. Now they beat against their bars all together, and beneath them, deeper than any theft, he felt something else turning over. Something that had been asleep since he was six years old and a woman with tired hands sang to him in a language no kingdom spoke.

*His mother's language.*

"Orsen," he said quietly. "What is this place?"

The boy turned. In the silver light his face looked carved from the same stone as the walls — ancient and young at once. When he spoke now, it was in words, real words, though his accent still bent them strangely, as if each one had been carried a long way before being set down.

"This is where the Choir kept its throat," Orsen said. "Before the usurper's grandmother burned it. The song lived here. Not in people. In *here*." He pressed his small fist against his own chest, then opened it toward the black basin. "And in there. The Vessel. The charter was made from its sister-glass. That is why we needed it. That is why the Choir needed *you* to take it."

Marguerite's mouth went dry. "You said the free cities needed it. Odile said—"

"Odile Corvane says many things." There was no bitterness in the boy's voice, only a terrible gentleness that was worse. "Come. It is time. And there is little of it."

* * *

Sister Veyra climbed.

The pass above the sanctuary was a knife-edge of ice and scree, and her soldiers cursed it, and she did not hear them, because Sister Veyra had stopped hearing anything that was not written down. Her ledger rode against her heart in an oilcloth wrapping, and she touched it the way other women touched relics.

Forty-one pages. Forty-one entries since the choral anomaly had first appeared in the eastern villages: names of the dead, names of the sheltering, names of the burned. She had written them all in the same hand, in the same ink, at the same hour of each night, because irregularity was how heresies began. A village called Lowmark, ninety souls, entered and closed. A wayhouse keeper named Brann who had given the boy a name — *Orsen*, the ledger said, *subject designated by unlicensed baptism* — entered and closed. A smuggler named Marguerite Dravenmoor, memory-thief, unregistered practitioner, entered and—

She paused on the switchback, breath smoking, and read the entry again. *Unresolved.*

It troubled her. Not the man himself; men were simple, men were sums. What troubled her was the column beside his name, where she recorded disposition. Every other name in forty-one pages had a disposition. This one had a blank, and the blank had survived three revisions of the ledger, and Sister Veyra did not permit blanks.

Below her, the army wound up the mountain like a black river flowing uphill. Above her, the ruined sanctuary crouched against the sky. From somewhere within it came a sound that made her soldiers stop and look at one another, and made Veyra's hand press flat against her ledger.

Singing. Many voices from one throat.

She wrote, in the falling snow: *Anomaly converging on primary site. Disposition pending.* Then she climbed on, because the ledger demanded it, because the Empress-Regent demanded it, because a woman who has burned ninety souls cannot stop climbing without asking why she burned them, and Sister Veyra had spent eleven years not asking.

* * *

Inside, the Choir woke fully.

Marguerite felt it happen — felt the song rise out of the stone and out of the boy and out of the sleeping thing behind his own ribs until the air itself seemed to have texture, seemed to press against his skin like a hand. The black basin began to glow from within, deep red, then gold, and across its surface moved images: mountains unbroken by roads, cities that sang instead of speaking, a bloodline kneeling while ten thousand voices held it in check like a leash of light.

Then fire. Then silence. Then centuries of the song passing hand to hidden hand, carrier to carrier, shrinking smaller and smaller, until all that remained of it fit inside a starving child on a smuggler's doorstep.

"The verse is incomplete," Orsen said. His voice shook. He was crying without noise, the way children cry when they have learned that noise brings soldiers. "One voice alone can carry the melody. But the last verse needs *weight*. It needs a life poured into it — memories, freely given, everything a person is. That is what the Keepers did. That is why there are no Keepers left. Each one became part of the song so the song could live."

Marguerite looked at the basin. He understood then, completely, in the way one understands a fall only after stepping off the edge.

"And you're asking me—"

"No." Orsen grabbed his coat with both fists, and the boy's eyes were furious and terrified. "I am *not* asking. I will do it. I am the carrier. It should be me. I only came to say goodbye properly, because Mama taught me goodbyes must be proper—"

"You'll do no such thing."

"I am the last—"

"You're a *child*." Marguerite knelt, gripping the boy's shoulders, and heard his own voice crack like river ice. "You're a starving boy who eats with his elbows and hums when he's frightened and calls snow 'sky-flour' because some wayhouse keeper told him it was funny. You are not dying on this mountain so an old thief can walk away with a song and a clean conscience. Do you hear me?"

Orsen stared at him. "But your memories. All of them. Everything you stole. Everything you *were*. You would forget your mother. You would forget—" His face crumpled. "You would forget me."

"Yes," Marguerite said.

Outside, faintly, steel rang on stone. Boots. Many.

"They'll be here soon," he said. "So listen to me, Orsen. Twenty years I sold pieces of people. Grief. Love. Last words. I told myself it was trade, that everyone sells something, that the road doesn't forgive sentiment. And every coin I earned sat in a box under my floor because I couldn't spend blood-money without hearing them. I have never kept anything I couldn't sell. Never let myself. Because keeping things means losing things, and I decided losing was a country I'd already emigrated from." He laughed, wet and short. "And then a mute boy sat on my doorstep humming a tune that opened every lock I ever built, and I learned I'd been lying to myself for twenty years. I *kept* something after all. I kept you. I didn't even notice myself doing it."

"Marguerite—"

"So this isn't sacrifice." He stood, and drew the boy into the circle of his arm, and walked him toward the basin. "This is the first honest sale of my life. Everything I am, freely given, for the one thing worth buying." He looked down at the glowing glass, at the images moving there — his mother's face, suddenly, young and singing, and he hadn't known the Choir kept her too. "Hello, Mama," he whispered. "I found the song. Took me long enough."

The Choir spoke then, not in words but in understanding, vast and patient and old: *Freely given?*

"Freely given," Marguerite said. "Every theft. Every sale. Every lonely year. Take the lot. Consider it a debt paid — mine, not yours."

*And what remains will be empty,* the Choir said, sorrowful as winter. *A vessel with nothing in it.*

"Then fill it with whatever's left over," Marguerite said, and smiled at the boy one last time — memorizing nothing, because there would be nothing left to memorize with, and somehow that made the sight of Orsen's tear-streaked, furious, beloved face the easiest thing he had ever looked at. "Mind the shop, kid."

He stepped into the light.

* * *

Sister Veyra reached the sanctuary doors as the singing crested.

It was not a sound. That was her first coherent thought, standing in the shattered doorway with her ledger clutched to her chest and forty-one pages of dispositions fluttering in the wind of it. Sound went *through* a person. This went *around* them, and *under*, and rose up through the soles of her boots and the roots of her teeth, and every entry she had ever written lifted off its line and hung in the air around her, glowing, legible, true.

She saw Lowmark burning. She saw herself writing it down. She saw the space between those two facts, the eleven-year gap where a question should have been, and the question arrived now, all at once, wearing ninety faces.

The song did not accuse her. That was the unbearable part. It simply *showed* her, the way a ledger shows, without mercy and without malice, and asked nothing except that she read it.

In the center of the ruin, a man stood dissolving into light — willingly, arms open, pouring himself out like wine — and beside the basin a small boy sang the last verse with tears streaming down his face, and the two melodies braided together, the given and the carried, and rose through the broken roof into the sky.

Far to the south, Veyra knew without knowing how, a blood-oath older than the usurper's grandmother was coming apart thread by thread. Armies would feel it. Charters would go blank. Crowns would sit suddenly crooked on heads that had forgotten why they deserved them.

Veyra opened her ledger.

Her pen hovered over the final page. *Subject: Marguerite Dravenmoor. Disposition:* —

The blank waited. It had always waited.

Behind her, her soldiers were lowering their weapons, one by one, like men setting down burdens they had forgotten they carried. Somewhere above, the singing swelled toward its final note, and Sister Veyra — chief inquisitor of the usurper's throne, keeper of forty-one pages of ash — wrote, in a hand that trembled for the first time in eleven years:

*Forgiven. By whom, I do not yet know.*

And the mountain answered her.
