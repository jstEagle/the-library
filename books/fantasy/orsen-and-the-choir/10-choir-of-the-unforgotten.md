## 10. Choir of the Unforgotten

*Orsen*

The song did not end. Orsen had thought, all his small life, that songs ended — that they were like bread, like warmth, like mothers, things consumed and gone. But the Choir did not end. It only waited for enough voices to hold it whole.

He stood in the ruined sanctuary with his hands pressed flat against the cold stone of the resonance-vault, and the charter — the usurper's blood-oath, her claim stitched in ink and iron and old, old murder — hummed beneath his palms like a trapped wasp. The Choir poured through him now, through the hollowed place where Marguerite's memories had been, and Orsen understood at last why the song had chosen a thief.

Because a thief knows what things are worth. And because a thief, when he gives, gives everything.

The first note left him and went out over the mountains like light leaving a lamp.

Orsen felt it travel. He felt it slide down the passes where the usurper's armies crawled like black ants on white snow, felt it find the soldiers' ears and then, gently, gently, their blood. The blood-oath was a chain every one of them wore without knowing — sworn in their grandmothers' grandmothers' names, bound to a bloodline that had bought its crown with a silenced choir. The song did not break the chain. It *unmade* it, the way morning unmakes a dream: no violence, no shattering, just the slow sweet realization that the thing holding you was never real.

Down in the valley, ten thousand soldiers stopped marching.

Orsen heard them stop. The Choir let him hear everything now — every ear in the Thornfell, every heart that had ever hummed a lullaby older than kingdoms. He heard a sergeant named Brannoc drop his spear and sit down in the snow and weep without knowing why, except that suddenly he could remember his own name without anyone else's oath wrapped around it. He heard officers shouting orders that dissolved into the air like breath in cold. He heard the banners go slack.

And he heard Sister Veyra.

She stood at the sanctuary's broken gate with her ledger open in her hands, snow gathering on her gray shoulders, and the song reached her last of all — or perhaps she simply fought it longest. Orsen felt her resistance the way you feel a stone in your shoe. She had built herself out of entries, out of names recorded and names crossed out, out of the terrible tidy arithmetic of a regime that believed a person was a line in a book. The song offered her back every name she had ever crossed out.

She stood very still for a long time.

Then Marguerite — hollow-eyed, empty Marguerite, who had given away every stolen year of himself so the verse would have room to live — turned toward her, and Orsen saw the inquisitor see him: a man with nothing behind his face, nothing but the quiet after a storm.

Sister Veyra closed her ledger.

She did not burn it. She did not weep, or kneel, or cry out. She simply closed it, the way you close a door on a room you will not enter again, and set it down in the snow, and walked away down the mountain alone, her footsteps filling behind her with white. Later, Orsen knew, she would spend the rest of her life walking from village to village reading aloud the names she had once recorded as condemned — an act of penance no song had demanded of her, only suggested. But that was later. That was another chapter of the world.

In the vault, the charter crumbled. Not burned — *remembered*. It came apart into the dust of its own making, into ink and ash and the faint smell of the orchard where the first usurper had been born, and the blood-oath went with it, and above the free cities the war-flags of Cael Harrow rose unburned against a clean sky.

The Choir settled around Orsen like a coat.

It was warm. It was enormous. It held the voices of everyone who had ever carried it — the keepers massacred in the high valleys, the singers drowned in the old purges, his mother, who had put the last verse into him with her final breath and told him to run south and run *singing*. They were all here. He was not alone anymore, and he never would be again.

But there was a hole in the middle of it, shaped like a man who smelled of pipe-smoke and cheap wine and careful lies, a hole shaped like calloused hands and a voice that pretended to be hard and never quite managed it when it said his name.

*He gave freely,* the Choir said, in ten thousand voices and none. *What is given freely may be returned.*

*Not all of it,* Orsen said. He was crying, and the Choir held that too. *You took all of it. He doesn't know me. He looked at me this morning like I was a stranger at his door.*

*All of it cannot come back,* the Choir said, gentle as snowfall. *What was spent to finish the song is spent. But one memory — one — we can give. Choose.*

Orsen closed his eyes.

There were so many. The wayhouse where the keeper named him, flour on her hands. The night on the smuggler's road when Marguerite had covered him with the only blanket and shivered himself. The moment in the vault when Marguerite had stepped between him and Veyra's blade without hesitating, this man who had sold memories for coin his whole life, stepping in front of death for a boy he'd known three months.

But Orsen chose the first one. Of course he chose the first one.

*Give him the door,* he said. *Give him the beginning.*

*Marguerite*

He woke to birdsong and the smell of woodsmoke, and for a long, drifting moment he did not know where he was, which was not unusual — Marguerite Dravenmoor had woken in ditches and wayhouses and one memorable hayloft in Vell — except that usually the not-knowing lasted a heartbeat before the hoard stirred and told him who he was.

The hoard did not stir.

He lay still and listened inward, the way he'd listened inward for thirty years, and found — nothing. No merchant's grief bought in Cael Harrow. No soldier's courage, no widow's wedding day, no countess's first betrayal. No memories of deals struck along the Thornfell roads, no faces of fences and buyers and the woman in Vell whose name he almost remembered. Even his own childhood was a country with the maps burned out of it. He knew *facts*, the way you know facts about a stranger: that he was a memory-thief, that he had lived alone, that he had never kept anything he couldn't sell.

That last one sat strangely in his chest, like a coat that no longer fit.

He sat up on a pallet in a ruined sanctuary that someone had half-swept, and a boy was watching him from across the cold hearth. Dark-haired, thin-faced, somewhere between eight and twelve years old, with the kind of stillness that Marguerite's professional eye recognized as either training or survival. The boy was looking at him with an expression Marguerite had no name for — hope and grief holding each other up, two drunks leaning on the same wall.

"You're awake," the boy said.

"Apparently." His voice sounded wrong to him. Rusty. "Who are you?"

Something flickered across the boy's face — pain, quickly mastered, mastered so well that Marguerite might have missed it if he hadn't spent a lifetime reading faces for a living. "My name is Orsen."

"Orsen." He tried the name. Nothing. No buyer's ledger in his head, no debt, no job. Just a boy in a ruin. "And I am—"

"Marguerite." The boy said it carefully, like setting down something fragile. "Marguerite Dravenmoor. You're a thief. You steal memories. You sold them along the Thornfell roads for eleven years, and you never kept anything you couldn't sell." A pause. "Until now."

"Until now," Marguerite repeated slowly. "I don't remember any of it. Any of me."

"I know."

"What happened?"

The boy came and sat at the foot of the pallet, cross-legged, hands folded, and told him. He told it plainly, without ornament, which Marguerite somehow understood was the greatest kindness available: the song, the Choir, the usurper's armies stopped in the snow below, the charter turned to dust, the inquisitor who had laid down her ledger and walked away. And then the cost. Every memory Marguerite had ever owned — stolen, bought, or born with — poured into the last verse like wine into a cup, spent so completely that even the spending was gone from him now.

"The war is over," Orsen finished. "The free cities are free. You did that."

"*I* did that?" Marguerite laughed, and it came out cracked. "I don't even know what I had for breakfast."

"Bread. You complained about it. I told you it was the same bread as yesterday and you said that was an argument against tomorrow existing." The boy's mouth twitched. "You say things like that. You'll say things like that again. You just won't remember saying them."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. "Why are you telling me all this? Why not let me wake up blank and make my own mistakes about you?"

"Because you'd want to know." Orsen's voice caught, steadied. "You always wanted to know. It's how you survived. Knowing what things were worth."

"And what am I worth now?" The question came out before he could stop it, bitter and honest, a stranger's bitterness in his own mouth. "A thief with an empty vault. There's nothing left in me to sell, boy. Whoever I was, he's gone."

"He's not gone." Orsen leaned forward, fierce suddenly, and his eyes were wet and ancient and eight years old all at once. "He's *here*. He's just — quiet. And I'm going to tell you who he was until you believe me. However long it takes. Because—" The boy stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "Because he took me in off his doorstep when he didn't have to. Because he covered me with his blanket. Because he gave up everything he had, twice — once for the world, and once for me. And I'm not going to forget him. One of us should get to remember."

Marguerite opened his mouth to say something sensible and distant, something a stranger would say —

And the Choir, merciful, kept its promise.

It came like warmth returning to a cold room: a doorstep, dusk, rain coming down the mountain in gray sheets. His own door, worn at the latch. And on the step, small and soaked and starving, a boy with dark hair plastered to his forehead, humming — humming a melody that made the stolen years inside Marguerite stir and turn over like sleeping animals catching a scent. He remembered the wariness. He remembered the calculation, automatic, shameful: *what could this be worth?* And underneath it, quieter, older, something he had buried so deep even theft had never reached it: *he looks hungry. When did I stop noticing that people look hungry?*

He remembered opening the door wider.

The memory settled into him and stayed, one single coal in a house of ashes, and Marguerite Dravenmoor sat on a pallet in a ruined sanctuary with tears running down a face he was relearning, looking at a boy he had never met and had always known.

"I remember you," he said. His voice broke on the second word. "Rain. My door. You were humming something I'd never heard, and I thought — I thought, *that melody is worth more than anything I've ever stolen.*"

Orsen made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and crossed the space between them, and Marguerite held him — awkwardly at first, a stranger's arms learning a familiar shape, and then not awkwardly at all.

Outside, the snow was stopping. Somewhere below, the free cities were waking to a morning with no oath in their blood, and messengers were already on the roads, and there would be questions, and councils, and a world to rebuild that had forgotten how to stand without a tyrant's weight holding it crookedly upright. There would be work. There would be danger; there always was. Somewhere in the high valleys, the Choir sang on in ten thousand throats, and it had not finished with any of them.

But that was tomorrow's weather.

Tonight there was a fire to build, and bread to complain about, and a boy asleep against his shoulder breathing the slow, trusting breath of the finally safe, and one memory burning bright in an empty house — a door opening, rain on the stones, a melody worth more than gold.

Marguerite Dravenmoor, who had never kept anything he couldn't sell, held on to it, and did not let go.
