## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There are three rules to surviving in Vellharrow with a forbidden gift stitched into your bones, and I had learned all of them before I was old enough to be hanged for breaking them.

One: never let them see you listening.

Two: never let them see what you heard move your face.

Three: never, under any circumstances, answer back where anyone can watch your lips.

I broke rule three exactly once a night, in the shadow between Maren's pie stall and the tanner's reeking fence, and I told myself that once didn't count. That's the arithmetic of survival, friend—you shave a hair off every law until the law is thin enough to slip through, and then you call it living.

The night market on Candlemas Row smelled of tallow smoke and fried onions and the particular desperation of people who would pay two coppers for a charm against bad luck because good luck had gotten too expensive. I had my tray out on its strap across my chest, my little wares laid in rows: knotted red thread against cramps, river-stones drilled and strung against nightmares, dried foxglove in wax paper "for a peaceful house," which is a polite way of saying *keep your husband quiet*. Nothing in my inventory worked. That was the beauty of it. A charm that did nothing could never be proven magic, and unproven magic couldn't get you a cell beneath the Warden's Hall with a drain in the floor.

I'd been at this three years. Long enough that the regulars called me Tam and didn't look too closely. Long enough that I'd almost stopped flinching when a warden's boots rang on the cobbles behind me.

Almost.

"Charms, love?" I said to a woman with flour on her sleeves. "Bundles of protection, gentle as milk."

She bought a knot of red thread for her daughter's wrist and haggled me down from two coppers to one and a half, which meant she'd haggled me down nothing at all, since I'd priced it at one. This is also survival: let them win. Winning customers feel clever, and clever customers come back.

It was near midnight when the horse started screaming.

Not whinnying—*screaming*, the high torn sound of an animal that has looked into something wrong. It was a big bay gelding hitched to a coal cart by the fountain, and it had gone mad against its harness, eyes rolled white, hooves striking sparks off the stones. Its driver, a barrel of a man named Hobb, was swearing and hauling on the bit, and a ring of gawkers was forming the way rings do, hungry for spectacle and useless as furniture.

"Whoa! Whoa, you devil—"

"He'll break his legs," someone said helpfully.

"He'll break *mine*," said Hobb.

And the horse said, in the way horses say things—not words, but meanings that arrive whole in your skull like cold water poured down your neck—

*Dark. Dark coming. The sky is going dark. They're going OUT.*

My hands went still on my tray.

Here is what nobody tells you about hearing beasts: it isn't a voice. It's weather. It moves through you and rearranges everything, and afterward you have to decide whether to pretend it rained or admit you were standing in a storm. Three years of practice said *pretend*. Three years of practice said walk away, sell the man a calming knot for his horse's bridle, take his copper, go home.

But the horse's terror was pouring off him in sheets, and underneath it, worse, was something I'd never felt from an animal before. Not fear of a whip or a dog or fire.

Fear of the *sky*.

*Going out,* he said again, to anyone, to no one, to me. *All the little fires. Going out.*

"Hobb." My mouth moved before my sense caught up. "Let me try."

He glanced at me—the charm-seller, the skinny one with the ink-stained fingers—and desperation overrode his pride. "If you can shut him up, first pick of my load next market day."

I set down the tray. I walked to the horse the way you approach anything frightened and enormous: slow, sideways, hands open, heart hammering somewhere around my tonsils. Up close his sweat smelled sharp as vinegar, and his whole body trembled like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

*Easy,* I pushed at him, careful, wordless-adjacent, the way you'd murmur without moving your lips. *Easy. Tell me what you see.*

*I don't SEE,* the horse said, and there was such raw bewilderment in it that my chest hurt. *That's the thing. There's nothing to see yet. But I can FEEL it. Like before a quake. Like the ground forgetting how to be ground. The little fires are going to go out, soft one. All of them. And when they do—*

He shuddered so hard his harness rattled.

*—when they do, the dark won't be empty anymore.*

I put my hand flat on his neck. His heartbeat slammed against my palm, fast as a sparrow's. Around us the crowd murmured; Hobb was saying something about a natural gift; a woman asked if I sold charms for mad horses, and I heard myself say, "Two coppers," in a voice that came from very far away.

Because I was doing the other thing now. The forbidden arithmetic. Adding up what the horse knew against what I knew, and finding the sum unbearable.

Beasts know things. That's the dirty secret under all the crown's clean laws about magic. The Sundering burned the mages and outlawed the old tongues, but nobody outlawed a horse's skin, and a horse's skin remembers being prey to things older than kingdoms. If a coal-cart gelding in Vellharrow could feel the stars going out—

Then it wasn't superstition. Then it was coming.

I soothed him as best I could—I fed him calm the way you'd feed a frightened child warm milk, image by image, the stable, the oats, the smell of straw—and slowly, slowly, the screaming wound down to trembling, and the trembling down to standing. The crowd lost interest, disappointed. Crowds always do, when the disaster agrees to wait.

Hobb pressed a copper into my hand and called me a wonder. I smiled the smile I keep for that word. Wonder. Gift. Blessing. In Vellharrow those words are just the rope with the courtesy of a handle.

I packed my tray. I should have gone home. I want that written plainly, wherever such things get written: I should have gone home, bolted the door, pulled the blanket over my head, and in the morning sold twice as many charms to people who'd need comfort after a strange night.

Instead I stood by the fountain and looked up.

Vellharrow keeps few stars even on clear nights—the city smokes too much—but there are always some. The Shepherd and his three dogs low in the south. The pale scatter over the harbor like salt spilled on black cloth. I used to find them with my mother, before. She'd point and name them in the old tongue, softly, softly, and tell me the dragons had hung them as lanterns for the covenant, so neither side would ever lose the road home.

They took her for less than that. For a bracelet of charms that actually worked.

Midnight came across the city in bells—Saint Aldric's first, then the harbor chapel, then the great cracked bell of the Warden's Hall itself, tolling the hour in a voice like a stone dropped down a well.

On the last stroke, the sky went out.

Not clouded. Not veiled. *Out.* Every star, gone at once, snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers, and the darkness above Vellharrow wasn't night-dark, wasn't storm-dark—it was a darkness with weight, a lid, pressing down. The smoke-haze still glowed orange over the foundries. The moon, mercifully, was a thin rind and hung stubborn and small. But the stars were simply not there, and the absence had a shape, and the shape was watching.

Around the fountain, people laughed nervously. Someone said *clouds*. Someone said *eclipse*. Someone said the Warden's Hall would post an explanation by morning, and everyone nodded, because explanations by morning are the city's true religion.

Then the wards shattered.

You couldn't see the border wards—they lived fifty miles out, a ring of woven light older than the kingdom, the thing every schoolchild learned kept the wild dark beyond the maps from leaking in. But you could hear them, if you were cursed the way I'm cursed. Every beast in Vellharrow heard them. Every beast began to scream.

Dogs first—a rising chorus from every yard and alley, howling at nothing. Then the cattle in the stockyards lowing in one long terrified note. The gulls off the harbor rose in a single white sheet, wheeling and shrieking against the starless black. And underneath it all, faint as a finger drawn on glass, I heard—or felt—the wards themselves break. A sound like the world's largest pane of ice giving way. A thousand years of weaving, snapping thread by thread by thread.

The horse at the coal cart screamed again, and this time Hobb couldn't hold him. Harness leather snapped like celery. The gelding tore free and thundered up Candlemas Row, scattering the crowd, and I understood every word of his panic:

*It's through. It's through, it's THROUGH, the dark isn't empty—*

People were shouting. Wardens' whistles shrilled from three streets away. Somewhere a bell began ringing alarm instead of hours, ragged and urgent, and was joined by another, and another.

I stood very still by the fountain with my tray of harmless charms strapped to my chest, and I did the only honest thing I've done in three years.

I was afraid.

Not of the dark, though the dark deserved it. Not of whatever had eaten the stars, though I could feel it now, a pressure at the edge of my senses like a vast attention turning toward the world. I was afraid of the smaller, nearer thing: that by morning, the Wardens would be hunting answers, and answers in Vellharrow have always been found in the same place—in the low quarter, among charmsellers and hedge-healers and anyone whose grandmother spoke with an accent. Among people like my mother. People like me.

Three years of invisibility. Three years of knots and river-stones and foxglove and the careful, practiced nothing of my little tray. One breath—one bell-stroke—and the life I'd built cracked straight down the middle like the wards themselves.

A warden's whistle sounded close now, at the head of the street. Boots on cobbles. Lantern light swinging.

I pulled my hood up, turned from the fountain, and walked home the way mice walk: fast, quiet, along the walls.

Behind me, above the smoking rooftops, the starless sky pressed down like a held breath.

And far off, past the harbor, past the hills, something answered the screaming dogs—not a howl, not a cry, but a long, hollow note that no throat I knew could make, and every hair on my arms stood up, because some animal part of me, older than any law, whispered what it was.

The dark wasn't empty anymore.

And it had begun to sing.
