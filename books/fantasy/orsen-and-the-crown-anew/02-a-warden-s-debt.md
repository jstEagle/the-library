## 2. A Warden's Debt

The wards fell at midnight, and by the third bell of morning I was standing in a field of broken glass that had never been glass at all.

Ward-stones don't shatter. That was the first wrong thing. I'd served the crown for eleven years, six of them as a full warden of the Second Circle, and in all that time I had seen ward-stones cracked, scorched, pried from their settings by desperate smugglers, and once—memorably—swallowed whole by an ox with a taste for enchanted minerals. But I had never seen one *shatter*. Not the stone itself, but something inside it, some lattice of power that lived in the rock like a soul lives in a body, gone all at once, leaving the shell behind like a shed skin.

There were forty-one of them along the Vellharrow border, set into granite posts older than the city charter, and every single one lay open and empty as a birdcage after the cat's been through.

I crouched beside the nearest post and did what I always do first, which is nothing. Observation before conclusion. My old instructor beat that into me along with everything else, back when I still believed instruction was a kindness. The post stood at the edge of the Ashen Fields, where the farmland gave up and the border-march began, and the morning fog lay over it all like a sheet drawn up to the chin of a corpse.

The residue came second. After the nothing.

It glittered on my fingertips when I touched the shattered stone—a fine dust, iridescent, shifting color when the light caught it wrong. Green to bronze to a deep, burning red, like embers seen through smoked glass. I knew what it was before I let myself know what it was. Every warden does. We're taught the name of it in our first year so we can be taught to fear it in our second.

Dragon-scale.

Not actual scale—the dragons were ash and legend long before my grandfather drew breath—but the residue their fire left on the world, the mineral ghost of a covenant that ended a century ago and took the stars' good behavior with it. Dragon-scale residue meant covenant-work. Covenant-work meant magic of the oldest kind, the kind the Sundering was supposed to have burned out of the world forever.

The kind I used to execute people for.

I wiped my fingers on my cloak and stood, and told myself the cold in my stomach was just the fog.

* * *

Captain Verrek arrived an hour later with twelve men, a cart of replacement stones, and the particular energy of a man who has decided the problem is smaller than it looks and intends to keep deciding that no matter what the evidence says.

"Hailstorm," he said, before I could greet him. "Ice expansion in the lattice. Freak weather event. We'll reset the stones by week's end."

"Captain." I held out my hand, the residue still dusted across my fingertips. "This isn't ice damage."

He looked at my hand the way you look at a beggar who's grabbed your sleeve. "It's frost-shatter. It happens."

"It doesn't happen. And even if it did, frost doesn't leave scale-residue." I turned my palm toward him. In the grey light, the dust shifted green to red, patient as a heartbeat. "This is covenant-work, Captain. Old work. Which means either someone out there is working magic the law says died a hundred years ago, or the law has been lying to us about something for a very long time."

Verrek's jaw worked. He was not a stupid man, which made him more dangerous than a stupid one, because stupid men can be dismissed and careful ones cannot. "Or," he said slowly, "someone wants us to *think* it's covenant-work. Residue can be faked. Ground scale-glass, powdered bone—there are apothecaries in the low quarter who'd sell you a pouch of it for two silvers and no questions."

That was true. That was exactly the kind of comfortable, plausible lie a man builds his career on, and I recognized it intimately, because I had built several of my own out of the same material.

"Run your analysis if you want," Verrek said, already turning away, signaling his men toward the cart. "File your report. But hear me, Hale: the report you file will say hailstorm. Because if it says anything else—if it says the word you're thinking of saying—then every mage-hunter in the realm gets mobilized, the temples start burning people again, and I will not preside over that. Not over rumors. The stars went out. People are frightened. Frightened people need order, not archaeology."

"The stars going out *is* the archaeology," I said. "Something ate them, Captain. Something that leaves dragon-scale on the border stones. Those two facts want to be in the same report."

For a moment—one long, dangerous moment—I thought he might actually listen. His eyes went distant, the way men's do when they're doing arithmetic they don't like. Then the distance closed, the shutters came down, and he was a captain again.

"Reset the stones," he said. "Report says hailstorm. That's an order, Warden Hale."

I saluted. It's remarkable how well the arm remembers the motions even when the rest of you has stopped believing in them.

* * *

They found the rumor within two days, which surprised no one who understood how fear travels through a city. Fear moves faster than cavalry and never needs provisions.

A beast-speaker, they said. In the low quarter. Someone who could put words in animals' mouths—or take words out of them, depending on who was telling it. A horse that wept. Dogs that spoke of the sky going dark. A charm-seller by the night market whose trinkets worked too well, whose animals fell silent and calm around them as though listening to something no one else could hear.

By the fourth day, the rumor had a shape and a price. By the fifth, it had my name attached to the hunt, because Verrek, having ordered the truth buried, apparently decided the next best thing was to hand me a shovel.

"You wanted a real report," he said, dropping a folded paper on my desk in the garrison house. "Here's your real report. Crown intelligence confirms a beast-speaker operating in Vellharrow's low quarter. Unregistered gift. Pre-Sundering classification. You know what that means."

I did. Pre-Sundering classification meant the old statutes applied, and the old statutes had exactly one remedy, administered at dawn, in front of the temple, with the family billed for the rope.

"I want you on it," Verrek said. "You've got the nose for it. You found the residue; you'll find the speaker. Quietly. No mobilization, no temple involvement—not yet. Bring them in for questioning and we'll see what they know about the wards."

"And if they know nothing?"

"Then they know nothing and we've frightened a charm-peddler." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't match the tone, and I understood then that he knew exactly what he was asking. He'd been at the executions too. Everyone senior had been. We were all carrying the same weight; we'd simply agreed, collectively and without ever speaking of it, to pretend the weight was feathers.

Three years ago, I had stood at the temple steps on a grey morning and read the names off a list. Seven mages. Unregistered gifts, pre-Sundering classifications, the full machinery of the law grinding forward exactly as designed. I had checked each name against the registry myself. I had confirmed each sentence myself. I had done it *correctly*, which is the word I hid behind, then and now. Correctly. As if correctness were a moral quality. As if the law being followed precisely were the same as the law being right.

One of the seven was a woman named Maren Ravensworth. I remember her because she didn't struggle. They almost always struggled, or wept, or cursed, or prayed, and I had learned to hold all of it at arm's length the way you hold a hot pan. But Maren Ravensworth had looked at me—just looked, calm as deep water—and said, "You'll wish you'd listened to the beasts instead, Warden."

I hadn't known what she meant. I still didn't. But I had thought about it more times than I could count, usually at night, usually after the third drink, and every time I thought about it, the sentence grew a little heavier, like a debt accruing interest in a currency I couldn't identify.

Now the crown was sending me to hunt another beast-speaker. Possibly the last one. Certainly the only one rumored since the Sundering's ashes cooled.

And some part of me—the part that had filed the hailstorm report with a steady hand and hated itself for the steadiness—understood that this time, the debt had come due.

* * *

I went into the low quarter alone, at dusk, out of uniform, wearing a fishmonger's coat that smelled of brine and old money lost. Verrek had offered me six men. I'd refused, telling him a crowd would spook the quarry. That much was even true. But the larger truth was that I didn't want witnesses for whatever I was about to do, because I genuinely did not know what I was about to do, and a man should have the privacy to disappoint himself without an audience.

The low quarter at dusk was all smoke and lamplight and commerce conducted in murmurs. Charm-sellers lined the night market's edge, hawking luck-knots and fever-wards and love charms that worked exactly as well as belief allowed. I moved through them slowly, buying things I didn't want, asking questions shaped like small talk, watching hands and eyes.

It didn't take long. It never takes long, once you know what you're looking for. Not the seller with the loudest stall—that one was a fraud with a gift for patter. Not the herb-wife with the raven—ravens talk anyway; it proves nothing. What I looked for was silence. Animals go quiet around a real speaker, the way crowds go quiet around a king. Not frightened-quiet. *Attentive*-quiet.

I found it at a narrow stall near the fountain, where a figure in a hooded coat was fitting a charm-bracelet onto the wrist of a nervous apprentice girl while a carthorse tethered nearby stood with its head lowered, ears forward, utterly still, listening to something.

The figure's hands were quick and gentle. Their voice, when they spoke to the girl, was low and warm and pitched under the market noise. And when they finished and the girl hurried away, the charmed bracelet glinting, the figure scratched the carthorse's nose and murmured something too soft for me to catch—and the horse exhaled, slow and deep, like a man setting down a weight carried too many miles.

I had come to arrest them. That was the mission. That was the order. That was the correct procedure, and I had followed correct procedures my entire adult life, and three years ago, following them correctly, I had put a rope around the neck of a woman who told me I'd wish I'd listened to the beasts.

The beast-speaker turned, as if feeling my gaze across the market noise, and pulled back their hood to see who watched them.

Underneath was a face I didn't know—and around their wrist, half-hidden under a charm-bracelet of their own, a small silver sigil on a leather cord. A circle crossed by a single flame.

I knew that sigil. I had catalogued it myself, three years ago, among the effects of a woman named Maren Ravensworth, executed at dawn for the crime of talking to horses.

The market noise fell away. The lamplight narrowed to a tunnel. And Orsen Hale, Royal Warden of the Second Circle, who had never once failed to follow an order, stood in a fishmonger's coat in the low quarter and felt the debt come due all at once, with interest, in a currency he finally recognized.

It was guilt. It had been guilt all along.

The beast-speaker met my eyes, and their own widened—because they'd seen the warden in me even through the fishmonger's coat, the way animals see the wolf under any skin—and they took one step backward, toward the dark mouth of the alley behind their stall.

"Don't," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "Please. I need to ask you about your mother."
