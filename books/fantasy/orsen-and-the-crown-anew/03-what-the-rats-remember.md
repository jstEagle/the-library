## 3. What the Rats Remember

The grate above me slammed shut with a sound like a verdict.

I crouched in the dark of Vellharrow's underbelly—knee-deep in water I was choosing not to think about too hard—and listened to boots thunder across the street overhead. Six pairs, maybe seven. They'd seen me bolt from the charm-stall the moment the warden's men started asking questions about a "beast-speaker in the low quarter," and now they were fanning out through the Warrens like spilled ink.

"Charming," I muttered to myself. "You spend nine years being nobody, and it takes one bad night and one dead horse's opinion to undo it."

Well. Not one horse. A dozen animals over the years, all whispering the same wrongness since midnight: *the sky is dark, the sky is dark, the sky is dark.* The cart-horse had simply been the loudest, and I'd been stupid enough to soothe him aloud, in front of witnesses, with my hand flat on his neck and my voice doing that thing it does—the thing that makes beasts go quiet and makes people who hear it go very still, and then very interested.

Somewhere above, a soldier shouted an order. The word "sewers" drifted down through the grate like a bad omen wearing a uniform.

They were coming *down*.

"All right," I told the darkness. "Options."

The darkness did not answer, but something else did—a rustle along the wall, a skittering chorus of small clawed feet. My eyes had adjusted enough to make out shapes: the tunnel stretching two ways, one sloping down toward the river outfalls, one climbing back toward the tannery district. And everywhere, at the edges of everything, rats. Dozens of them. Watching me with the patient black-eyed attention of creatures who have heard every secret this city ever tried to drown.

I knelt, which put my knees in the water, which was a sacrifice I made without complaint because my life was possibly on the line and dignity is a luxury for people who aren't fugitives.

"Hello," I said, in the way that isn't really words—not to them. In the way that's pressure and warmth and intent, the language underneath language. "*Friends. Neighbors. Landlords, if we're being honest about who actually owns these tunnels.*"

A big male detached himself from the pack and flowed toward me over the wet stone. He was old—scarred ear, grey threading his fur, the swagger of a rat who had survived things younger rats only dreamed about. He stopped an arm's length away and regarded me with what I can only describe as professional interest.

"*You're the one who talks,*" he said. Rat-speech tastes like smells and hunger and memory, but I'll translate as best I can. "*We've smelled you for years. Bread and fear and something old underneath.*"

"Bread and fear," I repeated aloud, wincing. "That's going on no tombstone anywhere."

"*Why do you come?*"

"Soldiers are hunting me. Men with torches and crossbows and very poor senses of humor. I need a way out of the tunnels that doesn't end in a net."

The old rat sat back on his haunches. Around him, the pack murmured—that chittering ripple that means conversation, or gossip, or possibly just the rat equivalent of a crowd going *ooooh*. I got the distinct impression I was being appraised not as a person but as a resource.

"*Help has a price,*" he said.

Of course it did. Everything in Vellharrow had a price; I'd built my whole living on that principle. "Name it."

"*Food,*" he said promptly. "*And the fat one's grain sacks by the tannery—leave the lids loose. And you will owe the Underkin a debt, to be called when we call it.*"

"That's ominous," I said. "But fine. Loose lids, food, one unspecified terrifying favor. Do we shake on it or—"

He bit my finger. Lightly. Rat contracts are apparently binding via minor bloodletting. I decided not to litigate.

Then he turned and the pack moved, flowing down the left-hand tunnel like a river deciding which way to run, and the old rat looked back at me with unmistakable *well?* energy.

I followed.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing for your life: it's mostly walking. Fast, terrified, ankle-deep-in-unspeakable walking, but walking nonetheless. The rats kept ahead of me in a loose dark ribbon, and whenever they split around some obstacle, I learned to read it as signage. Twice they froze, and I froze, and torchlight swept across a junction behind us while I pressed myself into an alcove that smelled of a century of regret and tallow.

The second time, I heard voices.

"—warden says sweep every tunnel below Gilders' Row. Says the speaker's gift is real, confirmed by three witnesses." A pause. "Three witnesses saw a charm-peddler pet a horse and the horse stopped screaming. That's confirmation?"

"The sky went out, Corvel. Half the city's confirming miracles this morning. Warden Hale wants the speaker found before someone else finds them first."

Warden Hale. I filed the name away in the drawer where I keep things that might kill me later.

When the boots faded, the old rat nudged my boot and we moved again. But something had shifted in me during those long minutes of listening to my own heart hammer. Fear was still there—it hadn't gone anywhere, fear rarely does—but underneath it, something harder had settled in, like sediment after a flood.

I was tired. Not sleepy-tired. Bone-tired, soul-tired, the tiredness of nine years spent shrinking. Of biting my tongue when merchants called beast-speakers abominations. Of watching my mother's sigil-charm gather dust in a hidden pocket because wearing it openly felt like painting a target on my chest. I had made invisibility my whole architecture, and last night the stars had gone out anyway, and the crown had come for me *regardless*, and I realized—with a clarity that felt almost like relief—that hiding had never once saved anyone. It had only delayed the bill.

My mother hadn't hidden. She had spoken, and healed, and been dragged to the pyre for it while I watched from a crowd and did nothing. Maybe courage wasn't the absence of the fear currently making my hands shake. Maybe courage was just deciding whose side of the story you wanted to be standing on at the end.

"Where are you taking me?" I asked the old rat, somewhere past the third turning.

His answer came back layered, and it stopped me cold.

"*Not taking. Showing. You asked for escape, little speaker, but you should see what you're escaping through. You should know what holds up your city.*"

"What holds up—" I splashed to a halt. "What does that mean?"

"*Come and smell,*" he said, which is rat for *come and see*, and also, I suspect, rat for *you're going to lose your mind a little.*

* * *

The tunnel opened into a chamber that must once have been a cistern, vaulted ceiling lost in black above, the water here still and glassy. And the walls—

The walls were bone.

I don't mean decorated with bone. I mean the foundation itself, the great curved ribs of the vault, the pilasters sunk into the stone, the entire skeleton of old Vellharrow's deep places—they were *bone*. Enormous, ancient, mineral-grey with age, but unmistakably bone, ribbed and jointed on a scale that made my brain stutter and refuse. A rib the width of a rowboat arced overhead. Vertebrae the size of wine barrels were mortared into the stonework like keystone after keystone.

"Oh," I said faintly. "Oh, that's—those are—"

"*Dragon,*" said the old rat, with the satisfaction of a tutor whose slowest student has finally read a word correctly. "*Old ones. Dead before your grandsires' grandsires. The soft-builders found the bones and built on them, and the bones remembered fire, and the remembering became your wards.*"

I stood in the freezing water with my mouth open like a landed fish, and the truth assembled itself piece by piece in my head, each piece worse than the last.

Dragon-bones threaded through the foundations of Vellharrow. Dragon-bones under every quarter, every district—I'd bet the whole city sat atop a graveyard. And the border wards, the wards that had shattered like glass at midnight? Ward-magic didn't come from human craft at all. It couldn't have. Humans had outlawed magic so thoroughly they executed mothers in public squares. No—the ward-magic was borrowed. Leached. Drunk from the bones of dead dragons for generations by a crown that pretended magic didn't exist while guzzling it like cheap wine.

And when the dragons—all dragons, even the living ones, if any lived—when their fire finally guttered, the wards didn't fail because of an enemy. They failed because the account was empty. Because the crown had been spending coin it never minted.

"The Hollow King," I whispered. The beasts on the road rumors had been muttering about it since dawn—*the one who ate the stars*. Maybe nothing ate the stars. Maybe the stars went out because the covenant that tied sky to earth had rotted through, and everyone who could have renewed it had been burned.

"You knew," I said to the rat. "Your kind knew. All this time."

"*We remember what two-legs bury,*" he said simply. "*Bones hold memories. We live in the bones. Now you have lived in them too.*"

I looked up at the vast dead rib overhead and felt something turn over in my chest—not dread this time. Something closer to purpose, arriving unwelcome and undeniable, the way truth always does.

Because here was the thing, the horrible, obvious, inescapable thing: I could speak to beasts. Beasts remembered what humans buried. If dragon-bones held memory, and if anything living still spoke the old tongues of flame and covenant—

Then I wasn't just a fugitive. I was a map. Possibly the only map left.

"That debt," I said slowly. "The Underkin's favor. Can I call part of it early?"

The old rat's whiskers twitched. "*Ask.*"

"I need to reach the river gate before the soldiers seal it. And then I need to get out of Vellharrow entirely, north, toward Cinderfall." Saying it aloud made it real, and real made my stomach drop, and I said it anyway. "There's a fire dying on that mountain, and I think I'm the only person alive who can hear it."

For a long moment the pack was silent, dozens of black eyes catching the thin grey light from some grating far above. Then the old rat made a sound I'd never heard a rat make—a low, rattling hum, almost reverent.

"*The bones have waited a hundred years for a speaker to walk them,*" he said. "*Go north, little one. Go loudly. And when the fire asks who sent you—*" he bared his teeth, which in rat is either a threat or a grin, and with him I was learning it was both, "*—tell it the small ones remember too.*"

Above us, muffled through stone and earth, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm bell. The *summons* bell—the crown calling every warden in the city to the citadel, which meant they'd stopped searching street by street, which meant something had changed, which meant whatever came next would be worse and faster.

The old rat flicked his scarred ear toward the river tunnel.

"*Run,*" he said.

I ran.
