## 4. An Arrest That Isn't

There is a particular smell to abandoned temples that no amount of incense can disguise: dust and pigeon droppings and the slow rot of forgotten faith. The Temple of the Sundered Flame had been closed for sixty years, ever since the crown decided the gods were bad for business, and it smelled like every year of it.

I stood in the doorway with my sword drawn and my lantern raised and thought, not for the first time, that my life had become a series of increasingly stupid rooms.

Behind me, Captain Vess was organizing a sweep of the low quarter with the methodical patience of a woman who alphabetized her grudges. Ahead of me, somewhere in the dark between fallen pews and shattered votive niches, was the beast-speaker. The one I'd been ordered to bring in alive, if convenient, and in pieces if not. Vess's words, not mine. She'd smiled when she said them. I'd made a note to never play cards with her.

The trail had been easy to follow, which should have worried me more than it did. Rats don't scatter for just anyone; they scatter for the thing they fear most or the thing they serve. These rats had scattered *respectfully*, streaming out of the temple drains like a congregation dismissed early. Whatever was in here had friends in low places.

"Beast-speaker," I called into the dark. My voice echoed off stone carved with dragons—old carvings, pre-Sundering, defaced by some zealous chisel but stubbornly recognizable under the damage. Wings. Coils. Open mouths. "I know you're here. The whole city knows you're here. You left a horse singing in the night market and a dog telling fortunes outside the tannery. You're about as subtle as a house fire."

Silence. Then, from somewhere near the ruined altar, a voice—light, dry, younger than I expected:

"You're the one who followed me through three districts and stepped in the same puddle twice."

I looked down at my boot. It was, in fact, soaked. "That was tactical."

"It was squelching."

I moved into the nave, lantern high, sword low. Old habit. You point the blade at what you mean to kill and keep your eyes on everything else. "Come out where I can see you."

A shape unfolded from behind the altar rail—a slight figure in a patched coat, hood back, hands raised in a gesture of surrender so theatrical it bordered on insult. Tamsin Ravensworth. Twenty-some years old, sharp-faced, dark-haired, with the kind of eyes that looked like they'd already catalogued every exit in the room and found two of them insulting.

"The famous Warden Hale," they said. "They describe you to children as a warning."

"They describe me to criminals as a warning too. It works about half the time." I circled left. They shifted right, keeping the altar rail between us. Smart. "You know why I'm here."

"Because the sky went dark and someone has to be blamed, and the crown finds it easier to blame a charm-seller than to admit it broke something it can't fix." They tilted their head. "Did I get that right? I've only been running from you people for two days; I might have missed nuance."

"You didn't miss anything." The words came out flatter than I intended. Because they were true, and because I'd spent three nights reading the ward-collapse reports and finding, in the margins of the official account, the small dishonest silences that meant someone up the chain knew exactly what had broken and why. Dragon-scale residue. Magic that hadn't existed in the world for a hundred years, except—apparently—it had, all along, quietly, underneath the city, and the wards had been drinking from it like a secret tap.

But that wasn't the assignment. The assignment was the beast-speaker.

"Tamsin Ravensworth," I said. "Charm-seller. Unlicensed practitioner. Suspected beast-speaker, first class." I recited it the way I recited everything, flat and official, because the flatness was armor. "By order of the Crown Wardens, you're under arrest. Come quietly and you'll see trial. Run and—" I gestured vaguely at my sword, at the room, at the general state of the world.

They laughed. It was not a nice laugh. It was the laugh of someone who had done the arithmetic of their odds and found the sum funny. "And if I come quietly, what happens? Be honest. You're the honest one, aren't you? That's your reputation. Orsen Hale, who never lies, who executed eleven mages without blinking because the writ said so."

Twelve, I almost said. Twelve. But I didn't say it, and I've wondered since why the number rose in my throat like that, hot and specific, like a splinter working toward the surface.

"Come quietly," I repeated, "and you live."

"That's not living," they said softly. "That's just slower dying with paperwork."

And then they ran—which I had expected—and they ran *well*, which I had not. Over the altar rail, through a side door I hadn't mapped, into the cloister walk with its collapsed roof and its moonless dark. I went after them. Behind me, faintly, I heard Vess's whistle: the signal that meant the outer cordon was closing. Ten minutes, maybe less, before this temple was full of soldiers and the arithmetic got much worse for both of us.

I caught them at the old baptistry, where the font had cracked decades ago and the floor was slick with rainwater that came in through the broken dome. They slipped; I lunged; we both went down hard on wet stone, and I ended up with their wrist pinned and my knee on their chest and my lantern knocked askew, throwing light in wild arcs across the ceiling.

"Stop," I said. "Stop fighting. It's over."

"It's been over since the stars went out," they gasped. "For everyone. You just haven't told your face yet."

Their arm was bare where the coat sleeve had torn—torn in the fall, or earlier, I couldn't tell—and the lantern light swung across it and caught the bracelet.

I have described this moment to no one, because there is no way to describe it that doesn't make me sound either mad or guilty, and I am, at minimum, one of those. But here is what happened: the light caught the bracelet, a cheap thing of knotted cord and wire-wrapped charms, the kind sold in market stalls to people who want magic the way they want luck—cheaply and without commitment. And among the charms hung a small silver sigil on a loop of chain. A flame inside a crescent, split down the middle.

I knew that sigil. I had seen it last on a warrant, and before that on a door lintel in the Weaver's Quarter, and before that on the wrist of a woman standing very straight on a scaffold while I read her crimes aloud in a voice I had practiced until it sounded like nothing at all.

Maren Ravensworth. Hedge-mage. Beast-tongue, unregistered. Sentence carried out by Warden Orsen Hale, per crown writ, third day of Harvestmonth, three years past.

She had begged, at the end. Not for herself—for the child she wouldn't name, the child she'd hidden with neighbors, the child whose existence she took to her grave rather than give the crown another leash. I remember thinking, with the cold professionalism I wore like mail in those days, that she died well. I remember being proud of that thought.

Her child was under my knee, breathing hard, watching my face change.

"You knew her," Tamsin said. Not a question. Their voice had gone very quiet, the way voices do when they're standing at the edge of something deep. "Your face just did something. What did your face just do?"

I should have arrested them. That was the job. That had always been the job—the writ, the oath, the clean machinery of duty that let me sleep at night by never asking it questions. I had executed their mother with that machinery humming smooth as a loom. One more arrest. One more name on a manifest. One more line in a ledger kept by men who had never smelled a scaffold.

Instead I heard myself say: "Where did you get this?"

"My mother made it." Their eyes narrowed. "Why."

"When."

"Why do you want to know *when*—"

"*When.*"

"Three years ago. Before they—" They stopped. I watched them arrive at it, watched the understanding move through their face like weather. "Before the Wardens came for her. You weren't asking when she made it. You're asking when she..." Their breath caught. "You were there."

The rain came harder through the broken dome, drumming on stone, filling the silence I couldn't. Somewhere beyond the cloister, boots on gravel. Vess's cordon, tightening. Two minutes, maybe three.

"I read the sentence," I said. It was the closest to confession I could manage with my throat full of glass. "I carried it out. There was a writ. There is always a writ."

"There's always a writ," Tamsin whispered. "Is that what you tell yourself? Does it work?" And then, horribly gently, the way you speak to a man bleeding out: "Does it still work?"

"No," I said. "It stopped working about four minutes ago."

I got off them. I don't fully remember deciding to; I remember the weight of my knee lifting and the strange lightness that followed, as though I'd set down something I'd been carrying so long I'd mistaken it for my own body. Tamsin scrambled back against the font, watching me the way you watch a snake that has, inexplicably, stopped striking.

Outside, torchlight bloomed against the cloister arches. Voices. Vess's voice, clipped and carrying: *"Nave and crypt teams, converge on the baptistry. Hale, report."*

Hale, report. Report that the fugitive was cornered. Report that the arrest was effected. Report, in ten minutes' time, a bound beast-speaker delivered to the citadel, and a clean line drawn under the whole ugly business, and a promotion probably, and a night's sleep certainly, and Maren Ravensworth's child in a cell awaiting the same machinery that had killed her mother.

Or lie.

I had never lied to a superior. It sounds like a small virtue. It is not. It is an enormous one, load-bearing, holding up the entire architecture of the man I had built. Every execution, every raid, every family I'd turned out of a suspected mage-house at midnight—all of it stood on the foundation of *Orsen Hale does not lie*. Take it out and the whole building came down, and I would be standing in the rubble holding a sword and asking what it had ever actually held up.

"Hale!" Closer now. Torchlight on the baptistry wall.

Tamsin was staring at me. Waiting. Doing their own arithmetic, maybe—the odds of the honest warden, the famous one, the warning they told children about. I saw them decide, with visible effort, to hope anyway. It was the bravest thing I saw anyone do in the whole course of this affair, and I include the mountain in that.

I pulled off my cloak and threw it over them. Wet wool, warden grey, smelling of rain and road. Then I drew my sword and raked the flat of it across the font's stone rim, hard enough to raise a spark, and shouted toward the arches:

"In the crypt! They went down through the crypt—collapsed stair, old ossuary tunnels! Get lights down there, now!"

Boots thundered away toward the crypt stairs. Torchlight swung and shrank. In the sudden dark, under my cloak, Tamsin breathed: "What are you doing?"

"Lying," I said. It tasted like copper and freedom. "Apparently it's easy. No wonder everyone does it." I hauled them up by the arm. "There's a postern gate behind the vestry. We have perhaps ninety seconds before Vess realizes the crypt doesn't connect to anywhere and comes back to ask me difficult questions."

"We?" They said it like a word in a foreign language.

"You're the only person in this city who knows why the stars went out. And I'm the only person in this city who knows how the crown plans to bury that reason along with you." I was already moving, pulling them through the dark toward the vestry, toward the gate, toward the enormous unmarked country on the other side of my own oath. "So yes. *We.* Gods help us both."

Behind us, faint and furious, Captain Vess began to shout my name.

* * *

The postern gate opened onto the drainage cut behind the temple, black water sliding toward the river. Tamsin paused at the threshold, the mother's sigil glinting at their wrist, and looked back once—at the temple, at the city, at whatever life was burning down behind us.

"She hid me for three years," they said quietly. "In plain sight. Selling charms. Being nobody." A breath. "You were the one thing she couldn't hide me from."

"I know," I said. "Come on. We can hate each other properly on the road."

And we went, together, into the starless dark.
