## 5. The Road Under Ashen Skies

The first thing you learn about fleeing the crown is that nobody tells you how boring it is.

Three days out of Vellharrow and our grand conspiracy consisted of: me, a wanted beast-speaker with a charm-bracelet worth more than my life; Orsen Hale, a disgraced royal warden who'd lied to his own captain for me; one mule named Duchess who had opinions; and a road that went up. Forever. Toward Mount Cinderfall, where the last dragon-fire in the world was supposedly guttering out like a candle in a drafty temple.

"You're sulking," Orsen said.

"I'm strategizing."

"Strategizing while facing backward on a mule."

"Duchess prefers it. She says I have a calming face." This was true, incidentally. Duchess had said so, in her slow grass-and-thunder way, though what she'd actually meant was that my face made her sleepy and she appreciated that in a human.

Orsen didn't answer. He never answered when I quoted the animals at him, which was his way of pretending he didn't believe me, which was his way of coping with the fact that he absolutely did. Three days of watching him flinch every time a crow spoke near the road. It was almost funny. Almost.

The sky above us stayed wrong. That was the thing nobody warned you about either — not the dark itself, but the *texture* of it. A night sky without stars isn't black. It's a lid. You keep expecting to see your breath fog against it.

I turned back around before the lid could press down too hard, and we walked on toward Cinderfall.

* * *

I have executed forty-one people.

I know the number because I was required to know it. Required to say it aloud at each dawn muster, required to carry it the way a soldier carries weight — evenly distributed, unexamined. Forty-one mages, burned or beheaded under the Law of Ash, and I had told myself every time that the law was the law, that mercy was a crack in a dam, that someone had to hold the line between Vellharrow and the old chaos.

Then the stars went out, and the wards fell, and I learned that the line I'd been holding had been holding *nothing*. And now I walked beside number forty-two's child — no. Not child. Tamsin Ravensworth, who sold luck-charms to farmers and talked to horses, and who had every reason to put a knife between my ribs on some quiet stretch of road and every apparent reason not to.

"Why Cinderfall?" I asked, mostly to hear something besides my own accounting.

"Because Ember-that-Was is the last fire," they said. "Because the covenant has to be remade by someone with a voice the dragons can hear, and as far as I know, that's me. Because the alternative is standing around waiting for the Hollow King to finish eating the sky." They glanced sideways at me. "Also because you haven't arrested me yet, and I'm curious how long that lasts."

"Tamsin—"

"No, I'm genuinely curious. It's been three days. At this rate you'll arrest me somewhere around the foothills, and honestly? The suspense is doing more for my cardio than the walking."

I almost laughed. I hadn't laughed since the night the sky closed, and the sound came out rusty and strange, and Tamsin looked so pleased with themselves about it that I regretted it immediately.

We camped that night in the lee of a fallen waystone, one of the old covenant-markers that lined the roads before the Sundering. Its carved dragon had weathered to a suggestion, wings worn smooth by centuries of rain. Tamsin pressed their palm flat against it and went still in that particular way of theirs — eyes unfocused, head tilted, listening to something below hearing.

"What's it say?" I asked, before I could stop myself. Asking was admitting.

Tamsin opened one eye. "You want to know?"

"I'm asking, aren't I."

"That you are." They patted the stone. "It doesn't say anything. It's dead stone. But there's a hedgehog under the roots, and *he* says the ground here tastes like smoke and old promises, and that something big passed through heading north two nights ago and left the worms too frightened to come up."

"A hedgehog said that."

"Hedgehogs are very serious creatures. You'd respect them if you could hear them."

I thought about the forty-one. I thought about how many of them might have heard hedgehogs. "Teach me," I said.

Tamsin blinked. "Teach you what?"

"To hear. What you hear." I kept my voice level, the way I'd once kept it level reading execution orders. "If I'm to guard you to Cinderfall, I should know what's coming. Beasts knew the stars were going out before my entire garrison did. A horse panicked in the night market hours before midnight. If I'd known what that meant—" I stopped. There was no version of that sentence that ended anywhere good.

Tamsin studied me for a long moment, and whatever they saw in my face seemed to decide something. "Okay," they said softly. "But fair warning, Warden. Most of what animals say is boring, rude, or about food. You've been trained your whole life to think magic is thunder and glory. Mostly it's a hedgehog complaining about worms."

"So I've been told. Twice now."

* * *

Lesson one: sit still. Apparently I am bad at this.

Lesson two: breathe slower. Apparently my resting breathing rate is, per Tamsin, "the respiratory pattern of a man being chased."

Lesson three, on the fourth morning, in a birch grove where the leaves had gone grey instead of gold: listen with the back of your neck. Which is nonsense. Except it isn't, because when I finally stopped trying to *hear* and just let the world press against me — the creak of branches, the tick of a beetle in bark, Duchess dozing — something underneath all of it shifted, like a current under water.

And a jay screamed at me from a low branch, and I understood it.

Not the words. There weren't words. It was more like — meaning arriving whole, the way you understand a door slamming. *Danger-north-many-feet-cold-wrong.* The jay's feathers were slicked flat with fear, and its meaning tasted of iron.

I sat back hard against a tree trunk.

"There it is," Tamsin said quietly. They'd been watching me for who knows how long. "What did it say?"

"Danger. North. Many feet. Cold and wrong." My voice sounded foreign to me. "That's — those aren't words. How did I—"

"The covenant used to run through everyone," Tamsin said. "Every living thing, threaded together. When it broke, most people lost the thread entirely. But it doesn't fully die, Orsen. It just goes quiet. You spent your career killing people like me, and the whole time the door was never locked. It was just shut." They held out a hand and helped me up, and their grip was warm, and I hated how much I noticed that. "Congratulations. You're exactly what you were trained to kill."

"I'm not sure whether to thank you."

"Don't. Buy me dinner when this is over. Assuming there are restaurants after the apocalypse."

* * *

The beasts along the road all told the same story, and none of them would tell it twice.

A flock of sheep huddled against a collapsed barn wall, and their collective voice was a low woolly dread: *the star-eater walks, the star-eater walks, the fields go grey behind it.* A fox I bribed with half our salt pork spoke in quick red flickers — *Hollow King, hollow king, wears a crown of nothing, ate the little lights, coming for the big ones next.* Even the worms, according to a deeply offended hedgehog, had opinions, and the worms' opinion was simply to go deeper and stay there.

"They call him the Hollow King," Tamsin said that evening, turning a stick in the fire. "Every animal, same name. Like they've all seen him."

"Animals don't lie," I said. It wasn't something I'd have said a month ago. It was something I knew now, bone-deep, the way you know a blade's balance after ten years in your hand.

"No. They don't. Which means he's real, and he's ahead of us, and everything between here and Cinderfall is going grey." Tamsin looked up, firelight catching their face. "My mother used to sing a song about the dragon-fires. About how they weren't just flame — they were *anchors*. Pin the sky down so it can't blow away. One by one they've gone out, and nobody noticed because the crown buried the news, and now there's one left, and the sky is already lifting at the edges." They smiled, and it didn't reach their eyes. "Cheerful, right?"

"Very."

"Your turn. Tell me something true, Warden. Fair's fair — I've told you about the worms."

I fed a branch into the fire and watched it catch. Forty-one names sat in my chest like stones in a riverbed. I picked up the first one.

"Her name was Maren Ravensworth," I said. "Three years ago. I read the order myself. She asked me if I had children, and I said the law doesn't permit me to answer, and she smiled at me like she pitied me, and I have thought about that smile every day since." The fire popped. "I don't expect forgiveness. I'm not asking for it. I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from me and not find it written in some archive."

Tamsin was silent a long time. Duchess shifted her weight in the dark and said nothing, which from Duchess was a kindness.

"She sang that song," Tamsin said finally. "The anchor song. To me. When I was small." Their jaw worked. "I'm not forgiving you tonight, Orsen. Maybe not ever. But you told me the truth when lying was easier, and that's — that's not nothing. So. We keep walking."

"We keep walking," I agreed.

And that was the moment, I think, though neither of us said it — sitting in the grey dark under a lid of sky, sharing a silence heavier than any accusation — that the wary thing between us became something else. Something with weight and warmth and terrible timing.

* * *

Two days later, the birds stopped.

Not went quiet — *stopped*, mid-flight, mid-song, gone. Tamsin stood in the middle of the road with their head cocked, and their face went white as ash.

"Orsen." Their voice came out thin. "There's nothing. For miles in every direction. No beasts. Nothing small, nothing burrowing. The land's been *emptied*."

Ahead of us the road climbed through hills that had gone the color of old bone, and on the wind, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I had no training for and Tamsin clearly recognized: a vast, slow exhalation, like a bellows the size of a cathedral drawing breath.

And beneath it, threading through the emptiness like a wire through wax, a voice — not beast, not bird, not anything alive — speaking in a tone of patient, bottomless hunger:

"*Little speaker. Little liar-warden. I smell the last fire burning. Come closer. I have been so hungry since the stars.*"

Tamsin's hand found mine in the dark.

"He knows we're coming," they whispered. "He's been waiting for us."
