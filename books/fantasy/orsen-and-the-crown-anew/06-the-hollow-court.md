## 6. The Hollow Court

I woke to the smell of cold ash and old incense, which is a combination I'd recommend to no one, and found myself lying on a stone floor beneath a ceiling painted with stars that had been scratched out.

Not faded. Scratched out. Deliberately, with something sharp, every one of them gouged from the plaster so that only pale wounds remained where constellations used to be.

"Well," I said to the ceiling. "That's not ominous at all."

"You're awake." The voice came from my left, dry as winter grass. "Good. The last one slept for two days and we thought we'd have to bury him."

The speaker was an old woman in robes the color of soot, seated on a chair that might once have been a throne and had since been demoted to furniture. Behind her, in the gloom of what I now realized was a vast hall—pillars, broken windows, the whole dramatic package—stood perhaps thirty figures. Mages. Real ones, marked by the faded brands on their wrists where the crown's iron had seared away their gifts' licenses, back when licenses were a thing anyone pretended existed.

My hands were bound. Not with rope—with woven grass, of all things, knotted in patterns that made my fingers itch. Beast-craft. Someone here knew the old knots, the ones that held because the material *agreed* to hold them.

"Where's Orsen?" My voice came out rougher than I intended.

"The warden? Alive. Being talked at." The old woman smiled without warmth. "He's proving resistant. They usually are, at first."

"And you are?"

"Mother Veyra." She said it the way other people said *your executioner*. "Once Royal Mage to King Aldric's father. Once, before I understood what the throne actually eats."

Around us, the court shifted. Thirty pairs of eyes, and not one of them curious about me the way people were usually curious about a stranger. They looked at me like I was a delivery they'd been expecting. A parcel labeled *beast-speaker*.

"You've been following us," I said. "Since the wards fell."

"Since before the wards fell, child. We *made* them fall."

The words landed in the silent hall like stones into still water. Somewhere above us—I felt it rather than heard it—the absence of stars pressed down through the broken roof.

"You," I said. "You're the Hollow King."

Veyra laughed, and it was a terrible sound, because there was nothing hollow in it at all. It was full of grief, the way a well is full of water. "There is no king. There never was. We needed a story the crown would chase instead of us—a monster to blame for the dark. So we built one out of rumor and shadow, and the crown, being experts on monsters, believed it instantly." She leaned forward. "But the truth is worse than a monster, beast-speaker. The truth is arithmetic."

"I hate arithmetic," I said, mostly to buy time while I tested the grass-knots. They didn't budge. Of course they didn't. The grass liked its job.

"A hundred years ago," Veyra said, rising, her joints cracking like green wood in fire, "the covenant between humans and dragons was simple. Dragon-fire anchored the border wards. Dragons gave their flame freely, and in exchange, humans kept the deep roads open, tended the nesting grounds, spoke with beasts as kin. One voice among many voices. Do you know what changed?"

"The Sundering," I said. Everyone knew the Sundering. It was in every schoolbook: the night magic went wild, the dragons withdrew, the mages were outlawed.

"The Sundering was a *story* too." Her voice dropped, and the whole court leaned in with her, thirty exiles breathing the same bitter air. "There was no catastrophe. There was a ledger. King Aldric's grandfather discovered that dragon-fire could be drawn off slowly—siphoned—fed directly into the wards to make them stronger, cheaper, eternal. Why honor a covenant of mutual labor when you could simply *take* the fuel? So he took it. Quietly. A little each year. And when the dragons noticed—when they came asking why their fire dimmed—the crown called it treachery and wrote the Sundering into the histories. Outlawed the speaking. Branded the mages. Burned the ones who remembered."

My mouth had gone dry. "The executions."

"Three hundred and eleven speakers in the first decade alone." Veyra's eyes found mine and held them, and I understood suddenly why she'd had me brought here instead of killed. "Including, I'm told, a woman named Elara Ravensworth, who could speak to moths, of all things, and who died telling her executioner that the wards were drinking something they shouldn't."

The floor tilted. Or I did. Elara Ravensworth. My mother, who had taught me to listen to sparrows before she taught me letters, who had gone to the market one morning three years ago and never come home, whose death I'd been told was for sedition, for conspiracy, for crimes I never asked anyone to name because naming them made them real.

"She told them," I whispered. "She tried to tell them."

"She told everyone. The crown called it madness. The wardens called it treason." Veyra came closer, her shadow falling over me like a lid. "So yes, beast-speaker. We broke the wards. We ate the stars—we hid them behind our working, a veil, so the realm would feel what the dragons feel: abandoned in the dark. We did it to force the truth into the open. To force the crown to send someone to the mountain. To force *you*—" her finger stopped an inch from my sternum, "—to be found. Because the covenant can be reforged only by a speaker, and you are the last one born with the gift unburned."

* * *

They let me walk after that. Unbound, even, though the court watched me the way cats watch a bird that has foolishly entered the house. I found Orsen in a side chamber, sitting very straight on a bench, surrounded by four exiles who were, indeed, talking at him. He looked like a man enduring a tax audit with dignity.

"Tamsin." Relief crossed his face and then, immediately, guilt about the relief, which was very Orsen.

"They told you?" I asked.

"They told me their version." His jaw was set in the line I'd learned meant *duty arguing with itself*. "That the crown siphoned the dragon-fire. That the Sundering was fabricated."

"It's true." I sat beside him. "Orsen. My mother tried to warn them. Three years ago. She told the wardens the wards were feeding wrong, and they executed her for it."

He went very still. I watched him do the arithmetic himself—watched him arrive at the number he must have already suspected. Warden Hale had carried out executions; he'd never hidden that from me, had worn it like a stone in his chest. But this—

"Which district?" he asked quietly. "Which year?"

"The third year of the purges. Autumn."

His hands opened and closed once, empty. "I was there," he said. "I don't remember her face. I remember the moths. Afterward, there were moths everywhere, and no one could explain it, and I dreamed about them for a month." He looked at me, and his eyes were awful with it. "Tamsin, I—"

"Don't." I said it gently, but I said it. "Don't apologize yet. Apologizing is easy. Listen first. That's the whole covenant, isn't it? Listening first."

He closed his mouth. Nodded. And for a long moment we just sat there, two people on a bench in a starless hall, while somewhere beyond the walls the exiles' veil-work hummed against the sky like a held breath.

* * *

The trouble with learning your enemy is also your savior is that nobody warns you how much it hurts.

I spent the evening walking the court, listening—not to people, at first, but to the small lives threaded through the ruin. Mice in the walls, bold and chatty. A colony of bats in the bell tower who had opinions about everything and volume settings limited to *loud*. And everywhere, under everything, the bones.

Dead dragon-bones, like the sewers of Vellharrow, but older here, vaster. This place had been a temple once—a temple of the covenant, built on a spine of the great wyrm who had first given flame freely. I could feel her through the floor, patient and dead and still faintly warm, the way embers stay warm long after the fire forgets them.

"She's still listening," I told Veyra that night, standing in the nave while the exiles worked their veil-spells in shifts, holding the stolen sky shut. "The bones. Whatever the crown took from the dragons, whatever was broken—she's still listening underneath it all."

Something moved across Veyra's face that might have been hope, quickly suppressed, like a candle flame cupped against wind. "Then you understand why we did what we did."

"No." I turned to face her, and my voice shook, and I let it. "I understand why you're *right*. That's different. You broke the sky, Veyra. Every farmer who couldn't navigate home. Every child terrified of the dark. Every sailor, every traveler, every beast that reads the stars to migrate—you hurt them all to make a point. My mother died telling the truth gently, and you chose to tell it with a hammer."

"The gentle truth was executed," Veyra said. "Autumn, third year of the purges. You were there."

And that was the wound of it, laid open and honest: she wasn't wrong. Fury and grief tangled in my chest until I couldn't tell which was which. The crown had murdered the messengers for a century and called it law. The court had blackened heaven and called it justice. And caught between them, the realm went on dying—one dimmed ward, one starving dragon, one silenced voice at a time.

"Take me to Cinderfall," I said finally. "That's the only apology any of us gets to make. Not words. Fire."

Veyra studied me for a long time. Then she smiled, and it was almost kind, and somehow that was the worst thing yet.

"Oh, we'll take you to the mountain, beast-speaker. We'll carry you in triumph if we must." She gestured, and two exiles stepped forward—but not toward me. Toward the doors, where boots were suddenly ringing on stone, where someone was shouting in a warden's cadence, where torchlight bloomed against the walls.

"But first," Veyra said softly, "it seems the crown has come to collect its warden. Captain Dreth and forty swords, closing fast. So you have a choice to make, Tamsin Ravensworth, and you have about ninety seconds to make it: the man who killed your mother's memory—or the truth that needs him alive."

The doors burst open.
