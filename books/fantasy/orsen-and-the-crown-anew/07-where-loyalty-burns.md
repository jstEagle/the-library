## 7. Where Loyalty Burns

I have broken a great many oaths in my life, but I'd always done it quietly—small betrayals, the kind you can fold up and put in a pocket. Lying to Captain Dreth about a charm-bracelet. Looking away from a smuggler's cart because the smuggler had children. Telling myself that duty was a scale and I was only shifting a grain or two.

Burning your oath is not quiet. Burning your oath smells like your whole life on fire.

The doors of the Hollow Court burst open and there he was: Captain Dreth, forty swords at his back, torchlight turning his scarred face into something out of an old cautionary tale. He'd been my commanding officer for eleven years. He'd taught me how to hold a blade so it didn't chatter. He'd stood beside me at nine executions and never once flinched, which I had mistaken for strength.

"Orsen Hale," Dreth said, and his voice carried the way it always did, flat as a ledger. "Deserter. The crown wants you breathing. The beast-speaker it wants however it can get them."

Forty swords. One of me. And somewhere behind me, bound to a chair of black bone, Tamsin Ravensworth—the person I had lied for, fled with, and loved with the specific, catastrophic thoroughness of a man who should have known better.

I did what any sensible warden would do.

I negotiated.

"Captain." I raised my hands, palms out, the old surrender-gesture from training yard drills. "Let's not make this messy. You want the beast-speaker. I know where the mountain road runs. Walk with me to the door and I'll tell you everything—where Ember-that-Was sleeps, how deep her fire's guttered, all of it."

Dreth's eyes narrowed. Eleven years, and he still couldn't read me, which I counted as the one gift my father ever gave me—a face like a locked drawer.

"You'd sell them out?" he said. "You? The man who lied to me in a temple?"

"People change, captain. It's the whole reason we have executions."

It was a cruel thing to say and I said it anyway, because cruelty was the coin Dreth trusted. He took a step forward. Then another. His men fanned out along the walls, torches high, and the exiles of the Hollow Court melted back into their alcoves like smoke deciding where to settle. Veyra watched me from the shadows with an expression I couldn't parse—amusement, maybe, or appraisal. *This one might be worth saving after all,* or possibly, *this one will be useful as kindling.*

Dreth came within arm's reach. Close enough that I could smell the ward-oil they'd started coating blades with since the stars went out. Close enough that when I moved, he wouldn't have time to be surprised.

"I'm sorry about this," I told him, and meant it, which somehow made it worse.

Then I drove my elbow into his throat, tore the sword from his scabbard, and put the burning brazier between us before the first shout finished leaving anyone's mouth.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fighting the people who trained you: they know all your tricks, because they gave them to you.

Dreth recovered fast—I'd hit him hard enough to drop a mule, but Dreth wasn't a mule—and came at me low, going for the legs, the way he'd drilled into every recruit for a decade. I gave ground, gave more, let him think the brazier had me cornered against the wall of bones. The Hollow Court's great hall was threaded with dragon-ribs, vast pale arches that had been dead longer than Vellharrow had been a city, and I backed up under the biggest one and waited for the moment Dreth committed his weight.

He committed. I dropped, rolled, and came up behind him with the flat of my blade across the backs of his knees—not the edge. Not yet. Forty of his soldiers were converging, and I needed thirty seconds, not a corpse.

"Orsen!" That was Tamsin's voice, raw and furious, from the chair. "What are you *doing*?"

"Rescuing you badly," I called back. "As is traditional."

An exile cut the ropes off them while I held the line—or held the illusion of holding it, which is mostly what swordwork is. Two soldiers broke from the formation and came at me together, the paired-blade drill, left then right, and I took the left one's wrist on my crossguard and turned the right one's thrust into his partner's shoulder. They went down tangled. I felt sick doing it. These were men whose names I knew. Halvard, who owed me three coppers from a card game in Kettering. The boy on the right couldn't have been more than nineteen.

"Hale!" Dreth was up again, blood in his mouth, and now his voice cracked across the hall like a whip. "Hale, stand down. Whatever they've promised you—whatever witchery this is—it isn't worth your name."

And there it was. My name. Orsen Hale, Royal Warden, Third Oath, the man who had carried out the crown's justice without question until the night the questions got too loud to smother. My name was the last thing I owned that the crown hadn't already taken.

I looked at Tamsin—free now, standing among the exiles with ash on their sleeves and fury in their eyes, looking at me like I was either a miracle or an idiot, and honestly both were accurate—and I understood that the choice had stopped being a choice somewhere around the temple, around the bracelet, around the moment I'd looked at a dead woman's sigil on a living wrist and felt my whole architecture of duty groan like a ship taking water.

"Halvard," I said, to the man on the ground. "Take your boy and get out of the way. Please."

Halvard stared at me. Then he grabbed the kid's collar and dragged them both toward the wall, and something in my chest cracked open, because mercy was contagious, apparently, even here.

"The rest of you," I said, raising the stolen sword, "I'm sorry too."

* * *

They say the Hollow King doesn't walk; he arrives, the way cold arrives, everywhere at once. I believe it, because one moment the hall was chaos—steel, shouting, Veyra's exiles pouring out of the dark with knives made of starless glass—and the next, everything stopped, the way a room stops when the wrong person enters.

He stood at the head of the hall where the throne of fused vertebrae rose out of the floor. He wore no crown, which was its own kind of statement. His face was the worst part: ordinary. A tired administrator's face. A man who audited things.

"Enough," he said, and the word fell through the hall like a stone through still water. Even Dreth's soldiers froze mid-step. "Warden Hale. Or what's left of a warden."

I kept my blade up. Sweat stung my eyes. Somewhere behind me Tamsin was moving, Veyra's hand on their arm, pulling them toward a side passage, and I angled my body to keep the King's attention on me and off them. Old trick. Oldest trick.

"You've cost me a good deal tonight," the Hollow King said mildly. "A court's dignity. Several hours. And I imagine, shortly, forty of the crown's finest, if this continues, which would be wasteful. So let me make you an offer instead, and spare us all the arithmetic."

"I don't want anything from you."

"No. You want to give things away. It's very tiresome." He descended one step, unhurried. "But consider. The crown you serve has already discarded you. Your captain carries orders to bring you back in chains—or a box, whichever's lighter. Meanwhile, I am building something in this starless dark. A realm where magic isn't a crime, where people like the beast-speaker aren't executed in courtyards by men who—" he paused, delicately, "—well. By men like you."

The words landed exactly where he aimed them. Nine executions. Nine faces. My mother's sigil on a stranger's wrist.

"And?" I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"And I'll give you a crown, Orsen Hale. Not the rotten one in Vellharrow—a new one. Stand aside. Give me the beast-speaker—they're the keystone, nothing more, a voice to speak the covenant *my* way, with the dragons leashed instead of free—and I will make you a king of the new order. Marshal of the dawn that comes after this long night. Every wrong the crown made you commit, unmade. Every mage you burned, honored instead."

Behind me, faintly, I heard Tamsin stop struggling against Veyra's grip. Listening.

"That's quite an offer," I said.

"It is."

"And what happens to Tamsin, after? Once they've spoken your covenant."

The Hollow King smiled. It was the smile of a man reading a favorable ledger entry. "The voice spends itself. That's the nature of speaking. But they'd die *meaningful*, Warden. Most people don't manage even that."

* * *

I want to record, for whoever finds this account, that I did not hesitate. I want that written down plainly, because everything else about me deserves asterisks.

I laughed. Actually laughed—short, ugly, incredulous—because the sheer size of it, the crown he was offering, the kingdom built on a corpse's borrowed voice. Eleven years I'd served a throne that fed dragon-fire to its wards and called the resulting starvation *justice*. And here was another throne offering me the same meal with a different napkin.

"You know what my mother used to say?" I said, walking toward him, sword lowered—not surrendering, just done. "'A man who sells his friends buys enemies at retail.' She was a fishwife and a drunk and smarter than everyone in this room."

"She sounds charming."

"She'd have hated you on principle." I stopped ten feet from him. Close enough to see that his eyes had no reflection in them at all, which explained a great deal. "Here's my answer. No. No crown, no marshal's sash, no unmaking. You don't get Tamsin. You don't get their voice. You don't get *one word* spoken your way, because the covenant isn't a leash, it's a promise, and promises are the only currency I've got left that isn't counterfeit."

The hall went very quiet.

Then the Hollow King sighed, the sigh of a clerk closing a file, and gestured with two fingers, and Dreth—who had been waiting, patient as winter—gave the order, and forty swords came down the hall toward me all at once.

I turned my back on the Hollow King. Deliberately. The most insulting thing a dying man can do.

Tamsin had broken free of Veyra and was running toward me through the chaos, and I shouted the only tactical instruction that mattered anymore: "*The mountain! Go!*"

"I'm not leaving you!"

"You are absolutely leaving me, this is the plan, I'm very good at plans—"

I caught the first blade on mine, the second on my vambrace, and went down swinging under the biggest rib-arch in the hall, buying seconds with blood I could ill afford, while behind me Tamsin's voice rose—not words, not yet, but that other voice, the one that spoke to beasts—and every rat, raven, and half-starved hound in the Hollow Court's foundations answered it at once.

The dark, it turns out, has its own cavalry.

And I fought my way toward the sound of it, laughing again, bleeding freely, a deserter twice over and a traitor forever, happier than I had ever been in the crown's clean, well-lit service.
