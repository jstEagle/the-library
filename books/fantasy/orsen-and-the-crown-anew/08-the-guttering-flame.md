## 8. The Guttering Flame

*Tamsin*

The mountain had been visible for two days before we reached it, which felt like a cruel joke. You spend weeks walking toward the last fire in the world, and it just sits there on the horizon like a pot on a stove, patient and enormous, letting you imagine all the ways you're about to be boiled.

"Cheerful thought," Orsen said, when I said as much out loud.

"I'm a charm-seller from Vellharrow. Cheerful thoughts are my entire inventory."

He almost smiled. He'd been almost-smiling more often lately, in that way of a man remembering how the motion worked. The road behind us was littered with everything he'd burned away—his oath, his rank, his brothers-in-arms, his name in the crown's good graces—and somehow he walked lighter than I'd ever seen him. Grief does that to some people. It had done the opposite to me.

The mountain's slopes were black glass, fused by old fire, and they rang under our boots like the bells of a drowned church. Nothing grew here. Nothing *spoke* here either, which frightened me more than the silence of the stars had. All the way from the Hollow Court, beasts had murmured to us at the edges of the road—crows with gossip, foxes with warnings, one extremely opinionated mule who had attached himself to our party for reasons he declined to explain. But Cinderfall's slopes were empty of voices. As if even the wind had agreed to hold its breath.

We climbed through the ash-fog until the air turned warm and tasted of copper and struck flint. And then the mountain opened.

Not a cave. A throat. A vast hollow descending into the mountain's heart, its walls veined with channels where lava had once run like blood through a living body. Most of those veins were dark now. Cold stone, cracked and grey, like the hearth of a house abandoned generations ago.

But not all of them.

Far below, at the bottom of the throat, something still glowed. Faintly. The way coals glow an hour after everyone's gone to bed—the light that stays only because no one has told it yet that it's allowed to die.

"Oh," I said. My voice came out very small in all that dark. "Oh, she's still *here*."

"You knew she would be."

"I hoped. There's a difference." I started down the switchback path cut into the wall, ancient steps worn smooth by claws far larger than mine. "Hoping is what you do when you've got nothing better. Knowing is what you do when someone's already paid."

Orsen followed without argument. He'd gotten good at that too.

* * *

*Orsen*

I had executed mages in temples, in market squares, once in a flooded quarry where the water rose around the condemned woman's knees and she never stopped singing. I tell myself I remember these things because forgetting them would be worse. I am no longer certain that's true. Some memories are carried like a debt; others are carried like a wound you keep reopening to check if it still hurts. It always hurts. That is rather the point of wounds.

Descending into Cinderfall, I understood I had never actually seen magic before. Not truly. The wards I had policed were machinery—cold, lawful, humming with borrowed power. This was different. This was a body. The mountain breathed. Slowly, so slowly I might have imagined it, but the warm updraft against my face rose and fell in a rhythm, and Tamsin's hand found mine in the dark without either of us deciding anything, and their pulse matched it exactly.

At the bottom, the last vein of fire pooled in a shallow basin of black glass, and lying in it—or perhaps being held by it, I could not say which—was the dragon.

She was dying. There was no honest word but that. She was vast beyond sense, her wings folded like the ruins of cathedral roofs, her scales the color of embers going grey at the edges. Her chest rose once, then waited a long time before rising again. Around her, the pool of fire guttered in time with her breath, flaring weakly, sinking, flaring.

Tamsin knelt at the basin's edge. I watched them do the thing they did—the thing I had been trained my whole life to kill—and go silent in the way of listening. Their lips moved slightly, shaping words back. Then they went very still.

"What did she say?" I asked.

Tamsin didn't answer right away. When they did, their voice was careful, the way you speak when the floor might give way.

"She said: *at last, a speaker. Tell me, little tongue, are you the one who comes to beg, or the one who comes to pay?*"

"Beg or pay," I repeated. "Those seem like the only options left in this world."

"She says the covenant was a bargain, not a gift. Fire for voice. Dragons anchored both realms because humans gave them their words to hold—gave them *meaning*. When the crown stopped paying, when it started taking instead—" Tamsin's hands closed into fists. "You know the rest. We saw what the Hollow King showed us."

"The crown broke it first," I said. The words tasted like ash, and I had earned that taste. Three years of executions. Three years of hunting speakers while my king drained dragons dry to light his wards. "Yes. I know the rest."

Ember-that-Was opened one eye.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last discipline I have left: the eye was the size of a cart wheel, and it was gold, and it looked at me the way a judge looks at a man who has already confessed. There was no malice in it. That was the terrible part. Only a long, exhausted knowing.

"She can see me," I said stupidly.

"She can see *through* you," Tamsin said softly. "She says warden-blood smells like smoke and broken promises. She says—" They paused, listening again, and something crossed their face that I couldn't read. "She says you've already burned your oath, so she supposes you'll have to be judged by what's underneath it."

"And what's underneath it?"

Tamsin looked at me. Not at the dragon. At me.

"Me," they said. "Apparently."

* * *

*Tamsin*

Here is what nobody tells you about the end of the world: it takes forever to arrive and then it happens in the space between one sentence and the next.

Ember-that-Was spoke slowly, each word arriving like a stone rolled down a long hill, and I translated as best I could, though translation is a liar's trade and I knew I was losing things. Her voice in my head was not sound. It was heat. It was the memory of every hearth that had ever kept anyone alive through any winter.

*The covenant can be reforged,* she said. *Fire remains in me. Enough. Once.*

*Enough for what?*

*For a new speaking. But hear me, little tongue, and do not look away, for I will say this only once.* Her great head shifted, scales grinding like millstones. *A covenant requires payment. The old speakers gave their voices freely—not their words for a day, not their songs for a season. Forever. The voice goes into the flame and becomes part of the binding. Every beast-tongue you carry, every whisper you have ever traded with sparrow or rat or horse—it leaves you. Entirely. You will live. You may even live long. But you will never speak another word, in any language, to any creature, for as long as you draw breath.*

The silence after that was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

I should tell you what I thought first, because it wasn't noble. My first thought was petty and small and human: *I finally learned to use this gift properly, and now it costs everything.* My second thought was of my mother, who had died for a lesser version of this same bargain, though she hadn't known she was bargaining. My third thought—

My third thought was Orsen, standing behind me with his hand on his sword hilt out of pure habit, watching me with an expression I had seen on him only twice before: once when he lied to his captain for me, and once when he burned his oath in the Hollow King's hall.

"Say it plainly," he said to me. "All of it."

So I did. Voice for the flame. Forever. No take-backs, no clever loopholes, no charm-seller's fine print. I told him the way I tell hard truths, quickly, like pulling a splinter.

He was quiet for a long moment. Below us, the fire guttered. Above us, the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

"No," he said.

"Orsen—"

"*No.* Find another way. There's always—" He stopped himself, because we both knew how that sentence ended, and we both knew it was a lie, and we were past lies, he and I. That was the whole point of us. That was the only thing we'd built that was worth anything.

"There isn't," I said gently. "You taught me that, actually. Back in the temple, when you told me the truth about my mother. You said the realm doesn't survive on comfortable fictions. Well. Here's the uncomfortable one."

"Tamsin."

"You knew, coming up this mountain. Don't pretend you didn't. Ember told me you smelled of smoke and broken promises, but you don't, not anymore. You smell like a man who did his arithmetic on the road." I stood up and faced him, and my knees were shaking, and I made them stop, because some things you get to choose. "Ask me the question you're afraid to ask."

His jaw worked. For a man who had faced down the Hollow King's court with nothing but a burning oath and a bad attitude, he suddenly looked like the boy he must have been before the crown got hold of him.

"If I ask," he said, "you'll answer honestly. That's the problem with you."

"That's the *point* of me."

He closed the distance between us. His hands were steady—he was always steady, curse him—but his voice wasn't, and I loved him a little more for that, which annoyed me, because I was trying to be brave and he kept making it complicated.

"Marry me," he said. "After. If there's an after. Marry me with no voice and a dead crown and a realm full of dragons, and I will spend the rest of my life learning your hands the way I once learned to read warrants. Say yes now, so I know what I'm fighting for when they come up that mountain."

"They?"

As if summoned—as if the world had been waiting for precisely the wrong moment—the mountain shuddered. Ash sifted down from the throat above us. Far off, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of horns. Many horns. Marching in step.

Ember-that-Was laughed, or what passes for laughter in a creature that old—a low rumble like a landslide deciding to be polite about it.

*They come,* she said. *The hollow one and his hungry court. So, little tongue. Little warden. Decide quickly now: beg, or pay?*

Orsen drew his sword. The blade caught the guttering firelight and threw it back, one small stubborn spark against all that dark.

"Yes," I said. "Obviously yes. Now hand me something sharp, and stand next to me, and whatever happens—don't let them stop me halfway."

Below us, the last fire in the world took a long, slow breath, and waited for my voice.
