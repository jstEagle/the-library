## 9. Covenant Anew

The army came up the mountain like a tide of shadows given orders.

I heard them before I saw them—of course I did. The mountain itself was talking, every crack and scree and stone complaining in a language of groans and grinding that only I could parse. *They climb,* said the rocks. *They climb, they climb, they do not tire, because there is nothing left inside them to tire.*

"Nothing left to tire," I repeated, my mouth dry. "That's comforting."

*It is not meant to comfort,* said a marmot from the scree slope, with the brisk rudeness of its kind. *It is meant to be true. You should hear what the lichen says about them. Lichen has opinions now. It's very upset.*

"Lichen is always upset," Orsen muttered beside me. He'd taken up position at the mouth of Ember-that-Was's crater, sword drawn, warden's coat long since burned of its insignia but somehow still hanging on him like a habit he couldn't quit. He looked like a man standing at the edge of the world and pretending it was Tuesday. "Tamsin. Are you certain about the words?"

"No," I said. "I'm certain about the meaning. The words are—" I flexed my hands, feeling for the thousand voices I'd carried since childhood, the horse-warnings and rat-gossip and hawk-scoldings, all of them layered under my skin like sediment. "The words will come. They always come when something needs saying badly enough."

"That's not how language works."

"That's not how *magic* works," I corrected. "Language is just what we settle for."

Below us, the first ranks of the Hollow King's court crested the switchback trail. They moved wrong—too smooth, too quiet, boots finding purchase on loose ash as though the mountain had agreed to carry them. Their king came last, wrapped in a cloak that drank the firelight, and even at this distance I could feel him listening. Feeling for my voice the way a spider feels for vibrations in its web.

Ember-that-Was stirred beneath us, vast and dim, her scales banked coals, her breath a shallow tide. *Little tongue,* she rumbled. *When you give it, you cannot take it back. Your voice will live in me and in my children, in every wingbeat and every roar until the end of things. But your throat will be an empty house. Do you understand what you are buying?*

"A world," I said. "For a song. It's the best deal anyone's offered me all year, and I once sold a love-charm to a man who was already married."

Orsen made a sound that was half laugh, half something much worse. His hand found mine—calloused, scarred, steady—and held it for exactly three seconds, which I counted, because I am a merchant's child and I have always known the value of things.

"I should have arrested you in that temple," he said quietly. "It would have been simpler."

"Simpler isn't the same as better."

"No." He squeezed once and let go. "It never is. Go. Sing. I'll hold the door."

"You'll die holding the door."

"Probably." He turned to face the climbing dark, and there was something almost peaceful in his profile, a man finally setting down a weight he'd carried since the Sundering. "But I've been dying by inches for three years anyway, Tamsin. This way at least it's honest work."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to say a hundred things, most of them unkind about his self-sacrificing streak and all of them true about my heart. Instead I kissed him—quick, fierce, tasting of ash and iron and the last three weeks of running—and stepped past him onto the rim of the crater, where the dying fire breathed below me like a sleeping giant.

And I opened every door in myself at once.

* * *

You have to understand what it means, speaking in every beast-tongue simultaneously. Human throats were built for one voice. Mine had spent twenty-three years learning to be a chorus.

The horse-language came first, because horses taught me fear and courage are the same word said twice. Then rat-speech, quick and gutter-clever. Hawk, which is mostly contempt with grammar. Wolf, which is hunger and loyalty braided into one long note. Serpent, which has no words at all, only temperature. And beneath them all, older than any of them, the deep slow tongue of dragons—the one Ember-that-Was had been teaching me in fragments across our flight here, the covenant-tongue, the words the first speakers sang when the world was young and the stars were dragon-eggs hung in the dark.

The old words rose in me like water finding a crack.

*I am the small voice,* I sang, in tongues that have no letters, that I can only describe now the way you'd describe a dream after waking. *I am the borrowed breath. I come to pay the debt the crown forgot: fire for warding, voice for fire, blood for blood, and silence for the singer. Hear me, flame. Hear me, sky. Let the covenant stand anew—not on thrones, not on treaties, but on this: one voice, freely given, forever.*

The Hollow King reached the crater's edge.

He was close enough now that I could see what the starless dark had made of him—a man-shaped absence, a hole wearing robes, eyes like two eclipses. He raised a hand and the army raised its weapons, and Orsen met the first wave of them with a battle cry that cracked something open in my chest, because it was the cry of a warden defending a mage, and every law he'd ever sworn said he shouldn't.

The King spoke, and his voice was the sound of a library burning. "*Stop.*"

The word hit me like a wall. My song stuttered. My knees buckled. Every beast-tongue in me went silent at once, smothered, and for one horrible heartbeat I was just a girl on a mountainside with a sore throat and no magic at all.

Then the rats spoke.

Not out loud—they were miles away, back in Vellharrow's sewers—but through the deep tongue, through the covenant-thread that runs under all the world, the one the crown had bled dry for a century without knowing it was a vein. *Sing,* they said. *We remember every door you ever opened for us. Sing, charm-seller. Sing, friend-of-rats. No hollow thing can stopper every mouth in creation.*

And the horses remembered. And the hawks remembered. And the wolves on the far slopes lifted their heads and howled the first note for me, and the serpents in their winter dens poured warmth into the second, and Ember-that-Was—blessed, ancient, dying Ember—that-Was opened her failing jaws and gave me the third, a note so low it wasn't sound so much as geology.

I sang the fourth note myself.

The Hollow King screamed and threw everything he had at the mountain, and it wasn't enough, because you cannot arrest a chorus. Orsen fell—I saw him fall, saw red bloom on his side, saw him go down swinging—and I kept singing, tears cutting tracks through the ash on my face, because the song doesn't stop for grief. That's the terrible secret of covenants. They don't care what it costs. They only care that you pay.

*I give my voice,* I sang, and meant it with everything left in me. *Take it. Take all of it. Let it burn brighter than it ever did in me.*

The fire took me at my word.

* * *

It didn't hurt. That's the strange part. Everyone assumes sacrifice must hurt, and maybe some do, but mine felt like exhaling after holding my breath for twenty-three years. My voice left me the way a bird leaves an open hand—one moment weight, the next moment sky.

It went into the flame, and the flame remembered what it was.

Light roared up out of Mount Cinderfall like the mountain had been holding its breath since the Sundering. Dragon-fire, true fire, the kind that wards and warms and *witnesses*. It raced down the slopes and through the dead dragon-bones threaded beneath Vellharrow and out along the old covenant-lines to every border-stone in the realm, and where it passed, the Hollow King's army came apart—not burned, nothing so kind—simply *emptied*, their stolen shadow pouring out of them like water from punctured skins, leaving behind confused, ordinary people blinking in sudden light and wondering why their hands smelled of ash.

The King himself lasted longer. He turned toward me as the fire took his court, and for a moment—just a moment—I saw what was left under the hollow: a mage, exiled and starving, who had once loved the stars enough to be broken by losing them.

Then the flame reached him, and he came apart into ash and a wind that smelled faintly of libraries, and he was gone, and I envied him a little, and hated myself for it, and kept singing anyway because the song wasn't finished.

The last verse goes upward.

I felt it leave me—my final words, my truest ones, streaming up through the rekindled fire into the wounded sky. And above us, in the great black bowl of heaven, the stars caught.

One. Then ten. Then thousands, reigniting like a city lighting its lamps at dusk, each one a dragon-egg relit, each one a promise kept. The light fell over the mountain and over Orsen, who was still breathing—still breathing, gods be thanked, one hand pressed to his wound and the other reaching blindly for me—and over Ember-that-Was, whose scales began, at long last, to glow.

She rose. All of her, the whole impossible length of her, wings unfolding against the returned stars, and her fire roared back into her the way spring comes back to a valley—all at once and then gradually and then everywhere.

*Paid,* she said, in the deep tongue, in my own voice now, my voice living in her mouth. *Paid in full, little tongue. Sleep now. You have earned the silence.*

I tried to answer her.

Nothing came.

I opened my mouth and shaped the words—I could feel them, every one, lined up and ready—and what emerged was breath. Just breath. The empty house she'd promised me, doors open, rooms bare, no one home.

I sat down hard on the crater's rim, next to Orsen, who was laughing and crying and pressing his forehead against my shoulder, saying my name over and over—*Tamsin, Tamsin, Tamsin*—and I couldn't say it back. I couldn't say anything back. I would never say anything back.

The stars wheeled overhead, bright and countless and utterly indifferent, and somewhere below us the world was waking up saved, and I put my hand in Orsen's and my other hand on Ember's warm flank and I wept without sound, because sound was no longer mine to make.

But here is the thing no one tells you about giving your voice away:

It doesn't die. It just moves.

And sometimes, in the deep places of the night, when the fire is low and the stars are watching, I can feel it out there—singing without me, in a hundred thousand throats, telling the whole listening world stories about a charm-seller from Vellharrow who paid her debts.

And sometimes, just sometimes, it sings back.
