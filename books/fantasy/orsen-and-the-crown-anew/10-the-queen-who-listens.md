## 10. The Queen Who Listens

They gave me a crown and I spent the first week trying to give it back.

Not literally—though I did leave it on the council table twice, once on purpose and once because I'd forgotten it was there, which the chroniclers recorded as *a gesture of profound humility* rather than what it actually was: a man who kept sitting down without checking his head. The realm had decided, in its infinite wisdom, that the man who burned his oath in front of three hundred witnesses and then helped kill a Hollow King was exactly the sort of person you wanted holding the reins. Nobody asked me. That's the thing about thrones. They're like debts. They find you whether or not you signed for them.

So now I am Regent Orsen Hale, Warden of Nothing, Keeper of a Realm Half-Assembled, and every morning I wake in a palace that still smells faintly of ash and pretend that this was somehow my idea.

The mornings are the best part, actually. Because the mornings are when she comes home.

* * *

Tamsin Ravensworth does not live in the palace. They made that clear before the ashes of the Hollow King had finished cooling, and they have never once wavered from it. They keep rooms above the old temple—the one where I cornered them with a drawn blade and a lie already half-built in my mouth—and they ride down to the city each day on a dragon the size of a fishing skiff, because apparently if you're going to be silent royalty you may as well arrive like weather.

Her name is Serevain, though Tamsin signs her name as something more like *grey-wind-that-remembers*, and she is the first of the new brood hatched under the covenant. Small, as dragons go. Still large enough to eat my horse, which she has threatened to do twice, and both times Tamsin laughed—a soundless laugh, shoulders shaking, eyes bright—and I laughed too, because what else do you do when your life has become a farce written by someone with a cruel sense of humor and excellent instincts for romance?

Tamsin cannot speak. I want to write that plainly, the way they would want it written: not tragically, not poetically, just *plainly*. Their voice went into the fire at Cinderfall, poured out word by word in every beast-tongue at once, and the flame took it and roared back across the world and lit the stars again, and the price was paid in full and without refund. What remains is their hands, quick and eloquent as birds; the dragons, who carry their words to any ear that will listen; and a silence that is somehow more articulate than most people's shouting.

I have learned the hand-speech. Badly, at first—I once signed *good morning* as *your mother is a wet dog*, which Serevain found so funny she fell off the temple roof—but I've learned it. We talk in the evenings, on the balcony, our fingers moving through the lamplight while below us Vellharrow rebuilds itself street by street, ward by ward, this time with the dragons' blessing woven into the stones instead of their bones ground up beneath them.

Last night Tamsin signed: *You wore the crown today.*

*I did,* I admitted.

*Did it fit?*

*Crowns don't fit. That's how you know they're crowns.*

They smiled—that crooked half-smile I'd have crossed the Sundering itself to protect—and then signed something slower, more careful: *You could refuse it. The council would listen. You've earned rest.*

And here is the thing I have never said aloud, because my hands are clumsy where theirs are fluent, and because some truths only come out sideways: I don't want rest. I want this. I want the tedious council sessions and the petitions about grain tariffs and the endless argument about whether the eastern wards should be keyed to moonrise or dawn. I want all of it because it's *ours*—because we broke the old world open and found the rot inside it, and now we get to build one that doesn't grind up living things to fuel its walls. Refusing the work would feel like walking away from the only thing I've ever done right.

But try signing *I love rebuilding a kingdom with you* without your hands shaking. Go on. Try it.

* * *

The anniversary came in autumn, a year to the night since the stars went out.

We marked it quietly, at Tamsin's insistence. No parade—they had endured enough parades to last several lifetimes, and besides, they'd signed, *the last time people celebrated me in public I lost my voice, let's not tempt fate.* So instead there was a small gathering at Cinderfall: the surviving wardens who hadn't tried to kill us, a delegation of mages no longer required to hide, a handful of dragons perched along the caldera rim like gargoyles who'd decided to take an interest in municipal affairs.

And Ember-that-Was, ancient beyond reckoning, her fire banked low but steady now, tended by the covenant the way you tend a hearth you intend to keep forever.

She spoke to me that evening. Not through Tamsin—directly, mind to mind, which she reserves for occasions when she wants to make a point about my character.

*You are staring at them again, little warden.*

"I'm supervising," I said.

*You are staring. There is a difference. Supervision involves looking away occasionally.*

Ember's voice in my skull feels like standing near a forge—warm, vast, faintly amused in the way of very old beings watching very young ones fumble. I'd stopped arguing with her around month two.

"They gave their voice for all of this," I said quietly. "Some days I think they regret it."

*Do you?*

"That's not—"

*Answer.*

I watched Tamsin across the caldera, signing with a young mage, patient and precise, correcting the child's grammar with a gentleness I had never once seen in any teacher of my youth. The wind moved their hair. The new stars—brighter than the old ones, Ember swears this isn't favoritism—caught in their eyes.

"No," I said. "I think some days *I* regret it. For them. I'd give anything to hear them say my name."

*Would you?*

"Anything."

*That,* said Ember, and there was something in her tone I couldn't parse—old sorrow, maybe, or old hope, or the particular patience of a creature who has watched covenants made and broken and made again—*is why the word will find you both. Words given freely always find their way back. It takes longer than anyone likes. But they come home.*

I didn't understand her then. I'm not entirely sure I understand her now. But I've learned that about dragons: they tell you true things early, and you spend years catching up.

* * *

It happened at midnight, because of course it did. The universe has a flair for the dramatic that I can only describe as vindictive.

We were on the temple roof—our spot, the one with the broken bell and the view over the low quarter where Tamsin once sold charms to people who needed them more than they knew. A year to the night since the sky went dark. Below us, the city had lit candles in every window, thousands of small flames answering the stars overhead, and the sight of it was almost too much: a whole city saying *we remember, we remember, we remember* in the only language everyone speaks.

Tamsin sat close against my side, their hands folded in their lap, watching the sky. I was watching them, because I am predictable and Ember delights in mocking me for it.

And then a star flared.

Not one of the new ones. This was different—a single point of light above the northern ridge that brightened, held, brightened again, like a pulse. Like a heartbeat. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep after a very long time.

Serevain's head snapped up from the street below. Across the city, I felt rather than saw every dragon go still at once, a silence rolling outward like a stone dropped in water.

And Tamsin—

Tamsin went rigid. Their breath caught. Their hands flew up—not signing, not quite, fingers trembling through shapes too fast and too raw for grammar—and their eyes went wide and wet and fixed on that flaring star as if it were speaking directly into them.

Then they turned to me, and their hands moved, slow and shaking:

*I heard it. Orsen. I heard—* They stopped. Started again. *One word. In my head. Like before, like the beast-tongues, but—it wasn't a dragon. It was older. It said—*

Their fingers stilled. When they resumed, each sign was deliberate, carved out of the air like something being set down carefully so it wouldn't break:

*Home.*

The star pulsed once more and dimmed—not gone, never gone, but settled, like a lamp trimmed for the night. Around us the city's candles flickered in a wind that smelled, impossibly, of mountain air and deep places and something green growing where nothing had grown in a hundred years.

Tamsin was crying. Not grieving—I knew their grief by now, knew its shape and weight. This was something else. This was the face of someone hearing a voice they'd buried, calling from a place they'd thought closed to them forever.

I took their hands. Held them still between mine.

"What does it mean?" I asked. "Is it the covenant? Is something wrong?"

They shook their head. And then, slowly, they signed—not to me, but upward, toward the star, toward whatever vast and patient thing had spoken—one-handed, the other still clasped in my grip, as if answering were not optional even now, even mute, even here:

*It means the fire remembers me. It means my voice didn't die. It means somewhere, it's still singing—and it learned my song back.*

I am not a man who weeps easily. I executed people without weeping. I burned my own oath without weeping. But sitting on that broken rooftop, holding the hands of the person who gave everything so the sky could exist, watching them answer a star in the language of the silenced—I confess it freely. I wept.

* * *

Later—much later, when the candles had burned down and the city slept and Serevain had curled herself around the temple like a very large, very smug cat—Tamsin stood at the roof's edge, looking north, toward the mountains, toward Cinderfall, toward whatever waited beyond the edge of the maps.

They signed without turning around:

*It will call again. And next time, I'll have to answer.*

"Where?" I asked. Though I already knew. Some questions you ask only to hear the truth confirmed, the way you touch a bruise to prove it's real.

They turned. Starlight on their face. That crooked smile, but underneath it, something resolute—the same look they'd worn on the road under ashen skies, the same look they'd worn walking into the fire.

*Wherever the voice comes from. Wherever the song goes.* A pause. Then, softer: *Come with me?*

As if there were ever a version of this story where I stayed behind.

I signed back the only answer that has ever been true: *Always. To the last star and past it.*

Above us, far to the north, the star flared once more—brief, bright, unmistakable.

An invitation. A summons. A beginning.

And this time, when the silence spoke, we both heard it.
