## 1. The Charm Seller's Winter

The frost came to Corvane the way debt came to poor men — early, quietly, and all at once — and by the third week of Embermonth it had claimed the city so thoroughly that even the river forgot how to move. Sable Montrose watched it from behind the fogged window of his stall in Tallowmarket, breath ghosting against the glass, and thought, not for the first time, that winter was the honest season. Winter stripped everything down to what it truly was: cold, hungry, afraid.

He liked the cold for that reason. Cold made people small. Small was safe.

"More coal," muttered the brazier at his elbow, or rather Sable muttered it, feeding the little iron basket another lump with tongs he'd owned longer than some of his customers had been alive. The stall itself was a masterwork of deliberate shabbiness — a lean-to of grey planks wedged between a chandler's shop and a cobbler who never seemed to have customers, stocked with the sort of trinkets that invited no second glance. Tin rings gone green at the seam. Ribbon in colors that had been fashionable eleven years ago. Dried lavender in twistings of brown paper. And, in a shallow tray near the front where the light could find them, a modest scatter of charms.

Luck-charms, mostly. Love-charms, occasionally. A warding charm or two, sold as "keepsakes against bad dreams," which was technically true if one's bad dreams happened to be of the thieving, feverish, or unfaithful variety.

Not one of them was magic.

That was the trick of it, the whole architecture of Sable's survival, and he had built it plank by plank over twelve careful years: nothing in the stall worked. Not really. The luck-charms were pewter horseshoes on leather cords, blessed by no one, charged by nothing. The love-charms were rose oil and suggestion. The dream-wards were dried wormwood, which did genuinely keep moths away, and moths were at least something. If an Inquisitor tore the stall apart board by board, dipped every trinket in reveal-salts and read every knot of thread, they would find exactly what ten thousand other peddlers in Corvane sold: junk, sentiment, and the ordinary human appetite for believing tomorrow might be kinder than today.

The magic lived elsewhere. The magic lived in Sable's hands, in the small warm weight of them, in the way he could hold a charm for three heartbeats and pour a little of himself into it — a whisper of intent, a thread of the old craft — before wrapping it in its paper and handing it over with both hands and a smile that said, plainly, *this is only tin, friend, but tin is lucky if you want it to be.*

He hadn't done it in months. He told himself that often enough that it had nearly become true.

* * *

The noblewoman came at mid-morning, when the market was at its greyest and the snow had settled into that fine, sifting fall that didn't so much land as hang in the air like doubt.

Sable saw her before she saw him — a habit, not a gift, though these days the two were hard to tell apart. She came up Tallow Row under a maid's umbrella, which fooled no one; the maid walked two paces behind her like a soldier guarding a retreat, and the woman herself wore a cloak of storm-grey wool that cost more than Sable's entire stall, roof included. Youngish. Thirty, perhaps. Pretty in the way that sleeplessness makes people pretty — hollowed, luminous, fragile as ice over moving water.

She stopped at the tray of charms and looked at them the way starving people look through bakery windows.

"Good morning," Sable said, mild as milk. He kept his hands visible on the counter. He had learned long ago that visible hands put people at ease, and ease made them talkative, and talkative people told you things you needed to know — chiefly, whether they were about to ruin your life. "Cold enough to freeze the bells, madam. Can I interest you in lavender? Keeps the moths out of furs."

"I don't have furs." Her voice was thin. Her eyes went down the row of charms and snagged, as eyes always did, on the luck-charms. "What are those?"

"Luck-charms. Pewter and leather. Very popular with sailors' wives."

"They work?"

Here it was. The question arrived a dozen times a day, and every time it arrived Sable performed the same small theater: he laughed, gently, apologetically, and spread his hands. "Madam, if I sold working charms I'd be selling them from a palace. They're keepsakes. Pretty ones, I hope. The luck is in the wanting." He tapped the tray. "Half of luck is believing you've got some. The other half is staying home when the roads are bad."

Something flickered across her face — disappointment, then something sharper underneath it, something that looked almost like hope refusing to die. She glanced over her shoulder. The maid studied the middle distance with professional blankness.

"My husband," the woman said, low, "has been having dreams."

"Dreams," said Sable, and waited. Weary vigilance was mostly waiting.

"Bad ones. About fire." Her fingers found the edge of the tray and gripped it, knuckles whitening. "Every night this week. He wakes shouting. And there's — there's a man at court, an inquisitor, everyone says he can *tell*, he can look at a person and know if they're — " She stopped herself. Swallowed. "I want a charm. A real one. I'll pay real coin."

The word *real* dropped into the space between them like a stone into a well, and Sable listened for the splash.

There it was. Faint, but there: the splash of genuine need, and beneath it, fainter still, the particular resonance of a person standing too close to the fire and pretending not to feel the heat. He had spent twelve years learning to hear that splash and walk away from it. Walk away, Sable. She's frightened, and frightened women with money and husbands at court are how men like you end up hanging from the Saltgate with their names chalked on their shirts.

But her hands were shaking. And it was cold, and she was alone in the way that mattered, and somewhere under twelve years of careful smallness there was still, damn it all, a Charmwright.

"The dreams," Sable said slowly, "are they his? Or yours?"

Her head came up sharply. For a moment the fragile prettiness cracked and something older looked out — something tired and fierce. "Mine," she whispered. "They're mine. He doesn't know. Nobody knows. But the inquisitors have been asking questions at the winter court, and Lady Vess was taken last month, and she was *nothing*, she just grew roses out of season, and now I can't sleep for fear of sleeping—" Her voice frayed to threads. "Please. I heard there was a seller in Tallowmarket. Just a rumor. Someone said the charms here were different."

Someone had said. Sable filed that away with a cold turn of his stomach. Rumors were how the Inquisition fished; rumors were baited hooks, and half of Corvane would sell their neighbor's name for a sack of coal this winter. But the woman's eyes were wet now, and her grip on the tray had gone white-knuckled, and she was whispering *please* in a market full of people who would step over her body to reach the coal-seller first.

Twelve years, Sable thought. Twelve years of being small and forgettable, and it had bought him nothing but the privilege of watching.

"Wait here," he said.

He ducked into the back of the stall, into the cramped dark where the stock chests lived, and knelt before the false bottom of the oldest one. Under the floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth inside a hollowed brick, lay his working kit: three blanks of pale horn, a spool of red silk thread, a stub of beeswax candle, and a pinch of salt in a vial. Enough to hang him twice over, if anyone ever found it. He took one blank and left the rest.

When he emerged, the woman was staring at the snow with the expression of someone counting the days remaining to her.

"This is a keepsake," Sable said clearly, loudly enough for the maid to hear, laying the horn blank on the counter along with the whole theater of his innocence. "Pewter and horn, madam, five coppers, and I make no promises, because promises are for priests and frauds."

Then, under the counter's shadow, out of every sightline but hers, he took the blank in both hands.

The craft, when he finally let it move, felt like breathing after a long held breath. Warmth gathered in his palms — not fire-warmth, nothing so dramatic, just the small deep heat of intent given form. He thought of quiet. Of doors closed against wind. Of a mind lying down to sleep like a dog circling twice before settling. Three heartbeats. Four. The horn drank it in and gave back a faint, almost imperceptible hum that only he could feel, the way a struck bell keeps humming after the sound dies.

He wrapped it in brown paper, tied it with red silk — the thread mattered, the color mattered, the old guild knots mattered, though he tied them loose and sloppy, deniable — and slid it across the counter with both hands.

"For your husband's sake, wear it openly," he said. "For yours, keep it under your pillow. And madam — " he held her eyes, and let a little of the truth show through, just a spark, just enough — "if anyone asks, you bought a pretty trinket from a peddler who talks too much. You never heard the word *different*. You never heard any word at all."

She stared at him. Understanding moved through her face like dawn through fog. "You're — "

"A peddler," said Sable pleasantly. "Five coppers."

She paid with silver and fled before he could make change, umbrella bobbing, maid scurrying, gone into the grey like a fish into deep water. Sable watched the corner where she'd vanished for a long moment, then looked down at his open palm, where the warmth of the working still lingered like a handprint in snow.

*Fool,* he told himself. *Sentimental, reckless fool.*

But his hands were steady, and for the first time in weeks the cold in his chest had loosened, just slightly, and he hated how good it felt to have been, for four heartbeats, exactly what he was.

* * *

He closed the stall early. That was the rule he'd set himself after days like this: do the thing, then vanish, then be boring for a week. Boring was armor. Boring was a second skin he'd stitched himself into, thread by thread, until sometimes he couldn't remember what he'd looked like underneath.

He wound home through the back ways of Corvane, past the chandlers' row where tallow smoke hung yellow in the frozen air, past the fountain in Coopers' Square where the water had frozen mid-spill into a sculpture like a horse rearing out of stone. Children were sliding on the trough ice, shrieking, and Sable gave them a wide berth out of an old, unexamined ache he preferred not to name.

His rooms were above a dye-shop in Tanner's Leak, three flights up, reached by stairs that smelled of vinegar and wet wool. One room. A bed, a stove, a table, a chair. On the wall above the bed, the only decoration he permitted himself: a small brass hook, empty, polished bright by years of absent touching.

Something used to hang there. He knew that the way he knew his own name — absolutely, and without detail. There was a hole in his memory shaped like a charm, and around the hole, other holes, larger, older: no mother's face, no father's voice, no childhood before the age of fourteen, when he had simply *appeared*, so far as his own recollection went, in the ruins of the Charmwrights' Guildhall with singed sleeves and no explanation he could give or get. The guildmaster had taken him in without questions. The guildmaster had taught him knots and salts and patience. The guildmaster had died six years ago and left him a ring he never wore and a grief he never unpacked.

Everyone assumed the Inquisition's purges had burned those years out of him. Trauma, the sympathetic said. Punishment, the cynical said. Sable himself had stopped asking, because asking led nowhere and nowhere was a place he'd learned to live.

He lit the stove, boiled water, made barley tea, and sat at the table with the guildmaster's ring in front of him, turning it in the lamplight. Heavy gold, worn smooth. A dragon coiled around the band, tail in mouth, and inside the band, tiny as a grain of rice, the guild-mark: a wheel with eight spokes.

Eight spokes. He'd counted them a thousand times. He'd never once wondered why eight, when every text he'd ever read described the covenant-wheel as having seven.

Tonight, for no reason he could name — unless it was the noblewoman's shaking hands, or the smell of the craft still on his fingers, or the way the snow kept falling past his window like ash from some distant, patient fire — tonight he wondered.

He fell asleep at the table with the ring under his palm, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of fire.

* * *

Two mornings later, the heralds came.

Sable was at his stall early, chipping ice off the shutters, when the drums started — not the market drums, the *court* drums, deep and slow, the rhythm that meant stop what you're doing. The crowd in Tallowmarket congealed. Down the Row came six riders in crown livery, white and black, and behind them a herald on a grey horse, unrolling a scroll the length of an arm.

"By order of His Majesty's court and the Prince Aldric, regent of the realm," the herald read, voice carrying flat and clear over the frozen square, "the Edict of Cleansing is renewed and extended. All persons bearing the tainted gift of charm-craft, hedge-magic, or any art of the old covenant are commanded to present themselves at the Inquisition houses for assessment and curing. Shelterers and concealers will share the guilt of the sheltered. Rewards are offered for true report." A pause, the scroll rattling in the cold. "The hunt does not end. The hunt does not tire. The hunt does not forget."

The words rolled over the market like a tide. Sable stood very still among the frozen faces, chisel in hand, and let his own face do what it did best: nothing.

*Curing.* As if the gift were a sickness. As if the cure weren't the block, the pyre, the long grey silence afterward. He'd heard the term explained once by a drunk warden, laughing: the Inquisition cured you of caring whether you lived.

Around him the crowd began, carefully, to breathe again. A woman pulled her child closer. A man spat, thought better of it, swallowed. Somewhere a shutter closed — softly, softly, the sound of Corvane minding its own business, which was the only business Corvane had left.

And Sable Montrose, last active member of a guild that officially had never existed, stood in his stall of harmless tin and felt the truth settle over him with the snowfall: the safe life was ending. He didn't know how he knew. He knew the way animals know weather. The noblewoman's silver in his pocket had the weight of a marked coin. The rumor that had brought her to his stall had the shape of a net. And the herald's words — *the hunt does not forget* — landed on him with the strange, specific intimacy of a sentence spoken directly into his ear.

That night he burned his ledger. Page by page, every customer, every sale, every name, fed to the stove until the room stank of ink and ash. Then he sat looking at the brass hook above his bed, empty and shining, while outside the snow went on falling, patient as an army, and somewhere across the frozen city a woman slept — or failed to sleep — with a horn charm under her pillow, humming very softly, very quietly, with borrowed fire.

Three days, he gave himself. Three days to close the stall, scatter the stock, and be gone before the net finished closing.

He was wrong about the three days.
