## 2. The Proclamation

The bells of Corvane rang wrong that morning.

Sable Montrose noticed it before he was fully awake — a discordance in the seventh stroke, a flatness where there should have been brightness, as though the metal itself had caught a chill. He lay in his narrow bed above the shop and listened, the way he listened to everything: quietly, completely, without ever appearing to listen at all. Twelve years of practice had taught him that the world announced its dangers in small ways first. A bell out of tune. A dog barking at nothing. A neighbor's shutters opening too early.

He rose, dressed in the gray wool of a man who wished to be mistaken for furniture, and went down to open the shutters of Montrose Curiosities & Sundries.

Frost had come hard in the night. It sheathed the cobbles of Lamplighter's Row in white fur and turned the gutter-ice into rows of small glass knives. His breath plumed. Across the street, old Widow Casserly was sweeping her step with the grim economy of a woman who had buried two husbands and did not intend to bury her patience as well. She nodded at him. He nodded back. Neither spoke. This was the correct amount of conversation for seven o'clock in Corvane in winter, and Sable had long ago memorized the grammar of it.

Inside, he lit the stove, put water on for tea, and took down his ledger from beneath the loose floorboard.

It was a plain book, bound in scuffed calfskin, its pages ruled in a careful hand. To anyone who found it, it would read as the accounts of an unremarkable tradesman: three copper bits for candle-wax, five for thread, a shilling owed by the cooper's wife for a tin of salve. But between those honest lines ran another ledger entirely, written in a cipher of his own devising — a charm sold here, a ward renewed there, a fever-knot untangled for the fishmonger's daughter on Wren Street. Eleven entries this winter alone. Eleven small acts of outlawed kindness, each one sufficient, under the Edicts, to see him hanged or worse.

He had told himself, as he told himself every morning, that he would stop. That the ledger would stay closed. That being small and forgettable was a kind of survival, and survival was not cowardice — it was arithmetic.

Then Mrs. Ellery would come with her arthritic hands, or the cooper's wife with a child burning with marsh-fever, and the arithmetic would fail, the way it always failed, because Sable had never once in his life managed to be the kind of man who could watch suffering and do sums about it instead.

He poured his tea. The steam curled upward in a slow spiral — too slow, too deliberate, the way steam moved when he was near and unguarded. He frowned at it and thought *down*, and it obeyed, collapsing into ordinary wisps. Small magics leaked out of him constantly, like heat through a bad window. He'd spent half his life learning to patch that window.

Outside, the bells began again. And this time they did not stop.

* * *

By noon, every bell in Corvane was ringing, and the streets were flowing toward Meridian Square the way water flows toward a drain.

Sable stood at his shop door with a broom in his hands, performing the universal theater of a man who has nothing better to do than sweep frost off clean stone. The crowd moved past him in thickening streams: fishwives with their baskets, apprentices running on errands they'd already abandoned, clerks with ink-stained fingers tucked into armpits against the cold. There was a strange holiday quality to it, an unease dressed up as festival. People talked in low voices. Children were lifted onto shoulders.

"Proclamation," said Widow Casserly, materializing beside him with the inevitability of weather. "First one since the old king died. You coming?"

"I've stock to mind."

"Stock will keep." She squinted up at him, eyes bright and black as currants. "A man ought to know what his king wants of him."

"He's not my king yet," Sable said mildly. "Prince Aldric holds the regency."

"Aldric, then." She spat, delicately, into the gutter. "Worse."

She shuffled off toward the square, and Sable stood a moment longer with his broom, feeling the wrongness settle over the street like a change in pressure before a storm. Every instinct he possessed — instincts honed not by courage but by twelve years of careful hiding — told him to bar his door, bank his fire, and wait until the city had finished whatever it was doing.

But he had learned one other lesson in twelve years, and it was this: a man who does not look at the thing hunting him will never see it coming.

He leaned the broom inside the doorway, locked the shop, and followed the crowd.

* * *

Meridian Square had been built for grandeur and used for commerce, and today it held both awkwardly. The bronze equestrian statue of King Aldous the Founder wore a cap of snow; around its base, the market stalls had been cleared back in a wide ring, and into that ring rode a column of the Crown Guard, thirty strong, their lacquered armor dull as pewter under the gray sky. Behind them came six heralds in the royal blue-and-silver, mounted on white horses that steamed like kettles, and behind *them* — and this was what made the crowd go quiet, all at once, the way a room goes quiet —

Behind them came the Inquisition.

There were only four of them, but four was enough. They rode black horses and wore black coats cut severe and simple, no braid, no ornament, only the inverted flame of their order stitched in gray thread at the collar. Their faces were the worst part. Not cruel. Not even stern. Simply *attentive*, in the way that owls are attentive, and everyone in the square understood, without being told, that these men and women were not here to read a proclamation. They were here to collect.

At the statue's base, a platform had been erected overnight. A herald dismounted, climbed it, and unfurled a scroll long enough to reach the snow.

His voice, amplified by some device or artifice Sable chose not to examine too closely, rolled across the square:

*"Hear now the word of Aldric, Prince Regent of Veyra, Protector of the Realm, in the matter of the Edict of Cleansing."*

The name landed on the crowd like a dropped stone. Cleansing. Someone near Sable whispered the word to someone else, and it passed outward through the frozen air, mouth to ear to mouth, gathering weight.

*"Let it be known throughout the kingdom and its holdings: that magic, in all its forms and manifestations, is declared a corruption of body and soul; that those who bear the gift, whether by birth, bargain, or practice, are afflicted persons in need of cure; and that the Crown, in its mercy, offers that cure freely to all who present themselves."*

*Mercy.* Sable felt his jaw tighten. He knew what the Crown's mercy looked like. Everyone did. There had been the weaver in Marrowgate who surrendered voluntarily, two years ago, and come back three months later able to speak only his own name. There had been the others who came back not at all, whose families received sealed letters and a sum of money and instructions to move along.

*"Let it further be known: that any gifted person who conceals their affliction commits treason against the realm; that any person who shelters, aids, trades with, or fails to report such a person shares their guilt; and that the penalty for such treason is forfeiture of goods, and of life."*

A sound moved through the crowd — not a gasp, exactly. Something quieter and worse. The sound of several thousand people simultaneously recalculating their relationships to their neighbors.

Sable did not move. He had learned stillness the way other men learned swordsmanship, and he stood in it now, gray and forgettable among grays, while the herald's voice went on.

*"Informers presenting true and verified reports shall be rewarded from the treasury. Denunciation is a duty. Vigilance is a virtue. So speaks the Prince Regent, by the grace of the Covenant—"*

And there the herald paused. Just slightly. A hitch, a breath, the smallest stumble — and Sable saw him glance, quickly, at one of the black-coated Inquisitors below, as though checking whether he was permitted to say the next words.

"—by the grace of the Covenant," the herald repeated, steadier, "*and in the name of the Crown, so let it be heard in every town and every hall, from the mountains to the sea.*"

The scroll rolled shut. The trumpets sounded. And the square exhaled into a roar of noise that was not quite cheering and not quite anything else.

That was when they seized the singer.

* * *

She had been performing at the edge of the square since before the heralds arrived — a thin girl of perhaps seventeen, wrapped in a coat too big for her, singing ballads for coppers with a voice that cut through the cold like a wire. Sable had half-listened to her while watching the platform. She was good. Better than good. There was something in her voice that made the crowd linger, made the cold feel less cold, made tired faces turn soft —

And Sable had realized, with a lurch of horror, that it wasn't only training. He knew the taste of worked charm the way a locksmith knows the touch of a false key. The girl was weaving warmth into her songs. Faintly, clumsily, desperately — the way a starving person steals bread. She was gifted, and she was using the smallest edge of her gift to earn her supper, because the alternative was hunger, and until this morning the risk had seemed worth it, the way it always seems worth it right up until it isn't.

Two Crown Guards took her arms at the end of the last verse.

She didn't scream at first. That was the terrible part. She looked confused, the way people do when the world breaks a rule they thought was solid — *they don't take singers, singers aren't taken* — and then a black coat detached itself from the Inquisitors' group and walked toward her with unhurried steps, and the confusion left her face all at once, and she began to scream.

"*I'm not — I didn't — please, please, I only sing, I only sing—*"

"It's the warming," someone near Sable murmured. "You could feel it. Everyone could feel it."

"You could feel it?" said another voice, sharper. "Then why didn't you report it?"

Silence. The first speaker found somewhere else to look.

They dragged her toward a wagon — not the guard wagons, Sable noted, but a covered thing painted gray with no windows, the kind of wagon that had begun appearing in Corvane over the past year and which no one had ever seen arrive or depart. The girl twisted in the guards' grip. Her cap fell off. Her hair came loose, dark and wild, and for one moment her eyes swept the crowd — hundreds of faces, hundreds of eyes, all of them carefully looking elsewhere —

Her gaze snagged on Sable's.

Perhaps it was nothing. Perhaps it was simply that he was the one face in the crowd that hadn't looked away fast enough. But for a single suspended instant, across forty feet of trampled snow, the girl looked at him with the particular, knowing desperation of one gifted creature recognizing another, and her voice cracked into something that was not words at all — a raw, rising note that bent strangely in the air, that made the nearest torch-flames lean toward her like flowers toward sun —

And then an Inquisitor raised one hand, almost lazily, and the note died. The flames straightened. The girl sagged between the guards like a cut marionette, and they threw her into the gray wagon, and the door slammed, and the lock turned, and the crowd made the sound crowds make when they want very badly to believe that nothing has happened.

Sable's heart was going like a hammer. He kept his face empty. He kept his feet planted. Inside his chest, behind his ribs, his own suppressed gift writhed in sympathy with the girl's dying note, straining toward it like a dog toward a whistle, and he clamped down on it with twelve years of practiced force, *still, be still, be nothing,* until it subsided into the dull ache that lived permanently beneath his breastbone, the ache he had long ago stopped noticing and could not stop noticing now.

The Inquisitor who had silenced her turned slowly, scanning the crowd. Gray eyes. Young, for the office — perhaps thirty — with a narrow face and a mouth that looked as though it had been designed for smiling and repurposed. The gaze crossed Sable like a searchlight and moved on.

Mostly.

For half a heartbeat — less — it hesitated. A flicker. The way a compass needle trembles before settling.

Then the Inquisitor turned away, and the column formed up, and the gray wagon groaned away through the south gate of the square with its silent cargo, and the crowd, released, burst into motion and chatter as though waking from a shared dream.

Sable walked home. He did not run. Running was memorable.

* * *

The shop was cold when he entered it. He did not light the stove.

He stood in the middle of his own front room — the shelves of harmless curiosities, the drawers of buttons and thread, the counter worn smooth by ten years of his elbows — and he looked at it all the way a man looks at a house he already knows he is leaving.

*Any person who shelters, aids, trades with, or fails to report.*

Mrs. Ellery's hands. The cooper's wife's daughter. Eleven entries in a ciphered ledger, and every one of those customers knew — not certainly, not provably, but in the way neighbors know things — that Montrose's charms *worked*. Under the new Edict, eleven households were eleven nooses, and every day he remained was a day he tightened them.

And beyond that — the larger arithmetic, the one he had been refusing to do for years — the hunt had changed. He had watched the Inquisitors in the square with a professional eye, and what he had seen frightened him more than the proclamation itself. Four of them, for a mere reading of an edict. Wagons without windows. A herald afraid to say the word *Covenant*. Whatever the Crown was doing, it was not merely sweeping up hedge-witches and charm-sellers. Something had shifted in the deep machinery of the kingdom, some great weight moving in the dark, and small creatures like Sable were not the quarry. They were the disturbance in the grass that revealed where the larger prey was running.

He went to the hearth. He knelt. He pulled up the loose floorboard and took out the ledger.

For a long moment he held it on his knees, thumbing the worn calfskin, reading the ghost of his own handwriting. Ten years of entries. Ten years of small mercies, each one a crime, each one the only proof that he had been, in however modest a fashion, a Charmwright of the Guild — the last active member of a brotherhood that had numbered four hundred when he was a boy, before the purges, before the guildhall burned, before the world decided that what he was made of was filth.

Burning it was surrender. Burning it was admitting the Guild was truly dead.

Burning it was also the only sane thing left to do.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud, to the book, to the dead, to the fourteen-year-old apprentice he had once been who had believed the Guild would outlast kingdoms. Then he fed the ledger page by page into the cold hearth, struck flint to the kindling already laid there — he was not a sentimental enough fool to burn documents without kindling ready — and watched ten years of quiet goodness curl into orange light and rise as smoke.

When it was done, he washed his hands. It was not a magical necessity. It was a human one.

Then he worked.

He worked fast and without ceremony, the way he had not allowed himself to work in years. A forgetting-charm laid over the shop's threshold, keyed to his own absence, so that passersby would find the shuttered premises dull and slip past without curiosity. A dissolving-ward on the hidden drawer where the last of his genuine stock sat — three luck-charms, a fever-knot, a ward-stone — set to crumble to ash if touched by any hand but his. His coin, sewn into the lining of his coat in uneven weights so no search would find a suspicious purse. His mother's ring — the one thing he owned that mattered for reasons that had nothing to do with magic — onto the cord around his neck, beneath his shirt, against his skin.

Last, he took down the sign. *Montrose Curiosities & Sundries.* The paint was peeling; he had been meaning to repaint it for two years. He carried it inside, laid it flat on the counter, and stood looking at it for a long time.

This was the moment, he understood, where the safe life ended — not with trumpets, not with proclamations, but with a man holding a wooden sign in a cold shop, deciding.

He thought of the girl in the gray wagon. He thought of the herald stumbling over the word *Covenant*. He thought of the ache under his breastbone, which had never once, in twelve years, let him forget what he was, no matter how small and forgettable he made himself.

"They'll cure me anyway," he said to the empty shop. "Or they'll hang me. Or worse." His voice sounded strange in the silence — rusty, as if it hadn't been used for true speech in years. "So I may as well be guilty of something."

He wrapped the sign in sacking and slid it behind the loose floorboard, in the space where the ledger had lived. Let whoever came find an empty hole and an ordinary shop. By tomorrow night, Montrose Curiosities & Sundries would be a memory with no keeper, and by spring, no one in Lamplighter's Row would quite remember what had been sold there, or by whom. He was good at being forgotten. It was, he reflected grimly, pulling his coat on and banking the stove dead, the one great talent the Guild had never needed to teach him.

He went out the back, through the yard, over the wall into Tanner's Close, and turned north — away from the gates, away from the roads, toward the one place in Corvane where no Inquisitor would ever think to look for a living man.

The ruins of the Charmwrights' Guildhall still stood in the old quarter above the river. Twelve years burned and roofless and cursed in the public mind, haunted by ghosts that were only pigeons and bad memories. No one went there. No one *dared* go there.

Which meant, Sable thought, climbing the frozen slope with the wind cutting his face and the bells of Corvane still tolling behind him in that wrong, flat key — which meant it was the safest place in the city.

And which meant, though he could not yet have said why the certainty settled on him so heavily, so finally, like snow — that something there had been waiting for him.

He did not look back at the shop. The safe life was behind him, and it was already burning, and there was nothing in it left to save.
