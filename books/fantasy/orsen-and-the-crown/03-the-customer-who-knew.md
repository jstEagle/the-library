## 3. The Customer Who Knew

The knock came at dusk, three days after the noblewoman left with her luck-charm, and Sable knew before he opened the door that it was trouble. Trouble knocked politely. It always had.

He had spent those three days the way he spent most of them: small, quiet, forgettable. He had sold two more charms — a fever-knot to a fishwife, a salt-thread bracelet to a boy who wanted courage for his first voyage — and he had kept his voice low and his hands chapped-looking and his eyes down. That was the whole art of survival in Corvane now. Not hiding the gift; anyone could hide a gift. Hiding the *shape* of yourself, sanding off every edge that might catch an inquisitor's eye, until you were nothing but a tired shopkeeper with cold hands and a wife who'd died of lung-rot and no children worth mentioning. He had built that man the way a mason builds a wall, stone by stone, and he lived inside him.

But the knock was polite, and polite knocks at dusk were never customers.

He looked through the shutter's gap and saw four men in the grey-and-silver of the Inquisition, and behind them, wrapped in sable fur that matched his own unfortunate name, Lady Vessia Corlowe. She wasn't looking at the door. She was looking at her gloves, turning them over in her hands as though they were the most fascinating things in Veyra, and something about the set of her shoulders — the careful stillness of them — told him she was ashamed.

Good, he thought, with a bitterness that surprised him. Be ashamed. Wear it under your furs where no one can see it, the way I wore my gift for twelve years.

They would not have marked the shop yet. If they had, there would be no knocking. He moved through the back room on feet he'd trained to be silent, gathered the go-bag he'd packed the night the heralds read the Edict — he had known, even burning his ledger, even telling himself he had time, that time was a charm no one could sell — and slipped out through the coal cellar into the alley behind the tannery.

The stink of it covered everything else. He walked north at a widow's pace, past the rendering yards, past the shrine where someone had smashed the little statue of the Kindly Mother because her gentle magic smelled too much like the old ways, and only when he reached the mouth of Tanner's Stair did he let himself look back.

Torches were moving through the streets below, converging on his block like iron filings drawn to a lodestone. They had marked the shop. Which meant she had marked the charm — brought it to someone who knew what genuine work looked like, or worse, had been watched from the moment she bought it. Either way, the small gray life was dead. He had buried it himself, in a way, the night he burned his ledger. But knowing a thing is dying and watching it die are different griefs.

He climbed.

* * *

Corvane's bell-towers rose above the city like the fingers of a drowned giant reaching up through fog. There were nine of the great ones, and dozens of lesser spires, and between them ran a web of rooftops, buttresses, clotheslines, and gutter-bridges that the watch never bothered with because respectable people did not travel by roof. Sable had learned the web as a boy — a boy whose childhood began, as far as he could ever remember, at fourteen, which was a thought he kept in a locked room at the back of his mind and did not open — and his body remembered it even when his mind grew tired.

He went up the outside of Saint Ferrel's like a spider, fingers finding the old handholds worn smooth by generations of bell-ringers' apprentices, and came out onto the lead roof with the whole city spread beneath him, smoking and golden in the last light. Somewhere down there, men were kicking in his door and finding a cold hearth and an empty coal cellar. Let them. He had left nothing. Twelve years of leaving nothing, and tonight it finally paid.

Except that it didn't feel like payment. It felt like loss. He crouched against the bell-cage with the wind pulling at his coat and let himself feel it for exactly ten breaths — the shop, the regulars, the fishwife's fever-knot that would keep working whether he was there to renew it or not (it would; he built them to outlast him) — and then he stood and ran.

The rooftops took him east. Slate, then tile, then the broad lead plain of the Wool Exchange, then a gap of twelve feet over Cordwainer Lane that he crossed without breaking stride because he had crossed it a hundred times, once carrying a basket of oranges. Below, the city glowed and muttered. Above, the first stars were coming out through tears in the cloud, and for one absurd moment Sable felt almost free — the old feeling, the young feeling, the one from before the Edict, when running across the roofs had been play and not flight.

Then a crossbow bolt shattered the tiles a hand's width from his heel.

He threw himself flat and rolled, and saw them: three shapes on the tower behind, grey cloaks, lantern-shields that threw their light forward and left the bearers dark. Wardens. Inquisitor Hale's wardens, if the cut of the cloaks meant anything, and it meant everything, because Hale did not send three men after smugglers. Hale sent three men after *gifted*.

How? He had been so careful. The coal cellar, the tannery stink, the widow's pace — how had they —

The answer arrived with a second bolt, and it was simple: they hadn't followed him at all. They had *herded* him. The torches below weren't searching; they were driving, closing street by street, funneling anything that fled upward onto the roofs where the wardens waited. Someone had told them how a charmwright would run. Someone who knew the roofs. Someone who knew *him*.

Lady Vessia's ashamed shoulders floated up in his memory, and he snarled at himself. Sentiment. He had seen her shame and read it as innocence, because he wanted the world to still contain innocent people. The world did not. The world contained people who bought luck-charms and sold luck-charmers, and the coin she'd paid him was probably already sitting in an evidence tray with a warden's mark chalked beside it.

He ran.

The chase went on for the better part of an hour, and it was not a chase so much as a slow closing of a fist. Every route he knew, they had anticipated. The rope-bridge over Chandler's Wynd was cut. The loose slates on the dye-house were guarded. Twice he doubled back through his own trail and twice found lantern-light already blooming behind him. They weren't trying to catch him. They were walking him somewhere, patient as shepherds, and the somewhere was the Bell-Tower of the Ninefold Hours — the tallest thing in Corvane, isolated by a moat of empty rooftops, with one way up and one way down.

He understood it fully when he reached the tower's base and found the last bridge gone, its anchor-line sliced clean, the frayed end swinging in the wind like a hanged man's rope. Behind him, lanterns. Ahead, ninety feet of bare stone with old handholds that he could climb but not quickly, not with archers above.

They were above. He could see their silhouettes on the belfry gallery, three of them, waiting with the terrible patience of men being paid by the hour.

Sable pressed his back against the cold stone and made himself breathe. His gift stirred in his chest, the old warmth, the guild-warmth, begging to be used — a veil-charm would blur him, a wind-knot would carry him to the next roof, a dozen tricks the Charmwrights had perfected over three centuries of outlawry. And every one of them would announce him to whatever scrying-glass or gift-sniffer they had trained on this tower. Use the gift in front of the Inquisition and you confirmed everything. You died as proof.

So he did the hardest thing a charmwright can do, harder than any spell: nothing. He stood in the dark with his hands empty and his heart slamming and let them come down for him.

Boots on stone. Two of them descending the gallery stair, unhurried. A third holding the high ground with a cocked crossbow. The lantern-light swelled around the corner of the tower base, and a voice said, conversationally:

"There's no shame in it. Come down, keep your hands visible, and the Inquisitor will treat you gently. He always does, for the cooperative ones." A pause. "The uncooperative ones, we cure anyway. It just takes longer."

Sable looked at the speaker — a broad man with a warden's badge and a face like a closed door — and felt something settle in him, calm and cold. So this was how it ended. Not in fire, not in glory, but on a freezing tower with a liar offering him gentleness. His guildmaster — the memory surfaced unbidden, bearded and half-remembered, smelling of beeswax and solder — had said once: *A charm is only as strong as the truth in it, lad. Same goes for men.* This man's truth was rotten, and Sable could smell it, and somehow that helped.

"I'll come quietly," Sable said, and raised his hands.

And that was when the third warden, the one on the gallery above, said: "Sir. Orders say the prisoner is to be taken alive and *unmarked*. Inquisitor Hale's compliments."

"Unmarked," the broad man repeated, annoyed. "Then put the bow away, you idiot, before you—"

"Those aren't Hale's orders," said a new voice.

It came from the gallery, clear and level, a woman's voice with an edge of authority that sat strangely on its youth. Boots crossed the lead above them. A figure leaned over the balustrade into the lantern-light: young, dark-haired, wearing the silver sash of a full inquisitor — not a warden, an *inquisitor*, and far too young for the rank unless she had earned it in ways that left scars.

"Inquisitor Vale," the broad man said, and his closed-door face opened slightly, into something wary. "We weren't told—"

"You weren't told because the order came an hour ago and couriers are slow. The prisoner is wanted for questioning regarding the Corlowe matter. Unmarked, uninjured, and *unspoiled* — which means no gift-suppressants, no mind-tinctures, nothing that would muddy him before examination." She descended the stair into the lantern-light, and Sable got his first real look at her: sharp-boned, tired-eyed, with a fresh scar along her jaw that she wore the way other people wore jewelry. "You were about to take him in shackles. Shackles chafe. Chafing leaves marks. Do you see the problem?"

The broad man saw the problem. Wardens always did, eventually; the hierarchy of the Inquisition was a machine for producing problems and assigning blame. He unlocked the shackles he'd been carrying with bad grace.

Inquisitor Vale turned to Sable, and here was the strange thing, the thing he would turn over in his mind for weeks afterward: she looked at him *directly*, eye to eye, and her eyes were doing arithmetic he couldn't read. Everyone else in the Inquisition looked at gifted folk the way you look at a rat in the pantry — with disgust managed into procedure. She looked at him like a person reading a letter written in a language she pretended not to know.

"Name," she said.

"Sable Montrose."

"Occupation."

"Charm-seller. The legal kind. Trinkets and blessings, nothing—"

"The legal kind don't run across rooftops for an hour." She said it without heat, almost without interest, and then, quieter, in a tone the wardens couldn't quite catch: "The coal cellar behind the tannery. Clever. Most of them use the drains."

He stared at her. She stared back, expressionless, and touched two fingers briefly to her own temple — a gesture so small, so idle, that he might have imagined it. Except that it was the old guild sign, the one every Charmwright used to give another: *I see you. We are the same.* It hadn't been used in twelve years. It was supposed to be extinct, like the guild itself, like the dragons, like every soft thing the crown had burned.

His heart stopped and restarted sideways.

"Bind his wrists," Vale said aloud, to the wardens. "Front, not back. He'll need balance on the descent." And then, as the broad man stepped forward with the cord, she frowned suddenly at the eastern sky, where clouds had begun to boil up black and fast, a storm rising out of a clear evening with a speed that no honest weather could manage. "What in the Nine is that?"

Every head turned. Every lantern tilted toward the impossible storm — and in that half-second of collective blindness, Maren Vale did three things with the speed of long practice: she stepped between Sable and the wardens, she cut the cord the warden had begun to tie with a flick of her belt-knife that looked like clumsiness, and she said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the gallery above and no farther:

"He went over the west edge. After him! *After him!*"

And Sable, understanding nothing except that he had been given a door, went over the west edge.

There were handholds. Of course there were; he'd climbed this tower as a boy, and his body kept its own ledger of secrets the way his mind never could. He dropped the last ten feet, hit the rooftop rolling, and ran blind through the rising wind while behind him the wardens' boots hammered eastward, chasing a fugitive who existed nowhere except in an inquisitor's lie.

* * *

He didn't stop until the river district, until he was crouched in the lee of a customs shed with the black water sliding past a yard away and his lungs full of knives. The impossible storm broke overhead then, rain hammering the tin roof like applause, and Sable sat in the dark and shook — not from cold, though the cold was real, but from the arithmetic he couldn't finish.

She had let him go. An inquisitor of the crown of Veyra, sworn to the Edict of Cleansing, had looked at a captured charmwright, given the guild sign — the *extinct* guild sign — and lied to her own wardens to free him. People did not do that. People especially did not do that for strangers. Which meant she was not doing it for a stranger. Which meant she knew him, or knew *of* him, or knew something about him that he did not know about himself — and the locked room at the back of his mind, the one marked *before age fourteen*, rattled on its hinges.

Rain ran off his chin. Somewhere across the river, the bell of the Ninefold Hours rang the midnight hour, and the sound rolled over the drowning city like a warning.

He had a sealed letter in his coat, taken from the ruined Guildhall, written in his dead guildmaster's hand, naming three relic-keys and speaking of a covenant failing and a dragon chained beneath a mountain. He had an enemy who herded charmwrights like sheep. And now he had an inquisitor who gave extinct signs in the dark.

Three days ago he had been a small gray man selling luck to nervous nobles. The small gray man was dead on a bell-tower, and what remained was something with a mission he hadn't asked for and a debt he couldn't account for.

Maren Vale. He turned the name over like a coin of unknown minting. Whatever game she was playing, he owed her his freedom, and in Sable's experience debts were the only charms that always came due.

He pulled his collar up, checked the letter's oilskin wrapping one more time, and slipped down toward the water, where the ebb tide would carry him out of Corvane before dawn — east, toward the tidal flats, toward the drowned chapel, toward the first key.

Behind him, on the far bank, a lantern kindled in the Inquisition tower's highest window, and held steady, and did not follow.
