## 4. Ashes of the Guildhall

The Charmwrights' Guildhall had once been the second-finest building in Corvane. Sable remembered that the way a man remembers a face in a dream — not the details, but the certainty of them. He had been fourteen when the guild fell, and in the twelve years since he had walked past its gates a hundred times, always on the far side of the street, always with his collar up, always telling himself that one day he would go back.

One day had arrived at last, and it had come because every other door in the city had closed.

He came at dusk, when the lamplighters were still on the main avenues and the streets of the Weavers' Quarter stood empty as a drained cup. Snow had fallen through the afternoon and lay soft on the ruin, and for a few minutes, in the blue half-light, the old hall almost looked like itself again — the white softening the scorch-marks on the lintel, the snow filling the cracks in the steps, the broken sigil above the door wearing a hood of white like a mourner.

The sigil had been a compass rose woven through with a thread of gold, the mark of the Charmwrights: *we mend what the world has broken.* Someone had taken a chisel to the gold thread long ago. Sable had always assumed it was the Inquisition. Standing there now, breath smoking, he found himself hoping it had been. Better vandals than something worse.

The doors had been sealed with wax and lead after the guild's dissolution — the Inquisition's way of marking a dead thing — but the seal had been broken years since, and the right-hand door hung ajar on a single hinge, groaning when he shouldered it. The sound went through him like a hand through water. Twelve years, and the hall still knew his weight.

Inside, the dark smelled of ash and pigeon and old rain. He stood a long while just past the threshold, letting his eyes adjust, letting his heart do the opposite.

The great workroom had been the heart of the guild. He could still map it with his eyes closed: the long benches along the north wall where apprentices learned to bind a luck-knot without letting their own fear bleed into the thread; the hearth big enough to roast an ox, where Master Corvane — no, Master Ellery, the city had merely lent the guild its name — where Master Ellery had brewed the winter tonics and told terrible jokes; the gallery above, where the finished charms had hung on silver hooks like sleeping birds, a thousand small glimmers in the lamplight, each one a promise that the world could be made kinder in increments.

All of it was black now. The benches were charred stumps. The hearth had collapsed into a heap of stone and soot. The gallery had come down in the fire and lay across the workroom floor like the ribcage of some enormous dead animal, and where the charms had hung there was nothing at all.

They had burned the inventory, of course. He had watched it from a rooftop, twelve years old — no. Fourteen. He had been fourteen. The thought snagged again, the way it always snagged, a burr in the wool of his memory. He had watched the Inquisition's wagons carry out the guild's ledgers and its relics, and he had watched the fire, and he had run home to the tenement and told himself that the Montroses had never had anything to do with any of it, that his mother's little stall of stitched blessings was just cloth and thread, that his father's silence was just a man's tiredness.

He had been very good, in the years since, at not finishing those sentences.

*You're here for shelter,* he told himself. *Nothing else. Walls and a roof and a night where no one knows your name.* He had learned in three hard days of flight that a man on the run needs a place to stop moving, or he starts making the mistakes that stopped men make.

He made his camp in the corner of the workroom farthest from the door, behind the ruin of the apprentices' bench — his bench, once; the third from the hearth, where the wood still bore a faint scorch-shadow of the inkpot he had knocked over in his first week. He ate half a loaf he'd bought with the noblewoman's coin and immediately regretted spending, wrapped his cloak around himself, and told his body to sleep.

His body declined. It had opinions, his body, and all of them were about the dark.

Because the dark in the guildhall was not empty. He knew the guildhall's dark. It had a texture. And under the ash and the pigeon-musk, beneath the cold and the rot, there was a thread of something else — faint as a voice through a wall, faint as the last warmth leaving a cup — the residue of a thousand small workings, soaked into the stone over a century of craft. The guild's leavings. The ghost of kindness, done in increments.

He had never been able to feel it before. He was sure of that. He had been here twice as a boy, after the fire, and felt nothing but grief.

But three days ago a noblewoman had pressed a charm to her breastbone and wept with gratitude, and yesterday a street-singer had been dragged through the snow for a song with an old charm-word in the refrain, and this morning Sable had felt the luck of a loaded dice cup across a whole tavern room, a bright wrong note in the air. The Edict had not changed the world. It had changed him. It was as if the hunt itself — the fear of it, the running — had scraped something off him, some seal or salve laid over his senses long ago, and now the magic of the world came through to him raw.

He lay in the dark and listened to the guild's ghost, and grief rose in him like floodwater.

It was not the sharp grief of the fire. That had burned out long ago. This was the slow grief, the one that came of standing in the place where your life had been and measuring how much of it you could no longer reach. He thought of Master Ellery's terrible jokes. He thought of the smell of the winter tonics, cloves and iron. He thought of the old guildmaster's hands — scarred, precise, always warm — folding a charm-cloth with the corners matched exactly, because *a charm is a promise, boy, and promises should be neat.*

He had never gone to Ellery's funeral. He had been told of it a week after, by a woman selling pies, who had heard it from a man who swept the temple steps. *The old Charmwright master, dead in his sleep, poor soul, all alone in that little house on Tanner's Row.* Sable had bought his pie and eaten it and said nothing, and had said nothing for twelve years, and the nothing had grown so large around the grief that he could hardly find the grief inside it anymore.

*You could have gone to him,* the dark said. *You could have knocked on the door on Tanner's Row any day in twelve years. You knew where he lived. You chose not to.*

*He chose not to see me,* Sable answered the dark, and knew it for the coward's arithmetic it was.

Sleep, when it finally took him, was shallow and full of snow.

* * *

He woke to grey light and the sound of wings — pigeons exploding up through the broken roof, startled by something. He was on his feet with a knife in his hand before his mind caught up, heart slamming, every nerve singing the new song it had learned in three days of flight: *they've found you, they've found you, they've—*

Nothing. Only the pigeons, and the snow-light falling through the shattered roof in long grey columns, and the dust of their wingbeats turning in it.

Sable stood in the ruined workroom and let the fear drain out of him, and in the quiet that followed, he noticed what the fear had been hiding.

There were footprints in the ash. Not his. Old ones, weeks old at least, half-filled with the sift of the broken roof — a single line of them, coming from the direction of the master's stair at the back of the hall, going to the hearth, and stopping.

Going to the hearth. And stopping.

A man did not walk across a burned guildhall to look at a hearth. Unless the hearth was not a hearth.

He crossed the room slowly, his own prints joining the old ones, and knelt at the heap of collapsed stone. The old prints ended a hand's breadth from the largest fallen block — a slab of hearthstone as long as his forearm, lying face-down in the ash. He worked his fingers under its edge. It was heavier than it looked, and cold as a grave, and when he heaved it over, the underside came up clean out of the soot as if it had been wiped yesterday.

Cut into the stone, small and deep and neat — corners matched exactly — was the compass rose and thread of the Charmwrights. And beneath it, in Master Ellery's unmistakable cramped hand, a single line:

*For the boy who was never told. The third bench knows his weight.*

Sable sat back on his heels. The grey light moved in the broken roof above him. Somewhere in the walls, a pigeon cooed, soft and idiotic and indifferent.

*The boy who was never told.* Told what? *The third bench knows his weight.* His bench. The one whose wood still carried the shadow of his spilled inkpot. He looked across the ruined room at the charred stump of it, and then back at the stone, and felt the burr in his memory catch hard and hold — because Ellery had died on Tanner's Row years ago, alone, and yet these footprints in the ash were weeks old, and yet the stone had been wiped clean, and yet—

*No.* He made himself stop. Made himself be the craftsman instead of the frightened boy. One question at a time. The stone was a message. The message pointed to the bench. Go to the bench.

The third bench was a knee-high ruin of blackened wood. He knelt and laid his hands on it the way he had laid them on it a thousand mornings as an apprentice, in salute, in readiness, and felt — with the new raw senses that the last three days had stripped bare for him — a thread. Faint as a spider's line across a doorway, but real: a working, old and patient and keyed to a weight. Keyed, he understood with a lurch, to a boy of fourteen, some fifty pounds lighter than the man he had become.

He pressed down. Nothing. He pressed harder, putting his whole weight on the old wood, and felt the thread strain toward him and fall short, like a hand reaching for a hand that had grown.

*Knows his weight,* Ellery had written. But Sable was no longer the weight he had been. The charm had been tied to the boy.

He sat in the ash and thought. Then he took out his knife and began, methodically, to strip his pack and his coat and his boots, weighing each thing in his hands, adding and subtracting, until what remained of Sable Montrose — the man, the clothes, the knife, the guilt, all of it — came, by his best guess, to the weight of a fourteen-year-old boy with ink on his fingers.

He set it all on the bench. He knelt beside it. He put his hands on either side, on the charred wood, and leaned his whole life into the third bench from the hearth.

The thread caught.

It went through him like cold water through cloth — not painful, but total, a working so old and so patient that it seemed to have been waiting the way stone waits. The ruined bench did not move. The ash did not stir. But beneath the floor, with a grinding whisper of stone on stone, something opened.

* * *

The vault under the guildhall was small — a mason's afterthought, dug when the hall was built and forgotten by everyone but its maker. Sable descended the narrow stair with a candle stub burning in his hand, and the flame behaved strangely, leaning always toward the chamber at the bottom as if it, too, had been waiting.

The room was bare except for a table of black oak, and on the table, a letter.

It lay in the center, squared to the table's edges, corners matched exactly. The wax seal bore the compass rose. It was addressed in Ellery's cramped, neat hand, and the address was not a name but a sentence:

*To the one who comes after the fire, when the world has gone afraid.*

Sable stood over it a long time. The candle leaned. The cold of the vault was the cold of deep places, older than winter, and he felt it through his boot-soles like the touch of something patient.

"He knew," Sable said aloud, and his voice cracked in the small stone room. "He knew I'd come. He knew I'd — the weight, the bench — he built a lock that only opens for—" For the boy. For the apprentice he had never come for, never written to, never once in twelve years sent so much as a stitch of thread. Anger flared in him, sudden and scalding, and just as quickly went out, because anger needed a living man to burn against, and Ellery was ash on Tanner's Row, and all that was left of him was a letter that had waited in the dark for the boy he had failed.

Sable broke the seal.

The letter inside was four pages, written close, the hand growing shakier toward the end. He read it by candlelight, standing, and then he sat down on the cold floor and read it again, and the second reading was worse, because the first time he had been able to pretend some of the words meant something other than what they said.

*If you are reading this, then the seal I laid on your mind has begun to fail, and the world has grown dangerous enough to force you home. I am sorry for both. I am sorry most of all that you will learn this from my hand instead of my voice, but by the time you read it, my voice will be beyond use, and there are ears everywhere that must not hear.*

*You were fourteen when the covenant broke. You were there. You will not remember it, because I took it from you — I, who taught you that a charm is a promise, broke the oldest promise of all, the one a master makes to his apprentice: that his memory of his own life belongs to him. I did it to save you. I tell myself I did it to save you.*

*Hear the truth of it, and judge me as you must. Twelve years ago, the crown came to the guild with a demand no guildmaster could refuse and no guildmaster could obey. The old king was dying of fear — fear of the fae realm beyond the veil, fear that had been fed to him for years like poison in a cup. His son shared it. They wanted the guild's help in a great working: not to mend the covenant between the realms, as our order had sworn to maintain it, but to weaken it. To draw the fire of the last dragon, Emberveil, whose flame is the lock on the fae door. They believed the fire could be taken without the door opening. They were wrong, and they would not be argued with, and they had soldiers.*

*I went to Mount Kaelen with the delegation. You came too — against my orders, as you did everything in those days. What happened on the mountain, I took from you because no fourteen-year-old should carry it. What I did there, I took from you because I could not bear to be seen by you as what I am.*

*I broke the covenant, Sable. Not the crown's mages — me. They had my guild's survival in one hand and your name in the other, and I chose, and I have spent twelve years choosing it again every morning, and every morning the choosing costs more. The door did not open. But it has been thinning ever since, and it is thinning faster now, and the fire of Emberveil gutters as the lock gutters, and when the lock fails there will be no curing and no edict and no crown, because there will be no world with edges to it.*

*There is a way to mend what I broke. The covenant was forged of three bindings, and each binding has its key, and the keys survive — I made sure of that, in the years after, when the crown thought me a broken old man and stopped watching. I hid them where only a Charmwright would think to look, and I have written their hiding places below. Bring them to the mountain. Reforge the bond. The rite is written in the oldest of our books, and the oldest of our books is the first key's keeping.*

*But know the price before you climb the stair. The covenant is a thing of memory — it is the remembering, realm to realm, that holds the door. To reforge it, the stone will take a memory from the one who works the rite. Not a trifle. Not a day. It will take what the worker loves best, all of it at once and forever. I could not pay that price. I was too much a coward at the end, and I paid instead with the only coin I had, which was you.*

*Three keys, then. The first lies where the salt keeps what the sea was given — you will know it when you stand there, for you have stood there before, though you do not remember it. The second sleeps with the bones of the abbey at Thornhollow, under the keeping of the warden. The third — the third I could not hide, because it is not a thing. The third key is memory itself, boy. The door to it is the seal I laid on you, and the only hand that can open it is yours.*

*I do not ask your forgiveness. I ask your craft. Mend what the world has broken. It is what we were for.*

*— E.*

The candle had burned down to his fingers. Sable did not feel it.

He sat on the floor of the vault with the letter in his hands, and the twelve-year-old burr in his memory — the snagged thought, the sentence that never finished, *I was fourteen when* — sat up and looked at him, and he understood at last that it was not a burr at all. It was a seam. A join in the cloth of him, neat and precise, corners matched exactly, the work of a master craftsman who had cut a boy's life open and taken the heart of it out and sewn him shut again and sent him into the world to be small and forgettable and safe.

*You're here for shelter,* he had told himself. *Nothing else.*

He laughed. It came out of him wrong, a single harsh note that the small stone room made enormous, and then it was not laughing at all, and he knelt on the cold floor with his forehead against the black oak table and let the grief have him — for the master who had betrayed him, for the master who had loved him, for the boy on the mountain whose screaming he could almost, almost hear through the seam — and when it had wrung him out, he knelt there a while longer, because there was nothing else in him.

Then he got up. He folded the letter with the corners matched exactly. He put it inside his coat, over his heart, where a charm would go.

The first key, where the salt keeps what the sea was given. He turned the words over as he climbed the stair, and something stirred at the edge of the seam — not a memory, not yet, but the shape where a memory had been cut away, a socket waiting for its jewel. The tidal flats. The drowned chapel of Saint Mereth that the sea took two hundred years ago and gives back for one hour at the lowest ebbs. He had been there. Ellery had written that he had been there, and Sable's body knew it was true, knew it the way his hands knew a knot they'd tied ten thousand times.

He came up into the grey light of the ruined workroom. The snow had stopped. Through the broken roof, high and cold, a single thread of blue showed where the sky was clearing, and far off across the city a bell began to ring the hour — and then a second bell, and a third, out of rhythm with the first, urgent, the pattern the watch used for a search.

They were looking for him. Of course they were. The noblewoman's face floated up in his memory, grateful and wet-eyed, and he understood now — with the terrible clarity of a man who has just learned that his whole childhood was a locked room — that she had wept because she knew what she held, and had sold it before the tears were dry.

Sable stood in the ash of his childhood and did the arithmetic a craftsman does. The tide would be low at the flats in two days. The Inquisition would search the city for three. The road east ran through the Weavers' Quarter gate, and the gate would be watched by now.

He looked down at the letter against his chest, and at his hands, which had begun, very faintly, to glow — the residue of the vault's working still moving in him, the guild's last charm waking in its last apprentice.

"All right, old man," he said to the ash, to the dark, to the dead. "You set the lock. I'll pick it."

He pulled his collar up, and went out through the groaning door into the white and silent street, east, toward the sea.
