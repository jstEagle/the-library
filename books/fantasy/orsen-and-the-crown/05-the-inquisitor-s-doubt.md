## 5. The Inquisitor's Doubt

The confession room smelled of tallow and old iron, and Maren Vale had learned to breathe through her mouth so the smell would not settle into her clothes.

She sat across the table from a cooper's daughter named Ilse Brandt, seventeen years old, wrists bound in cold-iron cuffs that wept a faint frost where they touched skin. The girl had been brought in two days ago, seized at the fish market because she'd been overheard humming a charm-lullaby her grandmother taught her — the kind of song every grandmother in Veyra used to sing, back before singing it became a crime.

"You've confessed," Maren said gently. "You told Brother Cassian that you can call light to your hand. That you've done it since you were six."

"Yes, Inquisitor." Ilse's voice was flat as slate.

"Look at me when you say it."

The girl raised her eyes. They were red-rimmed and dry — past crying, into something worse. "I can call light to my hand. I have done it since I was six."

Maren nodded slowly and made a show of writing in the ledger before her, though her pen recorded nothing true. *Confession consistent. No coercion observed.* The words felt like stones dropping into deep water.

Because there was one thing wrong with the confession, one small wrong thing that no one else in the Tower of Quiet Bells seemed to notice, and it was this: Ilse Brandt could not do what she said she could do.

Maren knew it the way she knew rain coming — not by seeing it, but by feeling it. There was a pressure in the air around a gifted person, a warmth like standing near a hearth through a wall. She had felt it all her life and learned, early and thoroughly, never to let her face show what she felt. It was the first lesson her mother ever taught her, pressed into her palm along with a sprig of dried rowan: *Never tell them what you are, little bird. Never even tell yourself where they can hear.*

Ilse Brandt was cold. Not gifted-cold — empty-cold. Whatever faint flicker the girl might once have carried, it was nothing, less than nothing, a candle-snuff of talent that wouldn't have lit a moth's way home. And yet here she sat, confessing to powers she did not have, in words that came out smooth and rehearsed, like a litany memorized from a page.

*Brother Cassian's questions,* Maren thought. *Or his answers.*

"Thank you," she said aloud, and closed the ledger. "Your honesty will be weighed mercifully. You'll be taken to the curing ward within the week."

Ilse flinched at that — everyone flinched at the curing ward — but she said nothing. The wardens led her out, and her footsteps faded down the stone corridor, small and even, like someone walking to a well.

Maren sat alone in the confession room for a long moment, listening to the tallow spit in its dish.

Then she opened the ledger again, turned back three pages, and read the last five confessions she had taken. She laid them side by side in her mind, and the pattern rose off the paper like heat off summer cobbles.

A ferryman who confessed to charming fish into his nets. A hedge-widow who confessed to brewing storm-salve. A choirboy who confessed to making his voice carry without breath. Five prisoners, five confessions — and every single one of them had begun with the same phrase, word for word, in the same cadence: *"I have carried the gift since childhood, and I have hidden it from righteous eyes."*

Five different mouths. One voice.

Maren pressed her thumb hard against the ledger's edge until the pain gave her something honest to hold onto.

* * *

The Tower of Quiet Bells earned its name honestly. Sound died in its corridors; the architects had built the walls with hollow cores packed with river-sand, so that screams — if any occurred — would trouble no one's sleep. Maren walked those muffled halls each morning feeling like a woman moving underwater, and each morning the feeling grew heavier.

She found Brother Cassian in the scriptorium annex on the third floor, where he kept an office far finer than a mere questioner deserved: walnut desk, leaded windows, a brazier of good coal burning even in autumn. He was a spare man, gray as ash, with neat hands and a voice like a file drawn slowly across soft metal. He looked up when she entered and smiled with genuine warmth, which was somehow the worst thing about him.

"Inquisitor Vale. Come in, come in. How is our cooper's daughter?"

"Cooperative." Maren set the ledger on his desk. "Perhaps too cooperative."

"Oh?" He turned pages with those neat hands, unhurried. "Doubting her sincerity?"

"I'm doubting its origin." She kept her tone level, professional, the tone of a junior officer raising a procedural point. "Five confessions this month, Brother. All beginning with the same sentence. All describing gifts in identical terms — 'the warmth in the blood,' 'the light behind the eyes.' Those aren't phrases villagers use. Those are phrases from the Seminary catechism. Someone is teaching these people their confessions before they reach us."

Cassian's smile didn't waver, but something behind it went very still, the way a pond goes still when a heron lands. "People echo one another. Fear makes poor poets."

"Fear makes stammerers," Maren said. "These people recite."

He folded his hands on the desk. For a moment he studied her with an attention that made the rowan sprig memory rise unbidden in her mind — her mother's voice, *never tell them* — and she realized, with a cold slide of understanding, that she had just done something dangerous. She had shown a spymaster that she noticed things.

"You have a fine mind, Inquisitor Vale," Cassian said softly. "Finer than most the Edict has given us. Tell me — does it trouble you? The work?"

"The work is the crown's will."

"It is. And the crown's will has grown... ambitious of late." He rose and went to the window, looking down into the courtyard where wagons stood loaded with iron cages. "You know His Highness Prince Aldric takes personal interest in the hunt now. Personal interest. He reviews the intake lists himself. Do you know why?"

"No, Brother."

"He believes the gift is a disease of the blood," Cassian said, still watching the cages. "And diseases, properly understood, have sources. Roots. He is not interested in pulling weeds, Inquisitor. He is interested in following them down to the root." He turned back to her, and his gray eyes were pleasant and bottomless. "Which is why confessions must be abundant, and uniform, and useful. The root, you see, must be found before winter."

"What root?" Maren asked. "What could stray hedge-witches possibly be rooted in?"

Cassian tilted his head, considering how much to give her — she could see the consideration happening, see him weighing her like a merchant weighing coin. Then, apparently deciding she was either trustworthy or harmless, he reached into his desk and drew out a single sheet of vellum, sealed with black wax.

"A name," he said. "One name keeps surfacing beneath all this chaff. A charm-seller in Corvane. Sable Montrose. Last confirmed member of the Charmwrights' Guild." He slid the paper toward her. "Inquisitor Hale wants him taken quietly and brought to the Tower alive. Unmarked. The prince has questions for him that require a subject who can still answer."

Maren looked down at the name, and her heart did something strange — a quick, traitorous lurch, like a bird striking a window.

Sable Montrose. She knew the name the way one knows a scar: from four years ago, from a raid on a guild safehouse in the Saltrow district, from a night she had spent four years trying not to remember. A boy of nineteen, thin and quick, holding a door shut against wardens with nothing but a luck-charm and sheer stubbornness while others fled through the cellar. She had been the one who broke the lock. She had seen his face for perhaps three seconds — frightened, furious, alive — before he went out a window and was gone.

She had told herself for four years that she'd forgotten him. Her body, apparently, had not.

"Why does the prince care about one charm-peddler?" she asked, and was proud of how steady her voice came out.

"That," Cassian said pleasantly, "is above your station and mine. Hale wants him found. Hale trusts you — you're careful, you're quiet, you don't ask questions in company." His smile returned, warm as the coal brazier. "Find Montrose, Inquisitor. Before the wardens do. Bring him to us whole."

* * *

That night Maren climbed to her cell at the top of the tower — cells, really; the inquisitors slept in cells too, a fact the recruitment brochures omitted — and barred the door, and did what she did every night, and hated herself for it every night.

She lit no candle. She simply held out her hand, palm up, in the dark, and let the thing inside her rise.

It came the way it always came: a warmth in the blood, a light behind the eyes — *their* phrases, Cassian's catechism phrases, and that was the joke of it, the filthy joke, that the confessions she'd been collecting were written in the language of her own body. A glow gathered in her palm, faint as foxfire, green-gold, trembling. It illuminated nothing more than her own fingers, her own face, the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a running hare.

Her mother had called it *the ember*. Said every gift started as an ember, and embers could be fed or smothered, and Maren had spent twenty-six years smothering hers until it was barely a spark — enough to feel others, enough to warm a cup of tea if she dared, never enough to save anyone. Least of all herself.

"Ilse Brandt has less magic than I do," Maren whispered to the hare-shaped stain. "Less than a moth. And she'll go to the curing ward for it."

The curing ward. Everyone in the Tower spoke of it in the same hushed, official tones — *where the afflicted are made whole* — and no one who went in ever came out to describe it. Maren had once asked a senior inquisitor, a tired woman named Ostrey, what actually happened there. Ostrey had looked at her for a long moment and said only: "They stop being afflicted, child. That's all any of us need to know." Two months later Ostrey herself had been transferred to the ward, quietly, after collapsing during a confession. Maren had gone to visit and been turned away at the door. Three weeks after that, Ostrey's cell had been cleared out, and her name removed from the rolls, and no one spoke of her at all.

*They stop being afflicted.*

Maren closed her hand. The ember died. The dark rushed back in, complete and total, and she sat in it and felt the shape of the trap she had lived inside all her life close another notch tighter.

Because here was the truth she had been circling for weeks, the truth that had started as a splinter and was now a beam: the confessions were scripted. The prisoners were mostly empty — hollow people, or frightened people, or people whose tiny gifts had been exaggerated into crimes. The hunt wasn't gathering magicians. The hunt was manufacturing them, filling the Tower with uniform confessions, building — piece by rehearsed piece — a portrait of some vast hidden conspiracy of the gifted. And beneath all that manufactured chaff, the only real thread, the one name that surfaced again and again in the interrogations of actual guild survivors, was the Charmwrights' Guild. And the last Charmwright.

*Follow the root down,* Cassian had said. But roots ran both directions. If the prince wanted Sable Montrose alive and unmarked, then Sable Montrose knew something — or had something — or *was* something. And whatever it was, it mattered enough that a spymaster was forging confessions by the dozen to justify reaching for it.

Maren thought about the boy at the door in Saltrow. Four years ago. Three seconds of face. She had broken the lock and he had gone out the window, and she had told herself she felt nothing but relief that the arrest list would be short.

She had lied to herself with great discipline for four years. She was finding it harder to maintain.

* * *

Two nights later, she went to see Inquisitor Hale.

Hale's office occupied the tower's second floor, and it was the only room in the Quiet Bells that smelled of anything — pipe smoke, mostly, and leather. Hale was fifty, heavyset, with a face like a dropped plate that had been carefully glued back together. He had interviewed Maren personally six years ago, when she'd passed the trials, and she had never decided whether he suspected what she was. Sometimes she thought he watched her a half-second too long. Sometimes she thought she was flattering herself.

He listened to her report on the confessions without interrupting, pipe smoke curling between them, and when she finished he was silent for a long time.

"You think they're false," he said finally.

"I think they're *written*, sir. By someone before they reach the questioners."

"And you're telling me this because?"

The question hung there. Maren had prepared an answer — duty, procedure, concern for accurate records — and heard herself abandon it. Perhaps it was the pipe smoke. Perhaps it was the glued-together face, which wore, tonight, an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of a person carrying something heavy and alone.

"Because I'm afraid of what we're becoming," she said. "Sir."

Hale grunted. He knocked out his pipe against the desk's edge, slowly. "When I joined the Inquisition," he said, "we hunted fire-starters. Men who burned barns with a word. Murderers. The gift in a wicked hand, that was our quarry, and I slept well." He looked at the window, at the dark beyond it. "This Edict isn't a hunt. Hunts end. This is a harvest. And I don't know what the crop is for." He fixed her with his cracked-plate eyes. "Say nothing of this conversation. But if you find Montrose first — find him *first*, understand me — bring him in gentle. Alive means alive. Not half-dead, not terror-mad. Hale's orders. Quietly."

"Yes, sir."

"Maren." It was the first time he'd used her given name in six years. "Whatever you find out there — however bad it gets — remember that some of us inside these walls are still men. It matters more than you'd think."

She left with her heart hammering, unsure whether she'd just been given an ally or a leash.

* * *

She rode for Corvane at dawn under a gray sky, with a warrant in her saddlebag and a lie in her mouth for every checkpoint. Officially, she was investigating reports of a fugitive charm-seller. Unofficially — in the country of herself, the small secret nation she had governed alone for twenty-six years — she was racing her own colleagues to a man she'd met for three seconds, for reasons she could not fully explain and did not try to.

The road east ran through country still green with late autumn, hedgerows golding at the edges, woodsmoke standing straight up from cottage chimneys in the windless cold. Ordinary country. Peaceful country. She counted, over two days of riding, eleven burned homesteads. Each one marked with the crown's black chevron daubed on the doorpost: *afflicted dwelling, cleansed.* In one, a child's wooden horse lay in the yard, half-melted.

By the second night she stopped counting and started riding faster.

Corvane rose out of the flats on the third evening, its bell-towers needled against a bruised sunset, frost already silvering the rooftops. Maren entered by the Saltrow gate with her hood up, leading her horse through streets that had the particular silence of a city holding its breath. Proclamation boards stood at every corner, the Edict of Cleansing nailed up in triplicate, and beneath one of them a knot of people stood reading in silence — and beside the board, fresh chalk on the wall, someone had scrawled a small mark: a circle with a flame inside it.

The old sign of the Charmwrights' Guild. Chalk washes away in rain. Whoever had drawn it wanted it found, briefly, by the right eyes.

Maren stood before it a long moment, snow beginning to sift down through the lamplight, feeling the ember stir faintly in her blood like a dog lifting its head.

Then she saw, half-buried in the chalk dust at the base of the wall, a scrap of paper — a torn corner of a letter, written in a cramped, hurried hand, the ink smeared with saltwater:

*—east, to the drowned chapel, before the tide turns twice. Trust no one in gray. If you are reading this and you are not him, burn it and forget the name of the sea.*

Maren looked up the street, then down. Empty. Snow thickening.

She was not him. Every rule she had lived by, every lesson her mother had pressed into her palm with a sprig of rowan, said: walk away, little bird, walk away, stay small, stay cold, survive.

She folded the scrap into her glove, mounted, and turned her horse east, toward the sea.
