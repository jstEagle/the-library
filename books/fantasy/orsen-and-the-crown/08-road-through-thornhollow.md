## 8. Road Through Thornhollow

The snow came down in fat, silent flakes as Sable Montrose crested the last hill before Thornhollow, and for a moment he simply stood there and let the cold bite his face, because the village below looked like something out of the letter's margins — one of those small marginal drawings Guildmaster Orsen used to sketch when he thought no one was watching: crooked roofs, a single bell tower, smoke rising straight and slow from chimneys like prayers that had given up on being answered.

"You're smiling," Maren said beside him. She had her hood up and her hand near the hilt of the short sword she wore under her cloak, because three days out of Corvane she still moved like a hunted woman, which was exactly what she was now. "I didn't think you knew how."

"I'm not smiling. My face is frozen."

"Your face is frozen *into* a smile. There's a difference." She shifted the strap of her pack, and beneath it he could see the outline of the salt relic wrapped in oilcloth — the drowned chapel's key, which hummed faintly against his hip whenever he forgot to ward it, the way a struck bell keeps humming after the sound has supposedly ended. "Thornhollow. You've been here before?"

"No." But the name sat strangely in his mouth, familiar the way a word becomes familiar when you've said it in a dream. He frowned at the feeling and let it go, as he'd learned to let go of a hundred such feelings since the unsealing had begun. Twelve years of missing time left holes in him, and things kept falling into them. "The letter said the second key is in the abbey catacombs. The abbey's here."

"The abbey's here," Maren agreed, "and so, presumably, is whatever guards it. And so, if we're unlucky, is news of two travelers asking after old stones." She glanced at him sidelong. "So we don't ask after old stones. We ask for beds. We ask for stew. We are what we look like — a charm-seller walking to the coast with his sister."

"Sister."

"Would you prefer wife? Wives get asked more questions."

"*Sister* is fine."

They went down through the snow together.

* * *

Thornhollow smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, underneath both, faintly of tallow and dried herbs — the smell of a place where someone still rendered fat for candles the old way and hung bunches of yarrow from rafters. Sable noticed the herbs first. Then he noticed where they hung: over doorways, not windows. Thresholds. That was guild-craft, or the memory of it, worn down to superstition the way a coin wears down to a blank disc after too many hands.

The inn was called the Drowned Bell, which made him laugh once, shortly, and Maren look at him sharply until he shook his head and led the way inside.

The common room was warm and half-full: farmers thawing out, a carter, an old woman mending netting by the fire even though the nearest river was a day's walk. Conversation dipped when they entered — strangers were strangers everywhere now — and rose again when Sable produced coins and asked, in his flattest market voice, for a room, two beds, and whatever the pot held.

It was while they waited for the stew that he saw the children.

There were four of them, sitting on the floor by the hearth where the adults wouldn't see, playing a game with knucklebones and a circle scratched into the stone. As he watched, the smallest girl tossed the bones and whispered something over them — quick, breathy, half-swallowed — and the bones landed in a pattern that made the other children gasp and shove her affectionately. A winning throw. A charmed throw.

Sable's chest tightened. He knew that whisper. Every Charmwright began as a child whispering over knucklebones, and every Charmwright learned within a season to stop, because whispers got noticed. But these children hadn't stopped. They whispered openly, carelessly, the way children sang songs their mothers sang without knowing the words meant anything.

"They don't know," Maren murmured. She'd followed his gaze. Her spoon had gone still in her bowl. "They can't know. Nobody's told them what the world outside has become."

"Somebody will." He kept his voice low. "Hale's lists reach every market town eventually. And children talk."

"Then maybe somebody should tell *them*, first, how to be quiet about it." She said it lightly, but he heard the thing underneath it — the same note he'd heard in the drowned chapel when she'd flung her light against the wardens' lanterns and admitted, at last, in fire, what she carried. Maren Vale, inquisitor, gift-bearer. A woman who had spent years hunting her own reflection.

"Eat your stew," he said gently. "We find the abbey tomorrow, at dawn, before the village wakes. In and out. No entanglements."

Maren looked at the smallest girl, who was whispering over the bones again, tongue between her teeth, utterly absorbed in her small forbidden miracle.

"No entanglements," she repeated, in the tone of a woman promising herself something she already suspected she would break.

* * *

They should have left at dawn. They did leave at dawn — but the snow, which had fallen all night like a soft gray curtain, had drifted waist-deep against the abbey gate, and the gate itself was chained, and the chain was warded.

Not crown-warded. Guild-warded. Sable knew the knot-work the moment his bare fingers brushed the links: a sevenfold binding, the kind apprentices spent a year learning and a lifetime perfecting. Someone had locked this door who loved the craft. Someone had locked it hoping only the right hands would open it.

He worked the knots with his eyes closed and his breath fogging, and when the chain sighed loose he felt the ward acknowledge him — a warmth traveling up his arm like a cat rubbing against a leg — and he stood there a moment in the falling snow with his heart hammering, because the wards of the Charmwrights' Guild had not recognized him in twelve years, and he had not known, until just then, how much he'd missed being known.

Inside, the catacombs were dry and dark and smelled of dust and older dust. The salt relic woke against his ribs as he descended, humming its struck-bell hum, pulling him deeper the way water pulls a drowning man — or guides him; with relics it had never been entirely clear which. Torches lit themselves as they passed, guttering awake in wall-brackets that hadn't held flame in decades, and Maren watched each one catch with the expression of a woman revising her opinion of a building.

"It knows you," she said quietly.

"The wards do. Not the same thing."

"Isn't it?" She caught his elbow as he stepped over a collapsed slab, steadying him without thinking, and neither of them remarked on it, though both of them noticed. "Sable. Whatever's down here — the trial, the guardian, whatever the first relic showed you waits at each of these places — you don't have to pretend it's nothing. I watched the bone-chapel nearly take you apart at the tidal flats. I'd rather you were afraid out loud than quiet inside."

He almost laughed. "You've known me three weeks."

"And in three weeks I've seen you lie to wardens, lie to me, lie to yourself, and stand in front of a bone-warden without blinking. Fear isn't the thing you hide well." She paused at the mouth of the burial gallery, where niches opened into the walls like shelves of forgotten books, each niche holding its folded skeleton. "It's grief. You hide grief beautifully. It looks exactly like competence."

He had no answer for that, so he gave her none, and walked on, and the salt relic pulled him to the third niche on the left, where a skeleton lay folded with its hands arranged around a small lead casket — and the casket was empty.

For a long moment Sable just stared at the hollow where the second key should have been. The dust in the niche was undisturbed. The ward on the gate had never been broken. And yet—

"It's not gone," he said slowly, because the relic was still humming, still pulling, and the pull had not lessened. If anything it deepened, bending toward the skeleton itself, toward the folded hands. "It's been *moved*. Taken into—"

The dead man opened his eyes.

They were not eyes. They were two points of grave-light kindling in the sockets, and the whole folded skeleton unfolded in a single soundless motion, rising out of its niche like a spider out of a crack, joints clicking, and the light in its skull swept across them both and fixed on Sable.

"Wright," it said. The word came out like a door opening in an empty house. "*Prove.*"

"Oh," Maren said, drawing her sword, "you have *got* to be joking—"

"Put that away, it won't help—" But the warden was already moving, faster than bone had any right to move, and Sable threw up the only ward he had ready, a simple shield-knot, and the warden's finger-bones struck it and the whole gallery rang like a cathedral bell and the shield shattered and threw him backward into the niches, into the folded dead, dust and old linen raining down.

*Prove.* Not kill. Test. The trials always tested — the salt chapel had tested his patience, the drowned reliquary his honesty. This one wanted proof of what, exactly? Of craft? Of worth?

Of *identity*. The realization arrived cold as the grave-air. The guild had sealed its keys against thieves, and a thief could steal a face but not a self. The warden wasn't asking for a password. It was asking for *him* — all of him, including the parts still missing.

"I don't—" he started, and the warden seized him by the throat with a grip like winter iron, and lifted him off the floor, and the grave-lights bored into his eyes, into his mind, searching for the man whose hands had tied that gate-chain — and finding, instead, a house with rooms boarded shut.

What happened next, he only partly understood. The warden's search snagged on the seal in his memory — the great smooth scar where twelve years had been cut away — and the scar *pained it*, somehow; he felt its confusion as pressure behind his own eyes, felt it probe the edge of the seal the way a tongue probes a broken tooth. And the seal, touched by something older and stranger than it had ever been designed for, *cracked*. Just a hairline. Just enough.

Fire. Snow. Screaming that might have been his own. A woman's voice saying *"hold the line, little wren"* — and a ring, a guildmaster's signet, turning and turning in firelight —

The warden dropped him.

He hit the floor on hands and knees, gasping, blood in his mouth from biting his tongue, and the grave-lights dimmed, and the skeleton bowed — actually *bowed*, folding back down into something like courtesy — and gestured with one long finger-bone toward the niche it had risen from. Where, in the fold of the dead man's hands, the lead casket now sat again, solid and real, as though it had been there all along. Perhaps it had. Perhaps it had merely been waiting to see.

"Sable." Maren was kneeling beside him, her sword abandoned, her hands hovering over his shoulders without quite touching. "Sable, look at me. What did it show you?"

"A woman." His voice came out in pieces. "Calling me — 'little wren.' And a ring. His ring." He looked up at her, and the words came before he could weigh them, before he could decide whether to carry them alone a while longer, the way he carried everything. "Maren, I think I was *there*. Twelve years ago. When the covenant broke. I think I saw it happen."

She went very still. Above them the torches burned steadily, lighting the folded dead, and somewhere far above, muffled by stone and snow, the bell of Thornhollow's little church began to ring the hour.

"We need to leave," she said finally. "Now. Before anyone asks why the abbey lit itself up like a festival."

* * *

They were halfway down the high street when the horns started.

Three notes, repeated — a pattern Sable knew from the rooftop chase in Corvane, from the Edict, from every market town that had learned to dread it. Warden horns. Not one horn. Three, from three directions, closing like a hand closing.

"They followed us," Maren breathed. "From the flats. Hale put a tracker on the salt relic, or on me, or—"

"Doesn't matter." Sable caught her arm as villagers spilled from their doors, confused, pulling children indoors, and the smallest girl from the inn — the knucklebone girl — stood frozen in the middle of the street with her game-pouch clutched to her chest. "Maren. The inn. The children were playing charm-games in a public room. Wardens search houses. They'll find the yarrow over the thresholds, they'll ask questions, and some frightened mother will answer honestly—"

"I know." Her face had gone the color of the snow. "Sable, if I use my gift out here, in daylight, in front of witnesses—"

"I know."

The first warden patrol came around the churchyard wall: six men in gray-and-black, crossbows levelled, and behind them rode a lieutenant on a shaggy gray horse with a list in his gloved hand. The lieutenant's voice carried flat and clear down the street.

"By edict of the Crown! All persons harboring the gifted, all persons bearing the gift: surrender for assessment and curing. Resistance is heresy against the realm." He consulted his list. "The Drowned Bell inn. Search it. Take the keeper for questioning."

They dragged the innkeeper out — a big gentle man Sable had spoken to for ten minutes the night before, a man who had charged them half-rate and apologized for the thin blankets. His daughter was the smallest girl. He put himself between the wardens and his child without hesitation and without any plan at all, and a warden's crossbow-butt broke him across the mouth, and the little girl screamed, and the scream tore through the winter air like cloth tearing.

And everything in Sable that had been running since Corvane — running, hiding, being small, being forgettable — stood very still and refused to run another step.

"Maren," he said.

She was already moving.

What she did, she did openly, in the middle of the street, in full view of forty witnesses. Light came off her hands like heat off a forge — not the pale utility-glow of an inquisitor's lamp-charm but living gold, guild-fire, the old light — and it struck the wardens' weapons and wrenched them from their grips and sent the lieutenant's horse screaming sideways. She planted herself between the soldiers and the innkeeper's family, and her voice, when it came, was the voice Sable imagined she must once have used in interrogation rooms, turned inside out:

"By the authority vested in me as an inquisitor of the Crown — *stand down.*"

The wardens hesitated. Their lieutenant did not. "Vale," he said, and spat the name like a curse, and Sable understood in one cold drop that Maren had been listed too, had been hunted longer than either of them knew. "Inquisitor Vale. The traitor. Take her alive — the spymaster wants her mind intact."

Crossbows came up. Sable's wards bloomed around Maren a half-second before the bolts flew — sevenfold knots, thrown fast and ragged, catching steel in midair — and then the street dissolved into noise and snow and running feet, and Sable stopped counting what was lawful a long time ago.

It ended quickly, as such things do. Two wardens fled. The lieutenant lay groaning in a drift with Maren's boot on his chest and her gold still burning off her fists like a saint's fury. The villagers stood in their doorways, staring — at her, at the light, at the impossible fact of it — and the little girl had stopped screaming and was looking up at Maren with an expression Sable recognized from every mirror of his childhood.

Awe. The terrible, hopeful awe of a child discovering that the whispered thing over the knucklebones was *real*, and *hers*, and *doomed*.

"You have to go," the innkeeper rasped through broken lips, pushing himself upright against his daughter. He looked at Maren — bleeding, blazing, magnificent — and then at Sable. "Both of you. There's a shepherd's track north, past the mill, the snow hides it. Go *now*, before the garrison at the crossroads hears the horns." He paused, and his voice dropped. "And take Tam with you."

"Tam?" Maren asked.

The innkeeper nodded toward the little girl.

"She's marked," he said simply. "Her mother was. We've hidden it six years, and today it walked into the street wearing my daughter's face. There's nothing left here for her but the curing wagons." His jaw worked. "Take her where people like you are going. Take her somewhere the whispering over bones isn't a crime."

Maren looked at Sable. And Sable, who had planned to travel light and trust no one and remain forgettable until the world forgot him entirely, looked at the child clutching her game-pouch like a talisman, and heard himself say:

"Can she ride?"

* * *

They went north along the shepherd's track as the light failed, three riders and a led packhorse, the snow swallowing the sound of hooves. Tam rode pillion behind Maren, asleep inside the circle of her arm before the first mile was done, her cheek pressed against the traitor-inquisitor's shoulder, her knucklebones still clutched in one small fist.

Sable rode a little apart, and in the blue dusk he took out the second relic — the lead casket, warm despite the cold — and turned it over in his hands without opening it. Through the metal he could feel it waiting: the bone-key, the memory of the covenant, whatever vision it meant to pour into him next.

*A woman calling me little wren. A ring turning in firelight.*

Twelve years ago, someone had stood where he was standing and watched the world break. And the seal in his mind had cracked today, hairline-thin, and through the crack he could feel the rest of it leaning — all of it leaning — ready to give way the moment he found the third key, the final key, the one the grave had refused to give him because it was not buried anywhere at all.

Behind him, Maren shifted in the saddle to keep the sleeping child warm, and said nothing, because she had learned that about him already: when he went quiet like this, it was not emptiness. It was weight.

Ahead, far off through the trees, a light that was not any torch burned briefly against the clouds — green-gold, fae-colored, there and gone — where no fire had any right to be.

Sable watched it fade, and rode on into the dark.
