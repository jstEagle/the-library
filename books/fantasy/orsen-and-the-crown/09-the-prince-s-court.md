## 9. The Prince's Court

The map of Veyra hung behind Aldric's desk the way his father had left it: pinned at the corners with silver daggers, its borders inked in gold, its mountains rendered as small brown teeth. Mount Kaelen sat among them like a splinter too deep to draw. His father had never once looked at that mountain without flinching, and Aldric had spent thirty years learning why.

He did not look away now.

Snow ticked softly against the tall windows of the royal apartments, and beyond the glass the city of Corvane lay smothered white, its chimneys bleeding thin smoke into a sky the color of old pewter. Somewhere down there, heralds were reading the Edict of Cleansing from every market cross, their voices cracking in the cold. *All who bear the gift shall surrender themselves for curing.* He had written those words himself, late one night, choosing each one the way a surgeon chooses a blade. *Curing.* It was kinder than the truth. The truth was that he meant to cut the magic out of Veyra entirely — root, branch, and seed.

Aldric turned from the window when the door opened.

Inquisitor Hale entered the way he always did, quietly, as if apologizing for his own height. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, gray at the temples, with the patient eyes of someone who had spent too many years listening to liars and had learned to hear truth the way one hears rain — by what it silenced. He bowed precisely as far as protocol required and not a finger's width further. That, too, was something Aldric valued in him. Fear made men useless; Hale had passed through fear years ago and come out the other side into something more durable.

"Your Highness." Hale set a leather folio on the desk. "The weekly report."

"Leave it."

"You'll want page four."

Aldric raised an eyebrow. Hale was not a man for dramatics, which made his dramatics land all the heavier. He crossed to the desk, broke the folio's seal, and turned to the indicated page. A single name. A charm-seller from the Tanners' Row district, flagged by a customer's testimony, escaped custody through an error Aldric would be having words about. Beneath the name, in Hale's cramped hand: *Charmwrights' Guild mark confirmed on recovered goods. First verified guild artifact in eleven years.*

"Montrose," Aldric read aloud. The name meant nothing to him. "There were no survivors of the guild. My father saw to that."

"There were always rumors," Hale said. "Children apprenticed young. Records burned before we could count them. Your father preferred it that way, I think. Easier to bury a thing if no one ever knew its size."

"And this one survived."

"This one survived, and he's selling luck-charms to bored nobility like a fishmonger selling yesterday's catch." Hale's mouth twitched — not quite a smile. "He's small, sire. Careful. Forgettable. Which means he learned the guild's last lesson well."

Aldric closed the folio. For a long moment the only sound was the snow against the glass, soft as ash falling.

"Do you know what I remember about the guildhall, Inquisitor?"

"I couldn't say, Your Highness."

"Bells." Aldric walked to the window again, hands clasped behind his back. He watched his own reflection ghosted over the city, a pale king-shaped absence floating above the rooftops. "I was nine. My mother took me past the Charmwrights' Guildhall on our way to the river festival, and the bells were ringing because the guild was celebrating some feast day or other, and she stopped in the street and held my hand so tightly I cried. She said—" His voice caught, just slightly. He smoothed it flat. "She said, *listen, little star, they're ringing for you too.* She believed they were. She believed everything was singing for everyone, once."

Hale said nothing. He was good at saying nothing. It was, Aldric suspected, half of what the crown paid him for.

"My mother is in the west tower," Aldric continued. "Have you seen her lately?"

"No, sire."

"She sings to the walls. She has done for nineteen years. Sometimes she sings charms — real ones, broken ones, guild-cadences my father forbade anyone to teach her. The nurses transcribe what they can understand. Do you know what the last transcription said?" He didn't wait for an answer. "*The door between is thinning, and the thin folk are hungry, and they have found the shape of her name.*"

The silence stretched. Outside, a cart groaned through slush on some distant avenue.

"That is madness talking," Hale said carefully. "Grief and illness. I've read the physicians' reports—"

"The physicians' reports describe symptoms." Aldric turned. "They do not describe causes. They cannot, because I will not let them look where the cause lives. But you've interrogated enough gifted prisoners, Inquisitor. You told me yourself, two winters ago, that the fae-touched ones all speak of the same thing. The door. The hunger on the other side of it. The way it *knows* things it shouldn't."

Hale's jaw tightened. "I told you that in confidence."

"And I am telling you why it matters." Aldric crossed to the desk and laid his palm flat upon the map, over the little brown tooth of Kaelen. "Sit, Hale. What I say next does not leave this room, and it does not enter any report, and if you repeat it to your partner Vale I will know, because I always know, and you will spend your remaining years counting turnips in a customs house on the Saltmarch coast. Are we understood?"

Something flickered across Hale's face at the name *Vale* — quick, quickly buried. Aldric filed it away without letting it show. He missed nothing in this room. He could afford to miss nothing.

"We are understood," Hale said, and sat.

* * *

"The covenant," Aldric began, "was signed twelve years before my birth, when the last true dragons still flew free. You know the official version. The realms made peace; the dragons gave their fire to seal the border between our world and the fae; the Charmwrights' Guild was granted stewardship as neutral brokers. Peace, prosperity, pretty bells."

"You don't believe the official version."

"I believe it leaves things out." Aldric pulled a slim volume from his desk drawer — bound in black leather, unmarked, its pages soft with handling. "This belonged to my father's spymaster. My father burned most of his papers when he died. This survived by accident, or perhaps by design, because the dead man wanted someone angry enough to read it. Listen."

He read aloud, his voice level, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

"*The covenant is not a wall. It is a wound, cauterized. The dragons' fire does not destroy the fae realm's hunger — it holds the wound shut while the hunger presses against it from the far side, forever. And fire, however noble, must be fed. The dragons feed it themselves, willingly, year upon year, burning down their own long lives to keep the seal. There are those among us who ask what happens when the last dragon dies of giving. The guild answers: there will always be another dragon. The guild is lying.*"

Hale had gone very still. "How old is this document?"

"Nineteen years. Written the winter my mother broke." Aldric set it down. "Now the rest of it. The part my father's spymaster only wrote once, in cipher, because he was afraid even of his own handwriting. The seal doesn't merely hold the fae out, Inquisitor. It *leaks*. Both directions. Small wonders slip through — that's the pretty side, the frost-flowers in summer, the songs that come true if you whistle them right. But it leaks the other way too. It leaks *attention*. The things behind the door can feel us through the fire. And what they feel most clearly, what burns brightest to their senses, is a mind already cracked open."

He let that sit.

"Madness," Aldric said softly, "is a crack, Inquisitor. Madness is an open window in a storm."

Hale rose halfway from his chair, then forced himself back down. His big hands gripped his knees. "You're telling me the fae drove Queen Isolde mad. Through the covenant. Through the seal the guild swore to maintain."

"I'm telling you she heard them singing to her for six years before she broke, and that the guild's records — the ones that survived the fires — note *increased bleed-through events* in exactly those six years. The seal was weakening even then. The dragons were aging. And the guild knew, and the guild said nothing, because saying something would mean admitting their whole sacred stewardship was a slow funeral." Aldric's voice stayed gentle, which took effort. Gentleness always took more effort than rage. "My father raged at them. He demanded they find a solution. Their grandmaster told him — and this is in the record, Hale, under seal, you may read it yourself — their grandmaster told him that nothing could be done, that the covenant would hold for generations yet, that the crown should simply... accommodate the losses. Accommodate. As though my mother were a leaky roof."

"So your father burned them."

"My father punished them badly, and then died, and left me a kingdom with a dying dragon, a thinning seal, and a mother who sings to walls about the thin folk being hungry." Aldric finally allowed the anger into his voice, low and controlled, banked like coal. "I have thought about this every day for nineteen years, Inquisitor. Every single day. While the courtiers whispered that the prince was cold, that the prince felt nothing, that the prince visited the west tower only out of duty — I was in the archives. I was reading everything the guild ever wrote, everything the dragons ever promised, everything the old kings signed away. And I found the answer."

Hale said, very quietly, "The mountain."

"The mountain." Aldric nodded toward Kaelen on the map. "Emberveil. The last dragon. Chained — no, *bound*, bound by consent, by the original terms — beneath Kaelen, feeding the seal with the last of its fire. It has perhaps a decade left. Perhaps less; the bleed-throughs are accelerating, you see them in the wilds, the villages report them. When Emberveil dies, the seal dies with it, and everything my mother heard for six years comes through that door all at once, forever, into a kingdom that has forgotten how to fight such things." He met Hale's eyes. "Unless we take the fire first."

"Take it." Hale frowned. "Sire, the dragon-fire sustains the seal. If you drain it—"

"If we drain it *properly*, we don't need the seal." Aldric stood, pacing now, the argument he had rehearsed for nineteen years finally spoken aloud to another living soul, and it tasted like cold clean water. "Think, man. Why does the seal exist at all? Because the fae realm's hunger cannot be destroyed — only walled off. That was the ancient assumption. The guild's assumption. But the guild assumed wrong. The ritual engines my engineers have built can siphon the dragon's fire directly — all of it, at once, in a single controlled act instead of a century of slow bleeding. Directed fire, Hale. Not a wall. A *weapon*. Fire enough to burn the fae realm's borderlands to glass. Fire enough to cauterize the wound permanently — not sealed, but *closed*. Dead. Nothing leaking through either direction. No more thinning. No more singing through walls. No more children born with gifts that make them windows."

"And the cost." Hale's voice was flat. "The gifted. Your edict. All of this—" he gestured vaguely at the folio, the reports, the names — "is because the gifted are windows. Loose threads in the cloth. If the door is closed permanently, they become harmless. But until then..."

"Until then, every gift is a candle burning beside that door, and the things outside can see every flame." Aldric stopped at the window. Below, in the white square, a crowd was dispersing from the morning proclamation, dark figures scattering like startled birds. "You think I enjoy it? The hunts, the towers, the 'curing'? I have read the physicians' notes from the curing houses. I know what the suppression rites do to a mind. I signed the edict knowing that. Do you imagine that was easy?"

"I imagine nothing about you is easy, sire."

Aldric almost smiled. Almost. "My mother sits in a tower singing to stones because the old covenant needed feeding and no one had the courage to say so. Every gifted person we take off the board is one less beacon for the hungry things to find. Every year we buy with quiet streets is a year closer to Kaelen. I am not cleansing Veyra, Inquisitor. I am *vaccinating* it. And when the last window is shuttered, I will burn the door itself, and my mother will wake in a silent world where nothing sings to her but birdsong and bells — ordinary bells, rung by human hands, meaning nothing but joy."

For a moment — just a moment — Aldric saw his mother not in the west tower but in a garden, lucid, laughing at something he'd said at age nine, her hand warm around his. *Listen, little star.* He shut the vision away. It was not a weapon he could afford to carry openly.

Hale was silent a long time. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a man walking a floor he suspected was rotten. "And if you're wrong, sire? If the fire can't close the door — only open it wider? The guild's texts warn—"

"The guild's texts also warned that nothing could be done at all." Aldric's tone cooled by degrees. "The guild lied to my father for nineteen years while my mother screamed herself hoarse. Their opinion weighs nothing with me. Less than nothing." He straightened the folio on his desk with one finger, precise as a surgeon squaring a scalpel. "Which brings me to your charming fugitive."

"Montrose."

"A surviving Charmwright. Selling relics, perhaps. Perhaps holding relics." Aldric turned. "If the guild hid anything worth hiding, it will surface around a survivor like water around a stone. I want him found, Inquisitor. Alive. He may know things about the covenant-stone — the anchor point beneath Kaelen — that exist nowhere else. And I want to know how a boy of fourteen walked out of a purge that left no other survivors."

"He was likely just a child, sire. Forgotten in a cellar."

"Likely." Aldric smiled thinly. "But I have been *likely* wrong before, and it cost me nineteen years. Put your best people on it. Quietly. And Hale—" he paused, weighing whether to say the next part, deciding that a leash works better when the dog knows it exists — "your partner. Vale. She's shown... promise. Use her."

Another flicker, faster this time, and gone. "Yes, sire."

"Dismissed."

Hale gathered his folio and went. The door closed behind him with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

Alone, Aldric stood at the window a long while, watching the snow bury Corvane street by street, lamp by lamp, until the city became a sketch of itself, innocent, erased.

Then he took up his cloak and went down — down the spiral stair, past the guards who did not meet his eyes, along the corridor that smelled of lavender and lye — to the west tower, where his mother sat by a barred window with her hands folded, singing.

She stopped when he entered. She often did. For a heartbeat her eyes were clear, and green as his own, and ninety-nine years of queens fell away and she was simply Isolde, holding out her hand to a boy.

"Aldric," she said. "Little star. Have they rung the bells?"

"Not yet, Mother."

"They will." She turned back to the window, and her voice slid back into the cadence he had memorized across nineteen years, sweet and broken and terribly precise. "They'll ring when the fire is taken. The thin ones told me. They're *so* hungry, my love, and the dragon is so tired, and when the fire goes out—" she hummed a few bars, a guild-cadence, forbidden, beautiful, "—when the fire goes out, the door won't matter anymore, because they'll already be *here*."

Aldric stood in the doorway, snow melting on his shoulders, and felt the certainty he had carried for nineteen years shift — one grain, no more — like a stone at the top of a wall.

That night he gave the order. Mount Kaelen would be sealed: no travelers, no supply trains, no word in or out. The ritual engines would move at month's end, under triple guard, and the draining would begin the moment the last road was closed.

And in the archives below the palace, a clerk working late by lamplight found a discrepancy in a requisition ledger — two wardens' horses drawn, a coastal coach paid for in crown silver, a route bending south toward the tidal flats — and, being a diligent man, carried the page up to the prince's secretary, who carried it to the prince, who read it twice, and set it down beside the folio with the single name on page four.

*Montrose,* said both documents, in different hands, pointing the same direction.

Aldric looked out the window at the darkening sea of roofs, at the mountain waiting on the horizon like an unopened letter, and smiled without warmth.

"Run then, little charm-seller," he murmured to the empty room. "Show me what my father was afraid of."
