## 10. The Relic of Bone

The abbey of Thornhollow had been dead longer than it had ever been alive.

Sable felt that truth settle over him the moment they passed beneath its shattered gate — a sensation like walking into a room where a conversation had stopped mid-word. The stone walls still stood in places, blackened ribs of a church that had burned in some war whose name had been forgotten along with everything else. Snow drifted through the broken roof and lay undisturbed on altars no one had tended in a century. Above them, the bell tower leaned like a drunk against the sky, its bell long since looted or fallen, leaving only the empty yoke from which it had hung.

"Charming place," Maren said. She said it the way she said most things — flat, dry, her breath steaming in the grey afternoon light — but Sable had learned to hear the tension underneath. Her hand hadn't left her sword hilt since they'd entered the abbey grounds. "Remind me why we couldn't have dug for this relic somewhere with a roof."

"The letter says the second key sleeps 'where the faithful laid their dead, beneath the eyeless saint.'" Sable held the guildmaster's letter up against the failing light, reading the line again for perhaps the hundredth time. The ink had faded to the color of old bruises, but the words were carved into his memory now as surely as if he'd written them himself. *Where the faithful laid their dead.* Catacombs. *Beneath the eyeless saint* —

He found it before he finished the thought. Set into the wall beside the ruined altar, half-swallowed by soot and ivy, was a statue: a robed figure with hands folded over a book, face smooth and featureless as an egg. No eyes. No mouth. Only the suggestion of a face, worn away by time or by deliberate chiseling — and Sable's money was on deliberate. Someone, sometime, had not wanted this saint to see.

"There," he said, and pointed.

Maren crossed herself out of habit, then seemed embarrassed by it. "I haven't done that since I was nine."

"You're allowed. It's an abbey." He knelt at the statue's base, running his fingers along the seams between stones until he found what he was looking for: a hairline crack in the shape of a keyhole, too narrow for any real key, wide enough for two fingers and a whisper of magic. His gift stirred under his skin, that familiar warmth like banked coals, and he fed a thread of it into the seam.

The stone drank the magic and went cold.

Then, somewhere far below them, something began to grind.

Both of them froze. The sound came up through the floor itself, through the bones of the abbey, through the bones of Sable's feet — a slow, ponderous movement, like a door that had not been opened in a hundred years deciding to open anyway. Dust sifted down from the vaulted ceiling. Maren drew her sword with a scrape that sounded obscenely loud.

"That's not a good sound," she said.

"No," Sable agreed. "But it means we're in the right place."

The altar slid aside on hidden counterweights, revealing steps that descended into darkness. Cold air rose from below, carrying a smell he knew from his years of trade and could never quite name without flinching: dust and old stone and something faintly sweet, like flowers pressed too long in a book. Incense, maybe. Or memory.

He looked at Maren. She looked back. Somewhere in the last three weeks — through the drowned chapel and Hale's wardens and the salt-relic vision that had shown him a dragon in chains and a hooded figure wearing his own guildmaster's ring — they had developed a whole language of glances, and this one asked a question neither of them wanted to say aloud: *do we go down there?*

"We go down there," Sable said, because saying it made it true, and because the alternative was admitting that the safest thing to do was nothing, and he had spent twelve years doing nothing and it had bought him a stall in a frost-bound market and a ledger full of small lies and loneliness measured in coins.

Maren lit a lantern. "After you, charm-seller."

"After you, inquisitor."

They went down together, which was, he reflected, its own kind of answer.

* * *

The catacombs were older than the abbey above them.

That became clear within the first hundred paces, as the architecture shifted around them — the rough-hewn crypts giving way to corridors of fitted stone too precise for any mason's hand, walls carved with spirals and interlocking knots that predated every style of building Sable knew. The niches in the walls held bones, but not arranged the way the faithful arranged them. They were set in patterns. Circles. Spirals. A frieze of femurs and finger-bones that spelled out something in a script Sable almost recognized, and the almost was worse than ignorance would have been, because it itched at the edge of him like a word on the tip of the tongue.

"I know these markings," he heard himself say, and then stopped, because he didn't, and yet his fingers were tracing one of the spirals as though they'd traced it before.

Maren watched him with that careful look she wore when she thought he wasn't paying attention. "You said the same thing about the salt chapel."

"I did not."

"You hummed. Three notes, over and over, while you were decoding the letter. You didn't notice you were doing it." She lifted the lantern higher. Light crawled across the bone-frieze and threw their shadows huge against the far wall. "There are gaps in you, Sable. Big ones. You talk about your stall like it's your whole life because it is your whole life — fourteen years of selling charms and nothing before it. Nobody has nothing before it."

He didn't answer. There was nothing to answer with. He had asked himself the question a thousand times in the dark hours of a thousand roadside camps: *who was I before?* And every time, the answer came back the same — a smooth blankness, like the face of the eyeless saint. He remembered learning his letters. He remembered no one teaching them to him. He remembered knowing how to bind a luck-charm, and no memory of the first one, the failures, the burnt fingers every apprentice earns. Knowledge with no childhood under it, like a tree standing in air.

"Later," he said, which was what he always said, and which was becoming less true each time he said it.

The corridor opened into a chamber.

The lantern light reached only so far, but it reached enough. Pillars marched off into the dark on either side, each one wrapped in bone from floor to ceiling — not stacked, not piled, but *grown*, fused into the stone as if the pillars had been seeded with skeletons and left to flower. And at the center of the chamber, on a low dais of black marble, sat a sarcophagus. Plain. Unadorned. Its lid bore a single carving: a hand, palm up, holding nothing.

And standing between them and the sarcophagus was the warden.

It had been standing there all along, Sable realized. It simply had not bothered to move until they looked at it. Nine feet of articulated bone and corroded bronze, shaped vaguely like a man the way a cathedral is shaped vaguely like a box — joints where no man had joints, a skull crowned with a halo of fused vertebrae, and in the hollows of its eye sockets two points of pale light that were not fire and were not reflection and did not flicker.

"Oh," Maren said quietly. "That's not a good anything."

The warden's head turned. The motion was wrong — too smooth, too silent for something made of bone and metal, as if the world had simply agreed to let it move rather than making the effort of grinding parts together. When it spoke, its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, a dry rustle like pages turning in a closed book.

***TWO COME. ONE BEARS THE GIFT. ONE BEARS THE GIFT AND HIDES IT POORLY.***

Maren went white. Her free hand flew to her sternum, to the little iron medallion she wore under her shirt — the one she'd told him was a saint's token, the one Sable now understood was a suppression charm, crude and fading, barely holding back whatever faint gift the Inquisition's own testing had somehow missed.

"It knows," she breathed. "Sable, it can smell it on me—"

***THE DOOR DEMANDS A PRICE,*** the warden continued, uninterested in their alarm. ***THE GIFTED ONE WILL WALK THE TRIAL ALONE. THE OTHER WILL WAIT AT THE THRESHOLD. IF THE GIFTED ONE FAILS, THE OTHER MAY LEAVE. OR STAY AND DIE. THERE IS NO THIRD PATH.***

"And if I refuse?" Sable asked.

The pale lights in the skull's sockets brightened by a degree. It was, he realized, the construct's version of a smile.

* * *

Maren argued. Of course she argued. She argued while Sable checked his charm-bag, while he tied his sleeves back, while he walked to the threshold the warden indicated — an archway of braided ribs beyond the sarcophagus, breathing that faint sweet incense-smell. She argued in the low, fierce voice of someone who had spent her career losing arguments with institutions and had gotten very good at it anyway.

"This is exactly the kind of trap the Inquisition writes manuals about. Trial chambers kill gifted people, Sable. That's what they're *for*. Some guild-mage built this thing a century ago and it hasn't had anyone to kill since, and you're going to volunteer—"

"Maren."

"—and I'll be standing here holding a lantern like some useless—"

"*Maren.*" He took her hand. It was the first time he'd touched her on purpose, and they both noticed, and neither of them mentioned it. Her hand was cold from the catacombs and steady as a drawn blade. "Whatever happens in there — don't come after me. That's not bravery talking. The salt chapel taught me something. These trials aren't just locks. They're *questions*. And I don't think they like being answered by committee."

She looked at him for a long moment. Lantern light moved across her face and couldn't decide whether to make her look angry or afraid, and settled on both.

"When you get out," she said finally, "you're going to tell me what the question was."

"If I can answer it," he said, "I'll tell you everything."

The archway swallowed him.

* * *

Beyond the threshold, the chamber changed.

It was the same size — he could feel the walls when he stretched out his arms — and yet it was also vast, a darkness with depth to it that the physical space couldn't contain. The sweet incense smell thickened until it was almost syrup, and the floor beneath his boots softened, gave, became something else: ash. Warm ash, ankle-deep, still faintly glowing in places like a fire recently raked over.

And there were voices in it.

"Sable."

His mother's voice. He had no memory of his mother — no memory of *anyone* — and yet the voice reached into him and found the exact shape of a wound he hadn't known he carried, and pressed on it gently.

"Sable, you have to run. Don't look back. Whatever you hear, whatever you see — *don't look back*—"

"Who are you?" His voice cracked on the second word. Ash swirled around his ankles. Far off in the dark, something enormous exhaled.

The trial came for him the way winter comes — gradually, then all at once.

First the heat. It rolled out of the darkness in waves, gentle at first, then insistent, then unbearable, the heat of a burning building, and with it the smells: pitch and timber and roasting meat that his mind refused, absolutely refused, to name. Then the light — orange, flickering, sourceless — and shapes moving in it. Figures. People running. A woman's silhouette against flame. A man kneeling in the ash, hooded, his hands raised, and light pouring from those hands in a stream that looked like —

Like erasure. Like a candle-flame being pinched out, except the candle was a person.

*Sable, don't look back—*

He looked back. Of course he looked back. He was twenty-six years old and had spent his whole remembered life being careful, and here, in a pit of warm ash inside a dead abbey, he finally disobeyed the one instruction his buried past had left him.

He looked, and the vision seized him like a hook through the eye.

The fire became real. The screaming became real. He was small — he could feel it, the lowness of the ground, the way adults' legs became trees around him — and the guildhall was burning, the Charmwrights' Guildhall with its blue doors and its smell of beeswax and copper, and people he must have loved were dying in it, and through the smoke came the hooded man, and the man knelt, and the man's hands framed small-Sable's face, and the man said:

*"Forgive me. Remember nothing. One day you'll understand why remembering was the cruelest thing I could leave you."*

And light poured in, and the world went white, and —

The pain hit him all at once, everywhere, as if twelve years of erased memory were trying to force themselves back through a door that had been sealed with scar-tissue. Sable screamed. He felt his knees hit the ash. He felt blood on his lips, though he didn't remember biting anything. The visions kept coming — faster now, fragments, a ring pressed into a child's palm, a dragon's eye the size of a doorway, the guildmaster's voice saying *the covenant is breaking, boy, and we broke it* — and each fragment drove a spike of pressure through his skull like the memory-seal itself was fighting back, was *killing him rather than be questioned.*

Somewhere very far away, he heard Maren scream his name.

He heard her boots in the ash. He heard the warden's rustling voice pronounce some doom — ***THE THRESHOLD IS CLOSED TO HER*** — and he heard Maren reply with words he'd never heard her use, words that would have gotten her stripped of rank in the Inquisition tower, and then—

Then warmth.

Not the trial's furnace-heat. Something gentler, threading through the agony like water through cracked stone. He felt hands find his — small, strong, trembling — and he felt something *wake* in the contact, a resonance he'd felt only twice before: once from the salt relic, and once, briefly, accidentally, from Maren herself on the night she'd let him slip past Hale's men on the rooftops of Corvane.

Her gift. Faint, untrained, wild — and *hers*, offered freely, with no technique and no defense, just a frightened woman pouring everything she had through her palms because the alternative was watching him die.

The memory-seal met her gift and recoiled.

Not broke — recoiled, like a snake striking glass. The pressure eased. The visions dimmed to embers. Sable gasped back into his own body, into the ash-chamber, into Maren's arms, and the last thing he saw before the trial released them both was the hooded figure in the dying fire turning its head — deliberately, knowingly — to look at *her*.

* * *

He woke on the dais beside the sarcophagus, with the lid already slid open and Maren's cloak bundled under his head.

The sarcophagus was empty except for what lay in the bottom: a key. Not metal — bone, or something like bone, long as his forearm and carved with the same spiral script that covered the catacomb walls. It was warm. Warmer than his skin, warmer than a living body, and when he closed his fingers around it the warmth climbed his arm and settled behind his breastbone like a second heart.

Beside the key, placed with deliberate care, was a guild signet ring. Blue enamel. The Charmwrights' crest. His guildmaster's ring — the same ring the hooded figure had worn in the vision, the same ring that had appeared in the salt-relic's showing of the covenant's breaking.

Maren sat slumped against the sarcophagus, gray-faced, her suppression medallion cracked clean down the middle. She caught him looking at it.

"It broke when I — when I did whatever it was I did." She laughed, a thin, shaky sound with no humor in it. "Twelve years hiding it. Twelve years of iron tokens and praying no one tested me twice. And it takes one night in a hole full of bones to burn the whole disguise." She looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and brave. "So that's it, then. I'm one of you now. Officially."

"Maren—"

"Don't. Don't thank me and don't apologize and don't tell me I should have stayed at the threshold, because I know, and I didn't care." She scrubbed a hand across her face. "What happened in there? What did it show you?"

He sat up slowly. The world tilted and steadied. The key pulsed against his chest, and the ring sat in his other palm, and he looked at it for a long time before he answered.

"It showed me my guildmaster," he said at last. "Burning the guildhall. Erasing a child's mind." His voice came out level, which surprised him; inside, everything was falling like the abbey above them. "It showed me *me*, Maren. Before. And it showed me—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "It showed me that the man who raised me, the man who taught me everything, the man whose grave holds the third key — that man broke the covenant. And he made sure I could never remember it."

Silence filled the chamber. Even the warden had gone still, its pale eye-lights banked low, as if listening.

Maren reached over and took the ring from his hand. She turned it once in the lantern light, then pressed it back into his palm and closed his fingers over it.

"Then we go dig him up," she said, "and ask him ourselves."

Above them, far away, the abbey bell — the empty yoke where the bell had hung — began, impossibly, to toll.

Something had heard them coming. Something had known they were one key from the end. And in the silence after the last note faded, both of them heard it, drifting up from the deep places of the world: the sound of wings.
