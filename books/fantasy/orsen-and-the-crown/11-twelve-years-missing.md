## 11. Twelve Years Missing

They made camp in the hollow of a dead oak so vast that three grown men with arms linked could not have circled its trunk. The relic of salt hung warm against Sable's chest inside its oilcloth wrapping, and even now, hours after the vision it had shown him, he could still smell the burning — that phantom reek of scales and sanctity that had poured through the drowned chapel and into his skull.

Maren built the fire small. She was getting good at small fires; three weeks on the road toward Thornhollow had taught her the warden's trick of banking coals in a pit lined with green stone so that no light escaped above knee-height. She crouched over it now, turning a skewer of rabbit with the patience of someone who had once waited whole days outside a suspect's door.

Sable sat against the oak's root-wall and tried to remember his mother's face.

It should have been easy. He was — by his own best accounting — twenty-six years old. He had a trade, a name, a scar on his left forearm from a soldering slip at age nineteen (he remembered *that*; he remembered the smell of the hot iron and the taste of the curse he'd bitten back). But when he reached for the years before fourteen, before the guildhall, before Guildmaster Corvus Ash set a whetstone in his hands and said *a charm is only as honest as its maker*, he found —

Nothing.

Not fog. Not darkness. Fog and darkness were *something*; they had texture, edges, weight. What lay behind his fourteenth year was more like the space behind a wall. Smooth plaster. Painted, maybe, to look like a life. But when he pressed his palm against it there was no give, no depth, no room behind the surface at all.

He must have made some sound, because Maren looked up.

"You've gone grey," she said. "Greyer than the rabbit deserves."

"Tell me about your childhood," Sable said.

She blinked. "That's an odd request from a man holding a rabbit bone."

"Humor me."

So she did, in her flat, unhurried way, turning the skewer as she talked. A mill town on the Verren water. A father who milled flour and sang badly. A sister who could make bread rise without yeast and never told anyone but Maren, and only once, whispering under the covers, both of them terrified and delighted. The winter the sister's gift showed and their father burned her shoes because he thought the devil came in through the feet first. Maren described it all with the detachment of a woman reading someone else's letter home, but she described it *fully* — the name of the street, the crooked shutter on the mill, the exact shade of blue paint peeling off the kitchen door.

"And you?" she asked. "Where were you raised? You've never said."

"I was raised in the guildhall," Sable said. "Corvane. Charmwrights' Guild."

"Yes, I know that much. Before that."

"Before that—" He stopped. The wall again. His hand, in his mind, pressed flat against painted plaster. "I came to the guild when I was fourteen. Corvus took me in."

"Took you in from where?"

"From—"

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark above them, an owl called twice and went silent, as if it too were waiting for the answer.

"I don't know," Sable said quietly.

Maren set down the skewer. "You don't know where you lived for the first fourteen years of your life."

"It was twelve years ago. Things fade." Even as he said it, he heard how false it rang. Twelve years was nothing. He could remember the smell of the guildhall's tallow vats. He could remember the first charm he'd ever sold — a fever-knot, five coppers, to a fishwife with shaking hands. Memory did not fade like this. Memory did not fade *selectively*, taking every face and place and voice before one bright boundary line and leaving everything after intact, sharp as etched glass.

He had simply never stood far enough back to see the shape of the hole. It took another person, standing beside him, looking at him from the outside.

Maren was looking at him from the outside now, and her face had gone very still — the stillness she wore when a suspect said something that didn't fit the confession, something true.

"Sable," she said. "Say the alphabet backward for me."

"What? Why?"

"Humor me."

He got through it, frowning, and she nodded slowly. Then: "Count backward from two hundred by sevens."

"One ninety-three, one eighty-six—" He stopped. "I'm a charmwright, not a clerk. What is this?"

"A truth-probe," she said. "A crude one. Wardens use it on prisoners who claim memory loss — the mind stumbles differently when it's lying than when it's been broken. Liars get tired. They start refusing. Broken minds don't refuse. They just hit the wall, over and over, in the same place." Her eyes held his across the little ring of firelight. "You're hitting the wall, Sable. Every time. Fourteen years old, and then nothing, and then fourteen years old again."

The rabbit fat hissed and spat. Sable became aware of his own heartbeat, absurdly loud.

"There's a better way than parlor tricks," Maren went on. "There's a charm — a real one. Truth-oil, the guilds used to call it. It doesn't make you tell the truth exactly. It makes you *unable not to look*. Whatever your mind is hiding, you'll be looking straight at it while we talk. It hurts. Sometimes it hurts a great deal. And if what's behind that wall of yours is anything like what the salt relic showed you—" she glanced, deliberately, at the oilcloth bundle against his chest "—then it will hurt more than a little."

"The relic showed me a man breaking the covenant," Sable said. "Twelve years ago. The night it shattered. And it showed me a guildmaster's ring on his hand." He turned his own right hand over, looked at the plain iron band he'd worn since he was sixteen — Corvus Ash's ring, passed to him in a sealed box along with a letter full of warnings and relics and guilt. "Corvus's ring. My ring now."

"Do you want to know?" Maren asked. "Truly? Because I'll tell you something, Sable Montrose, and you can weigh it however you like. In six years with the Inquisition I have never once seen a man thank himself for digging up what was buried. But I have watched a dozen men destroy themselves by *not* digging, and every one of them dug anyway, later, alone, badly." She fed another twig to the fire. "Better here, with me, with a fire and a friend. Later will not be kinder."

*A friend.* The word sat strangely warm in his chest, and he distrusted it slightly, the way he distrusted all charms that worked too fast.

"All right," he said.

* * *

The truth-oil was not oil at all. It was a resin, Maren explained, scraped from a tree that grew only in churchyards, which told you most of what you needed to know about the people who'd discovered it. She carried a bead of it wrapped in waxed linen at the bottom of her kit, confiscated years ago from a hedge-priest she'd been ordered to arrest and had instead let walk into the marshes. She'd never used it. She'd never dared. Using it meant admitting she knew what it was, and knowing meant—

"Well," she said, and didn't finish, and Sable understood that her secrets were stacked behind walls too.

She melted the bead in a spoon over the fire and stirred in three drops of water and a pinch of salt — *from the chapel*, she said, and neither of them remarked aloud on what that might mean — until the resin turned from amber to a pale, luminous white, glowing faintly, like milk with moonlight in it.

"Drink it warm," she said. "And Sable — whatever you see, say it out loud. Don't hold it in your mouth. Held-in things curdle."

"That's charming advice."

"I'm an inquisitor. We're full of charming advice." But her hand, passing him the cup, was not quite steady, and he loved her a little for it, and hated himself a little for noticing.

The draught tasted the way snow smells — clean, cold, utterly without flavor, and then, underneath, faintly sweet, the sweetness of communion wine or funeral cake. He swallowed. For a moment, nothing.

Then the world tilted.

Not the world — his *attention*. It was as if someone had taken the lamp he kept pointed forward, always forward, at the road ahead, and turned it around to shine directly into his own face. He gasped. He was looking inward, and inward was vast, and lit, and arranged — shelves, he thought, dizzily, the mind presented itself as shelves — and nearly all of them were full. Full of guildhall mornings, of Corvus's voice saying *measure thrice*, of the bell-towers of Corvane at dusk, of Maren's face lit by a hundred small fires on a hundred cold nights since the rooftop. Richly, densely full.

And then the shelves ended. There was a gap in the wall of him, a clean-edged absence, and the lamp-light poured into it and found — nothing, nothing, nothing —

No. Not nothing. Because the oil would not let him call it nothing. The oil made him *look*.

At the very bottom of the gap, at the place where the wall met the floor, there was a seam. Hairline-fine, like the join of a well-made illusion, like the edge of a charm laid by a master hand. And through the seam leaked light. Fire-colored light.

"Talk to me," Maren said, from far away. "What do you see?"

"Fire," Sable heard himself say. His voice sounded strange, unmoored, coming from somewhere above him. "Fire first. Always fire first."

*The mountain.* No — a house near the mountain, a low stone house under Mount Kaelen's shadow, and he was small, he was *small*, seven years old maybe, eight, and the fire was not eating the house, it was *in* the hearth where fire belonged, and yet everyone was screaming, because the fire was talking. It had a voice like rockfall and it was speaking words he almost understood, words in the deep grammar that charms were only shadows of, and his mother — a woman's hands, brown and quick, pulling him behind her apron — was crying and laughing at once, and outside the window the sky was full of wings, real wings, vast wings, and the covenant was alive, the covenant was *whole*, the world hummed with it like a struck bell that never finished ringing —

"Sable. Keep talking."

"There's a woman. Hands. I think — I think she's my mother. I can't see her face. Why can't I see her face?" Panic rose in him like floodwater. "I can see her hands, her hands are right *there*, why is her face—"

"Because faces are names," Maren said softly, "and names were taken. Keep going. You're doing well."

The fire-vision guttered and changed. Older now — he was eleven, twelve, and the stone house was gone, replaced by travel, by roads, by the inside of a covered wagon smelling of dried herbs and char-smoke. A man sat across from him with a grey beard and tired eyes and a guildmaster's ring on one finger.

*Corvus.*

Younger Corvus. Corvus with sorrow already carved deep into him, Corvus holding Sable's small hands in his large ones, saying, in a voice rough as rope: *"Listen to me, lad. Bad men are coming. Men who want what lives under the mountain. Your mother sent you with me because I swore an oath older than kingdoms, and I mean to keep half of it."*

Half. Why half? What was the other half?

*"I'm going to do something to you that I will burn for,"* Corvus said, and his eyes were wet, and Sable — child-Sable — was not afraid, which seemed impossible, except that the man's hands were shaking and somehow that steadied him, the way a trembling lantern still gives light. *"One day you'll hate me for it. One day, if the world is kind, you'll understand it. I'm sorry, boy. I'm so sorry. Sleep."*

"No," adult-Sable said aloud, gripping his knees. "No, wait, don't—"

The seam split.

* * *

There was a hand on his face.

That was the first thing — a hand, broad and warm and terrible, pressing over his eyes and forehead like a benediction, like a burial. Behind the hand, Corvus's voice was chanting, and the chant was not any charm Sable knew, it was older and colder, a rite written in the margin of some book that should never have been opened, and it was *unmaking* him. Carefully. Lovingly. The way a surgeon unseams a body.

Twelve years ago. The guildhall. He could feel the guildhall around the memory now — the tallow-smell, the rain on the roof, the other apprentices asleep upstairs — no, wait, no, the others were gone, sent away a week before, *all the others sent away*, only Corvus and the boy remained, and through the windows of the workroom the sky was the wrong color. Green-white. Torn. The covenant was breaking somewhere far to the north and the whole world's light had gone sick with it, and Corvus chanted on, tears cutting tracks through the ash on his face.

Because there was ash. Because the guildhall's eastern wing was burning. Because three nights ago, King Aldric's father — no, *Prince* then, the old king still breathing his last upstairs in the palace — had sent men with a writ and torches, men who wanted the Charmwrights' registers, the guild's great ledger of every gifted soul in Veyra, names and natures and weaknesses, a weapon to hand to whichever prince won the succession —

And Corvus had burned the registers instead. And the price of burning them was the guild's destruction, unless — unless —

Unless the guild gave the new king something else. Something worth more than names. A bargain struck in whispers between Corvus Ash and the spymaster of a dying king: *the guild dies, the registers burn, and in exchange we give you the mountain. We give you the dragon. We show your mages where the covenant-stone sleeps and how the seals were laid and how they might be unwound — not tonight, not while the old king lives, but soon. And the Charmwrights' Guild, officially, formally, historically, is destroyed — so that no one will ever look for what it hid.*

The guild died to save the dragon. That was the oath. That was the half.

And the other half — the other half was the boy. Because the boy had *seen*. Had seen the spymaster come and go, had heard the word *Emberveil* spoken over candle flame, had felt the shape of the betrayal in the guildmaster's silence. And the spymaster's terms were clear: no witnesses. The boy dies, or the boy forgets.

Corvus had chosen forgetting. Had chosen it kneeling, had chosen it weeping, had chosen it with the same hand that had taught the boy to solder and to swear and to measure thrice — a hand laid over the child's eyes like a blessing, chanting the unmaking-rite, erasing mother and mountain and fire and wings and *everything*, carving him smooth as fresh plaster, painting a new life on the surface: an orphan of fourteen, taken in by charity, remembering nothing.

*So that one day,* the dying part of Corvus's intent whispered beneath the chant, beneath the tears, *so that one day, when he finds the relics, when he stands before the stone, he will be clean enough to touch it. Uncompromised. Unbargained. Guilty of nothing but his own erased past. Let the debt be mine alone.*

"Sable!" Maren's voice, sharp, close. "Come back. Breathe. Breathe!"

* * *

He came back to himself on the ground, though he had no memory of falling, with Maren's hands on his shoulders and the fire burned down to sullen orange and the taste of blood in his mouth — he'd bitten his tongue, or his cheek, sometime during the screaming. He had been screaming. His throat told him so.

"Maren," he rasped.

"I'm here."

"He erased me." The words came out flat and strange, each one placed down carefully, like cups on a shelf that might shatter. "My own guildmaster. The man who taught me everything. He stood in front of me with his hand on my face and he *unmade* me, and he wept while he did it, and the worst part—" His voice cracked. "The worst part is that I can still feel how gentle he was. I can feel that he loved me. He loved me enough to leave me a mission instead of a childhood."

Maren said nothing. She was holding his shoulders and looking at him with an expression he couldn't read — horror and pity and something else, something almost like recognition, and he realized dimly that she was thinking of her sister's shoes burning, of all the small violations done in the name of protection.

"The covenant," Sable went on. He had to say it all while the oil still glowed in his veins, while the shelves stood open. "Corvus traded it. The location of the covenant-stone, the rites, the seals — he handed it to the crown to buy the guild's death, because a dead guild can't be conscripted. And the spymaster of the old king — Maren, the spymaster is still alive. Still serving. He serves Prince Aldric now. Everything Aldric is doing, the Edict, the hunt, the draining — it's all built on what Corvus sold them twelve years ago. On what *I saw* and was made to forget."

"So the third relic," Maren said slowly, "the one the letter named—"

"Is the grave." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "The letter says the last key lies with the guildmaster. I thought it meant buried with him. Some token in his coffin. But it's not the grave, Maren. It's the *memory*. I'm the key. The last key is the thing he locked inside me — the witness, the knowledge, the debt. The relic refuses anyone who comes empty. It refused *me* because I came empty. I have to go back to Thornhollow, to his tomb, and stand there with a hole in my head shaped like everything that matters, and ask a dead man's magic to finish tearing the seal open."

He put his head in his hands. The fire ticked. Far off, the owl called again, indifferent.

"When I was under," he said, quieter. "In the memory. At the end — Corvus said something else. Something I haven't told you yet." He lifted his face. "He said the seal wasn't made to last forever. He said it would fray on its own, thread by thread, and that fraying would *feel like longing*. Like homesickness for a place I'd never been." He touched the iron ring on his finger. "He said: *when the boy starts dreaming of the mountain, the seal is failing, and the hour has come. Send him to my grave, and pray he is braver than I was.*"

Maren stared at him. "Have you been dreaming of the mountain?"

Every night since the drowned chapel. Wings of green-white fire. A woman's hands. A voice like rockfall calling a name he couldn't pronounce.

"Every night," Sable said. "The seal is failing, Maren. Which means the hour has come. And I have to go dig up the only father I ever knew, and take back what he stole from me, and I am *terrified* that when it's done — when I know everything — I won't recognize the man sitting here telling you this."

Outside the hollow oak, the wind shifted, and carried on it — faint, impossible, leagues from any sea — the smell of salt.
