## 12. Hale's Net

The letter came on a Tuesday, folded twice, sealed with grey wax that bore no crest at all — which was, in the Inquisition, a courtesy. Uncrested seals meant private correspondence, and private correspondence from Inquisitor Hale meant one of two things: an offer of trust, or a trap dressed as one.

Maren Vale read it three times in the narrow room above the chandler's shop in Thornhollow, where she had been sleeping badly for a week and pretending otherwise to everyone, including herself.

*Vale,* it said, in Hale's cramped, upright hand, the letters pressed so hard into the paper they nearly cut through. *Your partner Doss was taken two nights past on the Salt Road. He is alive. He is not comfortable. You know what I do to men who lie to me, and you know I have never once done it to you. Come to the tollhouse at Miller's Bend alone, before the moon sets Thursday, or I will stop needing him comfortable.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Hale's handwriting was a signature; it was practically a portrait.

Maren sat with the letter in her lap while the candle burned down another finger's width, and she thought about Doss.

Dennet Doss. Twenty-six years old, freckled, loud, constitutionally incapable of entering a room quietly. He had been assigned to her eighteen months ago as her partner, fresh from the training halls in Corvane, and he had spent the first month calling her *sir* by accident and the second month apologizing for it, and somewhere around the third month he had stopped being an assignment and started being a friend. He knew about her. Not everything — no one knew everything — but he knew her gift was real, that it lived in her like a low banked coal, a warmth behind the sternum that rose when she was afraid. He had caught her once, in the spring, coaxing a dying bird back to breath in the courtyard of the tower, and he had said nothing then and nothing ever after, except once, drunk on festival wine, he'd gripped her shoulder and said, *You're the best person I know, Vale, and if that makes me a bad inquisitor then hang me.*

She had laughed. She could still hear herself laughing.

And now he was in a cell because of her, because he had gone looking when she vanished from the tower after the Salt Road ambush, because he had asked questions in the wrong rooms with his terrible loud voice, because loyalty in the Inquisition was a disease and they had both caught it.

Maren looked at the letter again. *Come alone.*

He would be watching the road. Of course he would be. Hale missed nothing; it was the thing people found most frightening about him, and the thing Maren had privately always found most comforting, back when she still believed the institution he served deserved her comfort. If Sable were anywhere within thirty miles, Hale would see him. And Sable *was* within thirty miles. Sable was downstairs, asleep in the chandler's storeroom among crates of tallow, sleeping the way he always slept now — badly, curled small, one hand closed around the salt relic as though someone might pry it from him even in dreams.

If Maren went to Miller's Bend alone, Hale might let Sable go. Might. The word did a great deal of work in that sentence.

But if she told Sable, he would come anyway. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat. Three weeks of shared roads and shared fires and one terrible night in the drowned chapel had taught her the shape of the man: he carried guilt like other men carried knives, close and always ready, and he had already decided — she'd seen it in his face at Thornhollow, when the wardens came for the children — that he was done running while other people paid his debts.

So she would not tell him. That was the arithmetic of it, cold and simple, and she hated how easily it came to her, how naturally she slid into the institution's own grammar: *one life weighed against the mission, the mission outweighing.* She had watched Hale do this math a hundred times without flinching. Now she was doing it, and her hands were shaking.

*One life,* she thought, and then corrected herself, because the Inquisition's grammar counted lives but hers had never learned to. *Doss. His name is Dennet Doss and he laughs through his nose and he can't hold his liquor and he covered for me for eighteen months knowing exactly what I am.*

She fed the letter to the candle and watched it curl and blacken, and upstairs the chandler's dog began to bark at nothing, and Maren Vale sat in the dark and made her plans the way a condemned woman makes them: carefully, completely, and without any expectation of needing them for long.

* * *

Telling Sable nothing proved harder than she expected, because Sable noticed things. It was the charmwright in him, maybe, or just the particular attentiveness of a man who had survived ten years by reading every room for exits. On Wednesday morning he caught her staring at the fire and said, "You've got your thinking face on," and she said, "I'm always thinking," and he said, "Not like that," and she changed the subject to the route over the Kessick hills, and he let her, because he was kind, and kindness, she reflected bitterly, was going to make this so much harder than suspicion would have.

They spent the day doing what they had been doing all week: preparing. The bone relic rode against Sable's chest in a leather sling she had sewn for him. Maps of the mountain approaches lay open on the floor, weighted with candlesticks. Somewhere north, under Mount Kaelen, a dragon's fire was guttering, and somewhere south, in Corvane, Prince Aldric was building engines to drink that fire dry, and the third key — whatever it was, wherever it hid — waited for them like a held breath.

"You're quiet," Sable said again, near dusk.

"I'm tired."

"You're lying."

"Charmwrights don't get to call anyone a liar." She smiled at him, and it cost her something physical, like lifting a weight. "Get some sleep. We leave early."

He went down to the storeroom eventually, and she listened to the house settle around her — the tick of cooling wax, the dog turning circles on its blanket, the wind working at the shutters — and she wrote two things.

The first was a note, short enough to memorize, long enough to matter:

*Sable. By the time you find this I'll be gone. Don't follow. Doss is taken and Hale has offered a trade — my surrender for your freedom, and I mean to take it, because unlike you I have never once managed to stop being an inquisitor, even when the institution stopped deserving it. The relics are yours. The third key is yours. Whatever it costs, pay it. I'll get myself out. I always do.*

It was a lie, the last sentence. She had never gotten herself out of anything in her life; she had only ever been gotten out of things by luck, by Doss, by Sable. But lies were tools too, and a tool left behind should be one that worked.

The second thing she wrote was smaller, and she almost didn't write it at all. A single line, unsigned, tucked into the fold of the first:

*Thank you for the chapel. For the fire. For not asking more than I could give.*

Then she took her coat, her knife, her inquisitor's badge — she turned the badge over in her hand for a long moment, that dull iron disc with its stamped eye, the emblem of an order that hunted people like her, that she had served since she was sixteen because it was the only place that had ever wanted her — and she put the badge in her pocket instead of leaving it, because she was going to need it where she was going, and walked out into the dark toward Miller's Bend.

* * *

The tollhouse at Miller's Bend crouched beside the frozen river like something that had died there years ago and been too stubborn to fall over. Its windows were boarded. Its toll-gate leaned at a drunken angle across the empty road. Moonlight came off the ice in long white blades, and the cold was the honest kind, the kind that told you plainly it intended to kill you if you stood still too long.

Hale was waiting inside, in the old toll-keeper's room, seated on a stool with his hands folded, as though he had been there for hours, which he probably had. Two wardens stood by the door. A lantern burned low between them all, and next to the lantern, on the floor, sat Dennet Doss.

They had hurt him. Not badly — Hale did not waste damage — but his lip was split and one eye was swollen half-shut and his wrists were bound in front of him with the grey cord they used on gifted prisoners, the cord woven through with cold-iron thread that made the air around it feel wrong, feel *thin*. When he saw her his whole face tried to do several things at once — relief, shame, warning — and settled on saying her name, muffled, around whatever they'd used to gag him.

"Doss," Maren said. "Hello."

"Sit down, Vale." Hale's voice was quiet. It was always quiet. Men who shouted were advertising; Hale had never needed to advertise anything in his life. "You got my letter."

"I got your letter."

"And you came alone." His eyes went past her, through the doorway, out into the dark, holding there a moment, checking. She watched him check and understood that he already knew the answer, that this was ritual rather than verification. Hale believed in procedure the way priests believed in liturgy. "Good. Sit."

She sat, on an upturned crate, across the lantern from him. Up close she could see what the last months had done to him — the grey coming into his beard faster, the lines around his eyes carved deeper. He was fifty-two years old and he had spent thirty of those years hunting magic-bearers, and she had known him for six of them, and in all that time he had never once raised his voice at her, never once lied to her, and never once, until this exact moment, given her reason to doubt that the man and the office were the same thing.

"Where is Montrose?" he asked.

"Gone. South, I think. You'll not catch him."

"He was at Thornhollow during the raid. Witnesses described a man matching him defending the villagers." Hale's gaze didn't waver. "Witnesses also described a woman. Red hair, inquisitor's coat, throwing light from her bare hands."

"The light was a signal flare," Maren said. "Standard issue."

"Vale." He said her name the way other men drew swords. "I have read your file. All of it. I have read it four times since the Thornhollow reports crossed my desk, and I will tell you what I found in it, because you deserve honesty from someone tonight. I found a girl whose parents burned in the Vellmarsh fever riots, taken in by the Order at fifteen, tested clean, trained clean, promoted clean. I found commendations. I found restraint — eleven documented instances of using minimum force where maximum was sanctioned. I found a career that any inquisitor in this kingdom would trade their teeth for." He paused. "And I found that the testing was done by Master Corvel, who is dead now, and whose records I have lately had cause to reexamine. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That you think my tests were falsified."

"I think," Hale said, "that Master Corvel falsified a great many tests in his later years, and that I have spent the autumn discovering why, and that every answer leads somewhere I do not want to go." For the first time since she'd entered the room, he looked away from her — down at Doss, at the grey cord on the boy's wrists, at his own folded hands. "The Edict of Cleansing is not what the proclamations say it is. You've seen it. You've felt it. Confessions that arrive pre-written. Prisoners transported before trial. Orders from the spymaster's office that bypass mine entirely. I built my life on this institution, Vale. I have executed people under its authority, and slept afterward, because I believed the authority was sound." His jaw tightened. "It is not sound. It has not been sound for some time. And I am sitting in a freezing tollhouse at midnight negotiating with the only subordinate I ever trusted, whom I have just had beaten, in order to bait a fugitive I have orders to kill on sight — and I find that I cannot tell anymore which parts of me are the inquisitor and which parts are the man who signed all of this into motion by being too loyal to look up."

The lantern hissed. Out on the river, ice boomed and settled, some vast slow shifting beneath the surface.

"Hale," Maren said quietly, "what do you want?"

"What I have always wanted. Order." He laughed, briefly, without any humor in it at all. "That's the joke of it. I wanted order so badly that I helped build the machine that's dismantling it. And now—" He straightened. Something in his face closed, the confession shuttered away behind the office again, and when he spoke his voice was level and official and terribly tired. "Here are the terms, Vale, and I'll not offer them twice. Montrose goes free. My wardens stand down from the southern roads for seven days — after that, I can't promise anything, because after that the spymaster's own hunters take the field and they are not mine to command. In exchange, you come with me. You surrender your badge, your gift enters the record, and you go into custody pending review."

"Review," Maren repeated. "By whom?"

"By me." He met her eyes. "Which means you'll live. Which means you'll be in a cell instead of a grave, and cells have doors, and doors have locks, and locks fail. I'm not offering you freedom, girl. I'm offering you time. It's the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit."

Behind the gag, Doss made a noise — protest, argument, the beginning of one of his terrible loud speeches, strangled at birth. Maren looked at him. He looked back at her with his one good eye, furious and frightened and loyal clear through to the marrow, and she thought: *this is what they do to us. They take the people we love and they price them against our principles, and whichever we choose, we lose something we needed.*

She had already chosen. She had chosen it three nights ago by candlelight, and choosing it again now, out loud, in front of the man himself, was almost easy.

"Terms accepted," she said, and stood, and took the badge from her pocket, and set it on the crate between them with a small iron click that sounded, in the silence, like a door closing.

Hale looked at the badge for a long moment. Then he reached out and turned it face-up, so the stamped eye stared at the ceiling, and he said, very softly, as though to it rather than to her: "I'm sorry. Both of you. I suspect I'll be saying that a great deal more before this is finished."

* * *

They marched her south through the frozen night, Doss limping beside her with his wrists still corded, wardens ahead and behind, Hale riding at the rear like a man escorting his own funeral. Toward dawn the towers of Corvane rose out of the grey — black spires against a bruised sky, the Inquisition's tower tallest of all, its bell silent, its windows dark.

"They say the quiet bells," Doss murmured through split lips, as the gates opened, "mean they're questioning someone."

"I know," Maren said. "I've heard them."

She had heard them her whole career, that deep soft tolling that rolled across the city whenever the question-rooms below were in use, and she had always told herself it meant justice being done, slowly and properly, the way she'd been taught. Now she walked toward the sound itself, down stairs that spiraled below the waterline, into corridors where the stones sweated cold, and the bells overhead tolled once — for her, she understood, a kind of courtesy, a professional acknowledgment — and the door of the question-room opened, and inside it sat a thin man with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a ledger, who smiled at her with genuine warmth and said:

"Inquisitor Vale. At last. I've been so looking forward to our conversation."

And Maren, who had spent three weeks memorizing the date hidden in Aldric's requisitions, the date the draining engines would reach Mount Kaelen, folded that knowledge down small and deep behind her sternum, next to the banked coal of her gift, and smiled back.

"So have I," she said.
