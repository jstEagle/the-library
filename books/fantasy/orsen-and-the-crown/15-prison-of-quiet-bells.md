## 15. Prison of Quiet Bells

The bells of the Inquisition tower did not ring.

That was the first thing Maren understood about the place they had brought her — the Tower of Quiet Bells, the wardens called it, though no one said the name aloud within Hale's hearing. Its bell chamber stood empty, its bronze tongues removed and melted down years ago, cast into shackles for the gifted. A tower built to make noise that had been surgically, deliberately silenced. She thought of it as she sat in her cell on the first night, listening to the absence: a sound so total it became its own kind of ringing, a hum behind her ears that never stopped.

They had taken her boots, her belt, the thin silver chain she wore beneath her collar — the one with the charm Sable had made her, back when she still pretended to herself that she was only curious about his craft. They had taken everything except the thing they could not see: the faint warmth in her blood, the gift she had spent nine years burying under procedure and doctrine and the careful, practiced flatness of an inquisitor's face.

The cell was clean. That was how she knew it was meant for someone important. Whitewashed stone, a narrow cot with an actual mattress, a basin, a candle stub replaced each dawn by a silent attendant who never spoke and never looked at her. No irons bolted to the wall. No drain in the floor. Prisoners meant to be broken were kept in rooms designed for mess. This room was designed for conversation.

*They think I will talk,* she thought, lying awake in the dark, counting the seams in the stone above her head. *They think everyone talks.*

She had watched enough interrogations from the other side of the table to know the architecture of it. The first days were nothing — bread, water, silence, the slow erosion of certainty that came from not knowing whether it was morning or evening. The questions began soft: her name, her rank, her years of service. Then sharper. Then the names of everyone she had ever let slip through her hands during a raid, every warden she had ordered to look away, every file she had mislabeled or lost.

Maren answered what was safe to answer and held the rest behind her teeth like a stone.

On the fourth day, they moved her.

* * *

The interrogation room was at the top of the tower, where the bells used to hang. She knew this because the ceiling arched high above the single table, and iron hooks still studded the beams overhead, empty now, their shadows long in the lamplight. The window had been bricked over. The air tasted of cold metal and old smoke.

Two men waited for her. One was a clerk, gray and forgettable, who would write down whatever was said. The other —

The other was Corvane's spymaster.

She had seen him twice before, always at a distance, always in Aldric's shadow: Master Ellery Voss, a lean man in unrelieved black, with the pale, unblinking eyes of someone who had looked into too many minds and found them all disappointing. He did not carry himself like a soldier or a priest. He carried himself like a man reading a book he had already finished, waiting only for the last page to turn.

"Maren Vale," he said, when she was seated. Not a question. "Inquisitor, third circle. Eleven years of service. Commendations for restraint." He smiled slightly, as if the word amused him. "Your superiors valued you because you did not burn what you could quietly extinguish. I have read your case files. You are careful. You are controlled. You are, I suspect, exactly the kind of woman who believes she can sit across from me and give me nothing."

"I can try," Maren said.

Voss laughed — a dry, brief sound, like a branch snapping. "You may. But we will not begin with force. Force is for people whose minds are already broken; it teaches them nothing useful and ruins the material. We will begin with something gentler."

He drew from his coat a small object and set it on the table between them. It was a ring of dull brass, wide as a signet, set with a stone the color of deep water — not blue, not green, something between, something that seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at it.

"Do you know what this is?"

"A truth-stone," Maren said. "I've confiscated six of them."

"Seven," Voss corrected mildly. "You missed one. It belonged to a charmwright in the Saltmarket who sold it as a luck-piece. The guild's work was better than anyone credits — which is rather the point of this entire unfortunate season." He slid the ring toward himself but did not put it on. "Here is what will happen, Inquisitor Vale. I will wear this. I will ask you questions. Under its influence, you will find it difficult to say anything untrue — not impossible, the guild never achieved perfection, but difficult, the way it is difficult to hold your breath past the point of panic. And I will be watching for the tells. The hesitation before a lie. The slight thickening of the voice. The eyes that go somewhere else."

"And if I refuse to answer?"

"Then you will answer anyway, just more slowly, and we will both waste an afternoon." He folded his hands. "But I want to offer you something first, because I believe in economy. Tell me where Sable Montrose has gone, tell me what relics he carries, and you will walk out of this tower with your rank restored and your record sealed. The prince does not want you. He wants the Charmwright. You are, forgive me, a rounding error."

Maren looked at the truth-stone for a long moment. Then she looked at Voss.

"You're offering me my life for his."

"I'm offering you your life for a name and a road. Whether he lives afterward depends entirely on choices neither of us controls."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we do this the slow way, and you learn what the inside of your own mind looks like when someone else is walking through it." He put on the ring.

Something happened then that Maren felt before she understood. It was not pain. It was warmth — a low, spreading warmth behind her sternum, like standing near a hearth after years in the cold, and with it a terrible loosening, a sense that everything she had ever clenched shut was being asked, politely, insistently, to open.

*So that's what it feels like from this side,* she thought, and hated herself for the wonder in it.

"State your name," Voss said.

"Maren Vale." It came out easily. It was true.

"Are you gifted?"

And here — here was the moment she had been rehearsing since the day she first understood what she was, nine years ago, crouched over a dying beggar in the Ropewalk whose fever broke when she laid her hand on his brow and *willed*, stupidly, desperately, for it to break. The moment she had built her whole double life around.

"No," she said.

The lie caught in her throat like a fishbone. She felt it snag — felt the warmth flare, felt her own voice thicken half a shade, felt the impulse to cough rise up her windpipe. She coughed. Deliberately. Let her eyes water. Let the clerk note it.

Voss leaned forward, interested. "Let me ask again. Are you gifted?"

"No." The second time was easier. Not easy — nothing about it was easy — but she had learned something in the space between the two answers, something vital: the stone did not compel truth. It *punished* falsehood. It made lying feel like swallowing hot sand. But a lie spoken slowly, wrapped in genuine effort, cushioned by real physical distress, left traces no different from fear. Every prisoner she had ever questioned had trembled. Trembling was free. Trembling was everywhere.

"You're afraid," Voss observed. "Good. Fear reads honestly. Continue being afraid."

*I intend to,* she thought, and gave him the small, tight smile of a woman agreeing with her doctor.

For three hours he walked her through her life. Her childhood in the river districts — true, all of it, offered freely, because truth freely given buys credit. Her mother's death. Her recruitment into the Inquisition at sixteen. Her first burning, the charmwright named Odell Fen whose hands shook so badly at the stake that the faggots had to be stacked twice. She told that one truly too, and watched Voss file it away, and understood that he was building a map of her conscience so he could find its rivers and fords.

He asked about Sable Montrose. She told him about the rooftop in Corvane, about letting him go — that much was already known, already in Hale's reports, and denying it would only cost her credibility she might need later. She told him she had pursued him afterward out of duty. Also true. She did not tell him why the pursuit had become something else. When he pressed — *when did your duty stop being duty?* — she lied again, and coughed again, and let him read the cough as shame.

Shame was useful. Shame was what he expected to find. She fed it to him in spoonfuls.

By the time the clerk gathered his papers, the candle had burned down two fingers' width, and Voss looked almost satisfied. Almost. There was a crease between his brows that had not been there at the start.

"You are holding something," he said. "Not the thing itself — I'd have it by now if you were careless enough to carry it plainly. But there is a shape in you, Inquisitor Vale, a hole with weight, the way a locked drawer makes a cabinet heavier than its wood. I will find the drawer."

"Everyone has drawers," Maren said. "That's what makes minds worth the trouble."

Voss studied her for a moment with those pale, patient eyes. "We resume tomorrow," he said. "Rest well. Eat what they bring you. A starved mind lies badly even to itself."

* * *

They resumed the next day. And the next. And the day after that.

It settled into a rhythm that Maren came to think of as a kind of terrible music: the climb up the spiral stair each morning, the arching room, the ring of dull brass, the questions laid down like notes on a staff. Voss was methodical. He circled. He returned to the same memories from different angles, the way a jeweler turns a stone in the light looking for the flaw. He asked about Thornhollow. He asked about the abbey catacombs. He asked, twice, about a bone-warden, and each time she described it differently in some small detail — the number of its ribs, the color of the light in its skull — and each time he noted the discrepancy without comment, and she understood he was testing whether her memory was intact or whether she was constructing, improvising, feeding him a story.

So she gave him a story. A consistent one. She built it the way Sable had once taught her charmwrights built charms: with a true core and careful walls. Everything true that could safely be true went in — the roads they'd taken, the villages, the blizzard on the pass. Around it, walls of plausible misdirection: Sable had gone north toward the coast, she said, hunting a relic in a sea-cave; the third key was a physical object, a ring of dragon-bone, hidden somewhere in the Saltmarshes. None of it verifiable quickly. All of it expensive to disprove.

Voss listened. Voss probed. Voss occasionally put his hand flat on the table, six inches from hers, and she learned to recognize that gesture — it preceded the hardest questions, the ones where the truth-stone's pull ran deepest.

On the sixth day, he changed tactics.

"We are going to try something new," he said, and there was a new note in his voice, something almost gentle, which frightened her more than any threat had. "I am going to show you things. Images, placed directly into your mind — a technique we refined on the mad and the mute. You will see them whether you wish to or not. Your task is simply to watch, and to tell me what you feel."

What he showed her was fire.

A mountain. Snow. A cavern lit from below by a glow like a banked sun. And chained — she saw them clearly, great links of black iron each thick as her waist — a shape that could only be a dragon, vast and dim, its scales the color of embers going out.

Maren's heart slammed against her ribs. She kept her face still. She had gotten good at that, these six days; her face was a mask she wore so long she sometimes forgot it was a mask.

"What do you feel?" Voss asked.

"Fear," she said. True. Easy.

"What else?"

"Pity." Also true. God help her, it was true — the sight of the chained dragon had struck something in her that no doctrine had ever reached, some old animal grief for anything bound and burning alone in the dark.

Voss nodded slowly, and wrote nothing, and she realized the clerk was absent today — that this session was off the record, which meant it mattered most. "Do you know what you are looking at?"

"Mount Kaelen," she said. "Emberveil. The last dragon." Both facts were in the restricted archives; she had clearance; admitting knowledge of them cost her nothing and bought her honesty-credit she hoarded like grain against winter.

"Do you know what sustains the chains?"

"The covenant," she said carefully. "The old accord between the realms. Its fire feeds the seal that holds the fae behind the veil."

"Yes." Voss removed the truth-ring and set it on the table, and for a moment the warmth behind her sternum faded, and she felt the cold rush back into her bones like water into a footprint. "Now we come to it. The prince intends to end the draining debate permanently. Emberveil will be drawn down to the last ember, and what remains of the covenant will gutter out with her, and the fae realm will be sealed forever by her death rather than her life — a dead lock, if you will. He believes this will save his kingdom. Perhaps he is right. That is not your concern. Your concern—" and here he leaned forward, and his pale eyes caught the lamplight and held it, "—is the date. The ritual requires a conjunction. An alignment. The engines must be positioned before the thaw closes the passes. Do you know when?"

"I don't," Maren said, and it was true, and the stone let it pass cleanly, and she watched frustration move behind Voss's face like a fish under ice.

"But you believe Montrose knows."

"I believe he's trying to find out the same thing you are."

Voss sat back. He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, softly, almost to himself: "The solstice fires. Midwinter. The old rite wants the longest night — the covenant was forged on one, you know, though the archives disagree on which. The prince's astrologers favor the coming solstice. Nineteen days from now." He picked up the ring and turned it over in his fingers, absently, the way a man turns a coin. "Nineteen days to move an army through a blizzard and pierce a mountain. It will be done, of course. It will be done because it must be. But I confess to you, Inquisitor — man to woman, keeper to keeper — I do not enjoy working against a clock I did not set."

And Maren — careful, controlled, eleven-years-an-inquisitor Maren — felt the whole architecture of her composure tremble, because he had just handed her the date. Not under the stone. Not under compulsion. Freely, carelessly, the way powerful men hand things to people they have decided are furniture.

*Nineteen days.* Midwinter solstice. She repeated it behind her teeth, carved it into the floor of her mind, buried it in the same vault where she kept her mother's face and the smell of the river district after rain — the things no spell could reach because she would die before surrendering them.

She said nothing. She thanked him, in her fashion, by answering his next three questions promptly and truthfully, rewarding the disclosure, teaching him that openness earned cooperation. It was the oldest trick in her side of the trade, and she used it now with a steadiness that surprised even her.

When the session ended and the guards came to walk her down, Voss paused at the door.

"You are the best liar I have met in twenty years of this work," he said. "I want you to know that I know. It changes nothing. But I want you to know."

"Thank you," Maren said.

He almost smiled. Then he was gone, and the stair swallowed her, and the quiet bells hung silent overhead all the way down.

* * *

That night, in her whitewashed cell, Maren took stock the way she had once taken stock before raids: coldly, completely, item by item.

Her body: intact, fed, rested. They wanted her functional. Good.

Her secret: intact. Nine years of practice had held against a truth-stone and a mind-reader both. The gift slept in her like a coal under ash, and she had not dared wake it — waking it might leave traces Voss could read. But it was there. On the worst nights she could feel it, faint as a pulse in the wrist.

Her intelligence: one date. Nineteen days. Solstice. Kaelen. Engines. An army on the march already, perhaps — which meant Sable's ragged company was racing something that had a head start, and every hour she sat in this cell was an hour stolen from them.

Her options: escape, or wait.

Waiting was what the clever prisoner did; waiting was what the dead prisoner had also always done, right up until the day the questions stopped being worth asking. She had seen the ledgers. She knew what happened to inquisitors who fell from grace — not execution, usually; execution was wasteful. *Reassignment.* The lower cells. The ones with drains in the floors. Sooner or later, when Voss concluded the drawer he sensed in her held nothing he needed, she would become material instead of witness. Nineteen days was longer than her shelf life.

So: escape. Soon. Before the solstice, before the army reached the mountain, before Sable — alone, carrying a burden she was beginning to understand was heavier than any relic — arrived at Kaelen to find the prince's engines already biting into the rock.

She began, that night, to study her captivity the way she had once studied suspects. The attendant who brought her candle each dawn never spoke but always glanced, briefly, at the basin — checking it, Maren realized on the fourth morning, for signs she was hoarding water. Water meant damp. Damp meant mortar softened. Someone in this tower had escaped before, or tried to. The knowledge was in the routine like a fossil in limestone.

The guards rotated on a six-day cycle. Two of them were young. One of the young ones — Warden Tetch, second circle, a broad-faced boy from the mill towns — had a sister who had been taken in the first sweep of the Cleansing, three winters ago, and Maren had seen the file; she remembered, because remembering was the job, and because some part of her had flinched even then. He did not know she knew. He stood his watches outside her door with his jaw set hard and his eyes fixed on nothing, and twice he had looked at her through the grate with an expression that was not hatred.

Hate she could work with too. But that expression — that was a door.

On the twelfth night, the temperature dropped, and the pipes in the old tower groaned, and the guard change came late and short-handed because half the wardens were down with the flux sweeping the barracks. Maren lay on her cot in the dark and listened to the tower breathe, and counted, and planned, and let the sleeping gift in her blood warm by a single degree — just enough to feel, not enough to leave a ripple any watcher could catch.

Nineteen days had become seven.

Somewhere north, she thought, Sable Montrose was walking toward a mountain with a hole in his memory where his life used to be, carrying keys she had helped him win and secrets she had bled to keep. He would reach Kaelen believing he had time. He did not have time. He had her — and she was in a tower full of quiet bells, seven days from the solstice, with a warden's grief for a key of her own.

She closed her eyes and began, very quietly, to remember the route the candle-attendant took each dawn: twelve steps, left turn, the loose hinge on the third door, the stair.

Seven days. She would need six.
