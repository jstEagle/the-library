## 16. Breaking Maren Out

The Inquisition tower of Corvane had been built to be forgotten by the people it swallowed. It rose from the river's edge like a black tooth, its walls unglazed and unadorned, its windows too narrow for anything but a sliver of sky. Sable stood in the shadow of the fishmongers' arcade across the water and studied it the way he had once studied difficult customers — not for what it showed, but for what it was trying to hide.

Three gates. He counted them twice. The river gate, barred against smugglers, where the tide lapped at a rusted grate. The processional gate on the landward side, wide enough for wagons of prisoners, flanked by wardens in slate-grey who checked papers with the bored efficiency of men who had stopped believing in danger years ago. And above both, a third entrance that was not marked as an entrance at all: a chimney-flue for the bell-tower's signal fire, wide as a well, climbing the tower's spine like a throat.

He could not go through any of them as himself.

That was the lesson of the third key. He had carried it out of his guildmaster's grave still warm, still humming, a sliver of grey stone no larger than a thumbnail that fit into the hollow of a ring — *his* ring now, the guildmaster's ring, which had fit his finger all along though he'd never noticed until the unbinding. Memory, the grave had told him. The final key is memory itself. And memory, it turned out, worked both ways. What had been taken from him could be taken from others. Not stolen — he had tried that first, in a tavern outside Thornhollow, with a drunk warden and a shaking hand, and the guilt of it had kept him awake for two nights after he'd returned the man's evening intact. But *edited*. A door closed here. A face blurred there. An hour made soft and formless, like a dream that would not survive the telling.

It cost him. Every working took something — not memories, not yet, but heat from behind his eyes, a weariness that settled into his bones like wet snow. He was spending himself in small coins, and he did not know how large a purse he had. But Maren had six days, and he had arrived on the fifth night of her seven, and so the arithmetic left him only one direction to spend.

*She would be furious,* he thought, watching a lantern move along the tower's upper gallery. *Furious that I came. Furious that I didn't trust her plan.* Then, because he knew her better than she perhaps wanted him to: *And she would have come for me anyway.*

He crossed the river in the hull-shadow of a grain barge, cold water to his chest, and came up beneath the grate of the river gate with his teeth chattering and the third key already warm against his palm.

* * *

The warden on duty inside the gatehouse was a broad, red-nosed man named Osk, and Osk had been having a very good evening until the moment Sable walked through the wall of his attention.

Not literally through — the gift did not do that, however much Sable wished it did. But the third key let him walk *through the shape of being noticed*, and so he crossed the gatehouse floor while Osk looked directly at him and saw instead a delivery of lamp oil that had been expected since noon and was, frankly, overdue. Sable even heard the man mutter about it, aggrieved, as he passed.

The tower swallowed sound. That was its design, he realized as he climbed the service stair — the quiet bells of Maren's prison letters, the felt-lined corridors, the doors that closed without clicking. Everything in this place conspired to make the people inside feel as though they were shouting into wool. He wondered how many gifted souls had gone mad in these halls simply from the silence, their magic pressing against their skin with nowhere to echo.

He found the first prisoner by accident.

He had meant to find the records room — Maren's last letter, slipped to him by a bribed laundress before her capture, had placed her cell somewhere in the eastern range — but a draft pulled him down a side corridor instead, toward a door that breathed cold air through its seams. Inside, a girl of perhaps sixteen sat cross-legged on the floor of a bare cell, and around her, in patterns that hurt Sable's eyes, frost bloomed and melted and bloomed again. She was not doing it deliberately. Her whole body shivered with the effort of *not* doing it, and failing, and each failure left a lace of ice across the stones that vanished before it could be punished.

She saw him and went rigid. "You're not real," she whispered. "They send dreams. Hale sends dreams."

"I'm real." He crouched, slow, keeping his hands visible, an old merchant's habit of putting customers at ease. "What's your name?"

"Petra." Her chin came up, defiant, though her eyes were wet. "I'm not confessing anything. I don't care what they do."

"I'm not here to take confessions. I'm here to take you out."

Something moved across her face — hope trying to get past fear at a narrow door. "That's a lie. Nobody leaves."

"Somebody's about to." He held out his hand, and when she hesitated, he turned his palm up so she could see the faint grey shimmer clinging to his fingers like woodsmoke. "I'm one of you, Petra. Charmwright. Last of the guild, unless I'm not anymore. What can you do besides freeze things?"

She blinked. "I can... hear ice. When water wants to become it. I know which way it's going to break." Her voice dropped. "Why do you want to know?"

Sable looked back down the corridor, listening. Somewhere below, boots. Somewhere above, the felt-silence pressing down.

"Because," he said slowly, an idea assembling itself in him the way his old charms used to assemble, piece by patient piece, "in about ten minutes, this whole tower is going to want to know exactly where every pipe and drain and water-barrel is going to crack. And you're going to tell me."

* * *

The plan grew the way his best work had always grown — not from grand design but from what the materials offered.

There were thirty-one prisoners in the eastern range, Petra told him, counting on frozen breath. Most were minor cases: hedge-healers, a weather-singer, two boys who could light candles with a look. Six were held deeper, in the sealed cells behind the chapel, and those six never spoke. Petra had heard the wardens say they were *reserved* — which, in Aldric's tower, meant saved for something worse than curing.

"The spymaster comes for them," Petra said. "Vessic. He wears grey gloves. People who talk to Vessic stop talking altogether."

Sable filed that away with the cold weight it deserved. *Maren.* He had not let himself think the name too often on the road north; thinking it made his chest do things that interfered with planning. But now he let it surface fully, and with it the memory of her face in the drowned chapel, salt-wet and fierce, saying *we do this together or not at all* — and then, later, the other memory, the one Hale had given him like a knife between visits: her surrender at the ferry crossing, hands raised, buying him hours with herself.

Hours. She had bought him weeks, and spent them alone in a tower of quiet bells while a man in grey gloves came asking questions with truth-spells.

"Which cell is hers?" he asked.

Petra frowned. "The one they call the Bell Cage? Top of the east stair. They put the strong ones up high." She said it with a strange pride, as if strength were contagious.

Of course they had. Of course Maren Vale, who had hidden her gift through years of hunting her own kind, would be classed among the strong ones — and isolated for it, because the Inquisition feared nothing so much as its own instruments learning what they were.

Sable stood, and the frost-girl stood with him, and for the first time since his guildmaster's grave he felt something that was not dread.

"Right," he said. "Here's what we're going to do to their plumbing."

* * *

Corvane's tower drew its water from a cistern in the attic — gravity-fed, elegant, and, as Petra read it with her eyes half-closed and her fingers trailing the pipes, catastrophically over-stressed. Forty winters of freeze and thaw. Hairline fractures running down the main riser like spider silk. A joint in the third-floor washroom that wanted, *very badly*, to fail.

"You want me to make it fail," she said.

"On my word. Not before." Sable had spent three hours mapping the tower by candlelight stolen from a supply closet, and the map in his head now had the satisfying density of a completed ledger. Water was the tower's blood. Cut it, and the wardens would have to leave their posts to fetch buckets; flood the archive level, and every clerk in the building would descend on the paper like ants on spilled honey; and in the confusion, in the noise that this tower was built to smother — noise would finally be on his side.

But the true craft of it, the part that made his fingertips tingle with an almost-forgotten joy, was the memory-work. He moved through the lower corridors ahead of the chaos, and where he passed, he left small gifts: a sentry who would swear he'd checked the river gate at midnight; a turnkey whose recollection of the evening's roster quietly rearranged itself to show two men on the east stair instead of four; a sergeant whose certainty that everything was normal deepened with every report he failed to receive. Not erasure. *Editing.* He was writing the tower's night the way he'd once written charm-brochures — persuasively, economically, with an eye for what the reader already wanted to believe.

It should have felt monstrous. It felt, instead, like coming home to a trade he hadn't known he remembered.

At the third-floor landing, a young warden stepped out of a doorway directly into his path, lantern raised — and Sable felt the third key flare hot against his ring, faster than thought, and the warden's gaze slid off him like rain off wax. The man frowned faintly at the empty corridor, decided he'd heard a rat, and went back to his cards.

Sable stood in the dark a long moment afterward, hand pressed flat against the wall, breathing.

*Careful,* he told himself. *You're spending coins you haven't counted.*

Then the washroom joint gave way with a crack that traveled the whole tower like a snapped bone, and there was no more time for careful.

* * *

Water found every weakness the way truth finds a liar. It came down through ceilings in grey ropes, sheeted across the archive floor where clerks shrieked over ledgers three hundred years old, gushed from the cistern-room in a flood that Petra, laughing wildly now, guided along channels of her own choosing — past her enemies' barracks, into their stores, under their doors. The tower that had been built for silence filled, for the first time in its existence, with glorious, undisciplined noise.

Sable went up the east stair through the middle of it.

Wardens shoved past him carrying buckets; he was, in their memories, the plumber's assistant from the river gate, expected since noon, *finally*. Twice he paused to lay a hand on a locked cell door and whisper the third key through the iron, leaving the lock's keeper with a gentle conviction that the door had been seen to. Behind him, softly, cells began to open.

By the time he reached the top of the east stair, he had collected a strange procession in his wake: the two candle-boys walking hand in hand, the weather-singer humming a rising wind, a hedge-healer with a midwife's calm and a kitchen knife, and old Tomas the rat-catcher, who could call vermin and who had spent forty minutes cheerfully directing a stream of extremely confused mice into the wardens' boot storage.

They reached the Bell Cage door together, and Sable put his palm against it, and hesitated — not from the lock, which was nothing, but from the sudden animal fear of what he might find on the other side. Seven days, Vessic had had five of them.

He opened it.

Maren sat on the edge of a cot in a room stripped to whitewash and iron, and she looked up, and Sable's heart performed a maneuver that no amount of memory-loss could have explained away. She was thinner. There was a bruise fading along her jaw like a storm front. Her wrists bore the grey ghost-rings of manacles worn long. And her eyes — her eyes went from his face to the crowd of ragged strangers filling his wake, and widened, and then did something he had never seen them do.

They filled with tears, and she laughed.

"You absolute idiot," she said, standing, swaying slightly, gripping the cot-frame. "You were supposed to be halfway to Kaelen. You had a *schedule*."

"The schedule had a hole in it shaped like you," Sable said. "I'm told I'm bad at leaving holes."

Behind him, someone snorted — Tomas, delighted. Maren crossed the cell in three steps and gripped his arms hard enough to bruise, checking him the way she checked everything, inventory-first, sentiment-second, and whatever she found in his face made her grip tighten rather than release.

"They broke my partner out of me," she said quietly. "Dessa. They took what she knew and put it in Vessic's reports. That's how they knew your route. That's how they'll know you're here soon, if they don't already—" She stopped. Recalibrated. He could nearly see the inquisitor's discipline reasserting itself over the woman. "How many days?"

"One. Solstice is tomorrow night."

Her jaw set. "Then we ride tonight, and we fight tired, and we win anyway. Who are all these people?"

Sable turned. In the corridor, lit by the emergency-lanterns now swinging through the flooded halls, stood the strangest company ever assembled in the Inquisition's own house: a frost-girl, two candle-boys, a weather-singer, a healer, a rat-catcher, and a dozen more drawn up the stair behind them by rumor and opened doors — hedge-witches and hedge-wizards, a boy with moss growing between his fingers, an old woman who claimed, implausibly, to be able to sour milk at a distance and who had been imprisoned for eleven months on that charge alone.

They were looking at him. All of them. Waiting.

Sable Montrose, who had survived twelve years by being small and forgettable, by being nobody, understood in that moment that the trick was spent. Whatever happened at Mount Kaelen tomorrow night, he would never again be a man a crowd could look through. The third key hummed against his ring like a struck bell, and the faces in the corridor reflected its light, and every one of them was waiting for a word he had no right to give and no choice but to give.

"Maren," he said. "You've served under commanders before. Bad ones. Tell me if I'm about to be one."

She considered the ragged line of them, and then him, and something in her expression settled — the same look she'd worn at the drowned chapel, when mercy had become strategy and strategy had become something warmer that neither of them had named.

"You'll do," she said. "First order: nobody dies getting down the stairs."

"Second order," Sable added, raising his voice so it carried down the flooded corridor, past the opening doors and the freed faces and the smell of river-water and ozone, "is that we leave them something to remember us by."

Petra grinned, and the temperature of the hallway dropped five degrees, and every guttering lantern along the wall burned suddenly, beautifully blue.

They went down together, thirty-odd strong, through a tower drowning in its own secrets — and behind them, in the chapel of the sealed wing, six reserved cells stood empty except for scratches on the walls and, on one door, a message in dried blood that none of them had stopped to read:

*SOLSTICE IS NOT THE RITUAL. SOLSTICE IS THE BAIT.*
