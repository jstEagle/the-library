## 17. March to Kaelen

The blizzard found them on the second day out of Thornhollow, and it did not let them go.

Sable had imagined mountains before — the way every lowlander does, as shapes on a map, gray triangles with tidy names. Mount Kaelen had been one of those shapes his whole life, a smudge of ink in the kingdom's northern collar. Now it was a wall of white that swallowed the road, the sky, and finally the distinction between the two, so that the company moved through a featureless howl with only Maren's compass-work and the old guild maps to tell them they were still heading north at all.

There were nineteen of them when the storm began. Sable counted them each morning and each evening, a habit he'd picked up without deciding to, the way he'd once counted coins in a till. Nineteen souls pulled from the Inquisition tower's cells: two hedge-witches, a former guild journeyman named Osk who wept openly whenever the wind screamed, a one-eyed fletcher, three brothers from a smuggler clan who finished each other's sentences, and Tam.

Tam was sixteen. He'd been in the tower for eleven months for lighting candles with a thought when he was frightened of the dark, which, Sable learned over shared hardtack, was most nights. He walked beside Sable now because Sable had been the one to break his cell open, and gratitude in a boy that age is a fierce and simple thing. He kept asking questions. What was the covenant? What did the dragon look like? Would there be a guild again after?

"Ask me when we're alive," Sable said, not unkindly, and Tam grinned through chapped lips and asked anyway.

The strangeness began on the third day, when the blizzard briefly relented and they saw what the thinning covenant had done to the wilds.

Maren saw it first. She had been walking point, hood back despite the cold, her faint gift prickling along her skin like static before lightning. "Stop," she said, and raised a fist, and the column halted in the trampled snow.

Ahead, the pines stood in perfect concentric rings, each ring older than the last, the innermost trees silver-barked and hung with fruit that glowed a soft, impossible green — summer light in the dead of winter. The snow within the ring lay untouched, and warm air breathed out of it, smelling of cut grass and honey.

"Fae-leak," Osk whispered. "My grandmother told me about these. Before the Edict, before even the guild fell. Places where the seal wears thin and the other side bleeds through."

"We go around," Sable said.

They went around. It cost them half a day, and behind them, as the light failed, Sable looked back once and saw figures moving among the glowing trees — tall, slender, unhurried, watching. They did not follow. That was somehow worse.

* * *

That night, camped in the lee of a granite shelf, Maren sat apart from the fire and tried to make her hands stop shaking.

It wasn't the cold. She'd grown up in the northern garrisons; she knew cold the way she knew her own name. It was the leak. When she'd neared that ring of impossible trees, her gift hadn't just prickled — it had *sung*, a high sweet note like a struck glass, and for one heartbeat she had understood things she could not hold onto: that the green light was a language, that the snow was listening, that somewhere far beneath a mountain an ancient heart was beating slower and slower and everything thinning with it.

She was still holding her hands when Sable settled beside her, close enough for warmth, far enough for dignity. That was his way. He'd been a shopkeeper too long to crowd anyone.

"You felt it," he said. Not a question.

"I felt something I don't have words for." She flexed her fingers. "In the tower, when you came through the memory-fog with that third key burning in your hand — I felt it then too. Like the world had a seam and you were standing on it." She turned to look at him. Firelight caught the new lines around his eyes, the ones twelve missing years had carved without his knowledge. "Does it frighten you?"

"Everything frightens me," Sable said. "I've built a whole life on being frightened quietly. But this—" He shook his head. "This is different. This is like being frightened by my own reflection."

Below them, the sentries changed watch. The smuggler brothers argued softly about whose turn it was. Tam, on second watch though he'd begged for first, fed sticks to the fire with the careful devotion of someone who remembered what darkness used to mean.

"They trust you," Maren said.

"They shouldn't. Half of what I know about where we're going comes from visions a dragon showed me, and the other half from a man who betrayed everything he loved and then hid the evidence inside my skull."

"And the other half?"

Sable huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "You've been doing the arithmetic wrong, inquisitor."

"So have you," Maren said. "You keep calling yourself what Aldric's people called you — a criminal, a stray, a thing to be cured. I spent six years in that institution, Sable. I know its vocabulary better than my own prayers. And I'm telling you: those nineteen people down there don't trust a mission. They trust the man who opened the doors." She stood, brushing snow from her cloak. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we cross the Whitethroat, and I want everyone alive on the far side."

He watched her go toward the sentry line and thought, not for the first time, that if the covenant demanded his memories, it would be taking the best ones he had.

* * *

The Whitethroat crossing nearly killed them all.

The river ran through a gorge that the maps marked with a single ominous word — *fordable* — and the maps, Sable decided somewhere in the middle of it, were liars. The ice was thick at the banks and treacherous in the middle, where the current breathed beneath it, and the wind came down the gorge like something hunting.

They crossed in pairs, roped together, Osk muttering a stabilizing charm under his breath with each step — small magic, candle-magic, but it held the ice true beneath their boots, and no one mocked him for it. Halfway across, the ice groaned like a living thing, and a crack raced ahead of them, white and branching as frost on glass.

"Hold!" Maren shouted. "Everyone hold — weight wide —"

Tam was in the second pair. Sable heard rather than saw what happened: a crack like a musket shot, a scream, the rope snapping taut. He turned in time to see the boy down on his chest, arms spread across the splitting surface, one leg plunged through into the black water, the fletcher hauling the rope hand over hand while the ice spiderwebbed outward from the hole.

Sable did not think. Later, he would remember it as the first time in his life he had acted without calculating the odds of survival, which was perhaps what twelve years of erased courage had been waiting to do.

He went out onto the bad ice flat on his belly, distributing his weight, the gift rising in him hot and unfamiliar — not a charm, not a trick, but the raw covenant-adjacent force that lived in Charmwright blood, the thing his guildmaster had sealed and trained and died protecting. He pressed his palm flat against the ice and *asked* it, the way the old guild taught — asked the water underneath to be patient, asked the cold to knit — and the cracking slowed, stuttered, stopped.

"Leg up," he gasped. "Now, Tam, pull—"

The fletcher hauled. Osk threw his charm like a rope of light. Tam came out of the hole streaming, blue-lipped, sobbing, and they dragged him the last forty yards to the south bank while the ice behind them gave way in great groaning sheets and slid away downstream.

On the bank, wrapped in every dry blanket the company owned, Tam looked up at Sable with wet lashes frozen stiff and said, "You saved me."

"I made the ice wait," Sable said. "You got yourself out. Remember that part. It matters more."

But that night Tam slept pressed close to the fire, and Sable sat awake beside him, and Maren, walking the perimeter, saw them there — the outlawed charm-seller and the boy who lit candles against the dark — and had to turn away and blink hard at the snow.

* * *

The fae-touched country deepened as they climbed.

By the sixth day they had left the true road entirely, following the guild map's dotted line through high moorland where the covenant's thinning leaked like light through a torn curtain. They passed a stream that flowed uphill, patiently, as if it had never been told otherwise. They passed a stone circle where snow refused to fall, hanging instead in midair a foot above the ground, glittering, waiting. Twice they saw the tall watchers at a distance — once standing motionless on a ridge in a row, like courtiers awaiting an audience, and once, terribly, close: a single figure at the edge of the firelight, beautiful and sorrowful, who looked at Sable with an expression of such ancient recognition that he dropped his tin cup.

When he retrieved it, the figure was gone, and in the snow where it had stood lay a single flower, blooming, red as arterial blood.

"It knows you," Osk said quietly. He'd come up beside Sable, drawn by the commotion, and he was looking at the flower with the wariness of a man who had read the right forbidden books. "The other side. The fae. The covenant isn't just a wall, master Montrose — it's a promise, an exchange, old as both realms. And promises remember the people who made them." He hesitated. "Your guildmaster. Was he—"

"He was there," Sable said. "Twelve years ago. On the mountain. I know that much now." His hand closed around the guildmaster's ring he wore on its cord at his throat. "What I don't know yet is whether he broke the promise or tried to keep it. The memories say both."

Osk nodded slowly and said nothing more, but that evening Sable noticed him walking at the rear of the column, keeping watch on the ridgelines, and understood that the old journeyman had appointed himself warden against the other realm. Small magics. Loyal ones.

* * *

Maren's chapter of the march was written in blood and powder-smoke, though she would not learn until later how narrowly it had missed being written in hers.

It was the scouts who brought the warning, near dusk on the seventh day: riders behind them, maybe two hours back, flying no colors they could see but moving with the disciplined economy of crown troops. Aldric's vanguard, or Hale's wardens — by now the difference hardly mattered. Both wanted the mountain, and both wanted the ragged company scattered or dead.

"There's a pass," the eldest smuggler brother said, crouched over the map with Sable and Maren. "Garrow's Throat. Narrow. If we're through it before they reach the mouth, they can't flank us — they'd have to come single file, and we'd hold it all winter."

"And if we're not through?" Maren asked.

"Then we die in the open, tired," the brother said, with smuggler pragmatism, "which is the worst way."

They pushed through the night. No fires. Rationed words. Tam stumbled and was carried for an hour by the brothers, protesting weakly, then slept upright against Osk's shoulder while the old man murmured steadying charms to keep the boy's feet moving in his sleep. Somewhere past midnight the moon rose fat and strange through the thin places in the world, and for a quarter hour the whole company cast shadows in two directions, and no one spoke of it.

They reached Garrow's Throat an hour before dawn, breath steaming, legs shaking. The pass was exactly as promised: a slot of stone barely wide enough for three abreast, walled on either side by cliffs that leaned inward like conspirators. The company funneled through, and Maren stood at the mouth counting them into the dark — seventeen, eighteen, nineteen — and turned to bring up the rear herself.

Which was when the horns sounded behind them.

Not two hours back. Thirty minutes. The scouts had misjudged in the dark, or the riders had pushed harder than any sensible commander would push — and sensible, Maren was learning, was not a quality Aldric's officers possessed. Lantern-light bloomed in the valley below like a crop of poisonous flowers.

"They'll reach the mouth before the last of us clear the narrows," Sable said, appearing at her shoulder. His face was gray with exhaustion and set like the cliff above them. "Someone has to slow them at the chokepoint."

"Then someone will," Maren said, already turning.

"No." Sable caught her arm. "Not you. You're the only one who can navigate the high route beyond the pass — the map's yours, the reading's yours. If you fall, we all wander the moors until Aldric finds us. You know it's true."

She hated that it was true. She hated more the look on his face as he said it, because she recognized it: it was the expression of a man volunteering, and she had seen it before, in mirrors, in the tower, in every quiet person who had ever decided that their life was the cheapest coin available.

"Then take the brothers," she said. "Take Osk. Take anyone but—"

"Maren." He said her name the way you set down something heavy. "I've been forgettable my whole life. Small. Safe. This—" he gestured at the lanterns swarming up the valley, at the nineteen lives funneling into the dark behind him, "—this is the first thing I've ever been *for*. Let me have it."

And that was how Sable Montrose went to hold Garrow's Throat with the three smuggler brothers and a boy of sixteen who refused, absolutely refused, to be left behind.

* * *

Tam had never been in a real fight. He had lit candles with his mind, hidden in a cellar for a year, and spent eleven months in a cell learning which floorboards were warmest. He had followed Sable out of the tower because following Sable felt like the first true thing he'd ever done, and he had marched north because Sable was marching north, and when Sable turned at the mouth of the pass and said, "Tam, go with the others," Tam had shaken his head so hard his hood fell back.

"I can hold a line," Tam said. "I'm not — I'm not much, I know I'm not much, but I can stand somewhere and not move. Anybody can do that."

Sable looked at him for a long moment. Around them the brothers were wedging stones into the narrowest point of the throat, building a barricade waist-high, working fast and silent. Above, the stars were going out behind cloud. Below, the lanterns were closer.

"Anybody can do that," Sable agreed quietly. "Almost nobody does. Stand behind the wall. When they come — and they'll come fast, they always do — you give them your fear. All of it. Every candle you ever lit in the dark. Give it to the fire, and let it burn big."

Tam's gift woke in his chest like a swallowed star.

They came up the pass in the last dark before dawn, forty men at least, shields locked, boots striking stone in rhythm. The brothers' crossbows spoke from the barricade. Osk's charms — he had insisted on staying too, over every objection, saying only, "I am old, and my knees are done, and my magic is small, and all three are exactly suited to a chokepoint" — turned the stones slick and the footing treacherous. The first assault broke against the wall of rock and crossbow-fire and rolled back down the pass leaving men behind.

The second came with fire-arrows, and the third with a ram, and between them, in the grinding hours of false dawn, the line held.

It held because the smuggler brothers fought like one creature with six hands. It held because Osk's small magics never faltered, never grand, never wasted. It held because Sable stood in the center with his palm against the cold stone and poured everything the salt relic had taught him into the mountain itself, asking the pass to be patient, asking the rock to knit, and the mountain — old, half-asleep, threaded through with the dying covenant — answered him.

And it held because at the left end of the barricade, where the wall met the cliff and the footing was worst, a sixteen-year-old boy stood in the smoke with fire running down his arms like sleeves, and did not move.

Tam gave them his fear. All of it. Eleven months of cell-dark, a childhood of candles, the terror of the ice, the terror of the horns — he gave it to the flame and the flame answered, roaring off his shoulders in wings of gold, and the soldiers of Veyra, who had been told all their lives that the gifted were sick and pitiable, looked up the pass at a burning boy who would not step aside and felt something ancient move in their spines.

The fourth assault came at full light, and it was desperate, and it was led by an officer with a mace who had realized that dawn was bleeding away his advantage. He reached the barricade. He swung. Tam caught the blow on a shield of fire that burst like a struck sun —

— and the officer's second blow did not catch fire at all.

Sable saw it happen from thirty feet away and crossed the distance in a time he would never be able to account for afterward. He saw Tam stagger. He saw the boy look down at his own chest with an expression not of pain but of profound surprise, as if the world had broken a promise to him. He saw Tam lift his head, find Sable's eyes through the smoke, and say — clearly, almost conversationally, the way he'd asked his endless questions on the march:

"Will there be a guild again? After?"

And Sable, catching him as he fell, said, "Yes. Yes. I swear it. I'll build it myself."

Tam smiled the grin from the hardtack mornings, the fierce and simple one, and the fire went out of him gently, like a candle cupped by careful hands, and he was gone before his knees touched the stone.

* * *

They held the pass another hour. It didn't matter anymore, and they held it anyway, because Tam had held it and it seemed important not to let the holding be for nothing. Then the brothers rigged the barricade with Osk's last charms and a keg of smuggler's blackpowder, and they withdrew up the throat as the charges brought a curtain of rock down across the pass, sealing it, buying the company days that they had not earned and intended to spend anyway.

They buried Tam above the tree line, in a hollow the wind had carved, under a cairn they built from the mountain's own stone. There was nothing to say that Sable had not already said, so mostly no one said anything. Osk laid a charm-stone on the cairn — a small one, candle-class, the kind that keeps a flame steady in wind — and it burned there, tiny and stubborn, as they shouldered their packs and turned north.

Maren walked beside Sable for a long time before either of them spoke.

"The guild," she said finally. "You swore it. In front of witnesses."

"I know what I swore."

"Then hear me, Sable Montrose." Her voice was rough, and she let it be. "If the covenant takes your memories at that stone — every rebuilt year, every name, every one of us — then the oath passes to me. To all of us. We carry it forward. He doesn't get to be a footnote in a dead boy's story. None of us do."

Sable looked at her. Above them, for the first time in two days, the clouds tore open, and Mount Kaelen stood revealed against the morning — vast, black-shouldered, crowned in ice, close enough now to touch its shadow.

And from its heights, thin as a thread across twenty miles of frozen air, came a sound none of them had ever heard and all of them recognized: a long, shuddering, descending note, like a bell dying, like a heart slowing.

Emberveil's fire, guttering.

Somewhere on that mountain, Aldric's engines were already piercing the stone.

"Move," Sable said, and the company moved.
