## 19. The Draining Begins

The first engine pierced the mountain at dawn.

Sable felt it before he heard it — a wrongness in the stone beneath his boots, a shudder that ran up through the frozen switchbacks of the pilgrim road and into his teeth. It was not an earthquake's roll. It was finer than that, crueler: the sensation of a needle entering flesh, of something long and deliberate sliding into the body of the world.

He was halfway down the mountain with Maren when it happened, and they both stopped as one, breath steaming in the blue half-light.

"What was that?" Tam, one of the freed prisoners — a thin boy with a stonewright's gift and frostbitten ears — clutched at Sable's sleeve.

Maren had gone very still, her head tilted like a hound scenting smoke. "Engines," she said. "Aldric brought engines."

"I know what he brought," Sable said. "I felt them being built in the tower yards for three years and told myself they were siege-works." He had been wrong about so many things for so long; he was tired of adding to the list. "Draining-rigs. The spymaster's design — I saw the schematics when I erased my trail out of the Inquisition tower. Great brass throats, warded on every seam, made to drink fire and give nothing back."

Above them, where the peak of Kaelen wore its crown of cloud, the light changed.

It was subtle at first — the kind of change a shepherd notices and a courtier never would. The dawn glow on the snow went from gold to a thinner, more coppery shade, then to something almost gray, as if the sun itself were being filtered through dirty glass. And beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, came a rhythm that did not belong to any living heart: *chunk — hiss — chunk — hiss*, slow and patient, the sound of pumps working, of siphons opening their mouths.

"Emberveil," Sable whispered.

Maren looked at him. "Can you feel her?"

He could. He hadn't known he could until this moment, but now that the dragon's fire was being drawn away, its absence rang through him like a bell struck and stopped. For twelve years — no, longer, since before his memory began — Emberveil's fire had soaked into the mountain, into the covenant, into the deep aquifers of magic that ran under all Veyra. He had been swimming in it his whole life without knowing the water's name. Now he could feel the current slackening, feel the level dropping inch by inch, and the sensation was like watching a wound bleed.

"They'll have set the rigs on the north face," he said, forcing his voice flat and practical because panic was a luxury with a price none of them could pay. "Where the rock is thinnest over the old lava channels. Which means—" He turned, counting heads. Forty-one had marched out of Thornhollow. Thirty-three still breathed. Of those, perhaps a dozen could fight, and only four — Maren, himself, the stonewright boy Tam, and a gaunt woman named Odile who carried a hedge-witch's knack for unmaking wards — knew enough of magic to matter against trained mages.

"We can't stop the rigs," Maren said. She wasn't asking; she was making him say it.

"No. Even if we took the north face, Aldric has two hundred soldiers and Hale's wardens dug in behind earthworks. We'd die on the ice and the pumps would keep pumping." Sable pressed his palm flat against the mountain's flank, as if he could steady its pulse by touch. "But the rigs don't matter if the covenant-stone is sealed. That's what they're drilling toward — not the dragon herself, not yet. They need to breach the inner cavern where the stone sits in her fire, and the stone is the door. While it holds whole, everything they siphon is just — spillage. Waste."

"And you're going to reach the stone before they do." Maren said it quietly, the way she said the truest things.

"I'm going to try."

* * *

They made their plan in the mouth of a cave above the tree line, with the wind screaming past and the copper-gray light thickening overhead like a bruise.

"The tunnels," Tam said. He had been born in these mountains, or near enough, in a mining village the crown had emptied two winters ago, and he knew the old shafts the way other children knew nursery rhymes. "There's a warren under the north face — the Deepwork, my grandfather called it. Abandoned since before the Edict. If the Inquisition's surveyors missed anything on this mountain, they missed that."

"They won't have missed it," Maren said. "Hale is thorough. But thorough men guard what they expect to be attacked. If we come up inside their lines—"

"You don't come up inside their lines," Sable cut in. "That's the point. I go alone."

"Absolutely not—"

"Maren." He took her hands. Her fingers were freezing, and he held them anyway, and hated how little time there was to say anything properly. "Every person we bring is a person between me and the stone, and every person between me and the stone is a person Aldric's soldiers will kill. What we need isn't an escort. It's time. Time bought loudly, messily, somewhere far from the Deepwork — so loud that no one counts heads in the dark."

She understood him. He watched her understand him, and watched her hate it, and loved her for both.

"A diversion," she said at last. "A real one. Not a skirmish — a battle. Something that makes Hale commit his reserves and makes Aldric believe the threat is here, at the south face, at the pilgrim road. Something that keeps their eyes off the north while you crawl through a dead mine like a rat."

"Something like that."

"And when you reach the stone?" Odile asked, practical as a hammer. "What then?"

Sable looked at his hands. Somewhere in them, he supposed, was the shape of everything he was about to lose — Maren's crooked smile, the smell of the guildhall's cold hearth, the sound of his own name spoken by someone who meant it. He wasn't ready. He suspected no one was ever ready, and that readiness was just another word the living invented to comfort themselves.

"Then I pay," he said simply. "And the rest of you get to live in whatever world comes after."

* * *

MAREN

The south face of Mount Kaelen was a white stairway of switchbacks, and Maren meant to make it burn.

Not with flame — she had never been able to summon fire, only to feel it, the way she felt all magic, as a warmth behind her sternum that rose and fell with her courage. No; what she burned with was noise, and light, and the appearance of an army.

Odile's knack for unmaking proved stranger and more useful than anyone had guessed. Set loose on the snowfield below the first switchback, the gaunt woman pulled the *ward-signs* out of the air itself — the invisible threads of Aldric's perimeter alarms — and each thread she snapped went off like a struck match in the minds of every warden tuned to it. By the third hour of dawn, thirty false alarms had bloomed across the southern approaches, and Hale's wardens were chasing ghosts through the drifts.

Then Tam earned his place in a story Maren hoped someone, someday, would tell properly. The boy knelt in the snow with his bare palms against the mountain and *moved it* — not much, not dramatically, but enough. A shelf of ice calved from the cliffs above the pilgrim road and thundered down onto the army's forward camp, burying two supply sleds and convincing the soldiers on watch that the mountain itself had taken sides.

Which, Maren thought grimly, tightening her grip on her captured warden's blade, it had.

"Here they come," breathed Joss, the ex-soldier among them, peering down through the blowing snow. Below, torches were kindling along the army's picket lines — dozens of them, then hundreds, a slow orange constellation spreading across the white. Horns sounded. Not the hunting-horns of the wardens but the deep braided notes of Aldric's regulars, the signal for formed ranks.

"They think we're the vanguard of something bigger," Maren said. "Let them keep thinking it. Skirmish, fall back, skirmish again. Make them climb. Every hour they spend climbing is an hour Sable spends crawling."

She looked back once, up the slope, toward the black seam in the mountainside where the Deepwork's adit hid behind a curtain of wind-scoured ice. She could see nothing, of course. He was already gone — had been gone since before first light, slipping away with a kiss pressed to her forehead and a promise she had refused to let him finish speaking, because promises like that were debts the universe collected with interest.

*Come back with nothing,* she had told him instead. *Come back empty. Come back having forgotten my name and the color of my eyes and every single thing we ever said to each other — just come back.*

And he had laughed, that terrible light laugh of a man agreeing to die politely, and said, *You'll introduce yourself. You're good at interrogation. You'll get it all out of me eventually.*

Now she turned back to the coming battle and put him out of her mind the way a soldier puts a wound out of mind — not because it stopped hurting, but because the hurting had to wait its turn.

The first rank of Aldric's regulars reached the second switchback as the sun should have been clearing the eastern ridge. It didn't. The light stayed low and coppery and wrong, and Maren realized with a lurch that the draining-rigs were pulling harder now, drinking in earnest, and that the dimness was not weather. The whole sky was guttering like a lamp out of oil.

"Hold the line!" she shouted, though there was no line, only thirty scattered fighters crouched among the rocks. "Make them pay for every step!"

The arrows came first — a hissing flight out of the gray, and then the regulars themselves, climbing in good order despite the ice, shields locked, pikes bristling. Professional soldiers. Men who believed they served a prince saving the kingdom from sorcery, and who would kill her without hesitation and sleep well after.

*I was almost one of them,* she thought, and stepped out from cover, and let the warmth behind her sternum rise into her hands for the first time in front of enemies who could see it.

The shield-wall's front rank staggered as her gift caught their ward-charms and *rang* them — every protective sigil on their armor shrieking at once like disturbed glassware. Two men dropped their pikes to clutch their helmets. Joss's archers put shafts into the gap. The rank broke around its own confusion, and for one bright, impossible moment, the line of the crown's finest rolled backward down a switchback, pursued by nothing but a young woman standing alone in the snow with light in her fists.

Then the wardens arrived, and the real killing started.

SABLE

The Deepwork smelled of dead iron and older darkness.

He moved through the abandoned shafts by touch and by the small green glow of the guildmaster's ring, which had begun, in the last day, to shine on its own — as if the metal remembered what it was for and was eager, in its patient way, to come home. The tunnels sloped down and inward, following the mountain's buried veins, and everywhere around him Sable could feel the draining-rigs' work as a tremor in the rock, a sickness in the stone. Twice he passed junctions where the old timbering had cracked, and fine mineral dust sifted down through the dark like gray snow, falling and falling in the green light.

He tried not to think about what the dust was. The mountain was shedding itself. The mountain was dying slowly, from the inside, and everyone above was too busy fighting to notice.

At the third hour underground he found the first wardens.

They had not missed the Deepwork after all. Someone — Hale, or the spymaster, or some careful clerk whose name would never be recorded — had guessed at the old mine, and a squad of six had been posted at the junction where the Deepwork met the lava channel, exactly where Sable needed to pass. He crouched in the blackness forty yards off and watched their lantern-light bobbing, and did the arithmetic he always did, the small forgettable man's arithmetic: six wardens, one of him, no time.

But he was not entirely the small forgettable man anymore. He had the third key. He had memory.

He crept closer, close enough to hear them talking — bored talk, cold feet, complaints about rations — and he chose the youngest of them, a round-faced warden barely older than Tam, standing the worst post at the tunnel's mouth. Sable came up behind him soft as dust and laid two fingers against the back of the boy's neck, and *reached in*.

It was not like the rite he had worked on himself. That had been surgery with a broken bottle. This was gentler — a thief's touch, a pickpocket of hours. He took the last twenty minutes of the young warden's memory and left in their place a quiet, plausible nothing: a long dull wait, a draft, a lantern guttering. The boy swayed, blinked, and stared straight through the shadow where Sable stood, seeing nothing, remembering nothing, because there was nothing left in that stretch of his mind to see with.

One by one, moving down the line, Sable did it to all six. It cost him more than he expected — each theft left an ache behind his eyes, a bruise on the soul — but it cost them nothing permanent, and when it was done, six wardens stood their posts guarding a tunnel that no intruder had entered, according to memories that agreed perfectly and were all wrong.

He walked between them, and they nodded to him amiably, because in the shallow country of their recent recollection, a passing figure was simply weather.

Beyond their posts, the tunnel opened its throat, and Sable felt the heat.

It came up from below like a tide — dry, enormous, alive. The lava channel. The road to the cavern. And threaded through the heat, fainter now than it had been even yesterday, the pulse of Emberveil's fire, stuttering like a candle in a doorway.

*Chunk — hiss. Chunk — hiss.* The pumps above drank, and drank, and somewhere far below, the last dragon of Veyra grew colder.

Sable stood in the dark with the green ring glowing on his hand and let himself feel, for one full breath, the enormity of what was being lost — and then he ran, down into the heat, toward the stone, while behind and above him the battle on the south face rose to its roaring height and Maren held the crown's army with light and lies and thirty desperate lives.

* * *

MAREN

By midday she had stopped counting the dead.

There had been a moment — she would remember it later, in other years, in the rebuilt guildhall with its new-minted charter — when the line nearly broke. The wardens had found their rhythm, rotating fresh squads up the switchbacks, and Odile had spent herself snapping wards until the old woman collapsed, gray-lipped, behind a boulder, and Joss took a pike through the thigh holding the narrow step below the third turn, and Tam stood alone on the high ground moving stone with both hands and tears freezing on his face, holding a wall of rock between forty soldiers and the retreat path of everyone wounded.

Maren had climbed onto the broken wall itself, into the arrow-storm, because there was nowhere left to fall back to and nothing left to feint with, and she had done the one thing she had sworn she'd never do in front of the enemy.

She had shown them everything.

Light poured off her like water off a weir. Every charm the wardens carried curdled; every truth-spell aimed at her defenders slid sideways and struck its caster; and the soldiers of Veyra, raised on twelve years of edicts telling them that the gifted were plagues to be cured, saw a young woman stand unarmored in the killing ground and *shine*, and hesitated.

Hesitation was all she needed. Hesitation, and the horn.

It sounded from the north face — three deep notes, Aldric's own signal, ragged with urgency. On the slopes below, officers turned, shouting. Runners scrambled. And through the battle-noise Maren caught fragments carried on the wind: *"the rigs — the third rig's cracked — the siphon's failing — the prince wants every mage on the north face now—"*

Something had gone wrong with the draining. Or something had gone right. She would not know which for hours, and by then it would not matter, because either way the order rippled down the line like a lit fuse: withdraw, regroup, north face, *now*.

The pressure eased. The arrows stopped. And Maren Vale stood on a wall of Tam's making, in a sky gone the color of old pewter, and let herself slide down its inner side and sit in the snow and shake.

"Did we win?" Tam whispered, dropping down beside her, hollow-eyed.

"No," she said. "We bought an afternoon." She looked up at the mountain's smoking summit, where the copper light was failing by degrees, and thought of a man crawling alone through the dark toward the one thing in the world worth everything. "Pray he spends it well."

* * *

SABLE

The last gallery opened without warning, and there it was.

The covenant-stone stood in the center of the great cavern like a tooth of the world's first morning — taller than a man, veined with fire, humming a note so deep he felt it in the marrow of his legs. Around it, Emberveil's lake of light lapped and glimmered, though its shores had crept visibly inward since yesterday; whole stretches of cavern floor lay exposed now, dark and cooling, printed with the ghost-patterns of scales. Above, the dragon hung in her chains, dimmer, her breathing long and labored, each exhale a little less golden than the last.

And at the cavern's far side, where the rock had been breached by machinery beyond Sable's imagining, the first of the ritual engines thrust its brass throat into Emberveil's domain — and *drank*.

He could see the fire leaving. It moved through the engine's transparent veins like light through water, streaming up the drilled shaft toward the surface, toward the wagons and reservoirs and the waiting mages of Prince Aldric, and where it passed, the stone's hum faltered, and the lake withdrew another inch, and somewhere in the sealed fae realm beyond the veil, a thousand doors grew a thousand times harder to open — or easier. He couldn't know which. That was the horror of it. The covenant was balance, and balance dying was chaos for everyone, and the prince in his certainty was pouring both worlds out onto the snow.

Sable walked to the stone.

Up close, he could see the old damage — the crack that ran through it like a scar, twelve years old, the wound his guildmaster had made and had spent the rest of his life teaching a child, secretly, patiently, how to heal. The stone's surface was warm under his palms. It pulsed. And as he laid his hands against it, the hum changed, and he felt it *notice* him — felt the vast slow attention of the covenant turn upon the last Charmwright, weighing him, recognizing the ring, recognizing the debt.

Behind him, far above, boots rang on rock. Voices. The crash of the withdrawal being converted, with terrible efficiency, into a defense of the north face — Hale's wardens sealing the upper galleries, Aldric's mages hurrying to protect the engines, the whole apparatus of the crown turning at last to face the single small man standing at the heart of the mountain.

Maren was holding them as long as she could. It would not be long enough. He had perhaps minutes — enough time to begin, never enough to finish, unless he finished faster than any rite had ever been worked.

Sable Montrose closed his eyes. Behind them, in the warm dark, he arranged his life like a man laying a table: Maren's crooked smile. The guildhall's cold hearth. His name, spoken kindly.

Then he pressed his brow to the covenant-stone, and began to speak his memories aloud — and the first thing the mountain took was the sound of her voice saying his name, and he gave it willingly, weeping, without stopping.

Far above, the engines roared. And the stone, drinking deep of everything he was, began — at last — to glow.
