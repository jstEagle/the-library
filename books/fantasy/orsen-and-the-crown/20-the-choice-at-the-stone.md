## 20. The Choice at the Stone

The covenant-stone was smaller than Sable had imagined it would be.

Twelve years of half-remembered dread had built it into a monument in his mind — a black monolith, a throne of the old gods, something that would dwarf the dragon chained beneath it. Instead it stood barely taller than a man, a seam of white stone wedged into the living rock of the cavern floor, veined with hairline cracks of gold that pulsed like a heartbeat slowing. Emberveil's fire washed over it in long, failing waves, and where the fire touched the gold, the veins flared briefly bright and then dimmed again, dimmer each time.

Sable knelt beside it and pressed his palm flat against the surface. It was warm. Not dragon-warm — *alive*-warm, the warmth of a hand held too long, of a cheek pressed to a fevered brow.

*It's dying,* he thought, and the thought arrived with the strange calm of a man who has run out of terror and found stillness on the other side. *It's been dying for twelve years, and everything we've done has been a race against this exact moment.*

Behind him, far down the throat of the mountain, the battle still rang — the clang of steel on the ritual engines' iron housings, Maren's voice hoarse and commanding, the crackle of wardens' fire-lances answered by the wilder, stranger light of the freed prisoners. The mountain shuddered at long intervals as Aldric's drills bored deeper. Dust sifted from the cavern ceiling like grey snow.

Sable let his hand rest on the stone and reached, gently, with the gift.

The stone opened to him the way a door opens onto a garden long untended. He felt the covenant entire for one staggering heartbeat: two worlds laced together like fingers, the fae realm's wild dreaming flowing through the stone into the dragon, the dragon's fire flowing back, each sustaining the other, each *bound* to the other, and threading through it all — the guild. The Charmwrights. His guild, his people, the keepers of the hinge. He felt the shape of the rite that would reforge it, and he felt, with terrible precision, the shape of its price.

He was still reeling from it when the footsteps came.

He knew the cadence before he turned. He had heard it across a rooftop in Corvane, through the bars of a warden's cage, down the length of an abbey nave. Prince Aldric of Veyra climbed the last of the basalt steps into Emberveil's cavern alone — no guards, no Hale, no mages — wearing his ceremonial white scorched grey at the hem, a sword at his hip that he did not touch, and a look on his face that Sable recognized because he had seen it in his own mirror for twelve years.

The look of a man carrying something he could not put down.

"You're not what I expected," Aldric said. He stopped a dozen paces away, at the edge of the firelight. "The Inquisitor's files made you a monster. Charm-seller, poisoner of the faithful, archivist of forbidden craft. I imagined someone *larger*."

"I get that a lot," Sable said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "I'm very good at being small."

"So I've heard." The prince's gaze went past him, up, to the vast dim shape coiled in the cavern's depths — the slow rise and fall of scales like cooling embers, the great head resting on folded wings, one eye half-open and watching them both with the patience of a creature for whom a decade was a nap. "Emberveil."

"You know its name," Sable said.

"My mother taught it to me." Something moved behind the prince's eyes — a door opening on a lit room, then closing. "When I was six. She said the dragon's fire was the kingdom's oldest promise, and that promises were the only magic that mattered." His jaw tightened. "She said that before the fae got into her head and unmade her."

Sable rose slowly, keeping his hands visible, the way you'd move around a wounded animal. "Your Highness—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip. Then, quieter, with an effort Sable could almost see: "Don't plead the fae's case. Not to me. You don't know what they did. You've seen the thinning — the wonders leaking through. I've seen the other side of it. I've seen what a realm of dreaming things does when it gets a hook into a human mind." He stepped closer, and now Sable could see the exhaustion under the iron of him, the sleepless weeks of it. "She was the wisest person in this kingdom. She could out-argue the whole privy council before breakfast. And they *hollowed* her. Twenty years, Sable Montrose, of my mother sitting at a window talking to people who weren't there, laughing at jokes no one else could hear, and sometimes — sometimes — screaming at things only she could see, and my father burning every healer, every charmwright, every hedge-witch who promised a cure and failed, until there was no one left in Veyra who dared say the word *magic* in his hearing." He looked at the stone. "The covenant feeds them. The dragon-fire feeds *them*, and they give back madness. I am going to end that. Whatever it costs."

The mountain groaned. Somewhere below, an engine pierced something that should not be pierced, and Emberveil's fire guttered — Sable felt it like a fist closing around his own heart, the stone's pulse stuttering under his palm where it still rested.

"Your Highness," Sable said, "if you drain her, the seal doesn't weaken. It *fails*. Not in a generation — in a season. Everything behind it comes through. Not wonders. *Them*. The things your mother spoke to at the window — they're the small ones, the ones that slip through the cracks now. What's behind the seal is older, and it is not confused, and it is not sad. It is hungry, and it has been waiting twelve years for exactly the door you're building."

"I know what the archives say."

"The archives were written by people who never stood here."

"And you have?" Aldric's eyes came up, sharp. "Twelve years ago. The night it broke. You were there."

The words landed in Sable's chest like cold water. *He knows. He's known all along — the hunt was never for stray magicians. It was for the one who was there.*

"I was a child," Sable said.

"You were an apprentice of the Charmwrights' Guild, and the guild's masters were there, and the covenant broke that night, and every record of it burned or vanished, and every soul who might have testified is dead or scattered — except you." The prince's voice dropped, and for a moment it was not a prince speaking but a son, and the son was tired down to the marrow. "I don't want your death, Montrose. I want the truth. Tell me what happened that night, and if the guild broke it — if they broke it *knowingly* — then tell me why I should not let the whole rotten covenant die."

And because the stone was warm under his hand, and because the rite's price was already sitting in his chest like a stone of its own, Sable found he did not want to lie.

"The guild broke it," he said. "Knowingly. My guildmaster, Oren Vael, led it. The king — your father — had declared the guild illegal. He was burning charmwrights in the yard of his own palace. Oren went to the fae court and offered them a loosening of the seal in exchange for the guild's survival — a hidden charter, a shadow-patronage. The fae agreed. The covenant was cracked, not broken — a flaw, a hairline, meant to be a *valve*. But a crack in a dam doesn't care about intentions, Your Highness. It grew. It's been growing for twelve years, and it's the reason your mother's voices got louder, and it's the reason the wonders are leaking, and it is the single most shameful thing my guild ever did." He swallowed. "Oren spent the rest of his life trying to undo it. He hid the three keys. He erased my memory of that night — I was his apprentice; I'd have run my mouth, or been interrogated, or worse, *used* — and he left me a letter and a mission I didn't understand until three weeks ago. He was a betrayer and a penitent in the same body, and I loved him, and I am here to fix what he broke. That's the truth. All of it. Take my head if it helps."

The silence that followed was long. The firelight moved. Above them, Emberveil's half-open eye had not closed, and Sable had the distinct, humbling sense of being a footnote in a conversation between far older parties.

Aldric looked at the stone. And then — Sable saw it happen — the fae-touched air of the thinning covenant gathered between them, and the visions came.

They came for the prince first. Sable watched it take him: the air above the gold-veined stone shimmering like heat-haze, and in the shimmer, images — the fae realm's dreaming pressing close where the seal wore thin. Sable saw them too, because he was touching the stone: a court of impossible beauty under a sky like spilled ink, figures that were almost human and gloriously, terribly not, and among them, moving through a garden of silver grass—

A woman. Dark-haired, grey-eyed, with Aldric's exact jaw, walking and talking and *laughing*, whole and lucid and radiant, in a place where no human should be.

Aldric made a sound Sable would remember for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be. He took one step toward the vision, and another, his hand rising as if he could part the shimmer and step through. "Mother—"

"She's not there," Sable said quietly. "It's a dream. The realm's dreaming, leaking. It shows you what you—"

"*She's there.*" The prince's voice shook. In the vision, the woman turned, saw something — saw *him*, perhaps, or the shape of him — and her face broke into joy, and she raised her hand, and the vision folded away like a letter being closed. Aldric stood with his hand still raised, holding nothing.

And Sable understood, in that moment, the full cruelty of what the covenant's dying did. It didn't just leak wonders. It leaked *longings*. It showed every soul that came near the exact thing that would undo them.

The prince wavered. Sable watched it — watched the sword-hand drop, watched the certainty crack, watched twenty years of grief and filial love reach for that silver garden like a drowning man. And he thought, with a strange, aching pity: *he could be saved. Right now, this could end without another death.*

Then Aldric's hand closed into a fist, and the prince looked at the place where the vision had been, and Sable watched the door close behind his eyes and *bar* itself.

"No," Aldric said softly. "No. That's how it starts. That's how it started with her — a glimpse, a voice, a *gift*, and then twenty years of screaming. You think that vision is mercy? It's bait. Everything from that side is bait." He straightened, and the prince came back into his face like armor being buckled on, piece by piece, and Sable understood that he was looking at a man who had decided, long ago, that feeling anything about the fae was the first step to losing everything. "The draining continues. If your stone dies, it dies. I will build a new covenant — a *human* one, on human terms, with no dreams in it at all. And if the realms tear in the meantime, then at least they'll tear clean, with no hooks left in anyone's mind." He turned toward the stairs. "Kill him if he interferes. Gently, if you can manage it. He's only ever been someone's tool."

"Your Highness," Sable called after him, "the rite I'm about to perform — you should know what it costs. Because it's the same lesson your whole plan refuses to learn."

Aldric paused at the head of the stairs. He did not turn around.

"Power demands what we are," Sable said. "Not what we own. Not what we command. *What we are.* Your engines can't drain the dragon without killing the mountain, and my rite can't mend the covenant without spending the only currency it accepts. You're about to learn it the hard way. I'm about to learn it the harder one."

The prince stood a moment longer. Then he descended, and his footsteps faded into the ringing dark, and Sable was alone with a dying god-stone, a watching dragon, and the price.

* * *

He sat down in front of the stone, cross-legged, the way Oren had taught him to sit before any working. *Ground yourself in what's true,* the old man used to say, *because the working will take everything else.*

The rite was simple. That was the horror of it. All of this — the relics, the chase, the prison break, the march through the blizzard, the dead — and the rite itself was a child's rhyme. Speak to the stone. Tell it the truth. And then give it what it asked for, willingly, and hold nothing back.

He had felt the shape of the price when he first touched it, and he had spent the walk up the mountain not thinking about it, and now there was no more walking left to do.

The stone did not want his pain. It did not want his guilt, or his fear, or his twelve lost years — those had already been spent, spent by Oren, spent by the unbinding rite in the guildmaster's grave. The covenant was a bond of *love*. That was its whole architecture: two realms bound to each other not by chains but by affection, by the fae's delight in mortal dreams and the mortal gift for enchanting what they loved. A covenant of love could only be reforged with love.

It wanted his memories of everyone he had ever loved.

All of them.

He sat with that for a long time. The battle sounds below rose and fell. Dust fell. Emberveil breathed, slow as seasons.

He thought about Oren Vael — the smell of beeswax and hot copper in the guild workshop, the old man's laugh like a door creaking, his huge hands folding a child's fingers around the first charm Sable ever made. *You have the gift, boy, and the gift is a debt.* He thought about the guildhall in its living years, forty charmwrights and their apprentices, the winter feasts, the way the whole building had smelled of cinnamon and solder. He thought about Maren — and here his chest seized, actually seized, a physical pain — Maren's crooked smile, her terrible singing, the way she said his name like it was a joke only the two of them got, her hand finding his in the dark of the catacombs, her voice through the blizzard: *stay with me, Montrose, that's an order.* He thought about the villagers of Thornhollow, the children with their whispered charms, the widow who had fed them and asked nothing. He thought about the young prisoner who had died on the march holding the pass, whose name Sable had promised himself he would carry.

And he understood that the stone was not asking him to lose them. It was asking him to give them away. To pour every drop of his love for them into the covenant, so that the bond between realms would be rewoven out of the truest thread a mortal possessed.

And it would take the thread with it. He would wake with nothing but a stranger's face in the smoke and a warmth in his palm.

*I could stop,* he thought. *I could walk away. Let the seal fail. Let the realms tear. Let Aldric be right, or let him be wrong, and let the world sort itself out without me in it.*

But he thought of the woman in the silver garden — not as bait, but as a mother laughing somewhere, lucid, *happy*, in a dream her son would never be allowed to share. He thought of the fae realm not as a hunger but as a neighbor, locked out of its own house. He thought of Emberveil, the last dragon, who had kept the promise for a thousand years and was dying of it, and who deserved better than to end as fuel in a prince's engines.

And he thought: *this is what the gift is. Oren said it was a debt. This is the payment.*

Sable laughed — a small, cracked sound, absurd in the vast cavern — because he finally understood the whole of it. The guild's motto, carved over the ruined door in Corvane: *We sell small magics.* All his life he had made himself small to survive. Forgettable. A man who sold luck-charms and kept his head down. And the world had finally, at the end, asked him for the largest thing he had.

He pressed both hands to the stone.

"I'm Sable Montrose," he said, and his voice was steady, and he was grateful for that. "Last of the Charmwrights' Guild. Son of no one, apprentice of Oren Vael, friend of Maren Vale. And I come to pay the guild's debt."

The gold veins flared. The stone's pulse quickened under his hands — a heartbeat waking. And it *listened*. He felt it listening the way you feel someone take your hand.

He began with Oren, because it was right to begin there. He spoke the workshop into the stone — the beeswax, the copper, the creaking laugh, the huge hands, the letter that had smelled of the old man's pipe when Sable broke its seal three weeks and a lifetime ago. He spoke the guildhall entire, every feast and every face, and the stone drank it, and the gold veins spread, and somewhere in the mountain the firelight strengthened by one shade.

He spoke the widow of Thornhollow and her stew, the children's whispered charms, the young prisoner's name — *his name was Tam, his name was Tam, let it be carved somewhere* — and the stone drank, and the cracks began to close.

He spoke Maren last, because he was a coward, and because he was not, and because he wanted to save the best thread for the weaving. He spoke the rooftop and her inexplicable mercy. The drowned chapel and her gift flaring silver in the dark. The catacombs, her hand in his. The blizzard, her voice, her terrible singing, the way she looked at him across a campfire like he was a puzzle she intended, patiently, to solve. He spoke *stay with me, Montrose*, and he felt the stone gather it up, tender as a thief, and he felt the memory thinning as it left — not erased, not torn, but *given*, the way you give blood, the way you give bread from your own loaf — and he kept speaking, faster now, because there was so much, because there had been so little time and it had been so full—

The cavern shook. Below, the engines screamed; the mountain's bones groaned; and through the stone Sable felt Maren's battle like a drumbeat, felt her still fighting, still holding the line, and he poured that too into the stone — *she's holding the door right now, she's holding it for me, she's the bravest person in either realm and she doesn't know I'm—*

He was speaking her name when the last of it went.

* * *

The stone was cool now. Whole. The gold veins ran unbroken from edge to edge, and through them flowed a light that was not fire and not dream but both at once, and high above, Emberveil raised her head for the first time in twelve years, and the cavern filled with a sound like a thousand bells underwater, and the seal between the worlds — Sable could feel it even as he slid sideways, even as the floor came up gentle as a bed — the seal was *closing*, whole and bright and singing.

Somewhere very far away, a prince was on his knees. Somewhere closer, boots were running up basalt stairs, and a voice was shouting his name in a tone he did not recognize and would, someday, learn to love.

Sable Montrose, last of the Charmwrights' Guild, keeper of the covenant, lay down in the firelight with a dragon-scale charm warm in his palm and no idea whose voice was calling him, and did not hear the answer to the only question left:

*Who am I?*
