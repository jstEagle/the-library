## 22. A Stranger Named Maren

Sable woke to the smell of bread.

That was the first thing. Not fire, not salt, not the cold iron breath of the Inquisition tower or the sulfurous heat rolling off a dragon's dream — just bread, warm and plain, drifting up through a floorboard gap from somewhere below. It was such an ordinary smell that it took him several heartbeats to trust it. He had learned, in whatever life he'd lived before this one, that ordinary things were often disguises.

He lay still and let the morning come to him.

The room was small and clean, with whitewashed walls and a window that framed a slice of pale sky. A wooden chair held folded clothes that were not his — they were too big in the shoulders, too short at the cuff, someone else's kindness cut to no one's measure. On the bedside table sat a clay cup of water, half-drunk, and beside the cup lay a scale.

It was the size of his palm, deep crimson shading into gold at the edges, and when he picked it up it was warm. Not blood-warm. Not sun-warmed-stone warm. Warm the way a hand is warm when it has been holding yours for a long time and has only just let go.

He turned it over. There was no inscription, no maker's mark, nothing to tell him what it was or why it had been left for him. And yet his fingers closed around it with the ease of long habit, the way a hand knows a walking stick, a door latch, the curve of a lover's shoulder. His body remembered it even if he did not.

*You don't remember anything,* he told himself, and found that even that was not quite true. He remembered how to read. He remembered that kings could be cruel and that bread was good and that the color of the sky over Corvane in late winter was the gray of old pewter. What he did not remember was himself — no childhood, no name he trusted beyond the one stitched into the collar of the shirt he'd been found in, no face that rose behind his eyes when he tried to think *home.*

*Sable Montrose.* He said the name silently, testing it like ice on a pond. It held. Barely.

Below, someone was moving about — the scrape of a chair, the clink of crockery, a low voice humming something that snagged at him like a burr in wool. A tune he almost knew. He got up, dressed in the borrowed clothes, and stood a moment before the little mirror on the wall, studying the stranger there: dark-haired, hollow-eyed, a thin scar along the jaw he had no story for. The man in the glass looked like someone who had been poured out and only partly filled again.

The dragon-scale charm went into his pocket. He did not know why. He simply could not imagine leaving it behind.

* * *

The house below turned out to be a guildhouse — or what was becoming one. Sable paused on the last stair and looked out across a room that bore the marks of recent resurrection: scrubbed floors, new hinges bright against old smoke-darkened wood, shelves being restocked by two young women who argued cheerfully about the proper labeling of jars. Through a wide doorway he could see a workshop where a man with ink-stained fingers inscribed something delicate onto a strip of copper, his lips moving through the words of a licensing oath pinned above his bench — *for the common good, under covenant law, witnessed and bound.*

Covenant law. The words meant nothing to him and everything at once, the way a chord means something even to an ear that cannot name its notes.

And at the table by the window, with two cups of tea already poured, sat a woman with close-cropped brown hair and a sword-callused hand wrapped around a book she wasn't reading. She looked up as he came down, and her whole face changed — not with surprise but with relief, layered so many times over the weeks that it had worn smooth, like a stone in a river.

"You're up early," she said. "You're usually up early. I keep saying that like I know you." She gestured at the second cup. "Tea's getting cold."

Sable crossed the room slowly. Up close she had a faint scar at her temple and tired eyes that watched him the way people watch water they've nearly drowned in once — carefully, without pretending not to care.

"You're Maren," he said. "Maren Vale. You come every day."

"Most days. Some days you're asleep and I sit here anyway and drink both cups." She pushed the book aside. "How much do you remember from yesterday?"

"The healer came. She said my head is mending the way a broken bone mends — slowly, and it will ache, and I shouldn't rush it." He sat down across from her. "I remember the mountain. Not as pictures. More like... weather. Pressure before a storm. Something enormous happened there and I was at the center of it, and everyone keeps looking at me like I'm a hero, and I feel like a man reading a letter written to someone else."

"That's fair," Maren said quietly. "Because mostly you are."

She wrapped her hands around her own cup, and he saw that she was steadying herself — not for his sake, or not only for his sake, but because what she was about to do cost her something too.

"I promised I'd help you remember," she said. "So. Let me tell you a story. Interrupt whenever you want. Most of it sounds like nonsense, and some of it is."

"All right," Sable said. "Tell me about the stranger in your story. The one with my name."

A smile flickered across her face and died young. "He was a charm-seller in Corvane. The last of the Charmwrights' Guild, though he didn't advertise it — magic had been outlawed for years by then, and he survived by being small and forgettable. Luck-charms, headache-charms, a love-charm now and then that never worked because he had too much conscience to make them work properly. He was good at hiding. He'd been hiding since he was fourteen, though he didn't know why for a long time." She paused. "He doesn't know yet even now. But we're getting to that."

"And you knew him?"

"I arrested him." She said it plainly, without flinching, and Sable felt something twist — not pain exactly, more like the ghost of a pain whose cause had been amputated along with everything else. "Or I was supposed to. I was an inquisitor, back when the Edict of Cleansing made hunting people like him a career. I cornered him on a rooftop in Corvane and I let him go, because I'd realized the hunt wasn't really about magicians at all. It was about a dragon."

"A dragon," Sable repeated. The word landed somewhere deep and echoed strangely, and the scale in his pocket seemed to warm against his thigh.

"Emberveil. The last dragon in the world, chained beneath Mount Kaelen, burning slow and dying slow, and its fire was the only thing keeping the covenant intact — the old binding between our realm and the fae realm. Twelve years ago the covenant broke. Someone shattered it. And the pieces of it — three relic-keys — were scattered and hidden, and the crown wanted the dragon drained before anyone could put them together again." Her eyes came up to meet his. "Prince Aldric. He believed the fae realm had driven his mother mad, and he wanted the door between the worlds closed forever, even if closing it meant killing the last dragon and letting the magic gutters out of the world entirely."

"What happened to the keys?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment. "You found the first one in a drowned chapel. I helped — I revealed what I was, what I'd spent my whole life hiding, and after that we were partners, though neither of us would have used the word yet. You found the second in the catacombs beneath Thornhollow abbey, guarded by a construct made of bone that nearly killed us both. My magic saved us. Untrained, wild, terrified magic — but it saved us." She smiled briefly. "You called it 'a bucket brigade against a housefire.' You were very unimpressed."

"I have excellent taste in criticism," Sable said, and heard her laugh — a real one, startled out of her — and thought: *I want to be the kind of person who makes her do that often.* The thought arrived whole, without history, and he decided not to question where it came from.

"The third key refused you," Maren said, quieter now. "Because the third key wasn't a thing. It was memory. Your memory. Someone had sealed away twelve years of your life — everything before you turned fourteen — and the key wouldn't answer to a man with a hole where his childhood should have been." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "So you unbound it yourself. A forbidden rite, performed on your own mind, alone in your dead guildmaster's grave-chamber. And you learned the truth: that Orsen Vael — your master, the man who taught you charms and conscience — was the one who broke the covenant. To save the guild from the old king's purges, he traded the dragons' trust. And then he couldn't live with it, so he stole your memories and hid the path to repair inside your own head, waiting for you to grow up strong enough to walk it."

Sable sat very still. Grief moved through him like a current under ice — real, present, but unable to find the shape of its object. He mourned a man whose face he could not summon.

"He loved you," Maren said, watching him. "That was the worst part. That was the part you couldn't stop talking about for days. Betrayal you could have carried. Love was what broke you open."

"I can see that," Sable said softly. "Even now. Even empty." He flexed his hand. "And the mountain?"

"We marched. What was left of us — freed prisoners, villagers from Thornhollow, people you'd collected the way a magnet collects iron filings. We lost people. Wenna died holding a tunnel against Aldric's wardens; she was nineteen." Maren's voice caught and steadied. "Aldric's engines pierced the mountain and began draining Emberveil while you fought your way to the covenant-stone. And at the stone—" She stopped. Started again. "At the stone, the rite required memories. Fed willingly. Memories of everyone you loved, spoken into the rock, given up forever. That was the price. That was always the price."

"And I paid it."

"You paid it laughing," Maren said, and her eyes were bright and wet. "You looked at me first — before the others, before all of it — you looked at me and said, 'Worth it,' and then you began. Every memory, spoken aloud, burned away as you spoke. I held the tunnel. When I got back to the stone you were on the floor, and the dragon-fire was roaring through the veins of the mountain like a heart remembering its rhythm, and Aldric was on his knees in the ash because the restored covenant showed him the truth about his mother — that the fae hadn't driven her mad; his father's court had poisoned her for years — and he called off the draining with his own hands." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her palm, briskly, angrily. "And then the realms rebalanced, and the seals settled, and magic came back into the world lawful and licensed and bound by oath instead of hunted into cellars. And you slept for three weeks."

"I'm sorry," Sable said, meaning it absurdly, meaning *thank you*, meaning *I wish I remembered any of it, especially the part where you looked at me.*

"Don't apologize. You bought a world." She slid the book across the table toward him — the one she hadn't been reading. It was a ledger, half-filled, in a handwriting he slowly recognized as his own. "You started this two days ago. The healer said writing might help anchor things. You wrote down everything I told you, in your own words, so it would be *yours* and not just mine. Read it whenever you need. Add to it. It's not memory, but it's a map of where memory used to live."

Sable opened the ledger. His own hand looked back at him, careful and slanted, and near the front page a single line stood out, underlined twice:

*The woman who tells me the story is Maren. Trust her completely. This is the one thing I am certain of.*

He looked up. "Did I write this today?"

"No. Three weeks ago. First thing you wrote, before you could even sit up." Maren's composure finally cracked, just slightly, just at the edges. "I don't know what I was to you, Sable Montrose. I don't know what we were, or what we were becoming, or whether you'd have wanted— " She stopped. Breathed. "But I know what you gave up included me. Whatever I was to you, I was in there. And I intend to earn my way back in. However long it takes. Is that—"

"Yes," Sable said.

She blinked. "I hadn't finished the sentence."

"I know. The answer's still yes." He laid his hand flat on the ledger. "Whoever I was, he had good judgment. I can tell, because everything he chose — the guild, the mission, the people, you — everything I learn about him makes me proud to carry his name. So we rebuild. Memory by memory, page by page. You'll tell me, and I'll write it down, and eventually the map will be thick enough to walk on."

Maren laughed again — wetly this time — and reached across the table and gripped his hand, hard, the way you grip a rope in a storm. "Deal. But I warn you, there are forty-seven stories and at least six of them end with you doing something appallingly reckless."

"I look forward to all forty-seven."

* * *

They walked that afternoon through streets that were learning a new season.

Corvane had changed in ways Sable had no baseline to measure but could feel anyway — a loosening in the shoulders of the city. Charm-stalls stood openly in the market square now, their wares tagged with green licensing ribbons, their sellers wearing the new guild badge: a stylized flame within a circle, the mark of the re-founded Charmwrights' Guild. Children chased each other between the stalls wearing paper charms their mothers had bought for a copper apiece, harmless little things that made the wearer's hair curl or their voice squeak, and nobody seized anybody, and nobody ran.

At the center of the square stood a new statue, still smelling of fresh-cut stone: a dragon coiled around a tower, wings spread, and beneath it a plaque bearing words Sable read three times without fully absorbing:

*In memory of those who kept faith in the dark years, and to Sable Montrose, who gave what could not be returned.*

"People keep wanting to give me things," Maren said beside him. "Free bread. Free boots. One woman tried to give me her goat. I told them all the same thing — the man you're thanking doesn't remember any of it. You know what they say?" She glanced sideways at him. "'Then we'll thank him forward.' Like gratitude is a debt you pay to whoever comes next. I've started liking this city very much."

They climbed the hill at the city's edge as the light went long and amber, to a place Maren said he had asked to be taken — though he could not recall asking. A rise above the river, overlooking the rooftops, with a single bench whose wood was silvered with age.

"This is where you used to sit," Maren said. "Years ago. Before everything. You told me once — during the march, one night in Thornhollow — that when the world got too loud, you'd come here and watch the bells in the cathedral tower and pretend every ring of them was erasing one worry." She sat down and patted the space beside her. "Your body brought us here. I figured it was worth following."

Sable sat. Below, the city kindled its evening lamps one by one, and the cathedral bells began the hour, and the sound rolled over the roofs in great bronze waves. He closed his eyes and let it wash through him, hunting for an echo of the man who had once sat in this exact spot with worries worth erasing.

What surfaced was not a memory. It was smaller than that, and stranger: the feeling of the tune Maren had been humming that morning, the one that had snagged at him like a burr. He hummed it now, haltingly, and felt rather than saw Maren go still beside him.

"That's the song," she whispered. "The street-singer's song. From the day of the Proclamation. You told me about it — the singer the wardens dragged away while the crowd watched. You said you'd heard her melody for years afterward in your head and never known why it haunted you." Her hand found his. "Where did you hear it just now? Tell me exactly. Was it a picture? A sound? A—"

"It wasn't anything," Sable said slowly, turning the mystery over. "It was a *warmth*. Like the scale. Like—" He stopped. Brought the charm out of his pocket and opened his hand, and the dragon-scale lay in his palm catching the last of the light, and it was glowing. Faintly, gently, pulsing in time — he realized with a jolt — with the bells. With his heartbeat. With something vaster underneath both.

"Maren," he said quietly. "When I gave the stone my memories. All of them. Everything I loved." He watched the light swell and fade, swell and fade, patient as a tide. "Do you suppose it took them? Or do you suppose it *kept* them?"

Maren stared at the scale, and he watched understanding dawn across her face like sunrise coming up over the mountains — slow, and then all at once. "The covenant was reforged with your memories as its mortar," she breathed. "Which means they're not gone. They're *in it*. Holding the seal. Holding the door between worlds. Which means—"

"Which means somewhere in the weave of the world," Sable finished, wonder rising in him like water in a well, "there is a place where I still remember you. And if the covenant can be entered from one side..."

"...it might be entered from another," Maren said. "Oh — oh, you reckless, impossible man. You're going to try to get them back, aren't you? You're sitting here planning it already. I can see it. Forty-eight stories, and this one starts tonight."

"Not tonight." Sable closed his hand around the scale, and its warmth pressed into his palm like a promise returned. "Tonight, bread. Tomorrow, the ledger. And then—" He looked at her, at this stranger who had crossed a kingdom and a war and three weeks of empty rooms to sit beside him, and felt something in his chest settle into place, the way a compass needle settles, pointing home without needing to know the road. "Then we start walking. You and me. Toward the place where I remember."

Maren smiled — tired, fierce, hopeful, all at once, the way she seemed to do everything. "All right, Sable Montrose," she said. "Let's go find yourself."

Far below, the bells rang out the hour, and in the gathering dusk the dragon-scale glowed on, steady as a heartbeat, carrying its cargo of everything he had ever loved — waiting, patient as a tide, to give it back.
