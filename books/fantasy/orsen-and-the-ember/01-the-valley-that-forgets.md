## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The soldier came into Hollowmere on a Tuesday, which meant nothing, because no one in the valley kept track of days anymore.

Orsen watched him from the window of the Drowned Lantern, where she'd claimed her usual table—the one in the corner with a sightline to both doors and a crack in the plaster shaped like a river delta, if you squinted. She was squinting. She'd been squinting at that crack for six years, and it had never once been a river delta, but she kept hoping. Hope was cheap. Everything else in Hollowmere cost memory.

The soldier sat down heavily at the bar and ordered whatever was cheapest, which told her he was paid off or discharged or running. His boots were army-issue, resoled twice. His coat had the cut of the Vaskarren garrison, though the insignia had been unpicked—she could see the ghost of thread-holes where a crest used to sit. A man who'd stopped being what he was and hadn't yet decided what he'd be instead.

Those were the best kind. Those were the ones who didn't count their pockets.

She let him drink for an hour. She drank nothing herself—never did on working nights—and listened to him talk to the barkeep about weather that hadn't happened here, roads he'd walked, a posting in the hill country where the wine was terrible and the women were worse, all of it filler, all of it surface talk, the way men talked when they were keeping something precious buried underneath. Orsen knew the shape of buried things. She'd made a life out of digging them up.

He had a face like a closed door. But his hands kept moving toward his breast pocket—not to a locket, not to a letter, just touching the fabric over his heart, the way people touched things that weren't there anymore.

*There it is,* she thought. *There's the wound.*

She waited until he went outside to piss against the tavern wall, and then she followed him into the dark, and she took his daughter's face out of his head.

It went like this: magic could not cross the valley's borders. Whatever old catastrophe had carved Hollowmere out of the world—and there were as many stories about that as there were teeth in Mother Quill's head—it had left the place scoured clean. No spells worked inside the lines. No charms, no curses, no blessings. And because memory was the oldest magic there was—the first spell anyone ever cast, the one every child learned before language—memories faded here too. Not fast. Not all at once. They faded the way breath fades on glass, softening at the edges first, the details going blurry, until one morning you reached for your mother's voice and found only the shape of reaching.

Travelers who came through lost things. Small things at first—a name of an old friend, the taste of a particular meal. Longer stays took more. The valley ate slowly, politely, like a guest who didn't want to seem greedy.

But memories could be *moved*. Carried. Lifted out of one skull and tucked into another before the valley noticed them missing. It wasn't magic, exactly—or rather, it was the last magic, the residue, the ember-end of the craft that still smoldered in human hands even here, where everything else had died. A thief with the knack could take a recollection whole, intact, and hold it safe in her own mind indefinitely.

Which meant that in a valley full of forgetting, Orsen Pemberton was the only person whose head got heavier every year.

She crouched behind the woodpile while the soldier fumbled with his laces, and she reached into him the way you'd reach into a coat pocket—gently, apologetically, with fingers that knew their work. She found the memory by its heat. Precious memories ran warm; she'd never known why, but she'd stopped asking why about most things years ago. This one burned. She lifted it free.

A girl on a dock. Seven years old, maybe eight. Gap between her front teeth. Hair the color of wet sand, tied back with a strip of red ribbon that had been a soldier's—his, once, from his dress uniform. She was holding up a fish nearly as long as her arm, and her mouth was open mid-shout, mid-triumph, and behind her the water was doing that thing water does when it catches late sun, going all hammered gold.

His daughter. His whole face changed around the memory even now, unconscious, pissing drunk against a wall in a valley that would eat it by spring.

Orsen held it in her cupped mental hands and felt it pulse against her palms like a small animal.

*Don't,* said the part of her that still had a conscience left. *Give it back. He'll lose everything else anyway. Let him keep this.*

*And eat what?* said the part of her that paid rent. *Air?*

She gave the soldier back a harmless substitute—a market day, a haggling match over turnips, something bland and forgettable to fill the hole so he wouldn't feel the absence right away. He'd notice eventually. They always noticed eventually, standing in some doorway months later, reaching for a face that wasn't there. But by then he'd be gone from Hollowmere, and the memory would be safe in the Guild's vaults, and he could buy it back if he ever found out where it went. Some of them did. Very few could afford it.

She hated herself a little more. She was running low on hate these days; she'd have to start being more efficient with it.

* * *

The Guild of Kept Things kept offices above the chandler's shop on Rope Street, which everyone agreed was either a joke or a threat, depending on how much you thought about candles.

Orsen climbed the stairs at dusk. The stairwell smelled of tallow and old paper. On the second landing, a clerk was drawing fresh wards along the doorframe in silver-inked chalk—intricate spirals, tighter than the ones that had been there last month. Orsen noted this the way she noted everything: silently, completely, and without letting her face know about it.

"Evening, Master Corvane," she said.

Master Corvane sat behind a desk built for a much larger man, which made him look like a child playing at commerce, except that nobody who'd met his eyes ever mistook him for a child. He was small, gray, precise, with the kind of neatness that suggested a man who filed his own thoughts alphabetically. He looked up from a ledger and folded his hands.

"Pemberton." Her surname, always. Never her given name. She'd wondered, once, whether he even knew it. "You're early."

"I'm punctual. Everyone else is late."

"Indeed." He opened a drawer, removed a velvet-lined case, and set it on the desk without opening it. "What have you brought me?"

"A warm one," she said. "Recent. A soldier's. High emotional content."

Corvane's eyebrows rose a fraction of a millimeter. "Family?"

"Daughter. Maybe seven. Fishing off a dock. Red ribbon." She recited it flatly, inventory-style, because inventory-style was how you kept your voice steady. "Full sensory load. Sound of the water, smell of the fish guts, the works. He won't miss it for weeks, and by then he'll be three valleys away."

Corvane nodded slowly, the nod of a man appraising not the merchandise but the merchant. "Emotional memories fetch well. You know that."

"I know that."

"And yet you sound like a woman describing a shipment of nails."

"My voice doesn't change the price."

"No," Corvane agreed. "It doesn't." He opened the case. Inside lay a small glass jar, no bigger than a fist, stoppered with wax and copper wire. Empty jars always looked so ordinary. People expected vessels for memories to glow, or hum, or whisper. Mostly they just sat there, waiting to be heavy. "Standard rate, plus the family premium. You've earned a bonus this quarter—your yields have been consistent."

*Consistent.* Like she was a milk cow. Orsen kept her expression professionally blank and held out her hand.

The transfer took minutes. She breathed the memory out of herself and into the jar the way you'd pour honey—slowly, completely, scraping the sides. The dock. The gap-toothed shout. The hammered-gold water. The red ribbon that had been his, once, from his dress uniform, back when he'd been someone's son instead of someone's soldier.

When it was done, the jar glowed faintly, the way warm things do in cold rooms. Corvane stoppered it, wired it shut, wrote something in the ledger in his small exact hand, and slid coins across the desk. Good weight. Always good weight. The Guild paid fairly; that was the trap. Fair pay made honest work of theft.

"The wards," Orsen said, casual as a woman asking about the weather. "New chalk on the landing. Trouble?"

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—quick, controlled, gone. "Routine maintenance," he said. "Memories are delicate cargo."

"Mm." She pocketed the coins. "Well. If trouble comes through my end of town, I'll hear it first. I usually do."

"I don't doubt it." He returned to his ledger. "Good evening, Pemberton."

She climbed back down the stairs past the drying wards, past the candle-smell, out into streets already going blue with evening. Behind her, in a glass jar on a gray man's desk, a little girl held up a fish forever, shouting her triumph into wax and copper, waiting for a father who might never afford to hear her again.

Orsen walked home the long way, counting her sins. It was a short walk. She'd counted them many times.

* * *

Her rooms were above a cobbler's shop on Tannery Lane, two rooms and a leak, which in Hollowmere constituted luxury. She lit no lamp. She didn't need light to see the wall.

The wall was the problem. The wall was the confession.

Over the years, she'd filled it with scraps—little sketches, notes in her own hand, tokens. Not her memories; those were hers alone, locked away where no one could touch them. These were other people's. Details she'd memorized deliberately, so that the things she stole wouldn't vanish entirely from the world. The red ribbon had come from a dress uniform. The gap-toothed girl had a birthmark shaped like a comma under her left ear. The soldier's name was Dannet Halle. He'd taught his daughter to gut a fish before she could read.

She remembered for them, because no one remembered for her.

That was the arithmetic of her life, laid bare on a bad wall in a rented room: she had taken hundreds of memories, and she could tell you the contents of every single one, but if you asked her about her own childhood—if you asked her her mother's face, her father's trade, the color of the room she'd slept in—she'd look at you with pleasant, practiced blankness. There was a hole where Orsen Pemberton should have been. She'd woken up in Hollowmere eleven years ago with coin in her purse, a thief's skills in her hands, and nothing behind her eyes but fog. No before. Just the valley, and the work, and the wall.

She'd asked Corvane about it exactly once, early on. He'd smiled with his ledger-keeper's smile and said that the valley took from everyone, and that some arrivals simply arrived emptier than others.

She'd believed him for about four years.

Now she stood in the dark looking at her wall of other people's yesterdays, and she pressed her palm flat against the sketch of the dock, the fish, the ribbon, and she felt the familiar ache settle into her chest—the one that wasn't quite guilt and wasn't quite grief but lived in the house between them and paid rent to both.

Somewhere out there, Dannet Halle was drinking himself stupid over a loss he couldn't name yet. Somewhere in a jar, his daughter shouted. And somewhere in Orsen's chest, in the hole where her childhood should have been, the wind blew through with nothing to catch on.

"You're a coward," she told herself quietly. "You could give them all back. Walk into the Guild, open every jar, hand them out in the street like bread."

"But then I'd starve," she answered, "and worse, I'd be bored," and she laughed, because the alternative was crying, and she'd done her crying years ago, budgeted and brief.

She banked the fire, checked the locks twice—thief's habit, though the only thing worth stealing in her rooms was the wall—and lay down in the dark.

Sleep came slow. When it came, it brought the dream it always brought: a door, standing open, snow blowing through it, and someone small standing on the threshold saying words she almost understood.

She woke before dawn to knocking.

Not the dream-knock. Real. Three strikes, spaced wrong—too far apart, too deliberate, the knock of someone who had never knocked on a door before and had studied the concept from a distance.

Orsen sat up in the cold, knife already in her hand, and listened.

Below the window, in the blue hour, the snow lay unbroken on Tannery Lane—except for one line of small bare footprints leading up her stairs.

No one had crossed the valley border in the night. No one could have. The passes were sealed by storm, and even in fair weather, nothing came into Hollowmere without losing something on the way in.

Whatever was standing at her door had arrived without leaving footprints behind it.

Orsen tightened her grip on the knife and went to see.
