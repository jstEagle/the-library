## 2. A Knock at the Wrong Hour

The night the girl came, even the rats had sense enough to stay indoors.

Orsen knew it was a bad night before she'd finished her tea. The wind had turned from the salt flats in the afternoon and come up the valley carrying that particular dry cold that made old-timers mutter about the Year of White Silence, when the snow fell for forty days and Hollowmere forgot its own name — which, in a valley that forgot everything, was saying something. By dusk the streets of the Tannery Quarter had gone quiet in the wrong way. Not the hush of people sleeping. The hush of people listening.

Thieves read weather like farmers read sky. A night like this meant no travelers on the north road, no caravans sheltering at the Wayhouse, no drunk merchants stumbling off the ferry with their memories fat and unguarded. It meant hunger tomorrow. It meant staying in with the shutters barred and counting what you had.

Orsen counted. Three vials on the shelf, stoppered and labeled in her own cramped hand: *a wedding, secondhand; the smell of a harbor she's never seen; how to swim.* Eleven coins under the loose hearthstone. Half a loaf going stale. A jar of pickled onions she'd been saving for a day worth celebrating, which so far hadn't come.

She banked the fire, checked the bolt on the door twice — once out of habit, once because the wind kept worrying the latch like a dog with a bone — and lay down in her clothes.

She was almost asleep when someone knocked.

Three small raps. Polite. Patient. The kind of knock you give when you've been standing outside a long time and you're not sure anyone's home, or that it matters if they are.

Orsen opened her eyes in the dark and did not move.

Nobody knocked at her door. That was the arrangement she had with the world: she went to it, quietly, and took what she needed, and it left her alone. In nine years in Hollowmere she could count the visitors on one hand without using all the fingers. Fenna, when a lock stumped her. Mother Quill, bringing soup and gossip in equal measure. Once, a Guild clerk with a summons she'd already anticipated. That was the whole list.

And nobody — *nobody* — came out on a night like this. The cold tonight wasn't weather. It was the kind of cold that found the crack under your door and slid through it looking for skin.

The knock came again. Same three raps. Same patience.

Orsen sat up, took the knife from under her mattress — not because she expected to need it, but because in Hollowmere you learned that the things you didn't expect were the ones that got you — and crossed the room on silent feet. She put her eye to the gap in the shutters first. The street was a smear of white under a bruised sky, snow falling thick and slow, the flakes big as moths. Nothing moved in it. Nothing should have been moving in it.

Then she looked down, through the gap by the hinge where the wood had warped years ago, and saw feet.

Small feet. Bare. Blue-white with cold, planted in snow that had drifted halfway up the door, toes curled against the freeze as if the child standing on them had simply decided cold was something that happened to other people.

Orsen's stomach dropped through the floorboards of her ribs.

She unbolted the door and pulled it open, and the wind hit her like a wall, and there in the drift stood a girl of maybe six or seven years, wrapped in a man's coat far too big for her, hair full of snow, looking up at Orsen with an expression of profound and ancient calm — the look of someone who has walked a very long way and is not entirely sure this is the right house but has nowhere else to be.

"Gods' teeth," Orsen said. "Get in."

The girl didn't move. She looked at Orsen the way you look at a locked door, assessing. Then her gaze dropped to Orsen's hands — to the knife, still in her right fist — and back up, and something in her face shifted, some verdict reached, and she stepped over the threshold as if she'd owned it all along.

Orsen shut the door, threw the bolt, and turned to face the impossible arithmetic of a barefoot child alive after a night in this cold.

Up close the girl was smaller than she'd seemed, thin as a wren's bone, lips tinged gray. Her coat was good wool once, army surplus by the cut, sleeves rolled six times. And in her arms, held against her chest the way other children held dolls or dogs, she carried a lump of coal the size of a fist.

Except it wasn't coal. Orsen had grown up around coal, before — before whatever before was; her own childhood was a locked room she'd sold the key to — and coal did not glow. This thing glowed from within, a deep orange light pulsing slow and steady, like breath, like a heart. Snowflakes landed on it and did not melt so much as *vanish*, flinching away into steam before they touched the surface.

And the girl was warm. Orsen felt it as soon as she knelt to peel the coat off her: heat rolling off the child like she'd been sitting at a forge. Not fever-heat. Something steadier. The fire in the hearth leaned toward her, flames bending sideways in air that wasn't moving.

"What in the name of every dead god," Orsen said quietly, "are you?"

The girl looked at her and spoke.

---

It was not any language Orsen knew. She'd picked up scraps of half a dozen tongues in her years lifting memories at the border roads — trader dialects, soldier's curse-cant, the river-speech the ferrymen used. None of them sounded like this. The words were long and ringing, vowels that seemed to go on longer than lungs should allow, and each syllable landed in the little room with weight, the way a bell's note lands in your chest.

But here was the thing that stopped Orsen's blood mid-turn.

She *almost* understood it.

Not the words themselves. Something underneath them. It was like hearing a song you'd never learned and finding your foot tapping anyway. And deeper than that — it was like the vials on her shelf. Like the stolen things she carried. Somewhere in the smuggled cargo of other people's pasts stored behind her eyes, something recognized this language, and it *ached*, a low toothache of familiarity, a word on the tip of a tongue that wasn't hers.

The girl said something short and waited. Her eyes were dark and steady and much too old.

"I don't know what you're saying," Orsen said. "I'm sorry. I don't—"

The girl tried again, slower, and this time Orsen caught it: the cadence was different. Simplified. The way you speak to someone you're fairly sure is simple. She pointed at herself and said a single word, two syllables, round and resonant.

Then she pointed at Orsen and raised her eyebrows.

"Oh," Orsen said. "Names." She hesitated. Giving a name in Hollowmere was giving a handle; names were how the Guild's clerks filed you, how debts found you, how the valley remembered you just long enough to collect. But the girl was shivering now — finally, blessedly, shivering, which meant her body had remembered how to be an ordinary body — and you didn't withhold your name from a freezing child on principle.

"Orsen," she said. "Orsen Pemberton."

The girl repeated it. Her accent made it sound older, carved rather than spoken. Then she pointed at herself and said her own word — and Orsen heard it clearly, three notes rising, and felt that ache again, deep in the stolen places, like a key turning near a lock it almost fit.

"We'll find you something better than that," Orsen said. "Something that fits this valley." She looked at the girl — small, dark-eyed, fire-hearted, arrived out of a killing storm with no shoes and no fear. Birds survived winters. Birds turned up where they weren't planted and thrived anyway. "Wren," she said. "How does Wren sit with you?"

The girl considered it with the seriousness of a magistrate, then nodded once.

Wren. It would do.

---

Getting her warm was a battle fought in stages. Orsen stripped off the soaked coat, wrapped her in the blanket from her own bed, sat her in the chair closest to the hearth, and put water on for broth. Wren submitted to all of it with the grave cooperation of a soldier following orders she didn't fully understand. When Orsen lifted the girl's feet to check for frostbite — expecting black tips, expecting the worst — she found them pink and whole, warmed from somewhere inside, as if the cold had never gotten further than the skin.

The coal sat in her lap the entire time. Orsen gave it a wide berth.

She told herself she wasn't afraid of it. She was a professional. She handled other people's most precious possessions every week — a mother's face, a dead son's laugh, a first love's name — and she'd never once dropped a vial. But she caught herself circling the thing the way you circle a sleeping dog of unknown temper, and finally she made herself stop, crouch, and look at it properly.

Up close it was worse. The glow wasn't steady; it pulsed, and the rhythm was wrong for a fire. Fires flicker. This beat. Slow, patient, regular as a sleeper's heart. And when Orsen leaned close, the ache behind her eyes sharpened — that same terrible almost-recognition — and for half a heartbeat she smelled smoke that wasn't in the room. Woodsmoke and something under it. Something burnt that had once been a city.

She sat back hard on her heels.

"You're going to tell me where you got that," she said to Wren, "and who sent you, and how you walked into my valley, because nothing walks into this valley. Nothing crosses the border. That's the whole point of the place."

Wren looked at her with those too-old eyes and answered at length, in that ringing language, and Orsen understood none of the words and nearly all of the shape of it: *I ran. I ran a long time. They are coming.*

"They," Orsen said. "Who's they?"

Wren pointed at the coal. Then she pointed at herself. Then she drew her small fists together and pulled them apart, slowly, like tearing bread, and her face did something terrible and brief — grief, old and worn smooth with handling — before it closed again.

Orsen sat down across from her and drank her own broth without tasting it.

Here was what she knew, laid out plain in the firelight. Magic died at the valley's borders. Every smuggler, exile, and Guild clerk in Hollowmere built their living on that fact; it was the one law the place kept absolutely. Whatever this coal was, it shouldn't have been able to cross. Whatever the girl was, she shouldn't have been able to arrive. And yet here she sat, warming Orsen's chair, glowing like a hearth that had learned to walk, speaking a tongue that made Orsen's stolen memories stir and strain toward it like plants toward sun.

And she had said *they are coming.*

Orsen thought of Master Corvane's ledger-room, the smell of ink and cold stone, the way he'd paid double last month for anything old — *the older the better, Miss Pemberton, pre-Kingdom if you can find it, don't ask why.* She'd assumed vanity. Collectors were like that. Now, watching a child cradle a piece of burning night in her lap, she began to suspect the Guild's appetite had a purpose, and purposes, in Orsen's experience, always eventually had teeth.

Outside, the wind screamed down the chimney and the fire flattened, and Wren — Wren watched the window. Not scared, exactly. Watchful. The way you watch a road you know someone will come down.

Orsen rose and checked the bolt a third time.

---

The girl slept eventually, curled in Orsen's bed like a comma, the coal tucked against her stomach, its pulse slowing to match her breathing until Orsen couldn't tell which was keeping time with which. Orsen sat up in the chair with the knife across her knees and did not sleep at all.

She thought about the ache. That was the part she kept returning to, turning over like a coin of uncertain mint. Nine years she'd carried other people's memories — hundreds of them, thousands, whole lifetimes ferried through her head in vials and jars and the raw wet goods lifted straight from unwary minds. She knew every flavor of recognition there was: the jolt of a face you'd seen in a crowd, the tug of a song half-remembered. This was none of those. This was older. This was the memories themselves leaning toward the girl, straining at their stoppers, like dogs hearing their master's step.

Her stolen pasts knew Wren's language. Which meant somewhere, in whatever heads they'd originally lived in, someone had known it too — had spoken it, or fled from those who did — and had carried that knowing into the valley, where Orsen had picked it like a pocket.

Old memories. Pre-Kingdom. Corvane paying double.

Orsen looked at the sleeping child and felt the dread settle over her, cold and specific, taking measurements.

Because here was the last thing she knew, the thing she'd known since she opened the door: nobody finds this house by accident. Hollowmere's thieves had a saying — *the valley forgets, but doors remember* — and Orsen's door had been found tonight, in the worst storm in a decade, by a barefoot child carrying the one object in the world that should not exist here. Either Wren had chosen her out of ten thousand houses, or something had steered her. And either way, whatever was coming next was coming to this address.

Orsen had spent nine years taking futures away from people — a memory of a daughter's face was a future, though the soldiers never knew it when they sold it. She'd made her peace with that ledger the way you make peace with a bad knee: you learn to walk on it.

Now the arithmetic had reversed. Someone had walked out of the snow and handed her a future — a whole burning armful of it — and left without asking whether she wanted the weight.

Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something in that ringing tongue, and every stolen memory Orsen carried ached at once, a low chorus behind her eyes, and the coal pulsed twice, quick, like a heart startled from a dream.

Orsen gripped the knife and watched the shuttered window, and listened to the storm, and waited for morning, or for whatever came instead.
