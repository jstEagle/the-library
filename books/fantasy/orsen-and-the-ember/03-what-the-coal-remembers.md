## 3. What the Coal Remembers

The girl slept in my bed like she'd been born in it.

That was the first wrong thing. Thieves don't sleep like that—not in a stranger's house, not on the first night, not with their backs to the door and their hands slack and open. Thieves sleep curled around their exits. I'd spent twenty years sleeping like a knife folded back into its sheath, one ear open for the floorboard that creaked by the window.

She slept like someone who had never once needed to run.

I sat at the table across the room and watched her breathe and told myself I was keeping watch. That was half true. The other half was that I couldn't stop looking at her hands, because even in sleep they stayed cupped around the coal, the way a child cups a moth—gently, completely, as if letting go were unthinkable.

It was past midnight when I finally touched it.

I want to be honest about why. It wasn't courage. It wasn't some noble need to understand her suffering so I could help her. Hollowmere had taught me exactly one lesson about other people's burdens: everything can be sold, and knowing what something is worth is the same thing as being safe from it. I wanted to know what I was holding. I wanted to know what price it might fetch, or what trouble it might cost, and I wanted to know before the Guild's clerks put their soft white gloves on it and told me I didn't have the clearance to ask.

So I did what I always do with things that aren't mine.

I picked them up.

* * *

The coal was warm. Not hot—warm, the way a stone is warm after a full day of sun, the way a hand is warm when it's been holding yours too long to let go politely. It weighed almost nothing. It looked like a knot of black glass with a red seam down its middle, a seam that pulsed, slow, patient, like something breathing under a blanket.

For three heartbeats, nothing happened.

Then the valley forgot me.

That's the only way I can describe it. Hollowmere eats memory the way a riverbed eats rain—slowly, evenly, without malice. Everyone who lives here feels it. Names go first, then faces, then the reasons you came. You wake up some morning and there's a shape where your mother used to be, smooth and featureless, and you know she mattered and you cannot say why. The old-timers keep journals. I kept ledgers instead, because sentiment is just inventory you're afraid to price.

But this was different. This wasn't fading. This was *opening*.

The coal took hold of everything I had ever stolen—the soldier's daughter, the pilgrim's wedding, the smuggler's map of drowned roads, hundreds of small bright thefts I carried because no one else could—and it read them. All of them. At once. Like a clerk running his thumb down a ledger page, except the ledger was me.

And then it showed me what it had been reading all along, for years, for centuries, in other hands.

* * *

*A city made of white stone, terraced down a mountainside, every wall carved with writing. The writing is moving. The words are climbing off the walls and into the air, and the air is full of them, millions of them, a blizzard of letters—and then the city burns, and the burning is not fire. The burning is forgetting. Whole districts going blank, people standing in the streets opening their mouths and finding nothing inside, no names, no language, no selves, just standing bodies with weather behind their eyes.*

*I am seeing this through someone else's eyes. Someone tall. Someone whose hands are raised, palms out, and light comes out of the palms, and the light is made of memories that are not theirs.*

*They are spending other people's pasts like coin. And the coin buys fire.*

* * *

I came out of it with my chair tipped over and my back against the wall and the coal still sitting calm in my palm, warm as a sleeping bird.

My hands were shaking. I made them stop. That's a skill, making your hands stop—you bite down somewhere behind your teeth and the shaking goes where you send it. I learned it young. I don't remember learning it, which in Hollowmere means either I learned it long ago or I never learned it at all and simply always knew.

The girl was awake. She hadn't moved otherwise—still curled in my bed, still cupping the air where the coal had been—but her eyes were open and fixed on me, dark and enormous, and they weren't afraid.

That frightened me more than the burning city had.

"You saw," I said. Stupid. She didn't speak the trade-tongue, didn't speak anything I knew. But her head tilted, slow, deliberate—a yes.

I got up off the floor. I sat back down at the table like a person with dignity, which fooled neither of us. "What are you?" I asked her, and of course she couldn't answer, and of course I asked anyway, because terror makes everyone stupid in their own native language.

She uncurled. She crossed the room barefoot, silent, and stood in front of me, and held out both hands, coal and all, the way you offer bread to a stranger you've decided to trust.

Don't, I thought. Don't do that. I'm the person who takes things.

I took it.

* * *

*A battlefield, but the dead are not lying down. They are standing. Rows and rows of soldiers standing upright in the mud, weapons lowered, faces empty—and walking among them, men and women in gray robes, touching each standing corpse on the forehead, and where they touch, the corpses remember. You can see it happen. The faces fill in. Eyes focus. Mouths begin to move, saying names, calling for mothers, reciting the oaths they swore—and then the gray robes draw those memories out like thread from a spool, and the bodies fall over, truly dead at last, and the robes wind the thread onto spools of black glass.*

*Spools of black glass. Jars. I know jars. I sell to people who buy jars.*

*The war is not fought over land. I understand this now, in the boneless way you understand things in visions. The war is fought over remembering. Whoever holds the most past holds the most power, because the Ember—*

*the Ember—*

*a word like a furnace door opening—*

* * *

This time I dropped it.

It landed on the floorboards without a sound, which is impossible, and rolled two inches toward the girl and stopped, which felt like obedience, which was worse.

I sat there breathing. Sweat had soaked through my shirt and the fire in my hearth had burned down to almost nothing, though I could have sworn I'd fed it an hour ago. Outside, Hollowmere lay quiet under its snow, the tenements hunched together like animals sharing warmth, the Guild tower rising over everything with its green-lit windows, patient as a spider.

Old memories. That was the phrase Corvane used, every time, whenever I brought him something older than usual. *Ah, Orsen. Old memories.* He'd said it with a hunger he thought he was hiding, the way fat men hide hunger behind manners. He paid triple for anything predating the Kingdoms. I'd assumed it was scholarship. Collectors collect; rich men's hobbies are just greed wearing gloves.

It wasn't a hobby. It was provisioning.

* * *

The third time, I didn't touch the coal. I didn't need to. It had left something in me, the way a fever leaves something, and now that the door was open a crack, images leaked through on their own, thin as smoke under a door:

*A harbor city drowning in ash-fall, ships burning at anchor, and on the cliffs above, a line of children holding hands, and the children are the weapon. The children are always the weapon.*

*A mountain split open like a loaf, and inside it, a vault, and inside the vault, shelves, and on the shelves, thousands upon thousands of jars glowing faintly red, and gray-robed figures feeding them, one by one, into a mouth of light.*

*A woman's voice, speaking the girl's language, saying a word over and over—*Ember, ember, ember*—and each time she says it, a different meaning blooms: hearth, army, god, wound, seed, end.*

I pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes until I saw sparks that were only mine.

When I looked up, the girl was sitting across from me, cross-legged on the cold floor, watching me with the patience of the very young and the very old, which are the same patience. The coal sat in her lap. Its seam pulsed. In, and out. Breathing.

"Okay," I said to her, because nobody else was listening. "Okay. Let's work this through."

I talk to myself when I'm thinking. It drives partners away, which is why I don't have any. I laid it out the way I'd lay out a job: what I knew, what I could prove, what it was worth.

One. Magic doesn't cross the valley border. Everyone knows it; it's the whole reason Hollowmere exists, a hole in the world's fabric where the hunted and the haunted come to be ordinary. Whatever the coal was, here it was muted. Dormant. A banked fire in a wet climate.

Two. The girl had come *to* the valley. Through the snow, barefoot, at the wrong hour, to my door specifically. Things that travel that far with that much determination aren't lost. They're sent. Or they're fleeing, and they flee toward the one place their pursuers' power fails.

Three. The Guild bought old memories at prices that made no commercial sense, had done so for decades, and right now—this very week—was drawing new wards around its vaults. I'd seen the clerks mixing ward-ink on Tuesday. I'd thought nothing of it. Wards keep magic out. But if you were keeping something *in*—

Four. The visions. Cities burned with forgetting. Soldiers harvested standing up. Children as weapons. A vault of jars feeding a mouth of light. And threading through all of it, that word, that furnace-door word, the thing the whole machine existed to feed.

The Ember.

The girl wasn't cursed. Cursed things suffer their own afflictions; that's what a curse is, a private weather. She wasn't suffering anything. She was *carrying*. She was a jar herself—a living vessel, a container built to hold the last spark of the war-magic that had burned the world once already, kept safe the way you keep anything safe: by putting it somewhere no one would think to look.

Somewhere magic couldn't reach it.

Somewhere like Hollowmere.

"Oh," I said quietly. "Oh, you clever, desperate bastards. Whoever hid you."

Because that was the shape of it now, clean and terrible. The Ember couldn't be destroyed—that much the visions made plain; everyone who tried became scenery. So someone, in the last days of the last burning, had sealed it into a child and run for the one border in the world that magic dies at, and tucked her into the valley like a jewel into a lead box. And the valley had done what the valley does. It had eaten the years around her. It had eaten her pursuers' ability to sense her. It might even, I realized, with a fresh wave of cold, have eaten pieces of *her*—her history, her mission, maybe her voice itself, because what is speech but memory shaped into air?

She hadn't been mute when she arrived. I'd bet my best set of picks on it. The valley had taken her words the way it took everyone's, and whatever was left underneath was older than kingdoms, older than the valley, old enough to survive being forgotten.

And the Guild—patient, ledger-keeping, glove-wearing Guild—had been buying up old memories for decades not because they loved history, but because they were *feeding* something. Stocking a larder. Keeping a banked fire alive on a diet of other people's yesterdays, waiting for the day the vessel walked close enough to harvest.

Waiting for her.

Or waiting for whoever brought her in.

* * *

The girl reached across the table and touched my wrist.

Just that. Two fingers, light as snowfall. But the contact carried something—it must have, because suddenly I smelled woodsmoke and rain on hot stone, and I felt what she felt, or a translation of it, the way you feel heat through a closed door. Not fear. I'd expected fear. It was something more tired than fear and more stubborn: the feeling of having been carried a very long way by people who died doing it, and of knowing, with a child's absolute clarity, that dropping what you carry would kill more people than carrying it ever will.

She was asking me something. Her eyes asked it. Her fingers asked it against my pulse.

*Will you keep it?*

"I steal things," I told her. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "That's what I do. I take what people can't afford to lose and I sell it to people who shouldn't have it. If you knew what I was, you'd—" 

But she did know what I was. That was the sick joke of it. She'd knocked on my door, of all doors, in all of Hollowmere. Maybe the coal had chosen. Maybe the Ember, that furnace-word thing, that seed-end-hearth-god, had looked at the valley full of forgetters and found the one woman who *kept*—who hoarded other people's pasts in her head like a dragon of small mercies—and recognized a keeper when it saw one.

Maybe it had been reading me all along, the way it read me tonight. My whole ledger of stolen moments. The soldier's daughter's face. The pilgrim's wedding. Every bright thing I'd lifted from a traveler and couldn't bring myself to sell.

Every memory I carried because no one carried mine.

Her fingers tightened on my wrist. The coal's seam flared, one slow heartbeat of red, and in that flare I saw—not a vision this time, just an understanding, arriving whole, the way dread arrives—one more thing, the last piece, clicking into place with the sound of a lock turning:

The Guild pays so well for old memories because old memories are fuel. The Guild has wards on its vaults because the vaults are a hearth. And the Guild has sketches of a child on its clerks' desks because somewhere in Vaskarren, in a hall full of green-lit windows, Master Corvane is looking at a map of Hollowmere and waiting for a thief greedy enough—or kind enough—to bring him the last spark of the war that burned the world.

I had planned, tomorrow, to feel out what the Guild knew. To walk into the tower with my easy smile and my pockets full of nothing and see which way the wind blew.

Now I knew which way it blew. It blew toward my door.

The girl watched me decide. I could tell she saw it happen—I could tell because her shoulders came down half an inch, the way they do when a held breath finally gets spent. She picked up the coal. She cupped it. She looked at me with those enormous dark eyes, and for the first time since she'd appeared barefoot in my doorway, she smiled.

It was a terrible smile. Small and crooked and far too trusting, the smile of someone handing a knife to a stranger handle-first.

Outside, far off across the frozen rooftops, a bell began to ring in the Guild tower. Not the market bell. Not the hour bell. The deep one, the one I'd heard only twice in twenty years, the one that meant the wards were being redrawn and the clerks were working through the night.

They knew something had woken up.

I put my boots on.
