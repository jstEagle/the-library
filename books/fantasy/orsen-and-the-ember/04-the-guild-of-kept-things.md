## 4. The Guild of Kept Things

The Guild of Kept Things kept its offices in the old customs house on Ledger Street, a building that had once weighed cargo and now weighed something less honest. Orsen had been walking through its brass-bound doors for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once gotten used to the smell: ink and lamp oil and, underneath it, something faintly sweet, like fruit going bad in a locked room. She'd asked a junior clerk about it once. He'd looked at her as if she'd asked him to describe his own heartbeat.

*You get used to it,* he'd said.

That was the trouble with Hollowmere. Everyone got used to everything. The valley wore people smooth, like river stones, and what was left was easy to stack into walls.

She climbed the stairs to Corvane's office with a soldier's memory wrapped in oilcloth under her arm and her face arranged into its usual expression—bored competence, the look of a woman doing a chore. It was a good face. She'd practiced it against a cracked mirror until it stopped being a performance.

The clerk outside Corvane's door was new. That was the first wrong thing.

Corvane went through secretaries the way other men went through gloves, but he always hired the same kind: soft-handed, soft-voiced, forgettable by design. This one was none of those things. He was broad across the shoulders, with the kind of stillness that came from standing guard, and when Orsen gave her name he wrote it down—wrote it down, in a ledger, though everyone knew the Guild's clerks remembered nothing because they were paid not to.

"Master Corvane is expecting you," the man said.

Orsen had sent no word ahead.

"I'm sure he is," she said, and smiled with all the warmth of a drawn blade.

* * *

Corvane's office sat at the top of the customs house, behind two doors and a corridor lined with framed certificates no one could read anymore—the ink had faded decades ago, but the Guild hung them anyway, because the Guild believed in the shape of legitimacy even where the substance had rotted out. Orsen had stolen enough memories from enough Guild men to know that half of them couldn't have told you why the vaults existed if you held a knife to their throats. They just knew the pay was good and the questions were not.

Corvane stood at his window when she entered, hands clasped behind his back, watching the street below with the patience of a man who had never once hurried anywhere in his life. He was older than he looked, or younger—it was impossible to say in Hollowmere, where faces settled early and stayed. Grey at the temples, precise at the cuffs, a voice like dry paper folding.

"Pemberton." He didn't turn. "You're punctual."

"You said come."

"I said come when you were next selling." He turned then, and his eyes did their usual trick of making her feel inventoried. "And here you are. Sit."

She didn't sit. She unwrapped the oilcloth on his desk instead, letting the memory take shape in the lamplight—a small glass vial, stoppered with wax, holding a curl of grey light that moved like smoke underwater. The soldier's recollection of his daughter's face. She'd carried it three days, and in those three days she had looked at it more times than she would ever admit, because it was a beautiful thing, that memory. A man kneeling in a doorway while a small girl ran at him with her arms out, mud on her shoes, missing one front tooth. He'd thought about that gap in her smile every day for six years. He'd carried it across a war and a desert and half a continent, and then he'd walked into Hollowmere, and within a week it had begun to fade like breath on glass, and he'd sold it rather than lose it entirely.

Everyone in this valley made the same trade eventually. Something precious, exchanged for the mercy of not having to watch it die slowly.

"Soldier?" Corvane asked, lifting the vial without touching the glass directly. He always used a cloth. Superstition, or procedure—she'd never learned which.

"Mercenary. Passing through toward the coast."

"Recent?"

"Fresh enough."

Corvane held the vial up to the lamp. The grey light curled, and for a moment Orsen saw it again—the girl running, the missing tooth—and had to look away before her face betrayed her. Eleven years, and she still hadn't learned to stop seeing what she stole.

"Quality work," Corvane said. "As always." He set the vial down and slid an envelope across the desk. She didn't reach for it immediately, which was also part of the ritual; he would push it another inch, she would take it, the transaction would be complete, and neither of them would say anything true.

But today Corvane didn't push the envelope. Today he folded his hands on top of it instead, and looked at her, and said:

"How long has it been since you brought me anything old?"

Orsen kept her breathing even. "Old?"

"Pre-war. First War, I mean. Memories from before the burning." His tone was conversational, the way a man discusses weather right up until the moment he isn't. "The Guild pays a premium for them. You know that. And yet your inventory runs heavily recent. Soldiers, travelers, merchants. Nothing older than forty years."

"There's not much old left in Hollowmere," she said. "People fade fast here. Anything older than a decade is mostly mist by the time it reaches me."

"Mm." Corvane studied her the way he studied vials. "And yet Mother Quill remembers the valley's founding. And the ferryman at the north ford swears he recalls his grandfather's funeral, and his grandfather died sixty years ago. Memory fades unevenly, Pemberton. It fades fastest in the people who are trying hardest to hold on to it." He unfolded his hands. "Which makes me wonder what you're holding on to."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a clerk laughed at something, the sound muffled and distant, like a party in another house.

"I'm a thief, Master Corvane," Orsen said. "I don't hold on to anything. That's the whole profession."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

He pushed the envelope across the desk after all. She took it. It was heavier than usual.

"A bonus," he said. "For the soldier's memory. And because I have a commission I'd like to discuss, informally, off the books."

"I don't do commissions."

"You will want to hear this one." He came around the desk and stood by the window again, and now she understood that the window wasn't decoration—he was watching the street, and he had been watching it since she arrived, and there was a particular quality to his attention that raised every hair on the back of her neck. "We're looking for someone. A child. A girl, perhaps eight or nine years old, dark-haired, likely traveling alone or with strangers who are not her kin. She may be injured, or mute, or both. She speaks—" and here he paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—strangely. No language any of our people recognize."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree and settle. Orsen heard her own pulse in her ears.

"Missing child," she said. "Sad business. What's the reward?"

"Generous." He named a figure. It was obscene. It was four years of her earnings for a single delivery, no extraction required, no craft involved—just eyes and a word. "Alive and unharmed," he added. "That condition is absolute. The Guild wants her intact."

"Why does the Guild of Kept Things want a child?"

"The Guild keeps many things." Corvane smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen on him, because it was warm. "Some of them are large."

* * *

She made herself walk slowly down the stairs. Panic was a smell; she'd seen hounds track it, seen clerks mark it in their ledgers, and the last thing she needed was to leave a trail of it through the customs house. So she descended at a merchant's pace, counting steps, keeping her face bored, and only when she reached the ground floor did she let herself look around properly—at the corridors, at the doors, at the building itself.

The Guild's vaults lay beneath the customs house, three levels down, behind doors that cost more than most houses. Ordinary members like Orsen never saw them. But the corridors above the vault entrance were public thoroughfare, and public thoroughfares told stories to anyone who read them.

The story they told today was wards.

New ones. Fresh chalk-and-silver lines laid along the baseboards in patterns she'd never seen in eleven years of coming here—dense, interlocking, geometric in a way that made her stolen memories itch. She recognized fragments: a containment lattice from a battle-mage's mind she'd lifted off a dying officer years ago. A sealing script from a book she'd never read but whose pages she'd once glimpsed inside a scholar's head. These weren't theft-deterrents. Theft-deterrents kept things *out*. These were built to keep something *in*.

Two workmen knelt at the corridor's end, laying silver wire into mortar. One of them glanced up as she passed, and she saw the exhaustion on his face, the deep-boned tiredness of men who had been working nights.

"How long's the renovation?" she asked lightly.

"Renovation?" The workman blinked. "This ain't renovation, miss. We don't know what it is. Foreman says draw the lines where marked, ask nothing." He shook his head. "Been at it nine days. My hands won't stop shaking after. Silver does something to you, handling it that long."

Orsen thanked him and kept walking, and her heart was beating hard now, and she let it, because panic smelled different from fear and she was alone in this stretch of corridor, and fear she could carry.

Nine days. Nine days ago she had found a barefoot girl in the snow outside her door, clutching a coal that never cooled.

* * *

The clerks' hall occupied the customs house's eastern wing, a long room of desks and green lamps where the Guild's copyists transcribed inventories, drafted contracts, and recorded—in meticulous, joyless detail—every memory that passed through the building. Members weren't supposed to linger there. Members were supposed to drop their deliveries at intake and leave by the side door. But Orsen had spent eleven years learning which rules were load-bearing and which were paint, and the clerks' hall was paint. Clerks noticed nothing; that was their entire function. A woman pausing to adjust her boot near the desks was furniture.

She adjusted her boot for a long time.

The nearest desk belonged to a young copyist with ink-stained fingers and the vacant serenity of a man whose memory had been professionally maintained—which was to say, trimmed. His current page was an intake ledger, columns of dates and lot numbers. Boring. She shifted her weight, angling for the desk beyond, where an older clerk worked over a sheaf of loose papers weighted with a paperknife.

Sketches.

Pencil sketches, quick and repeated, the same subject drawn over and over with small variations, the way you sketch a face you've only heard described. A child. Round-cheeked, dark-haired, maybe eight, maybe nine. In some drawings the mouth was open, mid-word. In others the artist had tried different eyes—wide, narrowed, frightened—and none of them were right, because none of them were Wren's eyes, and Orsen felt a savage, dizzy relief in that wrongness even as her stomach dropped through the floor.

They didn't have her. They were looking for her. There was a difference between those two facts and the difference was everything, and it would not last.

Beneath the sketches lay a list, written in the cramped hand of a man taking dictation. Orsen read upside down, a skill from her first year in the trade, and the list was short and terrible:

*—sightings, north road (unconfirmed)*
*—ferryman questioned*
*—tenement wardens instructed*
*—handler Pemberton approached re: commission*

Her own name, sitting there in someone else's handwriting, filed between a rumor and an instruction. Not accused. Not suspected. Just—approached. A line item. A resource deployed.

She straightened up, flexed her foot as if her boot had actually bothered her, and walked out of the clerks' hall through the side door into the grey afternoon light of Ledger Street, and only then did she let herself think the whole thought, start to finish, without flinching.

They knew a child was in Hollowmere. They didn't know where. They were warding their vaults to keep something in—or to keep something from getting out—and they were paying obscene rewards for a little girl delivered alive, and Master Corvane, who had bought her stolen goods for eleven years with a paper-dry voice and never once asked her a personal question, had watched her from his window and smiled at her like a man closing a trap he'd built slowly, patiently, over years.

The safe life. The comfortable life. The life where she stole grief from travelers and sold it at fair prices and went home to her rooms above the chandler's shop and slept without dreaming. She had told herself for eleven years that it was freedom—the only freedom the valley allowed, freedom of a narrow sort, but hers.

Standing on Ledger Street with the envelope heavy in her coat, Orsen finally saw the shape of the thing she'd been living in.

A cage doesn't stop being a cage because the bars are comfortable. It stops being invisible. And the bars were visible now—chalk and silver beneath the customs house, pencil sketches on a clerk's desk, her name in a stranger's column—and every one of them had been there all along. She simply hadn't been looking. You didn't look, in Hollowmere. Looking was how memories faded faster. Looking was how you ended up selling the wrong things.

She walked home the long way, past the chandler's, past the market square where the fountain ran backward, past the corner where Fenna's lockshop glowed with lamplight. At her door she stopped, key in hand, and looked back down the street.

Halfway down, in the shadow of the cooper's awning, a man stood reading a broadsheet he was holding upside down.

Orsen went inside, barred the door, and sat down across from the sleeping child, and the coal in Wren's cupped hands pulsed once, softly, like a second heartbeat answering hers.

"They're coming," Orsen told her quietly.

Wren opened her eyes. She didn't understand the words—Orsen was almost sure she didn't—but she understood the tone, the way children and animals and anyone who had ever been hunted understood tone. She looked at Orsen with those too-old eyes and closed her fingers tighter around the ember, and said something in the language beneath kingdoms, low and urgent, and though Orsen couldn't translate a syllable of it, she caught the shape of the meaning the way you catch the shape of a knife in the dark.

It sounded, unmistakably, like *we have to run.*

Outside, somewhere down the street, the false reader folded his broadsheet and began, unhurried, to walk toward her door.
