## 5. The Knight Who Owed Her

Ser Aldric Vane had learned, in the six years since his exile, that shame kept better hours than most men. It rose early. It stayed late. And on nights like this one—when the wind came down off the salt flats carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet—it sat with him at the window of his rented room above the farrier's shop and refused to leave.

He was mending a strap. He was always mending something. A knight without a horse, an order, or a name worth speaking was mostly hands, and hands needed work or they started remembering things the rest of him had worked very hard to bury.

The strap was for a pack frame he'd bought off a tinker two winters back. Good leather, bad stitching. He pulled the awl through and thought, as he always did when his hands were busy and his mind was idle, about the worst night of his life.

Not the battle. Everyone assumed it was the battle—the rout at Cael's Ford, the broken banner, the order given too late, the forty men who died because Ser Aldric Vane hesitated at the wrong moment in the rain. That was the story they told in the taverns when they wanted to spit. That was the story the Guild had been only too happy to spread, because a disgraced knight asking questions in the wrong places was so much easier to dismiss as a drunk drowning his disgrace than as a man who'd seen what lived beneath the Ledger Hall.

No. The worst night came after. The worst night was quiet.

It was the night he'd walked into a memory-house in Hollowmere with his last coin and asked the woman behind the counter to take it away. All of it. The Ford, the rain, the faces of the forty—he'd wanted them gone the way you want a rotten tooth gone, all at once, root and crown, consequences be damned. He remembered standing there shaking, half-drunk still, saying *take it, take all of it, I don't care what it costs.*

And the woman—a thief called Orsen Pemberton, though he hadn't known her name then, only her eyes, which were the eyes of someone counting your grief for weight—had looked at him for a long moment and said:

"You don't want to forget it. You want to be able to carry it."

He'd told her she didn't know what she was talking about. She'd agreed cheerfully, taken his coin anyway—or pretended to—and told him to come back in three days.

Three days later she'd come to his room, sat down across from him without being invited, and put a small glass jar on the table between them. Inside it, coiled like smoke in water, was a thread of grey light. His memory. The Ford, the rain, the forty faces. She'd lifted it out of him while he slept—she never admitted how—and now it sat on his table in a jar, and he felt the absence of it like the absence of a tooth: a hollow place where something used to ache.

"Take it," he'd said. "Sell it. I don't care."

"I know you don't," she'd said. "That's the problem."

She'd stood, and paused at the door, and said the thing he had carried ever since like a stone sewn into his coat:

"I'm keeping this one. Not selling it. If you ever get to be a man who can hold it without it killing him, you can have it back. Until then, it stays where no one can buy it, drink it, or feed it to anything hungry." A beat. "Which, around here, is rarer than you'd think."

Then she'd left, and he'd spent a week convinced she'd sell it anyway, and then a month, and then he'd stopped checking, because the hollow place had started to fill—not with the memory itself but with the shape of it, the way a scar fills with new skin. He could remember that he'd failed. He could not relive it. She had taken the teeth out of it and left him the truth, and the truth, it turned out, was survivable.

He had never repaid her. He had tried, once or twice—left coin, done small kindnesses she pretended not to notice—but she waved it all off with that crooked half-smile, as if debts were just another kind of lock and she'd already picked hers open.

Six years. And in six years he had never once seen her look afraid.

Until tonight.

* * *

He'd been walking the long way home past the tenements—habit, not purpose; a knight keeps rounds even when there's nothing left to guard—when he saw her.

Orsen Pemberton stood in the shadow of the old grain arch at the mouth of Tanner's Row, and she was watching the road. Not glancing. Watching. The particular stillness of it stopped him mid-stride, because he knew that stillness. He'd worn it himself, once, on the walls of a fort that was about to fall. It was the stillness of a person counting exits.

She didn't see him approach. That alone was wrong. Orsen Pemberton noticed everything—pickpockets noticed everything, it was the whole trade—and yet here she was, fixed on the dark line of the south road like it might grow teeth, and she never turned her head until he was close enough to touch.

When she did turn, quick as a shutter, her hand was already inside her coat. Then she saw who it was, and the hand came out empty, and her face did something complicated that ended in a smile that fooled neither of them.

"Aldric."

"You're watching the road," he said.

"I'm admiring the road. It's a fine road. Very scenic."

"At midnight. In winter. Alone."

"Some of us have rich interior lives."

He looked at her until she stopped performing. That was another thing six years had taught him: under all the flippancy, Orsen Pemberton was the most honest person in Hollowmere. Thieves had to be. Everything else about them was false, so the core had to hold.

"The Guild," he said. "Something's happened."

Her jaw tightened. For a moment he thought she'd give him nothing—she was good at giving nothing, better than anyone he'd ever met—and then, quietly, in a voice stripped of all its varnish:

"There's a girl at my house. Maybe eight years old. Barefoot in the snow, no frostbite, which should be impossible. Speaks a language nobody knows. Carries a coal that doesn't cool." She paused. "And Corvane has readers on the streets asking questions about children."

Aldric felt the cold go through him, and it wasn't the weather.

Readers. Guild-trained sensitives who could taste magic the way sommeliers tasted wine. Except in Hollowmere there was no magic to taste—that was the entire point of the valley, the reason exiles and thieves and people running from things came here, the reason the Guild tolerated the place at all. Magic died at the border like a flame in a sealed room.

So why send readers?

Unless whatever they were hunting was strong enough to leave a trace even here. Even in the one place in the world built to smother such things.

"How many?" he asked.

"Two that I've counted. There'll be more." She was looking at the road again. "They're not searching like thieves search. They're searching like priests. Slow. Certain. Like they already know the answer and are just waiting for the valley to admit it."

"And you're going to do something stupid," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to do several stupid things, probably in sequence."

"Orsen." He stepped closer. In the dark, her face was pale and sharp and younger than her years, the way it always looked when the performance dropped. "Whatever it is. Whatever she is. You can't run it alone. You especially can't run it alone from the Guild."

"I've been running from the Guild since before you had your disgrace, knight. I'm very good at it."

"That's not what I mean and you know it." He heard his own voice come out rougher than intended, the old command cadence rising despite everything. He let it. Some things you didn't sand off. "One person is a target. One person with a child and a coal that doesn't cool is a funeral. You need hands. You need eyes that aren't yours. You need—" He stopped, jaw working. Six years of pride, and it came down to this, spoken plainly at last, in the cold, under a grain arch that smelled of rot and rat droppings. "You need me. And I need to pay a debt. So let's stop pretending we're both not going to say yes."

She stared at him.

For a moment, something moved behind her eyes—something startled, almost wounded, the look of a creature that has lived its whole life in a burrow and just been offered the sky. He understood it. Trust, for a woman like Orsen, wasn't a virtue. It was a vulnerability with a price tag, and she'd spent her whole life making sure the price was paid by someone else.

"You don't even know what you're offering," she said finally. "You don't know what she is. What they'll do if they catch her. What they'll do to anyone standing near her."

"No," he agreed. "Tell me."

And she did—or some of it. The coal. The language that made stolen memories ache. The wards going up around the Guild vaults, fresh-drawn, the sketches of a child on clerks' desks. She told it low and fast, a thief's briefing, and Aldric listened the way he'd once listened to scouts, sorting what mattered from what frightened, and what frightened him most was how little of it surprised him.

Because he had seen the vaults. Years ago. Before the disgrace, before the exile, when he'd still worn a white cord and served men whose names were struck from every ledger. He had seen what the Guild of Kept Things fed in the deep places, and he had deserted rather than carry that knowledge one more mile, and he had told himself for six years that exile was punishment when really it had been mercy—the Guild engineering his fall so precisely, placing him here in the valley where memories leaked away like water through cupped hands, where a man who knew too much would slowly forget he knew it.

Except he hadn't forgotten. Because a thief had taken the worst of it out of him and left the shape of the truth behind, and the shape of the truth said: *they are feeding something, and it is waking up.*

"The Ember," he said quietly.

Orsen went very still. "What did you say?"

"The thing she's carrying." His mouth was dry. "I don't know how I know the word. I think—I think part of me has always known. Since before the Ford. Since before any of it." He looked at her, and for the first time in six years, Ser Aldric Vane let himself be afraid where someone could see it. "Orsen. Whatever you're planning. However you're planning it. It needs to happen soon."

* * *

They parted at the corner of Tanner's Row, and Aldric walked home the long way, past the farrier's, past the chapel with its dead bell, past the well where the water tasted of iron and forgetting.

At his door he stopped. Somewhere across the rooftops, a shutter closed. Somewhere down by the salt flats, the wind changed direction, and for a moment—just a moment—he caught a smell underneath the snow-smell. Old smoke. Ash that had never fully cooled.

He stood in the doorway of his narrow room, strap-mending abandoned on the table, and looked at his hands. Knight's hands, once. Now just hands. But steady, still. Steady enough.

He owed her a debt six years unpaid. Tomorrow he would start paying it, and if the interest was blood, then so be it—he had forty reasons already in the ground, and room in his conscience for more.

From somewhere across the sleeping town, faint as breath on glass, he thought he heard a child singing in a language older than kingdoms.

The coal, he thought. The coal remembers.

And so, God help him, did he.
