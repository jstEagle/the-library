## 6. Thieves' Oath

The knock came at dawn, and it was wrong.

Not the rhythm—thieves had rhythms, three quick and one slow for a friend, two sharp for trouble. It was the weight of it. Whoever stood on the other side of my door knocked like a man who expected it to be opened, which in Hollowmere meant either a fool or an enforcer, and fools slept past dawn.

I put my palm over Wren's mouth before she could make a sound. She'd been sleeping in my bed for four nights now, curled around that impossible coal like it was a hearthstone, and she woke the way she did everything: silently, her dark eyes going wide and fixed on mine, asking questions in a language I couldn't speak but was starting, horribly, to understand.

*Stay. Quiet. Small.*

I signed it against her hand, the crude gestures we'd invented between us, and she nodded once. Then she did something new: she pulled the coal under the blanket with her and went still as furniture. A child who knew how to hide. That knowledge sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

The knock came again. Three quick, one slow.

Fenna.

I let out a breath I hadn't known I was holding and crossed the room, checking through the shutter's gap first out of habit. She stood on my landing in the grey light, red-haired and freckled and vibrating with urgency, her tool belt hanging loose off one hip the way it did when she'd dressed in a hurry. Behind her, the tenements of Lowmoor climbed the hillside in their crooked rows, chimneys smoking, laundry frozen stiff on the lines. An ordinary morning in the valley that forgot.

Except for the men at the bottom of Tanner's Stair.

There were six of them, maybe seven, wearing the grey-and-silver of the Guild of Kept Things, moving door to door with the unhurried confidence of people who had never been told no. One carried a ledger. Another carried something I couldn't see clearly—a case, long and narrow, held flat across both arms like an offering.

Like a coffin for something small.

"Orsen." Fenna's voice came low through the door when I opened it a hand's width. "They started at the river gate an hour ago. Working uphill. Asking after a girl."

"Asking how?"

"Asking *showing*." Her jaw tightened. "They've got a sketch. Good one, too. Corvane's clerks draw better than they count."

Wren. They'd found Wren. Four days I'd kept her fed and hidden and taught her the signs for *hungry* and *dangerous* and *sleep now*, and the Guild had closed the distance in four days, which meant they hadn't been searching at all. They'd been *tracking*. Something about that coal, or something about the girl herself, sang a line straight back to the vaults beneath the Ledger Hall, and all my careful invisibility had been a curtain drawn across a window already broken.

"Come in," I said. "And Fenna—I'm going to need everything you owe me."

She stepped inside and stopped dead, because Wren had come out from behind the blanket, coal clutched to her chest, staring at the stranger in our doorway with the fierce, evaluating gaze of a cat deciding whether the new thing in the house was friend or furniture.

"Oh," Fenna said softly. "Oh, you poor little scrap. Look at you."

"She doesn't speak," I said. "Not any language we've got. And before you ask—yes, that's the girl from the sketch, no, I don't know who sent her, and yes, I've touched the coal, and no, I'm never doing it again."

"You look terrible, by the way."

"I feel worse. Get the door barred."

* * *

By full light, I had counted what I owned.

Not coin—I had coin, though coin buys silence only once and cheaply. I meant the other ledger, the one every thief in Hollowmere keeps written somewhere behind the eyes: names, debts, favors banked against bad weather. I had spent eleven years in this valley lifting memories from travelers and selling them up the hill, and in eleven years I had been careful in ways that had nothing to do with the Guild. I'd returned a lockwright's stolen apprenticeship. I'd carried a dying woman's confession to her estranged son. I'd sat with old Mother Quill through three winters of nights and listened to her recite, from a book that no longer existed, the names of kings who had burned.

You survive in Hollowmere by being useful. You *matter* by being kind, if you can afford it.

I spread the tally out loud for Fenna while Wren watched from the corner, coal glowing faintly between her knees like a patient animal.

"Fenna. The twins. Mother Quill. Who else?"

"Depends what you're asking for."

"Everything. Hiding places, false papers, eyes on the stairs, a way out if it comes to that. I'm asking for a war, Fenn. A small one."

She was quiet a moment, winding a pick between her fingers the way she did when she was thinking with her hands. "You know what they'll do to us if they catch us helping you. Extraction's not gentle. I've seen what comes out of those rooms. People walk out with their mothers' faces gone."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm not asking." I looked her in the eye. "I'm telling you what's coming, so you can choose your side before it chooses for you. There's a difference. One of them is a favor. The other is an oath."

Fenna snorted, but her ears had gone pink, which in eleven years of partnership I had learned meant she was moved and furious about being moved. "You always were a dramatic bitch, Pemberton." She snapped the pick into its slot in her belt. "Fine. Oath. But I want it on record that I had a good life planned. Quiet. Respectable. Mostly burglary."

"Mostly?"

"A girl has to keep her hands busy."

* * *

Pim and Sable lived above the rope-walk in a room that smelled of hemp and lamp oil, and they were already awake, because the deaf never sleep deeply in a city that has learned to lie to them. They read my hands before I'd finished the first sentence. Twins born twenty-nine years ago to a fishwife who drowned, raised by the valley, fluent in a sign-language they'd built themselves out of work-rhythm and defiance—and, since the year I'd lifted a traveling scholar's memory of formal hand-talk and given it to them free, fluent in mine.

*I need eyes,* I signed. *Ears too. The Guild is hunting a child. Grey coats, working uphill from the river. I need to know where they stop, how long they stay, and whether anyone follows me home.*

Sable, the elder by minutes and by temperament, answered with the flat economy of a woman who had spent her life being underestimated. *How many favors is this?*

*All of them.*

*Then we collect later,* Pim signed, grinning, younger by minutes and by nothing else. *When you're rich and famous and ashamed.*

"They're in," Sable said aloud, for Fenna's benefit, her voice rough from disuse the way voices always were among the twins. "But Orsen." She caught my wrist, and her hands went still. Sable touching you was rare enough to mean something. "Grey coats took Doss Marlowe yesterday. From his own shop. No paper, no charge. His wife says they took his memory of his wedding day out of his head right there on the floor, to punish him for shorting a tithe."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the season. "That's not collection practice. That's terror."

"That's *new*," Sable said. "Whatever you're carrying, whatever that child is—the Guild is scared of it. Scared things bite. Be faster than teeth."

* * *

Mother Quill's room was at the top of the crookedest house in Lowmoor, up four flights of stairs that leaned like drunks, in a nest of blankets and books and the small amber bottles she traded to the apothecaries for tea. She was ninety-one years old and had lived in Hollowmere longer than the Guild had kept ledgers, and when I came through her door without knocking she didn't even look up from her knitting.

"Sit down, girl, you're letting the cold in. And bring that child where I can see her."

I froze on the threshold. "How—"

"Old eyes," she said, "see sideways. Half the street saw you buying children's boots two sizes too big Tuesday last, and the other half saw you lying about why. Sit."

Wren crept in ahead of me, coal hidden under her shawl but glowing through the wool like a lantern through a curtain, and Mother Quill set down her needles and looked at her for a long, long moment. The old woman's face did something complicated—grief and recognition fighting for the same ground.

"Say something, child," she said gently. "Anything at all."

Wren said something. Three syllables, soft and rising, in that language that made my stolen memories ache like old scars in rain.

Mother Quill closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet. "*Ash-keeper,*" she translated, or tried to. "It's a title, not a name. Or it was. My grandmother's grandmother heard it sung in the streets of a city that isn't there anymore, before the Ember War, before the borders went dead. It means the one who carries the fire so the fire doesn't carry itself." She reached out one knuckled hand and touched Wren's cheek. "They used to be honored. Then they used to be hunted. Then there weren't any left, and everyone agreed to forget why the world has a scar down its middle."

"The Guild knows," I said. "Corvane knows. They've been paying premium rates for old memories for years, and I never asked why, because the money was good and asking questions is how thieves die."

"Money's good," Mother Quill agreed, "right up until you find out what the stove's been fed." She fixed me with eyes gone pale as frost but sharp as tacks underneath. "You came here for hiding places and I'll give you mine—the cellar under the dye-house, the one with the false wall, tell Fenna I said the word *larkspur* and she'll know. But listen to me, Orsen Pemberton, because I'm old and I get to say true things. You can hide that child for a month. You cannot hide her forever, not from men who feed a god on stolen yesterdays. Sooner or later you have to stop hiding and start running, and when that hour comes—" she gripped my wrist, surprisingly strong, "—you run *outward*. Whatever the Guild tells you about the border, whatever the valley whispers about what waits outside, remember: magic dies here, but so does help. Nothing that hunts her can cross. Nothing that would save you can come in. Choose your cage."

* * *

We met at midnight in the back room of the Drowned Bell, which was empty because the Drowned Bell was always empty; it existed mostly as a rumor with a bar attached. Fenna brought maps. The twins brought news. I brought Wren, who had refused to be left behind with a ferocity that needed no translation, and Mother Quill, who had insisted on coming because, as she put it, someone sober should witness the crime.

"Here's the shape of it," I said, and laid it out the way I'd lay out a mark: the facts, the gaps, the angles. "Six enforcers working the tenements, maybe more at the gates. They have her face. They don't have her location yet—or they'd have taken her already instead of knocking politely. They will have it soon, because this valley leaks, and someone in Lowmoor has already done the arithmetic on what Corvane pays for a live find. We have days. Maybe less."

"And after?" Fenna tapped the map, the smudge of ink that was Hollowmere, ringed by the salt flats, ringed by the dead border. "Even if we ghost her through every cellar in the city, the valley ends. There are exactly three ways out, all watched, and outside—" she glanced at Mother Quill, "—outside is everything the valley keeps out."

"Outside is also where she needs to go," I said. "I don't fully know why. The coal showed me pieces. Burning cities. A war fought with memory. And a line of bearers, one after another, carrying the fire so it couldn't be *used*." I looked down at Wren, sitting on the bench between the twins, solemn as a small judge, the coal resting in her lap shedding heat but never light. "She's not cursed. She's a *keepsake*. Someone gave her the most dangerous thing in the world and told her to run, and she ran until she hit the one place in creation where nobody's magic works—including hers. Including the thing hunting her."

"So the valley's been her shield," Sable said.

"The valley's been her shield. And shields fail." I straightened. "So here's my oath, and I'm asking for yours. We keep her alive. We keep her free. And when the road opens, we take her out—not sell her, not trade her, not hand her to anyone who uses words like *asset* or *vessel* or *for the greater good*. Out. Whatever it costs."

Silence, of the particular texture that Hollowmere does well: the silence of people calculating, not whether, but how much.

Pim broke it first, signing fast, grinning: *I'm in. Obviously. This is the best thing that's ever happened to us and I include the flood of '41.*

Sable rolled her eyes and then signed, slower: *In. But I want it understood—if it comes to choosing between the child and one of us, we choose each of us, one at a time, and we get everyone out. I don't do martyrdom. Martyrdom is just theft with extra steps.*

"Agreed," I said. "Nobody gets spent. That's the whole point of the oath."

Fenna spat in her palm and held it out, which in Lowmoor is a contract older than any ledger. "Lockwright's word. Doors open, doors close, doors lie to whoever I tell them to lie to."

Mother Quill sighed like a woman settling into a bath, spat in her own palm, and pressed it to Fenna's. "Quill's word. I'm too old to run and too mean to catch. I'll be your memory, girl—all of yours. Every name, every debt, every promise made in this room tonight. If the Guild takes what you remember, I'll still have it, and I'll give it back." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "That's what I'm for. I'm the valley's spare copy."

Wren looked around the table at all of us—at the thief, the lockwright, the twins, the crone—and then she did something none of us expected. She set the coal down on the scarred wood, cupped her small hands around it, and spoke. Not the soft rising phrase from before. A whole sentence, low and deliberate, addressed to each of us in turn, and though the language was older than kingdoms, the meaning arrived in my chest without passing through my ears, the way heat arrives before light:

*Witnessed,* it meant. *Witnessed, witnessed, witnessed, witnessed.*

The coal flared once, briefly, gold as a struck match, and went quiet again.

"Well," said Mother Quill, after a moment, dabbing her eyes with her sleeve. "I've sworn oaths before. Never had one *notarized*."

* * *

I walked home the long way, through streets silvered with frost, Wren asleep against my shoulder inside my coat, her breath fogging the collar. At the corner of Tanner's Stair I paused, and below me I saw them: two grey coats at the mouth of the alley, standing very still beside a third man I recognized even at this distance, even in the dark, by the way he held his lantern low and his shoulders high.

Master Corvane himself. In Lowmoor. At midnight.

He wasn't searching. He was waiting, patient as arithmetic, and as I watched he turned his head—slowly, deliberately—and looked directly up at the corner where I stood, thirty yards and one shadow away, and inclined his head a fraction, the way a man greets an appointment he knew would keep.

He knew. He'd known since the first night. Everything since—the searches, the sketches, the door-to-door theater—hadn't been a hunt at all.

It had been a tightening.

I stepped back into the dark, my heart hammering a thief's rhythm, and began, silently, to plan a route out of the only home I had ever known.
