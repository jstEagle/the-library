## 7. The Language Beneath Kingdoms

Mother Quill had outlived three kings, two plagues, and every person who had ever called her by her given name, and she had done it by keeping one rule above all others: when the young come to you frightened, feed them first, and ask questions after.

So when Orsen Pemberton came up her crooked stairs with a small girl tucked under her arm like stolen bread, Quill didn't ask. She put the kettle on, cut thick slices of the dark bread she baked for no one but herself, and set the table for three.

The girl—Wren, Orsen called her, though the name sat on the child like a coat borrowed from a taller sister—wouldn't sit. She stood at the window with her fists pressed against the glass, watching the snow, and she was talking. Not crying, not muttering. Talking, in a low urgent stream, to the window, to the snow, to the coal she clutched in her left hand like a bird she was afraid would fly.

Quill's hands stopped moving.

The bread knife stayed in the air.

She knew that sound. Not the words—she had spent eighty-nine years in a valley that ate language for breakfast, and she had learned the hard way that no tongue survived Hollowmere's appetite for long. But the *sound* of it. The way the syllables rose at the ends of sentences like smoke finding a chimney. The way certain consonants cracked, dry as old paper folding. The rhythm of it, which no living mouth should have known, because the people who had spoken it had been dead for longer than Hollowmere had been a valley.

"Orsen," Quill said, and her voice came out older than she felt, which was saying something. "Where did you find her?"

"Roadside, past the mill. Night of the bad snow." Orsen's eyes were sharp and tired; the girl had been with her four days now, and it showed. "Why? Do you know what she's saying?"

"No." Quill set the knife down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "But I know what it isn't."

* * *

The book had been real once. Quill could vouch for that, because she had held it.

She had been nine years old, and the valley had not yet been the valley—it had been a river town called Mereholt, and her father had been the town's scrivener, a thin ink-stained man who kept the records of births and debts and who owed whom. The book had come to him for appraisal, brought by a wool merchant who claimed to have won it at cards from a pilgrim who had won it from a dying man who had won it from nobody, because some things have no honest origin.

It was small. Smaller than a hymnal, bound in leather gone the color of dried liver, and it had no title on the spine, only a mark pressed into the cover like a brand: a circle with a single point of light at its center. A coal in a ring of ash. Her father had turned it under the lamp for a long time before he opened it, and when he did, he made a sound Quill had never heard him make before or since—a small, involuntary noise, like a man touching a stove he'd been told was cold.

The pages were written in no alphabet he recognized. But between the lines of whatever language it was, there were drawings, and the drawings needed no translation. Cities burning. A figure standing in fire without burning. Crowns being offered, and hands refusing them, and hands that didn't refuse, and what came after the hands that didn't refuse: fields of ash with small figures walking through them, bent under bundles, and the bundles were not goods. Quill had been nine, and even she understood that the small figures in the ash-fields were carrying the dead.

Her father had sent the merchant away with the book and then sat up all night with his maps and his genealogies, and in the morning he had told Quill's mother something in a low voice, and her mother had gone white and said, "Then burn it."

"No," her father said. "Burying it is worse. If we burn it, we choose what it becomes."

In the end the town chose for him. The reeve came with four men and a warrant Quill's father never explained, and they burned the book in the market square on a wet Tuesday, and the fire had burned *wrong*—too bright, too fast, flames the color of a coal's heart rather than a wood fire's edge—and the smoke had gone straight up in a perfectly still column, like a plumb line dropped from the sky, and everyone who watched it forgot the details within a season. That was before the valley. That was just how some things were. Some things did not want to be remembered, and they defended themselves.

Quill remembered anyway. She had always been like that. The valley hadn't come for another forty years, and by then her particular stubbornness was already set like a bone that healed crooked: it worked, but it worked wrong, and it let her keep things she shouldn't. She remembered the column of smoke. She remembered the mark on the cover. And she remembered—because her father had spent one desperate week with it before the reeve came, cross-referencing it against every dead language in his library—that the book was old. Not kingdom-old. Not empire-old. It was from *before*. From the time the histories called the Ember Years and then stopped calling them anything, because the nations that kept histories had been part of what burned.

The age when the fire chose its bearers. The age when nations burned for them.

And the little girl at her window was speaking it.

* * *

"Say it again," Quill said.

Wren looked at her. The child's eyes were the color of a winter sky over slate, and they had the particular stillness of something that watches a great deal and is watched too much. She looked at Orsen first—asking permission, Quill noted, or offering protection—and Orsen gave a small nod, the nod of a woman who had decided to trust Quill and was not entirely comfortable about it.

Wren spoke.

The sound filled the kitchen like water fills a bowl. Quill closed her eyes and let it move through her, and beneath the strangeness she caught the architecture of it, the bones her father had mapped forty years ago and eighty years ago both: the rising terminals, the cracked consonants, the way the language seemed to lean forward, always leaning, as if every sentence were walking toward something.

"She's not speaking to us," Quill said, when the stream paused. "Listen. Her pitch doesn't change when she looks at us. She's narrating. She's—describing. The way you'd describe weather to someone who couldn't feel it." She opened her eyes. "Or the way you'd keep a record."

"Keep a record of what?"

Quill looked at the coal in the child's hand. It sat there, black and patient, and the firelight didn't reflect off it so much as it seemed to come from somewhere *behind* it, from a long way off.

"Of what it shows her," Quill said. "I'd wager my last tooth on it."

Orsen's jaw tightened. "You know what it is. The coal. You've gone pale as milk."

"I know what the book was." Quill lowered herself into her chair, and her knees complained, and she ignored them. "The one the reeve burned. My father appraised it, the winter I was nine. It was written in that language, girl. That exact tongue. And it was older than every kingdom on every map he owned. He checked. He spent a week checking, because he was that kind of man, and because the book frightened him enough to make him thorough." She folded her hands. "The age it came from had a name once. The Ember Years. You'll not find it in any history now, because the histories burned with everything else. But the shape of the story survives in nursery rhymes, if you know how to listen. A fire that chose people. A war fought with something other than swords. And at the end of it, the fire was taken out of the world and hidden, and the hiding cost—" She stopped. Her hands had gone tight in her lap without her permission. "The hiding cost more than the war."

Orsen was quiet for a moment. "And you never told anyone. A book like that, and you kept it to yourself for eighty years."

"I told the fire," Quill said. "I was nine. I stood in the square and I watched them burn a language, Orsen. The last of it, maybe, in all the world. And I learned the lesson the town learned, which is that some knowledge is a load-bearing wall. You pull it out and the whole house comes down on your head." She met Orsen's eyes. "I have kept quiet for eighty years because keeping quiet was safe. And now a child walks into my kitchen speaking the dead tongue like her mother's milk, holding a coal that doesn't cool, and safe is over. Safe ended the night she knocked on your door."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the snow kept its own counsel outside.

Quill had nothing left of the book but memory, and memory in Hollowmere was a leaky roof—what it kept, it kept crooked. But she had spent a lifetime being crooked in exactly the useful way. She made Wren speak, and she made her repeat, and she matched the child's sounds against the fragments her father had taught her to recognize: the word-shapes he'd sounded out phonetically from the drawings' captions, the ones he'd transcribed in his notes before the reeve came, the notes that had burned with the book and survived only in a nine-year-old's merciless recall.

Most of it was beyond her. But fragments surfaced, and each one landed like a stone dropped down a well.

There was a word Wren used constantly—a small word, two syllables, that she wove into nearly every sentence like a thread. Quill's father had transcribed its shape from the book's margins, where it appeared more than any other word, and he had guessed at its meaning from context and from the drawings it sat beside: the burning figure, the crowns, the ash-fields. He had guessed *fire*. But hearing it now, in a living mouth, Quill thought the guess had been too small. The word didn't sit in Wren's sentences like a noun. It sat like a name. Like something you addressed.

"It's not *fire*," Quill murmured. "It's *you*, or it's *thee*. She's not describing the coal. She's talking *to* it."

Orsen had gone very still. "Talking to it."

"Or for it." Quill watched the child. Wren had paused at the window again, and she was speaking in the low even cadence of recitation, and her free hand moved in small precise gestures—counting, Quill thought, or listing. "That's the thing your Guild pays you to forget, girl, if you ever knew it. The old stories don't say the Ember was a weapon. Weapons get made. The stories say it *chose*. It picked its bearers the way a hawk picks a wrist. And the bearers didn't carry it like a torch. They carried it like—" she searched, and found the word in a rhyme her grandmother had sung, a nonsense rhyme about a girl who swallowed a star, "—like a secret. Like something entrusted. The nations didn't burn *around* the Ember, Orsen. They burned *for* it. For the chance to hold it. For the right to say they were the ones it trusted. Men did what men do with anything holy. They made it a prize, and the prize made a war, and the war ate the world down to the ash in those drawings."

"And the child?"

"The child is a vessel." Quill said it plainly, because dressing it up would be cruelty. "The way a jar is a vessel. The Ember was hidden in something that could keep it safe, and keep it *asleep*, and someone—something—decided a child was the safest jar in the world. A child who can't tell anyone what she carries. A child who can't even speak the tongues of the people around her, so no one can ask." She shook her head slowly. "Except the valley. The valley took her voice the way it takes everything. And it couldn't touch the rest, because the rest isn't magic, girl. The rest is older than magic. The valley's borders don't stop it because it was never crossing *in*. It was already here, asleep in her, and the valley has no teeth for it."

Orsen's face had gone the color of the snow outside. "The Guild," she said. "The memories I've been selling them. The old ones. The ones they pay triple for."

"What about them?"

"They're not buying keepsakes." Orsen's voice was flat and careful, the voice of a woman walking on ice and listening to it. "They're buying *food*."

* * *

Dusk came down blue and cold, and Orsen went to the window to check the street, because that was what Orsen did—Quill had watched her do it a dozen times in an afternoon, a habit worn so deep it had become a kind of breathing. Wren had finally sat, finally eaten, and now she sat beside Quill's chair with the coal in her lap, and her narration had gone quiet, and she was looking up at Quill with an expression that Quill recognized from eighty years of looking into mirrors: the look of someone about to hand over a burden.

The child spoke one sentence. Slowly. Deliberately. Aimed, this time—her pitch changed, her eyes held Quill's, and the words were not narration. They were address.

And Quill, who had spent eighty years keeping a dead language alive in the crooked attic of her skull, understood it.

Not all of it. Three words out of perhaps eight. But the three words were the load-bearing ones, and the house came down.

*You carry it now.*

Or the shape of that. The grammar was strange, the tense was strange—there was a quality to the verb that Quill's father's notes had marked with a question mark, a tense for things that had happened and were still happening and had not yet happened, all at once—and maybe the true meaning was *you have always carried it, you will carry it, you carry it now*. But the heart of it was simple, and simple things cut deepest.

"She asked you something," Orsen said from the window. "What did she say?"

Quill looked at the child. The child looked back, patient as winter, and the coal in her lap breathed its far-off light, and Quill felt eighty-nine years settle onto her shoulders like a second spine.

"She told me," Quill said, "that I'm the first person in eighty years to hear her language and understand it. And she told me that means something. In her tongue, understanding is the same word as keeping." She reached out and, very gently, touched the child's hair. "She's made me her keeper, Orsen. Whatever that means. Whatever's coming."

Orsen turned back to the window, and Quill saw her go rigid.

"Orsen?"

"Riders," Orsen said quietly. "Three of them. Guild colors. They're stopping at every house on the lane." She looked over her shoulder, and her face had already rearranged itself into the calm of a woman with a plan, but her eyes were doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic was bad. "They're asking after a child."

Quill rose, knees be damned, and took the kettle off the fire, and put the room into its ordinary evening shape with the speed of a woman who had survived three kings by knowing when to make a house look like nothing was hidden in it. But her heart was hammering out a rhythm older than any of them, a rhythm from a book burned before she was born, and the words it beat out were the ones her father had transcribed from the last page of the last chapter, the words the drawings of ash had sat beneath, the words she had never spoken aloud in eighty years:

*The fire sleeps. The fire wakes. The fire chooses.*

And outside on the lane, the riders were knocking at doors.
