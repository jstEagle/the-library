## 8. Corvane's Offer

The knock came at the wrong hour, and it came politely, which was worse.

Orsen knew every species of knocking the way a fisherman knows weather. There was the drunk's knock, arrhythmic and apologetic. The debt-collector's knock, three hard raps with a pause for effect. The Guild's official knock—two measured strikes, the sound of a man who had never once needed to break down a door because no one had ever made him. This was that one. Two strikes. A pause long enough to be courteous. Then nothing, because Master Corvane did not repeat himself.

She'd left Wren at Mother Quill's an hour after the riders started working the lane, smuggled out through Quill's root cellar and along the drainage cut in the dark, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of tallow and old wool. The girl hadn't cried. She'd held her coal against her chest like a second heart and looked at Orsen with those eyes that were too old for the face they lived in, and she had pressed one small hand flat against Orsen's sternum—a gesture Orsen didn't understand yet but already feared—and gone.

Now Corvane stood on her step in the falling snow, hatless, gloved, his gray coat brushed clean of weather as though snow simply declined to land on him. Behind him, at the mouth of the alley, two figures waited beside a black carriage. Not enforcers. Enforcers were loud. These were the quiet kind, the kind the Guild kept the way other institutions kept ledgers: closed, balanced, and final.

"Master Pemberton," Corvane said. "Or may I say Orsen? You've earned familiarity, I think. Eleven years of excellent work."

"You could say it," Orsen said. "I wouldn't answer to it."

A flicker of something at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile; Corvane's face spent smiles the way misers spent gold. "May I come in? The snow is making a liar of my coat."

"It's doing fine," Orsen said. But she stepped back, because you always let them in, because refusing was a message and messages got people visited at worse hours by worse men. She'd learned that lesson before she'd learned anything else about Hollowmere. It might have been the first thing she'd ever learned here. The thought snagged on something, the way a sleeve snags on a nail—the thought of *first things*—and then slid free, leaving only the small raw place it always left.

Corvane entered her home the way he entered everything: as if taking inventory. His eyes went over the single room—the cold hearth she hadn't lit since Wren arrived, the shelf of chipped crockery, the loose floorboard by the bed where she kept nothing anymore because she'd moved everything the moment the riders hit the lane. He noted the empty board. Of course he noted it. He was the man who taught her that everyone notes everything; he'd just never taught her that he'd be noting *her*.

"No fire," he observed. "In this cold."

"I like the quiet it makes."

"The quiet," Corvane said, removing his gloves finger by finger, "is what I've come to discuss."

He sat without being asked, in her one good chair, and folded his hands, and for a moment Orsen saw him as the young clerk she'd met eleven years ago outside the Guildhall in Vaskarren—young then too, somehow, though he'd been old even then, the way certain men are born old, their youth spent elsewhere or never issued. He had found her in the memory-market, a half-starved valley girl selling a soldier's recollection of his mother's funeral for bread money, and he had not haggled. He had paid triple and asked her name, and when she told him she couldn't remember having one before Hollowmere, he had looked at her with something that wasn't pity—it was closer to appetite—and said:

*"Then we can build you a new one. On our books."*

Eleven years. He had built her exactly that: a trade, a reputation, a room with a loose floorboard, a life with edges neat enough to live inside. And now here he sat in the middle of it, gloveless, patient, and she understood with the cold certainty of a lock feeling its tumblers set that this was not a visit. This was a collection.

"You know why I'm here," he said.

"There's a child," Orsen said. "There are always children. Half this valley is children nobody remembers the names of."

"This child speaks the Old Tongue." He said it mildly, the way a man says *it's raining*. "This child carries a coal that has not cooled in—well, in longer than our records run, and our records run deep. This child was seen entering your tenement four nights ago, barefoot, in snow, and was seen leaving Mother Quill's house tonight through a cellar that officially does not exist. And this child, Orsen, is currently the single most valuable object between the salt flats and the sea."

"Object," Orsen repeated.

"A slip of the tongue." He almost smiled. "A vessel. If you prefer."

"I prefer *child*."

"And I prefer not to have this conversation twice." He leaned forward, and the lamplight found the lines of his face, all of them carved downward, all of them running toward his mouth like water finding a drain. "So let me be direct, because you were always my best student and my best students deserve directness. The Guild knows the girl is here. The Guild will have the girl. The only variable still in motion is you—or rather, what happens to you afterward. I would prefer the version where it goes well."

Orsen stood very still. In her chest, under the ribs, in the place where Wren had pressed her small hand, something sat heavy and warm and refused to be named.

"What version goes badly?" she asked.

"The version where you're obstructive, and we take the child anyway, and you become a problem, and problems get solved in vaults." He said it without heat, without threat, the way a carpenter describes joinery. "But I don't want that. I've invested eleven years in you. I'd like the investment to mature."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning—" and here he reached into his coat, slowly, showing her his hands the whole time, a courtesy he extended to no one else, "—meaning I brought you something to look at."

He set it on her table between the lamp and the chipped crockery: a jar.

Not a big one. Palm-sized, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, and inside it a light that wasn't light—that was the only way to describe it, the way memory looks when the Guild bottles it properly, a soft gray-gold shimmer that moves when you look away from it and holds still when you look directly, like something shy. Orsen had handled hundreds of these. She had filled hundreds of these. She knew their weight in her hand, their faint warmth, the way they whispered at the edge of hearing if you held them too close to your ear, which every thief eventually does and every thief regrets.

This one was different. This one, when it touched the table, made her teeth ache.

"Do you know what this is?" Corvane asked.

"It's a jar."

"It's *your* jar."

The words went into her like a splinter going into a fingertip—small entry, deep arrival. Orsen looked at the jar and the jar seemed to look back, and behind its shimmer she saw—or thought she saw, or remembered seeing, which in Hollowmere amounts to the same thing—a kitchen. A low ceiling. A woman's hands kneading dough, flour to the wrists, and a voice—

The ache opened under her sternum like a door opening onto a stairwell that goes down farther than any house is tall. She knew that kitchen. She did not know how she knew that kitchen. She had no business knowing that kitchen. There was no *before*, there was no road into Hollowmere in her past, there was no mother with flour on her wrists—there was a blank, the same blank there had always been, smooth as river stone, and she had built her whole self on top of it the way a house is built on rock, never asking why the rock was there or what it covered.

And now the rock had a label.

"That's impossible," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, which cost her more than screaming would have. "I never sold a childhood. I don't *have* one to sell. That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement."

"Yes," Corvane said gently. "That's what you believe. It's what everyone in the valley believes who arrives the way you arrived—with the border having done its work. Magic dies at the line, Orsen. Memory is magic's softest tissue. Whatever you carried when you crossed, the valley ate it. Everyone knows that."

Everyone knew that. She had staked her living on it. She had explained it to frightened travelers a hundred times, kindly, professionally: *the valley takes what you won't miss until it's gone.*

"But," Corvane continued, "the valley doesn't eat what's already been extracted. Extraction isn't magic, Orsen—it's craft. Bottled memory is an object. Objects cross borders. Objects sit in vaults. And objects"—he tapped the jar once, lightly, with one gloved fingertip, and the light inside flinched—"can be returned."

The kitchen again. The flour. The voice, singing something with a rising third, some tune that lived below language, and a small girl's laughter underneath it, and the specific golden quality of late afternoon light through a window that—

Orsen gripped the back of her chair. Her knuckles had gone white. She was aware of Corvane watching her do it, cataloguing it, filing it.

"You're lying," she said. "You keep it locked up to bait thieves. Standard practice. You taught me that too."

"I have never once lied to you," Corvane said. "It's inefficient. Lies require maintenance. Here is the truth instead, and you may check it against whatever you trust in yourself: you sold this. Eleven years ago, before I ever found you in the market. You walked into a Guild annex in a town whose name is also in this jar, and you sold your entire childhood—all of it, every year, voluntarily, at your own request—for a sum I can show you the ledger entry for. You signed. Your signature is in this jar's provenance. And then you crossed into Hollowmere, where the transaction conveniently vanished from your knowledge along with everything it contained, and you began a new life as a woman with no past, and within the year you were working for me, selling other people's memories, and neither of us ever spoke of it again."

The room tilted. Orsen sat down, because standing had stopped being a thing her body was willing to do unassisted.

"Why," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "Why would anyone—"

"That question is also in the jar," Corvane said. "Along with the answer. Along with your mother's name, your father's trade, the town, the year, the reason, the face you wore before this one finished forming. It's all there, Orsen. Every piece of you that you've spent eleven years pretending was never missing. I've read the ledger. I know what you paid. I'm the only person alive who knows what you paid, because you made sure of that, and I confess it's the thing I've admired most about you."

Outside, the snow ticked against the window. Somewhere across the tenements, a door slammed, and a dog began to complain about it. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of the life she'd built on the rock, on the smooth blank rock, and for eleven years she had called the blankness freedom. *No past, no chains.* She'd said it to younger thieves. She'd believed it the way you believe a wall is load-bearing right up until you hang something heavy on it.

*Why would anyone sell their whole childhood?*

Because it was worth something. Because someone desperate enough sells whatever's worth something. Because—

Because the alternative was that she had looked at everything she was, everything that had made her, and found it worthless. Found it cheaper than whatever the money bought. And some cold practical corner of her mind, the corner that priced soldiers' daughters' faces by weight, understood immediately that this was possible, that she was exactly the kind of person who would do it, that the woman sitting in this chair gripping a chair-back was built entirely from that original transaction and its consequences—

"Stop," she said aloud, and Corvane, to his credit, stopped.

They sat in silence. The jar shimmered between them on the table, patient as a banked fire.

"You could open it," Corvane said quietly. "Tonight. I'd leave it with you. Wax seal intact, provenance yours, no obligation. Consider it a gift, if gifts help. Open it, remember yourself, and tomorrow we speak again—woman to woman, employer to employee, and you'll give me the child, and the Guild will make you rich beyond the arithmetic you currently think in. You'll never lift another soldier's daughter from his head as long as you live. You'll have a house in Vaskarren with real windows. You'll have your *name*, Orsen. Your true one. Do you even know how much a true name is worth in a city full of people trading in false ones?"

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you keep the hole," Corvane said. "You keep it for the rest of your life, and you go on filling it with other people's leftovers, and someday you'll be old, and you'll die in this room, and the last thought in your head will be the same one that's in it right now, which is: *I almost took it back and I didn't, because of a child I've known four days.*"

The cruelty of it was its accuracy. That was Corvane's gift; it had always been his gift. He never threatened what you could dismiss. He aimed only at the load-bearing walls.

Orsen looked at the jar. The kitchen swam behind the shimmer—flour, light, the rising third of that song—and beneath the ache, beneath the want so large it had its own gravity, she felt the other thing, the unnamed warm heavy thing under her ribs where a small hand had pressed. Four days. A mute girl with a coal that never cooled, who had walked out of the snow and handed her, of all people, of all the doors in all the tenements in Hollowmere, *her* door. Who had looked at her with eyes too old for her face and chosen her. Chosen the thief. Chosen the woman with no past to protect and no future anyone wanted.

*The fire chooses,* something whispered, in a voice that was not hers and not Wren's and not anyone's, and she did not know yet that Mother Quill's father had transcribed those same words eighty years ago, but her bones did.

"If I give you the child," Orsen said slowly, "what happens to her?"

"She'll be cared for."

"What *happens to her*, Corvane."

He paused. It was a real pause; she'd known him eleven years and could count his real pauses on one hand. "That," he said, "is above my station. Which should tell you something about how much the Guild wants her."

Above his station. Corvane, who ran the Hollowmere office, who answered to no one she'd ever seen. Above *his* station. The vaults being warded anew. The sketches of a child on clerks' desks. The wards weren't to keep things *in*, she realized now, with the slow cold turn of a lock finally giving up its secret. They were to keep something from getting *out*.

"And the memories in your vaults," she said. "The ones I've sold you. Eleven years of them. What are they *for*?"

"They're inventory," Corvane said, and rose, and put his gloves back on, finger by finger, unhurried, a man closing a meeting on his terms. "Everything is inventory, Orsen. Even you. Especially you—I mean that as a compliment. You are the finest instrument the Guild has ever operated in this valley, and instruments that know what they are perform better than instruments that don't. So. Take the night. Take the jar. Remember yourself. Tomorrow at dusk, the carriage will be at the corner of Tanner's Row, and either you'll be in it, or you won't, and we'll both know what the valley's finest instrument decided she was."

He collected his hat from the table. At the door he stopped, half-turned, snow-light behind him.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I hope you open it. I've carried that jar for eleven years waiting for the right hour to set it down. It's heavy, Orsen. Not the glass. The *knowing*. I wouldn't wish it on anyone, and I've wished it on nearly everyone."

Then he was gone, and the two quiet figures folded around the carriage like water closing over a stone, and the lane was empty, and the snow went on falling with its terrible gentleness, erasing the wheel ruts, erasing the footprints, erasing everything, the way it always did, the way the whole valley did, patiently, forever, for everyone.

Orsen stood a long time in the cold room.

Then she picked up the jar.

It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything the Guild bottled ran warm; she'd told herself that for years, professional knowledge, trade trivia. But this warmth had a rhythm to it, faint, steady, and her palm knew the rhythm before her mind caught up, and her mind caught up and refused it, and her palm insisted anyway: it beat like a heart. Like a small heart. Like a heart beating under a small pressed hand, four nights ago, against her sternum.

She set it down. Picked it up. Set it down again.

*Open it and remember yourself. Give me the child and be rich and named and whole.*

Or she could walk out her own door, into the snow, away from the jar, toward Mother Quill's cellar and the drainage cut and a girl who spoke the language beneath kingdoms—and spend the rest of her life wondering what song that was, the one with the rising third, the one her mother sang in a kitchen she had sold for a price recorded in a ledger in a city she'd fled at whatever age she'd been when she'd chosen the hole over the hurt.

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off toward the Guild quarter, a bell rang the hour—wrong hour, some clerk's error, or maybe no error at all, maybe bells in this town had started keeping Guild time—and Orsen stood in the middle of her life with her past cupped in both hands, weighing it against a future that weighed nothing at all except a promise she'd made to a child who couldn't speak it.

Her thumb found the wax seal. The blood-colored wax. Warm as skin.

One push. That was all it would take. One push, and she'd know why. One push, and she'd have a mother's voice, a town's name, a reason, a self—

And somewhere across the sleeping tenements, small and clear through the frozen air, a sound reached her that no bell had ever made her feel: a child's laugh, thin with distance, bright as a struck spark, coming from the direction of Mother Quill's.

Wren was awake. Wren was laughing. Wren, who had given her the coal and the choice and the terrible weight of being trusted, was laughing at something an old woman had said in a kitchen that smelled, Orsen realized with a pain like the ache of a tooth finally admitting it was dead, exactly like flour and tallow and evening.

Exactly like the kitchen in the jar.

Orsen set the jar down on the table, in the lamplight, unopened.

She put on her coat. She blew out the lamp. And she went out into the snow toward the laugh, leaving her whole stolen childhood sitting on the table behind her, glowing softly in the dark like a coal that never cooled—while three streets away, at the corner of Tanner's Row, a black carriage settled into the shadows to wait for dusk, and the two quiet figures beside it watched her go, and one of them unfolded a paper sketch of a small barefoot girl, and held it up against the falling snow, comparing faces.
