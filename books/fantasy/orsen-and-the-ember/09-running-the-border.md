## 9. Running the Border

We left Hollowmere three hours before dawn, in the hour Mother Quill called the blind watch—too late for the night's honest work, too early for the day's dishonest kind.

Fenna had the route mapped on a scrap of oilcloth she'd burned rather than drawn, memorized, then eaten a corner of out of pure habit. Pim and Sable carried the packs between them, moving through the dark like a pair of hands that had been separated from their body and learned to manage anyway. Wren rode on my back, arms around my neck, the coal wrapped in wool and pressed against my spine where I could feel its warmth even through three layers of cloth.

It had never cooled. Not once. In six days it hadn't dimmed so much as a shade, and lately it had started to pulse—slow, patient, like something breathing in its sleep.

"Border's forty miles," Fenna said, falling into step beside me. "Salt flats after the ridge. Nothing out there but crust and old bones."

"And Guild riders," I said.

"And Guild riders." She didn't dress it up. Fenna never did. That was why I trusted her with my life and not with my feelings.

Aldric brought up the rear. He'd refused to carry anything except his sword, which he'd spent two hours sharpening by candlelight while pretending he wasn't looking at Wren. Exiled knights don't get to be knights again, but some of them keep the posture anyway—the straight spine, the way they check sightlines without meaning to. He'd been checking them all night, from Orsen's door to the tenement stair to the drainage tunnel under the tannery row, and every time his hand drifted toward his sword hilt I felt a little more certain we were going to need it.

The tunnel let us out past the last of the lamplighters' rounds, into fields gone silver with frost. Hollowmere fell away behind us in stages: first the sound—a dog, a drunk singing badly, the bell of the night-soil cart—then the smell, woodsmoke and tallow and the river's iron tang, and finally the shape of it, rooftops black against a sky just beginning to think about lightening.

I looked back exactly once.

I'd lived in that valley my whole remembered life. It was the only place in the world where magic couldn't follow you, couldn't touch you, couldn't reach into your chest and rearrange what you were. Every person in Hollowmere was there because something outside wanted them dead or broken or found. The valley was a wound in the world, and we were all living in it because wounds are hard to chase things into.

And I was leaving it. Carrying the one thing in creation that everything outside was hunting.

"You'll want to stop doing that," Aldric said quietly.

"Doing what?"

"Looking back. It slows the feet." A pause. "Old cavalry saying."

"Were you ever cavalry?"

"I was many things." His jaw worked. "None of them well."

Wren stirred against my back. She'd barely slept since the enforcers came to the tenements, and when she did sleep she dreamed loudly—not sound, exactly, but pressure, a hum in the teeth, the taste of smoke at the back of the throat. Mother Quill said the girl's language was surfacing in her sleep now, whole sentences of it, words older than any kingdom. I'd taken to sleeping with one ear open and had heard it myself once: a low, layered speech, like water over stones, like a fire deciding what shape to take.

She spoke it less in waking hours. But her eyes had changed. There was a depth to them now, an age, and sometimes when she looked at me I had the distinct, unsettling sense that whatever lived inside her was looking too.

* * *

The ridge came at midmorning—a spine of grey rock separating the valley's soft green lowlands from the flats beyond. We climbed it single file, breath ragged, Wren transferred to Aldric's shoulders for the steep part because she'd gone limp with exhaustion somewhere around mile twelve and my knees had filed a formal complaint.

From the top, the salt flats spread out white and blinding to the horizon, cracked into vast polygonal plates like the floor of a dried god's bathhouse. The border itself wasn't marked. It didn't need to be. You could feel it before you saw it—a stillness, a flatness, like the moment before a storm decides. Magic died at that line. Had always died there. Whatever ancient cataclysm had carved Hollowmere out of the world had left this scar behind, and the scar kept its own rules.

Beyond it, though—

Beyond it, the world woke up.

I'd never crossed. None of us had, not in years. And standing on the ridge with the wind pushing at my back, I felt it: a hum rising off the far side of the flats, faint as a struck tuning fork held miles away. Power. Real power, ambient and enormous, the kind of thing I'd only ever known secondhand, in stolen memories of mages and temples and burning towers.

"That's what it's like out there?" Sable signed. Pim translated aloud out of habit, his voice rough with disuse.

"That's what it's like," Aldric said. "You get used to it. Mostly."

"The Ember doesn't get used to it," I said.

Because Wren had lifted her head. She was staring out over the flats, and the coal against my spine—it was in Aldric's pack now—had begun to warm through the wool, through the leather, warm enough that I could have sworn I felt it against my shoulder blades from six feet away. Her eyes had gone bright. Not reflecting the morning sun. Bright the way coals are bright, from underneath.

"It knows it's going home," Fenna said, and spat, which was her version of a prayer.

* * *

We made the flats by noon, and that was when the first rider appeared.

He was maybe two miles out, a dark smudge against the white, moving parallel to us. Then a second smudge joined him. Then a third.

"Scouts," Aldric said. "They're not closing. They're herding."

"Herding us where?"

"North. Toward the pass at Greywater." He shaded his eyes. "Which is where the rest of them will be."

Fenna swore with the fluency of a woman who'd been fined for it in four jurisdictions. "How many?"

"If they know we're coming? Twenty. Thirty."

"We can't fight thirty."

"No," Aldric agreed. "But we can run from twenty-nine of them."

So we ran.

There's a particular kind of terror that lives in running across open ground with no cover and nowhere to hide, and I got to know it intimately over the next four hours. The salt crunched and shattered under our boots. The sun hammered down off the white and turned the world into a glare with no edges. The riders fanned out behind us, patient, letting the flats do their work—they knew we'd tire, that the child would slow us, that panic makes people stupid.

Wren rode my back again, lighter than she should have been, her cheek hot against my neck. Hotter than skin should be. The coal had stopped being a coal somewhere over the last mile. It was a brand now, and I could feel it seeping heat into the pack, into the air, and every few minutes Wren would shudder and murmur something in the old language, and each time she did, the hum from beyond the border grew a fraction louder.

"She's waking it up," I said to Aldric. "Crossing is making it stronger."

"Yes."

"How strong does it get?"

He didn't answer, which answered me fine.

Pim went down at the two-hour mark—turned an ankle in a crack in the salt, went sprawling, came up bleeding from both palms. Sable hauled him upright without breaking stride, and the two of them communicated something entire and complex in half a second of signing that ended with Sable taking his pack and him limping on, jaw set. Nobody suggested stopping. Stopping was dying with extra steps.

The riders closed to half a mile. Close enough now to see details: black cloaks, Guild grey beneath, and the long hooked poles they carried for netting quarry without damaging it. Damaged goods fetched lower prices. I knew that better than anyone. I'd been the one setting prices once, haggling memories down like fish at market, and now I was on the hook end of the pole, and the universe was enjoying the joke immensely.

"There," Fenna said, and pointed north.

The pass at Greywater was a seam in a wall of rust-red cliffs—a gap perhaps thirty yards wide where the mountains had simply given up. Through it, a thread of green: the pilgrim road, the world beyond the flats, everything we'd bled to reach.

Between us and it, riding slowly, deliberately, in a line across the mouth of the pass: eight riders. Lances. Nets. And behind them, on a rise, a figure in a clerk's coat holding a lantern that burned the wrong color—Guild ward-work, a snare-light, the kind that ate spells and screamed for reinforcements.

"They were waiting," I said. "They always knew the pass was the exit."

"Everyone knows it's the exit. That's why it's guarded." Aldric handed Wren to Fenna and rolled his shoulders the way men do when they're about to do something irreversible. "So here's how this goes."

"Aldric—"

"You take the girl south along the cliff base. There's a scramble, a goat track, maybe two miles of ugly climbing, comes out above the pass on the far side." He was unbuckling his sword scabbard strap, tightening it, checking the blade with his thumb. "I go through the pass loud enough that everyone watches me instead of the rocks."

"Eight riders," I said. "Eight."

"I've fought worse odds with worse men." He almost smiled. "I've *been* worse odds with worse men."

"This isn't repayment, you idiot, I stole that memory, I *kept* it, you don't owe—"

"Orsen." He said my name the way you set something heavy down carefully. "Six years ago you took the worst night of my life out of my head and you didn't sell it. Do you understand what that meant? Everyone sells. That's the whole economy of this valley. You looked at a knight's disgrace—good coin, easy sale—and you put it in a drawer and locked it, because you decided I shouldn't have to carry it anymore." He looked out at the riders, at the pass, at the green thread beyond. "I've been carrying the debt ever since. Let me put it down."

Behind him, Wren slid off Fenna's hip and walked to him and stood there looking up. She didn't speak. She reached out and touched his gauntlet, briefly, and something passed between them—I saw it, a flicker of gold light under the skin of her small fingers, there and gone—and Aldric exhaled like a man who'd been carrying weight he'd forgotten to mention.

"What did she do?" I asked.

"Made sure," he said. He didn't elaborate. He drew his sword, and the sound of it coming free was very loud in all that white silence.

Then he walked toward the pass, alone, unhurried, a disgraced knight in a patched coat going to hold a door against eight lances because a thief had once declined to profit from his worst day.

I watched him for exactly as long as I could afford—which was not long—and then I turned south and ran.

* * *

The cliff base was exactly as ugly as promised. We climbed with our fingers in the stone and the wind trying to peel us off, Wren strapped to my chest now, Fenna leading, Pim and Sable finding holds none of the rest of us could see and passing them back by touch. Twice the riders below spotted movement and wheeled toward us, and twice the sounds from the pass pulled them back—steel ringing on steel, a war cry I recognized from a stolen memory of some border campaign, Aldric's voice, furious and alive.

Once, a scream that wasn't his. Once, a scream that might have been.

I climbed faster.

The goat track topped out on a shelf above the pass, and from there I looked down and saw it: Aldric in the mouth of the gap, backed against the cliff wall, sword working, two riderless horses bolting across the salt, five riders pressing him in a rotating attack that any competent commander would've called excessive for one man. The ward-lantern on the rise flared and guttered—something in the pass interfering with it, something gold flickering at the edges of my vision where no light should be.

"He can't hold that," Fenna breathed beside me.

"He can hold it long enough." I tore my eyes away. "Move."

We came down the far side of the spur into the green, and the world *changed*. I felt it cross the threshold with me—the hum swelling from a whisper to a chord, sinking into my teeth, my blood, my bones. Grass actually smelled like grass here, impossibly vivid, every scent sharp as a blade. My stolen memories stirred like leaves in wind, all of them at once, a thousand borrowed lives rustling. Magic was everywhere out here. It had been waiting for me my whole life. And something else was waking up in the bundle on my chest, something vast turning over in its sleep, and the coal was glowing through the wool now, visible, unmistakable, a tiny sunrise strapped to a thief's body in broad daylight.

Wren opened her eyes. They weren't a child's eyes anymore.

*"Vesh-kara,"* she said. Her voice came out doubled, hers and something underneath it, harmonics where there should be one note. *"Vesh-kara, i-ometh."*

I didn't know the words. But some stolen fragment deep in my skull shivered in recognition, and a translation surfaced the way a body surfaces in drowned places:

*Ember-bearer. Come home.*

Down in the pass, the fighting had stopped. Five riders stood motionless, staring up the cliff face—at us, at the glow, at the thing we were carrying into their world. On the rise, the clerk with the lantern had dropped it. The snare-light lay guttering in the dirt, forgotten.

Every one of them was looking at the light.

"Orsen," Fenna whispered. "What do we do?"

I looked at the road ahead—open country, pilgrim traffic, the distant smudge of a shrine town where we could vanish among travelers. I looked at the light leaking from my pack like a secret told too loud. I looked back, one last time, at the pass, where a battered figure leaned on his sword in a field of fallen riders, watching us go, refusing to fall until he was sure.

Leaving meant becoming someone new. I'd understood that intellectually. Standing on the far side of the border with a god's heart warming my chest, I finally understood it in my ribs.

"We walk," I said. "Fast. And we pray the thing she's carrying likes us better than it likes them."

Wren laughed—or the Ember did, through her, a sound like a fire catching.

Somewhere behind us, a horn sounded. Not one horn. Many.

The pursuers outside the valley had stopped waiting.
