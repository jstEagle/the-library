## 10. Outside the Magicless Land

The border does not look like anything.

That is the first thing Wren understands about it. She has spent her whole remembered life inside Hollowmere, where the world is quiet in a way she never had words for—quiet like a room after a bell stops ringing—and so she expected the edge of the valley to be a wall, or a gate, or at least a line drawn in the salt. Something you could point to.

There is nothing. Only a place where the frost on the grass stops glittering and starts *humming*.

She feels it before she crosses it: a pressure behind her teeth, a warmth in her palms, the coal against her sternum shifting in its wrapping like a bird turning over in sleep. She stops at the last fence post, the one Aldric marked with a strip of red cloth, and Orsen stops beside her, and for a moment neither of them breathes.

*Go on,* Orsen signs, then remembers Wren cannot always read her hands in this light, and says it aloud too, though Wren's own tongue is still locked somewhere below her ribs. *It's all right. I'm right here.*

Wren steps over the invisible line.

The world *wakes up.*

It is not sound. It is not light. It is everything at once—the grass suddenly loud with growing, the sky suddenly deep as a well, the wind carrying flavors she has no names for: iron, honey, smoke, rain that fell a hundred years ago and is still falling somewhere. The magiclessness of Hollowmere had been a blanket over her whole body, and now it is pulled away, and underneath she is covered in eyes.

She staggers. Orsen catches her elbow.

And then—this is the part Wren will remember longest, if remembering is something she is allowed to do—Orsen makes a small sound. Not a word. A note. Halfway between a gasp and a hum, pitched low, like a struck bowl.

Wren knows that note. She has been trying to say it since the night she arrived barefoot in the snow, pressing her hands to her mouth because nothing else would come out. It is the first syllable of the old language, the one beneath kingdoms, the one Mother Quill heard echoes of in a burned book. It means *threshold*. It means *the place where you stop being what you were.*

Orsen heard it come out of her own mouth and went white.

*I'm sorry,* Wren thinks at her, uselessly, wordlessly. *I'm sorry. It follows me. It was always going to follow me.*

But Orsen doesn't let go of her arm. She tightens her grip instead, and says, in the flat steady voice Wren has learned means *fear being managed*: "Right. So that's going to happen now. Good to know."

They walk on, into the humming world.

* * *

By midday Wren has catalogued the way things feel outside the valley, the way she once catalogued the tenements of Hollowmere by their smells and their loose boards and which staircases sang under weight.

Water tastes like memory here. She cups a handful from a stream and it carries something—not words, but the shape of words, the ghost of everyone who ever drank downstream. She lets it run through her fingers fast, before it can settle into her.

Trees lean. Not visibly—they stand straight enough—but she can feel them leaning, the way a listener leans toward a story. Everything alive leans toward her, or toward the coal, and she cannot tell the difference anymore, and she is not sure there is one.

Aldric walks ahead with his hand never far from his sword, and his shadow is wrong. In Hollowmere shadows are just shadows, flat and obedient. Here his shadow drags a little, as if it remembers when he wore armor brighter than this rusted mail, and Wren looks away quickly because some things a person should get to tell you themselves.

Fenna complains constantly, which is how Wren knows Fenna is frightened. "The locks back home were honest," she mutters, kicking a stone off the pilgrim road. "You pick them, they open, they don't *sing about it.*" Because that is the other thing: tools sing out here. Fenna's picks hum faintly in their roll. Pim and Sable keep stopping to press their palms flat against fence posts and gate hinges, feeling the vibration, signing rapidly to each other in their private grammar. Once, Sable turns to Wren and signs something slow and deliberate, and though it isn't the old language, Wren understands it anyway, the way she understands everything more easily now:

*You're louder than you were.*

"I know," Wren says.

Her voice comes out cracked and small and real, and the whole company stops walking.

It is only two words. But they are the first two words she has spoken since the snow, since the door, since Orsen knelt down in the cold and did not run from her—and they land on the group like a dropped plate. Fenna's mouth opens. Pim grabs Sable's sleeve. Aldric turns around slowly, the way men turn when they think they've misheard something dangerous.

Orsen just looks at her. And Wren watches her understand, watch the arithmetic move behind her eyes: *the farther we go, the more of her comes back. The more of it comes back.* Two truths wearing one coat.

"Say something else," Orsen says quietly.

Wren wants to. She reaches for language the way a starving person reaches for bread, and finds that the words closest to the surface are not the soft trade-tongue of Hollowmere but the old ones, the burning ones, syllables that taste of cinder and have meanings like *oath* and *pyre* and *beloved-enemy*. She swallows them. She tries again.

"My name," she manages. "I want—I want to say my name."

"You don't have to," Orsen says. "Not yet. Not until you find one you like." And that is such an Orsen thing to say—practical and tender in the same breath, a thief's kindness, a kindness that knows the value of things—that Wren laughs, a rusty astonished sound, and the coal against her chest flares warm in answer, and far behind them, back along the road they've traveled, every bird in every tree goes silent at once.

They do not talk about that.

* * *

They make camp in the shell of a waystation, a pilgrim shelter with its roof half fallen and its hearthstone split down the middle by something very old. Aldric builds the fire; Fenna bars the broken door with a chair; the twins take first watch without being asked, sitting shoulder to shoulder in the doorway like a pair of carved guardians.

Wren sits apart, as she always does, unwrapping the coal to check it. This is her nightly ritual, her penance, her prayer: unwrap, look, rewrap. As long as she checks it, she tells herself, she is its keeper and not its vessel. The distinction feels thinner every day.

Tonight the coal is wrong.

It has always been dark at rest—a black lump, dense as a swallowed star, warm but not bright. Tonight it glows. Faintly, sullenly, the way coals glow an hour after a fire seems dead: veins of orange threading through the black, pulsing slow, in rhythm with—she presses two fingers to her own throat—with her pulse.

Every league, it wakes a little more. Mother Quill told her that before they left, holding her face in papery hands: *"It slept in the valley because the valley put everything to sleep. Outside, it will remember itself. And child—it will remember you."*

Wren wraps the coal faster than usual.

Behind her, Orsen settles cross-legged by the fire, and Wren watches her do the thing she does every night now: take out the little oilcloth packet of tokens, her mnemonic kit, and touch each one in turn. A button. A bent nail. A scrap of red thread. Orsen explained it once, embarrassed, in the tone of someone confessing a weakness: *I steal memories for a living, but nobody steals mine for me, so I keep my own by hand. If I hold these every night, tomorrow I'll know I held them yesterday, and the day before. A chain of proof. Otherwise the days blur.*

Wren had thought it was the saddest thing she'd ever heard, and also the bravest. A woman who cannot trust her own mind building a rope bridge across it out of buttons and thread.

Tonight, watching her, Wren feels something new stir in her chest—something that is not the coal. It is a wanting. A fierce, specific wanting: *let me be the one who holds your days. Let me be the chain. You kept everyone else's pasts safe; let me keep yours.*

She does not have the words for this yet, in any language. But the old language rises anyway, unbidden, and fills her mouth with a phrase that means, roughly, *I will carry what you cannot*, and before she can stop herself she says it aloud, softly, in the tongue beneath kingdoms.

Orsen goes still over her tokens.

"What was that?" she asks. Her voice is careful. "What did you just say?"

Wren hesitates. Then, because the fire is warm and the twins are watching the dark and Aldric is pretending not to listen while sharpening a blade he never uses: "It means... I will carry what you cannot."

Silence. The fire pops. Somewhere out in the humming dark, something vast and patient shifts its weight.

"That's a heavy promise," Orsen says at last, and there is something strange in her voice, something almost frightened, though Wren cannot imagine why a thief would fear being given things. "From a girl who's already carrying enough."

"It's not heavy," Wren says. "Yet."

Orsen looks at her for a long moment across the fire. Then she puts her tokens away, all except the red thread, which she winds once around her smallest finger—an old habit, Wren realizes, a knot tied against forgetting—and says, "Get some sleep, Wren. Tomorrow we hit the pilgrim road proper, and I'm told the ash country is ugly."

* * *

Sleep does not come kindly.

Wren dreams the way she always dreams now: in images that belong to someone else, or to something else. Burning libraries where the books scream in voices. A field of white banners advancing into a wall of fire and the fire *winning*. A tower full of jars, thousands of them, each one glowing faintly, each one whispering—and the whispers resolving, horribly, into faces she almost recognizes, faces from the market in Hollowmere, faces from the Guild hall—

She wakes with the coal blazing through its wrappings, throwing orange light on the waystation rafters, and every person in the room is awake and armed and staring at her.

"I'm sorry," she says. "I'm sorry, it was a dream, it—"

"We know," Aldric says. His voice is grim and even. He nods at the doorway, where Pim stands rigid, pointing out into the dark.

Sable joins him, and the two of them sign fast, and Fenna translates, her face drained: "Riders. South road. Four of them, maybe five. They stopped when the light flared." A pause. "They're asking the dark if anyone saw a child."

The words hang in the smoky air. Wren's hands go cold even as the coal burns hot. *Asking the dark*—as if the night itself might answer, as if out here beyond the valley, the world is full of ears, and they knew it, whoever they are, they knew exactly what kind of country they were hunting in.

Orsen moves first, the way she always moves first. She snuffs the fire with a sweep of a blanket, takes Wren's wrist—firm, certain, the grip of a woman who has smuggled a hundred things past a hundred watchers—and pulls her toward the back wall where the stones have crumbled into a gap.

"No more leagues of walking in daylight," she murmurs. "We travel at night now. We stay off the roads. And Wren—" She pauses, and in the darkness her eyes catch the coal's dying glow, and Wren sees that she is afraid, truly afraid, for the first time since the snow. "When it burns like that again, you wake me. Not Aldric. Not the twins. Me. Whatever it shows you, whatever it wants—you bring it to me first. Deal?"

Wren nods.

Because here is the thing she understood in the stream water, in the leaning trees, in the silent birds: out here, she is not a mute child anymore. Out here, every league strips another layer off the silence, and underneath the silence is the Ember, and underneath the Ember is a war that ate the world once and is dreaming of waking. Freedom and danger came through the border together, holding hands, and they are not going to separate.

But Orsen's hand is on her wrist, pulling her through the gap in the wall, into the enormous humming dark—and Wren finds, to her own surprise, that she is not only afraid.

She is *glad*. For the first time in her short, borrowed, burdened life, she is glad, loudly glad, in a voice that is finally, dangerously, her own.

Behind them, far down the south road, a rider's horn sounds once—a long, low note that Wren's blood recognizes the way it recognizes the old language.

It means *the hunt has begun.*
