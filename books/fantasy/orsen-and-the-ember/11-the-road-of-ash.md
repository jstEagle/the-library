## 11. The Road of Ash

The pilgrim road out of the salt flats was called the Road of Ash, and it earned its name honestly.

Orsen had heard about it in stolen memories—fragments passed from merchant to merchant, secondhand geography bought and sold like any other commodity. Pilgrims walked it to prove their devotion. Kings had walked it barefoot. Somewhere along its length, a thousand years ago, an army had burned so completely that the ground itself remembered, and nothing taller than grass had grown there since.

The memories hadn't lied. The land around them was a wound that never closed.

Black soil stretched to the horizon on either side of the road, broken only by the ribs of things Orsen couldn't name—arches of fused stone, half-buried walls with windows melted into glass, the occasional skeletal frame of something enormous that might have been a war engine or might have been a creature. The sky above it all was the wrong color. Not gray, exactly. More like the world had been seen through smoke for so long that even the clouds had given up trying to be white.

Wren rode on Aldric's shoulders and touched none of it.

That was the strange part. Orsen kept waiting for the girl to react—to point, or hide her face, or do whatever children did when confronted with the bones of a burnt world. But Wren just looked out at the devastation with an expression Orsen could only describe as *recognition*, her small hands resting in Aldric's hair, her eyes tracking the ruined arches as if she were counting them.

"She's done this before," Aldric said quietly. It wasn't a question. He'd been watching Wren too.

"Done what? Walking through a graveyard?"

"Walking home." He shifted the girl's weight. "Look at how she sits. She's not afraid of any of it."

Orsen looked. He was right. Wren sat the way travelers sat when they knew the road—loose, patient, unbothered by distance. A child who had crossed half a kingdom alone, barefoot in snow, arriving at the door of a thief in a valley where magic went to die. Of course she'd traveled before. Of course she knew these roads.

The coal in the pouch at Wren's chest pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat remembering itself.

"Don't," Orsen said, before she realized she was speaking aloud.

Aldric glanced over. "Don't what?"

"Talk about it like it's normal." She pulled her cloak tighter. The wind off the flats carried a smell she hadn't been able to escape since the border—ozone and char, the scent of something vast sleeping badly. "Nothing about this is normal. I stole memories from soldiers for six years and thought I understood what war does to people. This—" she gestured at the horizon, at all of it, "—this isn't war. This is what comes *after* war, when nobody wins."

They walked in silence after that. The road climbed gently through the ash country, and behind them Hollowmere sank below the horizon like a stone dropped into dark water—the last place in the world where magic couldn't follow them, shrinking away one step at a time.

Orsen tried not to look back. She failed every hour or so.

* * *

They found the first refugees on the third day.

Orsen smelled the camp before she saw it—woodsmoke and unwashed bodies and something else underneath, something sour and frightened that she recognized from tenements during bad winters. Aldric heard them before that: the particular rhythm of many people trying to be quiet at once, which is louder than noise.

"They'll have seen us already," he said, hand drifting toward the sword he no longer had a right to wear. "Best to walk in open. Looking like pilgrims."

"We don't look like pilgrims."

"We look like people with nothing. That's close enough."

There were perhaps forty of them, camped in the lee of a collapsed watchtower, families packed together against the cold with blankets strung between broken columns. As Orsen and Aldric approached, faces turned toward them—hollow-cheeked, wary, the specific wariness of people who had learned that strangers were weather, and weather killed.

An old woman detached herself from the group and came forward, leaning on a staff made from a broken spear shaft. Her eyes went over Orsen's stolen coat, Aldric's scarred face, and finally settled on Wren—and stayed there a moment too long.

"Pilgrims?" the woman asked.

"Travelers," Orsen said. "Heading east."

"There's nothing east but more ash and then Vaskarren."

"That's where we're going."

The old woman's mouth twisted. "Then you're fools, and God keep you." She stepped aside. "There's room by the fire. We've got barley tea and not much else. You can share it if you share news."

"What news?"

"News of the roads." Her voice dropped. "We came up from the south, from the river country. There were riders."

Orsen felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped into still water. "Riders."

"In grey and black. Guild colors." The old woman spat, carefully, away from the fire. "They stopped everyone on the southern road. Searched carts. Made folk open their bundles." Her gaze flicked to Wren again, and this time she didn't pretend she wasn't looking. "Asking after a child. A girl, small, dark-haired, traveling without kin. They had her likeness drawn on paper. Showed it to everyone."

The fire crackled. Somewhere in the camp a baby started crying and was hushed.

"How long ago?" Aldric asked.

"Ten days. Maybe twelve." The old woman shrugged. "They paid for information, too. Silver for word of her. Real silver, not Guild scrip." Something moved across her face—shame, maybe, or its cousin. "Some folk took it. Can't blame them. Hunger makes liars of everyone."

Orsen's mind was already moving, laying out the map she'd memorized from a dead courier's memory two years ago, marking distances, marking the choke points where the road narrowed between ridges. Ten days' lead. Maybe less now. Riders moved faster than families with children.

"We should go," she said to Aldric, low. "Tonight. Now."

"The girl needs sleep. So do you."

"The girl needs to not be in a cage."

But they didn't leave that night, because Wren had already fallen asleep against Aldric's shoulder, and because the old woman pressed barley tea into Orsen's hands and said, "Sit. Drink. Even running people rest sometimes." And because Orsen was tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking.

She sat by the fire and drank terrible tea and watched Wren sleep and thought about silver changing hands on southern roads, and about how every mile she'd spent building a life inside Hollowmere had been a mile someone else would now pay for.

* * *

It was near midnight when Orsen gave up on sleep entirely and walked the camp's edge.

The ash country at night was worse than by day. Without sun to soften it, the ruins became teeth. The wind moved through the broken arches and made sounds that were almost words, almost voices, and Orsen found herself listening despite everything—because listening was what she did, because somewhere under six years of theft there was still a girl who'd learned early that the world told you what it planned to do next, if you paid attention.

She heard the horses an hour before anyone else did.

Not many. Four, maybe five, moving slowly, no attempt at stealth—or rather, a lazy kind of stealth, the kind men used when they believed no one worth hiding from was within fifty miles. Coming up the road from the south. From the direction of the river country. From the direction the refugees had fled.

Orsen stood very still in the dark and did the arithmetic.

Ten days since the riders passed the refugee column. The column moved slow. The riders moved fast. If they'd doubled back, swept the side roads—

They were here.

She ran.

Through the camp, past banked fires, into the ring of broken columns where Aldric slept sitting up with Wren curled against his chest. She put her hand over his mouth before his eyes finished opening, and pointed south, and mouthed the words she didn't have breath to say:

*Riders. Coming. Now.*

He understood instantly. No questions—that was the thing about Aldric, he saved his questions for later, hoarded them like coins. He gathered Wren up and was on his feet, and Orsen was already moving through the camp waking Fenna, waking Pim and Sable, pressing fingers to lips, pointing south.

Fenna came awake the way she did everything—fast and silent and furious about being interrupted. She read Orsen's face in a heartbeat and started gathering her tools without a word.

They had perhaps four minutes.

The plan was simple because plans made in terror are always simple: out the north side of the camp, into the broken ground where horses couldn't follow, and east along a dry streambed Aldric had scouted that afternoon. No fires, no noise, no trace. By dawn they'd be ten miles gone and the riders would find cold ashes and forty refugees who had seen nothing.

It almost worked.

It worked right up until the baby cried.

Not Wren—one of the refugee children, startled awake by the movement in the dark, and its wail cut through the night like a blade, and Orsen heard the horses break from a walk into a canter, and knew.

"They heard it," she breathed. "Move. *Move.*"

They ran. The camp erupted behind them—not screams yet, just confusion, people sitting up in the dark, and then torchlight blooming, and then the hoofbeats were inside the camp and voices were shouting in Guild cadence, clipped and official, demanding names, demanding the child, and Orsen ran with Wren bouncing against her back because Aldric had turned around.

He'd turned around because Fenna wasn't with them.

Because Fenna had stopped.

Later—much later, in the weeks afterward, when Orsen replayed that night the way you probe a wound—she would understand what Fenna had done. Fenna had been last in line. Fenna had seen the rider cutting through the camp toward the fleeing shapes, had seen the direction of the pursuit, had seen that the smallest member of their company was the one the torchlight was sweeping toward. And Fenna had stepped sideways, out of the shadows, into the open, and shouted.

Orsen didn't hear the shout. She heard only the aftermath: a crash, a curse, the sound of a body hitting frozen ground, and Aldric's hand clamping onto her arm and hauling her behind a fallen wall as three riders thundered past close enough to smell the horses' sweat.

"Fenna," Orsen whispered. "Where's Fenna?"

Aldric didn't answer. In the torchlight spilling over the broken wall, his face was stone.

They lay in the frozen mud among the ruins while the raid crashed through the camp around them—refugees dragged from bedrolls, bundles slashed open, the old woman with the spear-shaft staff shouting curses until something struck her and she didn't shout anymore. Boots ran past. Lantern light swept the broken ground. Once, so close Orsen could have reached out and touched him, a rider paused ten feet away, scanning the darkness, and Wren—God, Wren—pressed her face into Orsen's neck and held perfectly, perfectly still, and the coal in her pouch gave no light at all, as if it too knew how to hide.

Then the rider moved on.

The search lasted two hours. Orsen counted every minute by the sound of her own heart. When it was over, when the riders regrouped on the road with their lanterns and their prisoners—because they had taken prisoners, three of the refugee men, though not the child they wanted—they rode south again, and the camp behind them wept in the dark.

Fenna was gone.

Pim and Sable found her tool roll twenty yards from where they'd last seen her, dropped in the dirt, lockpicks scattered like spilled teeth. Sable picked one up and held it in both hands and made a sound Orsen had never heard from the twins before, a small broken noise, and Pim put an arm around his brother and stared south with an expression that promised murder in fluent silence.

"She stepped out," Aldric said quietly. "So they wouldn't see you run."

"I know." Orsen's voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "I know she did."

She looked down at Wren, asleep against her chest now from pure exhaustion, small fists clenched in her collar. The coal rose and fell with the girl's breathing. Safe. Still safe. Bought with a lockwright's freedom and three strangers' bruises and a night of terror for forty people who owed Orsen nothing.

*This is what it costs,* said a voice in her head that sounded horribly like Corvane. *This is the exchange rate. One child, weighed against everyone who touches her.*

Orsen knelt in the ash and picked up one of Fenna's scattered picks—a slim bent thing worn smooth by twenty years of honest thieving—and closed her fist around it until the edges bit.

The guilt didn't crush her. That was the terrible discovery of that night. Guilt, it turned out, was combustible. It burned.

"Vaskarren," she said, standing, and her voice didn't shake at all. "They'll take her to Vaskarren. To the vaults. So that's where we're going."

Aldric looked at her—at the pick in her fist, at the ash dusting her hair, at whatever had changed in her face. "You understand what Vaskarren is. The Ledger Hall. The whole Guild, in one city, with wards on every door."

"Yes."

"And you're going to walk in anyway."

Orsen tucked the pick into her belt beside her own tools, where it fit like it had always belonged there.

"No," she said. "We're going to burn our way in."

Behind her, unnoticed by any of them, the coal in Wren's pouch glowed—briefly, fiercely, like something answering a vow—and went dark again.
