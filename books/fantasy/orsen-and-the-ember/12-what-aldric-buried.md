## 12. What Aldric Buried

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time Aldric finally spoke.

They'd made camp in the lee of a fallen waymarker on the Road of Ash, a pilgrim shrine toppled so long ago that lichen had eaten the saint's face. Wren slept curled against Orsen's side, her coal tucked in its wrappings against her chest, breathing slow. Fenna was two days gone—taken in a raid meant for a child—and every hour they sat still felt like an hour spent robbing her. But the horses needed rest, and grief needed it more, and Orsen had ordered the halt with a flatness in her voice that told Aldric she was holding herself together with wire and habit.

He sat across the fire from her, his sword across his knees out of thirty years of habit rather than any fear of this road. The dead land around them gave off that particular silence he'd never gotten used to—a silence with weight to it, like the whole country was listening.

"I have to tell you something," he said. "Before Vaskarren. Before you decide whether I walk into that city beside you or behind you."

Orsen looked up from whittling a stick into nothing. She didn't say anything. She never did, when someone was working up to a confession. She just waited, patient as a lockpick, and let you hang yourself.

Aldric almost smiled at that. Almost.

"I wasn't always disgraced," he said. "I mean—I was disgraced properly, at least. That part's true. Court-martialed, stripped, sent to Hollowmere to rot among thieves and amnesiacs. All true." He turned the sword over. The firelight ran along the blade like water. "But the disgrace itself—it wasn't mine. It was assigned to me."

"Assigned," Orsen repeated.

"Engineered. Crafted. Call it what you like." He took a breath, and it hurt going down, the way truth always did after you'd kept it too long. "I served the Guild of Kept Things for six years before Hollowmere. Not as a thief. As a knight. Their outer circle—the Wardens of the Ledger, we called ourselves. We guarded the vaults. We escorted memory-carts between cities. We did the Guild's quiet killing when quiet killing was required." He paused. "You'd be surprised how much of the realm runs on a handful of men willing not to ask questions."

"And then you asked one."

"And then I asked one." He laughed, but there was no humor in it. "One question. That's all it took. Six years of service, and one question ended me."

* * *

It had been winter, that year. He remembered the cold of the vault stairwell better than he remembered his mother's face now—that iron chill that came up through boot leather and sat in your knees. He'd been escorting a senior clerk down into the deep vaults beneath the Ledger Hall, some routine inspection, some paperwork errand. His job was to walk three steps behind and look intimidating. It was the only job requirement he'd ever been perfectly suited to.

But halfway down, the clerk stopped at a door Aldric had never seen open. A door at the bottom of everything, older than the stonework around it, its frame carved with spirals that hurt to look at too long.

"Why is this door warm?" Aldric asked.

That was the question. That was the whole rebellion. Why is this door warm.

The clerk had looked at him with something that wasn't anger. It was pity, maybe, or relief—Aldric could never decide which, even now. And then the man had unlocked it, because apparently the answer to that question was considered punishment enough.

Inside was a room that shouldn't have existed. Not grand. Not terrible, at first glance. Just a chamber of black stone, and in its center, floating above an iron basin, a coal.

Small. Barely the size of a fist. Dull orange, guttering, the way a hearth-fire gutters when it's starving. And around it—around it were the jars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Rows upon rows of memory-jars stacked floor to ceiling, each one faintly glowing, each one connected to the basin by threads of light so thin you'd miss them if you blinked. Memories flowing into that little dying coal like blood into a leech.

"What is it feeding on?" Aldric had asked.

"Everything we bring in," the clerk said. "Every recollection sold to us from the valley. Every stolen childhood, every lifted love, every purged sin. You think we buy memories to keep them, knight? We keep them because it eats them." The clerk had watched the coal pulse with each jar that emptied. "It has been starving since the war ended. Since its last bearer burned herself out sealing it. And the Guild has decided it will not starve again. So we feed it. Decade by decade. Jar by jar. Until it's strong enough to be... directed."

"Directed how?"

The clerk hadn't answered that. He'd only said, quietly, "Now you know why the door is warm," and led him back up into the cold, and Aldric had walked up those stairs a different man than he'd walked down them.

* * *

"They let me go," Aldric said, staring into the fire. "That's the part I couldn't understand for years. They caught me copying vault manifests. They caught me asking questions in taverns near the capital. Any other traitor would've vanished into a jar himself. Instead they court-martialed me on fabricated charges, stripped my rank, and exiled me—to Hollowmere. Of all the places in the world."

Orsen's whittling knife had stopped moving. She was watching him now with those gray eyes that missed nothing, and he could see her assembling it faster than he'd ever managed to. Thief's mind. Always three steps ahead.

"The border," she said slowly. "Magic can't cross it. Memory magic can't cross it."

"It's the one place in the world where the Ember can't reach," Aldric agreed. "And the one place where a child carrying it would have to surface eventually. The Guild didn't exile me, Orsen. They *stationed* me. A disgraced knight, broken and bitter, dropped into the valley where the thing they're raising would one day arrive—so that if it ever did, there'd be a friendly pair of eyes already inside the walls. Watching. Reporting."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the dead trees along the road, and Aldric thought about all the reports he'd never sent. All the years of silence. Because somewhere in his second year in the valley, watching Orsen Pemberton work a crowd with those quick gray eyes and quicker fingers, he'd simply... stopped writing. Stopped caring what the Guild wanted. Started caring what one particular thief wanted instead.

"So you were their spy," Orsen said. Her voice was level. Too level. "All these years."

"I was their spy for exactly zero years," Aldric said. "I received instructions twice. Both times, I burned them unread. I've spent eight years being the worst asset the Guild ever placed, and I'd do it again." He met her eyes. "But that doesn't change what I was sent to be. You deserve to know it before Vaskarren. If you want to leave me at the crossroads tomorrow, I'll leave you at the crossroads tomorrow. I'll hold the pass either way. But you'll hold it knowing."

Silence stretched between them, long enough that the fire ate itself down another finger's width. Wren stirred against Orsen's side and settled again, one small hand closing in sleep around the wrapped coal.

Orsen set down the stick and the knife.

"The soldier whose daughter's face I stole," she said. "First week I ever sold to Corvane. He cried in his sleep for a year after, calling a name he couldn't attach to anything anymore. I told myself it was survival. I told myself the valley made us all monsters, so none of us were really to blame." She looked at him across the flames. "I fed that coal too, Aldric. Every jar I ever filled. I just didn't know what was at the bottom of the pipe."

"You know now."

"I know now." Something shifted in her face—not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground where forgiveness might eventually take root. "Fenna's in a cell in that city right now having her past scraped out of her to feed your warm door. So here's my question, knight, and think carefully before you answer, because I'm very good at spotting lies. When we get to Vaskarren—when it comes time to burn the Guild's ledger to the ground—are you with us, or are you still theirs?"

Aldric set his sword aside. Deliberately. Where both of them could see him do it.

"I deserted once over a question," he said. "I won't desert twice over an answer."

* * *

He expected her to argue. To test him further. To make him swear something dramatic, because thieves loved oaths the way knights loved banners.

She didn't. She just nodded once, sharp, and said, "Then tell me about the vaults. Everything. Doors, wards, guard rotations, which clerks are bribable and which ones genuinely believe in the cause. If we're walking into Vaskarren, we walk in knowing more than they expect us to know."

So he talked. Past midnight, into the small hours, he talked—drew maps in ash at the edge of the firelight, named names, described the three locks on the deep archive and the one flaw in the outer wall that the Guild had never patched because no one alive remembered it was there. Wren woke somewhere in the middle of it and listened without understanding, her dark eyes going from Aldric's face to the ash-maps to Orsen's, and once, when he described the room with the warm door, she reached out and touched the wrapped coal at her chest, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you loved and feared in equal measure.

Near dawn, Orsen banked the fire and stood, rolling her shoulders.

"Get some sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we plan the impossible part."

"Which is?"

She glanced back at him, and for half a heartbeat, the thief who'd once picked his memory clean and kept his worst day safe in a jar under her floorboards almost looked young.

"Getting Fenna back," she said. "Without becoming fuel."

Aldric lay back on the cold ground and stared up at a sky going gray at the edges, and found—for the first time since the vaults—since the warm door, since the starving coal—he was not afraid of what waited in Vaskarren.

He was afraid of only one thing now: that when they reached the city, Orsen would learn the rest of what the Ember wanted.

Because he'd seen the coal's hunger up close. And he knew it didn't want memories.

It wanted a bearer.
