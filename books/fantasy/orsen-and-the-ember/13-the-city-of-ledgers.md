## 13. The City of Ledgers

The road of ash ended where the ledger began.

Orsen saw Vaskarren first as a stain on the horizon—a smudge darker than the morning, growing teeth. Then the teeth resolved into spires: the Ledger Hall with its black tower, the memory-markets fanning out beneath it like a spilled hand of cards, the tenements stacked on top of each other in defiance of gravity and decency. A city built on the principle that nothing should ever be lost, and everything could be sold.

She had lived here once. Three years, maybe four—she'd never been able to fix the dates, because she'd arrived without a past and left the same way. Hollowmere had been an escape from this place, though at the time she'd told herself it was simply a better market.

Now it was the last place on earth she wanted to be, and the only one that held Fenna.

"Smells the same," Aldric said behind her. He had Wren up on his shoulders, wrapped in a woolen cloak two sizes too big, her coal hidden in a lead-lined box strapped to Orsen's chest like a second heart. "Like ink and old meat."

"It is ink and old meat," Orsen said. "Everything else is marketing."

Wren made a sound—not a word, not quite a laugh. She'd been making more sounds since they crossed the border, little clicks and hums in that language older than kingdoms, and Orsen's stolen memories kept trying to answer them. That was the worst part of coming back outside the valley: every recollection she carried woke up hungry. In Hollowmere they slept. Here, they pressed against the inside of her skull like cats wanting out.

*Steady,* she told herself. *You've done this before.*

Except she hadn't. Not like this. Before, she had walked these markets as a seller—anonymous, unremarkable, one more hollow-eyed trader among hundreds. Now there were sketches of her face in Guild clerks' desks, and riders on the roads asking after a child who spoke the language of fire.

They came down off the ridge as the sun cleared the ash-haze, joining the stream of carts and pilgrims flowing toward the gates. Orsen wore the disguise they'd bought in a river town three days back: good gray wool, a widow's veil pushed back, the posture of a woman with somewhere to be. And underneath all of it, stitched into her like a lining—

"Again," Pim signed, riding beside her on the cart bench. His hands were quick, economical. *Say your name.*

"Maren Voss," she said. "Widow of Hallowford. I keep ledgers for the dye guild. I have a sister I don't speak to."

Sable, walking at the ox's head, glanced back and signed something fast to her brother. Pim translated with a flat expression: *"Your sister. What did she do?"*

"She married a man Maren disapproved of. Maren hasn't forgiven her. Maren is very good at not forgiving." Orsen flexed her fingers. The dead woman's memories sat in her mind like borrowed clothes—she'd lifted them from a grave-goods trader outside Hallowford, four days of a life compressed into an hour's work. A husband dead of lung-rot. A sister named Tess. A hatred of the smell of tallow. A small, stubborn pride in her own neat handwriting.

It was enough. It was also, she was beginning to realize, dangerous in a way she hadn't planned for.

Because Maren Voss had been lonely. Orsen could feel it—the shape of empty evenings, the habit of talking to herself, the way grief had calcified into routine. The memories fit her too well. A thief's rule: wear a stolen coat long enough and you stop noticing the weight. But this wasn't a coat. This was a self, and it was settling over her bones like snowfall, soft and silent and accumulating.

* * *

The gates took no notice of them. Vaskarren didn't care who entered; it cared what they carried. A bored examiner waved a detection wand over their cart—it found nothing, because magic couldn't be smuggled, only memories, and memories weren't illegal until someone wanted them back—and waved them through into streets that smelled of ink, smoke, frying onions, and under it all, faintly, the sweet copper scent of the extraction houses.

"We split at the Tannery Steps," Orsen said quietly, leaning close to Aldric so the words stayed between them. "Pim and Sable find us rooms near the Saltmarket—you remember the sign, the crooked fish. Aldric, you stay with Wren. You don't leave those rooms. You don't open that box. You don't let anyone hear her make sounds."

"And you?"

"I go to the markets. I sell something. That's how you learn things here—everything in this city is a transaction, including gossip." She adjusted her veil. "And then tomorrow night, we take Fenna out of the vault annex under the Ledger Hall."

Aldric looked at her the way he did when he was deciding whether to argue. He usually decided against it, which she suspected was less about wisdom than about the debt he still thought he owed her. He hadn't yet understood—or she hadn't yet told him—that the vaults he'd deserted were fed by valley-stolen memories, hers among them, and that his disgrace had been engineered to put him exactly where the Guild wanted a pair of eyes. She would tell him. After Fenna. One catastrophe at a time.

"Orsen." He said her name low, careful, the way you handle something cracked. "If Corvane offers you yourself again—"

"He already did once." She smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere useful. "I said no. I'll say it again. Get her inside."

* * *

The Saltmarket opened at midmorning, and by midday it roared.

Stalls in rows, awnings dyed every color theft could buy, hawkers calling their wares in the cant of the trade: *Fresh pulls, gentle extraction, childhood summers, first kisses, the taste of your mother's bread.* Memory was currency here, literally—traders paid in vials, weighed jars against scales calibrated to nothing physical, haggled over vintage the way other cities haggled over wine. The Guild's black tower loomed over it all, and every third stall flew the ledger-mark: a closed book with a keyhole in its spine.

Orsen moved through the crowd wearing Maren Voss the way you'd wear armor with the straps showing.

That was the trouble. She could feel the straps.

At the third stall she sold a fabricated memory—nothing valuable, a forged recollection of a festival in a town that didn't exist, crafted well enough to pass a casual tasting. The broker swirled it, sniffed it, paid her six vials without looking up. Routine. And while his attention was on the jar, she let her eyes do the real work: the Guild runners moving through the market in pairs, the new notices nailed to the ledger-posts, the sketch pinned at eye level.

Her own face. Younger than it felt, harder than it was. *WANTED FOR THEFT OF GUILD PROPERTY,* the notice said, and beneath it, smaller: *APPROACH ONLY WITH EXTRACTION TEAM. SUBJECT IS SKILLED.*

Skilled. She almost laughed. Somewhere a clerk had written that, and somewhere a supervisor had approved it, and neither of them knew that the property in question was currently walking through their market in another woman's memories.

She turned away—and stopped.

The vertigo hit her between one step and the next, sudden as a trapdoor. Because a woman at the neighboring stall had called out to her—*"Maren! Maren Voss!"*—and Orsen's body had answered before her mind caught up. She'd turned. She'd smiled. She'd felt the genuine flicker of recognition, warm and wrong, rising from memories that were not hers: *Tess's friend, from the wedding, before everything.*

"I'm sorry—" The woman's smile faltered. "You looked like someone I knew."

"You look like her too," Orsen heard herself say, in Maren's flat, unforgiving voice, and walked on before the conversation could dig deeper into a grave that wasn't hers.

But her hands were shaking.

This was the thing no one warned thieves about, or maybe everyone warned them and none of them listened: identity was a load-bearing wall. You could knock holes in it, steal rooms out of it, live in the spaces where other people's lives used to be—but eventually something leaned on the wrong spot and the whole structure groaned. She had spent twenty years being nobody on purpose. Hollow. Safe. Now she was full—full of Maren's grief, Maren's sister, Maren's stubborn unforgiving pride—and every hour she wore it, the line between the disguise and the wearer got thinner.

*Tess,* she thought, and couldn't tell whether the ache that followed was sympathy or inheritance.

She ducked into an alley between stalls, pressed her palm flat against cold brick, and breathed until the shaking stopped. In her chest, against her heartbeat, the lead box holding Wren's coal was warm. Of course it was. The Ember knew its own, even through lead. Even through lies.

"Not today," she whispered—to whom, she wasn't sure. The coal. Herself. Both.

Then a shadow fell across the mouth of the alley, and Orsen's hand went to her knife.

* * *

The man in the alley wore Guild colors, but wrong—dyed too dark, the ledger-mark stitched by an amateur. An informant, then, or a freelancer pretending to be one. He was young, nervous, sweating despite the cold, and he looked at her the way informants look at marks: hopeful and afraid in equal measure.

"Maren Voss?" he said.

"No," Orsen said.

He blinked. "You—I was told—gray wool, widow's veil, keeps ledgers for the dye guild—"

"That describes half the city." She let Maren's irritation surface, cold and precise. "Who told you?"

"A runner. Said you'd want to buy information about the vault annex. About a prisoner taken on the pilgrim road." He swallowed. "Said you'd pay in vials. Six or better."

Fenna. Her heart slammed once, hard, and she made Maren's face do nothing at all. "What information?"

"The extraction schedule." He licked his lips. "Your prisoner's due in the deep vaults day after tomorrow. Full harvest. But tonight—" he leaned closer, voice dropping, "—tonight she's held light. Annex cellar, two guards, rotation changes at moonset. There's a window. Small one."

"Why sell this? Why me?"

"Because the Guild pays for delivery, but someone else pays double for leaks." His grin showed bad teeth. "Everyone in this city sells to somebody, mistress. Even the walls have creditors."

She paid him eight vials and a forged memory of a lover's forgiveness—generous, memorable, the kind of payment that made freelancers loyal—and watched him vanish into the market crowd. Then she stood alone in the alley with the noise washing over her and let herself feel it: hope, sharp and terrible, because windows that small were usually doors painted on walls, and the Guild had known she was coming before she'd crossed the border.

*Unless they hadn't. Unless it was true. Unless Fenna was really sitting in a cellar two guards deep, waiting for people who might already be too late.*

Moonset. Tonight.

She straightened, settled Maren's memories around her shoulders like a shawl, and stepped back into the roar of the market. Above the stalls, above the awnings, the black tower of the Ledger Hall caught the afternoon light and gave none of it back. Somewhere inside it, in a vault lined with wards fresh-drawn for a child and a thief, Master Corvane was waiting. She could feel it the way you feel weather coming—in the ache of old memories, in the warmth of a coal through lead, in the thinning line between who she was and who she was pretending to be.

Hold on, Fenna, she thought. We're coming. And when we come, we're bringing the whole rotten ledger down.

She bought a paper of fried onions from a corner cart—Maren hated onions, and Orsen ate them anyway, defiantly, tasting both women's lives at once—and walked toward the Saltmarket's crooked-fish sign, where her family was waiting in a rented room, counting hours down to moonset.

Behind her, unnoticed, a Guild runner peeled away from a stall and followed—matching her pace, memorizing her walk, carrying word to the tower that the widow Maren Voss had just paid far too much for information she shouldn't have wanted.

And in the tower, in a room smelling of ink and cold iron, a clerk set down his pen and reached for a bell rope, because the report he'd just received contained one detail no one outside the Guild should have known:

the prisoner's name.
