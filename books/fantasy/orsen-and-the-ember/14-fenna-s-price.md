## 14. Fenna's Price

The cell had no door.

That was the first thing Fenna understood about the Guild's vaults: they didn't need doors when they could simply make you forget the way out. The walls were pale stone without seam or grain, and somewhere beneath her—she could feel it through the floor, a low animal hum—the Ember slept in its cage of ledgers and jars, dreaming on a diet of other people's yesterdays.

She'd been awake for what felt like three days. Time was unreliable down here; the light never changed, and neither did the temperature, which sat at exactly the degree of discomfort that kept you from resting. She'd counted her heartbeats to mark hours until she lost count at eleven thousand and decided counting was for people with better things to count.

Her hands were bound—not with rope but with rings of cold iron fitted over each finger, jointed like insect legs, so that her fingers couldn't curl. A lockwright's hands were her whole self. Whoever designed these restraints knew the trade.

"They know the trade," she said aloud, to no one. "That's the problem."

Speaking helped. Speaking was a lock you picked yourself, over and over, every hour, so the mechanism of *you* didn't rust shut.

When the door did come—and it came as a wall simply deciding to be elsewhere—it brought two clerks in grey and a woman in white with kind eyes and terrible hands.

"Good morning," said the woman in white. "I'm Sister Vael. We're going to help you remember."

* * *

They called it extraction, and it was nothing like Orsen's work.

Orsen stole memories whole—a clean lift, a pocket turned out, the thing gone from one head and sealed in glass. What the Guild did in the vaults was slower and worse. They didn't take the memory. They *siphoned* it, drawing it out thread by thread while you watched it leave, so that you experienced losing it in real time, and then experienced not having lost it entirely, because siphoning left residue, and residue meant they could come back tomorrow and draw again.

Fenna learned this on the first day, strapped into a chair that reclined like a confessional, a circlet of copper and bone fitted around her skull.

"What are you taking?" she asked.

Sister Vael consulted a ledger. "Everything mechanical," she said pleasantly. "Locks, tumblers, wards, mechanisms. You're a craftsman. Craft lives deep. It'll take several sessions."

"And after?"

"After, you won't know how a key works." Vael smiled. "You'll see one and feel a pleasant confusion. Like a bird looking at a window."

*It doesn't work that way,* Fenna wanted to say. *Skill isn't stored in a jar. Skill is the shape the memory leaves behind.* But she was beginning to suspect the Guild had spent decades proving otherwise, one hollowed thief at a time, and she kept the argument behind her teeth where it couldn't be harvested.

The machine started as a sound: a long, thin pull, like wire drawn through a die.

And then the memories began leaving.

She watched her mother's workshop go first—not the woman herself, thank every open god, but the room: the pegboard of picks arranged by pitch, the smell of oil and iron filings, the way morning light came through the east window and made every tool throw a shadow like a little black key. She watched herself at nine years old, failing to pick her first padlock, failing again, failing forty times, and then—there it was, the click, the sweetest sound in the world—

Gone. Not gone-gone. Thinned. She could still say the words *padlock* and *click*, but they were words now, not knowledge. A bird looking at a window.

She cried. She wasn't ashamed of crying. Crying was also a mechanism, and it still worked.

* * *

Between sessions they gave her bread and water and left her alone with the humming floor, and Fenna did what she had been doing since the moment the wall opened: she worked.

Because here was the thing Sister Vael hadn't understood, or hadn't bothered to understand, and it was the single most important fact in the world:

Extraction took the memory. It did not take the *making* of the memory.

Every time the machine drew a thread out of Fenna's head, that thread passed through the circlet, through the copper, through the apparatus—and Fenna, strapped down and weeping, had spent the entire first session studying the apparatus. Where the wires ran. Which contact sat loose against her temple. How the clerk adjusted the gain dial when she screamed, because screaming overloaded the intake and they had to throttle it back.

She was a lockwright. The circlet was a lock. Her skull was the housing. And every lock has tolerances.

On the second day, during extraction, she did something no subject had presumably ever done: instead of resisting the pull, she *shaped* it. When the machine drew out the memory of her father teaching her to file a key blank, Fenna held the shape of another memory against it—pressed them together the way you press two waxes together to cast a copy—and let the machine take both braided into one.

The machine recorded what it recorded. But what went into the jar was not only her father's workshop. Woven through it, invisible to any clerk who played the jar back, was a message.

She'd chosen the medium carefully. Memory-jars were read by immersion—a reader sank into the recollection and walked around inside it. So the message had to live inside the scene, in details a casual reader would take for furniture.

In the jarred memory of her father filing a key blank, there was a shelf behind him. On the shelf sat seven tools. Seven tools, in an order that spelled, in thieves' tap-code—the knock-pattern Pim and Sable used on each other's shutters—two words:

*VAULT SEVEN.*

A reader would see a workshop. A thief who knew the code would see a map reference.

Fenna smiled through the whole session. Sister Vael noted it in her ledger as "unusual affect."

*Unusual affect,* Fenna thought. *Lady, you have no idea.*

* * *

By the fifth day she had planted four messages, and by the fifth day she had also, through careful listening, assembled the shape of the Guild's plan—and wished she hadn't.

It came in pieces. Clerks talked in the corridor outside her cell-wall; the walls were thin in ways the stone pretended not to be. Sister Vael, believing her thoroughly broken, grew chatty in the clinical way of people who enjoy their own expertise.

"You're wondering why we bother with you," Vael said on the sixth day, adjusting the circlet. "A lockwright. There are other lockwrights. But you're *hers*. You built the crew's trust. And the child trusts what the crew trusts. That makes you a lever."

"I'm not a lever," Fenna said. "I'm a hinge. Levers get thrown. Hinges hold."

Vael patted her hand. "We'll see which breaks first."

But it was the corridor talk that filled in the rest. Two senior clerks, late shift, voices low:

"—the vessel's readings spike when she's frightened. Corvane wants controlled fear. Scheduled fear. He's drawn up a calendar."

"A calendar of frightenings."

"Don't call it that in front of the archivists. Call it *attunement sessions*."

And later, from a different pair:

"—forty years of harvest. Every valley-stolen memory since the founding. Do you know how much that is? Whole lifetimes. Villages. Wars nobody remembers fighting anymore because we own the remembering."

"It's not enough. Corvane says it's not enough. He wants a nation's worth. He's petitioned the outer circle to begin wide culling—"

"Culling."

"Selective. Border towns first. Places that won't be missed."

Fenna lay in the dark of her seamless cell and did arithmetic she hated.

The Ember was a fire, and fires needed fuel, and the Guild had spent forty years stockpiling fuel in glass jars, and now they had the hearth itself sleeping downstairs wearing the body of a mute girl who trusted a deaf lockwright to keep her safe.

They weren't going to control the god-fire. Nobody controls a fire. You can only control what you feed it and where you build the walls—and Corvane intended to feed it a nation and call the resulting inferno a tool.

Worse—much worse—was the smaller thing, the thing she turned over all night like a stubborn tumbler:

Corvane had said *the vessel*. He had said *the child trusts what the crew trusts*. Which meant the Guild believed the crew was still together, still moving, still coming.

Which meant Orsen hadn't run far enough, fast enough—or had run exactly where Corvane wanted, toward Vaskarren, toward the vaults, walking willingly into the one building in the world designed to take everything she was.

*Come anyway,* Fenna thought, pressing her bound fingers flat against the humming floor. *Come stupid. Come loud. I'll have the locks ready.*

* * *

The seventh day, they took something she'd sworn they wouldn't.

Not a skill. Sister Vael had been true to her word about craft—craft was valuable, craft was inventory. No, what they took on the seventh day was a person: the memory of Wren.

All of it. The night Orsen had brought the girl in from the snow, blue-footed and silent, and Fenna had grumbled about it and built her a cot anyway. The first time Wren laughed—at Pim juggling stolen apples, dropping every one. The morning Wren had pressed her small cold hands against Fenna's cheeks and made a sound like wind through a reed, and Fenna had understood it perfectly, the way you understand someone without language, and had understood that this was what having a daughter would have been, if life had ever offered her the shape.

The machine pulled it out in threads while she watched, and Fenna fought it, actually fought it, straining against the straps until her shoulders tore—and Sister Vael leaned close and murmured, almost tenderly:

"This one goes to the Ember directly. Consider it an honor. Your love will burn."

Afterward, Fenna sat in her cell knowing that a child existed whom she had loved, because the residue remained—the shape without the content, the window without the view. She knew the love the way you know a scar: evidence of an event, event missing.

So she did the only thing left to a lockwright whose hands were ringed in iron.

She rebuilt it.

Not the memory—she couldn't do that. But she took a scrap of tin from the seam of her bread tray, worked it against the floor-grout for six hours until it was a thin bright sliver, and scratched into the wall, in tap-code dots, the whole story: *girl came in snow. coal that wouldn't cool. name of wren. laugh like this:* —and here she tapped out, against the stone, the rhythm of Wren's laughter, the exact syncopation of it, preserved in percussion the way songs survive in deaf families.

If she died down here, the wall would remember. If she lived, she'd scratch it out before anyone read it and carry it in her body instead.

Both, probably. Both was the honest plan.

* * *

On the ninth day, they moved her.

Two clerks, a new corridor, deeper down. The hum in the floor grew teeth. And Fenna, being marched past racks of memory-jars stretching into lamplit dark—thousands of them, tens of thousands, each one a somebody's somebody—did what she had trained herself to do: she looked at everything and forgot nothing, because forgetting was a luxury the Guild sold and she refused to buy.

Vault Seven. She saw its door with her own eyes at last: a slab of black iron covered in wards old enough to predate the Guild, and newer wards chalked over the old ones in Corvane's cramped hand. And beside the door, on a clerk's trolley waiting for transfer, a row of fresh jars, each labeled in the same cramped script.

She read the labels upside down as they passed. She was a lockwright; reading upside down was a professional courtesy.

The labels were names. Not prisoners' names—*donor* names. Valley folk. The soldier whose daughter's face Orsen had lifted. Mother Quill's neighbors. Names Fenna knew from market days and roofline greetings, forty years of Hollowmere poured into glass and racked like wine.

And at the end of the row, one jar larger than the rest, double-sealed, its label written in a different hand—older, formal, almost reverent:

*PEMBERTON, O. — ORIGINAL STOCK — DO NOT DECANT WITHOUT AUTHORITY OF THE OUTER CIRCLE.*

Orsen's childhood. Sitting on a trolley. Being wheeled toward Vault Seven, toward the Ember, toward the fire that ate memories as fuel.

Fenna understood then, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in sideways: Corvane wasn't just feeding the god-fire. He was feeding it *specifically*. Curating. The Ember had preferences—it burned brightest on memories weighted with love, with identity, with a self—and he was assembling a menu.

And the centerpiece of the menu was the woman who had carried his cargo for years and never once asked what the jars were for.

They locked her in a side chamber off Vault Seven—"close to your work," Sister Vael said brightly—and left her alone with the hum.

Fenna put her back against the wall, closed her eyes, and got to work.

There was a maintenance panel in the chamber's far corner. She'd seen it on the way in: standard Guild pattern, forty years old, installed by a contractor who had cut corners on the hinge pins because nobody audits corners. She could pick it blind. She could pick it with her elbows. She could, if it came to it, pick it with her teeth.

She couldn't reach it yet. The rings on her fingers saw to that. But rings were locks too, and every lock in the world was a promise that someone, someday, would be foolish enough to bring it near a lockwright.

She began working the smallest ring, the one on her left pinky, testing its play. A hair of give. Another. Outside, beyond the black door, the Ember dreamed on its diet of the dead, and above it all Vaskarren went about its business, ledgers balancing, lanterns lighting, a city that did not know it was stacked like cordwood around a furnace.

*Hold on, Wren,* she thought, working the pin. *Hold on, Orsen, you magnificent idiot, wherever you are.*

*Come find me. I've already picked us a way out—I just need someone to carry the torch.*

Somewhere above, faint through forty feet of stone, she heard it: a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat.

Three knocks. Pause. Two knocks.

Tap-code. At the vault door.

Pim and Sable's greeting pattern—the one they used on each other's shutters, the one she'd woven into her father's workshop and buried in a jar.

Someone was knocking.
