## 15. The Vault Beneath the Ledger Hall

The rain in Vaskarren fell like a confession—slow, gray, and impossible to stop once it started.

Orsen crouched beneath the eaves of a chandler's shop across the square from the Ledger Hall, watching water gather in the grooves of the cobblestones and thinking about locks. Fenna had been in the vaults for nine days now. Nine days of extraction, if the Guild's clerks were as thorough as Orsen knew them to be, and every hour that passed was another hour of Fenna's past being ladled out of her like soup from a pot. Lockwrights were valuable. A lockwright's hands remembered ten thousand tumblers, and the Guild paid well for hands like that.

*Hold on*, Orsen thought at the dark windows of the Ledger Hall, as if the building could hear her. *Hold on, you stubborn woman. We're coming.*

"You're doing the face again," Pim said beside her.

Orsen glanced over. The twins had wedged themselves into the narrow gap between the chandler's wall and a stack of empty barrels, and they fit there the way water fits a crack—Pim all elbows and watchful eyes, Sable signing lazily at his brother even though neither of them needed light to read each other's hands.

"What face?" Orsen asked.

"The one where you look like you're going to rob something," Pim said. "It's your best face. I'd know it anywhere."

"Good," Orsen said. "Because that's exactly what we're about to do."

Sable signed something quick and sharp, and Pim laughed—a small, startled sound he immediately smothered against his sleeve, because laughing in Vaskarren was the kind of thing that got you noticed, and being noticed was the kind of thing that got you taken.

"What did he say?" Orsen asked.

"He said the Ledger Hall has forty-one doors and we only need to fail at one of them."

"He's right," Orsen said. "So we won't fail at any of them."

---

Fenna's codes had arrived on the second night, folded inside a beggar's cup that a street child pressed into Aldric's hands outside the Temple of Small Mercies. The child hadn't known who sent it. Fenna had paid her in bread and a story, which was how Fenna did everything—warmth first, tradecraft second.

The codes were beautiful. Of course they were; Fenna had built half the locks in Hollowmere and picked the other half, and she had spent nine days in a Guild cell doing what she always did: working. She'd left her messages stitched into her own extracted memories, buried in the jars like needles in hay, because Fenna understood something the Guild's archivists never had—that a memory is a container, and any container can hold more than it appears to. When the extraction clerks pulled her recollections of her mother's kitchen, they found a kitchen. They didn't find the number *seven* hidden in the pattern of cracks on the floor tiles, or the vault rotation schedule disguised as a recipe for plum preserves, or the fact that the third warding-stone in the lower corridor had been installed by a mason who owed Fenna money and cut corners, and could be bypassed by pressing the third brick from the left in the manner of someone knocking politely rather than forcing their way in.

*A lock is just a door that hasn't been introduced to you properly yet,* Fenna had told Orsen once, years ago, over cheap wine in Hollowmere. *Everything opens. You just have to ask it in the right language.*

The last message in the sequence was the simplest and the worst: *They keep the old harvests below the counting floor. Corvane goes down every night at the change of the watch. He stays for hours. Whatever he's feeding down there, it's hungry.*

---

They went in during the change of the watch, when the Guild's guards traded posts in the gray hour before dawn and the whole building yawned like a tired animal.

Aldric took the front—not through the doors, but around the side, where a delivery ramp descended toward the counting floor and a single sentry stood under an awning, miserable and wet. Orsen watched from the shadows as the disgraced knight walked up to the man openly, unhurried, wearing the faded livery of a border garrison he'd deserted a decade ago, and asked him for directions to the records office with the flat boredom of a functionary who had been walking in rain all night. The sentry, grateful for any interruption to his misery, gave them. Aldric thanked him, complained about the weather, mentioned a fictional sergeant by name, and kept the man's attention fixed on the north gate while Pim and Sable went over the ramp railings behind him like two drops of water running down glass.

By the time the sentry looked back at his post, the twins were already inside, and the door they'd opened—the delivery door, the one Fenna's codes said was barred from within by a bolt that could be slipped with a flattened coin and patience—was closed again, looking exactly as bored and locked as before.

Orsen came in last, hood up, heart steady. Steady was important. Fear was loud, and loud things got noticed.

The counting floor stretched above them as they crossed the undercroft: a cathedral of ledgers, shelves rising into darkness, thousands upon thousands of bound volumes recording every memory the Guild had ever bought, sold, or stolen. Orsen had walked those aisles once, years ago, selling a soldier's daughter and feeling sick about it. Now she moved beneath them like a rat beneath a granary, and the sickness felt different—sharper, more personal.

*Every book up there is somebody,* she thought. *Every page is a morning somebody loved and doesn't anymore.*

Sable signed: *Ward-stone ahead.* He'd stopped, hand raised, at the mouth of the lower corridor, where the air changed temperature and the lamplight turned the color of old honey. Orsen couldn't see the warding itself—magic died at Hollowmere's borders, but here, outside, it lived, and Guild wards hung in the air like heat shimmer over a road.

She counted bricks. Third from the left. She pressed it the way Fenna's coded message had described—not a shove, not a strike, but a knock. Three soft taps, patient, polite.

The shimmer sighed and parted like a curtain drawn by a considerate servant.

"Fenna," Orsen breathed, "you magnificent criminal."

Pim grinned in the dark. Sable signed something that was either gratitude or a promise to buy the woman a lifetime of wine, and then they were through, descending the spiral stair into the oldest part of the Ledger Hall—the part that predated the Guild's respectable façade, the part built when the vaults had a different purpose entirely.

---

The air below was warm.

That was the first wrong thing. Vaults should be cold—they kept things, and kept things kept. But this deep, the stone held a warmth like a body's, and there was a smell Orsen recognized from Wren's visions, from the burning cities that flooded her mind whenever she touched the coal: hot iron and ash and something sweeter underneath, like sugar left too long over a flame.

Rows of memory-jars lined the walls.

Not the small clay vessels the Guild used for retail—those neat little pots that held a first kiss or a dead father's laugh, sold in the memory-markets upstairs to people who wanted to feel something they'd lost. These jars were different. They were large, blown glass thick as a finger, banded in lead, and each one held not a single recollection but a *mass*—a swirling, faintly luminous sediment of hundreds of memories compressed together, turning slowly, like storms seen from very far above.

There were hundreds of them. Maybe thousands. The corridor ran deeper than lamplight reached.

"They've been harvesting for decades," Orsen whispered. "Whole valleys' worth. Whole *towns*."

Pim touched one jar—carefully, fingertips only—and flinched. "I can hear it," he said, and his voice had gone strange. "Through the glass. It's like... a market day. Hundreds of people talking at once, but none of them are saying anything anymore. They're just—" He pulled his hand back. "They're just noise now. That's all that's left of them."

Sable stood very still, staring down the corridor, and then he signed, slow and deliberate so Orsen would catch every word:

*Something at the end. It's breathing.*

Orsen listened. At first there was nothing—just the drip of condensation and the hum of the wards overhead. Then, beneath it, so low she felt it in her teeth rather than heard it: a rhythm. In and out. Patient. Enormous.

The Ember. Half-starved, fed on spoonfuls of stolen lives, kept alive in the dark beneath a hall full of accounting books. Aldric had told them what he'd seen, all those years ago, before his disgrace was engineered and he was planted at Hollowmere's edge like a nail holding down a board. He'd described it as a fire trying to remember how to be a war.

Orsen drew her knife anyway, out of habit, knowing it was useless, and led them down the corridor toward the breathing.

---

Master Corvane was waiting for them.

He sat on a plain wooden stool at the corridor's end, in the round chamber where the jars gave way to open floor, dressed as always in his clerk's grays without ornament, his hands folded in his lap like a man at a funeral he approved of. He did not look surprised. Orsen realized, with a cold drop in her stomach, that he had probably known they were coming since the moment Fenna's first coded message left her cell—or perhaps long before that, since the night a barefoot girl appeared at a thief's door in Hollowmere carrying a coal that never cooled.

Perhaps he had simply let them come. Perhaps this room was the trap, and the trap had always been shaped like a gift.

"Orsen Pemberton," Corvane said. His voice was mild. It was always mild. That was the horror of him—he sounded like a man discussing grain prices. "And the twins. And no knight, which means he's holding the upper corridors, which means you planned for retreat. Good. You were always my best student, even if you never signed your apprenticeship papers."

"I was never your student," Orsen said. "You bought things from me. There's a difference."

"A distinction without a difference, in my experience." He rose from the stool, unhurried, and gestured at the chamber around him. Behind him, set into the far wall, was a door of black iron banded in silver, and through its seams seeped a light that was not lamplight—red-gold, pulsing gently, keeping time with the breathing. "Do you know what this room is?"

"A tomb," Orsen said. "Yours, eventually."

"It's a *pantry*." Corvane smiled faintly, as if pleased with her answer regardless. "For sixty years the Guild has bought memories from Hollowmere. Every soldier's daughter, every widow's wedding day, every thief's childhood—you sold us hundreds, Orsen, and you were one seller among dozens. And do you know why we paid so well? Why we never haggled? Because the thing behind that door cannot eat gold. It eats *yesterday*. It eats the past. It is the last ember of the war-magic that burned the world once already, and it is starving, and starving gods make poor servants." He clasped his hands behind his back. "We have kept it alive on scraps for three generations. Imagine what it could do fed properly. Imagine what a nation's worth of harvested memory could build, or burn, or *be*. The Guild does not want to destroy the Ember, Orsen. The Guild wants to *own* it."

"And the girl?" Pim's voice cracked on the word. "She's eight years old. She's a *child*, not a—"

"She is a vessel," Corvane said, without cruelty, which was worse. "Vessels break. Vessels leak. What we need is a vessel that can be emptied safely and refilled with something we control." His eyes returned to Orsen, and there was something new in them—not the mild appetite of a buyer, but something almost tender, and that tenderness frightened her more than everything else in the room combined. "But you didn't come all this way for a lecture. You came for your friend. And I imagine, given who you are, that you also came hoping to see these."

He reached into the shadow beside the stool and lifted a jar.

It was small. Smaller than the storm-jars lining the corridor—clay, unremarkable, the kind the Guild used for retail stock, the kind Orsen had carried a hundred times herself. A paper label was pasted to its side, written in a clerk's careful hand:

**PEMBERTON, O. — ACQUISITION, ORIGINAL. AGE 6–12. VOLUNTARY.**

Orsen stopped breathing.

The world narrowed to the jar. To the label. To the word *voluntary*, sitting there in tidy ink like a knife laid flat on a table.

"That's not possible," she heard herself say. "My childhood wasn't stolen. It was *erased*. Nobody knows what happened to me before Hollowmere. Not even me. Especially not me."

"No," Corvane agreed softly. "Nobody knows. That was rather the point." He turned the jar in his hands, letting the lamplight catch the faint glow of the sediment inside—a child's worth of memory, six years of it, swirling slow and dim. "You came to us at twelve, Orsen. Do you remember coming? Of course you don't. You walked into the Guildhall yourself, alone, in the winter, and you asked us to take it. All of it. You said—" and here he recited it from perfect memory, because remembering was his trade and he had never once given a piece of himself away, "—you said, *'I don't want to be her anymore.'*"

The words went through Orsen like cold water through cloth.

"We paid you nothing," Corvane continued. "You wouldn't take payment. You only made us promise one thing: that no one would ever sell it back to you. That whoever she was—the girl before Hollowmere, the girl who walked out of somewhere terrible and asked a guild of strangers to cut her open and pour her out—we would keep her safe from herself." He held the jar out, slightly, not quite offering it. An invitation. A hook. "We have honored that promise for twenty years. I am prepared to break it tonight."

"Don't listen to him," Pim whispered. "Orsen. *Orsen.* Don't—"

"Give me the girl," Corvane said, "and this is yours. Every year of it. Your mother's voice. Your father's face, if he had one worth keeping. Whatever drove a child into the snow to beg strangers to unmake her—all of it, back, whole, yours. And the Guild will call off its riders. Your friend walks free. Your knight keeps his head. Everyone leaves this city breathing." He smiled his mild, terrible smile. "One child's yesterday, traded for everyone's tomorrow. You of all people understand that arithmetic, Orsen. You've been doing it your entire life. You've sold a hundred daughters' faces to feed yourself. This is merely the largest sum you've ever been offered."

The jar glowed faintly in his hands. Six years of a girl Orsen had never met—the girl under her own skin, the one whose absence she had carried so long she'd mistaken the shape of the hole for a self.

Behind the iron door, the Ember breathed in. Breathed out.

And Orsen, thief of Hollowmere, keeper of other people's yesterdays, reached out her hand.
