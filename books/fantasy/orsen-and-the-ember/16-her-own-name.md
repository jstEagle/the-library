## 16. Her Own Name

The vault beneath the Ledger Hall did not feel like a place where memories were kept. It felt like a place where they were *processed*.

Orsen had imagined rows of dusty jars, cobwebs, the sleepy neglect of a cellar. What she found instead was order. Racks of glass in perfect ranks, each jar stoppered with wax and sealed with a lead band, each one labeled in a clerk's precise hand. Date of harvest. Region of origin. A catalog number. Somewhere above them, ledgers recorded every stolen yesterday in Vaskarren the way a grain merchant recorded bushels.

Pim's fingers hovered over the nearest rack, trembling—not with fear, Orsen realized, but with something like reverence. Sable touched his shoulder, and he pulled his hand back.

"Steady," Aldric breathed behind her. He had a torch in one hand and a short sword in the other, and he stood the way men stand when they expect the room to close around them. "In, out, no lingering."

Fenna's codes had brought them down through three locked doors and a shaft that smelled of cold stone. The fourth door was already open when they arrived.

That was how Orsen knew, before she saw him, before she heard the soft scrape of his boots on marble, that Corvane had been waiting.

* * *

He sat on a plain wooden chair at the center of the vault's long aisle, as if the whole repository of stolen human lives were simply his study and he its patient master. He was not armed. He did not need to be. In his lap, cradled like something newborn, he held a single jar.

It was small. Smaller than most. The memory inside swam pale gold, shifting like candlelight through honey.

The label read: **PEMBERTON, O. — HARVEST 0 — ORIGIN: HOLLOWMERE BORDERLANDS — SEE MASTER INDEX, VOL. I.**

Her name. Not the name she went by in the valley, not the name she'd built out of habit and convenience. Her *true* name, written in a hand she didn't know, on a jar she'd never seen, holding years she couldn't remember living.

"You came by the maintenance shaft," Corvane said. Not accusatory. Almost approving. "Fenna's work, I assume. Clever woman. She'll be pleased to know it functioned perfectly—she designed half those locks herself, before we took her." He rose, unhurried, and the torchlight moved across his face without changing it. "I confess I hoped you would come this way. It saved me a great deal of tedious searching."

"Corvane." Orsen kept her voice level. Behind her, she heard Aldric shift, heard Pim and Sable fan out along the racks the way they'd planned—but the plan had ended at the door, and everything past it was improvisation now. "Where is Wren?"

"Safe. For the moment." He said it lightly, but Orsen's blood went cold, because Corvane did nothing for free, and safety was the most expensive thing he sold. "As are you. That is rather the point of tonight's arrangement."

"There's no arrangement."

"There is always an arrangement, Orsen. You of all people should appreciate that. You've made a career of them." He turned the jar slowly in his hands, letting the light catch it. "You steal memories. I keep them. We are, professionally speaking, colleagues."

"I'm nothing like you."

"No?" His eyes—gray, mild, terribly patient—lifted to hers. "You sell other people's pasts to feed yourself. I buy them to feed a god. The difference is scale, nothing more. And unlike you, I am prepared to give something back."

He extended the jar.

For a moment Orsen could not move. She had spent eleven chapters of her life—no, she corrected herself, bitter and swift—she had spent her whole adult life handling other people's memories. She knew their weight, their temperature, the way a fresh one trembled against glass. She had never once held her own.

"This," Corvane said softly, "is your childhood. All of it. Harvested at the border when you were seven years old—you crossed into Hollowmere with your mother during the last of the border purges. Do you remember the purges?" He smiled faintly at his own joke. "Of course you don't. That was rather the point."

"The valley erased it."

"The valley erased what leaked through its borders, yes. But magic cannot cross into Hollowmere, Orsen—which means neither can forgetting. Whatever crosses that line intact stays intact." He tapped the jar with one fingernail, a small bright sound. "Your childhood didn't fade. It was *taken*. Before you ever reached the valley. And it has been sitting in our keeping ever since, waiting for someone to claim it."

"Liar."

"Am I? Open it. One breath of it, and you'll know I'm telling the truth. You'll know your mother's face. Your first winter. The song she sang when the fever took your brother—" He paused, watching her flinch. "Ah. You didn't know you had a brother. You see? There is so much of you in here, Orsen. So much you've been walking around without, all these years, like a house missing its east wing. Wouldn't you like to walk back inside?"

The vault was very quiet. Somewhere far above, water dripped through stone. Pim and Sable had gone still among the racks. Even the torch flame seemed to hold its breath.

And Orsen—Orsen who had lied to soldiers and charmed clerks and talked her way past three Guild wardens before breakfast—found that she had no words at all.

Because she wanted it.

Gods help her, she wanted it with a hunger that scared her worse than any enforcer ever had. She thought of the hollow place behind her sternum, the shape of a person she could never quite remember being. She thought of all the memories she'd carried for others—the soldier's daughter, the pilgrim's wedding, the sailor's homecoming—and how she'd told herself she remembered for them because no one remembered for her. She thought: *if I take it back, I will finally be whole.*

And then she thought of Wren.

A barefoot girl in the snow. A coal that never cooled. A voice in a language older than kingdoms, saying words that meant, as near as anyone could tell: *you came back for me.*

"Where is Wren?" Orsen asked again, and her voice came out rough.

"In the harvest chamber upstairs, under quiet guard. Unharmed. Comfortable, even." Corvane tilted his head. "The Ember requires her alive and reasonably content—it feeds better on willing vessels, though I confess we have not always been able to afford willing ones. Your friend Fenna, for instance. She was... less cooperative. But productive. Very productive." He sighed, a bureaucrat regretting a paperwork delay. "You may have her back too, eventually. Everything is negotiable."

"What do you want?"

"The child. Delivered to the harvest floor, unharmed, willing or otherwise. In exchange—" he lifted the jar between two fingers, offering it the way a priest offers communion "—your childhood entire. Every year of it. Your mother's voice. Your brother's laugh. The face you wore before the world taught you to wear faces. And afterward, safe passage out of Vaskarren for you and your knight. The Guild does not forget its debts, Orsen. Unlike some people I could mention."

Behind her, Aldric said, low and dangerous: "Orsen."

She held up a hand. Waited. Looked at the jar.

*Take it,* whispered the hollow place. *Take it and be whole. Take it and be someone. You've been nobody so long you've forgotten even the shape of the wound—*

*If I take it,* she thought, *I become the woman who traded a child for her own reflection.*

*But if I don't—*

"But here is my difficulty," Corvane continued, conversational, terrible. "You are a thief, Orsen. A very good one. And thieves, given a choice between what they want and what they need, choose what they want nine times in ten. So I will make it simple." He stepped closer. This close, she could smell the ink on his hands, the paper-dust of a lifetime among records. "Hold the child's life in one hand and your own past in the other, and choose. That is all. No tricks. No wards. Just the oldest trade in the world."

He pressed the jar into her hands.

It was warm. Warmer than it should have been, warm as a living thing, and through the glass she could feel it—faint, insistent, *recognizing* her. The memory knew its owner. It wanted to come home.

Orsen looked at it for a long time.

Then she broke the seal.

* * *

The memory poured into her like light through a cracked door.

Snow. A kitchen with a crooked chimney. A woman with flour on her hands and dark hair escaping its braid, standing at a stove, singing—singing in a language Orsen didn't know and yet somehow knew, a lullaby with a melody that climbed and fell like breathing. The woman turned, laughing at something off-scene, and said—

Not words. Just the *feeling* of being loved, total and unconditional, the way only a very small child is loved, before the world gets its hooks in. Warmth. Safety. The smell of bread. The sound of a voice that would have sung to her every night of her life if the world had let it.

One moment. That was all the seal had given up—one moment, a taste, a coin from a hoard. And then the rest drew back behind the wax like a tide going out, leaving Orsen kneeling on cold marble with tears running down her face and no idea why, except that she knew exactly why.

Her mother's voice. She had her mother's voice now—one thread of it, torn from the whole cloth, and it was more than she'd had an hour ago, and it was nothing, nothing, compared to what remained in the jar.

"That," Corvane murmured, "is a single second. Imagine the rest. Twenty years of it. Imagine knowing yourself again."

Orsen knelt there, shaking.

And understood.

This was what the Guild did. This was what it had *always* done. It didn't just steal memories—it sold them back in pieces, made hunger itself into a product, let people gut themselves year after year chasing the shape of what they'd lost. Fenna in her cell, emptied drop by drop. The soldier whose daughter's face Orsen had bought. Herself, seven years old, crossing a border with a hole where her life should be.

They hadn't taken her childhood to punish her. They'd taken it because a person who doesn't know who they are will pay anything, *anything*, to be handed themselves again.

"No," Orsen said.

Corvane's smile flickered. "No?"

"No." She stood. Her legs were steady. Her voice was steady, and it surprised her, because nothing else in her was. "You're right about one thing. I'm a thief. And I know stolen goods when I'm holding them." She looked down at the jar, at the gold light swimming inside it, at the ghost of a kitchen and a crooked chimney and a voice that sang. "This isn't mine. It never was. Not since you put your label on it."

"It is literally yours—"

"It's *bait*." Her grip tightened on the glass. "You didn't keep it for twenty years out of sentiment. You kept it because someday a girl with a hole where her heart should be would come down these stairs, and you'd have exactly the thing she couldn't refuse. That's not a gift, Corvane. That's a hook. And I have spent my whole life watching what happens to people who bite."

"Orsen." His voice changed—softer now, almost gentle, which was worse. "Think. You will never have another chance at this. When you walk away tonight, you walk away from your own name. From your mother. From everyone you were before you learned to survive. Is that truly a price you're prepared to pay—for one child? One child who isn't yours, who has known you a season?"

Orsen thought about it. Honestly. She gave it the weight it deserved.

She thought of snow, and small bare feet, and a coal that never cooled. She thought of a girl learning to say *bread* and *bed* and *Orsen*, syllables handled carefully, like eggs. She thought of Fenna swearing at locks, of Pim and Sable signing jokes across rooms, of Mother Quill's stories, of Aldric standing in a doorway with his hat in his hands, owing her a debt he'd never once tried to escape.

She thought: *that's not one child. That's my family. I built it myself, out of nothing, because no one gave me anything—and no one can take it either.*

"Yes," she said. "It is."

And she dropped the jar.

* * *

It did not shatter on the marble.

It shattered in the air—because the moment it left her fingers, the memory inside *woke*, all of it at once, twenty years of a stolen childhood released in a single instant, and the vault filled with light and sound and a woman's voice singing, singing, filling the racks and the aisles and the stone overhead, pouring over Pim and Sable and Aldric and Orsen like warm rain.

For one heartbeat, Orsen stood inside her own life.

She saw all of it—the kitchen, the fever, the border, the road, her mother's hands, her brother's laugh, the last look back at a house that burned behind them. She saw the seven-year-old girl she had been, and she loved her, and she said goodbye to her, all in the same breathless instant, because the memory was not staying. Freed, it did not return to its owner. Freed, it simply... left. Rose through the stone like smoke. Went wherever unclaimed things go.

The last thing to go was the song. It hung in the air above her, complete now, every verse, and Orsen stood very still and let it pass through her and memorized—*chose* to memorize—the melody. Just the melody. That much she could keep. That much was hers by right of grief.

Then silence. Then darkness, and the ordinary torchlight, and the ordinary cold.

The empty jar lay at her feet in pieces, the lead band split, the label reading **PEMBERTON, O.** in a dead man's handwriting.

Corvane stared at the fragments as though they had personally betrayed him.

"Do you have any idea," he said quietly, "how rare that memory was? How *irreplaceable*? Pre-border harvests are worth more than this entire vault—"

"You'll have to bill me."

"Twenty years, Orsen. Twenty years of careful acquisition, wasted because you had a *sentiment*—"

"No." She picked up a shard of glass, turned it in the torchlight. "Wasted because you had a ledger. There's a difference." She looked up at him then, and let him see her face—all of it, the grief and the freedom tangled together, indistinguishable, both true. "I know who I am now, Master Corvane. Turns out I'm the woman who chose. That's more than you'll ever fit in a jar."

Something shifted in his expression—something old and cold and patient sliding aside to reveal what lived underneath. "Then you leave me no alternative," he said, and raised his voice to the dark at the end of the aisle: "Seal the vault."

Doors slammed. Chains sang. And from the racks on either side, from a hundred jars along a hundred shelves, a faint golden light began to pulse—quickening, quickening, like a thousand hearts remembering how to race—as somewhere far above them, in a chamber full of machines and prayers, the Ember began to wake.

Orsen spun toward the stairs, toward Wren, toward the sound of chains.

"Aldric," she said. "We're out of time."
