## 17. Ember Unbound

The vault opens like a mouth.

Wren feels it before she sees it—the moment Pim's picks find the last tumbler and the great door swings inward, something inside her *shifts*, a coal rolling in a tin cup. She has carried this heat her whole life, or so she understands it: a warmth behind her ribs that never cools, that speaks to her in the old tongue when no one else can hear. It has always been quiet. Patient. A sleeping thing.

It is not sleeping now.

*They opened the door,* it says, in the language beneath kingdoms, the one Mother Quill wept over, the one Wren was born knowing. *They opened the door, and they are so full.*

"Orsen." Her voice comes out small. She hates how small it is. She is eight years old and she has crossed a burning world and she does not want to sound like an eight-year-old now. "Orsen, something's wrong."

But Orsen isn't listening. Orsen is three steps into the vault, lantern high, face lit gold and stunned—because the room goes on farther than the light can reach. Rows and rows of shelves, and on every shelf, jars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one glowing faintly, each one holding a swirl of color that moves like smoke underwater: a memory, kept. A whole city's worth of yesterdays, harvested and hoarded, labeled in neat clerk's handwriting.

Wren knows what they are. She knew the first time she touched Orsen's hand and felt the stolen soldier's memory still warm in her pocket of herself. The Guild doesn't keep things. The Guild keeps *people*—or the pieces of people that mattered most—and Wren has been walking toward this room since before she could walk.

The coal in her chest turns over.

"Oh," she says softly. "Oh, no."

*So full,* the Ember says again, and its voice is not small at all anymore. It is not a voice in her chest; it is a voice in the shelves, in the jars, in the stone. *All these years I have starved, and here is a feast.*

Sable grabs her arm. The deaf girl's hands move fast, sharp: *What's happening? Your eyes—*

Wren tries to answer and can't, because her eyes are doing something she cannot see. She can feel it, though—a heat behind her face, a brightness. In the polished brass fitting of a shelf bracket she catches her reflection and flinches. Her eyes are lit from inside, ember-orange, and thin lines of the same fire trace down her throat like veins of ore.

The nearest jar begins to hum.

Then the next. Then ten. Then all of them, a thousand glass throats singing the same low note, and the note is not a sound so much as a *pulling*—Wren feels it in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the place where the Ember coils. The memories inside the jars are leaning toward her. She can see it: the colored smoke pressing against the glass from within, straining, reaching, the way flowers lean at a window.

*Feed me,* says the fire. *I have burned so long on scraps. One child's worth of nothing. Feed me what they stole, and I will show you what we were. What we did. What we will do again.*

"No," Wren whispers.

*You said yes once,* the fire says gently, terribly. *In the burning time. You always say yes. That is why I choose children. Children say yes.*

"That wasn't me. That was—" But she doesn't know who it was. That's the cruelty of it. The Ember has had bearers before—Mother Quill's book said so, nations burned for them—and every one of those bearers is ash and story now, and Wren is only the latest jar the fire decided to keep itself in.

The first jar shatters.

Not falls—*shatters*, burst from inside, and the memory comes out like a bird released: a swirl of gold-and-green light that spirals once around the room, screaming without a mouth. Wren hears it anyway. Everyone hears it. A man's voice, layered over itself a hundred times: *the orchard, the orchard, my daughter under the orchard tree, don't take the orchard—*

And then the light finds Wren, and instead of passing into her, it *ignites*.

She screams. Not from pain—from fullness. It is like drinking a river. The memory floods through the Ember and the Ember floods through her, and for one blinding instant Wren is a man standing under an apple tree with his daughter on his shoulders, and then she is herself again, kneeling on cold stone, blood in her nose, the memory gone through her like a swallowed star.

The other jars are shaking now. All of them.

"Fenna's codes," Orsen is shouting somewhere far away. "Pim, the far wall, there's more shelves—we have minutes—"

"You don't understand," Wren tries to say, but her voice comes out doubled, hers and the fire's braided together, and the words come out in two languages at once. "Orsen. ORSEN."

Orsen turns. And Wren watches the thief's face do something she has never seen it do: go completely blank with fear. Not for herself. Wren has learned to read Orsen the way she reads the old language—slowly, carefully, word by word. Orsen Pemberton is afraid of exactly one thing, and it is not dying. It is being responsible for someone else's ending.

Wren is about to be someone's ending. Maybe everyone's.

*Look what I am,* the fire says, spreading its wings inside her ribs. She can feel them now—wings, or the idea of wings, vast and folded tight, made of every war ever fought with borrowed memory. *This is what they fed me for. Decades of theft, funneled into my keeping. Corvane thinks he owns me. Corvane thinks a god-fire can be kept in a ledger. Let him come. Let him see what his harvest grows.*

Somewhere above them, alarms begin. Boots on stairs. The Guild has noticed its vault singing.

A second jar bursts. Then five. The ghost-voices layer into a storm—*my wedding day, my mother's hands, the road home, the road home, I never told him, I never told him*—and each released memory whips toward Wren like iron filings to a lodestone, and each one burns through her and leaves her a little less solid. She grips a shelf to stay upright. Her fingers leave scorch marks on the wood.

Sable is shaking her. Signing frantically. Wren can't read the signs anymore; the old language is rising in her like floodwater, drowning everything else. She thinks distantly, absurdly, of Fenna locked upstairs somewhere, leaving coded messages in her own emptied head, and of Aldric holding a pass alone against riders, and of snow falling on a rebuilt house none of them have built yet. Things that haven't happened. Things the fire will burn away if it gets loose.

*I could be gentle,* the Ember offers. *I could take only the Guild's jars. I could leave your thieves their little lives. Is that not mercy?*

"It's not mercy," Wren gasps. "It's a menu."

And the fire laughs, and the laugh cracks three more jars, and the storm of voices becomes a hurricane, and down the stairwell come the boots, many boots, and Corvane's voice ringing off the stone: *"Seal the vault! Seal it with them inside!"*

That decides her.

Because Wren has spent her whole life being a container. Carried from hand to hand, kingdom to kingdom, spoken about in languages she wasn't given translations for. The Ember chose her because she was small and said yes. Well. She is done saying yes.

There is one person in this world who steals things back. Who takes what powerful people keep and gives it away. Who looked at a mute child barefoot in the snow and didn't see a vessel—saw a kid, and made soup, and named her after a bird.

Wren tears free of Sable's grip and crosses the vault floor on legs that are mostly fire now, trailing embers with every step, and she reaches Orsen, and she takes the thief's face in both her scorched hands, and she says—in the common tongue, in plain words, so there can be no misunderstanding:

"Steal it."

Orsen stares at her. "Wren—"

"Steal it out of me. Like you steal everything. You're the best thief in Hollowmere—you told me, you bragged about it, you stole a man's daughter's face right out of his head—so steal this. Steal the fire. I can't hold it and neither can they, but you—" her voice breaks, and she lets it, "—you're the only person I've ever met who gives things back. Take it and give it nowhere. Take it and keep it. Take it and let it eat whatever it wants of yours, because you've got more to spare than anyone I know, and because—"

*Because if it burns me empty,* she doesn't say, *you'll carry me too. You'll remember me when I can't.*

"—because I'm asking," she finishes. "Nobody's ever asked you before. They've paid you, they've hunted you, they've needed you. I'm *asking*."

The vault groans. Jars burst in chains now, dozens at a time, the ghost-storm shrieking, and the doors at the top of the stairs boom as something heavy hits them from outside, and Corvane is screaming orders, and the Ember inside Wren rises like a tide saying *yes, yes, say yes, you always say yes—*

Orsen looks at her. Really looks. And Wren watches the fear drain out of the thief's face and something else settle in its place—that flat, bright calm Wren has only seen once before, the night enforcers came to the tenements and Orsen stood in the doorway with a kitchen knife and told four armed men the price of the door.

"All right," Orsen says. "Little bird. Hold still."

And Wren does the hardest thing she has ever done, harder than crossing the ash road, harder than the border, harder than silence:

She opens.

She unclenches the grip she has kept since before memory, since the burning time, since some dying bearer pressed the last spark of the war-magic into a newborn and ran—and she lets the Ember rise, all the way up, out of her chest, up her throat, a column of orange light pouring out of her open mouth and eyes like dawn coming out of a well—

—and Orsen Pemberton reaches into the light with both hands, the way she reaches into a coat pocket, the way she reaches into a stranger's sleeping mind—

—and steals a god.

The vault goes white.

* * *

Wren wakes on the floor with Sable's arms around her and a taste like cinders in her mouth, and the first thing she understands is the silence.

The jars are dark. All of them. Every shelf, every row—thousands of memories, snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers. The ghost-storm is gone. The humming is gone. Even the Ember's voice is gone, and its absence rings louder than its presence ever did, a great hollow quiet where a furnace used to be.

She is empty. Light. She weighs almost nothing. She starts to cry and can't tell if it's grief or relief, and decides, in the way of children, that it is both.

Above her, the vault doors have come off their hinges. Through the dust, silhouettes: Guild soldiers, frozen mid-charge, staring at the impossible dark shelves. And past them, at the center of the ruined room, stands Orsen.

Orsen is glowing. Faintly, guttering, like a lamp running out of oil—but glowing. Lines of ember-light trace her arms and throat, pulsing slow, and her eyes when she turns them on Wren are banked coals, orange going gray, orange going gray.

She smiles at Wren. It's a good smile. Warm. Right.

"Wren?" Orsen says. And then, softer, puzzled, like a woman waking in an unfamiliar bed: "Do I... know you?"

The doors at the top of the stairs explode inward, and Corvane comes through the smoke with fire in his fists and murder in his ledger-heart, and Orsen turns to face him with a god in her veins and no idea whose voice just spoke her name—

—and Wren scrambles up off the floor, screaming the only name she has left to give: "ORSEN! HER NAME IS ORSEN! REMEMBER IT'S ORSEN!"

But Orsen is already moving toward the fight, and the light in her is already hungry, and Wren understands, with terrible clarity, what she has done:

She has asked a thief to steal the one thing that eats what it steals.

And the thief said yes.
