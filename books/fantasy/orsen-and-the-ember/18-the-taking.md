## 18. The Taking

The vault came apart around them like a held breath finally released.

Ghost-voices poured from the shattered jars—thousands of stolen yesterdays igniting in the dark, screaming in a hundred tongues, laughing in others, some of them just weeping the names of people long dead. The harvest had been triggered. Fenna's codes had done their work too well, or Pim's hands had turned one lock too many, and now every memory the Guild of Kept Things had hoarded for sixty years was burning at once.

And above it all, tearing free of Wren like a root ripped out of shallow soil, hung the Ember.

It did not look like fire. Fire was kind. It looked like the moment before fire—the white-hot instant when a thing decides whether to be warmth or annihilation—and it filled the vault's central chamber with a light that had no source and no mercy.

Wren stood beneath it, arms raised, her small body shaking, her mouth open in a soundless scream. The Ember was leaving her. It had chosen this moment—chosen the storm, chosen the abundance of fuel—to shed its vessel and become what it had always meant to become.

A weapon. A god. A war, reignited.

"Orsen." Wren's voice cracked through the chaos, and it was the first time Orsen had ever heard her speak plainly, without the old language, without the coal's interference. Just a child's voice, raw with terror. "Take it. *Please.* Before they do."

Corvane was already moving.

He stood on the far side of the chamber, silhouetted against the burning shelves, and he wasn't reaching for the Ember. He was reaching for the levers—the great brass controls that fed the harvested memories into the vault's heart, the machinery of feeding. Sixty years of stolen pasts, and he meant to pour them all into the fire and ride whatever came out.

"You're too late to stop it," Corvane called over the roar. His voice was calm. That was the worst part—it was always calm. "But you're not too late to matter, Orsen. Help me shape it, and I'll give you back everything. Your name. Your mother. The girl you were before Hollowmere ate her."

Orsen didn't answer him. She was looking at Wren.

The child was collapsing now, the last of the Ember draining out of her, and Orsen understood—with the terrible clarity that comes only once in a life—that if she didn't move in the next three seconds, the thing she loved most in the world would be an empty shell standing next to a god made of everyone's grief.

She moved.

* * *

Crossing the chamber felt like wading upstream through other people's lives. Ghost-memories slammed into her as she ran: a wedding in a village that no longer existed, a soldier's last morning, a mother teaching a daughter to bake bread. Each one tried to be hers. Each one pressed against her skull like a beggar at a door, whispering *remember me, remember me, I'll be yours, take me instead.*

"No," Orsen said, to all of them. "I have my own."

It was almost true.

The Ember hung above Wren like a second sun, and as Orsen reached up toward it, she felt it notice her. That was the only word for it. It *noticed*—the way a predator notices movement, the way a drowning man notices a rope. It had spent centuries inside vessels: girls, kings, saints, children. It knew what bearers were for. It knew how to take.

*You will burn,* it said, in no language at all. The meaning simply arrived, the way heat arrives before flame. *Everything you are. Everything you keep. I am hunger wearing light, little thief, and you are offering me a feast.*

"I know," Orsen said.

She took it.

* * *

There was no pain at first. There was something worse: there was *room*. The Ember poured into her chest like water finding a crack, and it fit, and the fitting was the horror, because she could feel how much of her it intended to occupy.

Then the burning started.

Not her skin. Her skin was fine. It was the memories—the real ones, the ones no one had ever jarred or sold, the ones she'd built herself in the valley that forgets. She felt the Ember sort through them with a connoisseur's delicacy, tasting each one, deciding what to consume first.

It started with the oldest.

Her first night in Hollowmere—the rain, the borrowed room, the relief of borders where magic died—went soft at the edges and then went out, like a candle pinched between wet fingers. She gasped. She could feel the hole where it had been, smooth and clean, already healing over with nothing.

*Fuel,* said the Ember, satisfied. *Good fuel. You kept your hoard well.*

"Stop," she breathed.

*You knew,* it said. *You chose. Do not insult us both by pretending otherwise.*

She had known. In the half-second before she reached up, she had known exactly what accepting it meant, and she had reached anyway, because Wren was eight years old and deserved to grow up with a self intact, and because someone had to hold the fire, and because—this was the thought that had tipped her, the thought she would cling to until the very end—because she had spent her whole life keeping other people's memories safe. This was only more of the same. Bigger stakes. Worse pay.

She turned to face Corvane.

He had stopped working the levers. He was staring at her with an expression she had never seen on him before—not calm, not calculating. Hungry. The way the clerks looked at a rare vintage. The way collectors look at the one piece missing from the shelf.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you magnificent fool. You actually took it."

"Stay back."

"You're not a bearer, Orsen. You're a vessel with a leak. Every hour you hold it, you lose another year of yourself. In a day you'll be an empty jar with a god inside, and I'll be there to catch you when you fall." He began walking toward her, unhurried, hands open. "All you have to do is let go. Let me hold it. I have systems. I have *discipline*. I won't waste myself the way you're wasting yourself right now."

Behind him, Aldric was struggling up from the floor where the ghost-storm had thrown him, blood sheeting down one side of his face, sword still somehow in his grip. Pim and Sable were dragging Wren toward the corridor. Fenna was nowhere, and Orsen's heart clenched around the absence—*she's alive, she has to be alive, we came back for her*—and then the Ember, sensing strong feeling, drank the worry itself, and suddenly Orsen couldn't remember why she was afraid for someone. Someone was missing. Someone mattered. The name wouldn't come.

No. Not yet. Hold on. Fight first. Grieve later.

Corvane lunged—or rather, he gestured, and the vault answered him, because of course it did; he had spent decades learning this room the way she'd learned locks. A shelf of memory-jars toppled toward her, glass and burning recollection, and Orsen threw herself sideways, and the world slowed into the particular clarity of a fight to the death.

* * *

They fought through a landscape of other people's pasts.

He drove her back across the chamber and she grabbed a fallen jar—a farmer's memory of his wedding day—and hurled it at his head, and it burst against his shoulder in a spray of golden light, and for one absurd instant Corvane wore a stranger's joy like blood. He didn't even flinch. He swept her legs and she went down hard among the broken glass, and his boot came down on her wrist, grinding the small bones, and his face above hers was serene as a ledger's final line.

"The tragedy," he said, "is that you'd have been my best clerk."

Orsen spat at him and drove her free hand's knife up toward his thigh. He twisted away—too slow—and the blade opened him, and he stumbled back, and she rolled to her feet with the Ember roaring in her chest like a furnace door left open.

And mid-lunge, mid-fight, she forgot who he was.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next. The Ember found the memory of Master Corvane—his office, his tea, his voice saying *you have a gift, girl*, twenty years of careful transactions—and it pulled that thread, and the whole tapestry of him unraveled. She was fighting a man in fine grey robes whose name she didn't know, whose face meant nothing, who had called her *magnificent fool* in a tone that suggested history between them. History she no longer had.

She hesitated. One breath. That was all he needed.

His hand closed around her throat and slammed her into the shelving, jars shattering around her head in bursts of other people's childhoods, and he leaned close, and his eyes were wide with something like wonder.

"You don't know me," he whispered. "Look at your eyes. You're trying to remember me and you *can't*." He laughed—a real laugh, delighted, awful. "It eats forward, doesn't it? Newest first? No—no, it eats what burns brightest. And you burned brightest about *me*, you treacherous little—"

She got her knee into him and broke his grip.

The fight resumed, but now it was a stranger's fight, fought by instinct alone. Her body remembered what her mind no longer could: his reach, his favored angles, the way he led with his left. Muscle-memory, the Ember couldn't eat that—or maybe it simply hadn't gotten there yet. She fought him through the smoke and the ghost-light, and somewhere in it she lost her knife and gained a length of broken shelf-brace, and somewhere in it she heard Aldric shouting her name—

Except he wasn't shouting *Orsen*. He was shouting something else, a longer name, a name with weight and history, and she realized with a lurch that it must be hers—her real name, the one from the jar, the one she'd never learned—and the Ember heard it too, and it *took* it. Took the moment. Took the sound of it. Left her with the knowledge that she had a name and the fact of its loss, which was somehow crueler than ignorance.

"Aldric!" she screamed, because that name still worked, that name still meant something—tall, stubborn, blood on his face, owed her a debt she could no longer recall the shape of. "Get Wren out! *Now!*"

"She's not going anywhere without—" He caught sight of her face, and whatever he saw there stopped him cold. "Orsen. What's it doing to you?"

"It's hungry," she said. "Go."

* * *

The Ember ate in courses.

The valley went first after Corvane. Hollowmere itself—the salt flats, the tenements, the border where magic died, the whole geography of her survival—dissolved like sugar in rain. She felt it go and could not mourn it properly because mourning required remembering what was mourned, and the Ember had seen to that. She knew only that a place had mattered. The mattering remained, orphaned, pointing at nothing.

Then the crew. Not all at once—in pieces, cruelly ordered. Fenna's laugh went, though the fact that Fenna had a laugh stayed behind like a coat hook with no coat. Mother Quill's kitchen. Pim and Sable signing *safe* in the lamplight. Each loss was small and total, a room in a house going dark one lamp at a time.

She fought Corvane through all of it. That was the obscene miracle of it: the worse the burning got, the better she fought, because fear sharpened as everything else dulled, and fear was the one thing the Ember couldn't seem to digest. It tried. It wrapped itself around her terror of ending, of dissolving, of becoming a jar with a god inside—and the terror *burned hotter*, and the Ember recoiled, hissing, and reached for easier fare.

So she fed it fear. Deliberately. She looked at Aldric—at the stranger with the kind ruined face who kept putting himself between her and danger—and she made herself feel the love she could no longer source, and the love became fear of losing him, and the fear became armor, and the armor bought her another minute.

Corvane understood what she was doing. She watched him understand it. Watched the wonder drain out of his face and leave calculation behind.

"You're weaponizing your own destruction," he said. He was bleeding freely now, limping, backing toward the lever-console. "That's the most Guild thing you've ever done. Come work for me. Truly. Last offer."

"Last offer," she agreed, and charged him.

They hit the console together. Brass levers bent; dials burst; sixty years of feeding-machinery screamed and died under their combined weight. Corvane got his hands around the shelf-brace where it crossed her chest, and for a moment they were locked, face to face, close enough that she could see the sweat cutting tracks through the dust on his skin.

"You'll forget why you're fighting," he told her, gentle as a confessor. "Any moment now. You'll forget the child. You'll forget the knight. You'll forget the valley and the thefts and the oath and every reason any of this matters, and you'll be standing here holding a fire you can't explain, and I will be the only person in the world who can tell you what you are." His voice dropped to a caress. "Let go, and I'll be your memory, Orsen. I'll be the keeper of whatever's left."

And that was when she understood the endgame—not his, hers.

Because he was right. She was forgetting. The names were nearly gone. Wren—she clutched at it—Wren, the girl with the coal, the girl at the door in the snow—but the snow was going, the door was going, the girl's face was going, and soon there would be nothing but the bare fact: *a child. I did this for a child. Why?*

Why?

The question echoed in the hollowed-out cathedral of herself, and the answer, when it came, was the only thing left standing.

*Because I chose it.*

Not the valley. Not the crew. Not the girl's face or the knight's debt or the handler's betrayal. Those were all ash now or ash-in-waiting. But the decision—the decision itself, the moment she had reached up and taken the fire knowing its price—that was not a memory. Memories lived in the past, and the Ember ate the past. The decision was still happening. It was happening *now*, in every second she continued to hold on, renewed with every heartbeat like a flame that feeds on its own burning.

The Ember could consume everything she remembered. It could not consume the choosing, because the choosing was not behind her. It was the ground she stood on.

Orsen smiled at Corvane—a smile with no shared history left in it, nothing personal, pure as a blade—and said:

"You can't keep what I never stored."

She wrenched the shelf-brace free and brought it around in an arc that had no technique in it at all, only will, and it caught him across the temple, and Master Corvane of the Guild of Kept Things went down among his precious jars, and did not get up.

* * *

The vault was dying around her. Shelves collapsed in slow avalanches of glass and light; the ghost-voices were thinning, spent, sinking back into silence or into whatever waits for returned memories. Through the smoke she saw Aldric—she knew his name still, she checked, the way you check a wound—carrying Wren toward the breach in the wall, the girl limp against his shoulder, breathing.

Safe. The word surfaced from somewhere deep, one of the last floats after the shipwreck. Safe. That was the point. Whatever else burned, that was the point.

The Ember raged inside her, furious at the thinning feast, and flared—and in the flare she felt it reach for the final things. The decision. The will. The *now*.

She held on.

It pulled. She held. It pulled harder, and the world went white at the edges, and she thought distantly that this must be what a wick feels like, and she thought—her last coherent thought, or near enough—*let them tell me who I was. Let them love me without reasons. I'll make new ones. I'm good at making things up.*

Then the floor gave way beneath the ruined console, and the vault's heart caved into the dark below, and Orsen Pemberton—whoever that was—fell into the quiet with the fire still clasped to her chest, holding the only thing she had left:

Yes. I chose this. I choose it still.

The dark took her gently, like snow taking a footprint.
