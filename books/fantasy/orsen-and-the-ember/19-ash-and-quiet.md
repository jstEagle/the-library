## 19. Ash and Quiet

The silence came first.

Not the silence of an empty room—Aldric had known that kind all his life, the silence of barracks at night, of a chapel after the last prayer. This was different. This was the silence that follows a held breath, and he understood, in the marrow-deep way a soldier understands things without wanting to, that the whole world had been holding one.

Then Orsen fell, and the world exhaled.

He was moving before his mind caught up. That was the old training, the part of him the Guild had never managed to burn out: the body that answered catastrophe faster than thought. He crossed the collapsing vault floor in six strides, vaulting a cracked console still spitting ghost-light, and dropped to his knees at the edge of the breach where the floor had folded into the dark below.

She lay on a shelf of broken stone maybe ten feet down, cradled against a fallen beam like something set there on purpose. The Ember's light was gone from her. Where moments ago she had burned white to the edges of herself—where her eyes had been twin furnaces and every stolen memory she'd ever carried had flared up like a city burning—she was now just a woman. Small, for someone so much trouble. Pale as ledger paper. The coal that had been Wren's burden was fused into her cupped hands, gone dull and grey as river stone.

Dormant. He knew the word because Corvane had used it once, years ago, standing over a jar of somebody's wedding day, explaining the economics of fire. *It sleeps when it's fed enough. It sleeps deepest when it's fed something it loves.*

Aldric hadn't understood then what that meant. He understood now, looking down at her, and it made him sick in a way no battlefield ever had.

"Orsen." His voice cracked on it. "Orsen."

Nothing. Her chest moved—shallow, slow, but moving—and that was the only answer he got, and it would have to be enough.

Above him the vault groaned. The harvest storm had torn something loose in the Ledger Hall's foundations; he could hear the great shelves of memory-jars shedding their cargo, glass bursting on stone in bright little pops, each one releasing a puff of colored light—a first kiss, a mother's kitchen, a soldier's homecoming—that rose and thinned and vanished. A hundred lives leaking out of their jars and going nowhere. Free, and wasted, and free.

The ceiling above the breach shuddered. Dust sifted down through the light of the dying storm and settled on Orsen's face like ash, like snow, like the snow she'd pulled Wren out of a lifetime ago—or what felt like a lifetime; Aldric had stopped trusting his own sense of time somewhere around the moment the ghost-voices started screaming.

"Wren!" He turned, shouting up into the smoke. "Wren, where are you?"

A small shape detached itself from the shadow of a toppled shelf. The girl came toward him through the drifting motes of freed memory, barefoot again—he'd have sworn she'd been wearing boots an hour ago; nothing about this night obeyed rules—her face streaked with soot and something worse than soot. She wasn't crying. Aldric wished she were. Crying would have meant she was a child again. What she wore instead was the look of someone who had watched a god get born and die in the same hour, and who understood, at seven or eight or however old she was, that she would carry the watching for the rest of her life.

She reached the edge of the breach and looked down at Orsen, and her mouth worked.

No sound came out.

Aldric's stomach dropped. In the valley, Wren's muteness had been a mystery, a wound, a thing they'd all quietly believed would heal when the girl found her words. And she *had* found them—out past the border, on the Road of Ash, her voice had come back syllable by syllable like spring thaw, until she was saying Orsen's name in her sleep, saying *careful* and *hungry* and once, memorably, *Aldric snores.* But now—

Now the Ember had passed through her like a wave through a reed, and the reed was bent flat.

"No," Wren said. It came out as breath shaped like a word. She pressed both hands to her throat, and her eyes went wide with a horror that had nothing to do with the collapsing vault around them. She tried again. "*No.*"

"It's all right." He said it the way you say things to spooked horses, low and even, and he didn't believe a word of it. "Girl. Look at me. It's all right."

It was not all right. Nothing within a mile of them was all right. But she looked at him, and whatever she saw in his face—old scarred Ser Aldric, disgraced twice over, kneeling in the ruin of the Guild's great engine with his heart hammering like a recruit's—was apparently enough, because she stopped clawing at her own throat and pointed instead, urgently, down at Orsen.

*I know,* he wanted to say. Instead he swung himself over the edge of the breach and let go.

The drop was shorter than it looked. He landed badly, took it on his bad knee—the one the Guild's outer circle had given him along with his disgrace, though that was a story for a night with more wine in it—and crossed the rubble to her.

Up close she looked worse. There was frost on her eyelashes. Frost, in a burning vault, in high summer, three hundred leagues south of Hollowmere. The Ember had taken everything warm out of her on its way down into its long sleep, and what remained was a woman cooled from the inside like a forge left to die.

He put two fingers under her jaw. Pulse: slow, deep, stubborn. Like its owner.

"Aldric," Wren breathed behind him, and he nearly jumped out of his skin. She had come down after him somehow—climbed, slid, dropped, he hadn't seen—and she knelt on his left, and the word had sound to it, thin and rusty but real. "*Aldric. Is she—*"

"She's breathing." He got his arms under Orsen—shoulders and knees, the carry you learned dragging men off walls—and stood, and his back screamed its old complaint, and he ignored it the way he'd been ignoring it for twenty years. "Help me get us out of here. Can you walk?"

Wren nodded. Then, testing her own voice like a child poking a loose tooth: "*Yes.*"

"Good girl." He swallowed past the lump that word put in his throat. "Stay where I can see you."

They climbed. It took longer than it should have, because the vault was coming apart in slow motion around them—beams sagging, jars raining down and bursting their little ghosts into the dark—and because Aldric refused to hurry with Orsen's head lolling against his shoulder. Twice he had to turn sideways to let a shelf collapse past them. Once a jar burst close enough to spray him with glass, and the memory inside—a man's terror of deep water, salt-stung and blue—washed over him and was gone, leaving him blinking at nothing.

Somewhere in the smoke behind them, a voice was screaming. Not a ghost-voice. A real one, human, furious, cracking.

Corvane.

Aldric should have kept walking. Every instinct honed by two decades of surviving institutions told him to keep walking, that Master Corvane of the Guild of Kept Things was no longer his problem, that the man was buried alive in the wreckage of his own life's work and the world would not miss him.

But the screaming had a specific quality to it. Aldric had heard men scream like that before, on the walls at Carrowmere, in the Guild's own training yards. It was the scream of a man who has just realized that everything he built was a cage, and that he is inside it.

"Wait here," he told Wren, and set Orsen down gently against a fallen beam, and went toward the screaming, because he was apparently still, despite everything, the kind of fool who went toward screaming.

* * *

He found Corvane in the eastern gallery, pinned under a shelf that had come down across his legs. The master handler was not a young man, and the shelf was not a small one, and between the two facts lay a simple arithmetic that Corvane—who could calculate the market value of a stranger's childhood to four decimal places—had somehow never done.

Around him, scattered across the floor like shells after a feast, lay the jars.

He had been reaching for them. That was the first thing Aldric understood, seeing the scene whole. The man had been crushed reaching for his inventory. His fingers were bloody, and they were closed around a jar—not lifting it, just holding it, the way a drowning man holds driftwood—and inside the jar swirled a memory the color of late afternoon: a garden, a woman's laugh, sunlight through leaves. Somebody's yesterday. Maybe several somebodies'. Corvane had spent forty years collecting other people's yesterdays, and now he was dying among them, and he was holding one, and his face—

His face was the worst part. Not agony. *Confusion.* The look of a man who had done everything right according to every rule he'd ever been given, and could not understand why the rules had failed him.

"Aldric." Corvane's voice was wet. He coughed, and red came with it. "Ser Aldric. You're—still alive. I did wonder."

"You wondered wrong." Aldric crouched, careful of the glass, careful of the dust still sifting down. Above them another shelf groaned. They had minutes, maybe. "Can you move your legs?"

Corvane actually laughed. It cost him; it came out mostly blood. "No. No, I rather think not." He looked down at himself with mild interest, as if inspecting someone else's misfortune. "You know, I had a speech prepared. For if I ever caught you. Back when you ran. I rehearsed it in the bath." Another cough. "I can't remember any of it now. Isn't that funny? All those years of remembering things for other people, and at the end I can't remember my own speech."

"The shelf hit your head," Aldric said. "That'll do it."

"No." Corvane's eyes drifted to the jar in his hands. The garden-memory swirled, patient, indifferent. "No, I don't think it did." He turned the jar slowly, watching the light move in it. "Do you know what I was doing, when the ceiling came down? Do you know? I was saving these. One at a time. As many as I could carry." His voice went thin and wondering. "Forty years, Aldric. Forty years of telling myself it was stewardship. Custody. Someone had to keep them safe. Someone had to be the keeper." His grip tightened on the jar until the knuckles went white. "And when it all came down, do you know what I reached for?"

Aldric said nothing. The dust fell. Somewhere far above, the storm of ghost-voices had gone quiet at last, and the quiet was enormous.

"I reached for the jars," Corvane whispered. "Not the door. The jars." He laughed again, small and terrible. "So either I was a keeper all along, or I was never anything else. And I find I can't tell which. And I find—" his breath hitched, "—that it doesn't matter, because either way, I'm dying in a room full of other people's lives, and I never had one of my own."

For a moment—one long, dust-sifted moment—Aldric felt something almost like pity. He had served this man's Guild. Had worn its colors, run its errands, told himself the same comfortable stories about custody and keeping. If the shelf had fallen twenty years earlier and thirty miles east, it might have been him under it, clutching somebody's garden.

"There's a door," he said. "East gallery. I can't lift the shelf alone, but I can lever it. Enough for you to crawl. It won't be comfortable."

Corvane looked up at him. And for one naked instant, before the mask of the master handler reassembled itself, Aldric saw the boy the man must have been: some gray-faced clerk's son in Vaskarren's lower warrens, learning early that memories were currency and that he'd been born poor.

"Why?" Corvane rasped. "Why would you?"

"Because someone carried me out of a fire once," Aldric said, "and I've been trying to pay it forward ever since, and I'm running out of people." He got his hands under the shelf's edge. "On three."

He never counted three. Corvane's hand shot out—not toward freedom, not toward the leveraged shelf, but toward a second jar lying a foot beyond his reach, a jar glowing soft gold, and the movement tore something loose inside him that had been torn too many times already. He made a sound Aldric had only heard once before, from a horse with a broken spine, and slumped back against the stone.

The jar rolled from his fingers. It didn't break. Jars from the deep vaults were made to last; that had always been the Guild's pride. It came to rest against his open hand, and the gold light inside pulsed once, gentle as a heartbeat, and Corvane watched it with his fading eyes the way a starving man watches bread through glass.

"*Keep it,*" he mouthed. Or maybe he only thought it loudly. Either way, his hand relaxed, and the garden-memory in the other jar swirled on, unowned, unhurried, waiting for a keeper who was never coming back.

Aldric stood there a moment longer than was wise. Then he picked up the gold-lit jar, because someone should, and set it upright on a dry stretch of stone where the rising water of the broken cisterns wouldn't reach it, and walked away, and did not look back, and heard nothing behind him, and hated himself a little for checking.

* * *

Getting out of Vaskarren took the rest of the night.

The Ledger Hall was burning in places—real fire now, ordinary fire, wood and paper and varnish, almost a comfort after the other kind—and the streets around it were chaos. The harvest storm had been felt for a mile in every direction; half the city had poured out of doors to watch the sky fill with ghost-light, and the other half had barricaded themselves in and were praying loudly to gods who had never once answered in the Ember's lifetime. Nobody looked too closely at a big scarred man carrying an unconscious woman, a silent child trotting at his heel, and a satchel of memory-jars he'd grabbed off a shelf on pure reflex and couldn't have explained under torture.

They went out through the tanners' quarter, where the stink covered everything, and over the canal bridge where the toll-keeper was drunk, and down the pilgrim road until the fires of Vaskarren were a smudge of orange on the black horizon and the night was just night again.

Fenna met them at the third milestone.

She came out of the ditch beside the road like a wraith, and Aldric's hand went to his knife before his brain identified her—lockwright's hands, burn scars on the forearms, the particular way she moved like a door deciding whether to open. Pim and Sable materialized behind her out of the dark, the twins moving in their eerie wordless tandem, and for a moment the five of them—six counting Orsen, who weighed almost nothing now, as if the fire had cooked her down to essentials—stood in the middle of the road and looked at each other across the smoking ruins of everything.

Fenna looked at Orsen in his arms for a long time. Her face did something complicated and settled on grim.

"She did it," Fenna said. Not a question.

"She did it." Aldric's voice came out rougher than he intended. "Vault's down. Corvane's dead. The harvest engine is—" He gestured vaguely backward, at the glow on the horizon, at the whole unspeakable night. "Gone."

"And her?" Fenna stepped closer, peering at Orsen's slack face, the grey stone of the coal fused between her fingers. "Is the fire—"

"Asleep. Dormant. Sealed." He shifted Orsen's weight. "Sealed in her. Whatever that costs." He paused. "It already cost what it cost. She told me once what she was afraid of losing." He didn't say the rest. He couldn't, yet. Saying it would make it true, and it was already true, and some cowardly corner of him wanted one more hour of not-knowing.

Fenna read his face anyway. Lockwrights read locks; Fenna read people. Her jaw tightened.

"We'll figure it out," she said. "We're good at figuring." She turned, briskly, before anything could soften. "There's a farmstead two miles on, abandoned since the war. Roof holds. Water's clean. Pim and Sable swept it at dusk." She glanced at Wren, who had not spoken, not once, in all the miles. Something in Fenna's expression flickered. "And we'll want to talk about the girl. When there's time."

"There's never time," Aldric said. "That's the whole trick of it."

* * *

The farmstead was exactly as promised: four walls, a sound roof, a well, and the particular silence of places where something terrible had happened long enough ago that the land had decided to move on. Aldric laid Orsen on the pallet by the hearth—there was even dry wood; Sable had found a stack under oilcloth—and Wren sat down beside her immediately, cross-legged, hands in her lap, and did not move.

Aldric built the fire. It seemed important, somehow, to build a fire that was only a fire. He stacked the wood carefully, struck the flint three times, coaxed the flame, fed it. Ordinary combustion. Chemistry, if you liked. No ghosts in it, no voices, no hunger. Just warmth, doing what warmth does.

When he turned around, Fenna was standing over Orsen with a healer's frown, and Pim was signing rapidly to Sable, and Wren was still sitting like a small carved figure, watching Orsen breathe.

"How is she?" Aldric asked. "Truly. Don't spare me."

Fenna sat back on her heels. "Alive. Strong pulse. Cold as a well-stone, but warming." She pulled the blanket higher over Orsen's shoulders. "But Aldric. Her hands. Look."

He looked. Between Orsen's fingers, the coal—grey, dead-looking, cool to any eye—sat like a sleeping bird. And as the hearth-fire crackled, the coal pulsed. Once. Softly. In time, Aldric realized with a cold slide in his gut, with Orsen's heartbeat.

"When it wakes," Fenna said quietly, "it'll wake in her. And none of us know what that means. Not the Guild's books, not Mother Quill's burned book, nobody. We saved the world tonight and we don't even know what we saved it *into*."

"Then we learn," Aldric said. "We're good at figuring."

Fenna snorted—half laugh, half exhaustion—and went to check on the twins, and the house settled into the small sounds of survival: water being boiled, bandages being cut, Pim and Sable arguing in sign about watch rotations.

Aldric sat down on the floor beside the pallet, his back to the wall, his sword across his knees out of habit more than fear. Across the room, Wren finally moved. She lay down on the pallet's far edge, curled against Orsen's side the way she had slept in the valley, in the wagon, on the Road of Ash—always touching, always anchored—and her eyes stayed open, fixed on Orsen's face.

Just before sleep took him, Aldric heard her whisper. Her voice was still thin, still rusted, but the words were clear, and they were not for him.

"*I'll remember for you,*" Wren whispered, her small hand resting on Orsen's arm. "*All of it. Everything you gave away. I was there for all of it. So I'll tell you. Every day, I'll tell you, until you know her again—the woman you were. I promise.*"

The fire cracked. The coal pulsed, slow and patient, keeping its owner's time.

Aldric closed his eyes and let the exhaustion take him, and his last waking thought was not of victory—it was of debt. Of the living, who needed feeding and guarding and, in one case, remembering. Of the road ahead, which led north, back toward the valley that forgot, where a house would need rebuilding and a story would need telling and a woman would wake up tomorrow with no idea who any of them were.

He slept. Outside, the first grey light of morning touched the ruined fields, and somewhere far to the north, snow was beginning to fall on Hollowmere.
