## 20. The Girl Who Speaks Now

The first thing Wren understood about silence was that it had weight.

She had carried it for as long as she could remember—since before the valley, since before the snow, since the men in gray robes had pressed the coal into her hands and told her, in the language beneath kingdoms, *hold it and do not speak, for your voice is the door.* She had held it. Through the burning of her village, through the long walk south, through the frozen nights in Hollowmere where even thieves stayed indoors. She had held it when Orsen opened her door at the wrong hour and found a barefoot child standing in the drifts like something the wind had forgotten to put down.

And now, on the ash-strewn road outside ruined Vaskarren, with the Ember sleeping coiled and quiet somewhere behind Orsen's ribs, the silence was gone.

It hadn't left all at once. It had drained out of her the way water drains from a cracked jar—slowly, then all at once, somewhere between Aldric lifting Orsen's body into his arms and the vault collapsing behind them in a roar of dust and ghost-light. One moment Wren's throat was the locked door it had always been. The next, she was screaming Orsen's name into the smoke, and the name came out whole, came out *loud*, came out in a voice she didn't recognize because she had never heard it before.

*Orsen.*

That was the first word. Not her own name—she wasn't sure she had one, or wanted one. Not *help*, though she needed it. Not *fire*, though fire was everywhere. Just the name of the woman who had stolen the unstealable thing, who had taken the war-magic of the old world into herself and let it eat every memory she owned rather than let it eat the world.

Aldric turned at the sound. His armor was scorched; his face was gray with ash and exhaustion; he looked at Wren the way men look at wells in a desert—as if he couldn't quite believe what was coming out of her.

"You can talk," he said. It wasn't a question so much as a man testing whether the ground would hold him.

"I can talk." Her voice cracked on the second word, rusted from disuse. She said it again anyway, just to feel it. "I can talk."

Behind them, Vaskarren burned in the slow, sullen way that cities burn—not with flame but with collapse, ledger halls folding inward, memory-jars bursting in the heat and releasing their contents into the air as brief, bright ghosts: a wedding, a funeral, a first snowfall, a mother's voice singing. The ghosts rose over the rooftops and faded, and for once Wren felt no dread watching memories die in the open air. They were free. That was different from being lost.

"Is she—" Wren started toward Aldric, toward the still shape in his arms.

"She's breathing," Aldric said. "Sleeping, maybe. I don't know what else." His jaw worked. "I've seen men come back from worse. I've seen men not come back from better. I don't know which kind this is."

Wren reached up and touched Orsen's hand where it hung against Aldric's arm. The fingers were warm. Ordinary warm. Not ember-warm, not the terrible furnace-heat of the vault, just the warmth of a living woman who had run too far and paid too much.

Orsen did not open her eyes. But her fingers curled, faintly, around Wren's.

Wren stood there in the ash-fall and made herself a promise so large she could barely hold it in her new voice.

*I will tell you everything you gave away,* she thought. *Every piece. Even if it takes the rest of my life.*

* * *

They walked north for three days.

Aldric carried Orsen the first day, until his knees shook and Fenna—who had found them on the road, hollow-eyed and half-emptied by the extraction chairs but walking anyway, furious anyway—rigged a litter from a broken cart and two stolen blankets. Pim and Sable appeared out of the scrubland on the second morning as if they'd never been anywhere else, silent as always, falling into flanking positions without being asked. Nobody discussed it. Found families rarely do.

Wren walked beside the litter.

She talked almost constantly. She couldn't help it. Every word was a small miracle she kept testing, the way a child tests a loose tooth. She told Aldric about the snow in Hollowmere. She told Fenna about the taste of stolen bread. She described the salt flats, the pilgrim road, the refugees' fires—all the things she had seen and never been able to say—and Fenna listened with the grim patience of a woman relearning how to be a person, occasionally muttering curses at the Guild under her breath, which Wren decided was probably therapeutic for both of them.

But mostly Wren watched Orsen.

Orsen slept. Sometimes she murmured—fragments, half-names, words that trailed off as if she'd reached for something and found only smooth wall. Once she said *Fenna* clearly, and Fenna nearly dropped the litter pole. Once she said *the fence needs mending,* which meant nothing to anyone except, apparently, Orsen, whose face went soft and puzzled at her own words.

Once, waking briefly at dusk, she looked straight at Wren with clear gray eyes and asked, politely, "Who are you?"

Wren had prepared for many questions. That one went through her like a spear.

"I'm—" she began, and stopped, because the true answer was enormous: *I'm the girl you smuggled out of the only safe place you ever knew. I'm the vessel you emptied yourself to seal. I'm the reason you can't remember your mother's voice.* None of it fit in a sentence for a stranger.

"Wren," she said finally. "My name is Wren. You gave it to me."

"Did I?" Orsen smiled—a real smile, warm and unguarded, the smile of a woman with no past to complicate it. "I must have good taste." Then her eyes drifted shut again, and she slept, and Wren knelt in the road-dust beside the litter and cried until Sable put a silent arm around her shoulders and held her there among the shadows of the evening.

That night, camped in the lee of a fallen waystone, Wren took out the piece of charcoal she'd been carrying in her pocket—the ordinary kind, dead and cool, nothing like the coal she'd once held—and began, very carefully, to write.

Not in the language beneath kingdoms. In plain trade-script, the letters Fenna had taught her during the long winter, back when Wren's only voice had been chalk marks on slate. She wrote:

*Her name is Orsen Pemberton. She lives—lived—in Hollowmere, in the tenements above the tannery row. She steals memories at the border roads and sells them to the Guild, and she hates it, and she does it anyway because no one remembers anything for her and someone has to keep the world's pasts safe, even secondhand.*

She wrote until the charcoal wore down to a nub. She wrote:

*She likes her tea bitter enough to stand a spoon in. She cheats at cards and thinks no one notices. She once went hungry for three days rather than sell a soldier's memory of his daughter's face, and then sold it anyway, and cried about it where she thought no one could see.*

Pim, watching over her shoulder, tapped her arm and pointed at the page, then at Wren, then made the gesture they used for *why?*

"Because she'll want to know," Wren said aloud, and marveled again at saying aloud. "Someday. When she's ready. And if my memory ever fails—if the Ember ever wakes again, if anything takes me the way it took her—then the paper will remember instead."

Pim considered this. Then he fetched her a fresh stick of charcoal from the supply pack, set it in her hand with great ceremony, and sat down beside her to guard her while she wrote.

* * *

On the fifth day, Orsen woke properly.

They were camped in the ruins of a pilgrim shrine, its stone saint long since toppled, its alcoves good shelter from the wind. Wren was sitting close to the fire, writing the eleventh page of what she had privately begun calling *the book of Orsen*—a bundle of loose pages tied with string, growing thicker by the day—when she heard the litter creak.

Orsen was sitting up. Actually sitting up, propped against the cart frame, looking around at the camp with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like: four strangers, one child, one ruined city on the horizon, and no idea how any of it connected to her.

"Good," Aldric said quietly, from where he sat sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening. "You're awake. How much do you remember?"

"Remember?" Orsen frowned. "I remember waking up. I remember... fire, I think. A lot of fire. And a choice." Her hand drifted to her chest, pressed flat over her sternum, as if checking that something was still there—or still gone. "I remember deciding. Everything after that is..." She gestured vaguely. "Fog."

"And before?" Fenna asked, from the other side of the fire. Her voice was carefully neutral. Wren recognized the care in it; Fenna had lost chunks of herself to the extraction chairs too, and knew exactly what ground she was standing on.

Orsen was quiet a long moment. "There isn't a before," she said at last. "I keep reaching for it and there's just—nothing. Like a room with the furniture taken out. I know the room existed. I can see the marks on the floor where things used to stand. But the things are gone." She laughed, short and startled. "I don't even know what I don't know. Does that make sense?"

"It makes perfect sense," Fenna said, and looked away, and Wren saw her jaw tighten.

Orsen's gaze traveled the circle: Aldric with his knife, Fenna with her bandaged wrists, the twins with their watchful silence, and last, Wren, with her pages. Something moved across her face—recognition without content, the shape of knowing without the substance.

"I know you," she said slowly, to all of them, to none of them. "I don't know *how* I know you. But when Aldric talks, something in me wants to argue with him. When Fenna swears, it sounds like home. And when—" her eyes settled on Wren, and softened, and her brow furrowed like a woman pressing on a bruise to understand it, "—when this one looks at me, I feel like I've failed an appointment I don't remember making."

"You haven't failed anything," Wren said.

Her voice came out steadier than she expected. She set down the charcoal. She crossed the fire-circle and knelt in front of the litter so that she and Orsen were eye to eye, and she saw Orsen take in her face properly for the first time—the thinness, the burn scar along her jaw from the vault's ghost-fire, the eyes that had seen burning cities before she was old enough to have a country.

"You gave something away," Wren said. "Something enormous. You gave it so that the rest of us could keep what we have. I'm not going to tell you what it was yet—you're not strong enough, and honestly, neither am I. But I'm going to tell you *everything else*. Piece by piece. However long it takes." She held up the bundle of pages. "I've been writing it down. Your story. Our story. So it can't be lost twice."

Orsen stared at the pages. Then at Wren. Then, slowly, she reached out and touched the top sheet, as if checking it was real.

"What's on it?" she asked.

"The important things." Wren managed a smile. "You cheat at cards. You like your tea bitter enough to stand a spoon in. You once named a thief-child after a bird because she wouldn't stop making noise in the rafters."

"That last one sounds like a decision I'd regret."

"Everyone regrets it eventually," Fenna said. "Bird never shuts up now that she's started."

"Bird," Orsen repeated, tasting the word. She looked at Wren again, and this time the puzzlement in her face had changed into something gentler—something that didn't need the missing memories to function. "Okay," she said. "Tell me more."

So Wren told her.

She told her about Hollowmere, the valley that forgets, where magic dies at the border and memories fade like breath on glass. She told her about the Guild of Kept Things, and Master Corvane with his ink-stained fingers and his voice like a closing ledger. She told her about a knock at the wrong hour, a barefoot girl in the snow, a coal that never cooled. She watched Orsen listen with her whole body, leaning forward, hungry for herself the way starving people are hungry for bread—and Wren understood, with a tenderness that almost hurt, that this was what she had been made for. Not to carry fire. To carry *this*. The saved child, keeper of the savior's story, telling it back one piece at a time until the woman who had given everything remembered, if not the facts, then at least the shape of her own goodness.

When she got to the part about the taking—about Orsen opening her arms and letting the Ember pour in, about the fight where she forgot Corvane mid-struggle and forgot Aldric's name and forgot the valley itself, holding on only to the decision—Orsen went very still.

"I did that?" she whispered. "I chose that?"

"You chose it," Wren said. "You were terrified. You did it anyway."

Orsen looked down at her own hands, turning them over, as if expecting to find scorch marks or answers. "And I don't get to know what I lost."

"No," Wren said gently. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. But—" she took a breath, and took Orsen's hand, and laced their fingers together the way Orsen had once done for her, in a snowbound tenement, on the worst night of both their lives, "—you don't have to remember being loved to be loved. We're going to do that part for you. All of us. For as long as it takes."

For a moment nobody spoke. The fire crackled. Somewhere in the dark, Pim and Sable were keeping watch, silent as the valley they'd all left behind.

Then Orsen squeezed Wren's hand and said, "Start from the beginning. The knock at the door. Tell me everything."

And Wren did.

* * *

They reached the valley's edge on the ninth day.

Wren felt it before she saw it—that familiar deadness in the air, the place where the world's hum flattened into silence. Hollowmere's border. The line where magic came to die. Beyond it lay the salt flats, the gray tenements, the life Orsen had built out of other people's yesterdays.

Aldric stopped the litter at the boundary stone and looked back at the group—at Fenna flexing her healing hands, at the twins, at Wren, at Orsen, who had insisted on walking the last mile and now stood gazing at the mist-shrouded valley with the expression of a woman reading a letter addressed to someone else.

"Well," Aldric said. "Home, such as it is."

"It's not home," Fenna said. "Not yet. We're going to build one." She nodded eastward, to where the land rose out of the flats—a fold of hills, a stream, a stand of wind-bent trees. Far enough from the Guild's reach. Close enough to the valley that the Ember, if it ever stirred, would find nothing to feed on. "There. Water, timber, and no neighbors for twenty miles. I checked."

"We're staying *there*?" Orsen asked. "All of us? Together?" She sounded less alarmed than baffled, as if kindness were a language she'd heard of but never studied.

"You don't have to," Wren said quickly. "If it's too strange—"

"It's not that." Orsen was still looking at the hills. "It's just—I woke up nine days ago owning nothing but a decision, and somehow I've acquired a knight, a lockwright, two thieves, and a bird who writes books about me." She glanced down at Wren, and the corner of her mouth lifted. "I'd like to see the house we build. I'd like to see what comes next." She hesitated, then added, quieter: "And I'd like you to keep telling me the story. There are parts I still don't understand. Parts about me. About why everyone looks at me like I'm carrying something heavy."

Wren's stomach tightened. Because there *was* something heavy. She had felt it for days now, faint but unmistakable, like heat through a wall: the Ember, dormant in Orsen's chest, dreaming. And lately—just in the last day, just since they'd neared the valley—it had begun, very softly, to hum.

She said none of this. Instead she took Orsen's hand, and together they walked down toward the flats, toward the hills beyond, toward the house that didn't exist yet and the family that had already begun building it.

That night, while the others argued cheerfully about roof angles, Wren sat apart with her pages and added a final line to the newest one, in careful script:

*Day nine. She smiled today. Really smiled. But the coal in her chest is warm again, and getting warmer.*

*Whatever comes next, I'll be ready. I learned from the best thief in the world how to keep what matters.*

*Even if I have to steal it back from the fire itself.*
