## 21. Strangers Who Stay

**Ser Aldric**

The house went up wrong three times before it went up right.

Aldric blamed the ground. The valley's edge was all shale and old salt, the kind of earth that argued with every post you drove into it. But he knew, in the honest part of himself that had survived two armies and one disgrace, that the ground wasn't the problem. The problem was that none of them had ever built anything meant to last. Thieves built hides. Knights built siegeworks. Lockwrights built things designed to come apart in clever ways. Nobody in their ragged company had ever once put a roof over a person they intended to keep.

So the third frame came down, and Aldric sat on a stump with a hammer across his knees and watched Orsen across the clearing.

She was splitting kindling. She'd taken to it without being asked, the way she took to everything—quick hands, quick eyes, no questions about why five strangers had dragged her to this nowhere place and insisted she stay. When she caught him looking, she gave him a polite nod. The nod of a woman acknowledging a neighbor.

It went through him like a spear through wet leather.

*Ser Aldric,* she used to say, when she wanted something. *Ser Aldric, you great sad wall of a man, hold this.* She'd stolen his worst memory out of his head years ago and kept it, kept it like a coal banked in ash, and he had never told her what that was worth because telling her would have meant admitting what he was. Now she looked at him and saw a large man who was good with timber and bad with words, and nothing else. No debt between them. No history. Just a nod.

He picked the hammer back up.

"You're setting the post crooked," Fenna said, appearing at his elbow the way she did, soundless as a lockpick.

"I'm setting it straight."

"You're setting it sentimental." She squinted at the frame, then at Orsen, then back. "You want the roof high enough that she can stand on a chair and still not touch the beams. You've been building it for a woman your own height."

Aldric said nothing, which from him was an admission.

Fenna patted his arm with her scarred hand—the left one, the one the Guild's extraction rigs had chewed on for eleven days before they broke her out. "Build it however you want," she said. "She'll like it. She likes everything now. That's the worst part, isn't it?"

It was. A woman who liked everything, trusted everyone, laughed easily, slept deeply, and remembered none of them. A woman made new out of the ashes of the one they'd followed into a burning vault.

"She asked me yesterday why I call her captain," Aldric said.

"What'd you tell her?"

"Told her it was a habit." He drove the post home with one hard stroke. "Told her I served under worse officers."

Fenna snorted. "You never served under anyone worse. You deserted them all."

"That too."

Across the clearing, Wren came out of the treeline with an armful of deadwood, and Orsen turned toward her before the girl even made a sound—some instinct older than memory still living in her body—and smiled, wide and unguarded, and helped her stack the wood, and said something that made the girl laugh.

Wren's voice had come back whole. It was the strangest miracle of the whole impossible year: the mute child who had carried the last fire of the old war spoke now in a clear, low voice, and the first word she had ever spoken aloud was a name that meant nothing anymore to the woman who owned it.

Aldric hammered another nail. Built the roof too high. Kept the habit.

**Fenna**

Fenna had been keeping lists since Vaskarren. Not locks this time. Lists.

*Orsen takes her tea bitter, no honey — she thinks she's discovering this preference for the first time. Let her discover it.*

*Orsen hums when she works. Three notes, always the same three notes. Don't tell her it's the song her mother sang. We only know that because of what happened in the vault. Let her think it's hers.*

*Orsen flinches at raised voices but doesn't know why. Never raise a voice near her. Ever.*

The list lived in her head, which was the safest place left in the world, since the Guild had already been inside it once and taken what they wanted and left the rest scoured clean. Eleven days in the extraction chair. She'd come out missing her mother's face, her first theft, the name of the boy she'd loved at fifteen, and the taste of her grandmother's stew. She'd come out keeping everything that mattered about Orsen Pemberton, because she'd hidden it—coded it, buried it under false leads and broken patterns, woven the truth into memories so dull the extractors skimmed past them. A ledger entry here. A rainy afternoon there. The Guild had drunk a river and missed the one fish that mattered.

Now she spent her days doing for Orsen what Orsen had once done for strangers: carrying what couldn't be carried by its owner.

Except this time she didn't sell any of it. This time she gave it away free, in small doses, disguised as nothing at all.

"Pickled herring," she said, dropping a jar into the market basket Pim held open. "Six jars."

"Six?" Sable signed, scandalized, from the other side of the stall. The twins signed even when no one needed them to; their hands thought out loud.

"She eats one jar every four days," Fenna said. "I've counted."

"You count her herring," Pim signed.

"I count everything about her. That's the job now." Fenna paid the fishwife and moved down the row. "Salt pork. The dark bread from the widow Anthea, not the miller's—she says the miller's tastes like punishment, though she doesn't know why she has such a strong opinion about bread. Apples. Only the sour ones. She bites a sweet one, makes a face, gives it to Wren."

Sable loaded the basket with the ease of long practice. Then his hands paused. *Does it hurt?* he signed. *Doing it? Keeping her life for her?*

Fenna considered lying. She was good at lying; it had kept her alive in the vaults. But the twins had sat outside the extraction room door for eleven days, signing to each other through the wall because they couldn't shout, and she owed them truth the way some people owed money.

"Yes," she signed back. "It hurts like hell. And I'd do it until my hands fall off."

Pim took the basket. *Because she stayed,* he signed. *When we were nobody's children. She stayed.*

"Because she stayed," Fenna said aloud, and bought the sour apples.

That evening, back at the house, Orsen found the jars in the pantry and lit up like a festival. "Herring! How did—I was just thinking about herring. Isn't that strange? I don't even know if I like it. Do I like it?"

"You love it," Fenna said, not looking up from the lock she was re-pinning. "Trust me."

"How do you know?"

"Lucky guess."

Orsen opened a jar on the spot, ate three pieces standing up, closed her eyes, and made a small sound of pure happiness. "All right," she said, pointing the fork at Fenna. "Whoever taught you guesses deserves a statue."

Fenna bent over the lock so no one could see her face, and re-pinned a tumbler that didn't need re-pinning, and let the moment pass through her like weather.

**Pim**

Pim kept the stories.

Not on purpose, at first. It started because Wren started it—Wren, who had appointed herself keeper of Orsen's story the night the vault fell, and who told it back to Orsen in pieces, carefully, like feeding a fire one stick at a time. "You were a thief," Wren would say, on the walk down to the creek. "The best one in Hollowmere. You could take a memory so gently the person never felt it leave."

"A thief," Orsen would repeat, delighted. "Me? I can't even cheat at cards. Aldric catches me every time."

"You let him catch you," Wren said. "You always let him catch you. It made him feel useful."

And Orsen would laugh, and Aldric would pretend not to hear from across the yard, and go slightly red around the ears, and Pim would file the whole exchange away in the growing archive behind his eyes.

Because here was the thing about being deaf in a house full of hearing people: you learned to store what others spilled. You watched mouths instead of listening to them. You saw the way Orsen's shoulders dropped half an inch when Wren told a story about her—that was relief, the body recognizing a truth the mind had lost. You saw the way she reached for a hammer left-handed when she was tired, though she worked right-handed, some ghost of an old habit. You saw her pause at the top of the hill on cold evenings, looking north toward the valley, toward Hollowmere's grey line on the horizon, with an expression like a word on the tip of the tongue.

Pim began writing the stories down. Not in words—he and Sable had never fully trusted words—but in signs pressed into clay tablets they fired in the kiln Sable had built, so the stories would be baked permanent. *The soldier's daughter. The knock at the wrong hour. The coal that never cooled. The knight who owed her. The oath in the tenement cellar, six thieves and a child, hands linked in the dark while the enforcers walked overhead.*

One evening Orsen found the tablet rack drying by the hearth. She turned the tablets over, frowning at the sign-shapes she couldn't read.

"What are these?"

"Our book," Sable signed, then, remembering, said it aloud too, in his careful flat voice. "Stories."

"Whose stories?"

Wren answered, from the corner where she was mending a net. "Yours."

Orsen went very still. "Mine? But I don't—" Her hand drifted up to her temple, the gesture she made whenever the hole in her head made itself known. "I don't remember any of these."

"We do," Pim signed, and Sable said it. "That's enough for now."

Orsen looked at the tablets a long time. Then she set the top one back down gently, the way you set down something breakable, and said, "Will you tell me one? Someday? When I'm ready?"

"We'll tell you all of them," Wren said. "As many times as it takes."

"And if I never remember?" Orsen asked. It wasn't self-pity in her voice. It was practical, a thief assessing a job. "If they're gone for good?"

"Then we'll tell them again," Wren said simply. "Forever, if we have to. A story doesn't die just because one listener forgets it. That's the whole point of stories."

Orsen nodded slowly, accepting the logic the way she seemed to accept everything—gratefully, completely, as if kindness were a currency she'd only recently learned existed. Then she pointed at the tablet showing six linked figures. "Which one am I?"

"The one holding everyone together," Sable said.

"That seems unlikely," Orsen said, laughing, and went to check the herring.

Pim waited until she was gone, then added a new line to the newest tablet, pressing the signs deep: *She asks which one she is. Every time. We will answer every time.*

* * *

**Sable**

Sable took the night watch, same as he had since the valley. Old habits. In Hollowmere he'd watched for Guild enforcers; here at the edge of the magicless country he watched for—nothing, mostly. Deer. Weather. Once, a peddler who'd gotten lost and left by morning.

But tonight, watching, he saw Orsen come out of the house barefoot into the first snow of the season.

She stood in the falling white with her arms wrapped around herself, head tipped back, letting it land on her face. And Sable understood, watching her, something he'd been turning over for weeks: she wasn't sad. Everyone else in the house was sad—they grieved her in shifts, quietly, like men taking turns carrying a wounded comrade. But Orsen herself was not sad. She was *new*. She woke up every morning into a life with no shadows in it, surrounded by people who loved her, and to her it was all gift, all grace, all luck. She didn't mourn the woman she'd been because she'd never met her.

Only they had met her. Only they carried the weight of her.

He signed to himself, alone in the dark, the way he did when his thoughts got too big for his chest—a running commentary, a conversation with his own hands.

*She is happy. Is it a lie if she doesn't know what was traded for it?*

*No. Happiness doesn't need provenance. It's the one thing that was never stolen.*

Snow gathered on her shoulders. After a while Wren came out with a blanket and put it around her, and Orsen pulled the girl close, and they stood together in the white falling quiet, and Sable decided that was enough philosophy for one night.

He was about to go in when he saw it.

Down the valley road, far off, where the grey line of Hollowmere met the dark of the sky—a light. Not a lantern. Lanterns swung and guttered. This light burned steady and low and orange, the color of banked coals, the color of a fire that had learned patience.

It moved along the border. Slowly. Deliberately. Like something searching the seam of the world.

Sable's hands went still. Then, silently, he woke Pim with a touch, and signed: *Light on the border. Fire-colored. Watch.*

Pim watched. They watched together, twin silhouettes against the snow, while the orange light crawled the length of the valley's edge and stopped—stopped directly opposite their clearing, directly opposite the house where Orsen slept her dreamless sleep—and held there, steady as a held breath.

Then, faint across the miles, so faint that hearing men would never have caught it, came a sound on the wind. A voice, or many voices layered into one, singing three notes.

Three notes. Always the same three notes.

Sable turned toward the house, toward the window where Orsen slept, and his hands, moving fast and silent in the snow-light, signed the only thing that mattered:

*Wake her.*
