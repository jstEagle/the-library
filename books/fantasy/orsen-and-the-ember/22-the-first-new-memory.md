## 22. The First New Memory

The snow came down the way it always did in Hollowmere — sideways first, then all at once, as if the sky had been holding its breath and finally gave up.

Orsen stood at the window of the rebuilt house with a cup of something warm she didn't remember learning to like, watching it fall. The house was new to her. Everything was new to her. That was not an observation so much as the entire shape of her life now: a woman standing in a doorway, holding a cup, knowing nothing about how she'd come to be either one.

She had been told the facts, of course. They told her every morning, in a way, just by living near her. There was a girl called Wren who slept in the loft and spoke in her sleep in a language that made the fire lean toward her. There was a big man with a soldier's hands named Aldric who fixed things before they broke and never once asked for thanks. There was Fenna, who swore at locks the way other people prayed to gods, and two silent twins who communicated entirely in elbows and eyebrows. These people lived with her. These people loved her.

She did not know why.

That was the part nobody could fix, though they'd all tried in their different ways. Aldric tried by doing — by chopping wood she hadn't asked for and mending fences she didn't know were hers. Fenna tried by talking, filling the silence around Orsen with stories she clearly hoped would catch somewhere, like seeds thrown at frozen ground. Wren tried by writing. Every night the girl sat at the table with a stub of charcoal and filled page after page with an account of a life Orsen couldn't remember: a valley where memories faded, a Guild that bought them, a child barefoot in the snow, a vault beneath a city of ledgers, a fire taken into the body like a swallowed star.

Orsen read the pages. She read them more than once. And each time she finished, she felt the same thing she felt looking at her own hands — that they belonged to someone, just not yet to her.

"You're doing it again," said Fenna from the doorway, "the staring."

"I'm allowed to stare. It's my window."

"It's everyone's window. I paid for half the glass." Fenna came over and looked out too, shoulder to shoulder, breath fogging the pane. Below, in the white yard, Aldric was fighting the fence again. The fence was winning. It usually did. He'd knocked three posts back into their holes this morning and the snow had already shrugged two of them loose, and now he stood in the falling white with his sleeves rolled up despite the cold, hammering with the patient fury of a man arguing with geography.

"Why does he do that?" Orsen asked. Not why he mended the fence — why he mended *this* fence, in *this* cold, for a house he could presumably have anywhere.

"Because the fence is broken," Fenna said, which was not an answer, and both of them knew it wasn't, and neither of them said anything else about it.

* * *

Wren came down from the loft just after midday, hair full of static, dragging her blanket behind her like a tail. She was nine now, or thereabouts; nobody had ever known exactly, and she'd stopped counting birthdays in favor of counting snowfalls, of which this was, by her reckoning, the third since the mountain of ledgers came down.

"Morning," she said, though it was afternoon. She said it to Orsen specifically, the way she always did — first thing, every day, aimed like an arrow. *Morning, Orsen.* As if the name needed practicing. As if saying it kept it alive somewhere outside herself.

"Good afternoon," Orsen corrected gently.

"Morning," Wren insisted, and grinned, gap-toothed these days, and Orsen felt the grin land somewhere under her ribs in a place that had no memories attached to it and somehow hurt anyway. That was the strangest discovery of her new life: that feeling did not require remembering. Love, apparently, could live in the body without a single fact to stand on. She loved this child the way you love a warmth you've walked into from the cold — completely, immediately, without knowing when the cold began.

"Is it still coming down?" Wren asked, pressing her face to the glass.

"Sideways."

"Best kind." She spun away toward the kitchen. "Fenna, can we—"

"No."

"—you didn't hear what I was going to ask."

"The answer's still no."

"It was going to be *may we have honey bread*, and now it's going to be *may we have honey bread and also you're a miser*."

There was a pause. Then Fenna sighed the sigh of a woman defeated by superior tactics and started pulling flour down from the shelf, and Wren pumped her fist in silent triumph, and Orsen stood at the window and thought, with sudden clarity, *I want to keep this.*

Not forever. She couldn't keep anything forever; she'd learned that much about herself, whatever else was gone. But the moment was happening — right now, this exact arrangement of light and flour-dust and a gap-toothed girl gloating over honey bread — and Orsen understood, in the way you understand your own heartbeat, that moments only became yours if you decided they had. Nobody was going to steal this one for her. Nobody was going to sell it, or jar it, or file it in a vault beneath a ledger hall. If it was going to be hers, she would have to take it herself, honestly, the way ordinary people did: by paying attention.

So she paid attention.

* * *

The afternoon arranged itself around the baking. Pim and Sable materialized out of the snow like it had produced them, shaking off white, carrying — for reasons never explained and never needing to be — a sled, a coil of good rope, and one extremely indignant hen. The hen was installed in a crate by the stove, where it spent the rest of the day radiating grievance. Sable mimed something elaborate involving the rope and the fence and possibly the hen, and Pim nodded once, gravely, and went out to help Aldric, and whatever plan had been communicated was never revealed to the rest of them.

The bread rose. The house filled with the smell of it, and with steam, and with the particular golden light that snow makes even at dusk — light with no source, coming from everywhere, softening every edge until the yard beyond the window looked less like a place than like a memory of one.

Orsen watched Aldric through that light. He'd finally beaten the last post into submission and was now wrapping the rope around the rails in some configuration Sable had prescribed, tongue between his teeth, snow gathering on his shoulders like the start of a second, quieter man sitting on him. At one point he straightened up, pressed a hand to his lower back, and looked directly at the window — at her — and raised the hammer in something between a salute and a surrender.

And Orsen, without deciding to, raised her cup back.

It was such a small thing. A cup lifted through fogged glass to a man whose name she'd had to relearn twice. But Wren saw it, and went very still, and then smiled in a way that made Orsen suspect — not remember, just suspect — that gestures like that had happened before. Many times. That there was a whole history of cups raised across windows, and jokes with the same shapes, and silences that fit together like well-worn joinery. The history was gone. Its gravity remained. She orbited it daily.

"Honey bread's ready," Fenna announced, "and if anyone tells Mother Quill we ate it without her I will deny everything and also lie."

* * *

They ate at the long table with the mismatched chairs, because the matching ones had burned with the old house — Orsen knew this the way she knew most things now, secondhand, from Wren's pages, from Fenna's stories told casually over dishes. The old house had burned. She had built this one. Those two facts sat side by side inside her without connecting, like neighbors who'd never been introduced.

Mother Quill arrived as the bread came out, because Mother Quill always arrived as the bread came out, a coincidence so reliable it had become law. She was ancient and sharp and she treated Orsen with a courtesy that had edges — the courtesy of a woman who remembered everything Orsen had forgotten and had decided, deliberately, never to weaponize it. Sometimes Orsen caught her watching her across a room with an expression like reading a book she'd loved and lent out and gotten back water-stained. Fondness and grief in the same look. Orsen had stopped asking what the expression meant. The answer was always the same: *You used to know.*

But tonight Mother Quill only ate bread and complained about the weather and told a story about a lockwright she'd known in her youth who'd once picked a chapel door during her own wedding, and Fenna choked on her bread and denied everything, and the twins conducted an entire argument in eyebrow, and Wren laughed.

That was the thing. That was the thing Orsen had been waiting for all day without admitting it, the way you wait for a bell you've heard ring before even though you can't say when.

Wren laughed — really laughed, head thrown back, the laugh of a child who has been given back her own voice and intends to use it extravagantly — and the sound went through the warm room like a struck bell goes through water, and the hen squawked in solidarity, and Aldric's low chuckle rolled underneath it like a foundation stone, and Fenna laughed too, helplessly, swearing between laughs, and the snow fell past the windows in fat slow flakes, and the fire leaned toward Wren the way it always did, gently, like a dog toward its favorite person.

*This,* Orsen thought. *This one. I'll take this one.*

She did it deliberately, the way she imagined other people did it without thinking. She put down her bread. She let the moment come into her eyes instead of just her ears — the steam, the gold light, the gap teeth, the swear words, the snow, the hen's outrage, the man at the end of the table who owed her a debt she'd never collect because she couldn't remember lending. She held all of it at once, the way you hold water in cupped hands, knowing most of it will escape and refusing to let that stop you from trying.

And then — quietly, inside, in the place where a self lives — she said: *Mine. This one is mine. I choose it.*

No guild. No jars. No price. No one stealing it for someone else's hunger. Just a woman at a table, taking her own past as it happened, one instant wide, freely given and freely kept.

It was such a small thing to own. It was everything.

* * *

Later, when the others had drifted to beds and the fire had settled into its low orange mutter, Wren appeared at Orsen's elbow with charcoal and paper, as she did most nights.

"Tonight's page?" she asked. It was their ritual, or had become one — Wren writing the day into the record of a life Orsen couldn't hold, so that at least the record existed somewhere outside the failing vessel of her mind.

"Tonight's page," Orsen agreed.

Wren wrote. Orsen watched the charcoal move and found, for the first time in a long while, that she wasn't sad about it. The pages weren't a wound anymore. They were a gift — a girl spending her returned voice on another woman's history, keeping it safe, telling it back piece by piece, patient as snowfall. Someday, maybe, the pages and the woman would meet in the middle. Maybe they wouldn't. Either way, the story existed. It was being kept by someone who loved it.

"What are you writing?" Orsen asked.

Wren considered, then turned the page so Orsen could see. In careful letters:

*Tonight Orsen raised her cup to Aldric through the window, and he raised his hammer back, and nobody said anything, and everybody understood. First new memory. She chose it herself. I saw her choose it.*

Orsen looked at the words a long time.

"You saw that?"

"I always see," Wren said simply. "It's my job now. You saved me. Somebody has to save you back." She hesitated, charcoal hovering. "There's one more thing. For tomorrow's page. Do you want to hear it tonight?"

Outside, the snow kept falling, unhurried, covering the mended fence and the woodpile and the whole recovering valley, laying everything down soft.

"Tell me," Orsen said.

Wren leaned close, the way she did when the words mattered, and her voice dropped into the hush of someone opening a door that had been shut a long time: "Tomorrow it's going to snow hard enough to close the pass. And a traveler is going to knock at our door — someone from outside, someone who says she knew you before. And Orsen—" the girl's eyes were bright, scared, certain, "—she's going to ask you a question that none of us can answer for you. She's going to ask you who you want to be."

The fire shifted. The hen muttered. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked under Aldric's weight as he checked the latch, the way he did every night, the way he would do every night, whether or not anyone remembered why.

Orsen looked at the blank space waiting on Wren's page, and at the snow, and at the small warm country of the room — and found, to her surprise, that she was not afraid.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we'll write the answer."
