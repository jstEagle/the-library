## 1. The Taster's Ledger

The broth arrived at the king's table in a silver tureen, and Ada Yarrow knew before the lid came off that someone wanted the prince dead.

She could not have said how she knew. There was no smell of almonds, no telltale bitterness, no oily sheen on the surface. The broth was a pale gold, fragrant with leek and thyme, exactly as it should be. But twelve years of tasting had taught her that poison, like people, was most dangerous when it had learned good manners.

"Your Grace," she said, and her voice did what it always did—went quiet, went flat, went nowhere at all. "The broth."

Prince Ilan looked up from his argument with his uncle about grain tariffs. He was twenty-three, with his mother's dark eyes and a habit of drumming two fingers against the table when he was bored, which was often. He liked Ada the way one likes a well-made chair: comfortably, without ever really seeing her.

"The broth," he agreed, and held out his spoon.

Ada took the ladle herself. She filled a small porcelain cup—the tasting cup, white as an eggshell—and drank.

The poison hit the back of her throat like a whisper. Not the violent kind, then. This was something patient, something that meant to work over weeks or months, accumulating in the blood like silt in a river. She had tasted its cousins before: a slow numbing agent in Lord Cassel's wine three winters past, a dream-dulling tincture in the queen's honey-cakes the year before that. Someone at court had decided long ago that the prince would be easier to rule if he were slightly less himself, and they had been feeding him that decision one spoonful at a time.

Ada swallowed, set down the cup, and counted to ten while her heart hammered out its small complaint.

"Well?" asked Master Corvane from the end of the table. The spymaster ate nothing he hadn't watched being cooked, which Ada had always found to be either great wisdom or great hypocrisy, depending on the day.

"It's excellent," Ada said. "Leek, thyme, a little sorrel. The kitchen has outdone itself."

Corvane's eyes rested on her a moment longer than necessary. He had gray eyes, the color of weather about to change, and he looked at everyone the way a moneylender looks at a debtor. "You hesitated."

"I savored," said Ada.

It was, perhaps, the boldest thing she had ever said at the king's table, and it cost her nothing, because Corvane smiled thinly and returned to his pigeons—or rather to the letters beside his plate, though everyone knew the pigeons were the real correspondence. Ada refilled the prince's bowl with a steady hand and stepped back into her place along the wall, where she became, once more, furniture.

This was the art of it. Anyone could drink poison. The trick was to do it so smoothly that no one wondered why you'd paused. Ada Yarrow had spent twelve years perfecting the pause. She stood now with her hands folded, her face arranged into pleasant blankness, and let the poison settle into her stomach alongside all the others, where her body—trained like a hound, dosed like a patient—would grumble, adapt, and forgive.

*One more for the ledger,* she thought, and then corrected herself. *Not that ledger.*

* * *

The Vellum Guild kept its back rooms above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, behind a door that opened only for those who already knew it was there. Ada climbed the stairs at half past eleven, when the shop below had gone dark and the smell of tallow hung thick enough to chew. She unlocked three locks with keys she wore braided into her hair, because locks could be picked but hair, generally, was beneath suspicion.

The ledger room was hers alone after midnight. Mistress Sable saw to that—not out of kindness, but because Sable understood that a ledger-keeper who felt watched made mistakes, and mistakes in these particular books had a way of becoming permanent.

Ada lit the lamp, rolled up her sleeves, and got to work.

There were four ledgers, and she loved them with the uncomplicated devotion other people reserved for children or gardens. The Green Ledger recorded memories: bottled, borrowed, sold, and pawned. The Gray Ledger held favors, which were simply debts wearing nicer clothes. The Black Ledger was for names—true names, door-opening names, the kind of currency that never jingled—and Ada was permitted to record entries there but never, under any circumstances, to read them aloud. And the Red Ledger, thin as a hymnal, held the trades so impossible that the Guild preferred not to keep proof of them at all, except that Sable believed in proof the way other women believed in saints.

Tonight's entries:

*One memory, first snowfall of childhood, sold by a cooper's widow to pay for her husband's funeral. Bottled amber, third shelf. She wept beautifully. They always weep beautifully.*

*One favor, called in: the watch-sergeant of Bridgegate will look elsewhere on Thursday nights. Price: his mother's luck, borrowed against, interest accruing.*

*One small impossibility: a letter delivered to a house that burned down nine years ago. Delivered anyway. Recipient signed. Do not ask.*

Ada wrote in her fine, narrow hand, blotting each line with care. This was the hour she liked best in all her life, though she would have denied it under torture. The lamp was warm. The ink smelled of iron and oak galls. Outside, Candlewick Lane slept its honest, dishonest sleep, and here in this room every secret in the city lay open on her desk like patients waiting to be seen—and none of them, not one, knew her name.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of her existence, load-bearing and sound: Ada Yarrow, invisible. The taster whom the court forgot between courses. The ledger-keeper whom the Guild trusted precisely because she wanted nothing, owed nothing, and belonged to no one. She had built her invisibility plank by plank over twelve years, and she inspected it nightly the way a sailor inspects a hull, and she believed—it was almost a religion with her—that it would hold.

Invisibility, Ada had learned early, was not the absence of being seen. It was the presence of being *unremarkable*. It was knowing which wines the spymaster favored so you could refill his glass before he asked, and knowing never to meet his eyes while doing it. It was laughing at the right moments in the kitchens without ever saying anything worth repeating. It was having a face that people described afterward as "pleasant, I think, brown hair?" even to their closest friends.

She had been eight years old when the Guild took her in. She remembered—she was quite sure she remembered—a fire, and a woman's voice saying *run*, and then a long gray corridor and Mistress Sable's hand on her head like a benediction. Everything before the corridor was fog. She had assumed, all her life, that the fog was grief. Grief makes fog. Everyone knows that.

"You're late."

Ada didn't jump. Jumping was for people who could afford to be startled. She turned and inclined her head to Mistress Sable, who stood in the doorway in her gray traveling cloak, silver hair cropped short, looking as though she had been standing there for some time and had merely decided to become visible.

"The court ran long," Ada said. "The prince argued tariffs until the candles drowned."

"And the broth?"

"Clean." A pause, measured to the width of a hair. "Mostly clean. There was a softness in it. Slow-working. I've taken the dose."

Sable crossed the room and studied the ledgers, though Ada had already blotted the last line. "You'll build your immunity further tonight. Bitterleaf and ashroot, double measure." Her voice was not unkind; it was simply efficient, the way a knife is efficient. "You are the finest taster the crown has employed in a generation, Ada. Do not let sentiment make you careless."

"I'm never careless."

"No," Sable agreed, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes—something old and tired and almost tender. "You never are. That is precisely what frightens me about you."

Then she was gone, down the stairs, her footsteps making no more sound than snowfall, and Ada sat alone again with her lamps and her ledgers and the slow poison doing its patient work in her belly.

She finished the entries. She capped the ink. She blew out the lamp and sat a moment in the dark, listening to the building settle around her like an old dog circling twice before lying down.

Twelve years of nights like this one. Twelve years of swallowing other people's malice and writing down other people's secrets, asking nothing, owning nothing, seen by no one. Safe. Invisible. Sound.

Ada locked the ledgers, braided the keys back into her hair, and went home through the sleeping streets with her collar up against the autumn cold.

She did not see the man waiting in the shadow of the chandler's doorway. No one would have; he had made an art of waiting, and he had been waiting, by his own later account, for a very long time indeed. He watched her pass with eyes the green of new leaves, and when she had gone he pressed one hand flat against the door of the Guild as if greeting an old enemy, and whispered something in a language that made the lock turn over in its sleep.

But that was still hours away, and Ada, walking home beneath a fat and ordinary moon, knew none of it. She counted her steps—one hundred forty from the Guild to her room above the cobbler's, a number she had never once needed to think about—and told herself, as she told herself every night, that tomorrow would be the same as today, and the day after the same as that, and that this was not a cage but a craft, and that a person who was no one to everyone could never be taken from anyone.

The moon, fat and ordinary, said nothing.

It had thirteen turns left to say otherwise.
