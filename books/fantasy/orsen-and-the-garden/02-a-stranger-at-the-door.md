## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The Vellum Guild kept its back room the way other establishments kept their wine cellars: dark, cool, and locked against anyone who might want what was inside. Ada had spent six years learning its geography by feel—the shelf of bottled Tuesdays that all smelled faintly of rain, the drawer of borrowed luck sorted by denomination, the cabinet where names were kept in envelopes sealed with wax and good intentions.

She was cataloguing a shipment of secondhand courage when she heard the knock.

Three raps, uneven, like someone drumming with fingers that no longer fully obeyed them. Then silence. Then a sound worse than knocking: the soft, wet thud of a body deciding it could no longer stand.

Ada set down her pen. She did not light a lamp. Invisibility, as she had told herself many times over the years, was an art, and its first principle was this: never be where the trouble is looking.

But the back room had only one door, and the trouble was already leaning against it.

She opened it a hand's width. A man stood—or rather, occupied the doorway the way ivy occupies a wall—tall once, now folded in on himself like a letter nobody wanted to read. His coat had been fine a decade ago; his boots had been fine two decades ago; his face had been fine perhaps never, being the kind of face that suggested its owner had spent a great deal of time outdoors arguing with the weather and losing gracefully.

"Signatory," he said.

"I think you have the wrong door," said Ada. "There's a chandler's three streets down."

"Signatory," he repeated, with the patience of a man who had walked a very long way to say one word and intended to say it properly. "You're the last."

"Of what?"

He didn't answer. Instead he swayed forward, and Ada—who had spent years catching poisoned princes' plates before they hit marble—caught him without thinking. He weighed about as much as a bundle of dried reeds. Under his coat, his chest rose and fell in shallow, laboring breaths, each one sounding like wind through a keyhole.

"You're dying," she observed.

"Noted," he wheezed. "Thank you. Very thorough."

Despite everything—the fear beginning to pool coldly in her stomach, the knowledge that Guild rules stated plainly that strangers in the back room were to be reported to Mistress Vetch within the quarter hour—Ada felt the corner of her mouth twitch. There was something familiar in his dryness. It was the tone she used herself, at the royal table, when swallowing something suspect: *noted, thank you, very thorough.*

"Come inside," she said, "before someone sees you."

Getting him to the worktable was an exercise in mutual embarrassment. He insisted on walking, mostly. She insisted on pretending not to notice when he didn't. By the time he slumped into the chair by the ledger desk, he was gray as old paper and breathing in sips.

"Water," he managed.

She gave him water. He drank half and then, with great ceremony, poured the rest onto the floorboards beside the chair, which struck Ada as either madness or ritual, and with the garden-folk—as the ledgers called them—it was usually both.

"My name," he said, "is Orsen."

"That's not a name," said Ada. "That's a word from a children's rhyme. *Orsen in the orchard, Orsen in the dell*—" She stopped. The rhyme surfaced whole from somewhere below memory, somewhere deeper than the twelve years she could account for, and it left her feeling as though she'd swallowed something that hadn't been on the plate.

"So you do remember," said Orsen softly. "Good. That will make this faster, and I am running out of fast."

He reached into his coat. Ada's hand went instinctively to the little knife she kept for opening parcels and, if necessary, throats—a skill the Guild taught alongside penmanship—and then stopped, because what he drew out was not a weapon.

It was a map.

Or it would be a map, eventually. At present it was a square of pale felt, damp to the touch, and as Ada took it—because he pressed it into her hands and did not seem inclined to negotiate—it moved. Not fluttered. *Grew.* Green filaments crept outward from its center like moss given ambition, and where they spread, they raised themselves into ridges: hills, walls, hedgerows, a spiral of paths so intricate it made her eyes ache to follow them.

"It's alive," she said stupidly.

"It's a map of a living place." Orsen coughed, a long, ruinous business. "The Thirteenth Garden. And it knows its signatory. Look how it runs toward your hands. Like a dog."

The moss-map had indeed begun creeping up her wrists, gentle as a cat butting its head against a chin. Ada should have dropped it. Every rule she lived by said drop it, report it, forget it. Instead she turned it over, watching green veins thread across the felt, and felt something in her chest unlock—a door she hadn't known was bolted swinging open onto a lit room.

"Why me?" she asked.

"Because you signed," said Orsen. "Twelve years ago. Before you could write your own name properly, they sat you at a stone table and put a quill in your fist, and you signed a covenant between your realm and mine. You are the last living signatory, Ada Yarrow. The others are dead or unmade. And without a signatory, the Garden closes, and everything that ever passed through its doors—" he gestured weakly at the shelves around them, at the bottled memories and borrowed luck, "—unravels. Every trade. Every bargain. Everything your Guild has built on our backs."

"The Guild trades fairly," said Ada automatically. It was the catechism, learned young: *the Vellum Guild trades fairly, takes only what is offered, gives only what is promised.* She had believed it the way one believes in the tide—without examination, because examination seemed unnecessary.

Orsen looked at her with an expression of profound, exhausted pity.

"Child," he said, "who do you think taught your founders to bottle a memory? Who lent them the luck they sold? We did. And when they decided we were inventory rather than neighbors—" another cough bent him double, "—they took the thirteenth moon's name and locked it in a ledger, and the knot came undone, and my exile began. I have walked twelve years to reach this room. I chose it because you are in it."

Ada's mouth was dry. "I don't remember any of that."

"No," said Orsen. "That was the price. Your memories bought the hiding of the name. You paid without knowing you were paying. They made sure of that." His eyes were going dimmer, she realized. Dimmer and greener, like a pond at dusk. "Which is why the map must go to you while you still have hands to hold it. They'll come for it. The collectors. Sable. Promise me—"

"Don't die mid-sentence," said Ada sharply. "It's rude, and I'll have questions."

Something that was nearly a laugh moved through him. "Promise me you won't give it back."

And here was the moment, Ada would think later, where her life split cleanly into before and after. Because every instinct, every rule, every careful wall of invisibility she had built brick by patient brick said: *give it back. Surrender it. Be small. Be unseen. Survive.*

Instead she heard herself say, "I promise."

Orsen smiled. It transformed his face entirely, the way spring transforms an orchard, and for one heartbeat Ada saw what he must have been before the walking and the wasting: tall and green and crowned in blossom.

"Signatory," he said—his third use of the word, and this time it sounded less like a title than like a blessing. "Grow well."

Then he leaned back in the chair, closed his eyes, and came apart.

Not violently. That was the terrible gentleness of it. He simply loosened, the way soil loosens between seasons, and what was left in the chair was not a man but a drift of moss and petals and one perfect, impossible apple, ripe in the wrong month, rolling slowly off the seat to bump against Ada's boot.

She stood very still for a long moment.

Then she picked up the apple, put it in her pocket, wrapped the map around her forearm beneath her sleeve—where it settled snugly, warm as a sleeping bird—and got out the mop.

* *

Mistress Vetch received the report in her office above the counting room, a chamber furnished entirely in things that had been traded rather than bought: a clock that ran on regret, a mirror that showed the viewer five pounds more prosperous, a small fire that burned without wood because someone had once traded away their enthusiasm for winter.

"A stranger," said Vetch. Her voice was smooth as vellum. "In the back room. Dying."

"Yes, mistress."

"And you reported it within the quarter hour, per the rules."

"Within the quarter hour," said Ada, who had in fact waited until she'd mopped the floor, buried the moss under the compost heap behind the counting house, eaten the apple (it tasted like a summer she couldn't name), and hidden the map in the false bottom of her ledger case. The lie came out of her mouth smooth and easy, and that frightened her more than anything else that night. She had expected lying to feel like breaking a bone. It felt like taking off a tight shoe.

"What manner of stranger?"

"A vagrant. Ill. He asked after charity and moved on." Ada kept her eyes at the respectful midpoint—not meeting Vetch's gaze, not dodging it. Invisible. Unseen. Safe.

Vetch studied her for a long moment. Ada counted her own heartbeats and found them annoyingly steady. Six years of tasting poison had taught her body to lie even to itself.

"Mm," said Vetch. "The collectors found moss on the doorstep. Moss, Ada, in the middle of a drought. And petals. White ones." She laced her fingers. "If a stranger brought something into this house—something living—I trust you understand what is required of you."

"Fully, mistress."

"Anything found is surrendered to the Guild. Anything known is spoken aloud. Anything hidden—" Vetch smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes, "—is theft, and we have such tidy punishments for theft."

"Of course, mistress."

"Good girl. Off to bed."

Ada curtsied and went. In the corridor outside, she allowed herself exactly four steps of shaking before she mastered it, because a taster learns early that fear shows in the hands, and hands are always watched.

* *

Her attic room was narrow and cold and hers, the only place in the world where Ada Yarrow existed on purpose. She barred the door, lit one candle stub, and unwrapped the map.

It had grown.

Where it had covered her forearm, it now covered most of the desk, moss-ridges risen into tiny green hills, and along one edge—new since the evening, new since the lie—a path had unfurled itself, winding westward out of the city, through country she half recognized, toward a mark that bloomed as she watched: a small gate shape, and beside it, in letters that grew like seedlings pushing through soil, a name.

*Halloway.*

Underneath, smaller, almost shy:

*He remembers too.*

Ada sat down slowly on her cot. Her heart was doing something complicated—fear and wonder braided together so tightly she couldn't tell which strand was which. Somewhere below her, the Guild slept, and tomorrow the collectors would come asking questions, and Sable—Sable, who had taught her to hold a cup and smile—would look at her with those kind, terrible eyes.

And under her sleeve, warm against her pulse, the map grew another inch.

"All right," Ada whispered to it, to Orsen, to the twelve-year hole in her own head. "West, then. Halloway. Let's see what you remember."

The moss-map shivered, pleased as a cat.

Outside her window, low over the rooftops, the moon hung full and round—and just beside it, faint as breath on glass, a thirteenth sliver of light that no almanac had ever charted.
