## 3. What the Ledgers Owe

The oldest ledger lived in the bottom drawer of Ada's writing desk, beneath a false floor she had built herself from a broken bread board and three coats of varnish. It was not the largest book in the Vellum Guild's collection, nor the most valuable—that honor belonged to the ledger bound in moth-wing silk, which recorded every dream anyone had ever sold twice—but it was the heaviest, and it was the only one whose lock had rusted shut sometime before Ada learned to walk.

She had never been curious about it before.

That was the strange part, she thought later. Twelve years in the Guild's back rooms, and she had dusted that ledger, carried it, catalogued it, and never once wondered what was inside. It sat in her memory the way a clock sits on a mantel: present, ticking, invisible. Mistress Vell kept it locked, and Ada kept her eyes down, and between the two of them they had built a very comfortable silence.

But silence, Ada was learning, is a thing you can only keep if nobody asks you to account for it.

She opened the drawer at midnight, after the lamps in the Guildhouse had gone down and Sable's boots had stopped creaking on the stairs above. The map lay under her pillow where it had lain for six nights now, growing. That was the word for it—*growing*—though moss would have been more accurate and less alarming. Each morning it was a little greener, a little softer, its ink-lines creeping outward like frost across a window. And each morning, when she touched it, she felt something she could only describe as being *remembered*. Not remembered by it. Remembered *with* it, as though somewhere in the Garden's green arithmetic there was an entry with her name beside it, waiting to be balanced.

So she went looking for the other entry.

The rusted lock surrendered to a hairpin and forty minutes of swearing that would have gotten her fined twice over if Mistress Vell had heard it. The cover came up with a sound like a held breath released. The paper inside was the color of old cream, soft as cloth, and the handwriting was not one hand but many—a dozen different scribes across a dozen decades, each entry a small transaction in impossible things:

*Bottled: the last summer of the miller's daughter, 3 drams. Buyer: the miller himself.*

*Traded: seven years of good weather, to the orchard-folk, for a door that opens inward.*

*Sold: a name. Which name, withheld. Price: everything.*

Ada read until her candle guttered, and found nothing about gardens, nothing about moons, nothing about herself. The earliest entries were dull as any merchant's book. She was reaching to close it—half-relieved, half-disappointed, telling herself the stranger had been mad, that dying men say anything—when her thumb caught on the edge of a page that did not lie flat.

Twelve years ago. The date was written in the margin in a hand she almost recognized, round and careful, like a child taught beautifully and then taught to hide it.

*Received into care: one (1) child, female, approx. age six. Found at the garden wall, no family claimed. Payment rendered to the crown's office in full.*

Below it, in the same hand:

*Covenant clause, pending renewal. Signatory: —*

And then the page tore. Not torn recently; torn long ago, cleanly, by someone who knew what they were doing, leaving only the ghost of the next line pressed into the paper behind it—the faint mirror-image of words that had once faced this one. Ada held the page up to the candle and tilted it until the light raked sideways across the fibers.

She read the ghost backwards, letter by letter, her lips moving.

*...the thirteenth moon answers to no ledger. Its name is kept by the signatory alone. If the signatory forgets, the debt passes to—*

To whom? The rest was gone, torn away with the page itself.

Ada sat very still in the dark of her room, the way she had learned to sit at the royal table while poison moved through her blood and her face did nothing at all. But this was a different kind of stillness. At the table, stillness was armor. This was the stillness of a woman standing on a floor that has just told her, politely, that it may not be a floor.

Signatory. The dying man had said it. The clause said it. Her own name should have been written beside it—and instead there was a blank space, and a torn page, and twelve years of her life accounted for in a merchant's hand like so much received stock.

*Found at the garden wall, no family claimed.*

She had always believed she remembered her childhood. She believed it the way you believe your own hands. There were memories: a kitchen with a blue door. A woman singing off-key. Rain on a tin roof. Learning letters from a patient voice. Arriving at the Guildhouse at—she had always assumed—eight or nine years old, frightened, orphaned, grateful.

But when she reached for what came *before* the kitchen with the blue door, she found not darkness but smoothness. A wall, plastered white, painted with pleasant things. And walls, she understood now, standing in front of one, are built. Somebody had built hers. Somebody had paid "in full" to the crown's office, and taken a six-year-old girl from a garden wall, and filed her neatly into the world's most discreet trade, and torn out the page that explained why.

Her life wasn't a life. It was a ledger entry with the inconvenient parts removed.

Ada did the only sensible thing. She made tea. She drank it standing at the window, watching the moon—fat, a night or two from full, and somehow, when she looked at it too long, slightly the wrong color, a greenish cast at the edge like the bloom on a plum. Thirteen turns, Wren Halloway would have said. She didn't know Wren Halloway yet. She wouldn't for another week. But the moon looked wrong all the same, and the map under her pillow grew a quarter inch toward the window, as if it agreed.

She wrote nothing in her own ledger that night. For the first time since she'd learned to hold a pen, Ada Yarrow had nothing to record that she trusted herself to write down.

* * *

Corvane did not drink at court functions, which everyone agreed made him worse.

He stood at the edge of the autumn feast with a cup of warmed wine he had no intention of touching, and watched the royal table the way a falcon watches a hedgerow: patiently, without appetite, certain that something edible was in there somewhere.

"Your Grace enjoys the pheasant?" he asked, materializing beside the young Duchess of Merrow, who started so badly she nearly wore her plate.

"The pheasant is excellent," she said, recovering. "Everyone says so."

"Everyone does say so." Corvane smiled his filing-cabinet smile. "It has been excellent for eleven years. Remarkably consistent. One might almost think the recipe never changed."

"One might," the Duchess agreed, and fled.

Corvane turned his attention back to the table, to the slight figure at the prince's left hand who tasted each course with a small silver spoon and a face like a closed book. The food-taster. Ada Yarrow. Quiet, punctual, unremarkable—the sort of person Corvane's profession existed to notice precisely because nobody else did.

Three things bothered him.

First: the taster had survived, over her career, four confirmed poisoning attempts and eleven suspected ones, which put her odds somewhere north of a professional soldier's and suggested either extraordinary luck or extraordinary preparation. Corvane did not believe in luck. Luck was a story people told about preparation.

Second: a courier from the Guildhouse district had reported, two nights past, a disturbance—an old man dead on a doorstep, cause unknown, and a young woman seen leaving the premises at an unusual hour. The description matched the taster. The report had been filed and forgotten by everyone except him, because forgetting was his job and therefore he never did it.

Third—and this was the one that kept him up at night, staring at his ceiling like a man doing sums—the crown's own records contained a payment, twelve years old, marked *received in full*, regarding a child recovered at the palace garden wall. He had pulled the folio himself. The payment was real. The accompanying page—the one that should have named the child, the circumstances, the authority who ordered it—was missing. Torn out. Someone in this palace, twelve years ago, had erased a little girl from the paperwork, and someone had paid handsomely for the erasure, and the little girl had grown up to taste the prince's food with remarkable survival rates.

Corvane did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was a story people told about ledgers they hadn't finished reading.

Across the hall, Prince Ilan laughed at something his fool had said, bright and easy, and lifted his cup, and the taster rose smoothly to take her customary sip from it first—her mouth to his cup, her body between him and the world, as it had been for years.

Corvane watched her swallow, and set down his untouched wine, and began, quietly and with great pleasure, to make a list of questions.

The first question was for the archivist: *Who has access to the old covenant folios?*

The second was for the Guildhouse: *What does the Vellum Guild buy, exactly, and from whom?*

The third he saved for the lady herself, and he intended to ask it soon, at his leisure, over a table far less crowded than this one:

*Ada Yarrow—who were you, before you were ours?*

Somewhere across the city, under a pillow in a locked room, a map made of living moss grew another inch toward the palace, and waited.
