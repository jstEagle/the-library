## 4. The Gardener Who Fell

The map had grown overnight.

Ada discovered this at dawn, when she lifted the loose floorboard where she kept everything that mattered—which was, in truth, only the map and a stub of pencil—and found the thing had put down roots. Literally. Fine white threads, delicate as spun sugar, had threaded themselves through the underside of the board and were now, she was fairly certain, drinking from her house's foundations.

"Absolutely not," Ada told it.

The map did not answer. Maps of the ordinary kind never did, but this one had stopped being ordinary somewhere around the moment it began to smell like rain. It lay on her floor in a pool of morning light, and where yesterday it had shown the city in careful ink—canals, bridges, the palace rising at its heart like a paper crown—today there were new marks. Green ones. Tiny leaves unfurling along a street that ran east out of the city, toward the orchard country, and one leaf in particular, larger than the rest, pulsing faintly like a heartbeat made of moss.

Ada crouched over it with her tea going cold and considered her options, which were as follows: burn the map (she had tried; it had liked the flame), surrender it to Mistress Vell (she had watched a collector die for less), or follow it into the orchard country to find whatever gardener it wanted her to find.

She drank her tea. She told herself, as she dressed and braided her hair flat and plain, that she was going because curiosity was a debt, and Ada Yarrow paid her debts. She did not tell herself that for three days she had slept badly, dreaming of an orchard full of fruit that had no business existing, or that she'd woken each time with the taste of cherries in her mouth—out-of-season cherries, sweet and wrong—and no memory of ever eating them.

Some lies you tell other people. Some you tell yourself. Ada had always been scrupulous about keeping the ledgers separate.

* * *

The orchard country began where the cobblestones gave up and the road went soft underfoot. By midmorning Ada was walking between rows of apple trees so old their trunks had split and knitted again, gnarled like the hands of people who had done too much work and regretted none of it. The air smelled of fermentation and fallen leaves. Somewhere ahead, someone was singing.

Badly.

It was not a tune so much as a series of confident wrong notes arranged in an order that suggested the singer had once heard music described to him. Ada followed it around a bend in the road and found a man up a ladder, harvesting apples in deep autumn, wearing a coat that had been patched so many times the original fabric existed now mostly as a rumor. He had dirt to the elbows and a basket slung across his back, and he was singing to the trees, which—Ada noted with a small cold feeling in her stomach—were leaning toward him.

Not metaphorically. Leaning. Branches tilting his way the way wheat leans after wind.

"You're trespassing," the man said, without turning around. "Or lost. Or both. Those are the only two kinds of people who come out here."

"I'm looking for Wren Halloway," Ada said.

The singing stopped. The apples, Ada would have sworn, held their breath.

"Wren Halloway," the man repeated, climbing down with the unhurried care of someone who had fallen off enough ladders to respect them. He turned. He was perhaps fifty, weathered brown, with a face like a root vegetable—homely, honest, built for endurance—and eyes the exact green of new leaves. "Now that's interesting. Nobody's looked for me in eleven years. People look *for* things I've got, occasionally. Never for me." He set down his basket. "Who sent you?"

"A map," said Ada, and unwrapped it from its oilcloth.

The effect was immediate and strange. Wren Halloway went still in the way that trees go still—not frozen, but attentive, every part of him listening. His gaze dropped to the living green of it, to the little pulsing leaf, and something moved across his face that Ada recognized, because she had spent her life cataloguing expressions at a royal table where everyone lied beautifully. It was grief. Old grief, worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for years.

"That's Orsen's work," he said quietly. "That's Orsen's hand."

"You know it."

"I grew it." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "Eleven years ago I stood in the king's own orchard and told the court that the trees were bearing fruit out of season, and do you know what they said? They said Halloway's gone mad. They said the gardener's been at the cider. And then they took my badge and my cottage and put me on the east road, and every year since, the orchard has gone on bearing cherries in October and plums in March, and every year the court eats them and says nothing, because saying something would mean admitting they'd exiled the only man who told the truth." He wiped his hands on his coat. "So yes. I know the map. I helped make the garden it comes from. What I don't know is why it's walking around in the pocket of a girl who smells like ink and poison."

Ada blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"You smell of ledger-ink," Wren said, "and under it, very faintly, of the things you swallow so other people don't have to. I'm a gardener. I notice what grows and what doesn't. You—" he tilted his head, studying her the way he'd studied the leaning trees, "—you're growing something. Right at the edge of you. Something that was cut back hard, years ago, and has been trying to come up ever since."

The cold feeling in Ada's stomach spread. She thought of the oldest ledger, her name in faded brown ink beside a clause she could not read, the page torn out beneath it. Twelve years ago. A child's signature.

"I need your help," she said, instead of answering.

"Mm." Wren picked up his basket and began walking, and after a moment, because standing alone in an orchard with a stranger seemed worse than following one, Ada followed. "Everyone needs a gardener's help eventually. Question is what for. So tell me straight, ink-and-poison girl. Why does a Guild taster carry a piece of the Thirteenth Garden in her floorboards?"

Ada stopped walking. "How did you know I keep it in my floorboards?"

"How did you know I was the man you were looking for?" Wren countered. "The map brought you. It brings who it wants. That's rather the point of Orsen." He set the basket down beneath a tree whose branches, Ada now saw clearly, were heavy with ripe cherries—in November, in frost-country, absurd and shining. "Sit. Eat. You'll think better."

"I don't eat unverified fruit."

Wren grinned, and for a moment he looked twenty years younger. "A woman after my own heart. Go on, then. Test it." He held one out.

Ada took it. She turned it over. She did the thing she had trained ten years to do—read the skin, the bloom, the stem-scar, the faint bitter ghost at the center where trouble hides—and found nothing. No arsenic bloom. No bitter almond. Nothing but cherry, sun-warmed and impossible.

She ate it. It tasted like being seven years old.

She did not know why she thought that. She had no memory of being seven years old at all—her earliest recollection was the Guild's back room, aged nine, learning to read by ledger-light—but the taste reached past all that, down into some cellar of her that had been locked and sealed, and it *knew* her.

"Well," said Wren softly, watching her face. "There it is."

"There *what* is?"

"Recognition." He sat down against the tree trunk, pulling her gently by the sleeve until she sat too, the frost-wet grass soaking through her skirt, neither of them caring. "You've tasted Garden fruit before, haven't you. Long ago. Before the Guild got hold of you and taught you that everything must be tested before it can be trusted." He shook his head slowly. "They do that, you know. The Guild. They take children who remember too well and teach them to forget on purpose. Call it discipline. Call it professionalism." His voice roughened. "I know the trick. I married a woman who did it to herself so thoroughly she forgot she'd ever done it."

Ada's throat was tight. "What happened to her?"

"The Garden happened. Same as to me. Same as, if I'm any judge, to you." He looked at her sideways. "You came here following a plant. Most people run from those. What did it cost you to follow it?"

"Nothing yet," Ada said. Then, honestly, because lying to this man felt like lying to the orchard itself: "Everything, probably. My mistress wants the map. Her collectors want the map. There's a spymaster at court asking questions about a torn ledger page, and I'm fairly sure at least two of those threads end with me hanging. Following the map was the only direction left that wasn't backward."

Wren nodded as though she'd said something wise. "Good. Backward's no use to anyone." He was quiet a moment, thumb moving absently over a cherry stem. "I'll tell you why I was exiled. Not the court version—the true one. I didn't just see the fruit. I remembered the *tree*. Eleven years ago, the night the covenant failed, I was in the orchard, and I saw the thirteenth moon rise, and I saw the trees weep sap like blood, and I heard something in the ground say a name. And I told the court what I saw, word for word, because I am a man constitutionally incapable of doing otherwise." He smiled without humor. "Truth-telling. Worst career move available to a royal servant. You'd know."

"I taste truth for a living," Ada said. "And lie for one."

"And how's that sitting on your stomach these days?"

"Like the broth did last Tuesday," said Ada. "Before I threw it back up behind the kitchens."

Wren laughed—a real laugh, rolling out through the orchard, startling birds from the cherry tree. Then he sobered, and took her hand, and turned it palm-up in his big dirt-browned fingers, looking not at her palm but at the fine green thread Ada now noticed winding around her wrist like a bracelet she hadn't put on. It had not been there that morning. It matched the roots under her floorboards exactly.

"Huh," said Wren.

"What? What is it?"

"It likes you." He released her hand, looking troubled and pleased at once. "Orsen doesn't like anybody. Orsen *endures* everybody. But that thread's a grafting, girl—that's how the Garden claims a gardener. It's decided you're worth the trouble of growing onto." He sighed, long and resigned, the sigh of a man watching weather turn. "Which means I can't send you away. Grafted stock shares roots. Whatever happens to you happens to my trees now, and I've grown rather fond of my trees."

"So you'll help me?"

"I'll help *it*, which currently means helping you." He stood, brushing off his coat, and began hauling the basket toward a shed at the orchard's edge. "Come on. If we're going to save a dying world, you'll want to eat something first, and I want to show you something in my shed that'll explain why the moon's been wrong lately. Have you noticed the moon's been wrong?"

Ada had, actually. For three nights running, the full moon had hung in the sky a hand's width off from where the almanacs swore it should be, and the almanac-makers had blamed fog, and optics, and each other.

"It's the thirteenth turn," Wren said over his shoulder. "The intercalary moon. The knot-night. And this year, nobody's renewing the vow." He paused at the shed door, key already in the lock, and glanced back at her with those leaf-green eyes. "Unless, of course, a certain signatory gets herself to a certain door before it closes. Which reminds me—"

He pushed the door open. Inside, in the dimness, something that was definitely not gardening equipment glowed a soft, patient green.

"—how attached are you to that floorboard?"
