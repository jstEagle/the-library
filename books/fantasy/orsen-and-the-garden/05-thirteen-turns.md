## 5. Thirteen Turns

Wren Halloway had not slept well in exile, and she had slept worse in the palace, and she slept worst of all now, in a borrowed room above a chandler's shop, with a living map growing on her table like something she'd forgotten to weed.

She had tried to explain it to herself three times since dawn. The map was moss. The map was a plant. The map was, by every law of botany she had sworn an oath to and then broken by accident, impossible. And yet there it was, creeping a slow green inch across the tabletop whenever Ada Yarrow wasn't looking at it, and stopping—Wren was nearly sure of this—the moment anyone did.

"It knows we're watching," Wren said.

"Everything in this city knows we're watching," Ada said. She sat across from him with her hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea she wasn't drinking, because Ada Yarrow did not drink things she hadn't tasted first, even tea, even here, even from Wren's own kettle. "That's rather the point of being hunted."

"I mean it literally."

"I know you do." Ada looked down at the map with the expression of a woman who had decided to be brave about it later. "I've been trying not to think about that."

Wren pulled his chair closer. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with dirt permanently under his nails no matter how he scrubbed, and hands that still moved toward plants before they moved toward anything else. Twelve years ago he had been the royal gardener. Then he had said, out loud, at a harvest festival, in front of the Master of the Household, that the palace orchard was bearing plums in the dead of winter—and he had been right, which was the unforgivable part. Liars were forgiven daily in Amberhal. A man who told the truth about fruit out of season was a threat to the whole architecture of polite disbelief.

So: exile. A cottage outside the walls. A reputation as the mad gardener who talked to trees.

The trees, for the record, had never once answered him. He'd checked.

"Tell me what I'm looking at," Ada said. "Properly. You recognized it. You went white as a cut turnip and then you made me tea and didn't say another word all night, which for you is a kind of confession."

Wren laughed despite himself. "It is a kind of confession. All right." He flattened his palms on either side of the map, careful not to touch it. "But I want you to understand that everything I know, I learned from my grandmother, who learned it from hers, and half of it is probably wrong, and the other half I only believe because I've seen the orchard do what it does."

"The winter plums."

"The winter plums," Wren agreed solemnly. "Now. Have you ever counted the moons?"

Ada blinked. "What?"

"The moons. In a year. How many?"

"Twelve," Ada said. "Everyone knows—" She stopped. Wren watched her stop, watched the taster's discipline come over her face, the little hitch of a woman catching herself swallowing something unexamined. "Everyone *knows* twelve," she finished, more carefully. "Which is different from counting them."

"There's a thirteenth," Wren said. "Some years. Not on any calendar, because calendars are made by people who need the year to behave. It comes between the last moon of autumn and the first of winter—a night that belongs to no month. The old folk called it the Turn. My grandmother called it the thirteenth knot."

He reached into his shirt and drew out a cord he wore around his neck, and on the cord, thirteen knots in a line, twelve of them worn smooth and one of them—Wren felt his chest tighten, as it always did—still rough, still new-looking, though the cord was older than he was.

"Each full moon ties a knot," he said. "A knot between here and the Garden. That's what the Garden *is*, Ada—not a place you walk to. It's the other side of the knot. Every full moon, the two realms pull tight against each other, and everything that crosses between them crosses along the string. Bottled memories. Borrowed luck. Fruit in January." He nodded at her. "Your Guild trades in impossibilities. Where do you imagine impossibilities come from?"

Ada was quiet a long moment. "I always assumed they came from somewhere," she said finally. "We weren't encouraged to ask where."

"No," Wren said. "You wouldn't be. Asking where is how I lost my position." He turned the cord in his fingers. "And once a year—once in those years when the extra moon comes—the knots are untied and retied. Renewed. Both sides meet at the threshold and speak their vows again, fresh, so the string never rots. It's called the Thirteenth Turn. It's supposed to be a celebration. My grandmother described it like a wedding, almost. Two families who've been married so long they've forgotten they chose each other, choosing each other again."

"And the last one?"

Wren set the cord down. He found he couldn't hold it while he said the next part.

"The last one failed."

* * *

He told it plainly, because his grandmother had taught him that the worst stories should be told plainly, like weather reports.

Thirteen years ago, the intercalary moon had risen, and the garden-folk had come to the threshold, and the human signatories had not. No vows spoken. No renewal. One side of a marriage showing up to the altar and waiting until the candles burned down.

"Why?" Ada asked. "Why would they just not come?"

"Because someone wanted the trade without the treaty," Wren said. "Think about it. If the realms are bound by covenant, the trade flows both ways and fairly, and both sides keep their word. But if the covenant breaks—if the Garden starts closing—then the door narrows. Everything that gets through becomes rare. Rare becomes precious. Precious becomes *expensive*." He spread his hands. "Someone built a monopoly on the way in, and let the way itself die behind them."

Ada's cup had stopped halfway to her mouth. "The Vellum Guild," she said quietly.

"You said it, not me."

"But the Guild is old. Older than thirteen years. The ledgers go back—"

"The ledgers go back a long way," Wren agreed. "And somewhere in the oldest of them, I'd wager, there's a page about a stolen name. Names open doors, don't they? That's your own trade. And there's one name that opens the biggest door of all." He tapped the table beside the map, not quite touching the green. "The thirteenth moon has a name, Ada. It isn't a night; it's a *person*, or near enough. And if you lock that name away—in ink, in a vault, in a ledger nobody's allowed to read—then the Turn can't happen. You can't renew a vow with someone whose name you've hidden."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, the chandler's boy was singing tunelessly to himself, waxing wicks.

Ada set her cup down with great care, the way she did everything, as if the world might be poisoned. "And the Garden?"

"Closing," Wren said. "Slowly, the way a fist closes. Every year the door narrows a little more. The orchard used to bear out-of-season fruit twice a year; by the end it was once, and then the fruit came wrong—beautiful and hollow, like a promise from a stranger. The garden-folk have been leaving. Or being shut out. And here's the part that should keep you up at night—" he leaned forward, and lowered his voice, though there was no one to hear but the map, "—everything traded through the Guild is Garden-stuff. Bottled memories, borrowed luck, names. All of it is held together by the knots. When the last knot rots through, it doesn't just stop working. It *unravels*. Every memory ever bottled comes loose. Every borrowed luck gets called home. Every door-name stops opening doors and starts opening... whatever's underneath doors."

Ada had gone very pale. Wren realized, watching her, that she was doing arithmetic—he could see it, the taster's habit of measuring doses. She was calculating how many trades, how many years, how much unraveling.

"That's why the map came to me," she said. "That's why the stranger called me *signatory*."

"Yes," Wren said gently. "I think someone's finally paying the Guild's debts. And I think they sent the bill to you."

* * *

They went out at dusk, because Wren refused to show her the door in daylight.

"Not because it won't show in daylight," he said, leading her down the crooked lane behind the chandler's, past the tanneries, to where the city wall met the old river-gate that hadn't opened in living memory. "Because it shows *wrong* in daylight, and I'd rather you see it properly first."

"Comforting."

"I'm a gardener. We believe in planting things at the right hour."

The river-gate was a squat arch of black stone, bricked shut sometime before Wren's grandmother's grandmother, the mortar gone grey and furred with lichen. Weeds crowded its base. Above it, someone had once carved a lintel inscription, worn now to a few stubborn letters. Wren stood before it and, out of twelve years of habit, greeted it the way he greeted all growing things.

"Evening," he said.

And the lichen on the arch flushed faintly green.

Ada made a small sound. Wren pretended not to hear, because people deserved dignity while the world rearranged itself.

"This is a door?" she asked.

"This is a door the way a seed is a tree. Most days, it's nothing. Bricked-up gate, good for sheltering drunks." He touched the wall beside the arch, reverent, professional. "But on the thirteenth night—on the Turn—the mortar forgets it's mortar. The bricks remember being stones, and the stones remember being a doorway, and the doorway remembers being *asked*. For one night. One night in however many years, this opens onto the threshold, and the two sides can meet and speak."

"How long until the next Turn?"

Wren took a breath. This was the number he had been carrying alone for twelve years, the number that had made him mad, the number the astronomers laughed at because their instruments were built to find twelve.

"Nine days," he said. "The intercalary moon rises nine nights from now, and it will be the last one that finds the door strong enough to open at all. After that—" he patted the black stone, apologetic, the way you'd pat a dying horse, "—after that, the knot's too rotten to retie. Whatever happens has to happen on that night, with whoever's left to speak for us."

Ada stared at the sealed arch. The lichen pulsed, very softly, like something breathing in its sleep.

"My grandmother used to sing to the orchard on Turn-night," Wren said. "Silly song. About two gardens sharing one fence. I haven't sung it in twelve years." He smiled, and it hurt his face. "I suppose I'd better remember the words."

Behind them, back up the lane, a lantern swung. Then another. Voices—familiar voices, voices Wren didn't know but Ada clearly did, because she went rigid as a hooked fish and caught his sleeve.

"Wren," she whispered. "How many ways out of this alley?"

"One," he said. "Why?"

"Because there are four people coming in."

The lanterns rounded the corner, and the light fell on the first face beneath them, and Ada said, in a voice Wren had never heard her use—small, and young, and afraid:

"*Sable.*"
