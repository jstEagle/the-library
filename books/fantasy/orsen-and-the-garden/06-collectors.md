## 6. Collectors

The trouble with being invisible, Ada had learned long ago, was that the moment you needed to be seen, everyone saw you at once.

She had made it three days past the stranger's death before the Guild noticed she was lying. Three days of carrying the map against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth beneath her shift, where it grew—there was no other word for it—warm and faintly damp, like a loaf proving by a fire. Three days of recording her trades in the ledgers with a steady hand while her heart knocked at her teeth like a beggar at a door.

On the fourth morning, Sable came to collect her.

Ada knew it was Sable before she opened the door to her rooms above the tallow-chandler's shop, because the pigeons on the sill went up all at once in a gray clatter, and pigeons always fled Sable first. She was the Guild's finest collector: a small woman with iron-gray hair cut blunt as a hedge and hands that never seemed to be holding anything until they were holding you.

"Little Yarrow," Sable said, stepping inside without being asked. "You've been keeping something from us."

"I've been keeping everything from everyone for twelve years," Ada said. "It's rather my whole profession."

Sable smiled. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes; it stopped at the border like a traveler without papers. "The dying man. The map. Mistress Vell wants it back."

"Then Mistress Vell should have been quicker," Ada said, and heard her own voice do something it had never done before: refuse.

For a moment neither of them moved. Somewhere below, the chandler's boy sang tunelessly about a girl who loved a fishmonger. The map pulsed once against Ada's ribs, soft as a second heartbeat, and she thought absurdly: *don't let him hear you*.

"You trained under me," Sable said quietly. "I taught you how to stand so no one marks you. How to swallow poison and smile. How to lie with your ledger open." Her head tilted. "So tell me why I can read this lie on you like ink."

"Because you taught me too well," Ada said, "and I've finally learned from the best."

Something flickered across Sable's face then—not anger. Something older and sadder, gone before Ada could name it. "Run, then," Sable said. "You have until moonrise. After that, I send the others." A pause. "Not me. Not yet. Remember that, little Yarrow, when you're deciding whom to trust."

Ada remembered it later, in the dark, and could not decide whether it was mercy or the cruelest move in an old collector's game.

* * *

Moonrise found her on the rooftops above the memory-market, which was the only place in the city where being followed was a certainty and losing your pursuers was a matter of craft.

The memory-market spread across the old glassworks district like spilled honey: stalls of bottled recollections glowing amber and green, hawkers crying their wares—"First kisses! Summer days! Your grandmother's kitchen, still warm!"—and everywhere the soft murmur of people tasting other lives and sighing over what they'd never had. From the rooftops it looked like a festival. From the rooftops it always looked better than it was.

Ada ran the ridge tiles toward the bell tower, boots finding the old paths Wren Halloway had shown her two nights ago—the disgraced gardener walked roofs the way other people walked orchards—and behind her, right on schedule, came the collectors.

She knew their footsteps. That was the worst of it. She'd trained beside every one of them: Bramble, who moved like water finding a crack; the twins, Osk and Ost, who breathed in unison; quiet Pell, who had once shared his supper with her for a month when her rations ran short. People she'd laughed with. People who had taught her which poisons numbed and which burned, and held her hair back after the ones that burned.

They weren't chasing a stranger. They were chasing *her*, and they were good at it, because she had helped make them good.

A tile cracked under her heel. Below, a bottle-seller looked up, startled, and Ada dropped flat as a crossbow bolt—no, not a bolt, a thrown grappling line—hissed through the space where her shoulder had been. Bramble. Always showing off.

"Yarrow!" Pell's voice, carrying across the tiles, almost gentle. "Give it back. Nobody has to be hurt. You know how this ends—you've *written* how this ends, a hundred times, in the ledgers!"

That was true, and it landed like a stone. She had recorded a hundred retrievals. She had written down, in her neat clerk's hand, exactly what happened to keepers who ran: the memory-debt called due, the years stripped out, the person left standing hollow and polite, unable to say why they wept at lamplight.

She got up and ran anyway.

The bell tower rose ahead, black against the swollen moon—the twelfth turn, Wren had said, counting on scarred fingers, one left—and Ada took the gap between the granary roof and the tower's shoulder at a leap that would have horrified her ten-year-old self, and caught the gutter, and hung there, arms screaming, while below her the collectors fanned out through the market like ink through water.

There was a way down into the tower. There was also, she realized with a lurch, no way out of it. Dead end. Wren's paths ended here, and Wren had said, apologetically, *I never needed to leave in a hurry; I was always the one being chased out of places slowly.*

Boots on the granary roof. Two sets. Then a third, lighter, faster—Bramble, closing.

Ada pressed her back to the cold stone and did the arithmetic she'd spent her whole life avoiding. She could fight. She would lose; she'd trained them. She could surrender the map. And somewhere in the Garden, whatever was closing like a hand around a candle would finish closing, and Orsen—the name the dying man had breathed, the name the map whispered when she slept—would go out with it.

Or she could pay.

Every keeper knew about paying. It was the last resort written into the Guild's oldest rules, older than Mistress Vell, maybe older than the Guild itself: *a debt may be settled in kind.* The market traded in memories. A runner with nothing else could spend her own.

Ada had one commodity worth anything. Twelve years of careful, rationed, hidden happiness—the taste of plum wine stolen off the duke's own table and laughed over with Pell; the morning Wren had grinned at her across a seed catalog and she'd felt, astonishingly, *found*; the prince's rare, crooked smile when she'd told him the truth about his plate and he'd believed her. A year of it, the rules said. One full turning of the seasons, lifted clean.

She would not remember what she lost. That was the trick of it. She would just be a little colder afterward, in rooms she couldn't explain, at names she couldn't place.

"Last chance, Yarrow." Bramble, ten feet away now, crouched on the gutter, silhouetted against the moon. Behind her, Pell and the twins. All of them hesitating, she realized. All of them waiting for her to be sensible, the way she had always been sensible, the way invisibility was supposed to save her.

"It doesn't have to end the way I wrote it," Ada said aloud, mostly to herself. "Maybe that's the whole point."

She closed her eyes. Found the shelf inside herself where the Guild had taught her to store things—bottles within bottles, the taster's art turned inward—and chose. Plum wine and laughter. Seed catalogs and being found. A crooked smile in candlelight. A year of warmth, gathered up like armfuls of laundry, and offered out into the dark with the old words, the binding words:

"*Paid in kind. Witnessed by the moon.*"

It didn't hurt. That was the terrible part. It felt like setting down a heavy basket after a long walk—relief and lightness and a strange, echoing emptiness where the weight had been. Somewhere below, in the market, a thousand bottled memories flickered at once, and every hawker cried out as their wares glowed briefly brighter, as if the whole district had leaned in to watch.

When Ada opened her eyes, Bramble was staring at her with an expression Ada had never seen on that sharp face before. Grief, maybe. Or its cousin.

"You paid," Bramble said. "Off a rooftop, to *them*—" she jerked her chin at the sky, at the moon, at whatever stood behind it. "Do you even know what you bought your escape *with*?"

"A year," Ada said. Her voice sounded fine. That was the other terrible part. "I'll miss it, apparently. I'm told that's how it works."

Pell had come up beside Bramble, and he wasn't reaching for her anymore. He was looking at her the way you look at someone standing at the edge of something. "Sable said bring the map," he said slowly. "She said *bring the map*. She never said what to do if you'd already become the kind of keeper we strip hollow." He swallowed. "We've all seen them after, Yarrow. We've all *made* them after."

"Then don't make me," Ada said.

The twins arrived last, breathing in unison, and looked at Pell, and Pell looked at Bramble, and Bramble looked—for a long moment—at the empty space where Ada's year of happiness used to be, visible somehow, the way a pulled tooth leaves a hole the tongue keeps finding.

"We lost her in the tower," Bramble said at last, loudly, for the benefit of anyone listening from the dark. "Report it however you like."

Then they were gone, four shadows pouring back down into the glittering market, and Ada sat down hard on the cold lead of the gutter with the map pulsing against her ribs and no memory of why her face was wet.

She wiped it, puzzled. Checked the moon. Counted, as Wren had taught her.

One turn left.

And far below, at the mouth of the market street, a small figure with iron-gray hair stood perfectly still among the bottle-lights, looking up at the tower—looking, Ada was almost sure, directly at her—before turning away without raising any alarm at all.
