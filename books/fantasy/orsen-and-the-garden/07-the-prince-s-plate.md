## 7. The Prince's Plate

The summons came on a Thursday, which Ada would later think was typical of the crown. Not a dramatic midnight knock, not a hooded messenger in a rain-soaked alley—just a liveried page who appeared at the taster's quarters with the bland efficiency of a man delivering fish.

"The prince requests your presence at the evening service. The small table. His Highness wishes to discuss the menu."

Nobody discussed the menu. The menu was Ada's business and had been for six years, and Prince Ilan had never once in those six years expressed an opinion about it beyond "more of the pear thing." A private audience over the small table was not an invitation. It was a trap with good linens.

She considered running. She had the map pressed flat inside her bodice, warm against her ribs like a second heartbeat; she had Wren hidden in a rented room above a chandler's shop, muttering about moon-knots; she had Ilan's own name written three times in Corvane's interrogatory notes, which Sable had copied for her at a price Ada was still trying not to calculate. Running would mean abandoning all of it, and the Garden was closing whether she ran or not.

So she dressed in her grey service wool—the color of furniture, the color of nobody—and went to be trapped.

* * *

The small dining room off the prince's apartments held one table, two chairs, and a view of the inner garden where Wren Halloway had once been dragged out in irons for the crime of being right about apples. Ada knew every inch of it. She had tasted in this room a hundred times, seated on the stool by the wall that let her watch Ilan eat without being part of the conversation, invisible as cutlery.

Tonight there was no stool. Tonight there were two chairs, and Ilan gestured her into one of them, and a footman set a single covered dish between them before withdrawing and shutting the door with a click that sounded, to Ada's trained ear, very much like a lock.

Prince Ilan was twenty-four years old and looked, tonight, older. He had his mother's dark eyes and his father's habit of sitting very still when he was angry, hands folded, like a man keeping his temper on a short leash. He did not look pliable. But then, Ada thought, that was rather the point of what had been done to him.

"Sit," he said, though she already had. He lifted the cover from the dish.

Braised quail in elderberry glaze, on a bed of parsnip purée. His favorite. She had tasted it four hundred times. She could have identified it blindfolded, in a crowd, by smell alone—which was precisely why the poisoners had chosen it, she realized now, with a lurch. Nobody guards the dish they love.

"I'm told," Ilan said pleasantly, "that you are the finest taster in the kingdom's history. Six years of service without a single poisoning. Remarkable. Corvane tells me remarkable things about you."

Ada's mouth went dry. "His Grace is generous with the word remarkable."

"He also says you've been asking strange questions lately. About ledgers. About old contracts." Ilan turned the dish slowly on its axis, watching the glaze catch the candlelight. "He says you've been seen with a disgraced gardener. And that a man died in the Vellum Guild's back rooms last week with your name in his mouth."

The candle flames leaned in the draft from the window, all together, like courtiers bowing.

"And here is my difficulty, Mistress Yarrow," Ilan continued. "Corvane wants me to hand you to him. He has questions, and I'm given to understand his questions involve instruments. But—" he paused, and something moved behind the stillness, something almost boyish, "—you've kept me alive for six years. Whatever else you are, I'd rather hear it from you than from his instruments. So." He nudged the dish toward her. "Taste."

It was a test. Of course it was. And Ada sat there with the truth sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, weighing which lie would be lighter.

None of them would be. She had spent six years making herself invisible, and invisibility had failed her exactly when she needed it most. The Guild had lied to her. Corvane hunted her. Sable had offered her back her own childhood like a merchant offering change. Every loyalty she owned had turned out to be a ledger entry, debits against her silence.

Only this man, across the table, had trusted her blindly for six years—with his life, bite by bite, meal by meal.

Ada took a breath that hurt going in.

"Your Highness," she said, "I can't taste this dish."

Ilan's eyebrows rose. "Can't? Or won't?"

"Neither." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Because if I taste it the way I've always tasted it, nothing will happen to me. And if I told you it was clean, that would be true, and it would also be a lie, because the poison isn't meant for me."

She reached across and touched the edge of the dish, gently, the way you'd touch something sleeping.

"This quail has been laced for four years, Your Highness. Every serving. A slow compound—no fever, no collapse, nothing any physician would find unless he knew to look for a signature instead of a symptom. It doesn't kill. It *softens*. It erodes the will the way water erodes stone, so gradually that the man being dosed believes his own gentleness is his nature. It was meant to make you pliable. Agreeable. A prince who signs whatever he's handed and feels grateful doing it."

The silence stretched so long she could hear the candles burning.

"And you," Ilan said at last, very quietly. "You're telling me you've known. All this time. You've watched me eat it and said nothing?"

"No." She met his eyes. "That's the part I have to say next, and it's the part that may cost me everything, so I'll say it plainly: I have been tasting your plate for four years knowing what was in it, and taking the dose myself. Every serving, Your Highness. I built my tolerance the way I built it against everything else—one careful swallow at a time—so that none of it ever reached you. The reason you are not already your uncle's puppet is that I have been drinking your poison for four years and calling it dinner."

* * *

Later, Ada would remember that Ilan did not shout. That was the worst of it, somehow—that he simply sat there while the color left his face, hands still folded, the leash on his temper pulled so tight she could see the effort in his knuckles.

"My uncle," he repeated.

"Lord Corvane answers to him, and the compound comes through channels only the privy council could open. I don't have proof of the last link. I have the ledger trail, and I have the supplier's mark, and I have four years of my own blood, if anyone cares to test what I've absorbed." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Which is rather more proof than I'd like."

"Why?" The word came out raw. "Why would you do that? You didn't owe me. You're a taster. If I died, you'd be blamed, but if I grew *docile*—" he stopped, understanding arriving visibly, like dawn coming up ugly. "If I grew docile, no one would ever suspect anything at all. You gained nothing by saving me. You risked everything."

"You fed me," Ada said, before she could stop herself.

"What?"

"When I was new to the palace. Nineteen, straight out of the Guild's kitchens, terrified, eating alone in the servants' hall because I'd been taught that attachment was a liability. You came down—you used to come down, before the docility really took hold—and you put a honey roll on my tray because, and I quote, 'the cook makes them sad and someone should eat them bravely.'" Her eyes were stinging and she let them. "Nobody had ever fed me like I was a person. So yes, Your Highness. I drank your poison for four years over a honey roll. The accounting is absurd. I know."

Ilan stood up so abruptly his chair skidded. He walked to the window with his back to her, and his shoulders were shaking, and Ada understood with a jolt that the prince of the realm was laughing—or crying, or both, in the way men are taught to disguise as laughter.

"A honey roll," he said. "Four years of slow treason against my own family, purchased with a honey roll." He turned around. The fury was still there, but it had changed direction entirely, like a river finding its proper bed. "Who else knows?"

"One gardener who talks to apple trees. One memory-thief who owes me money. And a dead man's map growing moss in my bodice."

"That's the strangest sentence I've heard at court, and I sat through the ambassador's wedding toast." He came back to the table and, to her astonishment, served them both—a portion each, from the poisoned dish, like a toast between conspirators. He raised his fork. "To the honey roll."

"To the honey roll," Ada said, and ate, and for the first time in four years tasted the quail purely—as food, as craft, as something made well by hands that hadn't known what they were carrying.

Then she set down her fork and told him about the thirteenth moon.

* * *

They talked until the candles burned down to stubs. She gave him the shape of it—the covenant, the closing Garden, the name locked in the ledgers, the collectors who would come for her now that Sable's patience ran out. Ilan listened the way she imagined kings ought to listen: completely, without interrupting, asking only the questions that mattered.

"The ledgers," he said at last. "The Guild keeps them where?"

"In the counting house under Saint Verre's. Behind three locks and a woman named Sable, who trained me and would kill me by Thursday if it served the trade."

"Then here is what happens." He stood, and for the first time since she'd known him, Prince Ilan looked like a man whose will had come home to roost. "Tomorrow morning I convene a tasting audit—all dishes, all suppliers, full physician's examination, my own blood included. Corvane cannot object without explaining why objection suits him. While the palace tears itself apart looking for a poisoner, you will take whatever you need from wherever you need it. And if Sable sends collectors after you—" he smiled, and it was not a gentle smile, "—she will be sending them into a royal household that suddenly remembers, quite inconveniently, that tampering with the king's taster is high treason."

Ada stared at him. "You'd spend your political capital on this. On a map and a moon."

"On a woman who drank poison for me over a honey roll?" He shrugged. "My capital has been sitting idle anyway. Apparently I've been agreeing with everyone for four years." The humor faded into something more careful. "Mistress Yarrow. When you seal this covenant of yours. When the price comes due, whatever it is—I want you to tell me first. No more quiet absorptions. No more swallowing things alone. That's an order from your prince."

"It's a terrible order," Ada said. "I'm very bad at obeying it."

"I know. I've watched you disobey it for four years, magnificently." He blew out the nearest candle. "Go home. Take the gardener. Take the thief. Come back tomorrow before dawn and I'll walk you past the east gate myself."

She was halfway to the door when his voice stopped her.

"Mistress Yarrow. The dish tonight. Was it truly poisoned?"

She turned. "Yes, Your Highness. Same as always."

Something flickered across his face—not fear. Gratitude, fierce and unguarded, for four years of meals he'd never known were battles.

"Then thank you," said the prince, "for every single bite."

Ada walked back through the sleeping palace with the map warming against her ribs and her hands, for the first time in days, steady. In the courtyard below, a lantern bobbed toward the east gate—too punctual, too purposeful, moving like someone who already knew she'd be crossing it.

Sable had said Thursday.

It seemed Thursday had come early.
