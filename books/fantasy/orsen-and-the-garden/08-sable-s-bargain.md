## 8. Sable's Bargain

Sable had always believed that the hardest lies to keep were the ones you told yourself, and she had been telling herself one for twelve years: that Ada Yarrow was a tool, well-made and well-kept, and that a tool could not break your heart.

She sat in the back room of the Vellum Guild with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she had no intention of drinking, listening to the rain argue with the shutters, and waited for Ada to come home.

The room had not changed since the night the stranger died in it. The floorboards still held a faint stain shaped like nothing at all; the moss-map had grown from that spot once, Sable was nearly certain, before it learned to walk around in a girl's coat pocket. The Guild's collectors had torn three safehouses apart looking for it. They had found ledgers, vials, a memory of a wedding nobody remembered attending. They had not found the map, because the map did not wish to be found, and Sable had begun to suspect that maps—like apprentices, like daughters—went where they were needed, whether or not anyone approved.

"You're brooding," said Quill, from the doorway. He was the youngest collector, all elbows and earnestness, and he looked at Sable the way young men look at lighthouses. "You only make tea when you're brooding."

"I'm *thinking*," Sable said. "Brooding is thinking with better posture."

"Is she coming?"

"She comes home every third night to sleep in her own bed and check on her ledgers. She thinks I don't know." Sable turned the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "She thinks a great many things I don't know. That's my own fault, if we're honest. I taught her invisibility as an art form and then acted surprised when she applied it to me."

Quill hovered. He was good at hovering; he'd been trained by the best, which was to say by Sable, who had never once let him catch her doing anything worth reporting. "Do you want me there? When she comes?"

"No." She softened it with a smile, because Quill deserved softness even when the answer was no. "What happens tonight isn't collecting. It's confession. And confessions are best heard by one person at a time, or they turn into trials."

He left. The rain kept arguing. Sable drank the cold tea after all, because waste offended her more than bitterness did, and thought about the year Ada Yarrow had come to the Guild: seven years old, mud to the knees, a name written in a ledger older than the crown itself, and eyes like someone who had already learned that doors close.

Sable had told herself she'd taken the child in out of duty. Twelve years later, she could admit—to the rain, at least—that she had taken her in out of guilt, which is duty's uglier twin.

* * *

Ada came at midnight, as Sable knew she would, letting herself in through the window because doors were for people with less to hide. She moved quietly, but Sable had trained that quiet into her and knew its rhythm the way a mother knows footsteps on stairs.

"You can stop pretending to be asleep," Ada said, from the dark. "You snore."

"I do not snore. I *breathe with authority*." Sable lit the lamp. Ada stood in the doorway with rain in her hair and wariness all over her face, and something in Sable's chest folded up small. "Sit down, Yarrow. I'll pour. You look like a woman who's been eating her own cooking."

"That's slanderous. My cooking is excellent. It's everyone else's cooking that tries to kill people." But she sat, and she took the cup, and for a moment they were only two women in a lamplit room, and Sable let herself have the moment, because she was about to spend everything else.

"The map is growing," Ada said. Not accusing. Just noting, the way she noted everything, ledger-neat. "It's put out new borders since last week. There's a river now where there wasn't one. Wren says that means the Garden is deciding what it wants to be again. He says—" she hesitated, and Sable watched her decide to say it anyway, "—he says the map is *happy*. When I hold it."

"Of course it is." Sable set down her cup. "It's Orsen. And it has wanted to be held by you for a very long time."

The lamplight caught Ada's face going very still. Sable had seen that stillness before—in marksmen, in liars, in people standing at the edge of a truth they could feel coming like weather.

"Then tell me," Ada said quietly, "what I've forgotten."

And so Sable did what she had rehearsed in the rain-dark for six nights running. She told it plainly, because Ada deserved plainness, and because ornament would have been cowardice wearing jewelry.

"The Vellum Guild was founded by four people," Sable said. "Three of them you'd know from the histories, if the histories hadn't been edited—the fourth edited them. They didn't start as merchants of impossibility. They started as gardeners' children, back when the two realms still traded openly. Memories for luck. Names for songs. Small things, kind things. And every thirteenth moon, the two sides met at the covenant-stone and renewed their vows, and the trade stayed honest because both realms could see each other's faces."

"What happened?"

"Greed happened. It's never more interesting than that, though we dress it up in philosophy afterward. The founders realized that the whole covenant rested on one word—the true name of the thirteenth moon, spoken aloud at the stone, the knot that tied every other knot. One word. One word, and the trade between worlds belonged to whoever held it." Sable's hands were steady. She was proud of that. "So they stole it. Took it out of the air at the last renewal and locked it in a ledger, and the Garden began to close, and the garden-folk began to fade, and the Guild grew rich trading in the wreckage and calling itself a preservation society."

Ada's voice came out thin. "And me?"

"And you." Here was the part Sable had rehearsed least, because rehearsal hadn't helped; here was the part that simply had to be walked through barefoot. "A stolen name wants to be found. The Garden sent searchers—spirits wearing borrowed shapes, asking questions in markets and kitchens. The founders needed the theft hidden somewhere no searcher would think to look. So they hid it in a person. A child born under the intercalary moon, signatory by blood, bound by the old covenant clause to carry the debt. They wrote your name in the oldest ledger and they took your childhood as payment—every memory of the Garden, of the pact, of what you are—and they sealed the hole where those memories used to be with the moon's name."

"They hid a stolen word inside *me*."

"They hid it inside the one place the Garden would never search," Sable said. "Inside its own signatory. The lock and the key, wearing the same face." She reached beneath her chair and brought up the ledger—the oldest one, cracked leather, brass corners gone green—and laid it open between them. Ada's name sat at the top of the page in faded brown ink, dated twelve years past, beside a clause in a language that predated the crown. Below it, the torn stub where a page had been cut away.

Ada touched the stub with one finger, gently, the way she touched everything that couldn't fight back. "You were there," she said. "Twelve years ago. You signed the transfer."

"I countersigned." Sable did not look away. She owed the girl that much, at minimum. "I was young and ambitious and I told myself the child wouldn't miss what she couldn't remember. Then I spent twelve years making sure you'd never want for anything except the thing I'd taken. Do you understand now why I taught you immunity dish by dish? Why I made you invisible? A hidden vault should be as unremarkable as possible. I built you into the safest box I knew how to build, and then I had the indecency to love what was inside it."

The silence went on long enough that the lamp guttered and Sable trimmed the wick just to have something to do with her hands.

"Why tell me now?" Ada asked at last. Her voice was level. Sable admired it and hated it in equal measure. "Why not let me walk into the Garden ignorant? Ignorant girls survive negotiations. Informed ones get used."

"Because Sable the mentor is about to make you an offer," Sable said, "and you deserve to weigh it knowing exactly how crooked the scale is."

* * *

She rose and went to the iron cabinet at the back wall—the one Ada had opened a hundred times for inventory and never once questioned—and drew out a small coffer of black wood banded in silver. Inside, nested in wool, lay eleven glass vials, each no longer than a finger, each holding a color that didn't sit still: gold going green going gold, like light through leaves.

Ada's breath stopped audibly.

"Your childhood," Sable said. "Not all of it. Eleven years' worth, drawn off in the Guild's early days and stored against need. Your mother's kitchen. The orchard. The first time you saw the covenant-stone. The day you met—" she paused, and chose honesty over mercy, because mercy had gotten expensive, "—the day you met Orsen, before he was a map. Before he was anything but a spirit who liked you better than he liked protocol."

Ada's hand hovered over the coffer without touching it. Sable watched her want. It was the most naked thing she'd ever seen the girl do, and she had once watched her swallow hemlock without blinking.

"There's a price," Ada said. Flat. Braced.

"There's always a price; I run a business, not a charity." Sable closed the coffer but left it within reach, which was its own kind of cruelty, and she knew it, and did it anyway, because the offer had to be real or it meant nothing. "Give me the map. Give me Orsen. Bring him back to the Guild, and I'll return these to you tonight—all eleven years, poured back before dawn. You'll be whole. You'll remember your mother's voice. You'll remember why the garden-folk trusted you before any of us earned it." She leaned forward. "And you'll walk into the thirteenth night knowing exactly what you are, instead of guessing."

"And the Garden?" Ada asked softly. "And Wren? And Ilan, who's been poisoned on my watch for years while I called it loyalty? And every fading spirit behind that closing door?"

"The Garden survived the founders. It may survive us."

"It hasn't," Ada said. "You've seen the map grow *backward*, Sable. Rivers disappearing. Borders folding in. It's not deciding what it wants to be—it's forgetting." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and terribly clear. "You're offering me my childhood in exchange for everyone else's future. That's not a bargain. That's just the founders' trick with kinder handwriting."

"I know." Sable's voice cracked on it, finally, twelve years of posture failing all at once. "I know exactly what it is. I raised a girl who sees through locked doors, and then I handed her a key and asked her not to use it. I am not proud. I am not even clever. I am an old woman offering a child a bribe because the alternative is watching her die at a stone in the dark, and I find—" her breath hitched, "—I find I would rather be hated than bereaved."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The rain gave up arguing and settled into something gentler, almost apologetic.

Then Ada reached out—not for the coffer, Sable saw with a heartbreak that felt almost like pride, but for the oldest ledger—and turned it toward herself, and smoothed the torn stub flat with her palm.

"You countersigned the theft," Ada said. "Which means your name is on the covenant too. Which means—" and here she smiled, small and terrible and bright, the smile of a food-taster who has just identified the poison, "—you don't get to stand outside this anymore, Sable. Whatever happens on the thirteenth night, you're a signatory of the debt. The Garden will collect from you same as me."

Sable stared at her. "That's not how—"

"It's exactly how," said Ada, rising, tucking the ledger under her arm with the ease of long practice, because of course she'd steal it; Sable had trained her to. "Wren will want to see this. Ilan will want to see you. And Sable?" She paused at the window, rain-wet and resolute, twelve years old and twenty-four years old at once. "Keep the vials warm. I may yet come back for them—but it'll be *after* the pact is reforged, and I'll take them as a right, not a ransom."

The window banged shut. The lamp steadied. Sable sat alone with the empty coffer and the shape of everything she'd failed to buy, and after a while she began, very quietly, to laugh—because the vault had picked its own lock, and the thief had turned out to be the treasure all along.

Outside, over the rooftops, the moon was growing rounder. Nine nights remained. Somewhere across the city, a disgraced gardener was reading a stolen ledger by candlelight, and a prince was sharpening questions like knives, and the thirteenth moon was climbing toward the only night it would ever have.

Sable blew out the lamp and went to pack.
