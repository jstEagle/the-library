## 9. The Thirteenth Night

The moon rose where no moon should be.

Ada saw it first from the palace roof, a pale sliver hanging low over the eastern hills, thin and hungry, like a fingernail paring of silver. Wren had told her what to look for—the thirteenth turn, the intercalary moon, the one that appeared only when the old calendar strained at its seams—and still, seeing it made her breath catch. It looked less like a moon than like a hole in the sky, and the sky was leaning in to look through it.

"That's it," Wren said quietly. She stood at Ada's shoulder, her gardener's hands stained green to the wrist even now, even tonight, as if the soil refused to release her entirely. "Thirteen turns. The last knot is untying itself as we speak."

"Then we'd better go tie it back," said Ilan.

The prince had come dressed as a kitchen boy, which should have been absurd and was only mostly absurd. He still moved like a prince—spine straight, chin level—but he'd insisted on carrying the sack of provisions himself, and he'd stolen, with a thief's casual grace that Ada suspected he'd been practicing in secret, a wheel of cheese from his own kitchens.

"If we die," he said, "I want it noted that I died well-supplied."

"Noted," Ada said. "In the ledger."

She meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like a vow. The ledgers had been her whole life—every trade, every impossible thing, every careful line of ink in her own small hand—and tonight she was carrying the oldest of them under her coat, against her heart, with a page torn out of it twelve years ago and folded now into her sleeve. Sable had given it to her at the garden gate, pressed it into her hands with fingers that shook, and said only: *I kept it. I couldn't read it. It wouldn't let me.* Then she had turned and walked back toward the collectors, toward the Guild, toward the thing she had chosen instead of Ada, and Ada had not let herself cry, because crying takes time and the moon was already rising.

* * *

The door was where the map said it would be: at the bottom of the palace orchard, beneath the tree that fruited out of season, the tree Wren had been exiled for defending. The map—Orsen, though Ada didn't know that yet, wouldn't know it until later, and oh, how later would change everything—had grown overnight into something like a moss-covered stone with veins of silver, and it pulsed now in Ada's hands like a second heartbeat.

"Stand back," Wren said, and knelt, and did the thing she'd been doing to doors all her life, which was to *ask* them.

The ground opened like a book.

Not dramatically—no thunder, no light. The turf simply parted along a seam that had been waiting twelve years for someone to notice it, and steps went down into the dark, and the air that came up smelled like every summer Ada had ever half-remembered: cut grass, warm stone, rain on leaves, and underneath it all something sweeter and stranger, the smell of a place that had never needed to lie about what it was.

"Ladies first," Ilan said, and Ada, who had spent her whole life being invisible, went first, because that was what invisibility was *for*.

* * *

The Garden was dying, and it was the most beautiful dying thing Ada had ever seen.

They came up into a valley of glass-bright grass under a sky that held two moons—the real one, far and small, and the thirteenth, near and vast, so close Ada felt she could have touched it with a ladder and a brave afternoon. Flowers grew in spirals. Rivers ran uphill, politely, as if they'd been asked nicely. And everywhere, everywhere, things were *closing*. Blossoms folding like shutters. Paths unspooling behind them like ribbon. A tree at the valley's edge drew its roots up out of the ground with a sound like a sigh and walked away, unhurried, going wherever trees go when a contract expires.

"It's sealing itself," Wren whispered. "The covenant-stone's at the heart. Run."

They ran. The Garden, to its credit, seemed to want them to make it—paths lengthening to meet their stride, a fallen log flattening itself into a bridge at the last moment—but the Garden was also fighting itself, and the paths behind them were closing faster than the paths ahead could open. Ada heard, distantly, the sound of the gate breaking open behind them, and knew without turning that Sable had not waited politely at the threshold.

"Sable's in," she said.

"Then we run faster," said Ilan, who was, Ada noted, still holding the cheese.

* * *

They reached the covenant-stone as the last light of the false moon fell full upon it, and it was not what Ada expected, though perhaps she should have expected it, because everything in her life had turned out to be a ledger.

It was a stone, yes—waist-high, silver-veined, moss-grown—but it was also a book. The surface moved like the surface of the map had moved, words rising and sinking like fish under ice, and as Ada approached, the words rose and resolved, and she read them, and her knees nearly went.

*Let the signatory come forward and speak her true name, and the covenant be reforged.*

"I don't know my true name," she said aloud, stupidly, to a rock.

"You do," said Sable.

Ada turned. Sable stood at the edge of the clearing with the collectors fanned out behind her—Marrow and Quill and little Fen, who had been the youngest of them, who had once cried for a week over a bottled memory of someone else's dog. They looked terrible. They looked like people who had run a very long way through a dying country, which they had.

"The page," Sable said. She was breathing hard. "Read the page, Ada. I couldn't. It's keyed to you. It was always keyed to you."

Ada's hands shook as she unfolded the torn ledger page. Twelve years old, creased into lace, and the ink had waited for her, and when her eyes touched it, it bloomed.

*Clause the last: In payment for the keeping of the Thirteenth Name, the Guild shall surrender one child of the signatory line, memoryless, to be raised in the trade, that the signatory forget her covenant and the Name be kept secret. Signed: Ada Yarrow, aged six years, who agreed to everything, because she was six, and because they asked her kindly, and because she trusted them.*

The clearing went very quiet. Even the dying Garden seemed to hold its breath.

"They asked me," Ada said. Her voice came out small and terrible. "I *signed*. I was six, and I signed my own—"

"They asked all of us things," Sable said. "That's what the Guild is. That's what it's *for*. But you—" and here Sable's composure, that beautiful Guild-trained composure, cracked like an egg, "—you were the only one who was ever asked *that*. And I carried the page for twelve years because I couldn't give it to you, because giving it to you meant you'd hate me, and I couldn't bear it, and now look. Now look what my cowardice cost."

"Sable," Ada said.

"Read the rest."

*The Name so kept shall be returned to the stone by the signatory's own mouth, and a memory of equal weight shall be given in its place: the signatory shall surrender all she has stored, all she has kept, all the years of her keeping, that the ledger be balanced and the covenant renewed.*

Ada looked up. The stone waited. The moon waited. Behind Sable, Fen was crying again, and this time it wasn't about a dog.

"All of it," Ada said. "Everything I've stored. The immunity memories, the trades I've held in trust, the—the years. All of it goes into the stone."

"That's the price," Wren said softly. "Ada, that's *twelve years*. That's everything since you were six. You won't remember any of us. You won't remember—"

"I know what it is," Ada said. And here is the thing, the thing she would never be able to remember afterward and never needed to: she was not afraid. She had spent her whole life believing invisibility would save her, and it had, and it had also been a kind of dying, slow and comfortable, and she was tired of being a wall built around a stolen room. "I spent twelve years being no one so that no one could take anything from me. Well. Someone's taking it now. But *I'm* the one handing it over. That's different. That's the whole difference, Wren. It's the only difference there is."

"Ada." Ilan stepped forward, kitchen-boy costume and princely spine, and took her hands. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't," Ada said, "and even if there were—" she smiled, and it hurt, and it was real, "—you'd still be eating poisoned food if I hadn't decided some things were worth more than my own comfort. Let me decide one more thing."

She turned to Sable. "The Name. You kept it. Where is it?"

Sable reached into her coat and drew out a slim vial, and inside the vial something moved like a word trying to be spoken. "It was never mine to keep. It was never anyone's. That was the whole sin." She pressed it into Ada's hand. "I'm sorry. For all of it. I'll spend the rest of my life—" she stopped. "No. You won't remember my apology. So I'll spend the rest of my life making it true anyway."

Ada uncorked the vial over the stone, and the Thirteenth Name rose out of it like smoke with a spine, and the whole Garden turned toward her—not the trees and flowers, but the *Garden*, the presence in it, vast and green and patient beyond all patience—and Ada understood, in the last moment of her old life, that it had never been angry. It had only been waiting to be asked.

She spoke the Name.

She did not remember it afterward. But the stone remembered, and the moon remembered, and the sound of it went through the valley like a bell through water, and every closing thing in the Garden stopped closing, and opened, and the two moons in the sky leaned together like lovers, and the stone said, in a voice like roots growing:

*The Name is returned. Now the balance, signatory. What will you give?*

Ada looked at her found family one last time. Wren, weeping without shame. Ilan, holding the cheese like an offering. Sable, on her knees in the glass-bright grass.

"Everything," Ada said. "Gladly. It was never really mine anyway. It was built on a theft."

She stepped forward and laid her hand flat on the stone, and gave.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the strange thing, the thing she would carry forward without knowing she carried it: it did not hurt at all. It felt like setting down a load she had forgotten she was holding. Twelve years poured out of her like water from a jug—Sable's lessons, the taste of the poison-broth, the rooftop in the rain, the first time Ilan laughed at something she said, the smell of the ledger-room, the loneliness, the careful, artful, armored loneliness—and the stone drank it all and *thanked* her, and the last thing to go was the newest thing, which was Wren's hand in hers, and then—

* * *

Ada woke in the glass-bright grass under a sky with one moon, whole and round and ordinary, and the Garden was blooming, and she could not remember her own name.

But a woman with green-stained hands was holding it for her—her name, not her hand—saying it gently, like planting a seed: "Ada. Your name is Ada Yarrow."

"Ada," Ada repeated, and found it fit, like a key she'd never used in a lock that had always been hers.

"And I'm Wren. And this is Ilan, who is technically a prince, and that's Sable, who is going to spend a very long time being sorry, and—" Wren hesitated, and her eyes went to the covenant-stone, where something new was growing: a figure of moss and silver, unfolding itself upright, shaking leaves from its shoulders like a man waking from a very long sleep.

The figure looked at Ada. And Ada, who remembered nothing, who had no past at all, felt something in her chest that had no memory attached to it and needed none—recognition, warm and total, the way you know spring without being told.

"You," the figure said, and its voice was the map's voice, the moss-voice, the voice that had pressed itself into her hands in a back room while it died. "You carried me. You ran with me. You didn't give me up." It smiled, and the smile was summer. "My name is Orsen. I believe I owe you a garden."

Ada Yarrow, who had no yesterday, stood up in the middle of every tomorrow, and said: "Then let's start with breakfast. I'm told I'm very good with food."

And somewhere far away, in a palace orchard, a tree that fruited out of season quietly began to fruit in season, and out of season, and both at once, because the covenant was reforged, and the world had room in it again for impossible things.
