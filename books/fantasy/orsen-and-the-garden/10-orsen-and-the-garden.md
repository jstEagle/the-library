## 10. Orsen and the Garden

Ada woke the way honey pours—slowly, sweetly, without any sense of having been anywhere else.

She was lying on grass that was warm as fresh bread. Above her, branches moved in a wind she could not feel, and among the branches hung fruit of every season at once: cherries fat and dark beside apples still green and hard, pomegranates split open like secrets, a single pear so ripe it had begun to perfume the whole tree. She watched it all for a long time, because it was beautiful, and because she could not remember what any of it was called.

That didn't frighten her. That was the strange part. Somewhere inside her there was a shape where fear used to live, and the shape was empty, swept clean, waiting.

"Careful," said a voice above her. "The plum trees gossip. If you stare too long they'll start telling you things."

A woman crouched over her, soil on her knuckles, hair escaping its braid in every direction at once. Her face was weathered and kind and familiar in the way a house is familiar when you've lived in it your whole life but can't remember moving in.

"You're Wren," Ada said. The name arrived before anything else did—the first thing to come back to her, or perhaps the only thing that had never left.

Wren Halloway's eyes went bright and wet at once. "Yes," she said, as if confirming something enormous. "And you're Ada."

"Am I?" Ada sat up. The grass accepted her weight the way water accepts a stone. "I have the name. It fits. But I don't know what's under it." She looked down at her own hands—taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips, hands that had learned caution one careful swallow at a time—and found that even without memories, the hands knew themselves. They wanted, she realized, a spoon. A bowl. Something warm to make for someone.

"That's more than some people get back," Wren said gently. "Orsen says memory isn't stored in the head anyway. It's stored in the habits. In the hands." She hesitated. "Do you remember me? Really?"

Ada considered. "You grow things," she said slowly. "Out of season. You were punished for it. You're angry about it, but you've decided to be kind instead, which took work." She frowned. "I don't remember learning that. I just... know it. Like knowing salt from sugar by smell."

Wren laughed—a cracked, delighted sound—and pulled her into an embrace that smelled of loam and rosemary. "Oh, you're still *you*," she said into Ada's hair. "Thank every moon there is. You're still you."

* * *

They fed her breakfast in a garden that had no edges.

That was the first thing Ada understood about the place: it went on. Not vast, exactly—vastness was a thing with far sides—but *onward*, the way a story goes onward, each turn revealing another turn. There were orchards and herb-beds and a stream that ran uphill twice a day out of politeness, and everywhere the small busy presences of the garden-folk: figures like green shadows, like moss given manners, who watched her from the hedges with enormous curiosity and enormous relief.

"They've been waiting twelve years," Wren explained, walking her along a path that rearranged itself helpfully beneath their feet. "Since the last renewal failed. Every year the Garden got smaller. Every year the trades through your Guild got a little more... frayed. Bottled luck going sour. Borrowed names forgetting their doors." She glanced sideways. "You fixed it. Whatever you gave up—you fixed it."

"I wish I remembered giving it up," Ada said.

"No, you don't," said a third voice, warm as a hearth. "Believe me. I was there. You were very brave about it, and very frightened, and then you weren't frightened anymore, and then it was done. It's better this way. Trust me—I'm an authority on being given away."

He came around the hedge carrying a basket of eggs that were, on inspection, also small round stones, and possibly both at once. He looked like a young man the way a map looks like a country: accurately, but not completely. Moss grew at his collar. His smile was summer, and when he saw Ada looking at him, the smile softened into something older and sadder and glad.

"Orsen," Ada said. Again the name arrived ahead of everything else, like a scout.

"The one and regrettably only." He set down the impossible eggs. "How are you feeling? Honestly. People always say 'fine' after sacrificing everything they are, and I find it unsatisfying."

Ada took inventory. "Empty," she said. "But not hollow. There's a difference. Hollow is when something was taken. Empty is when you made room." She turned her hands over, studying them. "I keep reaching for things that aren't there. Yesterday morning I reached for a ledger that doesn't exist anymore. I knew exactly where it would be on the shelf. My hand knew." She looked up. "Who was I? Before?"

Orsen and Wren exchanged a look over her head—the particular look of two people deciding how much truth a person can carry at breakfast.

"You were a taster," Wren said. "For the prince. You swallowed poison so he wouldn't have to, for years, and nobody ever thanked you, and you did it anyway."

"And you kept books," Orsen said, "for a trade in impossible things. And you lied beautifully exactly once, to protect me, when I was dying and didn't deserve it. And you chose truth over the people who raised you, at the end, when it cost the most." He crouched so his eyes were level with hers. "That's who you were. The rest is detail. And the details—" he tapped her scarred fingertips, "—are still in there. Growing back. Like moss."

"Is that a promise or a threat?" Ada asked.

"With me, historically, both."

* * *

Prince Ilan arrived on the third day, through a door in a palace orchard that had recently begun behaving strangely, escorted by a royal gardener who had been quietly reinstated, un-disgraced, and promoted in a single furious decree.

He came through the door dusty and out of breath and stopped dead at the sight of her.

"Your Highness," Ada began, out of pure habit, and then heard herself, and winced. "Sorry. I'm told we were past that."

"We were extremely past that," Ilan said. His voice was steady but his eyes were not. He crossed the grass between them like a man crossing ice. "Do you remember me at all?"

Ada studied him. Tall, sleepless, a jaw built for argument, hands that fidgeted with rings when worried—which they were doing now. Somewhere in her body, unbidden, she knew the exact flavor of his favorite dish. Somewhere deeper, she knew why she'd never told him it was poisoned, and then why she finally had.

"You're angry," she said slowly, feeling it out. "Not at me. At yourself. For eating it for years without noticing. For letting someone else be poisoned in your place because it was easier not to look." She tilted her head. "And you're here because you couldn't stand not knowing if I was alive. Which means somewhere along the way you started thinking of me as a person instead of a service. I don't remember that happening. But I believe it happened."

Ilan let out a long breath. "That's the most anyone's ever seen me," he said. "And you did it with no memories at all. I'm almost offended."

"He's like this," Wren confided. "Takes everything personally."

"It's a princely discipline," Ilan agreed, and then, quieter, sitting down on the grass beside them like an ordinary tired man: "The palace is different. Since the covenant reforged. Corvane resigned—said the truth was suddenly everywhere and spying had become redundant. The Guild's ledgers burned themselves, did you know? All except one page. Sable sent it. With a note."

He handed it over carefully, the way one handles a sleeping animal.

Ada unfolded it. It was the torn page—she knew that instantly, the way her hands knew things now, the way they knew spoons. On it, in a child's careful handwriting, was a name. Her name. Her true name, the one she had spoken into the covenant-stone, the one that had opened everything.

Below it, in an old woman's sharp script: *You paid for all of us. I am keeping this page so someone remembers you did. It is the least dishonest thing I have ever done. —S.*

Ada read it twice. Then she folded it along its old creases and put it in her pocket, over her heart, where ledgers apparently lived now.

"She trained me," Ada said. "Didn't she. And she stole my childhood. Both true."

"Yes," Orsen said softly. "People are usually both true. It's terribly inconvenient." He plucked a plum from the air and offered it around. "Which is why gardens are better than ledgers. In a garden, both things can grow at once, and neither has to be torn out."

* * *

The seasons in the Garden did not follow one another; they mingled, argued, reconciled. Ada learned its rhythms the way she had once learned poisons—one careful taste at a time, building tolerance, building trust.

She cooked. That was the great surprise of her new life, or rather no surprise at all, because her hands had known it before she did. She cooked for Wren, who cried into her soup the first time and claimed it was the onions, and for Ilan, who visited through the orchard door whenever the palace grew heavy, and for Orsen, who ate everything with the astonishment of someone who had spent centuries being carried in pockets instead of fed at tables.

One evening—it was evening because the garden agreed it should be—they sat together under the tree that fruited in all seasons at once, and Ada asked the question she had been circling for days.

"If my memories come back—if they're growing back, like you said—will I want them? The ones I gave away. The Guild years. Will I miss her? The woman I was?"

Orsen was quiet a long moment. Fireflies assembled overhead into constellations that hadn't been invented yet.

"When you spoke your name into the stone," he said, "the pact demanded a memory of equal weight to what was stolen. Twelve years of hiding. Of swallowing. Of making invisibility an art." He shook his head. "You gave it freely. And here is the thing about freely-given things in a reforged covenant: they aren't destroyed. They're *planted*."

He nodded toward the tree above them.

Ada looked up. Among the impossible fruit hung small glass bottles, glinting—dozens of them, ripening like lanterns, each one holding a swirl of light. One bottle, nearer than the rest, glowed the color of lamplight on vellum, of ink drying late at night, of a lie told well to save a dying stranger.

"Those are mine," she whispered.

"Every one," Orsen said. "And here is the other thing about freely-given things: you may pick them again someday. When you're ready. Not to become her again—but to *know* her. To thank her." He smiled, and the smile was summer, but gentler now. "Memory outlasts forgetting, Ada Yarrow. That's the whole secret of gardens. Nothing planted in love is ever truly lost. It just waits for its season."

Ada reached up—not taking, just touching—and the nearest bottle warmed against her fingertips like a hand held briefly in the dark.

"Then I'll wait too," she said. "I'm told I'm patient. I must have practiced."

"You have no idea," said Wren, laughing, pouring the wine.

Above them the thirteenth moon rose—whole, named, home—and in the palace orchard far away, and in every hedgerow and forgotten corner of the human realm, impossible things quietly went on becoming possible, tended now by a pastless woman with a taster's careful hands, who had traded every yesterday for a garden full of tomorrows, and found, to her lasting surprise, that she had gotten the better end of the bargain.

Though sometimes, on quiet evenings, a bottle in the high branches would glow a little brighter, and Ada would look up from her cooking and wonder—

—and the wondering, she had decided, was its own kind of remembering, and it tasted, at last, of nothing bitter at all.
