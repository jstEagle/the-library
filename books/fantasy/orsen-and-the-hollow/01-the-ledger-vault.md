## 1. The Ledger Vault

The punishment for a botched run was supposed to be rope duty. Rope duty meant three weeks of coiling hemp in the cold yard, palms cracked raw, listening to Brannick tell the story of how Theo Vane had dropped a crate of emberwine down a ravine because he'd been watching the moon instead of the trail.

Instead, Corvess sent him below.

"The ledger vault," the Guildmaster said, handing him an iron key on a ring heavy enough to bend a belt. "Inventory it. Every shelf, every box, every book. Write down what's there and what's missing. You'll be alone, which suits you — you've proven you can't be trusted with company."

Theo took the key and didn't argue. Arguing with Corvess was like arguing with weather.

He'd been inside the vault exactly once before, at nine years old, when Old Maret had died and they'd carried her effects down to be catalogued. He remembered cold that lived in the stone, shelves receding into dark like the ribs of something swallowed whole, and the smell — dust, tallow, old ink, and under it all something sweeter and wronger, like fruit rotting behind a wall.

Nothing had improved.

The lamp he carried made a bubble of light maybe six feet wide. Beyond it, the archive went back and back, cut into the mountain's root beneath the Hollow itself, older than the guild hall above it, older probably than the guild. The smugglers' charter hung in the entry chamber upstairs, framed in blackwood: *By cunning and by silence, we hold the pass.* A hundred years of guild law grew out of those words. Theo had recited them at his oath-taking at twelve, voice cracking, Corvess's hand heavy and warm on his shoulder.

*You're one of ours now,* Corvess had said. *Nobody else will ever have you.*

Down here, nobody had anybody.

He started at the first shelf because starting anywhere else would just be stalling. Inventory work was simple: open the box, count what was inside, write it down. Deeds. Toll ledgers going back decades, columns of numbers in dead men's hands. Confiscated letters, bundled and tied. A strongbox of foreign coin nobody could spend without questions. A jar of teeth, labeled in Maret's handwriting: *assorted.*

He worked until his fingers ached and his lamp burned low, and it was somewhere past the middle — shelf fourteen, the deep rows where the mountain's damp got into everything — that he found the book that wasn't in any catalogue.

It sat alone on its shelf, which should have been his first warning. Everything else down here was stacked, bundled, crammed. This one lay centered, squared to the shelf's edge, as if someone had placed it recently and carefully. Bound in grey leather gone soft as skin. No title. No clasp.

He told himself it was a ledger. That was the job; that was the word for every book in this room. He opened it.

It wasn't a ledger.

The pages were dense with a hand he didn't recognize — not guild script, not merchant cipher, nothing taught in the lowland schools Theo had been schooled in between runs. The letters were tall and narrow, slanted like rain falling sideways, and the ink had gone brown with age except where it hadn't. Some entries were newer. Some were very new.

He couldn't read most of it. But the structure repeated, page after page, decade after decade by the look of it: a block of that strange slanting script, then below it, in plainer hand, a line or two of translation added later — guild additions, guild amendments, like footnotes nailed onto someone else's sermon.

*...the bearer comes when the hollow opens...*

*...the thorn remembers what the crown forgot...*

*...first snow closes the road and the debt comes due...*

Prophecy. Down here, in the sealed dark, a hundred years of prophecy about Thornfell and the pass and debts, and no one had ever mentioned it. Not in lessons. Not in oaths. Not once in seventeen years of being raised inside the only family he had.

His lamp guttered. He thumbed to the last written page, meaning only to see how far the thing went, whether it ended mid-sentence like everything else down here seemed to.

It didn't end mid-sentence. It ended precisely. Deliberately. Like a signature.

The final entry was short. The old slanting script filled three lines, and beneath them, in fresher ink — ink that still smelled faintly when he bent close, ink that could not possibly be more than days old — someone had written in plain trade-tongue:

*The Hollow-Bearer walks on the eve of first snow. His name is written in the vault. Theo Vane. He carries what was taken. The mountain is waiting.*

Theo read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you'd check a tally you thought was wrong.

Tonight was the eve of first snow. Everyone in the Hollow knew it; the air had that stopped, held-breath quality, and the old-timers had been squinting at the peaks all day saying *tonight, maybe tomorrow.* And his name — his actual name, spelled correctly, in a book that predated his birth by generations — sat there in ink that wasn't dry.

Forgery. It had to be forgery. Someone was having a joke, or setting him up, or — worse — someone had written this recently, which raised a question much uglier than fate: *who had been down here, and why?*

He stood very still in the lamplight and felt the vault rearrange itself around him. It had been a punishment an hour ago. Now it was a room someone had prepared.

He looked up the row toward the stairs, and the dark at the end of it, and heard — or thought he heard — a door close somewhere above. Soft. Careful. The way Corvess closed doors when he didn't want to be heard coming.

Theo shoved the book back onto the shelf, squared it exactly as he'd found it, and picked up his inventory list with hands that wanted to shake. Routine. He was doing routine. Counting boxes, writing numbers, a boy punished for spilled wine, nothing here but dust and dead men's accounts.

The footsteps came down the stairs anyway.

Corvess came alone, which was itself an answer. Guildmasters didn't descend into their own sealed vaults unescorted unless whatever they were checking on mattered more than ceremony. He carried his own lamp, and the light caught the grey in his beard and the flat patience in his eyes — the look he wore when a run went bad and he was deciding who paid for it.

"Inventoried much?" he asked.

"Fourteen shelves," Theo said. "Mostly toll records. Some deeds. A jar of—"

"I know what's down here." Corvess walked the row slowly, lamp raised, and Theo watched him and understood, with a sick downward lurch, that the man wasn't looking at the shelves. He was looking at the gaps between things. Checking placement. Checking *order.* His gaze snagged on shelf fourteen for half a heartbeat — half a heartbeat too long — and moved on.

"Good work," Corvess said. "Finish tomorrow."

"Sir?"

"You're done tonight." He set his lamp on a shelf and held out his hand. "Key."

Theo gave it over. Their fingers almost touched around the iron ring, and Corvess closed his fist around the key before it could swing free, and looked at Theo — really looked, the way he looked at cargo worth stealing.

"You dropped a crate," Corvess said quietly. "Cost us two hundred crowns. I should have you on ropes till spring."

"Yes, sir."

"Instead I'm doubling your guard duty. North stair, midnight watch, every night this week." He picked up his lamp. "Alone up there too. Seems you do your best work alone."

It was a punishment shaped exactly like a reward, and Theo had grown up fluent in Corvess, so he heard it for what it was: the boy who found things in the dark gets posted where he can be watched finding them. Or watched doing something. The north stair overlooked the vault door.

"Thank you, sir," Theo said.

"Mm." At the foot of the stairs Corvess paused, back turned. "Theo. Some books down here are mislabelled. If you come across anything that seems out of place — anything at all — you bring it to me. Not to Maret's ghost, not to Brannick, not to your own clever head. To me."

"Yes, sir."

"And Theo?" He didn't turn around. "First snow's coming. Best sleep fast."

His footsteps faded up the stairs. Then the sound Theo had never heard in seventeen years inside these walls: the vault door closing from the outside, and the lock turning, slow, deliberate, twice.

Theo stood in the dark with his little lamp and his useless list, sealed in with a hundred years of secrets, one of which had his name in wet ink.

They locked him in. They actually locked him in.

He sat down on the cold floor and laughed once, without any humor in it at all, because there was nowhere to go and nothing to do but think — and thinking was the one punishment Corvess had never managed to assign on purpose.

*He carries what was taken.*

Taken what? From whom?

Somewhere above him, muffled through a century of stone, the bell in the guild hall began to ring midnight. And through the high slit of a vent near the ceiling, thin as a knife cut, the first flakes of the season drifted down into the lamplight — melting even as he watched, but unmistakable.

First snow. Eve of it, anyway. Close enough.

Theo looked at the dark row where shelf fourteen waited, and understood that the choice had already been made for him the moment Corvess turned that key. He could sit here until morning and be the boy who read a book. Or he could find out what a smuggler's son of the Hollow Guild did with a truth that had his name on it.

The lock clicked again upstairs. Boots, moving away.

Then silence, and snow, and the long dark of the mountain holding its breath around him.
