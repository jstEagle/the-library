## 2. What the Charter Owes

Theo had spent three days being ordinary, and it was the hardest work of his life.

He ran the walls at dusk. He checked the seals on the south gate and signed the watch-book in his own neat, guilty hand. He ate stew with the other boys and laughed at Rennick's impression of the toll-warden at Grey Ford, and every laugh felt like a coin he was spending from a purse that didn't belong to him.

*Theo Vane. The Hollow-Bearer. Tonight's date, inked a hundred years before he was born.*

The vault had been locked with iron since Corvess found him reading. Two guards on the door now instead of one — that was the part that told him everything. You didn't post two men on an empty archive. You posted two men on a secret.

And Corvess hadn't punished him. That was the part that kept Theo awake. The Guildmaster had looked at him for a long moment — not angry, not surprised, which was worse than either — and said only, "Mind the inventory, boy. Some ledgers are older than your usefulness." Then he'd locked the door himself, with his own key, and walked away like a man closing a wound.

Which meant Corvess knew what the book said.

Which meant it wasn't a forgery. Forgers don't frighten Guildmasters.

On the third night, the fog came up the Hollow thick as spilled milk, and Theo did what he'd been born to do. He'd learned to pick locks before he could write his name; Sera Vane, his mother, dead six years now, used to say he'd been born holding a tension wrench instead of a spoon. The guild's own lock on the vault door was a good one — Hollow-worked, three tumblers and a false gate — but Theo had oiled its habits over years of quiet errands, and by the time the bell tolled midnight he was inside, the door pulled shut behind him, a sliver of candle burning low.

The prophecy book sat where he'd left it, as if it had been waiting.

He read it properly this time. Slowly. The early pages were dry stuff — harvest tithes, warden names, a treaty written in two hands, one human and one that looped and curled like frost ferns on glass. Two signatures. One seal: a mountain split by a road.

Then, a hundred years ago, the entries stopped being shared and started being human-only. And the handwriting changed to something Theo knew intimately, because it was carved over the guildhall door:

*By charter, the road is ours.*

Except it wasn't *by* charter. It was *in place of* one. He found the page near the end, pressed so thin it was nearly translucent, and read the words three times because his mind kept sliding off them like rain off oilcloth:

*Let it be entered that the Charter of the Hollow was taken, not given. The wardens of Thornfell kept the passes under bond of the old accord, and we took their bond-paper and called it our founding. Let it be entered further that the debt stands until the paper crosses back over the high snow, carried by one who owes the guild nothing and is owed nothing by it. Before first snow falls on Thornfell, let the truth be borne or let the Hollow be forfeit.*

Below that, in ink so fresh it might have been written yesterday:

*Theo Vane. The Hollow-Bearer.*

Tonight's date.

Theo sat down on the cold floor of the vault, in the dark, in the guild he'd given his whole life to, and felt something in his chest go quiet and cold.

They were thieves. Not smugglers-with-a-code, not the grey-market family that fed the valley when the lords' taxes starved it — thieves, plain and simple, who'd stolen a paper off a people who had kept these mountains safe for centuries, and built a hundred years of songs and rules and *belonging* on top of the theft. Every oath he'd ever sworn had been interest paid on stolen goods.

"No," he said aloud, to nobody, because saying it made it less true and he needed it to be less true.

It didn't work.

The charter itself lay in the last drawer of the iron cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth, exactly where a thing would be if it had been hidden rather than honored. He unwrapped it. Heavy vellum, both hands' writing, the split-mountain seal in wax the color of old blood. It was warm. That was the worst part. Paper shouldn't be warm.

Theo looked at it for a long time.

Then he thought about Corvess locking the door with his own key. About the doubled guard. About what a man does to a secret he means to keep buried forever — and about what such a man does to the boy who found it.

He put the charter inside his coat, against his chest, where the warmth stopped feeling strange and started feeling like a hand.

* * *

High above the tree line, where the wind spoke a language older than any guild, the wardens felt the paper move.

*It wakes,* said Ilen, standing barefoot in the snow that never melted at the crown of Thornfell, her ears tilted toward the valley. *A hundred years asleep in their iron box, and tonight it remembers its weight.*

*The bearer?* asked Old Vessha, whose antlers had gone grey as birch.

*A boy. Small. Afraid.* Ilen smiled without warmth. *They always are.*

*Will he carry it true, or will he carry it home to the ones who stole it?*

Ilen watched the fog swallow the lights of the Hollow below, one lamp at a time, like a hand closing.

*That,* she said, *is what first snow is for.*

* * *

He almost made the north stair.

The charter against his chest, the ledger left open on the floor as evidence or apology or both, Theo had his hand on the cold iron of the delivery hatch — the smugglers' exit, the one route out of the guildhall that didn't pass the guard room — when a lantern flared behind him.

"Going somewhere, little Vane?"

Brack Halloway filled the stairwell like the stairwell was a coat he'd outgrown. Corvess's enforcer. The man who'd taught Theo to throw a knife, then taught him to fetch the knives back out of things. He didn't look angry either. Everyone in this guild tonight was doing grief badly.

"The Guildmaster wants a word," Brack said.

"He had three days of words."

"He had three days of hoping you'd be smart." Brack came down a step. "Put the paper down, Theo. Whatever you think you read down here — books lie. Guild doesn't."

"The guild's whole life is a book," Theo said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "You should read it sometime."

Something shifted in Brack's face — not doubt, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a question he'd buried so long ago he'd forgotten the shape of it. For half a heartbeat, Theo thought that might be enough.

Then Brack moved, fast as a trap springing, and Theo did the only thing a small man in a narrow stairwell can do: he dropped the lantern and went dark.

Glass burst. Flame licked oil across the steps. In the shouting and the sudden light, Theo got the hatch open and rolled through into the cold — and Brack caught him by the ankle, and the knife went into Theo's side just above the hip, shallow, a hook more than a wound, meant to hold him not kill him.

Meant to hold him.

Theo twisted anyway.

He came out of the hatch into the drainage cut behind the guildhall bleeding freely, and he ran — not up the main roads, not toward the gates, but down, into the underside of the Hollow, the smuggler's veins that no honest map showed and no honest hunter remembered. The cistern run. The Widow's Crawl. The collapsed chimney behind the old mint where the bricks still bore his mother's chalk marks, *this way, love*, in her looping hand, guiding him out of the mountain she'd never gotten out of herself.

Behind him, boots. Whistles. The hunting-horn that meant *guild business*, blown by the family that had fed him and trained him and named him, and now wanted him back in one piece so that a man he loved like a father could decide what to do with him.

Loyalty, Theo discovered, didn't break all at once. It curdled. It went over slow, the way milk goes over, and there was a long stretch where it was both things at once, sweet and spoiled, and you couldn't spit it out because you couldn't stop swallowing.

He climbed out of the last tunnel as the sky over the valley went grey, and there they were — the peaks of Thornfell, white-capped, gleaming, beautiful.

And along their highest shoulder, fine as sifted flour, falling soft and silent and total:

First snow.

"Damn it," Theo whispered, pressing his hand to his bleeding side. "Damn it, I'm coming."

He turned his face to the mountain and started to climb.
