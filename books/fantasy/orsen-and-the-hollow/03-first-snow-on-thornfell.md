## 3. First Snow on Thornfell

The snow came down like a verdict.

Theo had crossed the high passes in every season the mountain allowed, and one or two it didn't. He knew the smuggler's roads — the switchbacks hidden under scree, the ledges wide enough for one man and no lantern, the caves where you could wait out weather that would strip flesh from bone. But this was different. This was first snow, and first snow on Thornfell did not fall so much as it descended, a white wall moving with intent, erasing the road behind him faster than his boots could leave it.

He was glad of it, in a way. If he couldn't see the way back, neither could they.

"You're limping," Ilen said, without turning around.

The warden scout moved through the blizzard like it was a rumor about someone else. No snow seemed to land on her; it bent away at the last moment, courteous and wrong. Her cloak was the grey of old ice, and her eyes caught what little light remained and gave none back.

"I'm walking," Theo said.

"You're limping *and* walking. The knife wound from your own guild's enforcer is opening again." She stopped, finally, and looked back at him, and the wind went quiet around them in a way that felt less like mercy and more like being listened to. "I could carry you. It would take nothing."

"That's exactly why I don't trust you."

Something moved across her face — not quite a smile. "Good," she said. "The last Hollow-Bearer trusted us completely, and look how that century turned out." She nodded up-slope. "There's a hollow under the ridge ahead. We shelter there tonight. The pass tomorrow."

"The pass is a day and a half."

"The pass is wherever I say it is. The mountain owes me favors."

They climbed. Theo kept his hand on the charter inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and told himself the heat he felt from it was only his own blood. Below them, somewhere in the whiteout, the guild's hunters were climbing the same road. He knew it the way you know a bruise is coming — not by seeing it, but by knowing your own people. Corvess wouldn't send men. Corvess would come herself, because Corvess never asked anyone to do what she wasn't willing to do, and that was precisely the problem. That was the whole trap of loving someone competent.

* * *

The hollow was a seam in the rock, older than the road, older maybe than the guild. Ilen built no fire. Instead she set three stones in a triangle and breathed on them, and warmth bloomed between them like something planted.

"Sleep," she said.

Theo sat with his back to the stone, charter against his chest, knife loose in his hand. "You said you'd test me. On the road. You said the wardens would want to know if I'm worth the truth."

"I said the truth would want to know if *you're* worth it." She settled across the warm stones, folding like water finding its level. "And it doesn't need my help asking. It needs only your sleep. Are you brave enough for that, smuggler?"

He should have said no. He thought about saying no. But the wound in his side was singing, and the warmth from the stones was soft as a hand on the back of his neck, and he was so tired of running from things that knew his name.

He slept.

The vault again. Of course the vault. Candles burning low, dust hanging in the light like it was nailed there, and the ledger open on the stand — but now the pages were turning themselves, backward, a hundred years of entries unwriting, and then the pages were faces. Rellik, who taught him to pick locks before he could read. Marda in the kitchens, who fed him when the rationing bit. Corvess, younger than he'd ever known her, laughing at something, and then not laughing, looking at him across the vault with an expression he'd never seen on her: grief, naked and unarmored.

*"You were mine,"* the dream-Corvess said. *"Not the prophecy's. Not theirs. Mine. And you chose a book over a family."*

"I chose the truth over a lie we all live inside," Theo said, and hated how young his voice sounded.

*"The lie keeps them alive. The truth will scatter them into the cold like seed. Is that mercy?"*

"It's the only honest thing anyone's offered them in a hundred years—"

The vault tilted. The candles guttered. And now it was the enforcer's knife in his side again, hot and real, and Rellik's face above him, disappointed, and Marda's voice: *"We starved before the charter. We'll starve after. At least before, we starved honest—"*

"No," Theo said. "No, that's not true, that's not—"

*"Then prove it,"* said the mountain, in a voice made of every voice he loved, *"in the morning, at knifepoint, when it costs you everything."*

He woke with frost on his eyelashes and Ilen watching him, unreadable.

"Well?" he croaked.

"You wept in your sleep and apologized to a kitchen," she said. "The mountain is satisfied. I am not, yet. But the mountain outranks me." She rose. "Come. First snow won't wait, and neither will your guild."

* * *

From the other side of the storm, Corvess watched the mountain close its throat.

She stood at the tree line where the pines gave up and the stone began, seven hunters behind her, and she let the snow hit her face until it stopped feeling like anything. Forty years on Thornfell. She had carried men off this slope on her back. She had buried three of them under cairns the wardens' magic would never trouble to find.

And now she was climbing it to kill a boy she'd pulled out of a gutter at nine years old, all elbows and stolen apples and a laugh like a lock picking itself.

"Guildmaster," said Vetch beside her, breath ragged. "The trail's gone. Snow's taken it. We should turn back, wait for—"

"We turn back, the boy reaches their court, and the charter comes home." She didn't look at him. "You know what happens then."

"They say the wardens are merciful now. That they only want—"

"The wardens were merciful a century ago too, and look what our founders did with the mercy." She started up-slope. The hunters followed because following her was the only skill any of them had ever mastered. "A guild built on a stolen deed is a guild standing on a cliff edge pretending it's ground. I will not be Guildmaster of the Fall. I'd rather be the knife."

But that was the lie she told herself, and she was too good a liar even for herself. The truth was simpler and worse: she could survive losing the charter. She could survive losing the guild. She could not survive the boy looking at her across a blade the way he must have looked at her since he found that book — like she was something he'd misread his whole life.

She found his tracks at dusk, half-filled already, and beside them prints that were no print at all: shapes pressed into snow that melted upward instead of down. Warden-craft. So the fae had sent an escort. So the boy had chosen, truly chosen, and was past calling back.

"Vetch. Kess. With me. The rest hold the treeline." She drew her knife and checked the edge out of habit, though the edge had never once failed her. "If it comes to it, nobody touches him but me."

"Guildmaster," Kess said carefully, "is that an order of protection or an order of precedence?"

Corvess looked at the white dark swallowing the slope above them and did not answer, which was an answer, and everyone heard it.

* * *

The pass came at dawn, or what the storm pretended was dawn — a grey brightening, wind dropping just enough to make the world legible. A saddle of bare stone between two walls of ice, a hundred paces across, and beyond it the descent into the warden valleys, where the air already smelled different. Older. Greener under the white.

Theo stood at the near edge and felt the charter pulse against his ribs like a second heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"She's here," Ilen said quietly.

He'd known. You always knew, with family — not their footsteps, just a pressure change, the sense of a door opening somewhere in you. He turned, and there she was at the far end of the saddle, hood back, silver-streaked hair whipping, three hunters fanned behind her and the knife already loose in her fist. Not drawn. Just *loose*, the way it always was, the way a mother's hand is always ready.

"Theo." Her voice carried clean through the wind. "Give it to me, and walk home. That's all. Give it to me and everything is as it was."

"As it was," Theo said. "Built on theft. Fed by a lie. Every run we ever ran, every coin we ever split — all of it spent money that was never ours." His voice cracked and he let it. "You taught me that the worst thing a smuggler can be isn't caught. It's *complicit.*"

"I taught you to survive."

"You taught me to survive *honestly*. That's what you called it. Your one rule." He stepped onto the saddle, one boot, then the other, stone slick under him, the drop on either side deep enough to swallow armies. "So here I am. Being honest. It's costing me everything, just like you always promised it would."

Corvess crossed toward him, and the hunters spread along the edges like a net closing, and Ilen did not move at all, which was somehow louder.

Ten paces. Five. Close enough that he could see she'd been crying, or the wind had been, and couldn't tell the difference anymore, and maybe neither could she.

"Last chance, boy." The knife came up — not threatening, not yet, just *present*, an argument made of steel. "Hand it over."

"No," Theo said.

The word was small. It was the smallest word he'd ever spoken, and the largest thing he'd ever done.

Corvess's face broke — not with rage but with something rawer, the expression from his dream, grief with nowhere left to hide — and the knife flashed, and he didn't reach for his own blade, didn't flinch, just stood there holding the charter and the truth of it, and the steel kissed his throat cold as the snow—

"Hold," said a voice like ice shifting, and the wind stopped mid-gust.

Wardens stood on the ridgeline above the pass — a dozen, two dozen, grey cloaks and eyes like frozen stars, arrived without arriving. And at their center, tall and terrible and sorrowful, a figure whose crown was made of the first snowfall of every winter for a hundred years.

"Guildmaster of the thieves," the warden queen said, "your road ends here. Yours began long ago, thief-child. Come down to the court beneath the ice, both of you — and bring the truth you've bled so differently to protect."

The knife stayed at Theo's throat. Corvess's hand did not shake. But her eyes, meeting his, finally did.

"Walk," she whispered, so only he could hear. "Walk down, and whatever they do to me — you *walk.*"

And Theo understood, with a clarity that hurt worse than the blade, that she had never been hunting him at all.
