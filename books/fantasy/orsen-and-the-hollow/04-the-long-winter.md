## 4. The Long Winter

The wardens' court was not what Theo had imagined. He had pictured thrones of black ice, a hall carved from the mountain's frozen heart, judges with antlers and eyes like winter stars. What he found instead was a hollow beneath the glacier — a cathedral of blue light where the ice itself glowed from within, thick as fortress walls, old as the mountain's memory. The fae stood in a ring around him, tall and thin and wrong in the way that beautiful things are wrong, their skin like frost over river water, their faces too smooth to read.

Ilen stood at his shoulder. She had not left him since the pass.

"Speak," said the eldest warden, a figure whose hair was white as the glacier and whose voice came from everywhere at once, like wind through a crevasse. "You carry the Hollow. Speak what it holds."

Theo's hands were still shaking. They had been shaking since Corvess's knife had kissed his ribs at the pass — a shallow line, more warning than wound, though it had bled enough to soak through two shirts on the climb down. He unwrapped the charter from its oilcloth with fingers that would not quite obey him.

The parchment was older than anything he had ever touched. Older than the guild. Older than the smugglers' roads he'd grown up running. The seal of the wardens sat broken at its foot, and above it, in ink gone brown with a century of hiding, the founding charter of the Hollow Guild — signed by men who had never owned what they signed away.

"The charter is stolen," Theo said. His voice echoed strangely under the ice, doubled and tripled until it sounded like many voices speaking at once. "The guild didn't found itself on empty land. It founded itself on this. On your claim. Your wardenship of Thornfell. A hundred years ago your people were exiled from the peaks, and the thieves who took this wrote themselves into history as founders instead of what they were."

He swallowed. "What they were. What we were."

The ring of wardens did not move. The blue light pulsed once, slow, like breathing.

"And you," said the eldest. "You are the Hollow-Bearer. The prophecy named you. You could have burned this. You could have buried it deeper than it was buried before. Instead you carried it through first snow, bleeding, hunted by your own blood-kin." A pause, long as a held breath. "Why?"

Theo thought about lying. Old habit. Smugglers lie the way other people breathe.

"I used to think the guild was mine," he said. "The only thing that was mine. Corvess pulled me out of a gutter when I was seven and I figured I owed her everything after that. But owing someone everything isn't the same as them owning you." He looked down at the charter. "And she taught me that herself, in a way. She sent hunters after me. She'd have killed me for this paper. So either the family was never real, or the truth matters more than the family. I had to find out which."

* * *

The judgment did not take long. Fae courts, Ilen told him afterward, do not deliberate; they simply decide, the way ice decides to crack.

The sentence for theft of the wardenship, spoken aloud beneath the glacier, was death. All of it. Every oath-holder of the Hollow Guild, every hand that had ever signed, every throat that had ever profited. The words went into the ice like knives into snow, and Theo felt them settle over him — because they were his family, guilty or not, and some part of him would always flinch when they were threatened.

"No," he said.

The blue light stilled.

"There's a mercy clause." He fumbled the prophecy book from his pack — singed at one corner from Corvess's fire at the pass, water-swollen from the blizzard, but whole. He opened it to the final page, the one with his name and tonight's date inked a hundred years ago, and turned one page further, to the clause no one had ever read because no one had ever gotten this far. "Here. *When the Hollow-Bearer returns the stolen truth, let the debt be paid in exile, not blood. Let the guilty be cast down from the peaks they stole, and let them live to remember why.*"

"You would invoke mercy," said the eldest, "for the woman who put a knife in you?"

"She missed anything vital on purpose," Theo said. "I've been cut enough times to know the difference. She wanted me to stop, not to die. That has to count for something." He lifted his chin, which took more courage than the whole climb up Thornfell had. "You want them to remember. Killing them makes martyrs. Exile makes witnesses. Let them walk down into the lowlands knowing exactly what they lost and exactly why. That's a longer punishment than a blade, and it's the honest one."

Silence, deep as the glacier. Then, somewhere far above, ice groaned and settled, and the eldest warden inclined her head by a degree.

"So be it. The debt is paid in exile."

* * *

They brought Corvess down from the pass two days later, walking between wardens, her wrists bound in frost-cord that melted and reformed with every struggle. Her hunters had scattered when the fae rose out of the snow — some fled down-mountain, some knelt, one or two fought and were disarmed without being harmed, which told Theo everything about how much fight the guild had left in it.

She looked smaller than he remembered. Or maybe he had simply grown.

They stripped her power in the court itself. The wardens unmade her Guildmaster's seal with a touch, and the brand on her forearm — the hollow circle every sworn member carried — faded to pale scar tissue, blank as unwritten paper. She did not cry out. She watched Theo the entire time, and her expression was not hatred.

It was something worse. It was pride.

"You invoked the mercy clause," she said afterward, when the wardens released her to begin her long walk down. They stood alone in an antechamber of ice, the first time they'd been within arm's reach since the pass. "I taught you that trick. Read the fine print. Find the clause nobody else bothered with."

"You taught me most of what I know," Theo said. "That's why I'm here instead of dead."

Corvess laughed — short, cracked, real. "The boy from the gutter. I should have drowned you in it." She flexed her bare forearm, looking at the faded brand like a stranger's handwriting. "A hundred years my family wore that mark. Now nothing. Now I'm nobody from nowhere going down a mountain in winter."

"I know how that feels," Theo said quietly. "I'll be doing it tomorrow."

She studied him for a long moment. Whatever passed behind her eyes, she kept it there — she'd always been good at that, and he'd always been bad at reading it, and maybe that had been the whole problem between them from the start.

"Keep the fine print close, Vane," she said at last. And then she walked past him, out of the ice, out of the mountain, out of his life, and did not look back.

* * *

The guild scattered the way snow scatters — all at once, silently, irreversibly. Some took the lowland roads toward Vell's Crossing, some toward the coast, some simply walked into the white and were seen no more. Theo watched from the mouth of the pass as the last of them went, small dark figures against the white, carrying their unbranded arms and their hundred years of stolen history down into a world that would never quite trust them again.

Ilen appeared beside him. Wardens moved quietly; he'd stopped being surprised by it.

"You could stay," she said. "The court offered you a place. Warden-friend. Guest-right under the ice. Not many humans have ever been offered that."

Theo looked back at the glacier, glowing faintly blue in the afternoon light, full of voices and memory and justice. Warm, almost. Safe, definitely.

"I know," he said. "But if I stay, I'm just trading one family for another. And I haven't figured out yet who I am when I don't belong to anybody." He shouldered his pack — lighter now, the charter surrendered, the prophecy book left in the wardens' keeping. "Maybe that's the point of the whole thing. Maybe the Hollow-Bearer was never supposed to keep carrying anything."

Ilen touched two fingers to his shoulder, brief and cold and oddly gentle. "Then walk well, Theo Vane. The mountain will remember you kindly."

"The mountain can remember whatever it likes," Theo said. "I'm just trying to remember myself."

* * *

He went down alone, as the prophecy had promised he would.

The passes were closing behind him even as he descended — he could hear it, the deep grinding boom of avalanche after avalanche sealing the high roads, winter shutting Thornfell like a book whose story was finished. By nightfall the way he'd come was gone. By morning it would be as if no road had ever existed there at all.

Below him, the lowlands spread out grey and patient: hedgerows, woodsmoke, the far glitter of Vell's Crossing where nobody knew his name and nobody owed him anything and nobody would take him in out of habit or history. He had a little money, a knife, a scar on his ribs, and the truth, which weighed nothing now that everyone knew it.

It was strange, how light honesty was. He'd expected it to be heavy.

Theo pulled his collar up against the wind and started walking. Somewhere behind him, the last snow of the season sealed the final pass, and somewhere ahead of him — a day off, maybe two — there was a town, a room to rent, a life to invent from scratch.

He caught himself smiling at that. Inventing things from scratch was, after all, the one trade he'd been trained in since childhood.

The snow fell soft and steady across Thornfell, covering the roads, the ruins, the old guilt, the new peace. And Theo Vane, belonging to no one, finally honest, walked down out of the mountains and did not look back.
