## 1. Ink Under the Fingernails

There are three ways to know a man, my first master at the Quiet Flame told me. Watch what he prays for, watch what he spends, and watch his hands.

By that measure I am a beggar, a beggar, and a liar with clean-looking nails that are never actually clean. Ink gets under everything. Ten years of copying and it lives in the whorls of my fingers like a second soul, one that only comes out under lamplight when I'm tired enough to look at my own hands and remember they belong to someone.

Tonight is one of those nights.

The scriptorium of the Quiet Flame sits above the temple's eastern cloister, and in winter the cold comes up through the floor stones like a debt collector with no sense of decency. There are forty desks. Thirty-nine of them are empty by the ninth bell. Mine is the fortieth, because my debt runs to four hundred and eleven marks, nine coppers, and something the bursar rounds up to "a lifetime," and because Master Ovett believes that a scribe who works after dark is a scribe who isn't drinking, gambling, or thinking.

He's right about two of those.

The ledger in front of me is the candle-tallow account for the month of Frostwane, which means I am transcribing, in a hand so beautiful it should be illegal to use it on tallow, entries like *one hundredweight, poor quality, Brother Aldous wept*. Somewhere below me a novice is sweeping the cloister, and the scratch of his broom keeps time with my pen. Scratch, scratch. The flame in my lamp does its quiet work. Everything here is quiet work. That's the whole religion, really: the Quiet Flame burns without crackling, the Quiet Flame speaks without raising its voice, and the Quiet Flame takes your life in installments so small you don't notice you've paid until there's nothing left to pawn.

I was seven years old when my mother sold me to the Order. Not sold, exactly—*indentured*, the contract says, though the difference between those two words is roughly the width of a coin. She owed them for my father's funeral rites, and the Order of the Quiet Flame does not do funerals on credit. So I became an asset with legs. Four hundred marks against my name, accruing interest at a rate the bursar explains to me once a year with the gentle patience of a man describing weather to a sheep.

Here is the arithmetic they don't teach novices: a first-year scribe earns three coppers a day against his debt. A tenth-year scribe earns eight. Room, board, ink, candles, and the annual robe allowance are all charged back to the account. I have been here ten years. Last Frostwane my debt stood at four hundred and six marks. This Frostwane it stands at four hundred and eleven.

I am losing ground to a ledger. It takes a certain kind of patience to find that funny. Fortunately, patience is the one thing this place grows in abundance, like moss.

My pen stops. Not because I've made an error—I don't make errors; that's not vanity, it's just the single marketable skill I own—but because the broom downstairs has stopped scratching, and in the Quiet Flame, silence where sound should be is worth investigating.

I listen. Wind in the cloister. The settling groan of old timber. Then footsteps—not the novice's soft shuffle but boots with a deliberate heel, coming up the outside stair, and a knock at the scriptorium door in the pattern the night-porter uses when he wants something and doesn't want to say it aloud.

"Brother Kestrel." Tam's voice, low. "You've a visitor."

"I'm dead," I say, without looking up. "Dead men receive no visitors."

"He says he's your cousin."

"I don't have a cousin."

"He's holding out a letter and looking at me like I owe him money."

"That's not how cousins look. That's how creditors look." I set down my pen and cap the ink, because some rituals matter even when they're lies. "Send him to the guest alcove. I'll be down when this page ends."

The page ends in about the time it takes me to decide I'm being foolish. Which is immediately.

* * *

The guest alcove off the porter's lodge is a stone box with a bench, designed, I suspect, by someone who hated visitors and loved benches. My "cousin" is waiting there: a drover named Pell Wick, whom I have never met before tonight and will never admit to meeting again. He has the broad hands and sunburnt neck of a man who walks livestock for a living, and he holds a letter the way people hold things they've been paid to forget they're carrying.

"Kestrel Nakamura," he says.

"Pell Wick," I answer, because Doss the chandler told me the name, and Doss tells me everything eventually. "You have something for me."

"And you're the—" He looks me up and down: the grey robe, the ink stains, the general air of a man whose most exciting recent event was a smudge. "You're the one?"

"I'm the one what?"

"The one who knows the roads." He says it quietly, the way you'd mention a disease. "Doss said if anyone can get a letter from Thornfell way down to Lowmark by week's end, it's the temple ghost. Said you memorize roads like other men memorize insults."

"Doss talks too much."

"Doss said you'd say that."

I sit down across from him and let the silence do its work. This is another thing ten years of the Quiet Flame teaches you: most men will tell you anything if you simply decline to fill the space. Pell lasted eleven seconds.

"It's a love letter," he said. "Sort of. Woman in Lowmark. Man up past Thornfell passes, herding for the Ashvale clan through the winter. They can't write openly—her husband doesn't know, or maybe he does, doesn't matter—and the herder pays me extra to carry word when I drive stock down. But the pass road's watched this season. Imperial surveyors, marking every face that goes over. I can't be seen twice on the same road."

"So you need someone invisible."

"You're a temple scribe."

"Exactly." I fold my hands, enjoying this more than I should. "Nobody looks at a scribe. Scribes are furniture. Next week I carry tallow accounts to the grange at Millbrook—it's sanctioned travel, signed pass, honest errand. Millbrook sits a half-day from the Lowmark road, and the Lowmark road is a drover track nobody surveys because nobody important uses it. Your letter rides in my account case between two columns of figures, and no imperial clerk in the world will open a case that boring."

Pell stares at me. "How long have you known that route?"

"Since I was nine." This is true, and it's also the truest dangerous thing about me, so I soften it. "I copy maps sometimes. For practice. Things stick."

Things stick. That's the polite version. The impolite version is that I have never forgotten a road I've seen, a page I've read, or a face I've studied, and I have spent ten years and considerable effort making sure no one at the Quiet Flame knows it. A scribe with a perfect memory is a marvel. A debtor with a perfect memory is a liability. Liabilities get audited, and audited assets get their privileges trimmed. So I am careful. I misremember small things on purpose—a toll amount here, a gate schedule there—just enough that the masters believe my head is as ordinary as my prospects.

It is exhausting, being forgettable on purpose. But it beats the alternative, which is being interesting.

"How much?" I ask.

"Two marks."

"Three. Half now."

"That's robbery."

"That's a risk premium. Surveyors hang smugglers, Pell. They only fine drovers." I hold out my hand. He counts three silver marks into it, biting each one first, which I choose to find flattering rather than insulting, and I take the letter—a thin thing, sealed with cheap wax, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and someone else's whole secret life—and slide it into the false backing of my account case, behind the tallow figures where no clerk would ever willingly go.

"One condition," I say. "If anyone asks, you came to pray. You prayed. The Quiet Flame was very quiet. You left."

"That's it?"

"That's it. Religion is mostly logistics, Pell. The Flame understands that better than anyone."

* * *

I finish the tallow ledger by midnight, which pleases no one, least of all me. Then I do what I always do on smuggling nights: I sleep badly, wake before the dawn bell, and spend the extra hour doing the thing that keeps me sane, which is drawing maps from memory on scraps of scrap paper that I burn afterward.

Tonight's map is the Lowmark drover track, all eleven miles of it, from the ford at Colder's Bend to the tannery wall. I draw it twice, once fast and once slow, checking the second against the first the way other men check their consciences. They match. They always match. Sometimes I think the memory is less a gift than a room I can't leave—every road I've ever walked still in there, lit and empty, waiting for me to walk them again.

At the dawn bell I join the procession into the sanctuary, forty grey robes, and I take my usual place: third from the back, slightly left, the spot where eyes slide off. Brother Heshel calls it hiding in plain chant. I call it survival. The Quiet Flame's liturgy is an hour long and consists largely of not speaking, which suits me. I use the time to plan the Millbrook trip, review the Lowmark turns, and consider, briefly and without much hope, whether any religion anywhere offers a doctrine of getting out of debt.

Afterward, in the refectory, Master Ilvane finds me.

This is unusual. Masters do not find scribes; scribes are summoned, processed, filed. But Ilvane moves through the refectory like weather, and she stops at my table with her hands folded in her sleeves and her smile arranged just so—the smile of a woman who has never once needed to raise her voice.

"Scribe Nakamura. Copying late again last night?"

"Tallow accounts, Master."

"Diligent." Her eyes rest on me a moment longer than diligence warrants. There is something in her face I can't name—warmth, maybe, or the shape warmth leaves behind when it's been used as a tool for years. "You have a steady hand and a steady head. Rare, both together. The archive vault took a crack in yesterday's quake, you may have heard. Recataloguing will need someone meticulous. Someone... unremarkable in his habits." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "I thought of you."

I bow my head. "I'm honored, Master."

"Mm," she says, which is not agreement, and moves on to the next table, leaving behind the faint smell of incense and the distinct sensation of having been measured for something.

Beside me, Brother Heshel snorts into his porridge. "The sealed archive. Ten years a scribe and she picks you. Either you're being promoted or buried."

"In the Quiet Flame," I say, "those are frequently the same thing."

But that night, before I sleep, I take out the three silver marks and stack them on my desk, and I allow myself, for exactly the length of one candle, to imagine the arithmetic of freedom. Three marks now. Two more when the letter lands. Eleven more like it and I could buy down a year of interest. A hundred and thirty-seven jobs like it and I could walk out of these gates a free man, at fifty, with ink under my fingernails and nothing else to show for a life.

The candle gutters. I blow it out.

Tomorrow I recatalogue a sealed archive that no one has opened in living memory, because a smiling master decided I was suitably unremarkable. Tomorrow I'll carry a love letter through an empire's blind spots, because a drover decided I was suitably invisible. Everyone keeps deciding I'm nothing in particular, and everyone keeps being right, and I keep taking the work, because a man has to eat, and because—

Well. Because some part of me, the part that draws forbidden maps by candlelight, has always suspected that being forgettable is just a story I tell the world. And stories, in my experience, have a way of turning around and reading themselves back to you.

The bell for compline rings out over the cloister—one, two, three, up to twelve, and then, faintly, a thirteenth stroke, cracked and wrong, that no one else seems to hear.

Or perhaps no one else bothers to listen.

I stand very still in the dark, counting again, but the bell has finished, and the Quiet Flame settles back into its silence, and I tell myself, in the voice I use for ledgers, that old bells make mistakes.

Old bells make mistakes. Old books keep secrets. And tomorrow, they tell me, I start work in the archive.

I don't sleep well. I never do.
