## 2. The Book That Waits

The earthquake came at the hour of second vespers, which was typical of it. Earthquakes, like debt collectors and temple masters, never arrive when it's convenient for you. They come when you've just settled into the belief that the day is finished with you.

I felt it as a shudder through the floor of the scriptorium, a long low groan in the bones of Thornfell itself, the mountain clearing its throat and deciding, this once, not to spit. Ink bottles rattled. Brother Ossian's candle went out and he swore with the fluency of a man who had been swearing quietly for forty years. Somewhere below us, deep under the temple, something cracked.

We all pretended not to hear the crack. That is a skill the Quiet Flame teaches better than any other: the art of hearing a thing and agreeing, silently, together, that it did not happen.

It lasted perhaps six heartbeats. Then Brother Ossian relit his candle, the novices resumed their copying, and Master Ilvane's voice came down the stairwell like cool water poured over a bruise.

"Nakamura. A word."

There are perhaps four sentences in the world that make a debtor's stomach drop, and "Nakamura, a word" is three of them at once. I set down my pen, capped the ink, squared my page — habits so worn into me they happen without thought, the way a man who has been struck before flinches before the blow — and went up the stairs to meet whatever the mountain had knocked loose.

* * *

Master Ilvane received me in her study, where she always sat with her hands folded on the ledger of my debt as though it were a beloved pet she occasionally fed. She was small and silver-haired and had a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of her face decided to agree with it. In nine years I had learned to read that half-second the way sailors read clouds.

"You felt it," she said. Not a question.

"The whole mountain felt it, Mistress."

"The vault felt it most." She unfolded her hands. Beneath them, I noted, the ledger lay closed. "The seal-stone over the archive door has cracked. Three fissures, running diagonal. The masons say the door itself will hold for now, but the wards are bleeding. Do you know what happens when wards bleed, Nakamura?"

"Paper rots," I said. "Ink runs. Mold finds a home."

Something flickered behind her smile — approval, or its cheaper cousin, surprise. "Precisely. The sealed archive holds eleven hundred volumes, give or take, none of them touched in a century, several of them older than the temple itself. If moisture gets in through those cracks, we lose irreplaceable works of devotion and history." She said *devotion and history* the way other people said *furniture*. "You will recatalogue them. Every volume, every folio, condition noted, contents summarized. You will begin tomorrow at dawn and you will work alone."

"Alone?" I kept my voice pleasantly blank. "That's a year of work for one pair of hands, Mistress. Two, if the paper's gone soft."

"It is a year of work," she agreed, "and you have years, Nakamura. It is one of the few things you possess in abundance." The smile widened by a degree, warming nothing. "Besides. Your memory for detail is the best in this house. It would be a waste of the Order's investment to send anyone less."

There it was, then: my photographic mind, the one thing about me the temple valued, being put to work in the one place in the temple nobody was supposed to go. I could think of three reasons a mistress might want a single expendable scribe alone in a sealed archive, and none of them were *devotion and history*.

But I owed the Quiet Flame more coin than I would earn in two lifetimes of honest copying, and debts do not care what you think of the errands they ride upon. So I bowed, thanked her for the trust — the word tasted like tallow — and asked only whether I might bring my own candles.

"Candles are provided," she said.

Provided candles burn shorter than bought ones. I made a note to steal wax from the refectory. Small sins keep a man honest; it is the large ones that get complicated.

* * *

Dawn in the under-temple is dawn in name only. There is no window down there, no sky, just lamps along the corridor burning with the patient orange indifference of things that have burned in the same place for a hundred years and intend to continue regardless of human affairs. My footsteps echoed ahead of me like another scribe walking the same errand.

The vault door was exactly as advertised: black iron-bound oak older than any living brother, and across its face three new cracks in the great seal-stone, thin as hairs, weeping faint threads of damp. Someone had chalked a ward-sign over the largest fissure — fresh, careful, Master Ilvane's own hand, I'd have wagered my next meal on it. Which meant she had come down here herself, in the dark, between the earthquake and my summons, to look at what had been opened before sending me down to look at it.

I filed that away with the other things I knew and pretended not to.

The key was heavy enough to use as a weapon. The lock turned with a sound like an old man clearing his throat after decades of silence. And the door swung inward onto cold air that smelled of stone dust, lamp oil long evaporated, and paper — that particular sweetness of old paper, somewhere between vanilla and grief.

Eleven hundred volumes. Give or take.

They lay on cedar shelves in ranks that receded into lamplight, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and the first thing I understood, standing there with my inventory book under my arm, was that Master Ilvane had understated the damage the way a physician understates a wound to spare the family. Water had gotten in. Not much — but water needs so little. Along the eastern wall, where the mountain's meltwater found its slow way through seams no mason could chase, the oilcloths were spotted and stiff, and beneath them the leather bindings had bloomed white and grey, furred like something sleeping.

I lit the provided candle, short and mean, added the wax I'd smuggled in my sleeve to its base, and began.

* * *

Recataloguing is a kind of prayer, if you do it long enough. Every volume must be handled, smelled, opened gently, its spine examined, its gatherings counted, its contents reduced to a line of neat summary in a hand that pretends to be objective. *Homilies on Stillness, water-damaged, gatherings 4–7 illegible.* *Rites of the Winter Veil, sound, unremarkable.* For hours at a stretch you stop being a person and become a pair of hands with a memory attached, and I will confess that for the first three days I was content to be exactly that. Contentment, for a man like me, means nobody is looking.

But hands wander. Memory wanders worse. By the fourth day I had developed the habit every archivist denies having: reading past the summary line. Just a paragraph. Just to confirm the catalogue entry. Just because a homily on stillness written by a man who clearly never met another person is duller than watching paint refuse to dry.

And it was on the fourth day, on the eastern wall, third shelf, that I pulled out a volume whose oilcloth wrapping had been tied with a knot I recognized.

Not recognized the way you recognize a face. Recognized the way your fingers recognize a thing before your mind catches up — the way mine knew the weight of a temple ledger, the particular double-loop-and-tuck the Quiet Flame used on its bound records, a knot I tied myself every accounting season. This knot was older, the cord darker, but the hand that tied it had learned where I had learned. Same house. Same teaching.

I stood very still for a moment, listening to the vault breathe.

Then I untied it.

The binding was calf-leather gone the color of weak tea, tooled along the edges with a pattern I knew from the masters' private shelves: interlocking flames, each flame swallowing the next, the sigil of the Quiet Flame rendered in a style the temple hadn't used in — I did the arithmetic without wanting to — eighty, ninety years. The title was inked on the first leaf in a fine, slanted, self-assured hand:

*The Oracle of Sable Turn.*

I knew the name the way everyone raised in these mountains knows it — as a story told to children who wouldn't sleep. The Sable Oracle had lived in the high caves above Thornfell a century ago, a woman (or a man, depending which grandmother was talking) who wrote prophecies in the dark and refused, to the end, to say how. The clans had revered her. The empire had wanted her ledgers. The war between them had eaten her legend along with everything else. Her cave was a ruin now. Her books were supposed to be ash.

Supposed to be. And here one sat in my hands, weighing about as much as a sleeping cat, tied with my own order's knot.

Wonder is a strange sensation in a man trained to be forgettable. It feels dangerous. It feels like standing up in a quiet room. I sat down on the floor — the vault had no chairs, a fact that told you everything about how often anyone was meant to linger — and turned to the first page.

The oracle's hand was beautiful. That was the first betrayal of it: you expect prophecy to look jagged, feverish, and instead it flowed across the page calm as a river, each letter placed with the confidence of someone writing things she believed were already true. The opening pages were mundane almost to the point of comedy — inventories, mostly. *One lamp. One stool. Wool for winter, insufficient.* A woman who saw the future and spent her prophecies complaining about her wool supply. I smiled despite myself. I have always trusted people who complain precisely.

Then, further in, the entries changed.

*The bell will crack in the spring of the centenary,* she wrote, *and they will pretend it did not.*

My eyes went, involuntarily, upward — toward the ceiling, toward the floors above, toward the bell tower where the great bronze bell hung, the one that rang the hours, the one whose tone had gone slightly wrong sometime in the last year, a hairline flaw the bellwright had inspected and dismissed. We had all agreed, silently, together, that it had not happened.

The candle guttered. Old air moving through old cracks. I relit it from my stolen wax and told my hands to stop shaking, and my hands, being better trained than the rest of me, mostly complied.

I should have stopped there. Any sensible man stops there. But cataloguing requires completeness, and completeness is the best lie a curious man ever tells himself, and so I turned the pages on, past seasons of wool counts and lamp oil, past a passage about *the lowland road running red with borrowed blood*, past marginalia in at least three different later hands — later readers, later owners, the book passed from shelf to shelf like a debt no one wanted called in — until I reached a gathering near the end where the paper changed quality. Newer stock. Inserted, not original.

And the handwriting on the inserted leaf was not the oracle's.

It was close. Deliberately close, the way a forgery studies its model. But the slant leaned the wrong way for a left-handed writer, and the ink was iron-gall of a newer recipe, and there — at the bottom margin, half-effaced with pumice but legible to a man who had spent nine years reading erased lines for a living — was a date.

Not a century-old date.

A date ten days from tonight.

I sat in the cold with my heart going like a novice's hammer and read the line twice, three times, the letters refusing to rearrange themselves into anything safer. It was a fragment. An instruction, maybe, or a note — *deliver to the bearer*, something crossed out, something about *the thirteenth turn* — and beneath the erasure, in that same careful borrowed hand, two words that had survived the pumice better than the rest:

*Sable Turn.*

I looked around the vault. Eleven hundred volumes, and the lamplight lying still on them, and the mountain holding its breath above my head. Somewhere up there, Master Ilvane was smiling at someone with that half-second delay, and somewhere up there a cracked bell was keeping time it shouldn't have, and I had four days left on the eastern wall and eleven hundred volumes to summarize in a neat, objective, forgettable hand.

I rewrapped the Oracle of Sable Turn in its oilcloth. I retied the knot — my knot, our knot — and set it back on the shelf in its exact position, spine flush, angle true, the way a man returns a thing he intends to deny touching.

Then I picked up my inventory book, wrote *Oracle of Sable Turn — binding unsound — contents devotional, fragmentary, negligible* in my best catalogue hand, and lied in ink, which is the most permanent form of lying I know.

The walk back up the corridor took longer than usual. Lamps I had passed a hundred times seemed to watch me go by. And at the top of the stairs, in the warm ordinary noise of the scriptorium, Brother Ossian glanced up from his page and asked if the archive was as dusty as they said, and I said yes, dust and nothing else, moldy homilies and rites nobody reads.

He nodded and bent back to his work, satisfied.

That night I lay awake counting the hours until my shift ended and the vault was mine again — four days, three days, two — and telling myself, with the full force of nine years' practice at believing convenient things, that a forged leaf in a dead woman's book was somebody's old joke. A test. Nothing. Dust and nothing else.

But I knew the knot. And whoever had inserted that leaf had known it too.

And in two days, when I unlocked the door again and lifted the Oracle down and turned past the forged leaf to the pages beneath it — the true pages, the oracle's own river-calm hand — I was going to find out what the Quiet Flame had been keeping in the dark for a hundred years, and why it had waited for a cracked bell and a disposable scribe to let it out.
