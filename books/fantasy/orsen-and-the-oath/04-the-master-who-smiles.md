## 4. The Master Who Smiles

The summons came at dawn, which was the first wrong thing.

Nothing good happens at dawn in the Order of the Quiet Flame. Dawn is when the masters count things—candles, coins, novices—and when they find a discrepancy, someone's week gets worse. A summons delivered by Brother Ossian, who normally wouldn't cross the courtyard to spit on me if I were on fire, meant the discrepancy had a name, and the name was mine.

"Master Ilvane will see you in her study after morning offices," Ossian said, not looking at me, which was his way of looking at me very hard indeed.

"Did she say why?"

"She did not."

"Did she seem angry?"

Ossian considered this with the gravity of a man asked to interpret scripture. "She seemed like Master Ilvane."

Which told me nothing, because Master Ilvane always seemed like Master Ilvane: warm as fresh bread, pleasant as a sunlit room, and about as easy to read as a ledger written in cipher by a drunk. In eleven years at Thornfell's temple I had learned every smuggler's path through the mountains, every back stair in the lowland towns, every face worth knowing and two hundred faces worth avoiding—but I had never once successfully read Ilvane Vess. She smiled the same way whether she was forgiving a novice's debt or signing off on a flogging. I'd seen her do both in the same hour.

I spent the morning doing what any sensible man does before walking into an unknown conversation: I prepared my lies.

This is a skill, lying well. Most people think it's about the words. It isn't. The words are the cheapest part. Good lying is about logistics. You have to know where your lie has been and where it intends to go, the way you'd plan a route through hostile country. If Master Ilvane asks what I was doing in the sealed archive, I say I was recataloguing, because that's true, and the best lies are built on load-bearing truths. If she asks what I found, I say water damage and bad binding glue, because that's also true, and it's boring, and boredom is armor. If she asks whether I read anything *unusual*—

Well. There was the problem. Because I had read something unusual, and unusual is a door, and once you open it even a crack, people like Ilvane walk through it and start rearranging your furniture.

I rehearsed while I ground ink. I rehearsed while I copied forty lines of grain tithes from the Ashford granaries, my hand moving on autopilot while the rest of me ran scenarios. I was so deep in rehearsal that I transcribed "barley" as "barely," caught it, corrected it, and decided it was probably an omen of some kind. Everything felt like an omen lately. That's what one night with a dead oracle's book does to you.

Because here's what I hadn't told anyone, including myself, in plain words: last night, by candlelight, in a book written a hundred years ago by a woman who died before my grandmother drew breath, I had found tonight's date. And beneath the date, in ink gone brown as old blood, I had found my own name. *Nakamura.* Not a common name. My name. With instructions attached, like a delivery note for a package I never agreed to carry.

I had done what any careful man does with a thing too large to hold. I had put it back on the shelf, aligned its spine with its neighbors, doused my candle, and lain awake until the bell rang matins, telling myself stories. Coincidence. The oracle wrote dates loosely; scribes copy names carelessly; a century-old prophecy describing a cracked bell means nothing when there are nine bells in this temple and three of them sound like they're dying anyway. I am Kestrel Nakamura, debt-scribe, letter-smuggler, forgettable man. Prophecies don't happen to men like me. Men like me are what prophecies happen *around*, the way weather happens around a fencepost.

The trouble with that story was the fencepost keeps dreaming about fire.

* * *

Ilvane's study sat at the top of the east stair, above the scriptorium, with windows facing the mountain instead of the valley—a choice that always struck me as either sentiment or strategy, and with Ilvane it was never sentiment. She received me standing, pouring tea herself, which was another wrong thing. Masters didn't pour for scribes. Masters had novices pour for scribes who poured for them.

"Kestrel." My name in her mouth sounded like a coin she was deciding whether to spend. "Sit. You look tired."

"I'm always tired, Master. It's part of my charm."

That got the smile—the full one, the one that reached her eyes and made novices feel chosen and made me feel like a mouse being told how lovely the trap looked. "Sit," she said again, gentler, and I sat, and the tea was good, which was the third wrong thing. Temple tea tasted like boiled regret. This was real leaf, lowland-grown, expensive. Someone had prepared for this conversation. Someone had *budgeted* for it.

"The earthquake," she began, settling across from me with her cup cradled in both hands. "The vault held, mostly. But the archive took water, and the shelves shifted. Tell me what you found down there."

"Water damage," I said. "Bad binding glue. About sixty volumes need rehousing before the mold spreads, and four are beyond saving. One of them was a tax ledger from the reign of old Emperor Cassian, which frankly no one will mourn."

She laughed. It was a lovely laugh. I hated how much practice I needed not to flinch at it.

"And beyond the ledgers?" she said. "You were down there three nights, Kestrel. A man doesn't spend three nights among books without reading something."

"A man spends three nights among books because the work takes three nights," I said, and let a little weariness into it, because weariness was true and truth was mortar. "There were hundreds of volumes, Master. Most of them water-ruined. I handled more dead mice than dead oracles."

The word *oracle* left my mouth before I could stop it, and I watched it land.

For half a heartbeat—less than a breath—something moved behind Ilvane's eyes. Not surprise. Recognition. The look of a woman hearing a word she'd been waiting years to hear, filed under the right name at last. Then it was gone, buried so fast and so smoothly that anyone else would have doubted they'd seen it.

But I had spent eleven years learning to notice what people hid, because noticing was how a debtor survived, and I had seen it. And I saw what came with it: her hands, wrapped around her teacup, had gone very slightly white at the knuckles.

She set the cup down. The porcelain clicked against the saucer, once, twice, as though her fingers couldn't decide if they were finished.

"Mice," she repeated warmly. "And oracles. You have a poet hiding under all that ledger-hand, Kestrel. I've always suspected it." She rose, drifted to the window, gazed out at Thornfell's grey shoulders. "Tell me honestly. Did anything in that archive strike you as strange? Anything at all. You won't be punished for curiosity. Curiosity is how we serve the Flame."

Here it was. The door. I could feel the draft coming through it.

I thought about the book. *The Oracle of Sable Turn.* I thought about the binding—temple work, our own binding style, the double-stitched spine the Quiet Flame used and no one else, which meant the book had been *ours*, had been hidden *from us*, locked in a vault behind a door that took an earthquake to crack. I thought about the date. Tonight's date. My name.

"No," I said. "Nothing strange. Strange would have been interesting. Mostly it was damp and depressing."

Ilvane turned from the window, and the smile came back, and it was perfect, and I understood suddenly that perfection was the tell. Nobody's smile is that good by accident. Hers was maintained, the way a wall is maintained, the way a mask is maintained.

"I'm glad," she said softly. "Damp and depressing is exactly what an archive should be." She poured me a second cup herself, unasked. "Kestrel, I want you to finish the recataloguing. Take whatever time you need. Work alone—I'll have Ossian keep the other scribes on the tithes. And if you should happen to find anything... *unusual*..." She touched my shoulder, light as ash falling. "...you'll bring it to me. Not to the other masters. To me. Can I trust you with that?"

"You can trust me with anything, Master," I said. "It's practically my defining feature. Nobody trusts me with anything."

Another laugh. "Go on, then. Back to your ledgers."

I went. At the door I glanced back—habit, caution, call it what you like—and caught her standing exactly where I'd left her, both hands pressed flat against the windowsill, head bowed, as if the weight of the mountain view had finally gotten to her.

Her hands were still trembling.

* * *

I want to be clear about what happened next, because it matters: I went back to work. I recatalogued. I copied tithes. I ate my thin supper and I did not go near the sealed archive, because every instinct I owned was screaming that the smartest thing a hunted man can do is stop leaving tracks.

But I was already hunted. I just didn't know the shape of the hunt yet. I knew only this: a master had lied to me with her whole face while her hands shook, and had asked me to bring her anything strange, alone, quietly, and had paid for good tea to sweeten the asking. In the smuggling trade we had a word for a buyer who pays too much and asks too gently.

We called him a mark. Except I was fairly sure that in this transaction, the mark was me.

So I lay in my cell that night listening to the bells ring the hours—one, two, three—and thinking about a dead oracle's handwriting, and about the phrase I'd read and reread until the candle drowned: *the bearer of the Ember Vow.* Thinking about how a book bound in Quiet Flame stitching had been locked away from the Quiet Flame, and how the only person who'd ever shown interest in my comings and goings had just handed me a key to the vault and called it trust.

Thinking, most of all, about her hands. Steady hands run temples. Trembling hands mean fear, and fear means knowledge, and knowledge means she knew what was in that vault long before the earthquake opened it.

The question that kept me awake wasn't *what does the prophecy say.*

It was *who told her to smile?*

* * *

From the private journal of Ilvane Vess, Master of the Order of the Quiet Flame, entered the same night:

He lied to me today. Beautifully. I almost applauded.

Three nights in the archive and he claims he found nothing but damp and dead mice—yet the word *oracle* slipped out of him like a coin from a torn pocket, and a man who knows nothing does not carry that particular word loose in his mouth. He knows. Of course he knows. He was always going to know; that is why he is here at all.

Let me set down what I have carried alone for nineteen years, since Mother Superior Ysolde passed it to me with her dying breath and a flask of poppy syrup to steady my nerves: the Oracle of Sable Turn is real. The original is real. What the clans recite around their fires is a forgery—we forged it, this order, a generation ago, to leash the mountain holds with a promise of a chosen bearer, and it worked, it worked beautifully, for fifty years the clans waited for their miracle instead of marching. But forgeries rot. The zealots grow impatient. Maren's faction wants their Oathbearer *now*, living and breathing, and they have begun asking questions our forgery cannot answer.

Only the original can answer them. Only the original names the true bearer, the true hour, the true price. And the original names *him*. Nakamura. The scribe. Bloodline of the last Oathbreaker, debt-bound, friendless, clever enough to survive and too expendable to be missed. I reviewed forty candidates over six years and he was the only one who fit every line of the text. The earthquake was convenient; I confess I prayed for it, which I suspect cancels out several years of quiet devotion.

He must claim the vow. He must complete it, publicly, at the thirteenth turn, and the war dies with it—or he must fail, and then the clans burn the lowlands and the empire burns the mountains, and either way this order survives by having arranged the kindling. That is the arithmetic. I have checked it a thousand times. It always comes out the same.

What I did not anticipate—he continues to surprise me—was that he would lie to me *well*. That he would see my hands shake. He noticed. I watched him notice, that flicker of the eyes, the small recalibration behind his face, and I thought: there it is. There is the mind that memorizes smugglers' roads. I have chosen a bearer who watches hands.

Good. A bearer who watches hands might live long enough to reach the thirteenth turn. A bearer who trusts me would be dead inside a month, and dead men cannot swear oaths.

Tomorrow I unlock the archive for him again. Alone, as promised. I will leave the Oracle where he can find it, and I will act surprised when he brings it to me, and he will pretend to believe my surprise, and we will proceed together, each convinced we are deceiving the other, which is the closest thing to honesty two careful people ever manage.

Forgive me, Flame. Forgive me, whoever listens. He seems kind. He makes jokes about his own debts. He deserves better than to be a hinge upon which a door swings.

But hinges are not remembered. Doors are. And I intend for this door to close.

The bell rang the midnight hour just now. Nine strokes, as always.

I counted them twice.
