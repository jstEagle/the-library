## 5. Thirteen Bells

The bell cracked at moonrise, and it should not have rung at all.

I want to be clear about that, because everything that followed depends on it. The Quiet Flame's bell had been broken since before my grandfather was born—split down the western face like a walnut some giant had given up on. Every novice learned the story in their first week: the crack was a lesson in impermanence, the masters said, a reminder that even bronze forgets its purpose. We were taught to find it beautiful.

I had copied out that lesson three times for three different donors' commemorative folios. I could recite it in my sleep, which is precisely what I was doing when the sound came through my window and dragged me upright with my heart slamming against my ribs like a prisoner at a door.

Thirteen strokes.

I counted them because counting is what I do instead of screaming. One. Two. By five I was standing. By eight I was dressed. By thirteen I had pressed myself against the cold stone of the window frame and was watching the courtyard below, where torches were blooming one by one like a fever rash.

Bells don't ring themselves. Cracked bells especially don't ring themselves—there's a flaw in the metal, a seam of dead air where the sound should live, and any ringer worth his rope knows you can't get a true note out of it. What I'd heard wasn't a true note. It was worse. It was the sound a cracked bell makes when someone is determined to make it speak anyway: a flattened, wounded clang, each stroke slightly wrong, like a voice talking around a broken tooth.

And there was no one in the belfry. I checked. I'm not proud of how fast I moved, or how little dignity I preserved getting down the novices' stair, but I looked, and the belfry was empty except for the rope, still swaying, and the great split mouth of the bell hanging silent above me as if nothing had happened.

Thirteen strokes from a broken bell with no hand on the rope. The Oracle of Sable Turn sat in my satchel under three ledgers and a stolen letter, and I did not need to open it to know what page was waiting for me. I had read it eleven times since Master Ilvane dismissed me. *On the night the dead bell speaks, the bearer will know his hour.* I had told myself, the eleventh time, that prophecy is a language, and languages can describe things that never happen, the way a map can draw roads through mountains that were quarried away a century ago.

That was before the bell.

* * *

Captain Sera Voss had been awake for thirty hours, and she had stopped pretending she enjoyed her work somewhere around hour twenty-two.

The Order of the Quiet Flame's gates were exactly what she expected: modest, immaculate, and designed to communicate wealth without admitting to it. Two granite pillars, an iron gate painted black, and above the arch a carved flame so stylized it might have been a tulip having second thoughts. She had read the temple's imperial filings on the ride up from Thornfell Station. Their declared annual income would have embarrassed a minor duke. Their declared charitable expenditures accounted for perhaps a tenth of it. Nobody had ever asked why, because nobody asked questions about temples that kept the mountain passes quiet and paid their road-taxes early.

Voss asked questions for a living. It was, officially, her entire function. In practice, most of her function was deciding which answers to write down.

"Captain." The gate warden was a soft-bellied man in grey robes, bowing at precisely the depth required by protocol and no deeper. "We received your bird this morning. How may the Quiet Flame serve the Throne?"

"A scribe," Voss said. "One of yours. Name of Nakamura. Kestrel Nakamura."

Something crossed the warden's face—not fear, exactly. Recognition wearing fear's coat. "A scribe. May I ask the nature of the Throne's interest?"

"You may not."

He bowed again, a fraction lower, and left them standing in the wind while he went to fetch someone who mattered. Voss let her eyes do the work while her face did nothing at all. Four novices crossing the courtyard, all of them walking too carefully, the way people walk when they've been told to look natural. A door on the eastern range standing open despite the cold. And high above, the famous cracked bell, motionless, its split face catching the torchlight.

Her sergeant, Dree, shifted beside her. "They knew we were coming."

"They knew we were coming," Voss agreed. "Which means they've known about the boy longer than we have. Which means whatever happens next, they've already decided how much of it they're going to let us see."

"And if they've hidden him?"

"Then they're hiding something worth hiding, and I'll want to know what it costs them to keep it hidden." She turned her collar up against the wind off the peaks. "Watch the hands, Dree. Faces lie. Hands are honest."

The person who came to the gate was not the warden. She was tall, silver-haired, robed in the deep charcoal of a temple master, and she smiled the way a banker smiles—warmly, and only with the part of the face that costs nothing.

"Captain Voss. I am Ilvane, First Scribe of this house. You seek Brother Kestrel."

"I do."

"Then I regret to inform you that you have missed him by less than an hour." The smile deepened by a degree. "The young man has fled."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about panic: it is loud, but it is stupid. It wants you to run for the front gate, the obvious road, the place your feet have gone a hundred times. Panic is a bad navigator. Fortunately, I am a good one, and I have spent six years in this temple learning every route that isn't on any map, because a man in debt learns early that the exits matter more than the entrances.

I didn't go to my cell. Cells are where they look first. I went down.

Under the Quiet Flame there are crypts—the show crypts, marble and candles, where donors buy wall niches at scandalous prices—and beneath those there are the real crypts, which are older than the temple, older than the Order, older probably than the empire that funds us all. When I first arrived, seventeen years old and already owing the temple more than my father had earned in his life, I was assigned to recopy the burial ledgers. Boring work. But boring work takes you places, and I noticed things. I notice things. It's the only talent I've ever been certain of.

I noticed that Crypt Nine's back wall was newer brick than its other walls. I noticed that the drainage channel behind it ran dry in summer, which meant it went somewhere the water didn't. And I noticed, one evening when a funeral procession blocked the main stairs, that the old bone-shute in Crypt Eleven still connected to the temple's foundations, and from the foundations you could reach the cistern gallery, and from the cistern gallery—

Well. From the cistern gallery you could reach anywhere, if you were thin, patient, and unafraid of the dark. I am all three. Being forgettable teaches you to be comfortable alone.

So when the alarm bells—real bells, living ones—began ringing through the upper floors, and I heard boots on the novices' stair, I did not run toward the gate. I lifted the flagstone behind the ossuary shelves in Crypt Eleven, lowered myself into a passage that smelled of cold stone and very old death, and pulled the stone shut over my head just as torchlight spilled into the crypt above me.

I lay in the dark and listened to them search. There were more of them than temple guards. Temple guards shuffle; these men moved like water finding cracks, coordinated, quiet, thorough. Imperial troops, then. Inquisitors, if the rumors held. And they weren't shouting "fire" or "thief." They were asking, in low voices, after a name.

My name.

I lay there in the dark with a dead oracle's book against my chest and thought, with a strange floating clarity: *they came within an hour of the bell.* Not a day later. Not a week. An hour. Which meant either the empire had a man watching this temple every night for a sign no sane person would believe—or the temple itself had sent word, and the word had gone out before the bell ever rang.

Both possibilities were terrible. Only one of them surprised me.

* * *

"The boy fled," Voss repeated. "Alone. At night. Into the mountains. On the same night our warrant named him."

Ilvane spread her hands, a gesture of helpless sorrow performed with the precision of a ritual. "Brother Kestrel has always been... restless. A debt-bound scribe, Captain, clever but troubled. When your herald's bird arrived, I summoned him to explain the situation. He listened. Then he excused himself, and by the time we understood—" she sighed— "he was gone. I take full responsibility. I should have posted guards at once."

"You should have," Voss said mildly. "Tell me about him."

"He copies beautifully. He keeps to himself. He owes the Order a considerable sum." Ilvane's smile flickered—genuine, for half a heartbeat, and what showed underneath was something colder and more tired. "He is not what you would call important, Captain. If the Throne wants him, I grieve that we cannot deliver him, but I confess I cannot imagine what he has done."

"That makes two of us," Voss said, and watched the master's hands.

They were folded at her waist, serene as statuary. But the right thumb was moving. Pressing the side of the left forefinger, over and over, small and rhythmic, the way a woman worries a splinter she cannot reach. Voss had interviewed parricides and coin-forgers and one archbishop, and she had learned that the composed ones always leaked somewhere. Most chose the eyes. This one had chosen her thumb, which meant she knew her eyes were being watched, which meant she was good, which meant Voss should stop trusting anything else about her entirely.

"Your bell rang tonight," Voss said. "Thirteen times. The cracked one."

Ilvane's brows rose, delicately astonished. "Did it? I sleep poorly these days; I fear I heard nothing. A pity—it hasn't sounded in a century. Some say it rang the night the last Oathbearer died." She said it lightly, an old wives' tale offered for flavor, but her thumb stopped moving, and Voss noted that too.

"Superstition," Voss agreed. "Thank you for your candor, Master Ilvane. My soldiers will search the grounds, of course. Protocol."

"Of course." The smile again, bank-warm. "Take whatever time you need. The Quiet Flame has nothing to hide."

*Everyone* says that, Voss thought. *It's practically a confession.*

* * *

The cistern gallery ran north-northwest under the temple's oldest wing, and I moved through it by touch and memory, because carrying a light down here would be suicide and I have always been attached to not dying. Six hundred paces to the junction where the old aqueduct met the foundation drain. Left at the carved stone—I'd found a weathered face down there once, some god or gatekeeper from before the Order, its features worn to a suggestion. Right at the fall of rubble. Up through the bone-shute, which was exactly as pleasant as it sounds, and out through the drainage grate into the ravine behind the temple's midden heaps.

I came up into starlight and snow-smelling air with my heart going like a drum and the book hugged to me like a child, and I allowed myself exactly ten seconds to stand there shaking.

Ten seconds is a luxury. I rationed it.

Then I made myself think, because thinking was the one weapon nobody had taken from me yet. Facts. One: the inquisitors wanted me alive enough to come quietly—that's why they announced themselves at the gate instead of burning the place down. Two: the temple had sold me, and sold me *fast*, which meant Master Ilvane's kindness yesterday had been a merchant measuring cloth. Three: the clans would also want me, eventually, once word traveled, and the clans would not bother with warrants. Four: I had maybe two hours of darkness, one satchel, a forged travel pass I'd made for a smuggler client and never delivered, four coppers, and a book of prophecies that had ruined my life before breakfast.

Five: I knew every road out of Thornfell that wasn't a road.

People think mountains are walls. Mountains are actually doors—dozens of them—if you know where the hinges are. I had spent six years guiding contraband through this range: salt, letters, occasionally a person with reasons to travel quietly. I had never once drawn a map. Maps get confiscated. Everything I knew lived behind my eyes, in the photographic clutter of a mind that couldn't forget a single switchback it had ever seen, and tonight, for the first time in my careful gray life, that mind was worth more than my hands.

There was a shepherd's track above the slate quarry that the temple maps marked as washed out. It wasn't. There was a hollow way behind the shrine of Saint Aldric that dropped into the pine forest without touching the toll road. And past all of that, two days hard walking, there was a courier trail into clan territory that I had walked exactly once, three years ago, guiding a clan messenger home through fog.

Brannoc, his name had been. Big, quiet, younger than me by a year or two, with a burn scar on his forearm and a habit of watching everything and saying almost nothing. He'd owed me his life by the end of that fog—I'd found the path when his own kin's directions failed him—and he'd sworn a courier's debt, which among the mountain clans is not a polite fiction. It's iron.

I had never intended to collect. Collecting debts means letting people close, and closeness is just another kind of ledger, and I had spent my whole adult life trying to owe nothing and be owed nothing.

But the ledger had been opened for me now, hadn't it? A dead oracle had written my name in her book a century before I was born, a cracked bell had spoken it aloud, and an empire and a temple had both come to collect. I was in the deepest debt of my life, and I hadn't signed a thing.

I pulled the travel pass from my satchel, checked it one last time against the seal I remembered forging, and turned my face north, toward the passes, toward the clans, toward the only person in these mountains who owed me a favor and might hate me slightly less than everyone else currently hunting me did.

Behind me, far below, the temple's living bells began to ring the alarm properly—frantic, overlapping, human. Ahead of me the mountains stood black against the stars, patient as creditors.

I took the first step onto the washed-out track, and I felt something shift in my chest that I didn't have a name for yet. It wasn't courage. Courage is calm. This was sharper, hungrier—the feeling of a man who has spent his whole life being invisible finally understanding that invisibility was never safety.

It was training.

Let them hunt a ghost, I thought, climbing. Ghosts know the roads too.
