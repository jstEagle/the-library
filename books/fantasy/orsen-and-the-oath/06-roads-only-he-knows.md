## 6. Roads Only He Knows

The first thing they teach you about running is that you shouldn't. The second thing they teach you—usually with a boot to the ribs—is that you'll run anyway.

I ran.

Not well. Not gracefully. I went out through the crypt tunnels behind the reliquary, the ones the temple masters pretend don't exist because acknowledging them would mean admitting their predecessors had once smuggled things worth smuggling, and I came up through the old drainage grate in the hillside above the tannery reeking of a century's worth of other people's secrets. My hands were shaking so badly it took me three tries to work the grate back into place.

Behind me, Thornfell's temple bell was still ringing. Thirteen times. It had rung thirteen times and then kept ringing, cracked and arrhythmic, like a heart that had forgotten how to stop.

*Coincidence,* I told myself, for about the fourth time since midnight. *Bells crack. Bells ring wrong. Oracles write nonsense by the gallon and someone a hundred years ago had a sense of humor.*

Then I remembered Master Ilvane's trembling hands, and the way she'd smiled when she asked what I'd read, and I stopped telling myself anything at all and started moving.

Here's what nobody tells you about being forgettable: it works beautifully right up until it doesn't. For six years I had been the gray man of the Quiet Flame, the scribe who copied ledgers and asked no questions and took one small letter at a time across the mountain passes in exchange for enough coin to eat and shave a sliver off a debt that grew faster than I could pay it. Nobody looked at me. That was the whole architecture of my survival—I'd built myself out of blankness the way other men built themselves out of muscle or money.

But somewhere between the archive vault and Ilvane's study, I had stopped being blank. Somewhere in those water-stained pages, a dead woman had written my name in ink older than my grandfather, and now every eye in the valley was looking for Kestrel Nakamura, bearer of something called the Ember Vow, and the only thing more dangerous than being hunted by people who know your face is being hunted by people who know your *name*, because names travel faster than faces and they don't need lanterns to find you in the dark.

So: the mountains. The smuggler roads. The one map in the world that couldn't be confiscated, because I'd never drawn it anywhere but inside my own skull.

* * *

Thornfell doesn't have roads so much as it has arguments—places where the mountain and the empire disagreed about what counted as passable terrain, and the mountain won. The imperial surveyors mapped eleven passes into the range. There are thirty-one. I know this because over six years of carrying letters for men who paid in food, I walked most of them, and memorized the rest from the stories of people who'd walked them before me.

Smugglers are terrible gossips and excellent cartographers. They'll lie to you about everything except the ground under their feet, because lying about the ground gets you killed, and dead couriers don't deliver.

I took the Larkspur Cut first, which isn't on any map because it isn't really a pass—it's a seam where the rock split in some ancient winter and healed crooked, leaving a slot barely wide enough for a man with a full pack and no sense of self-preservation. In summer it drips. In autumn it's dry as a sermon. I went up it in the dark, feeling the walls with my palms like a blind man reading scripture, and by the time the sky went gray I was three ridges beyond the temple's reach and my lungs were full of knives.

I allowed myself one rest at the top. Sat with my back against stone that smelled of frost and juniper, ate half a heel of bread I'd stolen from the refectory on pure instinct—the body plans its escapes even when the mind is busy having a theological crisis—and watched dawn come up over the lowlands I'd just betrayed.

From up here the temple was a smudge of slate roofs in the valley haze. Somewhere down there, inquisitors were asking questions. Captain somebody, probably, with an imperial warrant and a list of names, and mine would be at the top of it in Ilvane's neat, kind handwriting.

She'd sold me. I hadn't needed the prophecy to tell me that; I'd needed only her smile, which had been exactly as warm as it always was, and her hands, which had never trembled before in six years of perfect composure. People's faces lie all day long. Hands only shake when the truth gets loose underneath.

"Right," I said aloud, to the juniper and the frost and nobody. "Right."

Talking to yourself. First sign of a man who's been alone too long. Second sign, if you're wondering, is naming the marmots. I resolved not to do that.

I got up and climbed.

* * *

Two days. That's how long the good luck lasted, which by my standards was practically a festival.

On the third day I came down out of the high passes into the Greywater valley, following a route I'd taken twice before—a courier's path along the river gorge, all scree and goat-trail and one memorable stretch where you crossed the water three times on stones that the locals had named, because of course they had, because everything in Thornfell has a name except the people the clans don't want to have names. The stones were called the Sisters. All three of them. Someone had clearly run out of imagination or sisters.

I was halfway across the second Sister, ankle-deep in water cold enough to make my bones file complaints, when I heard the whistle.

Three notes, low-high-low. A clan signal. I knew it because I'd heard it before, years ago, on a night very much like this one, when a clan courier named Brannoc had gotten himself lost in the Larkspur Cut with a broken lamp and a satchel of messages he wasn't supposed to have, and a forgettable temple scribe had walked him out through the dark by memory because the alternative was explaining to the border patrols why there was a dead clansman in the pass.

He owed me for that. Clan folk keep accounts the way temples do—scrupulously, forever, and with interest.

The problem was that the whistle meant *friend, show yourself*, and I had spent three days becoming extremely invested in the opposite policy.

I stood very still on the wet stone and did the arithmetic. If I ran, whoever it was would know I'd heard, and a man who runs in the Greywater gorge is a man with something to hide, and men with something to hide get chased, and I was too tired to be chased. If I answered the whistle wrong, same result. If I answered it right—

If I answered it right, then for the first time in six days, someone in this world would know exactly where I was.

I answered it right. Low-high-low. Because apparently I'd rather be found than be safe, which is the sort of weakness that gets men killed, and also, possibly, the sort that keeps them human. Jury was still out.

Brannoc came around the bend of the gorge with a courier's staff in one hand and a knife already loose in the other, and when he saw me his face did three things in quick succession: recognition, relief, and then a slow, dawning sort of dread, like a man watching weather come over a ridge he has to cross.

"Nakamura." He said it the way you'd say the name of a storm. "They're saying your name in every holdfast from here to the Saddle. They're saying it in the *lowlands*. There's a price."

"There's always a price," I said. "It's the whole economy."

"The empire's offering two hundred crowns." He said it flatly, watching me. "The clans are offering something better than crowns."

"Let me guess. Glory."

"A place in the song." His knuckles were white on the staff. "Maren's people say the Oathbearer's come, and whoever brings him in—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Kestrel, why are they saying your name?"

And here's the thing I want you to understand about that moment, standing on a wet rock in a freezing river with a man holding a knife and a debt in the same hand: I could have lied to him. I'm good at lying. It's practically my trade—six years of being nobody requires a certain fluency in fiction. I could have given him a story about temple politics, a stolen relic, a misunderstanding, and he might even have believed it, because Brannoc wanted to believe it. You could see him wanting it, the way you want a forecast to be wrong about snow.

Instead I said, "A hundred-year-old book says I'm going to start a war. Or end one. The book wasn't specific, and honestly neither am I."

His face did the three things again, in reverse order.

"That's it? That's the whole of it?"

"There's a bell involved. And a birthmark. And a dead oracle who apparently knew my name before my parents chose it, which raises questions I don't have time for." I held out my arms, dripping, hollow-eyed, three days past my last real meal. "That's the inventory. Take me in if you want. Two hundred crowns buys a lot of winter stores, and Maren's song pays better than either."

The water pushed at my legs. The gorge waited. Somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity exhaustion gives you, that if he decided to sell me I wouldn't even blame him. Two hundred crowns. I'd have done it for less, back when I was hungry enough. I'd *had* done it for less—that was literally my job, carrying other people's secrets for bread.

Brannoc looked at me for a long time. Then he did something I didn't expect, which was to laugh—one short, unhappy bark of it, steam curling from his mouth in the cold.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I've been informed that's part of the prophecy."

"Get off the rock before the river takes you. If the river kills the Oathbearer, Maren will blame me, and I've heard her sermons." He waded out and offered his arm, and after a moment's stupid hesitation I took it, and he hauled me onto the bank like I weighed nothing, which flattered neither of us.

We stood there dripping in the shallows, two fools in a gorge, and I felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched. It took me a moment to identify the sensation, because it had been so long. It was the feeling of not being alone. It was appalling. I nearly wept.

"Don't," Brannoc said, reading my face with the blunt accuracy of a man who'd grown up reading weather. "Don't thank me and don't make it a thing. You walked me out of the Cut when you could've sold me for less than they're paying for you now. Clansfolk settle debts." He turned upriver and jerked his head for me to follow. "But hear me, scribe. I'm walking you to my mother's holdfast because I owe you, not because I believe any of this Oathbearer rot. And if the elders decide you're worth more to them as a bargaining piece than a guest, I won't die for you. Understood?"

"Understood." And I meant it, and it didn't even sting, which tells you something about the last six years of my life. "For what it's worth, I wouldn't ask you to."

"For what it's worth," he said, "I believe you. That's the worst part."

* * *

We walked the rest of the day along routes I knew and one I didn't—a clan path cut into the gorge wall, hidden behind a curtain of ivy gone red with autumn, the kind of thing no imperial map would ever hold and no lowland eye would ever find. Brannoc moved through it like water finding its level, and I followed, cataloguing every turn out of habit, filing it away in the archive behind my eyes where I kept all the roads. Even now. Even hunted. Especially hunted. A man's habits are the last thing to starve.

He talked as we went—not much, but in the way of mountain folk, in pieces. His sister was keeping the winter stores. His mother held the holdfast against the zealots' preaching with a stubbornness he described as genetic. Maren had come through in the spring, and the valley hadn't been the same since; she had a way of making hunger feel holy, and hungry people hate being told their hunger is ordinary.

"She'll want you," he said, as the light went long and gold across the water. "When she hears. She won't want to talk to you. Talking implies you might disagree."

"And you're taking me toward her anyway."

"I'm taking you to my mother's table, which is the safest place in the valley and also the most dangerous, depending on the year." He glanced back at me, and for a moment the hard courier's mask slipped and I saw the young man I'd guided out of the dark five years ago, scared and grateful and trying to hide both. "You gave me a road when I had none. I keep thinking maybe that counts for something bigger than a debt. I keep hoping it does."

"It doesn't have to," I said. "Debt's fine. Debt I understand. Debt comes with terms."

"Yeah, well." He faced forward again. "Terms are for merchants. We're in the mountains."

We camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, a fire small enough to cup in your hands, and he split his provisions with me without being asked—dried fish, hard cheese, a twist of salt—and when I tried to refuse he simply put the food in front of me and looked elsewhere until I ate, which is a form of hospitality so aggressive it borders on violence.

I sat there chewing and watching the fire and realized I was trying to remember the last time anyone had fed me without expecting a ledger entry. The temple fed me. Against debt. Smugglers fed me. Against payment. This—this fish, this fire, this man pretending not to watch me eat—this was the first meal in six years that wasn't a transaction, and it terrified me more than the inquisitors had.

Because here's the cost of having no one, the cost I'd been paying gladly, in installments, all this time: nobody can betray you, but nobody feeds you either. Nobody sells your name, but nobody calls you friend across a fire either. I had built my invisibility brick by brick and lived in it like a wall, and now a clansman with a knife and a debt had knocked a hole in it without even trying, and through the hole I could see everything I'd given up, laid out plain as a valley at dawn.

"You're doing the thing again," Brannoc said quietly. "Where your face goes empty."

"Habit."

"Well, stop it. It's unsettling." He poked the fire. "Tomorrow we cross the Greywater headwaters and come at the holdfast from the sheep paths. Two days, if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then three." He lay back with his arms behind his head. "Sleep, Nakamura. Whatever's chasing you, it hasn't found this path yet. And if it does—" a shrug, casual as a landslide, "—well. You know the roads. Show me one I don't."

I slept. Actually slept, deep and dreamless, for the first time since the bell rang thirteen times. When I woke, gray light was seeping into the hollow and Brannoc was already up, standing rigid at the mouth of the overhang, staring down the gorge.

Smoke. Thin and far, but wrong—too many fires in a place that kept few. And rising above the ridge line behind them, catching the early sun, the glint of metal. A column of it. Moving along the river road.

Imperial boots, on clan ground, in strength. Heading for the Greywater holds.

"They're not following you," Brannoc said slowly. His voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when a man is holding something together with his jaw. "They're going ahead of you. Somebody told them where you'd be."

I thought of Ilvane's trembling hands. Of smiles that lie and hands that don't. Of how thoroughly, how *professionally*, I had been set on this road—and how neatly every road I knew seemed to lead exactly where someone else wanted me.

"Brannoc," I said, "how fast can we warn your mother?"
