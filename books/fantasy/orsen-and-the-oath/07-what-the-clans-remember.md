## 7. What the Clans Remember

Brannoc's holdfast turned out to be less a fortress and more a stubborn argument the mountain had lost.

It clung to a shelf of granite above a frozen waterfall, a cluster of stone houses with turf roofs and smoke-holes, all of it leaning into the wind like old men sharing a secret. Sheep pens. A smithy with its fire banked for the night. Children—actual children, small and loud and unafraid of anything except bedtime—chasing each other between the walls until an elder barked at them in a language I only half knew from ledgers and trade contracts.

I stood at the edge of it, snow to my knees, and felt something I hadn't felt since before my father died: the absurd, dangerous urge to knock on a door and be expected.

"Stop that," Brannoc said.

"Stop what?"

"Looking like that. Like you're going to cry or buy the place." He shouldered past me, snow crusted in his beard, his limp more pronounced after two days of my company. "They'll smell hope on you and charge interest."

"You're a charming host."

"I'm not hosting you. I'm *storing* you, same as you'd store a barrel of something volatile." He banged on the nearest door with the flat of his hand. "Mother! I've brought trouble!"

The door opened on a woman built like the mountain's lawyer—broad, gray-braided, unimpressed. She looked at Brannoc, then at me, then at the sky as if consulting it about her son's judgment.

"He's thin," she said.

"He's trouble."

"Trouble can be fed. Come in, thin trouble, before the wind decides it wants you."

Her name was Sefa, and her house smelled of peat smoke, boiled mutton, and tallow, which is to say it smelled like every home I'd ever been thrown out of, and I loved it immediately. There were maybe thirty people in the holdfast proper, and by the time the stew went around, most of them had found a reason to walk past me and look. Not hostile looking. Worse. *Curious* looking.

I kept my eyes on my bowl and did what I do best. I made myself furniture.

It worked for about four hours.

* * *

The trouble started at dawn, when the elders came.

I woke on a bench by the hearth to find five of them arranged around the room like they'd been placed there overnight by someone with strong opinions about composition. Old men and women, faces seamed and wind-cured, wearing wool dyed in colors that probably meant things. Behind them, filling the doorway, stood Brannoc with his arms crossed and an expression that said he'd argued against this and lost.

"Sit up," said the eldest, a woman whose spine had opinions of its own. She lowered herself onto a stool across from me with the care of someone handling a debt ledger. "Boy. Give us your wrist."

"My wrist."

"The left one."

Now, here is a thing about being a scribe: you learn early that when powerful people ask to see a specific part of your body, the correct answer is never enthusiasm. I pulled my sleeve down instead. "I'm sure it's nothing. I write a great deal; my hands are ugly."

The eldest leaned forward anyway, took my arm with fingers surprisingly gentle, and pushed the sleeve back herself.

And there it was. The birthmark. My lifelong companion, the faint gray whorl just below the wrist bone that I'd spent twenty-six years explaining away as a scald, a stain, a quirk of ink absorption—anything but what it was, because I'd never known what it was. A spiral of interlocking lines, fine as hair, like a knot tied in skin.

Except now, watching the eldest woman's face, I saw it reflected. And reflected in hers was recognition.

She made a sound low in her throat, not quite a word, and the other elders leaned in, and one of them—an old man with clouded eyes—said, "Sable Turn," the way you'd say the name of a dead king.

"It's a birthmark," I said, with the calm of a man standing in a burning house insisting the fire is decorative.

"It's a sigil," said the eldest. "The Oracle's mark. The vow-knot. We burned it into our oath-stones a hundred years ago, boy, and there it sits on your arm like it grew there." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "Where is your mother from?"

"Lowland," I lied, automatically, smoothly, and then hated myself for it, because these people had just looked at me like I mattered and my first instinct was still to vanish. "...I don't know. I was raised in the temple. Thornfell's temple. The Quiet Flame."

That landed worse than the lie would have. I watched it move through them—the word *temple*, the word *lowland*, the whole architecture of everything they'd lost a war to—and I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives right before disaster, that I had just told five grieving people that their hundred-year-old sacred sign had grown on the wrist of a debt-slave of the enemy's church.

"That's not possible," one of them whispered.

"Everything is possible," said the eldest grimly. "Most of it is unpleasant."

Brannoc shifted in the doorway. "He didn't know. I'd stake my other leg on it. He thought it was a scald."

"A scald," repeated the eldest, flatly.

"I have a very boring life," I offered. "I fill it with lies about my own skin."

Nobody laughed. That was the moment I understood the shape of the trap I was in. For years, my survival had depended on being forgettable—a scribe so bland that masters walked past him, so careful that smugglers forgot they'd used him. And now five elders had looked at a mark I'd carried since birth and seen a prophecy, and there was no version of this where I stayed furniture. Being seen, I discovered, is infinitely worse than being hunted. Hunters want something from you. People who see you want you to *be* something.

"Fetch Maren," the eldest said.

"No," said Brannoc, fast.

"Maren will want to know."

"That's exactly why we shouldn't tell her."

"Brannoc." The eldest's voice could have frozen the waterfall. "This is clan business older than your grandmother's grudges. Fetch the priestess."

He looked at me. I looked back. Between us passed a brief, silent negotiation conducted entirely in eyebrows, the terms of which were: *you owe me, and I am about to collect in the worst currency.*

He went.

* * *

Maren arrived like weather.

Not the quiet kind. The kind that takes the roof off. She came through the door already speaking, a tall woman in gray robes stitched with red thread, iron rings on her fingers, and a voice pitched to carry across a battlefield—which, I would learn later, was exactly where she'd learned it.

"—and if the stones have woken, then the turn has come, and if the turn has come, then—" She stopped. Looked at me. Went very still, the way a hawk goes still, which is not calm but *aiming*.

"Well," she said softly. "There you are."

"I'm sorry?" I said. "I've been here all morning. I haven't been anywhere."

She crossed the room, took my wrist without asking—everyone in this country apparently considered my arms public property—and held the birthmark up to the firelight. Her breath caught. I heard it catch. That frightened me more than anything the elders had said, because zealots are only dangerous when they're certain, and certainty makes a very specific sound when it arrives.

"The Ember Vow," she breathed. "The Oathbearer lives. The Oracle spoke true."

"With respect," I said, "the Oracle also wrote a hundred years ago, and I transcribe tax records. One of us has clearly been overworked."

She ignored me, which I'd learn was standard. She turned to the elders, holding my arm aloft like evidence at trial. "You see it. You all see it. The knot of the vow, unbroken, on living flesh. A hundred years we kept the stones. A hundred years we taught our children the words in secret while the empire taxed our passes and named our mountains theirs. And the answer was walking the roads all along, wearing the mark under a foreign sleeve."

"That's lovely," I said, extracting my arm with the practiced gentleness of a man freeing it from a trap. "But I think there's been a clerical error."

"There are no errors in the Oracle's hand."

"There are *always* errors in every hand. I've spent ten years correcting them. Ink fades. Memory edits. Scribes make choices." I was talking too fast and I knew it, the way you talk too fast when the ground is tilting. "Whatever your stones say, whatever this mark says, I am not—I am a *scribe*. I copy things. I don't *do* things. If your prophecy needs someone, I can recommend several far more qualified people who would hate this slightly less."

Maren smiled. It was, I will note for the record, a beautiful smile, warm as a hearth, and it was the single most frightening thing I saw in all my weeks in Thornfell, including the raid, including the tower, including the erasure itself. Because it was the smile of a person who has already decided what you are and considers your opinion on the matter a charming formality.

"The vow does not need the willing," she said. "It needs the marked. Willingness is the empire's virtue. Sacrifice is ours."

"Sacrifice," I said carefully, "usually gets volunteered by the person doing the sacrificing."

"And yours is already given. You were born given." She turned to the elders, voice rising into that battlefield register, and I watched the room tilt toward her the way rooms always tilt toward conviction. "At the next dark moon, we wake the stones. We speak the vow through the bearer's mouth, and the clans rise as one flame, and Thornfell belongs to Thornfell again. As the Oracle promised. As the blood on our stones demands."

A murmur went through the elders—not unanimous, I noticed. The eldest woman's face was unreadable. One of the younger elders, a man with a soldier's bearing, looked at me with something that wasn't devotion. It looked almost like pity. I filed that away. Pity is a lever, if you're desperate enough, and I was becoming extremely desperate.

"I have questions," I said, raising my hand like a student, because humor is the last tool left when all others are confiscated. "First: what does 'speaking the vow' actually involve? Second: does it hurt? Third: is there any chance whatsoever it involves me going back to copying ledgers, because I really was quite good at it."

Maren regarded me with radiant patience. "The bearer need not understand the vow. Only speak it."

"Yes, but see, understanding is rather my entire profession, so—"

"*Enough.*" But she said it gently, absently, already turning away, already ascending into whatever cathedral existed inside her head. "Rest, bearer. Eat. At dark moon, you will be ready or you will be made ready. Either way, the flame wakes."

She swept out. The elders followed in ones and twos, leaving me alone with Brannoc, Sefa, the fire, and the growing certainty that I had traded a cage with a view of books for a cage with a view of mountains.

* * *

Night came down hard and cold. Sefa fed me again—these people expressed every emotion through stew—and then Brannoc sat across from me, working a whetstone along a knife he had no obvious plans for, and finally said:

"You're going to run."

"I'm going to *consider* running. Running requires exits, and I've counted the exits, and there are three, and all three have watchers, because your priestess isn't stupid."

"She's worse than stupid. Stupid people change their minds." He tested the edge with his thumb. "Maren's wanted this since she was a girl. Her mother died in the last skirmish before the peace. She doesn't want a war, Kestrel. She wants a *meaning* for the dying, and you're it."

"Wonderful. I've been promoted from debt-slave to grief-object. The benefits must be staggering."

"They're not." He looked up, and for once there was no irony anywhere in his face. "Listen to me. My sister Isolde thinks you're a miracle. Half the young ones do. They'll guard you day and night, and they'll call it protection, and it will be a cell with singing in it. But the old ones—the eldest, Dagny, and Corran, the soldier—they remember what vows cost. This clan buried a generation last time somebody spoke a holy word over the stones. Talk to them. Not Maren. Them."

"Why?" I asked. "What's in it for you? You dragged me here. You could have pointed me at the pass and washed your hands."

He was quiet a long moment. Snow ticked against the shutters. Somewhere outside, a child laughed and was shushed.

"When you guided me through the Narrows," he said finally, "I asked why you did it. You said, 'Because you paid.' It was a good lie. Very dry. Very you." He set the knife down. "But you took the long way around the icefall even though the short way was faster, because the short way kills couriers, and you didn't want me dead. I've guided with empire men and clan men and one mad priest of the river folk, and none of them took the long way. So." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own sincerity, and reached for the whetstone again. "That's what's in it for me."

I didn't have a reply. That's rare enough to record. I sat there in the peat smoke with a birthmark that had apparently been a declaration of war all along, and thought about how for ten years I had built my whole self out of being no one, and how the world had taken one look at my wrist and decided I was everyone's.

Being hunted, I could work with. Hunters follow rules: they chase, you run, terrain applies. But being *seen*—being needed, being claimed, being the answer to a hundred years of somebody else's grief—that had no rules at all. You couldn't outrun a meaning. Believe me. I checked the exits.

Outside, the watch changed. I heard Isolde's voice among the guards, bright and certain, telling someone that the Oathbearer had come, that everything would be different now.

Everything would be different now. Of that, at least, the girl and I agreed.

I lay down on the bench, pulled the blanket to my chin, and began, methodically, the way I catalogued everything, to list what I knew: the mark was real. The Oracle was real—or at least old. Maren intended to use my mouth to start a war at the dark moon, which gave me some number of days I couldn't yet count. The elders were divided. Brannoc was loyal, which was inconvenient, because loyalty is a debt, and I had sworn off debts.

And somewhere south of here, imperial inquisitors were asking after a scribe, and somewhere east, the temple masters who sold me were pretending they'd never known my name.

Three directions. Three kinds of hunters. One wrist.

I closed my eyes and did the thing I do best.

I memorized the exits.

* * *

They came for the holdfast at first light—but not many of them, and not loudly. I knew before anyone shouted, because the dogs went quiet first, and quiet dogs are louder than barking ones.

Corran burst through the door with snow on his shoulders and no pity left in his face. "Riders on the high trail," he said. "Imperial blue. Six. Maybe scouts, maybe not." His eyes found mine across the room, and I watched him decide, in real time, what I was worth. "They're asking at the steadings for a thin man with a scribe's hands."

The room went still. Sefa's ladle stopped mid-stir. Outside, Isolde's voice rose, excited, calling for Maren, calling that the test had come, that the Oathbearer would be proven—

And I stood there in the smoke with stew cooling in my belly and six riders coming down the mountain for me, and I understood that whatever I was going to be—miracle, weapon, martyr, corpse—it was going to be decided in the next hour, by people who had never once asked me.

Maren appeared in the doorway, backlit by snow, smiling her hearth-warm smile.

"Bearer," she said. "It begins."
