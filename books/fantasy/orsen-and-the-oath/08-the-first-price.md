## 8. The First Price

The dream came again that night, the one where I am copying the same line forever and the ink keeps turning to blood, and I woke with Brannoc's elbow in my ribs and his hand clamped over my mouth before I'd finished the first scream.

"Down," he breathed. "Horses. Many."

I listened past his fingers. Beneath the wind off the high pass there it was: hooves, more than a patrol's worth, moving with the patient discipline of soldiers who had all night and no doubts.

We'd slept in the hayloft of the holdfast's winter barn, because Brannoc's mother had looked at me once—looked at the sigil on my wrist, then at my face—and said, "You'll take the loft. Walls have ears and mine have opinions." I had thought she meant gossip. I had not thought she meant this.

"How many is many?" I whispered against his palm.

He lifted three fingers, considered, added two more. Ten. Maybe twelve. For one scribe who hadn't done anything except exist in the wrong margin of a dead woman's book.

"They followed you," he said, and there was no accusation in it, which was somehow worse than accusation. Brannoc was a man who kept accounts the way I did—silently, precisely, in a ledger no one else could read. I could see him entering the debt now. *One scribe. Cost: unknown.*

"I didn't lead them here," I said. "I didn't know where *here* was until you dragged me."

"I know." He rolled onto his knees and peered through a gap in the boards. Firelight below, orange and wrong. The holdfast dogs weren't barking, which meant someone had already dealt with the dogs, which meant this wasn't a raid. It was an errand. "But knowing doesn't unring it."

Below us, the door of the longhouse banged open and I heard Sefa—Brannoc's sister, seventeen, all elbows and mockery, who had spent the evening telling me my handwriting was cowardly because it never committed to a stroke—shout something defiant and half-laughing, the way she shouted everything.

Then a voice I knew. Smooth as oiled slate, carrying across the yard with the ease of a man who has never once had to raise it.

"Sera Voss," I whispered. "Inquisitor Captain. She interviewed me at the temple for two hours about a misfiled ledger. She remembered my ink brand better than my face."

"You're popular."

"I'm *documented*."

Boots on frozen mud. A woman's voice, older, sharp with fear: Brannoc's mother, ordering children into the root cellar. And then Sefa's voice again, closer, directly beneath us, bright as a struck flint:

"He went east! Over the shepherd's stair—you'll lose him in the scree if you stand here admiring each other!"

She was lying. Magnificently, instantly, without a flicker of hesitation, pointing them away from the loft, away from the barn, away from me—a boy she'd known for less than a day, whose only crime was being written about by a corpse.

Brannoc grabbed my arm. "Back way. Now."

I went. Of course I went. That's the thing I keep returning to, the sentence I copy over and over in the dark: *I went.* I didn't argue, didn't hesitate, didn't do anything so noble as refuse. When a girl offers herself as the diversion, you take the diversion, because the alternative is both of you dying and the arithmetic of survival is a cold little clerk that lives in my chest and does its sums without asking permission.

We dropped from the loft into the tack room, out through the gap where a board had rotted, into the goat pen, over the wall. Behind us I heard Sefa's voice climb—one last joke, pitched perfectly to hold attention—and then a sound like a door slamming, and then no voice at all.

Brannoc stopped. One heartbeat. Two. Every part of him wanted to turn around; I felt it through his grip on my wrist like a second pulse.

"Don't," I said, and hated myself for saying it, because it was true and true things are often obscene. "If you go back, she died for nothing. If we run, she died for something. That's all we can give her now."

"That's a scribe's answer," he said. "Tidy. Written small."

"It's the only answer that isn't a second body."

He ran. I ran after him, up through the pines, into the snow that had started falling an hour ago and was already filling our tracks behind us like a merciful clerk correcting an error. The last thing I heard from the holdfast was a single shout, wordless, from Brannoc's mother—the sound of a woman finding out what the night cost.

The snow kept falling. It fell on the holdfast and on the road east and on a girl named Sefa who would never mock anyone's handwriting again, and it did not care, and I hated it for that, and I walked.

* * *

Sera Voss stood in the holdfast's yard and let the snow accumulate on her shoulders because brushing it off would mean moving, and moving would mean admitting she was still in her body.

Twelve scouts. One order: recover the fugitive scribe Nakamura, alive if practical. One obstacle: a clan girl of perhaps seventeen who had stepped between the squad and the barn with her chin up and a lie ready, and who had gone for the sergeant's knife when the lie failed—not to kill, clearly, she'd held it wrong, held it like a girl who had seen knives used for cheese—and who was now lying in the trampled snow with her blood still steaming.

"Report," Voss said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, in the distant way one is proud of a stranger's trick.

"She drew on Sergeant Hale, Captain. Protocol was observed."

Protocol was observed. She looked at the girl's face. There was snow in her eyelashes. Someone would have to tell the mother, and the mother was standing in the longhouse doorway right now, held back by two scouts, making a sound Voss had heard exactly twice in eleven years of service and had hoped never to catalog a third time.

"Protocol," Voss said, "is a procedure for keeping soldiers alive. She weighed forty kilos and held the knife backwards. What protocol required her death?"

"The captain's own standing order, Captain. No witnesses to the fugitive's location."

Yes. Her order. Drafted in a warm room in Thornfell's garrison, four days ago, when the fugitive had been a name on a page and a set of instructions, not a boy with an ink-stained thumb and a birthmark the temple archives described in suspicious detail.

She crouched beside the body. The girl's hand was still half-closed around nothing. In her other fist, pressed flat under her palm as if she'd been protecting it, was a wooden token—a crude carved bell, the kind mountain clans gave siblings at midwinter. Thirteen facets. Voss had read enough seized clan correspondence to know what thirteen meant, and the knowledge arrived in her stomach like swallowed ice.

"Captain." Lieutenant Ashe, at her shoulder, breath fogging. "Tracker says the fugitive went east into the high passes. We can have him by dawn if we push."

She should say yes. The warrant in her breast pocket said yes. The Grand Inquisitor's seal said yes, and above the seal, faint but legible to anyone who knew how imperial paper was made, the watermark of the Order of the Quiet Flame—because the temple had requested this operation, paid for this operation, and Voss had told herself that was normal, temples cooperated with the law, that was the whole architecture of peace.

But she had interrogated Master Ilvane's messenger two nights ago, a soft man named Corvel who sweated through three shirts, and he had answered questions too quickly and too completely, and one of his answers had been: *"The Order asks only that the bearer be recovered before the thirteenth turn, and that no record of the recovery survive."* No record. Not even of success. You don't erase the paperwork of a lawful arrest. You erase the paperwork of something else.

And now there was a dead child in the snow, killed under Voss's own standing order, for a fugitive whose file contained no crime at all—only a name, a birthmark, and a date.

"Ashe," she said. "Take six and push east. Slowly."

"Slowly, Captain?"

"The storm's thickening. Men die in scree in weather like this, and I won't write those letters." She stood. Her knees ached; she was thirty-four and the cold had opinions. "I'll follow at first light with the rest. And Ashe—if the fugitive resists, he is to be taken alive. That is not a suggestion. If anyone's blade touches him without cause, I will personally conduct the inquiry, and I have never once filed a favorable report about myself."

Ashe blinked, saluted, and took his six men into the white.

Voss remained in the yard a moment longer. Snow filled the girl's open palm, covering the place where the token had been, so Voss took the token—evidence, she told herself, evidence of clan sympathies, relevant to the case—and put it in her pocket next to the warrant, where it lay against the imperial seal like a small wooden accusation.

Thirteen facets. The cracked bell had tolled thirteen times. The prophecy dated itself to tonight, and the temple wanted no record, and every road of inquiry led back to a smiling woman in grey robes who trembled only when she thought no one was counting.

Voss had joined the inquisition at nineteen because she believed the empire was the dam between the lowlands and a flood of old hatreds. Dams were supposed to be honest about where the water went.

She mounted up and did not look at the longhouse doorway, because looking would make it hers, and she was not yet ready to carry it.

* * *

By the time the moon cleared the ridgeline we were three miles east and climbing, and Brannoc had not spoken in an hour, which for Brannoc was a geological event.

The shepherd's stair was a smuggler's path I'd memorized years ago—a shelf of stone cut into the mountainside, wide enough for one nervous goat, dropping away on the left into black air. I knew it the way I knew everything: completely, uselessly, permanently. My mind kept maps the way other men kept grudges. Somewhere below and behind us, six imperial trackers were learning why that mattered.

"We should've gone back," Brannoc said finally.

"For her? They'd have taken you too. Or worse—they'd have taken you and asked you about me, and you'd have told them, because you're honest, and honesty under a hot iron is just a slower confession."

"You don't know what I'd have told them."

"No," I admitted. "But I know what they'd have done to find out, and I've decided I don't want to attend."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh and entirely grief. Then he sat down on the path, right there, with the drop yawning beside him, and put his head in his hands, and I understood that the walk was over for a while whether I liked it or not.

I sat too. The snow came down between us and the world, softening everything, making the mountain feel like a room with the doors closed.

"She held the knife wrong," he said. "I taught her. Three summers ago. She laughed at me the whole lesson and never learned the grip." His voice didn't break. It did something worse—it stayed level, the levelness of a man reading figures off a ledger he can't close. "If I'd taught her proper, maybe—"

"Maybe you'd be planning her funeral instead of attending it in your head. Brannoc. Listen to me, because I'm going to say the truest thing I own." I pulled off my glove and showed him my wrist, the sigil, pale as watered milk in the moonlight. "This mark has been on my skin since before I could walk. I never asked for it, never understood it, and in nine days it has cost your family a daughter. So believe me when I tell you I know exactly what it is to carry something you never agreed to. The difference between us is that yours is grief and mine is apparently destiny, and destiny is just grief that hasn't happened yet."

He looked at me for a long moment. Snow gathered on his shoulders and in his hair, and under all that beard and grimness his face was young—younger than mine, though he'd never admit it.

"What happens if they catch you?" he asked. "Truly. What does the vow do?"

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. The book names me bearer and then stops, like a scribe who got bored, or scared, or—" I stopped. The oracle's cave. The geography in the final pages, the valley shaped like a cupped hand, the words I'd skimmed because reading them felt like walking on a frozen lake. "*Or*," I said slowly, "like someone who wrote the ending somewhere else, where the people pressuring her couldn't watch her write it."

Brannoc frowned. "You think there's another book."

"I think there's always another book. I'm a scribe; it's the one article of faith I keep." I stood, and my knees reported their grievances, and I ignored them. "There's a cave in the high valleys. The oracle wrote there—I'm sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of a route you've walked in dreams. If there's an ending to this, it's written there, in her own hand, where no master could edit it."

"And if the ending is 'the bearer dies'?"

"Then at least I'll die informed. It's more than most people get."

He rose too, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and looked back west toward the holdfast, toward the small dark smudge where his home was and his sister wasn't. He didn't say goodbye to it. He just looked, the way you look at a page you've finished and can't revise.

"East, then," he said. "To the oracle's cave."

"To the oracle's cave," I agreed, and we climbed.

Behind us the snow went on falling, patient and impartial, covering the path, covering the blood, covering everything except the two things I carried out of that holdfast: a dead girl's laughter, and the growing certainty that whatever waited in the oracle's cave, I had already begun paying for it in a currency I couldn't refund.

Somewhere below, faint on the wind, a pursuit horn sounded—once, then cut short, as if the blower had thought better of announcing himself.

They hadn't given up. Neither had I. But only one of us knew where the story was written, and I intended to reach it first.
