## 9. Voss Interrogates a Ghost

The temple of the Quiet Flame smelled like money pretending to be incense.

I'd been in a hundred interrogation rooms by then—imperial ones with iron rings and drainage, clan ones that were just caves with better posture—but I'd never sat across from anyone in a room like this. The Master's parlor had cushions thick enough to drown in, a brazier burning something expensive, and a window angled so the mountain light fell exactly where she wanted it. On her. Not on me. Everything in that room was arranged to make you feel like the supplicant.

I made a note of it. Then I made myself comfortable anyway, because the fastest way to unsettle someone who has staged a room is to refuse to be staged.

"Captain Voss." Master Ilvane poured tea without asking if I wanted any. Of course she didn't ask. People who pour without asking have decided the answer already; it's the same instinct as people who write history. "You've come a long way from the capital for one runaway scribe."

"One scribe," I said, "who is apparently worth three warrants, two sealed orders, and whatever your bell-wardens were doing on the night of the thirteenth toll."

She smiled at me over the rim of her cup. It was a good smile. Practiced the way a sword arm is practiced—you don't notice the work until you try to get past it.

"The thirteenth toll was an accident," she said. "Old bronze cracks. Old bells ring strangely. The empire pays me to keep this temple quiet, Captain, not to explain acoustics to it."

"And yet here I am, explaining them to you."

I let the silence do some work. Interrogation is mostly carpentry: you build a box out of what someone said yesterday and see if they fit inside it today. Ilvane watched me build, and to her credit she didn't fidget. But her thumb moved once along the edge of her cup—a small, precise motion, like a woman smoothing a wrinkle out of a letter before sealing it—and I thought: *there*. That's where the seam is.

"Tell me about Kestrel Nakamura," I said.

"What is there to tell? A debtor. A competent copyist. Unremarkable." She said *unremarkable* the way other people say *disposable*, and I filed that too.

"He transcribed a century-old book of prophecies last week."

"Did he?" Her eyebrows performed mild interest. "How industrious."

"It named him."

Something happened behind her face then. Not fear—I want to be clear about that, because I spent a long time afterward trying to decide what it was, and fear would have been simpler. Fear I could have used. What happened was more like a door closing in an empty house: no expression changed, but the draft stopped. Whatever had been moving through her went still.

"That," she said, "would be a remarkable coincidence."

"Yes," I agreed. "Remarkable. Like a cracked bell ringing thirteen times on the exact night a dead oracle scheduled it. Like an earthquake opening the archive vault three days before the date in question. Remarkable how many accidents have been lining up around this temple lately, Master Ilvane. Almost as if they'd been *arranged*."

"You flatter us. We are monks and copyists, Captain. Our miracles are calligraphy."

"Then you won't mind showing me the Oracle of Sable Turn."

And there it was—the thing I'd come for, though I didn't know it yet. Because she didn't say "what oracle?" She didn't say "I'm afraid I don't know the volume you mean." A woman genuinely ignorant of the book would have asked. Instead Ilvane set down her cup with a small ceramic click and said:

"The Oracle of Sable Turn was destroyed decades ago. Water damage, rot, the usual enemies of paper. I signed the destruction order myself."

She said it smoothly. She said it like a line she'd rehearsed in front of a mirror, which meant it was a line she'd needed to rehearse, which meant somewhere in the last forty years someone had asked her about that book before and she'd decided never to be caught flat-footed again.

"You signed its destruction order," I repeated. "A prophecy concerning the Ember Vow. Concerning the war everyone up and down these mountains is terrified of reigniting. And the Order of the Quiet Flame burned it."

"We are not a museum of dangerous things, Captain. Some books are kinder as ash."

"Some books are," I said. "But you kept a copy, didn't you. Or you wouldn't have needed a destruction order at all—you'd just have let it rot quietly and told no one. You only sign orders for things someone might come looking for. Paper trails are for crimes, Master Ilvane. Honest losses leave no paperwork."

For the first time, the smile came off her face entirely, and I understood why people found her kind. Without it, she wasn't frightening. She was tired. Deeply, specifically tired, the way a person gets from carrying something heavy up the same staircase for thirty years.

"You're sharper than your reputation," she said.

"My reputation is that I follow orders. It's accurate."

"Then follow this one." She leaned forward, and the light did its work on her face, silvering her hair, softening everything. "The empire does not want this prophecy, Captain Voss. The capital wants the mountain clans quiet, the lowland granaries full, and the word 'war' confined to histories. If a scribe has run off into Thornfell carrying a hundred-year-old oath in his head, the kindest thing—the most lawful thing—is for him to die in the snow before he reaches anyone who might believe him. Your warrant says *apprehend*. Read between its lines. It says *contain*."

"I've read my orders," I said. "I wrote half of them."

"Then you know what I'm offering is simply efficiency."

What she was offering, dressed in efficiency's clothes, was murder with a signature page. I looked at her hands. They were steady now. Steadier than they'd been all morning, and that steadiness frightened me more than trembling would have, because trembling means conflict and steadiness means decision.

* * *

Here is what I knew going into that room, and what I'd pieced together on the ride up from the lowlands, reading dispatches by lamplight while my troopers complained about the cold:

Three weeks ago, an earthquake cracked the Quiet Flame's archive vault. Standard tremor; the range shrugs like that twice a decade. Within four days, the temple had petitioned the imperial chancery for an archival survey—which is unusual, because temples handle their own archives and always have. Within six days, the chancery had granted it, which is *very* unusual, because the chancery grants nothing in under a season unless someone senior wants it granted. Within nine days, a scribe employed by the temple had found a forbidden book, read his own name in it, and run.

Nine days. From crack to catastrophe. In my experience, catastrophes that assemble themselves that quickly aren't accidents. They're schedules.

And there was one more thing, the thing I'd carried up the mountain folded inside my warrant like a stone inside bread. Before I left the capital, an old records clerk—an archivist so far down the chancery's corridors that he'd gone translucent—had pulled a genealogical register for me without being asked why. He'd done it because twenty years ago I'd bought his daughter a lawyer, and men like him keep ledgers of kindness the way temples keep ledgers of debt.

The register traced the Nakamura line back five generations. Scribes, clerks, a ferryman, a midwife. Ordinary people, meticulously ordinary, right up until the fourth generation back, when the trail crossed the mountains and ended at a name the register recorded with a strange bureaucratic hesitation—one entry, struck through, re-entered, struck through again, as if whoever wrote it couldn't decide whether the man had existed.

The name was Aldric Vane. The last Oathbreaker. The man who, according to every surviving fragment, broke the Ember Vow a century ago and took the war's ending into his own grave.

Kestrel Nakamura wasn't just a random scribe who'd stumbled onto a prophecy. He was the prophecy's own bloodline, walking around in a debt-collar, filing ledgers, keeping his head down. Either the universe had a sense of humor, or someone had known exactly whose grandson they were assigning to recatalogue a cracked vault.

I walked back into the parlor after the noon bells—this temple rang twelve times, always twelve, pointedly—and I laid it on the table between us like a corpse.

"Aldric Vane," I said.

Ilvane didn't look at the register. She looked at me, and for a long moment neither of us breathed, two women measuring each other across a table that suddenly felt much smaller.

"Where did you get that?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters," she said slowly, "because it tells me whether I'm negotiating with the empire or with *you*."

"With the law," I said. "Which is supposed to be the same thing. Sit down, Master. You're going to tell me everything, and you're going to start with why a temple that claims to have destroyed a prophecy spent nine days engineering a situation in which its most expendable scribe would find it."

* * *

She talked for two hours. I'll give her this: when she finally decided to talk, she didn't ration it. Maybe she was tired of carrying the staircase. Maybe she judged, correctly, that I already had enough to hang her and honesty might buy something cheaper than rope. Maybe—and this is the possibility that kept me awake that night—she believed, sincerely, in what she'd built, the way zealots believe, the way the worst people always believe, with their whole clean heart.

"There was a forgery," she said. "My predecessor's predecessor made it. A generation ago, the clans were gathering—raids, oaths, a war-council at Thornfell that nearly became an army. The temple needed leverage. So we gave them a prophecy."

"You forged the Oracle of Sable Turn."

"We *amended* it. The original existed—we didn't create it, Captain, we're copyists, not poets. The original was worse. The original said the vow could be unmade. Publicly, by the bearer, freely given. Do you understand what that means? It means the war was never inevitable. It means a hundred years of quiet was purchased on a lie—that the oath must simply be *borne*, generation after generation, held shut by whoever carries it, until the bearer dies and it passes on and the mountain keeps holding its breath. We taught the clans fatalism. Fatalism is very cheap to maintain."

"And the amendment?"

"We added a clause. That the vow, once claimed, cannot be refused—that the bearer is bound, that refusal means ruin for both peoples. We made the oath a prison instead of a door. And then we made sure the right people read it." Her voice stayed level, but her hands had started moving again, smoothing invisible wrinkles. "It worked. The clans stopped gathering. Why march to war over an oath that can't be broken? We bought sixty years of peace with a paragraph."

"And now the original has surfaced, and a boy with Oathbreaker blood has read it, and your beautiful lie is standing in a room with the truth."

"Now," she said, "the boy is running toward the clans with the truth in his head, and if he reaches them—if he stands in front of a war-council and speaks the unmaking rite—then either the clans demand the empire honor a vow we've spent a century denying exists, or they take the unmaking as proof the whole prophecy is temple forgery and go to war over *that*. There is no version, Captain, where your empire survives this quietly. Except one."

"Where the boy dies in the snow."

"Where the boy dies in the snow," she agreed gently, "and the secret dies with him, and the lie goes on holding the peace together. Is that so monstrous? I have kept the peace, Captain. However I had to. Ask the granaries. Ask the villages that haven't burned. Ask any mother in the lowlands whether she'd trade one forgettable scribe for the war not coming to her door."

I should have felt triumph. I had her—cold, documented, signed-in-her-own-hand. Instead what I felt was colder than anything she'd offered, because I'd finally seen the shape of the machine I served. She hadn't beaten the system. She *was* the system, functioning perfectly: expendable instruments, engineered coincidences, a warrant written in language deniable enough that a captain could be steered toward murder without ever being ordered there. The empire didn't want the truth about the vow. The empire wanted the quiet. Ilvane had just been the quiet's local manager.

"You chose him," I said. "Nakamura. Out of however many scribes you have. You put him in that vault."

"He was suited."

"He was *expendable*. No family. Crushing debt—he can't run far because you hold his ledger. Forgettable by habit. And Oathbreaker blood, which means if the prophecy needed a bearer, the prophecy would find him plausible." I stood. My chair didn't scrape; even my anger has learned discipline. "You didn't pick him despite his bloodline, Master. You picked him *because* of it. If this goes wrong, a dead Oathbearer's heir proves nothing. If it goes right—" I stopped, because I realized I didn't know what *right* looked like in her plan, and that was the worst part. "What happens if he claims the vow? Did you ever even plan for that? Or was every branch of the tree just different ways for him to die?"

Ilvane looked up at me, and for one unguarded moment she looked old—not aged, but *old*, the way institutions are old, patient and hollow.

"Captain," she said, "in forty years, no bearer has ever chosen correctly. The vow doesn't offer good options. It offers a sealed war or an open one. I merely ensured the man holding it would be someone whose loss the world could absorb."

"The world," I said, "hasn't met him."

I gathered my papers. At the door I paused, because there was one more question, the one that had been sitting in my chest since the genealogy register, and I almost didn't ask it, because I suspected the answer would cost me something I wasn't ready to pay.

"The destruction order," I said. "For the Oracle of Sable Turn. You signed it decades ago. Who ordered you to?"

She was quiet long enough that the brazier popped.

"The chancery," she said. "Sealed directive. Signed by the Hand of the Emperor himself."

I walked out into the mountain cold, and the cold was honest, at least. Somewhere above us, in the white passes, a scribe with a dead man's blood was running for his life from everyone I worked for—and I had just learned that the order to erase him predated his birth by fifty years.

The law I served was rotten to the crown. Which meant, if I was going to keep calling myself its servant, I'd have to decide what part of it I was actually serving.

By nightfall I'd written two reports. One said the interrogation was inconclusive and the trail had gone cold past the high passes.

The other one I didn't send anywhere. Yet.

But I kept it. Folded small, against my ribs, where I keep the things I'm not ready to be true.
