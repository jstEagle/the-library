## 10. The Oracle's Cave

The prophecy's map was a lie in the way all good maps are lies: technically accurate, spiritually hostile. It gave distances in "paces of a penitent," which is the kind of unit invented by someone who has never had to walk anywhere with a full belly and a bad attitude. It marked landmarks that no longer existed—a ford, a shrine to some river god the empire had taxidermied into a footnote—and it described the cave's entrance as "where the mountain shows its teeth."

"Very helpful," I said, squinting up at a cliff face that showed nothing but teeth for half a mile in every direction.

Brannoc, who had been walking behind me in patient silence for six hours—the silence of a man conserving warmth and words like coinage—said, "You've read this thing how many times?"

"Enough times to hate it properly."

"And you're sure it's real?"

I touched my wrist, where the sigil sat faint as breath on glass. "The bell cracked on schedule. The inquisitors came on schedule. I'm standing in the snow on schedule. At this point I'd believe it if it told me the moon owed me money."

We climbed. Of course we climbed. Prophecies never send anyone downhill; there's no dignity in descent. The wind off Thornfell's shoulder came sideways and personal, finding every gap in my coat like it had studied me specifically. Brannoc moved through it the way he did everything—like the weather was a distant relative he tolerated but didn't respect.

It was Brannoc who found the entrance, because of course it was. He stopped so abruptly I walked into his back.

"There," he said, and pointed at what I'd taken for shadow.

Where the mountain shows its teeth. The rocks above us jutted in a jagged row—upper jaw—and beneath them, a cleft just wide enough for a man sideways, black as an inkwell. Someone had wanted the place found by the right person and ignored by everyone else. That, more than anything, convinced me we were in the right spot. Concealment is a language, and I'd been fluent in it my whole life.

"You first," Brannoc said.

"I'm flattered by your faith."

"It's not faith. If it collapses, better you than me. You owe me still."

"The debt is acknowledged," I said, and went sideways into the dark.

* * *

Inside, the air changed. That's the thing about very old places—they have their own weather. The cold stopped biting and started watching. My boots crunched on something that wasn't quite stone, wasn't quite sand, and when I held the lantern low I saw it was ash. A fine grey drift of it, ankle-deep, spread across the floor like snowfall from a fire that had happened before history got around to caring.

Then I raised the light, and forgot how to breathe.

Frescoes covered the walls. Not painted—painted things fade, flake, surrender—but burned in, seared into the rock itself, the way a brand sears into hide. Figures in ochre and bone-white and one deep red that seemed to pulse when I looked away from it and look back. A procession of them circled the chamber: robed figures ascending a mountain, a figure kneeling at an altar of stone, hands outstretched over a flame that was drawn not as fire but as a word—I could see lettering coiled inside the flames, tiny, dense, a script I half-recognized from the margins of the Oracle's book.

And everywhere, everywhere, names being erased. That was the part I understood only slowly, the way you understand a wound is serious after the pain arrives. In panel after panel, figures wrote on scrolls, on stone tablets, on the air itself—and then other figures scraped the writing away, or the writing simply dissolved mid-stroke, ink lifting off the page like smoke returning to a fire.

"She lived here," Brannoc said quietly. His voice does that sometimes—goes quiet in places where mine wants to fill the silence with jokes, because his quiet is heavier than my noise. "The oracle. All those years."

"How can you tell?"

He pointed at a shallow hollow worn into the stone near the far wall. Kneeling-shaped. "Somebody knelt right there long enough to wear rock soft."

Deeper in, past the procession frescoes, the cave opened into the oracle's actual workroom, and this was the part that undid me. Everything was preserved mid-motion. A writing desk of living stone, and on it, sheets of vellum pinned under a paperweight of river-smoothed granite. An inkwell, dry now, its rim stained black. A stylus lying where a hand had set it down—or dropped it—and beside the stylus, a chair.

Not a grand chair. A wooden chair, plain as a temple scullery bench, the kind of thing I'd sat in ten thousand times. And I stood there looking at it and felt something crack open in my chest that I hadn't planned on.

She'd been a working writer. That was all. Whoever she'd been—the Sable Oracle, the voice of the thirteenth turn, the woman whose words were currently trying to kill me—at the end of the day she'd been somebody hunched over a desk in a cold room, chasing a sentence, running out of light.

"I don't know why that makes it worse," I said.

"Because she's real," Brannoc said. "Easier when they're stories."

On the desk, the last sheet. I didn't touch it at first. I looked at it the way you look at a door you already know what's behind. Then I lifted the granite weight and turned it toward the lantern.

The handwriting deteriorated across the page. It began in that same disciplined hand as the book I'd transcribed—every letter a soldier at attention—and by the final lines it had gone ragged, urgent, letters leaning like people fleeing. The last legible line said:

*They are making me write it as a summons. It was meant to be a warning. Find the second hand.*

My mouth went dry. "Brannoc."

"I see it." He'd come around the desk without touching anything, which I was learning was his way—he treated other people's histories with a care most men reserve for loaded crossbows.

"'Find the second hand,'" I read again. "There's another manuscript. She wrote two—one they let stand, one they tried to bury. Or—" I turned the page over. Blank. I checked under the desk, along the wall seams. Nothing. "Or someone got here first."

"Empire's been climbing these mountains for a hundred years," Brannoc said. "They burn what they can't tax."

But I kept thinking about Master Ilvane's smile, and her trembling hands, and the way she'd asked me—not ordered me, *asked* me—what I'd read. The temple knew things. The temple always knows things. It's practically their trade.

* * *

We explored the rest of the chamber while the light lasted, and that was where I found the ritual.

It was the largest fresco, dominating the back wall, and unlike the others it was complete—finished, deliberate, the work of a steady hand rather than a desperate one. Which told me she'd carved it early, when she still believed someone might need instructions. Or late, when she'd decided someone deserved the truth.

It showed the vow entire. The bearer kneeling at the altar-flame. The clans' representatives on one side, bearing iron. The imperial seal on the other, bearing wax. And above it all, rendered larger than any figure, a single glyph—the thirteenth-bell mark, the cracked circle—and beneath it, in the old script, the terms.

I read them three times. My reading is fast—it's the one gift the temple ever gave me, this habit of swallowing pages whole—but I made myself go slow anyway, because some things you want to be sure of before they rearrange your life.

The vow binds war and peace both, the fresco said. While the Ember Vow stands unclaimed, the clans cannot rise and the empire cannot march on Thornfell. But an oath unworn decays. Within a generation of its oracle's death, it must be claimed by a bearer—or it will seek one out on its own, and the seeking is never gentle. Hence me. Hence the bell. Hence everything.

A claimed bearer may carry the vow, sealing the truce for another century. Or the bearer may *unmake* it—speak it backward, publicly, at the turn of the moon's thirteenth shadow—and then neither side holds the leash.

That much I knew. What I hadn't known—what nobody had written in any version I'd seen—was the price. It was carved small, tucked beneath the altar figure like a footnote, because apparently even prophets understand that fine print is where the truth lives.

*The bearer who unmakes the vow surrenders their name to it. From every record, every memory, every mouth: removed. As though they had never been.*

I read it a fourth time, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something else. They declined.

"Kestrel." Brannoc had been watching my face, not the wall. "What does it say?"

I should have lied. I'd built a whole life out of well-placed lies; it would have been easy. Instead I heard myself say, "If I break the vow, I stop existing. Not dead. Worse. Dead leaves a body and mourners. This—" I tapped the carved line. "This takes the name. Every ledger I copied, gone blank. Everyone who ever knew me forgets. My mother's face goes smooth as an egg. There's just a hole where Kestrel Nakamura used to be, and nobody remembers there was ever a hole."

The cave was very quiet. Even the wind outside seemed to be listening.

"And if you don't?" Brannoc said. "If you carry it? Or pass it?"

"Then the oath lives, and someday it comes due again, and it finds some other scribe with a birthmark and bad luck. And Maren gets her war either way, because she'll claim I carried it for the clans whether I do or not."

"So." He folded his arms. He does this when he's thinking, which took me weeks to distinguish from when he's annoyed. "One road costs you yourself. The other roads cost everyone else. And you're standing there doing sums."

"I'm a scribe. Arithmetic is the family business."

"Kestrel." He said my name the way he says it—carefully, like it's worth something, like it's a thing he intends to keep using. "Look at the wall again. Look at what she left you."

I looked. And this time I saw it—the thing I'd been too busy drowning to notice. The fresco of the unmaking didn't show the bearer alone. It showed figures around him. Faces turned toward him. And each of those faces had been carved holding something: a token, a keepsake, a scrap of cloth or iron or bone. Small objects. Anchors.

Below the central figure, in that same careful early hand, a single explanatory line:

*Names live in things longer than they live in minds. What is given in love resists the erasing. This is not mercy. It is a flaw in the design. I leave flaws deliberately.*

I stared at it for a long time.

"A flaw in the design," I repeated.

"She wanted a way out," Brannoc said. "Even then. Even while she was writing their damned oath, she was hiding exits in the walls."

Temptation, I want to be clear about this, doesn't always look like temptation. Sometimes it looks like relief. Standing in that cave, I felt the pull of oblivion the way a tired man feels the pull of a bed—oh, to be *unmade*. No debt. No inquisitors. No name whispered in prophecy, no priestess hunting me, no masters selling me by weight. Just a hole in the world, clean-edged, painless. Every problem I had ended at the boundary of my own name. Erase the name, erase the hunt.

It would be so easy to want that honestly. To dress suicide up as sacrifice and call it heroism.

But the oracle had known that too. That's why she'd left the flaw. She'd stood exactly where I was standing—probably in exactly that spot, probably wearing that exact expression—and she'd understood that a man offered oblivion will reach for it unless someone builds him a reason to hold on. So she built one, a hundred years in advance, out of tokens and love and stubbornness.

"What are you thinking?" Brannoc asked.

"That I need to keep something." I looked down at my wrist, at the faint sigil. "When this ends—when I speak the vow backward—everything about me goes. Except what people hold onto. She says so. Objects remember better than heads."

"So give us things."

"*Give* you—"

"You owe me a favor," Brannoc said, with the ghost of a grin. "I'm calling it early. When the time comes, you'll leave me something of yours. Something that means you. And I'll hold it, and I won't forget, because I'm clan-born and we are famously terrible at letting things go." He paused. "My sister would have liked you. She'd have volunteered to hold something too."

Something in his voice on the last word made me look at him sharply, but his face had closed, the way it did whenever the subject brushed against home, so I let it lie. There would be time. There was supposed to be time.

Outside, the light through the cave mouth had gone amber, then bruised purple. We'd been in the oracle's parlor for hours and it felt like minutes—that's the trick of places that heavy; they eat clock-time and leave you the important kind.

"One more thing," I said, and turned back to the great fresco. "The second manuscript. 'Find the second hand.' She wouldn't have written that unless she believed someone could follow it. Where would a scribe hide a second copy of the most dangerous document in the mountains?"

"Not in the mountains," Brannoc said slowly. "You hide a thing where your enemies aren't looking. Clan lands, they'd search every holdfast. Temple vaults, they'd burn on principle."

"Which leaves—"

"The lowlands." He said it reluctantly, the way you'd say *plague*. "The capital."

"The capital," I agreed. "Imperial archive, imperial libraries, a hundred years of imperial clerks filing things alphabetically into obscurity. Nobody hides anything better than an empire. They misplace whole provinces."

Brannoc looked at me for a long moment. "You're talking about walking into the heart of the empire that's hunting you."

"I'm talking about the only direction nobody will expect. Everyone's watching the mountains for the Oathbearer." I picked up my lantern and watched the flame gutter and steady. "Let them watch. Scribes know how to be invisible. It's the one art the temple taught me properly."

We started back toward the cave mouth, and behind us the frescoes held their silence—all those kneeling figures, all those dissolving names, all that careful hope hidden in the margins of a curse.

And that was when Brannoc, who had stopped at the entrance to check the weather, went rigid and put a hand on my chest.

Smoke, he said. Woodsmoke, and torchlight, down in the valley below.

Too many fires for travelers. Too orderly for bandits.

Someone had found the oracle's cave after all—and they hadn't come alone, and they hadn't come to read.
