## 11. Maren's Sermon

I want it noted, for whatever record survives me, that I did not sleep through the ambush. I was awake for all of it. I simply had no part in stopping it, which is a different failure entirely.

We had made camp in a hollow below a ridge Brannoc called the Shepherd's Tooth—a spine of black rock that supposedly kept watch over the valley the way a shepherd watches sheep, except this shepherd had apparently been drinking on duty. Brannoc's wound was worse than he admitted, which is the national sport of mountain men, and he'd finally taken the fever-dreams' advice and slept. I sat up with the fire down to coals and the prophecy pages spread on my knees, reading them again by ember-light, hunting for the loophole I was certain existed because I refused to believe the universe had written a contract without one.

That's when I heard the bells.

Not real bells. Small ones—handbells, bronze, rung in a pattern I didn't know but my skin apparently did, because every hair on my arms stood up like they'd been briefed ahead of me. Then the hollow filled with torchlight from three directions at once, and figures came down the slopes with the unhurried confidence of people who have already decided how this ends.

"Brannoc," I said, and got a knife to his throat before he finished opening his eyes.

He went very still. So did I. There were perhaps twenty of them, wrapped in grey wool with iron rings at their collars, and at their center walked a woman in white so clean it looked like an accusation against everyone else's dirt.

I knew her name before she gave it. Maren. The priestess from the holdfast—the one who'd looked at my wrist-birthmark the way a jeweler looks at a stone she intends to cut.

"Scribe," she said pleasantly. "You've led us a long way."

"I've led myself a long way," I said. "You're just following."

Her smile didn't move. "The Oracle's cave. You found it. You read what was written there." She held out her hand, palm up, patient as winter. "Give me the pages."

"And if I don't?"

One of her people shifted his grip on Brannoc's hair, and the knife drew a thin red line, and I learned something about myself in that moment: I am not brave. I am a man who does arithmetic very fast under pressure. Twenty against two, one of us bleeding, the other of us holding paper.

I gave her the pages.

She took them the way you take communion—two hands, head slightly bowed—and then she did the worst thing. She thanked me. Warmly. Like I'd handed her a gift instead of handing over the only leverage I had left in the world.

"You'll be treated gently," she said. "You are the most precious thing the mountains have carried in a hundred years."

I've been called many things in my life. Forgettable. Adequate. In arrears. Never precious. It turns out being called precious feels exactly like being called cargo.

They bound my wrists—not cruelly, which was somehow more insulting—and put me on a horse I wasn't allowed to steer. Brannoc they bound separately and marched behind me where I could hear him arguing theology with anyone within earshot, mostly involving words I'd been told scribes shouldn't know. They took us north and east, up out of the valley, toward a place the clans call the Hollow of the First Snow, where the dark moon would come in nine days.

Nine days. I lay awake that first night doing sums. Nine days until they meant to make me say words I hadn't agreed to say, in front of people primed to hear them as a war drum. Nine days to find a way out of a camp of zealots with a wounded friend and no pages, no plan, and no name worth anything outside a ledger.

I'd escaped temples before. Temples have rules; rules are doors. But Maren's people didn't seem to have rules so much as convictions, and convictions are walls.

Still. I'm a smuggler's-road kind of soul. Every wall has a road. You just have to find where somebody's already been paid to look away.

* * *

Maren watched the scribe sleep—or pretend to sleep, which amounted to the same thing in her experience, since liars and dreamers wore the same slack face—and felt the old sweetness rise in her chest, the one that came whenever the Flame's design became visible in the world like bones under skin.

He was smaller than she'd imagined. That was the first thing. The prophecies spoke of bearers the way scripture speaks of kings, and here was a thin man with ink stains he couldn't fully scrub from his knuckles, curled around a fire like a cat conserving heat. She had expected to feel disappointment. Instead she felt tenderness, fierce and terrible, the tenderness of a woman holding a candle she knows must burn down to nothing.

The elders murmured around the council fire. Old Torvald wanted the vow spoken at the high altar among all the holds. Younger voices wanted it sooner, before imperial scouts found them. Maren let them argue. Argument was weather. She dealt in climate.

"The erasure," Torvald said, lowering his voice as though the word might bite. "You've read the cave writings, Maren. The bearer's name dies with the vow. Every record. Every memory. Is that not a price too great to pay without his full consent?"

Maren almost laughed. Consent. The lowlands had infected even the mountain elders with their soft vocabulary.

"He consented the moment he was born under that sigil," she said. "Torvald. Look at him." She nodded toward the sleeping form by the far fire. "No family. No hold. No debts but coin, and those will burn with the records anyway. The Flame has spent a hundred years preparing a vessel with nothing to lose, and he thinks this makes him unlucky." She folded her hands. "It makes him chosen."

"A man still feels fear."

"Yes," Maren said, and her voice went gentle, which frightened Torvald more than shouting would have. "And we will honor that fear. We will tell him everything. The erasure clause, the cost, all of it. He will walk to the speaking-stone knowing precisely what he surrenders. That is what separates sacrifice from murder, elder. Knowledge." She smiled at the fire. "The empire kills men who don't understand why. We will kill—no." She corrected herself, tasting the word. "We will *complete* a man who understands completely. There is no cruelty in it. There is only clarity."

Torvald looked at her for a long moment, and Maren recognized the look: a good man trying to find the flaw in a perfect argument and failing, because the argument had been built by someone who'd spent thirty years sanding down every rough edge of doubt.

"And after?" he asked. "When the vow is spoken and the clans rise? A war, Maren. You are asking the mountains to bleed."

"I am asking the mountains to stop bleeding slowly." Her voice hardened now, the sermon coming up through her like water through stone. "A century of tribute. A century of our children taken as hostages and returned as strangers. A century of lowland roads carved through our graves. The Quiet Flame has waited patiently, and patience has bought us nothing but smaller portions of a shrinking world. The oracle saw one door out. One. And the Flame, in its mercy, marked a man to open it—a man whose death costs the world a single forgotten name, and whose life, unspent, costs us everything."

She rose, and the council rose with her, and she walked to where the scribe lay and stood over him, watching the firelight move across his face. Even asleep—or pretending—he looked like a man keeping accounts.

"Poor creature," she thought, with a love that would have horrified him. "You believe you're being hunted. You believe this is about you. But you are the hinge, not the door. When you swing, the whole gate moves, and you will never have to see what comes through." She crouched and, in a gesture she permitted herself once per day, touched the edge of his blanket, straightening it against the cold. "We will give you a martyr's clarity and a martyr's silence, and in a hundred years the clans will speak your name—no. They won't." The correction arrived on schedule, as it always did, and she welcomed its sting. "They will speak of the Oathbearer, nameless, and the namelessness itself will be holy. You will be the only saint in history with an empty niche, and the emptiness will draw more pilgrims than any face could."

Behind her, Torvald cleared his throat. "He'll try to escape. Or talk. He's a scribe; they talk."

"He can try," Maren said, standing. "But hear me, all of you—no blade touches him. No rope. If he runs, we run after. If he talks, we listen. If he curses the Flame, we thank him for the exercise of our patience." She turned back to the sleeping camp, to the thin man dreaming his small clever dreams of loopholes. "Nothing harms the vessel before the pouring. Nothing. Whatever he asks for, within reason, he receives. Let him grow comfortable. Comfort is also a kind of consent."

She said it and believed it, wholly, the way she believed everything, and that was the most dangerous thing about her: Maren had never once in her life done evil knowingly. She had simply defined the word to fit her hands.

* * *

On the third day of captivity, they moved us into the Hollow of the First Snow proper, and I got my first real look at the speaking-stone.

It was older than the prophecy. Older, I'd have bet my outstanding debts, than the temple, maybe than the empire. A ring of grey monoliths around a flat slab, all of it worn smooth by weather and by feet—thousands of years of feet, gathered in circles like this one. Someone had built a stage for oaths before anyone alive had invented the language to swear them.

Maren's people were building scaffolding around it. Wooden platforms, banner poles, fire pits laid out in the old clan geometry. A congregation of four hundred and growing, streaming in from holds I'd only ever seen as lines on smugglers' maps. And everywhere, being stitched, painted, carved: the thirteenth-bell glyph. On shields. On tent flaps. On the foreheads of children too young to know what they were wearing.

"They're going to make it beautiful," Brannoc muttered beside me. We were allowed to sit together now, under guard, because Maren had decided visible kindness was good doctrine. His color was better; someone had actually treated his wound properly, which told me they planned to keep him alive at least until the ceremony. "Look at it. They're dressing up a war like a wedding."

"That's the craft of it," I said. "Ilvane would approve, gods rot her smiling heart. You don't sell people a war. You sell them a ceremony, and the war comes free inside."

"You have a plan, then." He said it flatly, not hopefully. He'd known me long enough to calibrate.

"I have the beginning of a plan, which in my experience is the same thing with worse odds." I nodded, very slightly, toward the supply wagons at the camp's eastern edge. "Four hundred people eat a lot of barley, Brannoc. And barley comes up the imperial roads, past checkpoints, on schedules. Somebody in this camp is a quartermaster, and quartermasters keep books."

He stared at me. "You're going to *talk* to the quartermaster."

"I'm going to *read* to the quartermaster," I said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole plan. Half these pragmatists you mentioned—the ones who came for the ceremony but haven't decided about the war—they already suspect Maren's arithmetic doesn't balance. All they need is a scribe showing them the columns." I flexed my ink-stained fingers. "I've spent ten years making myself forgettable. Turns out the skill runs both directions. Give me three conversations and a piece of charcoal, and by the dark moon, half this congregation will be wondering whether the Oathbearer is a miracle or a mark."

"And the other half?"

"The other half will be watching the first half," I said. "Which means nobody's watching me."

Brannoc snorted, then winced, then grinned despite himself. "You know what she'll do if she catches you."

"She won't catch me," I said. "And if she does—" I looked across the hollow at Maren, standing on the scaffolding in her immaculate white, directing the building of her beautiful machine, radiating a certainty I could feel from forty yards. "If she does, I'll remind her that martyrs work best unblemished. She needs me perfect, Brannoc. Perfection is a cage with very specific bars. I intend to count every one."

That night, the dark moon came one turn closer, and the bells rang vespers in their strange pattern, and four hundred voices sang the old rising-songs around fires that could be seen, I was sure, from the imperial watchtowers thirty miles south.

And I sat among them, memorizing guard rotations, wagon schedules, and the face of a tired quartermaster who looked, when he thought no one was looking, like a man doing sums he didn't like.

Everyone in that hollow believed the next nine days belonged to Maren's god.

I intended to prove otherwise—one column of numbers at a time.
