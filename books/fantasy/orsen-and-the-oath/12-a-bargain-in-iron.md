## 12. A Bargain in Iron

They tied my hands in front of me, which I took as a compliment. Behind the back is what you do with cargo; in front is what you do with a man you might still need to hold a pen.

The zealot camp sat in a bowl of grey stone below the tree line, eleven tents and a fire that smoked more than it burned because Maren's people burned wet pine out of some conviction that suffering smoke was holier than warm fire. I'd been their prisoner for two days, which was long enough to learn three things: they prayed six times daily, they fed me last and least, and of the forty-odd souls in camp, maybe a dozen actually wanted war. The rest wanted to go home.

You can do a great deal with a dozen and a rest, if you're patient. Patience was the one skill the Quiet Flame had given me for free. Everything else had come itemized.

* * *

Maren came to my tent on the second evening with two guards and a bowl of barley soup she set down just past the reach of my bound hands. She'd done this before. It wasn't cruelty for its own sake — or rather, it was, but it was *efficient* cruelty, cruelty with a lesson attached: the Oathbearer eats when the priestess wills it.

"You slept," she said. "Good. A vessel should be rested."

"I'm not a vessel," I said. "I'm a scribe. There's a difference. A vessel holds whatever's poured into it. A scribe decides what gets written down."

Her eyes narrowed. She was younger than her voice, which was a voice built for courtyards and cliff-tops, something that could carry over wind. Up close she had chapped lips and ink stains under her own fingernails, which interested me enormously. Zealots who write things down are zealots with paperwork, and paperwork can be fought.

"You spoke to Torvald today," she said. "And to the widow Sef. And to old Hesk, who should have known better."

"I told them stories about the lowlands."

"You told them the empire's supply road through Greywater Pass runs on a schedule so fixed you could set a clock by it. You told them the granary at Ford Ansel has a guard rotation with a gap at moonrise. You told them—" her voice dropped, and this was the dangerous part, the part where the sermon-voice failed her and something colder came through— "you told them the clans could win."

"Did I?"

"Don't play simple with me, scribe. I've read your prophecy. I've read it more times than you have." She crouched, bringing us level, and her breath smelled of pine smoke and fasting. "The Ember Vow, spoken at the dark moon, unbinds the old pacts. The mountain wakes. The lowland walls crack. That is what the Oracle wrote, and you are the hand chosen to speak it."

"And then what?" I asked.

She blinked. I don't think anyone had asked her *and then what* in years.

"After the walls crack," I said, "the lowlands send soldiers. Not the garrison rabble you've been raiding — the real ones, the Fourth Legion, the ones who burned Hearthmoor two generations back. I've copied their muster rolls, Maren. I've sat in a warm scriptorium transcribing quartermaster ledgers while your elders told stories around fires, and let me tell you what the ledgers say. The empire loses battles. It does not lose wars. It has never once lost a war, because it treats every battle as an installment. You'd get your one glorious morning, and then twenty years of ash."

"They tell us the same thing," she said. "Every temple, every imperial priest, every collaborator chief. *Don't fight, you'll only lose worse.* Fear is their oldest weapon."

"It would be," I said, "if I were lying. But here's the difference between me and every priest who ever told your people to sit down: I'm not telling you not to fight. I'm telling you how to fight *later*, better, from positions I can name off the top of my head because I memorized them for coin." I leaned forward as far as the rope allowed. "Greywater Pass. Ford Ansel. The salt road above Kellin's Stair. Ask your pragmatists — ask Torvald, he was a raider before he was a believer — whether any clan elder alive knows those names. They don't. I do. All of them. Every road, every cache, every rotation."

"Why would you give us that? You're their creature. Temple-made."

"I'm nobody's creature. That's rather the point of me." I sat back. "But understand what I'm offering, because it isn't treason and it isn't salvation. I'm offering you a bargain. Don't force the vow at the dark moon. Give me until the thirteenth turn — nine days. Let me finish reading what the Oracle actually wrote, all of it, including the parts your copy leaves out. If I'm wrong, if the vow is truly the clans' salvation, I'll speak it myself and gladly. But if there's another way — a way that doesn't end with Hearthmoor's ashes spread across every holdfast in Thornfell — you'll want to have heard it first."

"The parts our copy leaves out," she repeated. Her eyes did something complicated. For half a heartbeat she looked less like a prophet and more like a woman who had staked everything on a document she'd never been allowed to proofread.

"Every forgery needs a faithful copyist," I said gently. "Ask yourself why yours was written so neatly."

She stood abruptly, and the coldness came back down over her face like a shutter. "You'll eat when you've earned it."

"Maren." She paused at the tent flap. "Torvald fought at Redmere. Ask him what the retreat looked like. He'll tell you. He already wants to."

She left. The soup steamed reproachfully beyond my fingertips. I waited until the guards outside settled into their bored rhythm, then began working the rope against the tent peg behind me, slowly, patiently, the way water works stone.

I hadn't lied to her about anything except the most important thing: I didn't yet know if there *was* another way. But a bargain doesn't need to be true. It needs to be *open*. As long as they believed the thirteenth turn might offer them something better than war, they wouldn't spend me early. And doubt, once planted in a camp, grows like bindweed.

Torvald had listened to me that afternoon with his arms crossed and his jaw working. The widow Sef had asked me twice to repeat the part about the granary gap. Old Hesk had simply nodded, the way old men nod when a young person confirms something they'd suspected for decades.

A dozen believers. Thirty people who wanted to go home. And now, thirty people who knew the name of a pass they'd never heard of, and couldn't stop thinking about it.

* * *

On the third night, someone whistled.

It was a small thing, four notes, descending — a smuggler's signal I'd taught Brannoc two winters ago on the ice road above Hollowmere, when he'd been a courier with frostbitten ears and a debt to the same kind of men I ran letters for. Four notes down means *trouble, stay put.* Five notes up means *worse trouble, move anyway.*

What I heard, faint and off-key, was neither. It was seven notes, ascending, which meant nothing in the smuggler's code, which meant it was Brannoc being Brannoc: too big for stealth, too loyal for patience, improvising.

I slipped the rope — three nights of quiet work, and the peg had been rotten besides; even the gods help a man whose enemies buy cheap equipment — and eased out the back of the tent into the blue dark.

The camp was half-asleep. Two sentries walked the perimeter with the grim diligence of people who believed in their work but not their wages. Beyond the woodpile, at the edge of the firelight's reach, a shadow moved against the rocks with all the grace of a landslide.

Brannoc crouched behind a boulder the size of a shrine, holding a sling staff, looking like a bear that had been talked into burglary by smarter friends. His left arm was bound in a sling of his own making, badly. His face, when he saw me, went through relief, fury, and calculation in the space of a breath.

"You're supposed to be rescued," he whispered. "Quietly. By professionals."

"There are no professionals within fifty miles. Who's with you?"

"A horse. She's tied in the birches. She's also furious with me, so mind your fingers." He squinted at my hands, my face, the state of me. "They starve you?"

"Portion control. It's devotional." I glanced back at the camp. "Listen. Before we run — I need three days, not one."

He stared. "Kestrel. They're going to tie you to a stone at the dark moon and make you say words that start a war. We are *leaving.*"

"In three days the camp splits." I kept my voice under the wind. "Half of them believe Maren. Half of them now believe me, or at least believe her less. Torvald's been asking questions about Redmere. Hesk has been asking about the Oracle's original text. When the split comes, Maren's faction shrinks, and a shrinking faction either negotiates or does something desperate. I'd rather be here watching which, than miles away wondering."

"That's not rescue logic. That's *ledger* logic."

"I'm a scribe. It's what I have." I gripped his good arm. "Besides — you came with one arm and one bad-tempered horse. What exactly was the extraction plan?"

"...Ride fast," he admitted.

"See? You need me conscious and persuasive. Three days. If I'm wrong, I'll walk out to the sentry line myself and surrender loudly enough that you can hear it from the birches."

He ground his teeth, which I could hear, which tells you something about the size of his teeth. "Three days. Then I come back with everyone I could find, and that includes people you won't like."

"Everyone I don't like is usually useful. It's the people I like who get me killed." I squeezed his arm once. "The sister's token — you still carry it?"

His hand went to his chest without his permission. "Always."

"Then whatever happens in the next three days, that's the truest thing in this camp. More true than Maren's book. More true than mine. Remember that, if I start sounding clever."

"Clever is what got you caught."

"No," I said. "Being *important* got me caught. Clever is what's getting me out." I melted back toward my tent before the sentries turned, and heard him withdraw through the rocks, slow and heavy as a tide going out.

* * *

The split came on the fifth day, and it came over soup.

Not my soup — I'd graduated to eating with the others, a privilege Maren granted partly as policy and partly, I think, because Torvald had stopped pretending not to glare at her when I went unfed. We sat in the long fire-shelter while the rain came down outside, forty zealots and one scribe, and Hesk — old, half-deaf Hesk, who had fought in no wars and buried two sons in one — stood up with his bowl in both hands and asked, in the carrying voice of the very old, why no one had answered the scribe's question.

"What question?" said one of Maren's captains.

"The one about after." Hesk looked around the shelter, mild as milk. "The boy asks what happens after the walls crack, and everyone shouts at him, and nobody answers. I've been waiting five days for the answer. I'd like to hear it before I die, and I'm in no hurry, but still."

Silence. Rain. Maren rose from her place at the head of the shelter, and I watched her gather the sermon-voice like a woman gathering a cloak — and falter, because Hesk was not a courtyard crowd, Hesk was somebody's grandfather, and sermons slide off grandfathers like rain off slate.

"The Oracle promises victory," Maren said.

"The Oracle promised my sons victory," Hesk said, "at Redmere, according to the priests of that year. My sons are under a cairn. The priests of this year say the Oracle promises differently now." He sat down. That was all. He just sat down, and the silence he left was worth more than any speech.

Torvald stood next. "I was at Redmere," he said, and his voice was flat as a road. "I know what the retreat looked like. The scribe asked me if I did. I said yes. I'd like to know why the priestess thinks this time ends different." 

Maren looked at him. She looked at me. And I gave her the only mercy available: I didn't smile. I sat very still, hands open, a scribe awaiting dictation, and let her see that I would take her answer seriously if she ever managed to produce one.

She didn't, that night. She ordered the camp to break for the Oracle's cave at dawn — moving the ritual site, moving *me*, consolidating before the rot spread — and I lay in the dark doing arithmetic. A march to the cave was three days through country I knew. Country I knew meant routes, and routes meant choices, and choices were the one currency I'd never entirely lacked.

Near midnight, seven notes ascended through the rain, off-key, unmistakable.

Brannoc, coming back early, with whoever he'd found — and from the sound of it, several someones, none of them quiet, at least one of them arguing in clan-tongue about whether this counted as a raid or a rescue.

I sat up in the dark, flexed my freed hands, and permitted myself the first honest smile in five days.

They thought they'd captured a vessel. Tomorrow morning, Maren was going to discover she'd captured a ledger — and ledgers, unlike vessels, always balance.
