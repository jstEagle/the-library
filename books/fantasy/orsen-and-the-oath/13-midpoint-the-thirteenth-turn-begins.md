## 13. Midpoint: The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The storm came down the mountain like a debt collector.

I'd spent my whole life learning to read weather the way other men read ledgers—clouds as entries, wind as interest accruing—and what I saw building over Thornfell's western peaks that evening was a ledger about to be called in full. The kind of storm that erased roads, buried trails, and made honest people stay indoors and dishonest people very grateful for cover.

Maren's zealots had been on our trail for two days. Brannoc said they'd stop at nothing short of a rockslide. I suggested we find a rockslide and stand behind it. He did not laugh. Brannoc rarely laughed, but when he did it was like watching a door open in a wall you'd assumed was solid, and I found I missed it even before it stopped happening.

"We move tonight," he said, tightening the strap across his chest. "In the storm's teeth. They'll expect us to hole up."

"They'll expect us to die of exposure, is what they'll expect."

"Then we disappoint them twice." He shouldered his pack and looked at me with that flat grey patience of his. "You know these paths better than anyone alive, scribe. Better than Maren's trackers. Better than me, and I was born under this mountain."

"That's not a compliment, that's an accusation."

"It's both." He almost smiled. The door opened a crack. "Come on."

* * *

I want to say something noble about that night—the storm as trial, the mountain as crucible—but the truth is that most of it was misery punctuated by terror, which is to say: Tuesday, if you've lived the life I've lived.

We went up instead of down, because everyone fleeing a hunt goes down, toward the lowlands, toward roads. There was an old shepherd track above the treeline that existed mostly in my memory and partly in legend, a switchbacking scar along the shoulder of Crag Duvain that no sane person would use in clear weather, let alone in a gale that wanted to peel your skin off and wear it as a flag.

The rain came sideways. Then it came upward, which felt like a personal insult. Lightning turned the world white once every few breaths, and in those flashes I memorized the path ahead in gulps—a shelf of black stone, a cairn leaning like a drunk, a gap where the track had sloughed into nothing and we had to press ourselves against wet rock and shuffle sideways over a drop that the darkness generously hid from us.

Brannoc went first through the bad stretches. He never asked whether I could make them; he simply positioned himself so that if I fell, I would fall into him rather than past him. It wasn't tenderness. It was arithmetic. A courier who owed a life-debt does sums like that without thinking.

"Tell me again," he shouted over the wind at one point, "why we're doing this."

"Because," I shouted back, "there are three groups of people who want me, and I've decided to inconvenience all of them equally."

"You're a terrible investment."

"I'm a diversified portfolio."

He laughed then, actually laughed, one bark torn away by the wind, and I remember thinking—I remember *choosing* to think—that whatever happened next, I'd gotten that. I'd gotten the door opening.

That's the thing about thinking things. The mountain hears you, and it takes it as a challenge.

* * *

It happened at the descent, where the track dropped through a notch between two shoulders of stone into the sheltered fold where the old shrine sat. We could see the shrine below us—a squat granite structure older than either empire or clan, roofed with slabs, half-swallowed by the hillside. Refuge. Half an hour more and we'd be out of the wind.

Maren's trackers were faster than we'd estimated. Or perhaps they'd simply stopped sleeping, the way zealots do, as though sleep were a form of doubt.

I heard them before I saw them—a whistle, three notes, answered by two—and Brannoc heard it a heartbeat later, because he always heard things a heartbeat after me but understood them a heartbeat sooner. He turned. He looked up-slope. And then he looked at me, and I saw him do the arithmetic again, the same sums, but this time the numbers had changed.

"Kestrel."

"No," I said, though nothing had happened yet. "No, don't—"

"The notch. You go through. I hold it."

"There are six of them, Brannoc, there might be ten—"

"And one of me," he said, drawing his axe, "which is plenty, because they have to come single file through that gap, and I am wider than the gap." It was true. It was insultingly true. "Go down to the shrine. Barrel of oil inside, left of the altar, if it's still there. Light it if they break through."

"If they break through, lighting a fire won't—"

"*Go*, scribe."

There is a specific quality to being ordered around by someone who has decided to die for you. It isn't gratitude. Gratitude comes later, if you're lucky, when there's a later. In the moment it's just horror wearing obedience's clothes. My legs carried me down into the notch because my legs had always been better at survival than the rest of me, and behind me I heard the first clash of steel, and Brannoc roaring—not pain, not yet, just volume, the volume of a man informing six people that they had made a scheduling error.

I went through the notch. I reached the shrine. I wrenched the door—it wasn't locked; shrines never are; the gods apparently believe in open access—and stumbled inside, and then, because I am the man I am, I turned around and went back out into the storm toward the sound of fighting.

I got three steps.

The lightning came down.

Not near us. Not on the ridge. It struck the crag directly above the notch, close enough that the air itself cracked like the world's largest bell, and the whole slope let go. Stone and water and forty years of accumulated scree came down through the notch in a brown roaring wave, and I saw—one flash, one glimpse, burned into me the way routes are burned into me—Brannoc turning from his attackers, turning toward *me*, and throwing himself against the slide's edge, arms wide, holding back a slab of rock the size of a temple door with nothing but his shoulders and his stubbornness, buying me the half-second I needed to dive aside as the rest of it thundered past and buried the track, the attackers' approach, everything.

Then the world went white, then dark, then quiet except for rain.

I dug him out with my hands.

I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger, in some margin: Kestrel Nakamura, who calculated everything, who kept himself forgettable, who smuggled letters and copied prophecies and never once put his body between anything and anything else—dug through frozen mud and shattered stone with bare hands until his fingers bled, because the alternative was a ledger entry he refused to write.

I found his arm first. Then his shoulder. Then the rest of him, folded into a hollow the slab had made, alive, breathing, bleeding from a gash along his scalp and another across his ribs where the stone had kissed him on its way past.

"Brannoc. Brannoc, look at me."

One eye opened. Grey, flat, patient even now. "Told you," he rasped. "Wider than the gap."

"You absolute mule, you could have died."

"Could still," he agreed. "Help me inside? Rain's getting in my boots."

* * *

The shrine was one room, dry, cold, smelling of old smoke and older stone. Whoever had built it had worshipped something pre-empire, pre-clan, maybe pre-name; the walls held carvings worn to suggestions, figures that might have been dancers or might have been weather. There was a hearth, and wood that some long-dead keeper had stacked with the optimism of the organized, and it lit on the third try, which in my experience is the exact number of tries the universe permits before it starts charging interest.

Brannoc lay by the fire while I did what I could, which was not much. I cleaned the head wound with melted snow. I bound the ribs as tight as he'd let me, which was tighter than he pretended to tolerate. The gash along his side was deep but had missed anything I couldn't bear to think about, and I told him so, and he grunted, and that was our entire conversation about how close he'd come to dying for a man he'd known for a season.

"You should sleep," I said.

"You should stop lying to yourself," he said, which was unfair, because I hadn't lied to myself in hours.

But he slept, eventually, because bodies keep their own ledgers and his was badly overdrawn. And I sat by the fire with the Oracle of Sable Turn wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, and I took it out, and I looked at it the way you look at a letter you know contains bad news, and finally—because the storm outside was doing its best impression of fate, and because Brannoc had bled for me, and because a man who won't face a page doesn't deserve the people who bleed for his sake—I opened it to the final page.

I had avoided it. Let's be honest about that. From the first night in the archive, when I found tonight's date and my own name inked by a woman dead a century, I had read forward carefully and backward carefully and never simply turned to the end, because the end of a prophecy is where prophecies keep their teeth. Scribes know this. We bind the books; we know where the bite goes.

The final page was different from the others. The others were written in the oracle's sure hand, the confident script of a woman receiving dictation from whatever she received dictation from. This page was cramped. Hurried. The ink was blotched where her pen had pressed too hard and lingered too long, and the margins were crowded with corrections, crossings-out, a sentence begun three times and abandoned twice.

And at the top, in letters smaller than any she'd used elsewhere, she had written:

*They watch me write. Know that everything before this page may be true, and everything upon it is truest, and neither fact will save you.*

My skin did something complicated.

I read on.

*Hear me, bearer-who-comes. I have written the Vow as I was made to write it, and the Vow is real, and the Vow is cruel. But I have hidden in its lines what my watchers could not read, being men of power and therefore poor readers.*

*You were told—or you will be told, time being the liar it is—that the Ember Vow must be borne. Sealed or reignited, sealed or reignited, the mountain holds only those two doors. This is the map they gave me to copy.*

*But maps are drawn by the people who want you to travel certain roads.*

*Look at the thirteenth turn. Look at what the vow demands and when. A bearer who reaches the thirteenth turn unsworn holds not a burden but a hinge. What is sworn can be spoken backward. What is written can be unwritten. They made me hide this truth inside the lie, because a prophecy that offers no choice gathers no believers, and they needed believers. So they let me leave a door in the wall and trusted that fear would keep anyone from walking through it.*

*Fear works. I am proof. I never walked through it myself.*

*I chose the wrong thing, bearer. I wrote the war into the world because I was afraid not to. Do not be me. The choice was always yours. That is the part they never understood, and the part I paid everything to tell you:*

*The vow does not choose its bearer. The bearer chooses the vow.*

Below that, the hand deteriorated into a single scratched-out line, and beneath the scratch, pressed so hard the pen had torn the page:

*If you are reading this in a shrine on Crag Duvain in a storm — hurry. They are closer than you think, and kinder than you fear, and both facts are weapons.*

I sat there for a long time.

The fire popped. The storm threw itself against the shrine's stone roof and lost, the way storms always lose against old work honestly done. Brannoc breathed slow and even by the hearth, alive because he'd been wider than the gap.

*Both facts are weapons.* Which "they"? The temple masters who'd sold me out? The clans? The empire? All of them, probably. Everyone who'd ever handed me a map had wanted me to walk a particular road—the temple wanted a disposable vessel, Maren wanted a miracle, the empire wanted a problem dissolved, and every map agreed on one thing: that my only options were to seal the vow or ignite it, carry the fire or feed it.

And here was a dead woman, writing under duress a hundred years ago, telling me the map was forged. Telling me the doors were painted on. Telling me that the whole architecture of fate I'd been fleeing since the cracked bell was, at bottom, a clerical error—a lie of omission, hidden in plain text, waiting a century for a scribe too stubborn to stop reading at the scary parts.

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and I put my hand over my mouth, and Brannoc stirred.

"Scribe?" His voice was gravel and sleep. "What is it?"

I looked at him—at the bandages I'd tied, the blood I'd cleaned, the sister-shaped grief he carried that I'd helped carve—and I thought about what the last line meant. *They are closer than you think, and kinder than you fear.* Closer than I thought. Kinder than I feared. Both facts, weapons.

Someone was coming. Someone who would be kind. And in my experience, kindness arriving on schedule, right when a hunted man is tired and bleeding and desperate to trust someone, is the most expensive commodity in the world.

"Nothing," I said, closing the book. "Get some sleep, Brannoc. Tomorrow we're going to need to be smarter than the map."

He watched me a moment with those grey eyes that understood things a heartbeat before I said them. "Smarter than the map," he repeated. "That a scribe joke?"

"It's a eulogy," I said. "For the version of me who believed what he read."

Outside, the storm was breaking apart, and through its last ragged edges, far down the valley, I saw a light moving. Steady. Unhurried. Coming up the road toward the shrine like someone paying a visit to an old friend.

Like someone who already knew exactly where we were.
