## 14. Ilvane's Confession

I had been waiting in the shrine at Greyfall for three hours when the storm finally drove them into it, and I will confess—since confession appears to be the theme of the evening—that I enjoyed every minute of the waiting.

Not the cold. The cold was obscene. My knees have opinions about stone floors that they express in a dialect of pure spite, and by the second hour I had begun composing my own eulogy out of boredom: *Here lies Master Ilvane of the Quiet Flame, who froze to death in a ruined shrine while pursuing a scribe across half a mountain range, as one does.* But there is a particular pleasure in being early. In watching the door. In knowing that when it opens, everything inside the room will belong to you before anyone else has finished shaking off the snow.

The door opened at dusk, because Kestrel Nakamura has never once done anything at a convenient hour.

He came in first, low and sideways, the way he does everything—a man built from the habit of not being noticed, entering a room like an apology. Brannoc came after him, propped against the doorframe with an arm bound in filthy linen and a face that suggested he would fight me on principle if standing didn't cost him so much. Good. A hostile witness is a credible witness. Nothing I said tonight could sound like a trap if the big clan courier visibly hated me throughout.

They saw me. Kestrel went very still—that particular stillness of small animals and excellent liars—and his hand found the knife at his belt with the weary resignation of a man picking up a tool he'd hoped never to need again.

"Master Ilvane," he said. His voice was flat as ledger paper. "You're a long way from your desk."

"As are you, scribe. And yet here we both are, far from our desks, in a shrine to a god whose name nobody remembers." I gestured at the worn alcove behind me, where some forgotten devotion had carved a spiral into the stone and left it to the weather. "Sit down. Both of you. I'm alone—I assume you checked, you seem to be thorough lately—and I have neither soldiers nor zealots nor any interest beyond talking."

"People say that," Brannoc rumbled, "right up until they don't."

"Yes, well. People also say the clans can't be beaten in the high passes and the empire can't be beaten on the plains, and everyone involved seems determined to test both propositions this season." I let a little weariness into my voice—not performed; there was plenty in stock. "Sit. Please. You can keep your knives drawn if it makes you feel better about listening to an old woman."

Kestrel looked at Brannoc. Brannoc looked at Kestrel. There was a whole negotiation in it, silent and fast, and I confess I found myself envying them the luxury of having someone to negotiate with. Then Kestrel sat, on the far side of the fire ring, equidistant from me and the door, which told me he'd already mapped the room the way he maps everything. The boy memorizes roads. He'd have memorized exits too.

"I'll stand," Brannoc said, and stood, radiating disapproval like a stove.

* * *

"You've read the rest of it," I said to Kestrel. Not a question. "The final page."

His face did something very small and very controlled. "How would you know what's on the final page?"

"Because I put some of it there." I held up a hand before either of them could react. "No. Not the way you think. Let me tell it properly, or I'll lose you both to your assumptions, and your assumptions are currently armed."

The storm pressed against the shrine like a creditor. Somewhere above us, thunder rolled along the ridge with the patience of a thing that has all night.

"A generation ago," I began, "the Quiet Flame had a problem, and the problem was peace."

Brannoc made a sound like a rockslide clearing its throat. I raised a hand and continued.

"You look at me like that because you think temples love peace. Most do, in the way merchants love honesty—it's good for business. But the Quiet Flame in those days had been built on the border, funded by both sides of the mountain war, and when the Treaty of Ashen Ford ended the fighting, the donations stopped. Both courts decided a temple that served no strategic purpose was a temple that deserved no gold. Within five years we were eating our endowment. Within eight we were selling reliquaries." I smiled, because the truth deserves a smile now and then, just to keep it off balance. "And then a young archivist named Ilvane found a book in the sealed archive. An oracle's work. Sable Turn, she was called. And in her pages, a prophecy about an Ember Vow that could reignite the war between mountain and plain, sealed or loosed by a single bearer, at a single turning of the moon."

"And you forged it," Kestrel said quietly.

"I *edited* it." The distinction mattered to me then, and I suppose it matters to me now, though I've had thirty years to notice how thin a wall stands between those two words. "There was a genuine prophecy underneath—I want that understood, Kestrel, because you have spent these weeks believing the whole thing was ink and ambition, and I need you to stop believing that, tonight, before you do something brave and fatal. The oracle was real. Her warning was real. What I did was add to it. Details. Dates. A name left conveniently blank. A sigil description precise enough that a certain birthmark would match. I gave the clans a miracle with a schedule attached, and I sold the empire a guarantee of early warning, and the Quiet Flame ate well for another generation."

The silence had a texture. Kestrel was looking at his own wrist—the birthmark, the faint spiral he'd carried since birth without knowing its name—and I watched him arrive at the arithmetic I'd known he'd arrive at eventually. If the sigil was real, and the birthmark matched the sigil...

"The original is real," he said. "The part underneath."

"The original is worse," I said. "That's why I'm here. That's why I crossed the mountains alone at my age, which, if you're wondering, is a sentence no physician would endorse."

"Then say it." He leaned forward, and the firelight did something unkind to his face, hollowing it. "All of it. No edits."

* * *

So I said it.

"Sable Turn wrote two manuscripts. You know the first—you transcribed it, you ran from it, it has been chasing you across Thornfell ever since. The second she kept closer. It was the working copy, the one with her doubts in the margins, her corrections, the passages she struck through and rewrote. It came to the imperial archive forty years ago as part of a confiscation, mislabeled, uncatalogued. I know because I spent eleven years and most of the temple's remaining discretionary funds arranging for it to be burned."

"You burned it?" Brannoc again, sharp.

"I tried. The archive fire took perhaps half of it. The rest survived, water-damaged, in a crate that some diligent clerk moved before the flames reached it, and it has sat mislabeled ever since, waiting for exactly the sort of person who reads things he isn't supposed to." I nodded at Kestrel. "You. Naturally you."

"What's in it?"

"The truth about the price." I let the words sit a moment. "In the manuscript you copied, the Ember Vow is a weapon. Speak it, seal it, and the bearer holds the power to loose or bind the war. That much is hers, and it's true. But the second manuscript says something the first only hints at. The vow cannot be held. It can only be *spent*. Once spoken, it begins consuming its bearer—not the body. The name. Every record, every memory, every mark he has ever left on the world, unwritten, until there is nothing left to prove he existed at all. She watched the last Oathbearer vanish mid-sentence, in front of witnesses who could not afterward agree on whether anyone had been standing there. She called it the kindest cruelty the gods ever devised, and she wrote that she could not decide whether she meant it as praise."

Kestrel had gone white. Not the dramatic pallor of a man hearing terrible news—the other kind, the slow drain, the color of a man doing sums he'd already feared.

"That's why the prophecy names a scribe," he said. His voice came out level, which cost him; I could see the cost. "Someone used to being overlooked. Someone whose disappearance wouldn't—" He stopped.

"Wouldn't matter," I said, gently, because cruelty done gently is still cruelty but it goes down easier. "Yes. I chose the blank name in my forgery carefully, and thirty years later the archive handed me a debt-bound copyist with an oracle's sigil on his wrist and no family to ask questions. I did not make you the bearer, Kestrel. The birthmark did. But I certainly did not stand in the way of it."

Brannoc shifted, and I heard the creak of leather and the specific silence of a large man deciding whether violence would improve anything. "You sold him," he said. "Your own novice. To a prophecy."

"I offered him to a prophecy, and I fed him, clothed him, taught him letters, and forgave a portion of his debt each year he survived, which is more than the empire offers its soldiers and considerably more than the clans offer their orphans." I spread my hands. "Judge me. I insist upon it. But judge me *after*, because there is one more piece, and it is the reason I'm sitting on a cold floor instead of warming my hands in the scriptorium."

Kestrel hadn't moved. "The forged parts," he said slowly. "If the vow is unmade publicly—if the bearer speaks it backward, unravels it—what happens to your additions?"

"Ah." I closed my eyes briefly. There it was. "You see the problem."

"If he unmakes it, the forgery comes apart too. Every forged line named, every lie exposed. Your temple falls. *You* fall."

"If he unmakes it, yes. The unmaking requires the truth to be spoken—all of it, the real and the false distinguished, in public, at the thirteenth turn. That's in the second manuscript too. The vow is an oath, and oaths are undone the way knots are: by showing every strand. Which means my edits, exposed. Which means the Quiet Flame's thirty years of careful stewardship revealed as fraud. Which means—" I opened my eyes and looked at him straight, because this part deserved his actual face and not my performance of it. "Which means I am asking you not to do the one thing that would save the world and ruin me. I am asking you to complete the vow instead. Bear it. Spend it. Let your name be eaten, and mine stay intact, and the forgery buried under the weight of a genuine miracle, and the war die unborn, and everyone live happily except you."

The fire cracked. Outside, the storm leaned harder against the walls.

"And if I refuse?" Kestrel asked. "If I walk out of here and do it your way—the public way, the true way?"

I sighed, and for once did not bother making the sigh elegant.

"Then I report your location to Captain Voss within the hour, and to Maren's people by morning, and you will spend whatever remains of your life running between two armies who both want to use your mouth for their own words. I don't relish it. I've watched you since you were fourteen, scribe. You were the best forger of marginalia I ever taught, and the only student I ever had who corrected *my* handwriting errors in the margins of *my* ledgers, thinking I wouldn't notice." Something in my chest did a thing I declined to examine. "I would rather not feed you to the machine I helped build. But I built it, and I will operate it, because the alternative is a war that burns both the mountains and the plain, and I am too old and too guilty to pretend I care more about my soul than about ten thousand dead."

"You could confess," Brannoc said. Flat. Simple. The way large honest men say devastating things. "Stand up at their turning-of-the-moon. Tell them yourself. Take the fall."

"And the clans hear 'the prophecy was partly forged' and conclude the *whole* thing was forged, and stop preparing for a war that is genuinely coming, and the genuine prophecy arrives on schedule anyway, and no bearer has been prepared, and no vow is spoken, and Sable Turn's real warning dies in the noise of a scandal." I shook my head. "The truth needs a vessel, courier. It always has. That's the dirty secret of every religion and every archive: truth doesn't survive on its own. It needs someone to carry it, and carriers get broken. I chose a carrier who could be broken cheaply. I have regretted it precisely as much as was consistent with continuing to function."

Kestrel stood. Slowly. He looked down at me with an expression I couldn't fully read—there was hatred in it, certainly, but layered under the hatred was something worse, something almost like recognition, one professional appraising another.

"You're giving me a choice," he said. "You actually mean to give me until morning."

"I mean to give you until dawn, and a hot meal, because I brought one and my knees refuse to climb back down the pass tonight regardless of what you decide. Villainy keeps regular hours, scribe. Even mine."

He almost smiled. Almost. "And if we're gone before dawn?"

"Then I keep my word, and the hounds come, and we all find out what we're made of." I settled back against the cold stone and closed my eyes, the picture of an old woman surrendering to exhaustion, which required no picture at all. "But you won't leave before dawn. You'll want to search my saddlebags for proof of the second manuscript, and you'll find the map to the imperial archive crate in the false bottom of the food tin, because I put it there an hour before you arrived, because a confession without evidence is just a story, and you, of all people, know the difference."

Silence, then the soft sounds of him moving toward my bags, and Brannoc's growled objection, and the tin scraping open.

I lay in the dark of the forgotten shrine and listened to a dead oracle's map change hands, and I thought about names being eaten, and about which of us, in the end, had less time left than the other—and somewhere below the ridge, faint through the storm, I heard a bell crack tolling thirteen.
