## 15. The Lowland Road

The capital smelled like money that had been left out too long.

I'd been in cities before—Thornfell's market towns, the river port at Callow's Bend—but Ashfall was a different animal entirely. It didn't sprawl so much as accumulate, layer upon layer of ambition and ash, which was apparently where the name came from: some old imperial habit of burning things and building on top of them. The archive district sat in the oldest quarter, which meant it stood on the ashes of whatever archive had come before it. I found this darkly funny. Nobody else would.

I came down off the mountains with frostbitten ears, Brannoc's cloak around my shoulders because he'd refused to take no for an answer, and exactly eleven coppers to my name. Eleven coppers buys you a week of bread ends and a spot under a drying shed if you don't mind sharing it with tanners. I did not mind. I had spent three years being forgettable in a temple; being forgettable in a city is just the same work with worse incense.

Here is what nobody tells you about walking away from two armies and a temple: they let you. Not because they've given up—because you're more useful lost than caught. Maren wanted me found at the right moment, at the dark moon, in front of her faithful. Ilvane wanted me found by *her*, gently, somewhere without witnesses. And Voss wanted me found at all, which made her the most dangerous of the three, because she was the only one whose interest in me was professional.

So I did the one thing none of them expected.

I walked into the lion's mouth and started reading.

* * *

The Imperial Archive of Ashfall does not admit the public. It admits scholars with letters of introduction, clerks with imperial seals, and—this is the loophole I loved—copyists hired for piecework when the archivists fall behind. And the archivists of Ashfall were always falling behind, because the empire generates paperwork the way a wound generates pus: constantly, painfully, and as proof that something underneath is still alive.

I forged a letter of introduction on my second day. It took me four hours, and I want you to understand that this was not forgery in any meaningful legal sense—it was *transcription*. I copied the format of a temple credential from memory, adjusted the seal to the lowland chancery style I'd seen on documents passing through Thornfell, and wrote myself a modest recommendation as a journeyman scribe named Corren Vale. The name was important. You don't walk into the empire's memory wearing your own name when your own name is currently the most dangerous word in two kingdoms.

They hired me within the hour. The pay was terrible. The access was everything.

For nine days I worked in the outer stacks, copying customs ledgers and land grants, and every evening I stayed late—legitimately late, the kind of late that earns you a reputation for diligence—and taught myself the archive's cataloguing system. It was beautiful, actually. Whoever designed it a century ago had a mind like a well-kept road: every route marked, every dead end signposted. Including, if you knew how to read the marginalia, the routes that had been deliberately closed.

That was how I found the gap.

The Oracle of Sable Turn—the book I'd transcribed in Thornfell, the book that had ruined my life in elegant verse—was listed in a catalogue from eighty years past as part of a *collected works*. Two volumes. The second volume was cross-referenced to a shelf mark in the restricted vaults, and then, in a later hand, struck through. No note. No transfer record. Just a line through ink, the archival equivalent of a body buried in the garden.

Books don't vanish from the Imperial Archive. They get moved, misfiled, or burned—and burned ones get a destruction certificate, because even arson requires paperwork here. There was no certificate. Which meant the second manuscript hadn't been destroyed.

It had been *disappeared*. And there was exactly one person with the authority to disappear a manuscript quietly, decades ago, from a mountain temple with reach into the capital.

Master Ilvane had gotten there first. Or her predecessor had, and she'd inherited the crime along with the smile.

Either way: the manuscript existed. Somewhere in these stacks, or somewhere these stacks fed into, there was a second book written by a woman who had seen the end of the last war coming—and if the first book could name me a hundred years before my birth, the second might name a way out.

I want to be honest about why I believed this, because it wasn't logic. Logic said the oracle was probably a fraud, the prophecy a tool, and I a fool chasing comfort through a burning house. But I had read her. Under all the formal verse, under the parts that had clearly been shaped by whoever commissioned her, there was a voice—a dry, tired, stubborn voice that kept slipping in little annotations, like a woman writing in the margins of her own captivity. *"This passage was dictated. What follows is mine."* She had fought, inside the prophecy, to leave something true.

I was betting my life that she'd won.

* * *

On the eleventh night, I found the vault ledger.

It was in the archivist's private office, which I entered through a window I'd spent three evenings learning the patrol schedule around, because some habits from Thornfell don't die, they just change employers. The ledger recorded every item removed from the restricted vaults in the last sixty years. Most entries were dull. One was not:

*Item 4471. Collected works of the Sable Oracle, vol. II. Removed by warrant of the Office of Inquisition, spring census year 41. Disposition: sealed evidence, case unresolved.*

Sealed evidence. Case unresolved. Forty years of *unresolved*.

I stood in that office holding a lamp and felt something I hadn't felt since the bell cracked outside my window: vertigo, but the good kind, the kind that comes when the floor turns out to be a door. The inquisition had taken the manuscript. Not the temple. Which meant it wasn't hidden in some Ilvane-controlled cache—it was sitting in an evidence vault in the inquisitors' own fortress, waiting for a case that had never been closed.

All I had to do was break into the most secure building in the empire.

I put the ledger back exactly as I'd found it, blew out the lamp, and allowed myself one long breath of pure, undiluted despair. Then I started planning, because despair is a fine meal but a poor diet, and I have always been better at problems than feelings.

That was when the door opened.

* * *

Captain Sera Voss had learned three things about Kestrel Nakamura since Thornfell, and all three of them annoyed her.

First: he was smarter than his file suggested, and his file had already suggested quite a lot. Second: he was kinder than was strategically wise—he'd carried a dying clan girl's token out of a burning holdfast rather than save the minutes, and Voss had read enough after-action reports to know what those minutes cost him. Third: he was predictable, in the way that honest people are predictable, and she had used that predictability to track him across half a kingdom while feeling increasingly like she was hunting a deer to interrogate it about the weather.

The fourth thing she learned on the eleventh night, standing in the doorway of Archivist Pellam's office with her hand on her sword and her warrant in her pocket, was that he didn't run.

He was just standing there in the dark beside a dead lamp, holding very still, the way small animals do when they've calculated that flight is worse. His eyes went to the window behind him—measuring, always measuring—and then, visibly, deliberately, back to her.

"Captain Voss," he said. "You're between patrols."

"You know my patrol schedule." She kept her voice flat. "That's a confession, incidentally."

"It's an observation. Confessions require intent." He tilted his head. "You're alone. No squad, no irons ready. You watched me climb in and waited until I found what I was looking for. That's not an arrest, Captain. That's an ambush without a purpose. So either you're here to rob the archivist—which seems beneath you—or you want to talk."

Voss had planned a dozen openings for this conversation. He'd skipped past all of them. She found, to her considerable irritation, that she respected it.

"Sit down," she said, and indicated the archivist's chair.

"I'd rather stand near the window."

"Fine. Stand near the window. Why are you breaking into my archive?"

"*Your* archive?"

"The inquisition holds the warrant over restricted materials. Everything in this building is ultimately mine to answer for." She let that settle. "Including, I'm told, a certain second manuscript."

Something changed in his face—not fear, exactly. Recalculation. "You know about the Oracle's collected works."

"I know a century-old prophecy named a temple scribe as the bearer of an oath that starts wars. I know my superiors want that scribe in chains before the thirteenth turn. And I know—" she paused, choosing the words with care, because the next part was either treason or sanity and she genuinely could no longer tell the difference— "that the last three orders I received regarding you made less sense each time. So I pulled the old files. Quietly."

"And?"

"And the file on the Sable Oracle is a shell. Surface pages, summary only. The substantive material was removed forty-one years ago by a warrant signed by an inquisitor who died conveniently soon after. The case was never closed because closing it would require someone to read what was taken." She folded her arms. "Someone buried a live question in my order's basement, Master Nakamura, and everyone who might remember why has been promoted, retired, or killed. I don't like unexplained holes. Holes are how institutions lie."

Kestrel laughed. It was a short, disbelieving sound, gone almost before it arrived. "You're telling me the inquisition has a ghost in its cellar and you can't sleep for wondering about it."

"I'm telling you," Voss said, "that I spent six days deciding whether to arrest you or ask you a question, and I've decided to do both, in whichever order you prefer."

He looked at her for a long moment. Lamplight from the corridor caught the edge of his face—the exhaustion there, the frostburn scars, the eyes of a man who had done arithmetic on his own survival every day for a season and was tired of the sum always coming out the same.

"Question first," he said. "Then we'll see about the arrest."

"Why haven't you claimed the vow?" She stepped closer. "You've had a month, safe houses, clan zealots begging to crown you. Every road you took led away from power. A man running from arrest hides. A man running from *destiny* goes to ground in the enemy's library. So tell me what you're looking for, and tell me why I should believe it isn't a weapon."

"It's the opposite of a weapon." He said it simply, without performance, and that was what convinced her—she'd heard zealots perform sincerity until it shone. "The prophecy I read names me the Ember Vow's bearer. Fulfill it, and the clans rise; the vow burns hot enough to make war feel holy. Destroy it, and maybe nothing happens—or maybe it comes back worse, because oaths like that don't die, they wait. But the oracle wrote two volumes, Captain. Everyone's been fighting over the first one. The second one was taken by your office and never read by anyone living." He paused. "I believe the second volume tells you how to *unmake* the vow. Publicly. Permanently. At a price. Because she was that kind of writer—she never gave anything away free."

"What price?"

"If I knew, I'd be sleeping better."

Voss studied him. Here was the thing she had not put in any report, the thing that had been growing in her like rot in a beam since Thornfell: she had served the law her whole life because she believed the law was the shape justice takes when it grows up. Lately she kept catching glimpses of something else wearing the law's clothes. A temple master who smiled too readily. Orders that wanted the scribe silenced rather than questioned. A prophecy that fit its purposes a little too neatly, like testimony rehearsed by a witness who'd been coached.

And now this man, standing in a dark office he'd broken into with more skill than any mere scribe should possess, offering her the truth unprompted because it was the fastest route to what he wanted.

"If the second volume says what you think," she said slowly, "then fulfilling it or destroying it are both wrong answers, and everyone hunting you is fighting over the wrong prize."

"That's been my working theory, yes."

"And if it doesn't? If it's blank, or burned, or says exactly what the first book says?"

"Then I'll have spent a month of my life finding out I was a fool with extra steps." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something—she saw it land in his shoulders. "But Captain, consider your position. If I'm wrong, you arrest me tonight and deliver me to people whose orders you already distrust. If I'm right, you're the only officer in the empire who knows the vow can be ended instead of used. I know which version of you I'd rather exist."

Flattery, Voss thought. Except it wasn't; it was worse. It was an accurate description of her actual dilemma, delivered without warmth, by a man who had correctly identified that her loyalty to her order was now conditional.

She hated him a little. It was becoming a familiar sensation.

"The evidence vaults are under the fortress of the Ninth Circle," she said. "Warded, sealed, and inventoried by people who report directly to the Lord Inquisitor. Even I can't walk in and browse."

"No," Kestrel agreed. "But you can open a case."

She blinked. "What?"

"Case number whatever-it-is, forty-one years unresolved. Unresolved cases get reviewed when new evidence surfaces." He tapped his own chest, once. "New evidence just surfaced. The Oathbearer walked into your archive and tried to steal state secrets. Arrest me, Captain. Formally. File the report. And when they ask what prompted the capture, the truthful answer is: he was searching for the missing second manuscript of the Sable Oracle—which means that manuscript is now material evidence in an active investigation, and *you* are the investigating officer with lawful access to every page."

Voss stared at him.

It was elegant. It was obscene. It turned her warrant into a library card and her prisoner into a co-conspirator, and it required her to write down, in official language, the exact thing her superiors had buried—because once it was in the file, it existed, and files in the inquisition were the closest thing the empire had to immortal.

"They'll read the report," she said.

"Yes."

"They'll know what I know."

"Yes. And they'll have to decide whether to destroy the manuscript properly this time—with a certificate, with witnesses—or let their own investigating officer see it. Either way, Captain, it stops being a ghost. Ghosts haunt. Evidence gets *handled*." He almost smiled. "Sunlight through paperwork. It's the only kind I can afford."

Voss stood in the dark office of a minor archivist and understood that she had just crossed a line she couldn't find again if she walked backward. She thought of the shell file. Of the convenient death forty years ago. Of the cracked bell tolling thirteen, and the look on her own commander's face when she'd reported it—an expression she had filed away as *concern* and now recognized, with cold clarity, as *recalculation*.

"Give me your hands," she said.

Kestrel held them out, wrists together, without a flicker of fear. As she bound them—loosely, professionally, the knots correct but merciful—he looked up at her.

"For the record," he said, "I'd have preferred the window."

"For the record," Voss replied, "so would I. Walk."

And as she marched her prisoner out through the sleeping archive, past the shelves that held the empire's whole remembered life, she found herself thinking about the second volume—sealed in iron below the Ninth Circle, unread for forty years, waiting with the patience of all written things for a hand to turn its page.

By morning, the report would be filed. By evening, she suspected, certain people in high places would be very interested in a captain who had asked one question too many.

Good, she thought, and tightened her grip on the prisoner's arm—not to restrain him, but because it was the nearest thing to an alliance she could offer in front of the night guard.

Let them come asking. She had questions of her own.
