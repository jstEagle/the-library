## 17. Zealots at the Gate

### Kestrel

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a city decides to burn. I had heard it once before, in a border town whose name I've since been careful to forget: dogs gone quiet, shutters drawn, and the whole street holding its breath like a debtor pretending not to be home.

The old quarter of the capital was breathing like that now.

I stood at the shuttered window of Voss's safehouse—a borrowed clerk's flat above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and old fear—and watched Maren's people arrive. They came down the hill road in no formation at all, which was somehow worse than formation. Clansfolk don't march; they *flow*, gray-cloaked and iron-shod, filling the streets the way water fills a boot. At their center rode Maren herself on a white mare, her zealot's brand bare to the sky, and even at this distance I could see she wasn't looking at the walls.

She was looking at the bell tower. The ruined one. Our tower.

"They think you're in the garrison cells," Voss said behind me. She'd said it three times now, each time as if testing whether repetition could make it true. "That's what my own reports say. That's what *I* filed."

"And we both know how long your paperwork holds against a priestess with an army and a grudge."

Voss didn't answer. She was doing something with her sword belt that was less about the sword and more about having something to do with her hands. I'd noticed that about her over the weeks—Captain Sera Voss, terror of smugglers and bane of my former life, fidgeted like a novitiate when the law ran out of words for her.

Below, a horn sounded from the gate. Not Maren's horn—an imperial one, thin and official, the sound of a man reading from a list because he cannot improvise. Then another horn answered it, deeper, rougher, and the crowd at the gate shifted like a body turning in sleep.

"That's not hers," I said.

"No," Voss said, and something crossed her face that I'd never seen there before. Hope, maybe, or its close cousin, dread. "That's a clan war-horn. But the wrong clan."

I knew before she said it. You spend enough years memorizing roads and you learn the sounds that travel them, and I knew that horn the way you know a voice through a wall.

"Brannoc," I said.

* * *

### Voss

My oath has always been a simple thing, which is why I loved it. Protect the empire. Uphold the writ. Ask questions later only if the paperwork permits.

Standing at that window, watching two armies assemble outside a gate I was sworn to hold, I understood for the first time that my oath had been used as a lever, and I was the stone it was meant to move.

Ilvane's warrant sat in my breast pocket. Ilvane's forged prophecy sat in my head, next to the burned fragments of the true one, and between those two documents lay everything I had believed about my career, my order, and the clean bright line between traitor and servant.

"You should go," Nakamura said. He had a way of saying things that made them sound like bookkeeping. "Out the back, down the chandler's stair, into the tanner's row. Nobody follows a scribe into tanner's row. Even zealots have noses."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"You're under orders to arrest me. Those orders are still technically valid." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who had spent his whole life being forgettable and had finally, luxuriously, stopped. "You could still be on the right side of this, Captain. All you have to do is hand me over and let a priestess start a war on the steps of your own garrison. Very tidy. There'd probably be a commendation."

"I don't want a commendation."

"No," he said quietly. "You want the truth to have consequences. I know. It's why I trust you, which frightens me more than Maren does."

The horn sounded again, closer, and then came the thing I will remember on my deathbed if I'm permitted a deathbed: the crowd at the gate *parted*. Maren's flow broke around something moving through it—not forcing, not fighting, just arriving with the terrible confidence of a man who owes everyone favors and has decided to collect them all at once.

Brannoc of the Ashvale clans walked his sister's road into a hostile square with perhaps forty pragmatists at his back, half of them limping, all of them armed, and planted himself in front of the imperial gate like a fence post that had opinions.

He was supposed to be dying in a shrine two hundred miles north. Instead he was here, half-healed, gray-faced, leaning on a spear he clearly intended to use mostly as a walking stick, and shouting up at the walls in trade-tongue so the whole quarter could hear him:

"MAREN OF THE SABLE TURN! YOU SAY THE OATHBEARER IS IN THESE CELLS! FINE! OPEN THE GATE AND LET US ALL LOOK TOGETHER! OR ADMIT YOU'D RATHER HAVE A WAR THAN AN ANSWER!"

Maren's answer was an arrow.

It missed him by a hand's width—which told me everything, because Maren's archers did not miss by accident. A warning shot. A message: *join me or be next.*

Brannoc didn't flinch. He turned slowly, deliberately, to face his own countrymen in the crowd—the pragmatists scattered among the zealots, the ones who had come for a miracle and were starting to smell smoke instead—and I watched forty years of clan politics happen in the space of ten seconds.

Then the gate horns screamed, and someone below gave the order I had given no one permission to give.

The gate began to open.

* * *

### Kestrel

Here is what nobody tells you about watching your own escape being arranged without your consent: it feels exactly like being sold, except this time the price is being paid in the right direction.

"The gate's opening," I said. "Who ordered that?"

"I did," Voss said. "Twenty minutes ago. Before either of us knew Brannoc was coming." She met my eyes, and her jaw was set in the shape of a woman who had already argued herself across a line and was now simply describing the far side of it. "Officially, a prisoner escaped during the confusion of the siege. Officially, I pursued and lost him in the tanner's row. Officially, Captain Sera Voss failed, and will be writing a very lengthy report about it."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, the bearer of the Ember Vow is going to walk out of this building, through my garrison, past my soldiers—who have been paid, promoted, or persuaded to see nothing—and out a postern gate into a square full of people who want him dead, so that he can stand in a ruined bell tower in four days' time and unmake a lie that my entire order helped build." She exhaled. "And then I arrest the woman who gave me my career, using a warrant I forged the authority to issue, and I spend the rest of my life explaining myself to auditors."

When she put it that way it sounded almost reasonable, which I suspect was the point. Voss had learned my trick: make the monstrous thing sound like clerical work, and people will sign anything.

"Why?" I asked anyway. Not the tactical why. The other one.

She looked out the window at Brannoc, standing alone in the widening space between two armies, and her voice came out lower than I'd ever heard it.

"Because eight years ago I executed a boy of nineteen for carrying a letter. A real crime, by the writ. I did my duty and slept fine, because duty is a very good bed." Her hand found the warrant in her pocket, the false one, the useful one. "Last month I learned the letter was temple business. The boy died keeping Ilvane's forgery warm. So no, scribe—I am not choosing between my oath and the truth. I've finally figured out they were never on the same side. Everything else is just paperwork."

Somewhere below, iron groaned against iron, and the gate of the imperial garrison swung wide enough to let out its prisoner.

Me.

* * *

They dressed me as a garrison clerk, which was almost too easy; I'd been dressing as forgettable men my whole life. We went down through the barracks, past soldiers who studied the ceiling with tremendous discipline, and Voss walked beside me with her captain's stride, daring anyone with her posture alone to ask why a prisoner was being escorted toward the postern rather than the cells.

At the postern door she stopped.

"There's a courier waiting beyond the wall," she said. "Ashvale colors. He'll take you to Brannoc. After that—" she paused, and for a moment the captain fell away and there was just a tired woman in armor who had run out of bedrock to stand on, "—after that I can't follow you. Whatever happens at the tower, I have to be visibly doing my job when it happens. It's the only cover any of us will have left."

"Voss." I dug in my sleeve and pressed the folded page into her hand—the draft I'd been working on nights, the unmaking rite copied fair, every forged line named, every true danger stated, in my best scribe's hand. "If I don't live to read it aloud, this does. Get it into the record. Any record. Records are how the empire remembers, and remembering is the whole war."

She took it the way people take relics. Then, because she was still Voss, she ruined the moment on purpose: "Your handwriting is atrocious."

"It's beautiful and you know it."

"It's legible, which for a fugitive is showing off." She shoved the postern bar aside. Gray light and cold air and the noise of two armies poured in. "Go. And scribe—if you die out there, I want you to know the report I write about your escape will be *scathing*. Nothing personal. Just standards."

I stepped through, and the door closed behind me, and for the first time since a cracked bell rang outside my window, no one was chasing me.

Everyone, of course, was waiting.

* * *

The square had gone quiet in the way crowds do when they're deciding something. Maren sat her white mare twenty paces off, and her eyes found me the instant I crossed open ground—found me, and *recognized* me, and I watched the recognition travel through her zealots like fire through dry grass. Murmurs. Pointing. Someone dropped to their knees, which would have been flattering if kneeling crowds had ever once meant anything good for the person knelt to.

Brannoc got there first. He always did.

"You look terrible," he said, leaning on his spear, gray as old ash but grinning like the mountain in spring.

"You're supposed to be dead in a shrine."

"I'm a bad patient." His grin faded, and he nodded back at the pragmatists behind him—at the faces I recognized from his holdfast, from the raid, from the snow where his sister had bought my life with hers. "They came to hear it from you. Not from me. Not from Maren. From the man the prophecy named." He swallowed. "Kestrel. Four days. Thirteenth turn. Are you certain? Truly certain? Because if you stand in that tower and the vow doesn't break—"

"Then Maren gets her war and gets to say she warned everyone." I looked past him at the crowd, at Maren, at the ruined tower standing black against the sky like a finger pointing at nothing. "But it will break. The second manuscript says how. The price says when. And the price—" my voice caught, just slightly, on the part I hadn't told even Brannoc, "—the price is mine to pay, and I intend to pay it in full, publicly, where every clan and every inquisitor can watch the receipt."

Behind us, Maren's voice rose over the square, sweet and terrible, preaching the oath, preaching glory, preaching the thirteenth turn as a coronation.

And Brannoc turned to face her, lifted his sister's token high where both crowds could see it, and called out in a voice that cracked down the middle and kept going anyway:

"WE CAME FOR AN ANSWER! LET HIM SPEAK!"

The square held its breath.

Four days, I thought. Four days until the moon's last shadow, until the tower, until I traded my name for everyone else's future.

Above the gate, small and sharp against the gray sky, a figure in a captain's cloak watched from the wall—and raised one hand, briefly, in something that was not quite a salute and not quite a farewell.

Then the bells of the capital began to ring vespers, and every soul in the square flinched at the sound, and I started counting the days.
