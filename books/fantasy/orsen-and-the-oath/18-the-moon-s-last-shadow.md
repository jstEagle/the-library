## 18. The Moon's Last Shadow

The bell tower had no roof, which meant the moon had nowhere to hide from me, and I had nowhere to hide from the moon. Fair trade. Tomorrow night it would hang over this ruin full of people—clansfolk with iron in their fists, inquisitors with warrants in their pockets, temple masters with lies stitched into their hems—and I would stand beneath it and unmake myself.

Tonight it was just me, a candle stub, a plank across two barrels for a desk, and eleven sheets of good paper. Twelve, if you counted the one I'd already ruined by writing "Dear everyone" at the top and then staring at it until the wax pooled.

A scribe learns early that the hardest part of any document is not the words. It's deciding who the words are *for*. A ledger is easy: it is for the person who will audit you. A letter is easy: it is for the person who will weep or rage or pay. But a confession that has to survive being read aloud to three factions who all want you dead or useful—that is a different discipline entirely. That is architecture. Every sentence a load-bearing wall. One weak joint and the whole thing collapses into "the ramblings of a frightened clerk," which is exactly how Ilvane would spin it if I gave her an inch.

So I did what I always do when the work frightens me. I made it small. Line by line. Ink under the fingernails.

I started with the forgery, because forgery was something I could prove, and proof is a comfort to a man who has spent a year being told he is a symbol.

*Item the first.* The Oracle of Sable Turn, as held in the sealed archive of the Order of the Quiet Flame, contains forty-one lines added after the oracle's death. I knew this because I had spent three nights in Ilvane's private scriptorium with the original quires and the temple's own colophon records, and because Voss had put her inquisitor's seal on my findings, which meant they were now legally true whether or not they were factually true. There is a difference. Empires run on it.

The forged lines were elegant work—I'll grant the dead masters that much. They'd taken a genuine prophecy about a vow that could be *unmade* and rewritten it as a prophecy about a vow that must be *kept*, then salted the margins with clan sigils so the mountain folk would read their own war-drums in it. A century ago some tired master with ink-stained cuffs had decided that a controlled war was safer than an honest peace, and had set his best scribes to editing fate.

I know the type. I have been the type. I once altered a grain tally by two sacks to keep a widow's debt from compounding, and I slept fine. The distance between two sacks and forty-one lines of destiny is shorter than anyone wants to admit. It's just a matter of how many people are standing downstream when the water moves.

*Item the second.* What remained true underneath. This was harder, because the truth was worse than the lie, and I had to decide how much of it to say aloud to a crowd holding weapons.

Here is what the original oracle actually wrote, in her own cramped hand, before someone with better penmanship got to her pages: there *is* an Ember Vow. It is real. It is old. And it does exactly what the frescoes in her cave showed—it binds the bearer's name out of the world. Not death. Erasure. Every record blurred, every memory slackened, every face that ever knew you forgetting why your face mattered. The clans' zealots thought this was martyrdom. Maren had preached it as holy: the Oathbearer ascending beyond name.

The oracle's last page—the one I'd avoided reading until Brannoc was bleeding out beside me in a roadside shrine—said otherwise. She'd written it under duress; you can see it in the pressure of the strokes, the way the letters lean like people leaning against a door. And what she'd managed to smuggle past her captors, hidden in a colophon flourish only another scribe would catch, was this: *the vow may be spoken backward, publicly, at the thirteenth turn, by the bearer freely choosing—but the price does not forgive. The price is the point.*

You cannot unmake an oath without paying what the oath cost. The oath cost a name. So the unmaking costs a name.

Mine.

I dipped the quill and wrote it plainly, because plainness was the only armor I had left:

*I, Kestrel Nakamura, scribe of the Quiet Flame, debtor of the same, bearer of the Ember Vow by blood and by ink, do hereby speak the vow backward before witnesses, on the thirteenth turn of the moon, freely and without coercion, accepting its price entire.*

Then I sat back and looked at my own name sitting there on the page like a man waiting to be arrested, and I laughed, briefly, at the symmetry. Ten years I had worked to be forgettable. Careful, quiet, head-down Kestrel, forgettable Kestrel, the scribe whose face slid off memory like rain off slate. I had built a whole life out of being nobody, and now the universe itself had agreed to help, permanently, and all it took was a war nearly starting first.

There's a joke in there. I couldn't find it. Maybe tomorrow the crowd will.

* * *

Brannoc found me near midnight. He didn't announce himself—he never did, a habit from courier years—but I heard him testing the stair before I saw him, weight going to the sound side, favoring the wound Maren's people had given him in the storm. Half-healed and climbing anyway. Stubbornness is the clan religion nobody admits to practicing.

"You're writing at midnight," he said, lowering himself onto a fallen lintel with the care of a man negotiating with his own ribs. "In a roofless tower. In winter."

"The acoustics are excellent."

"For what?"

"Dramatic sighing." I turned the ruined sheet toward him. "Also drafts."

He looked at the page for a long time. Firelight moved on his face and did nothing to soften it; Brannoc's face doesn't soften, it just occasionally stops being granite. "That's tomorrow," he said. "That's the actual thing."

"That's the actual thing."

"And after it—" He stopped. He knew. Everyone who'd read the cave frescoes knew. He said it anyway, because Brannoc is the kind of man who makes you say things out loud so you can't pretend they're not real. "After it, no one remembers you."

"Not no one." I tapped the stack of finished sheets. "Paper remembers. Paper's loyal that way. Voss keeps copies under imperial seal. Somewhere in a lowland archive there will forever be a file that says Kestrel Nakamura existed, owed money to a temple, and died of bureaucratic causes. Very flattering. Very cold."

"That's not the same."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

He was quiet. Down in the old quarter, someone's cookfire smoked, and the smell of it climbed the broken stairs to us—woodsmoke and onions, the smell of ordinary people eating ordinary dinners on the last ordinary night they'd get. I found I was memorizing it. Old habit. New purpose.

"I need to ask you something," I said, "and I need you to say yes before you hear all of it, because if you hear all of it first you'll argue, and you argue like a landslide."

"That's true," he agreed.

"Serra. Your sister."

The name landed between us the way it always did—like a stone dropped down a well that never quite reaches bottom. Senna? No—Serra. Serra, who had stood in a doorway in a holdfast in the snow and bought me thirty seconds with her life. Serra, whose token Brannoc carried on a cord around his neck, a carved knot-bead she'd made at nine years old because she was bored during a sermon.

"She died because of me," I said. "Not because of the prophecy. Because I ran, and she stood where I'd been. And tomorrow, when the vow takes my name, it takes that too. Nobody will remember why she died, or who she died for, or that there was ever a skinny temple scribe who owed her everything. The debt dies with the debtor. That's the whole cruelty of it. The vow doesn't just erase me. It erases what I owe."

Brannoc's jaw worked. "Say the rest."

"Remember her instead." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Not me. Her. You keep the bead, you tell the holdfast, you tell your children if you ever have them—Serra Nakamura-adjacent, whatever her name was, died so a stranger could finish a job. Don't remember me. I'm a margin note. Remember the girl who actually paid."

"You're asking me to carry a grief that isn't mine."

"I'm asking you to carry mine, because I won't be able to. Yes. It isn't fair. Nothing about this has been fair since a cracked bell rang thirteen times outside my window."

He was silent long enough that the candle guttered and I had to shield it with my hand. When he spoke, it was low, the way clansmen speak oaths—not soft, just heavy.

"I'll remember her," he said. "And I'll remember you too, as long as I'm able. Fragments. Voss says fragments survive, for those holding tokens. So I'll hold two. The bead, and—" he pulled off his glove, worked at something at his belt, and held it out—a scrap of parchment, creased, travel-worn. I recognized it instantly. The route map I'd drawn him two years ago, the night I guided him through Thornfell's passes ahead of an imperial patrol. Smuggler's roads, drawn from memory, in my hand. "This is yours. Your lines. I've looked at it every day since. If the world forgets your name, it won't forget your handwriting. Not while I'm alive to squint at it."

I took it. My hands were not entirely steady, which annoyed me professionally.

"That's cheating," I said. "The vow specifies names."

"The vow never met a courier," Brannoc said. "We're very literal about maps."

I laughed—really laughed, once, sharp in the cold air—and it hurt somewhere behind the sternum, in the place where grief had been sitting for weeks doing carpentry. Grief transmuted into purpose, the priests would call it, if priests were honest, which is why they aren't. But that's what it felt like: the same lead weight, recast. I could not save Serra. I could not save myself. But I could spend what remained of me buying a peace that would outlast both of our memories, and that, at least, was a transaction a debtor could understand.

"One more thing," I said, and turned back to the plank desk. "Read this. All of it. Tomorrow, when Ilvane stands up there claiming forgery and Maren starts screaming about holy vessels, I need one voice in that crowd that knows every line by heart. Yours. You don't argue well, but you *recite* like a rockslide."

He almost smiled. It was terrible. I treasured it.

"Give me the sheets," he said.

* * *

I worked until the moon began its long slide west. Eleven sheets became fourteen. I named the forged lines—one through forty-one, quoted verbatim, each with the date I believed it was added and the hand I believed had held the brush. I stated the true dangers: that the vow was real, that the clans' grievance was real, that the empire's hunger for the mountains was real, and that none of these required a war to resolve but all of them would feed one if left to men like Ilvane and women like Maren. I wrote the rite itself, backward, syllable by syllable, checking it against the cave frescoes and the burned second manuscript's surviving fragment, three times, because a scribe who errs in a ledger loses coin and a scribe who errs here loses everything including the losing.

Near the end, my quill slowed. Not from doubt. From something worse: the strange tenderness of finality. The last line I wrote was not legal language at all. It was small, and I almost struck it, and I didn't.

*If anyone reads this who never knew me: I was a scribe. I copied other people's words for ten years and kept my own mouth shut. These are mine. All of them. I am glad, in the end, that they were worth saying.*

I blotted it. Folded the sheets. Set them in the oilcloth wrap Voss had given me, alongside the imperial warrant copy that made them law.

Below, in the dark, the old quarter was settling into sleep, and somewhere east the mountains stood black against the stars, keeping their own counsel. Tomorrow the thirteenth turn would rise over that ridge, full and white and merciless, and I would stand in this tower and trade my name for a generation's quiet.

I picked up the candle and went down the stairs to find Brannoc, to learn my own confession by heart one last time.

At the bottom step, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the doorway—Voss, arms crossed, warrant gleaming faintly at her belt, and behind her, half-hidden, a third silhouette wearing temple grey.

Ilvane smiled at me in the moonlight.

"We need to talk before dawn," Voss said. "She's brought something you'll want to burn."
