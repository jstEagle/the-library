## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The compass had never once pointed north in Petra Joubert's hands.

It spun instead — a lazy, drunken circle, the needle shivering like a bird that could not decide which way the wind blew — and every cartographer in the realm would have called it broken. Petra knew better. The needle wasn't broken. It was honest. It was trying to point at everything at once, because the valley refused to be *anywhere*, and a thing with no edges cannot be found by iron that listens for them.

Petra sat on the shoulder of the western ridge with their knees drawn up and their satchel open across their lap, and they did what they had done nearly every morning of their twenty-three years: they drew the valley again.

The paper was good stock, cream-colored, traded for at the mill from a peddler who asked no questions about why a surveyor's apprentice needed so much of it. Petra's pencil moved in short, sure strokes. The river Vell came down from the northern crags in three white braids before it went quiet and gray where the valley floor began. The mill sat at its elbow. The orchards fanned south in neat rows that were no longer quite so neat, because old Tomas Verre had stopped tending the third row after his wife died and nobody had the heart to say anything about it. Petra drew the gap in the row. A map that left out grief was not an honest map.

And honesty was the whole of Petra's gift.

Not fire. Not flight, or prophecy, or the whispering of beasts — none of the wonders the traveling preachers railed about when they passed through on the high road, safe inside the wards without knowing what kept them safe. Petra's gift was small enough to hold between two fingers, and it was this: *they could see what was true.* Not the truth people said aloud. The other kind. The shape of it under things, the way a bone shows through skin.

When Petra looked at the valley, they did not see a valley. They saw the ward — a vast slow turning of pale light, older than the mill, older than any family name, wound through stone and root and rain like thread through cloth. It had been woven long ago by someone whose name even the oldest gravestone had forgotten, and it did one thing, perfectly, endlessly: it forgot. Anything that crossed it carrying magic forgot how to carry magic. Spells unraveled to nothing at the boundary line. Enchanted compasses spun. Gifted blood grew quiet as a held breath. The valley was a place where magic could not follow, and so it was a place where magic could not find you either.

Petra had been born inside that forgetting, and it had saved their life, and it had made them lonely, and they had never once been able to untangle which debt outweighed which.

Their mother had been born outside it.

*Elara Joubert, burned as a witch in Ashfall Square on the ninth day of Harvestmonth, in the eleventh year of King Aldous the Second.* The sentence was written in the parish record, in ink that had gone brown as old blood, and Petra had copied it out once, letter by letter, just to know what it felt like to hold it in their own hand. Their mother had come into the valley pregnant and hunted, crossing the ward with her gift screaming in her veins, and the forgetting had taken it from her the way a fever breaks — suddenly, in the night, leaving only the ache where the heat had been. She used to tell Petra about it when Petra was very small, before she learned better than to say such things aloud even here. She would stand at the window looking toward the ridge and say, *It was like losing a language I dreamed in.*

She had taught Petra to draw maps instead. *If you cannot keep what I had,* she said, *keep what I loved. The world is worth knowing exactly.* And so Petra mapped: the valley first, then the passes, then the high country beyond the ward where they were permitted to travel as a surveyor's apprentice with the guild stamp sewn into their collar. Petra mapped rivers and roads, quarries and shrines, the ruins along the salt road with their standing stones worn faceless. And always, always, Petra drew one more thing that appeared on no guild ledger anywhere in the realm: the true shape of the ward itself, rendered so faintly — a hairline wash of silver, invisible unless you tilted the page to the light — that even if someone found the maps, they would find nothing.

That was the discipline of it. That was the whole architecture of Petra's life. *Be exact, and be invisible.* Their mother's voice said it in their head some mornings, clear as church bells: exact, and invisible, and alive.

* * *

By midmorning the sun had cleared the eastern crags and laid a bright bar across the valley floor, and Petra could hear the village waking below — the mill wheel turning, dogs, someone beating a rug with the patient violence of a person who has given up on the morning being kind. Down there, Petra Joubert was nobody. That was not self-pity; it was craft. Petra was the quiet one who took measurements for the drainage ditches and copied deeds and never stayed for the second cup at anyone's table. Twenty-three years of careful dullness, polished smooth as river stone. People spoke around Petra the way water speaks around a rock — constantly, carelessly, revealing everything except the rock itself.

It worked. It worked so well that sometimes Petra could go whole days without remembering there was anything under the surface at all.

But secrets kept too long stop feeling like secrets. They become the walls of the house you live in. Petra had been hiding so long that the hiding had eaten down to the marrow and become the person, and some mornings — like this one, sitting alone on the ridge with the compass spinning its useless honest circles — the loneliness of it rose up in their throat like floodwater.

There was no one to show the silver line on the map to. There never had been. There was no one who would understand why Petra's chest went tight when the light hit it right and the ward shimmered faint as breath on glass. No one who would understand that when Petra stood at the boundary and looked out at the wide world — the real world, the loud and dangerous world where their mother had died for what she was — they felt both terror and a longing so sharp it was nearly indistinguishable from homesickness for a home they'd never had.

"You're talking to yourself again," Petra said aloud, just to hear a voice, and winced at how young it sounded in all that empty air.

They packed up. Pencil, spare leads, the bread wrapped in cloth, the compass that would never work. They paused, as they always did, with one hand flat against the largest of the standing stones near the ridge path — a marker older than the ward, maybe, or maybe part of it; Petra had never decided — and gave it the little tap of two knuckles that was as close as they came to prayer.

"Still here," they told it. "Still nothing special."

The stone, being a stone, said nothing. But beneath Petra's palm, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the ward turned on.

* * *

It happened on the walk down that Petra noticed the smoke.

Not chimney smoke — chimney smoke rose straight and domestic and blue-gray, and this was wrong. This was thin and black-tinged and rising from the direction of the mill square, and beneath it, carried up the slope on the morning wind, came a sound Petra had never heard in the valley before: many voices, all saying the same words at the same time.

Petra stood still on the path. Below, the villagers had gathered in the square — Petra could pick out the shapes of them, Tomas Verre hunched at the back, the miller's boys perched on the fence rail, the smith with his arms folded like a man guarding his own door. And in front of the well stood a man in crown livery with a scroll in his hand, reading aloud in the flat carrying voice of someone paid by the word.

Petra should have walked on. Should have gone around by the orchard road, the way they always did, arriving late and unremarkable. Instead they found themselves moving closer, quiet as a shadow along the hedgerow, until the crier's voice resolved into words:

"—by decree of His Majesty, throughout all provinces and holdings of the realm: let it be known that the practice of sorcery, witchcraft, and the unsanctioned gift is hereby declared treason against crown and church. All persons possessing such gifts shall surrender themselves to the appointed Inquisitors for assessment. All persons harboring, concealing, or failing to report—"

The words went on. They had the practiced rhythm of something read many times in many squares, and that was the worst of it — the boredom in it, the routine. This was not panic. This was policy.

Petra looked at the faces below. Old Tomas, blank. The smith, watching his neighbors watch each other. The miller's wife, whose eyes moved slowly across the crowd with a new and terrible diligence, cataloguing. In twenty years Petra had never seen these people look at one another that way, and they understood, with a cold settling certainty, that they were watching the beginning of something that would not end quickly.

*All persons harboring, concealing, or failing to report.*

Concealing. The word sat in Petra's chest like a swallowed coal. Twenty-three years of careful dullness, and it had never once occurred to them that invisibility itself might someday be the crime — that silence could be read as guilt, that the careful life their mother had built for them was not shelter but evidence, waiting only for someone patient enough to read it.

Their mother had hidden too. Right up until the day someone didn't look away.

Petra turned and walked back up the hill, unhurried, a surveyor's apprentice with measurements to take, and did not run until the bend of the path took them out of sight. Then they ran.

At home — the low stone house at the edge of the commons, the one with the herb garden and the crooked door — they pulled up the third floorboard and took out the bundle. Her letters. Eleven of them, written in a slanting hand, sent to a cousin in the valley in the years before Petra was born, full of ordinary news and one extraordinary sentence each, buried like needles in wool: *the gift runs in our family, so mind yourself.* *If the child shows it, cross the ward and never write me.* *I have hidden the true maps where only you would think to look.*

These were the most dangerous objects in the valley. Petra had known that since they were nine. They carried the bundle to the hearth anyway, knelt, and fed the letters to the fire one by one, watching eleven lifetimes of careful words curl and blacken and go.

Ten went easily.

The last one caught on the corner and Petra snatched it back — reflex, pure animal reflex, faster than thought — and stood there holding a half-scorched page while the rest of the bundle became ash. On the page, in their mother's slanting hand, half-eaten by flame but still legible, was a single line Petra had never read before, because it had been folded inside where the fire hadn't reached:

*The fire remembers us, little one. Whatever else they burn, the fire remembers.*

Petra stood in the smoke-smelling room for a long time, the page trembling slightly in their hand.

Outside, far down the valley, past the ward, past the mountains, a bell began to ring — not the chapel bell, but something deeper, older, a sound felt in the teeth rather than heard by the ear. The compass in Petra's satchel, spinning all these years, went suddenly still.

It pointed east. Toward the mountains. Toward the sound.

And it did not move again.
