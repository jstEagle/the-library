## 2. Proclamation at the Mill

The criers came on a Tuesday, which Petra would remember afterward as the cruelest detail of all. Tuesdays were mill days. The whole valley came down from its terraces and out of its timber camps to weigh flour and argue about prices, and so there was no one left to hear the proclamation alone.

Petra heard it in a crowd of forty neighbors, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the mill yard, close enough to smell the sour sweat of fear rising off them like steam off a spring field.

The crier was a young man with a crown stitched in gold thread on his breast and a voice trained to carry across parade grounds. He stood on the mill's loading platform beside a soldier who held a scroll he never unrolled. Behind them, nailed to the mill's great door, hung a sheet of vellum that caught the morning light and threw it back white as bone.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third," the crier called, "and by counsel of the Holy Office of Inquiry: let it be known throughout the realm that the practice of the old arts is hereby declared treason against crown and covenant alike."

Petra's hands went still around the strap of their satchel. They had come for flour. Two sacks, coarse-ground, the same as every week for eleven years. They had rehearsed this errand the way they rehearsed everything — the nod to Widow Kessel, the joke about the weather with the Brenner brothers, the exact amount of coin counted out with a stammer that made them seem slow rather than strange. Eleven years of rehearsal, and now the script had been torn up overnight.

"Those who harbor the gifted," the crier continued, "those who conceal them, those who fail to report knowledge of them, share equally in their guilt. Informants will be compensated from the royal treasury. Names may be given in confidence."

*In confidence.* The words slid through the crowd like a knife finding the gap between ribs. Petra watched it happen — watched the mill yard rearrange itself in a single breath. Old Tomas Vey shifted his weight so he could see both the crier and the door behind him. Marta Brenner's hand found her husband's sleeve and gripped it, not for comfort but for anchoring, the way you hold something down in wind. Someone near the back coughed, and three heads turned toward the sound before catching themselves and turning away again, too quickly, the way people look away when they've seen something they shouldn't have.

Petra had spent eleven years learning to read a crowd the way other people read weather. Now they read this one and felt the ground tilt beneath them. These were faces they had mapped. The mole on Kessel's chin like a landmark. The way Brenner's youngest always stood slightly apart, daydreaming — Petra had once thought of him as a fellow creature of margins. Every face was a feature on a map they'd drawn by heart, and every one of those features had just been reclassified. *Here be watchers.*

"The gifted are known by signs," the crier said, and began to list them, and Petra listened with the detached horror of a person hearing their own autopsy described. Lights seen where no lamp burned. Animals uneasy in their presence. Crops that grew wrong, or grew too well. Dreams shared between strangers. A compass that would not settle.

That last one landed like a fist. Petra's compass sat in their satchel not four inches from their hip, its needle swinging in its lazy, useless, telltale circle whenever they crossed the valley's edge. They had carried it openly for years precisely because it looked like nothing — a surveyor's tool, a quirk, an eccentricity. *Invisibility through plain sight,* their mother had written once, in one of the letters now ash in the stove. *Be so ordinary that suspicion slides off you like rain off slate.*

But the crier had just made ordinary evidence. The needle that wouldn't settle was no longer a quirk. It was a sign. It was a name waiting to be given *in confidence.*

Petra did the only thing their training allowed. They yawned. A long, theatrical, bored yawn, the yawn of a man thinking about lunch, and shuffled two steps sideways, and muttered to no one in particular, "Crown's finally spending coin on us, eh? Must be an election year." And Brenner laughed — a short, startled bark — because it was absurd, because the valley was a grain speck on the king's maps, because nothing ever happened here, and the laugh broke something in the crowd's terrible new attention, loosened it, gave everyone permission to be bored again.

Eleven years of rehearsal. It still worked. Petra walked home with their two sacks of flour and their heart hammering against their ribs like something trying to get out.

* * *

The letters were under the third floorboard in the bedroom, where they had been since Petra was nineteen, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. There were nine of them. Their mother had written them over six years, from wherever she had been kept before the pyre — no, Petra corrected themselves, not kept. She had written them before. Before the arrest, before the trial that lasted an afternoon, before the square and the smoke. She had hidden them the way Petra hid them, and they had passed through whatever chain of careful hands to reach the valley, and Petra had read them so many times they could recite them, which meant, some cold voice in their head observed, that burning them might cost less than feared.

They built the fire up hot anyway. If it was going to be done, it would be done properly, thoroughly, the way everything in their life was done — rehearsed, controlled, invisible.

The first letter went in and curled at the edges like a living thing flinching. *My Petra. By the time you read this I will be a story, and stories about women like me end one way. So listen instead to what I leave you...*

Petra fed the letters one at a time, reading each fully before surrendering it, a rite, a wake. *Never map the same road twice in company.* *Let them think you dull; dullness is armor.* *The gift is not yours to use, only to survive.* Each page went into the flames and became orange lace and then nothing, and Petra knelt on the cold stone watching their mother become smoke for the second time in their life, and felt — nothing, at first, which frightened them more than grief would have. Then the grief arrived late and sideways, the way it always did, as a physical fact: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of copper.

The ninth letter was the last. It was also the shortest, and the oldest, dated the year Petra turned thirteen, and Petra knew it by heart too, but they read it anyway, kneeling there, holding it over the fire:

*You asked me why we hide. Here is the truth I should have told you sooner: hiding was my choice, made for my reasons, and I have let you believe it was your nature. It is not. You were not born to be small. I made you small because I was afraid, and fear is a poor inheritance. One day you will meet someone or something that makes the hiding feel like the greater danger. When that day comes, do not mistake my caution for your conscience. Burn these letters if you must. But do not burn the knowing.*

Petra held the page over the fire.

The heat took the bottom edge. The paper browned, buckled, and the first line of their mother's handwriting began to blacken — *do not burn the knowing* — and Petra's hand jerked back without any decision being made at all, pure animal reflex, and the page came away scorched at one corner but whole.

They stared at it. Then they tried again, deliberately, jaw set, because a thing half-done was worse than a thing done, because half-measures got people burned —

— and could not. Their arm simply refused the motion a second time, and then a third, and Petra sat back on their heels in the firelight and laughed, one dry, disbelieving note, because they understood suddenly what had happened. They had spent eleven years building an invisible life out of other people's instructions, and they had just discovered that somewhere underneath all that careful masonry there was still a self, and it had opinions, and one of them was *no.*

They folded the scorched page along its old creases and put it inside their shirt, against their skin, where a compass used to sit.

* * *

That evening the valley changed its shape.

Petra saw it from their window, the way they saw everything — from slightly above, slightly outside, the cartographer's vantage. Lamps lit earlier than usual. Doors latched with more care than the mild night required. And movement: figures crossing between houses after dark, brief silhouettes against lit windows, neighbors visiting neighbors at hours when no neighbor visited anyone. Nobody was saying anything yet. But the traffic itself was the message. The valley had begun talking to itself in a new language, and every conversation was the same conversation, and Petra was its subject whether named or not.

Widow Kessel came by at dusk with a basket of late apples she didn't need an excuse to deliver, because she had never needed an excuse in her life, and stayed exactly as long as politeness required, and asked three questions that were not questions — *had Petra heard the proclamation, what did they make of it, surely nothing of that sort would come here* — and Petra answered each one with the right blandness, and watched her leave, and understood that Kessel would stand in her kitchen tonight deciding what kind of neighbor she was going to be. Everyone in the valley was deciding that tonight. That was the true genius of the proclamation, Petra realized. It didn't hunt the gifted. It conscripted everyone else.

They ate bread and hard cheese standing at the window. Below, a lantern bobbed along the lane toward the Vey farm, paused at the gate, moved on. Toward the Brenners'. Away again.

Petra touched the scorched page through their shirt. *Do not burn the knowing.*

Their mother had been wrong about one thing, Petra thought. She had written that hiding was her choice, made for her reasons — as if invisibility were a coat Petra could one day take off and find themselves unchanged underneath. But eleven years was a long time to wear a coat. It had grown into the shoulders. The stammer, the yawns, the dull eyes, the rehearsed laughter — Petra no longer knew where the performance ended and the performer began, and that, they understood now, standing alone in a house full of ash, was the deepest wound the hiding had dealt them. Not the loneliness. Not even the fear. The theft of the question itself. They had never once been permitted to find out who they might have been.

Well. The crown had ended that today. Inquisitors did not come to valleys like this one unless they already suspected, and criers did not list compasses among the signs unless someone had reported one, and reports did not travel out of a closed valley unless the wards — the old, quiet, unquestioned wards their mother's generation had raised — were failing, or being failed.

Someone in this valley had talked. Or something had slipped. Either way, the arithmetic was simple and Petra had always been good at arithmetic: the invisible life was over. The only choice left was how it ended — in ash and a cage, or on their own terms, on roads only they had mapped.

Outside, far up the western pass where the valley met the mountains, lightning flickered against a cloudless sky. Once. Twice. Petra stood very still, flour dust on their sleeves, and watched it, and felt — faintly, distantly, the way you feel a knock through a thick wall — something answer.

Then hooves on the lane below. A stranger riding up through the dusk toward the valley gate, and every lamp in every window turning toward the sound.
