## 3. Orsen at the Gate

The valley announced itself to Orsen three miles before he reached it, in the way his scars began to ache.

He had learned to read pain the way other men read weather. The old burn that climbed his left side from hip to jaw—the Inquisition's parting gift, pressed into him with a heated sigil-iron when he was nineteen and too slow to flinch—flared when magic was near. It ached in the presence of wards, of charms, of anything the Church's manuals called *residue*. For eleven years he had used that ache as an instrument, hunting with it, and for two years since deserting he had hated it, because it never stopped telling him where the world was wounded.

Now it told him this: the valley ahead was wrapped in quiet so complete it rang.

He stopped on the ridge where the pines thinned and looked down at it. A bowl of green and grey stone, terraced fields stitched along its flanks, a river running silver through the middle like a vein someone had forgotten to close. Smoke rose from chimneys in unhurried columns. Sheep moved on the high pasture like scattered chalk marks. It looked, from above, like the kind of place maps labeled with a single word—*vale*, *hamlet*, *pastoral*—and travelers rode through without remembering they'd passed.

Orsen knew better. He had spent six weeks walking here from the coast, following rumors the way water follows cracks: a valley where compasses spun, where the crown's criers were met with polite deafness, where no witch had burned in living memory. The Inquisition's own archives called it by a number instead of a name. *Site Nine.* The designation alone had made him turn cold when he found it, because Site Nine was not on any list of places to be purged. It was on a list of places to be *studied*.

And now the hunt had come to study it, which meant the hunt had come to empty it.

He started down.

* * *

The gate was not a gate. That was the first thing he noticed, and the thing that confirmed everything.

There was a mill at the valley's mouth, a squat stone building with its wheel turning slow and brown in the current, and beside it a footbridge, and beside the bridge a post where travelers' names were supposed to be chalked according to some old custom nobody enforced anymore. The chalkboard hung there, weathered soft as cloth. But when Orsen crossed the threshold of the bridge—when his boot came down on the far side, onto valley stone—he felt the wards take his measure.

It was not a wall. It was not a barrier he could have drawn on a map. It was more like stepping into a room where someone had just stopped speaking. The air attended him. Something vast and patient turned its attention toward the scarred man on the road and asked, without words, *what are you?*

His scar answered for him. It always did. The ache sank from flare to murmur, the way it did around dead charms, around cold altars, around things that had been bled of power long ago. Whatever watched him found nothing in him worth keeping. He felt its attention slide off him like rain off waxed canvas, and he understood, with a lurch of something between relief and insult, that the valley had decided he was harmless.

A deserter with a burn scar and a soldier's walk. Nothing under the mask. Nothing worth looking twice at.

He let it believe that.

The miller's daughter—or a girl who worked the mill; she had flour to her elbows and a miller's squint—looked up from the sluice gate as he approached. "No rooms," she said, before he could speak. "No work either, if you're wondering."

"I'm looking for a guide," Orsen said. "Someone who knows the passes east. I'm told the folk here know ground no one else does."

"We know our ground." She went back to the sluice. "That's not the same as knowing yours."

"It isn't my ground I need to cross," he said. "It's the mountains past it. And I'll pay in coin that spends anywhere."

That got her eyes back up. Coin that spends anywhere meant crown mint, and crown mint in a valley like this was both temptation and suspicion wearing the same coat. She studied him the way farmers study weather that might go either way.

"There's a surveyor," she said finally. "Does the mapping for the parish. Petra Joubert. They'll want paying less than you're offering and they'll know the passes better than anyone breathing. You can find them up-slope, where the old cairn line starts." She jerked her chin northward. "But I'll say what everyone will say anyway: strangers bring eyes, and eyes bring the criers, and we've had enough criers this season to paper the church."

"I'm not with the criers," Orsen said.

"Nobody is," she said. "That's how they get everywhere."

* * *

He found Petra Joubert at the top of the cairn line, kneeling on a shelf of granite with a board across their knees, drawing the world.

Orsen stopped thirty feet off and simply watched, because watching was the one skill the Inquisition had taught him that he didn't hate.

The surveyor was young—not much past twenty-five—with ink-stained fingers and hair tied back with what looked like a strip of sailcloth. They worked with a compass out, and Orsen saw, even at that distance, that the needle was swinging in slow, useless circles, and that Petra Joubert was ignoring it entirely, drafting by eye and sun and some private arithmetic of their own. Their lines were beautiful. He could tell that from thirty feet. Not the careful, dead beauty of official cartography but something alive, the contours breathing, the river drawn like it was still moving.

And there was this: the moment Orsen's shadow fell across the granite, the surveyor's hand paused. Just a fraction. Just long enough for the pen to hover.

They felt him. Not heard him—he'd been downwind and silent—but felt him, the way you feel a door open in a far room. Orsen filed that away with the care of a man filing evidence, and then, deliberately, scuffed his boot on the stone so the next pause would have an honest cause.

"Road's behind you," Petra said without turning. "Mill's behind that. If you've come for directions, you've walked past the person who gives them free."

"I came for the ones that aren't free." He came closer, hands visible, away from his sword—a habit so ingrained it had outlived its honesty. "I need a guide over the eastern passes. Past the valley. I'm told you're the only one who's mapped them."

"They're not mapped." Now Petra turned, and Orsen got his first full look at their face: watchful, wind-burned, with the particular stillness of someone who has practiced being unremarkable until the practice became bone. "That's rather the point of them."

"Then I'm hiring the only person who knows that," Orsen said. "Which makes them mapped, doesn't it? Somewhere in your head."

Something flickered in the surveyor's expression—annoyance, or maybe the ghost of amusement deciding whether to be born. "Who told you about me?"

"The mill."

"The mill tells strangers about me?"

"The mill tells strangers about the surveyor," Orsen said. "You and the surveyor can fight that out between you."

Petra stood, brushing granite dust from their knees, and looked at him the way he was learning people in this valley looked at everything: as if measuring it against some standard of harm that lived entirely inside their own heads. His scar chose that moment to murmur again—that low, cold ache—and he watched Petra's eyes flick, briefly, involuntarily, to the burn climbing his jaw.

Not with pity. With *recognition*. The look of someone seeing a mark they understood the grammar of, if not the letters.

"You've been burned by iron," Petra said. "Sigil-iron. That's a hunter's mark."

"It's a lot of things," Orsen said.

"It's a hunter's mark," Petra repeated, quieter. "I've seen one before. Once. When I was small." Their voice did something complicated on the last word and then flattened itself flat. "Hunters don't usually hire the hunted to carry their packs."

"No," Orsen said. "They don't."

Silence, then. Wind moving the grass below the cairns. Somewhere downslope, a dog barking at nothing, or at everything.

Orsen had rehearsed this conversation for six weeks, and every version of it had been a lie of one size or another. *I'm a merchant. I'm a scholar. I'm fleeing debt.* All of them designed to pass through the valley's attention the way his scar had passed through its wards: unnoticed, unexamined, unworthy of a second look. And now he stood in front of the one person in the valley whose second look he actually needed, and found that the lies sat in his mouth like stones.

Because he had seen the compass spinning. Because he had seen the surveyor feel his shadow before hearing his boots. Because he had watched a whole valley wrap itself in silence like a held breath, and he knew—knew with the certainty of a man who had caged children for knowing less—what lived under that silence.

Petra Joubert was gifted. Had been since birth, probably, hidden the way such children were hidden: carefully, completely, until the hiding became the whole architecture of a life. Orsen had carried three of them once, in salt-iron cages, to a facility whose name he still couldn't say aloud. He knew what the hiding looked like from the outside. It looked exactly like this: competent, guarded, unremarkable, alone.

And Petra was looking at his scar the way people look at a scar they've seen made.

Two masks, facing each other on a mountain. Neither one willing to be the first to lift a corner.

"I was a hunter," Orsen said. "Eleven years. Inquisition third rank, field division. I did—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "I did things I won't describe to a stranger on a hillside, and I won't pretend they weren't done. Two years ago I walked away. They burned me on the way out, as a receipt. Since then I've been running, and running has taught me one thing worth money: the hunt is coming here. Not someday. This season. Maybe this month."

Petra had gone very still. Not the stillness of fear—Orsen knew fear's stillness, its shallow breath, its darting eyes. This was different. This was the stillness of a person re-sorting everything they believed about their safety, drawer by drawer, and finding each one already open.

"Why here?" Petra said. "Why now?"

"Because the hunt changed what it wants." He held their gaze because looking away would cost more than holding it. "It isn't burning the gifted anymore. Not primarily. It's collecting them. There's a purpose, and the purpose is old, and I only saw the edges of it before I ran. But I saw enough to know that valleys like this one—the ones the archives call by numbers—are not being overlooked. They're being saved for last. Like cellars."

"Cellars of what?"

"Of everything the valley's been hiding," Orsen said. "For generations."

The wind came up the slope and moved between them, and Petra pulled their coat tighter, and Orsen watched the surveyor do the thing he would see them do a hundred times after: decide. Quickly, privately, with no consultation of anyone. The decision arrived in their face like weather arriving over a ridge.

"One day," Petra said. "I'll take you as far as the eastern col. One day's pay, half up front. And you'll tell me, on the trail, exactly what you saw at the edges—because if hunters are coming to my valley, I'll know it from you before I know it from them."

"That's fair," Orsen said.

"And if you're lying," Petra added, shouldering their satchel, already turning away, "I'll leave you on the mountain. I know places up there where no one's been found in forty years."

"I'd expect nothing less," Orsen said.

They started down together, three feet apart, two strangers who had each just shown the other a corner of a mask and pretended it was the whole face. Orsen's scar murmured its cold song, and he wondered—not for the first time, and not, as it would turn out, for the last—whether the valley's wards had judged him harmless because he was, or because whatever watched from the deep green quiet of the place had already decided he might yet be useful.

Behind them, far to the south, past the ridgelines, a sound reached the valley for the first time in its memory: the measured, carrying beat of a proclamation drum.

Petra stopped. Turned. Listened.

"That's early," they said. "The criers aren't due till month's end."

Orsen listened, and his stomach dropped through the mountain, because he knew that rhythm. Every hunter learned it in their first year. It wasn't a crier's drum.

It was a cage-train's.

"They're not early," he said. "They're already here."
