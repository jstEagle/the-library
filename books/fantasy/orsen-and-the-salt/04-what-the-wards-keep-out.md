## 4. What the Wards Keep Out

Petra knew the valley was waking before they understood why.

It came as a wrongness in the morning light—the way frost clung to the south faces of the stones instead of the north, the way the millpond's skin held its mist a heartbeat too long, like breath caught in a throat. Petra had spent twenty-six years learning to read this valley the way other people read scripture: line by line, obsessively, looking for what was hidden between the words. And the valley was telling them something now. The wards were straining.

They stood at their window with the last page of their mother's letters pressed flat beneath their palm on the sill, and watched the crows lift off the chapel roof all at once, in silence, without circling back.

"Orsen," they said quietly, to the man sleeping in the chair by the cold hearth, and then again, louder, when he didn't stir. "Orsen. The valley's frightened."

He woke the way soldiers wake—no slow surfacing, no confusion, just eyes open and hand already at the knife under his coat. For a moment he looked at Petra without recognition, and Petra looked back at him without flinching, and that exchange—two strangers' masks held up to the light of dawn—had become, over three days, almost a language between them.

"What is it?" he asked.

"The birds. The frost. The pond." Petra lifted their hand from the page. "Something's pressing on the wards from outside. Something heavy."

Orsen rose, went to the window, and went very still. He had a stillness Petra had learned to distrust; it was the stillness of men who had seen things and arranged them neatly inside themselves where no one could reach them. But this morning his stillness looked less like arrangement and more like dread.

"How long," he said, "since you've been past the tree line?"

"I've never been past it."

"You've *mapped* it." He turned. His face was half shadow, half gray light, and both halves were grim. "I've seen your work. You've drawn every fold of those mountains from memory, from rumor, from triangulation off your ridiculous spinning compass. You know the pass better than anyone alive, because no one else has ever bothered to look."

"The pass is closed," Petra said. "The wards keep everything out. That's the whole point of them."

"That's what I told myself for eleven years too." Orsen pulled on his boots. "And then I learned what wards actually are. They're not walls, Petra. They're promises. Old ones, made by old people, and promises only hold as long as someone honors them. Hale doesn't break promises. She makes them *irrelevant*."

"Hale."

"Inquisitor Hale." He said the name the way you'd set down something heavy. "She'll have come up the river road with six salt-iron wagons and forty sworn men, and she'll have stopped at the boundary stone by now, and she'll be unpacking the cage."

Petra's mouth had gone dry. "What cage?"

"A cage made of salt and iron, woven like basketry, big enough to swallow a village ward whole. It doesn't shatter magic. It *starves* it. Sits over the source like a lid on a pot until everything underneath goes quiet and cold." Orsen met their eyes, and there it was again—that terrible gentleness he wore whenever he spoke of the Inquisition, the gentleness of a man describing his own crimes. "I helped build the first one. Eleven years ago. In Carrowmere. There was a woman there who could make bread rise with a song. When the cage came down over her house, the bread in every oven in the street fell flat at once. That's how we knew it worked."

The room felt smaller. Petra wrapped their arms around themselves and made their voice stay level. "Then we run."

"Where?"

"Through the pass." Petra was already moving—pulling the oilskin roll of maps from under the floorboards, taking down the satchel, the flint, the dried fish, the small hoard of coins they'd kept against a day they'd never let themselves imagine. Their hands shook and they let them. Fear was allowed to shake. "There's a way through the northern ridge. It isn't on any map but mine. It isn't on any map because it barely exists—a goat track, a dry waterfall, a shelf of stone above the gorge. Two days' walk if the weather holds. The wards thin near the ridge because the founders never thought anything would come *from inside* going out."

"And on the other side?" Orsen asked quietly. "Outside the valley, Petra. What happens to you out there?"

Petra stopped with the satchel half-buckled.

They had thought about it, of course. Every night of their life they had thought about it, the way you think about a door in a dream—you know it opens, you know what's behind it, and you know you must never turn the handle. Outside the valley, the gift their mother had died for would not be a secret humming under their skin. It would be a *fact*. The land itself would answer them. Their mother's letters had said so, in that careful coded language: *the valley keeps us quiet, little stone, but quiet is not the same as silent.*

"I don't know," Petra said. And then, because lying to Orsen had begun to feel like lying to a mirror: "I'll burn brighter than I've ever been. And every hunter in the realm will feel it."

Orsen nodded slowly, as if this were the answer he'd expected and perhaps even hoped for. "Then we'd better be fast."

* * *

They left by the sheep path behind the tannery while the church bells were ringing the hour after dawn, and the bells sounded wrong—flattened, muffled, as though the sound itself were being smothered. By the time they reached the upper pasture, the mist over the millpond had not moved at all.

From the ridge shoulder above the village, Petra looked back once.

The valley lay below them in the early light, green and ordered and beloved: the mill, the chapel, the crooked lanes, the pasture fences their grandfather had mended, the graves on the hillside where their mother did *not* lie because there had been nothing left to bury. It looked exactly as it always had. That was the horror of it. It looked untouched, and it was already dying. Somewhere beyond the southern boundary stone, a cage of salt and iron was settling over the valley's oldest promise like a lid on a pot, and everything underneath was going quiet and cold.

"They won't follow fast," Orsen said beside them. "Hale never does. She'll seal the roads first, take names, ask her gentle questions. She believes fear does half her work for her." A pause. "She's usually right."

"Then we give her the other half." Petra turned north and did not look back again. Looking back was a luxury of people with homes to return to.

The goat track began where the pasture ended, a thread of pale stone stitched along the gorge wall, and Petra had walked it in their mind ten thousand times. Now their feet found it as though the mountain had been waiting to teach it to them. Up through the scree. Along the shelf where the rock overhung and you pressed yourself to cold stone with the drop breathing on your neck. Past the dry waterfall, its limestone lip hung with icicles like the pipes of some frozen organ. Petra navigated by compass that spun, by memory, by instinct—and by something else, something new, a faint pull in their chest like a fisherman's line running off a reel, drawing them north and up.

By midday they were above the tree line, and the world opened.

The high country spread around them in waves of white and gray-blue, wind-scoured, enormous, indifferent. Petra had mapped these ridges from below their whole life, had drawn their profiles in careful ink, and none of it—not one stroke—had prepared them for the actual scale of it, the way the sky leaned down and the land leaned up and a person became a punctuation mark between them.

They stopped to breathe at the saddle below the final ridge, and that was when Petra felt it.

At first they thought it was the altitude—a pressure behind the sternum, a ringing in the ears. Then they realized the ringing had a pitch, and the pitch had a direction, and the direction was *everywhere*. The ground under their boots. The wind combing the snow grass. The ice in the waterfall, the stone in the scree, the thin fierce sunlight itself. All of it humming, all of it awake, all of it turning toward them the way sunflowers turn toward the sun.

"Oh," Petra said. It was not a word. It was a small involuntary sound, the sound of a lock opening.

"What—" Orsen began, and then he saw their face and stopped.

Petra knelt and put their bare hand flat against the snow, because they could not *not*.

The world answered.

Warmth bloomed up through their palm—not melting the snow, not burning, but meeting them, the way a hand meets a hand. Beneath the crust of winter, deep in the bones of the mountain, something ancient stirred and stretched and *recognized* them. Petra felt the ridge lines like veins, felt water moving in the dark of the rock, felt the slow patient attention of the land itself fixing on them with something that was not quite sight and not quite love but partook of both. Their gift—which they had carried like a coal banked in ash for twenty-six years, hidden, hushed, denied—took one long breath.

And ignited.

Light ran out from them across the snowfield in a slow ring, silver-gold, silent, impossibly gentle, and everywhere it passed the snow grass lifted though there was no wind, and the ice on the rocks sang—one clear bell-note, then another, harmonics chasing each other down the gorge. Petra gasped, and the light pulsed with the gasp. They laughed, and it pulsed again, wilder. Terror and wonder braided so tightly together in their chest that they could not have said which was which; they were the same cord, pulling taut.

Every hunter in the realm will feel it, they had said. And they understood now, kneeling in the ring of their own radiance, that this was true—but they also understood, with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, that they would rather be *seen* than spend one more year being nothing.

"Petra." Orsen's voice, rough, close. His hand on their shoulder, grounding them. "You're glowing. You're—gods, you're *beautiful*, and you're a beacon, and we need to move *now*."

The light guttered. Died. The mountain's hum sank back beneath audibility, but it did not disappear—Petra could still feel it, a hand still holding theirs, patient, waiting.

They got up. Their legs shook. Their eyes were wet and they did not wipe them.

"It knows me," Petra whispered. "Out here—it *knows* me. My whole life I thought the gift was mine, something I owned and hid. But it's not. It's a conversation. And the valley was me talking in my sleep."

Orsen was scanning the southern sky, jaw tight. "Then pray whatever's listening on the other side wasn't paying attention. Because—" He stopped. Went still, that soldier's stillness, and pointed.

Far to the south, down where the valley lay folded in its green, a light was rising. Not firelight. Not sunlight. A column of pale, hungry luminescence climbing into the noon sky like a scream made visible, and beneath it, faint on the wind, came a sound Petra had never heard and knew instantly: the groan of iron taking hold of something living.

"The cage," Orsen said. "That's the wards dying."

Petra stood on the saddle with their mother's last page against their heart and their gift singing in their blood, and looked north, where the pass dropped away into country no map had ever honestly drawn.

"Then the valley's gone," they said. "And everything it kept quiet is about to get very loud."

Behind them, far to the south, the light pulsed once more—and this time, Petra felt it *answer* them. Not the wards. Something older. Something vast, and failing, and calling their name in a voice like banked coals.

North, the pull in their chest said. North, and hurry.
