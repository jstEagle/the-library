## 5. Salt Roads

The road out of the mountains was salt.

Not the white crust of a dried lakebed, but something older—a gray rime that furred the stones like frost that had forgotten how to melt. Orsen had walked these roads before, years ago, in a gray cloak with an Inquisitor's sigil stitched at his shoulder, and he had told himself then what he told himself now: *keep moving, keep your head down, the road doesn't remember you.*

But roads remembered. That was the whole problem with roads.

He glanced back at Petra, three paces behind, wrapped in a borrowed coat two sizes too large, their thumb hooked through the strap of the satchel that held everything left of their old life: a broken compass, a water skin, and—though they hadn't told him—one page of a burned letter they'd pulled from the fire with bare fingers. He had seen them do it. He had said nothing about that either. His silences were becoming a country of their own, and Petra was living in it without knowing where its borders lay.

"The pass forks ahead," Petra said. "Left goes down to the river towns. Right—" They stopped walking. Orsen turned to find them standing very still, head tilted like a deer hearing something past human hearing.

"Right?" he prompted.

"I don't know." Their voice came out strange, flattened with wonder. "Right is... loud."

"Loud how?"

Petra didn't answer. They turned right instead, off the road entirely, boots crunching over salt-rimed scree, toward a wall of black stone where nothing grew. Orsen watched them go, felt the familiar cold weight settle behind his ribs—the weight of knowing things he shouldn't know—and followed.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the rock wall, a fire small enough to hide under a blanket of woven branches. Petra sat cross-legged with the map they were always drawing, quill scratching in the dark by feel more than sight. Orsen watched the shape take form: the valley behind them, the fork, the black wall—and beyond it, where no mapmaker's hand had ever gone, a series of lines that curved inward, spiraling toward some center point high in the peaks.

"What's that?" he asked.

"I don't know yet." Petra's quill paused. "It's what the land keeps telling me."

"The land talks to you now."

"It talked to me in the valley too. I just couldn't hear it properly there. The wards..." They trailed off, shaking their head as if clearing water from their ears. "Everything outside the valley is so much louder. I can feel the roads. The rivers. There's a place up there—" they tapped the spiral's center "—that's been calling since we crossed the pass. Like a voice through a wall. Getting clearer every mile."

Orsen knew what lived in high places where the land sang wrong. He'd stood at the edge of a caldera once, four years ago, holding a lantern while men in gray unloaded cages from mule-back. He said none of this.

Instead he said: "You should sleep. We move before dawn."

"You've been saying that every night." Petra set down the quill. Firelight caught the sharp line of their jaw, the exhaustion beneath their eyes. "And every night you don't sleep either. You sit there listening for hoofbeats."

"Habit."

"Hale's a week behind us, maybe less. Sitting up all night won't change that."

"No," Orsen agreed. "But it'll mean I'm awake when she finds us."

The silence stretched between them, and inside it Orsen heard everything he wasn't saying pile up like snow against a door. Four days on the road. Four days of Petra asking small questions—*where did you learn to read trail sign, why do you know Inquisition hand-signals, why did you really leave*—and accepting his half-answers with a patience that was worse than anger. They deserved better than half-answers. They deserved better than a man who had learned, in gray halls, that confession was just another form of capture.

But tomorrow the spiral lines would lead them somewhere, and if it was what he feared it was, Petra would need to trust him completely. And trust built on silence was a bridge over nothing.

"Petra," he said.

They looked up.

"There's something you should know about me before we go any further. And after you know it, you might not want me sitting watch over you while you sleep."

* * *

He told it plainly, because he had learned that plainness was the only mercy in confession.

"I joined the Inquisition at nineteen. Not for belief—for bread. My village had burned the year before, my family with it, and the Order fed you and clothed you and told you your suffering had meaning." He kept his eyes on the fire. "I was good at the work. Tracking. Reading sign. Knowing which villages would shelter the gifted and which would give them up. For six years I led retrieval teams across the northern reaches."

"Retrieval," Petra repeated. The word sat in the air between them, flat and terrible.

"That's what we called it. What it was—" He made himself look up. Made himself meet their eyes. "We took children, Petra. Gifted children. There was a wagon, iron-lined, with cages in it, and my job was to find them before they understood what their gift was, before anyone could teach them to hide. I carried a girl out of her grandmother's house in Harrowmere with her hands tied in her apron strings. She was eight. She kept apologizing to me. She thought if she was sorry enough I'd let her stay."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through stone with a sound almost like breathing.

"I told myself it was mercy. The alternative was what happened to women like your mother—pyres in the square. The Order said the children went somewhere safe. Somewhere they'd be *understood*." His voice went rough. "I believed it until the day I saw the wagons' destination. A mountain. A caldera. And I understood that nothing we delivered there ever came back, and I deserted that same night. They've hunted me since. Hale most of all—she trained me. She knows how I think. Which means she knows exactly where I'd run, and who I'd run with."

He let the last word hang. *Who.*

"So when I say we're being followed, I'm not guessing. And when I say I know what the hunt wants—I know because I helped feed it." He spread his hands, empty. "That's what I am, Petra. Not a guide. Not a soldier. A man who put eight-year-olds in cages and called it kindness, and ran only when it became inconvenient to stay. If you want to leave, the fork is half a day back. I'll draw you a map myself. It's the one honest thing I can still give you."

He finished, and the night closed around the words, and Orsen sat in it feeling lighter and more naked than he had in eleven years.

Petra didn't speak for a long time. When they finally moved, it was to pull the satchel into their lap and hold it, both arms around it, the way they had held it since the burning.

"My mother wrote letters," they said quietly. "Forty of them, maybe. I never got to read most of them—she hid them before they came for her, and I found them when I was sixteen. Every letter was about me. What I was. How to hide it. How to be small enough that the world wouldn't notice." Their thumb moved over the leather. "I burned thirty-nine of them yesterday because the criers made me afraid, and I hated them for making me afraid, and I hated *her* a little too, for teaching me that fear was the same thing as love. But she also wrote this—" they tapped the satchel "—and this is the only thing I have of hers, and I chose it. Out of everything. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Orsen shook his head slowly.

"I'm telling you I know what it is to carry something you can't put down. Something that's part shame and part inheritance and you can't cut either free." Petra's voice stayed level, but their eyes shone wet in the firelight. "You caged children. You also walked away from the only life you'd built rather than deliver one more. Both of those are true, and I'm not going to pretend the first one doesn't make me sick, because it does. But you're telling me now, on a road, with Hale coming, when lying would have been easier. So."

"So?"

"So I need time. I need you to know that 'I need time' isn't the same as 'go away.'" They looked at him then, fully, and there was no softness in it—but there was no dismissal either. There was something harder and more valuable: a person deciding, deliberately, with open eyes. "Walk ahead of me tomorrow. Show me the way. And we'll see what I decide when we get wherever it is you know we're going."

Orsen nodded. It was not forgiveness. He hadn't earned forgiveness. But it was a road, and roads were more than he'd hoped for.

* * *

They climbed for two days.

The salt thickened as they rose, crusting the switchbacks in pale scales, and with every mile Petra grew quieter—not afraid-quiet, Orsen noticed, but *listening*-quiet, head tilted, lips sometimes moving as though repeating words spoken too softly for him to hear. On the second morning they stopped using the trail altogether, striking off across bare rock toward a saddle between two peaks, and when Orsen asked how they knew the way, Petra only said, "It's pulling. Hasn't stopped since the pass."

By the third day the air itself had changed. Warmer, though they were higher than the snowline. It smelled faintly of hot iron and, beneath that, something sweeter—incense, or amber, or memory of summer. Petra stopped at the rim of a rise and pressed a palm flat to the stone.

"It's close," they whispered. "Whatever it is. Orsen—it's *hurt*. I can feel it hurting."

He could have told them then. He should have. He opened his mouth to say *I've been here, I know what's at the bottom of this mountain, turn back now while turning back is still possible*—

—and below them, through a vent in the rock, a light breathed.

Red-gold, guttering, vast. Once. Then dimmer. Like a heart deciding whether to beat again.

Petra went white. Their hand found Orsen's sleeve and gripped it, and he felt them trembling—not with fear. With recognition. Whatever called them up this mountain had just felt them feel it, and answered.

"Oh," Petra breathed. "Oh, it's *alive*. Orsen, whatever's down there—it's alive, and it's dying, and it's been waiting for—"

"For us," Orsen said hoarsely. Because there was no point hiding it now. "For someone. Petra, listen to me—what's down there is the reason the Order hunts people like you. It's the reason I—" He stopped. Started again. "I helped load those wagons. I know where they were going. I've stood at that rim."

Petra stared at him, and he watched understanding arrive in their face piece by piece, like a map assembling itself from fragments—the cages, the caldera, the pull they'd felt since the pass.

"Then you're going to show me the way down," Petra said.

Below them, in the dark of the earth, the dying light flared once more—and held.
