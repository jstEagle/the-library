## 6. The Guttering

The pull had been growing for three days.

Petra felt it in their sternum first—a warmth like a coal wrapped in wool, faint and patient, tugging them off the salt road and into the high country where the maps went soft at the edges. Orsen had stopped asking why they turned when they turned. He simply followed, which was its own kind of language, and Petra was learning to read it: the set of his shoulders when he approved of a route, the way his hand drifted to the knife at his hip when the wind changed.

By the fourth morning the warmth had become a voice—not words, not yet, but a pressure, a leaning, as if the whole mountain range were a door and something behind it had heard them coming.

"She's close," Petra said.

Orsen looked up from the scree field, squinting against the glare. "You've started saying *she*."

Petra hadn't noticed. They turned the observation over like a stone found in a boot. "I suppose I have."

They climbed through ash-pine forest that grew crooked and sparse, then above the treeline into country of black rock and rust-colored lichen, where the air tasted of sulfur and old iron. The pull strengthened until it wasn't a pull at all but a summons, and Petra walked with their head bowed like a pilgrim, one hand pressed flat to their own chest to hold the heat in.

Near dusk they crested a ridge of slag and glass—glass, fused by heat older than any kingdom—and looked down into the caldera.

It was vast. A bowl of shadowed stone two miles across, its floor veined with channels that had once run molten and now held only a dull red memory of fire, cooling toward gray. And at the center, coiled around a spire of basalt like a moth too tired to fly, lay the dragon.

Even dying, she was enormous beyond sense. Her scales were the color of deep water over slate, dulled now with a gray bloom, cracked along her flanks like drying mud. Her wings lay folded against her sides, the membranes translucent where the light caught them, shot through with holes that had not healed. She did not lift her head. But her eye opened—an amber disc the size of a cartwheel—and it found Petra on the ridge with a precision that made their skin prickle.

*You came,* said the pressure in their chest, and this time it was almost words. *At last, you came.*

"Petra." Orsen's hand closed on their arm. His face had gone the color of the ash. He had seen war; he had served the Inquisition; he had caged children, he'd told them, weeping on the third night with his hands shaking around a cookfire. Nothing in all of it had prepared him for this. "That's—" He couldn't finish it. There was no word big enough in the common tongue.

"I know," Petra said softly. "I know what she is."

They descended together, down switchbacks of shattered stone, the air growing warmer with every turn though no fire burned anywhere they could see. It took an hour to reach the caldera floor. It took another twenty minutes to cross it, and every step of those twenty minutes Petra felt the dragon watching them, not with menace but with something worse: hope.

Up close she smelled of hot metal and rain and, beneath it, something sweet and sad that Petra could not name until they stood close enough to touch the basalt spire she was coiled around—and understood.

The spire was full of light.

Not firelight. Not sunlight. It glowed from within like a lantern made of everything at once, and inside the glow, moving slow as fish under ice, were shapes. Faces. Hands. A woman laughing with her head thrown back. A boy running through wheat. An old man teaching small fingers a knot. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Memories, whole and luminous, packed into the stone like seeds in a granary.

And threaded among them, unmistakable, a page of handwriting Petra would know anywhere in the world or out of it. Their mother's slanted script, the ink brown where she'd mixed her own mordant, the letters marching up the margin the way she always let them drift—

Petra made a sound they didn't recognize as coming from themselves.

"No," they whispered. "No, she was *burned*. I saw the—"

*The pyre took her body,* the dragon said, and now the voice came clear, low and vast, resonating in Petra's ribs more than their ears. *But the gifted do not end at the body, child of the valley. What they are returns to me. What they loved, what they remembered, what they carried—it comes home to the flame.*

Vess lifted her head at last. It rose and rose, on a neck longer than the mill back home, until her skull blotted out a quarter of the darkening sky. Her breath washed over them, warm and smelling of the same sweetness, and Petra realized with a lurch what the sweetness was: it was the smell of other people's joy, stored so long it had become perfume.

"You're dying," Petra said. Their voice sounded very small under all that sky.

*I am guttering.* The great eye dimmed as if to demonstrate. *I am the last flame, and a flame must be fed. For a thousand years the gifted fed me gladly—each generation adding its memories to mine, and my fire keeping the borders of the world sealed against what waits beyond them. You have heard the old stories. The war before names. The things that came down from the north when the sky tore.*

"The wardens' tales," Orsen said hoarsely. "We were told they were stories."

*Everything your Inquisition knows, it learned from the ones it burned.* Vess's lip curled from teeth like plowshares, though there was no anger in it—or none aimed at them. *Do you understand yet? Come closer. Look into the flame and understand.*

Petra stepped forward. Orsen caught their hand and did not let go, and together they approached the dragon's breast, where between two great scales a hollow showed—and in the hollow, a flame no larger than a candle.

It was the most terrible thing Petra had ever seen. That candle-flame, guttering in a hollow the size of a cathedral, flickering with each of the dragon's slow breaths, and every flicker sending a shudder through the mountain, through the ground, through the strange warm thread that ran from the caldera into Petra's own chest and had been pulling them for four days.

*Your mother is here,* Vess said gently. *She has been here eleven years. She sings, sometimes, when the flame sinks lowest. She does not know she is dead. She only knows she is keeping something alive.*

Petra's knees nearly went. Orsen's grip kept them upright.

"They told us," Petra managed. "The criers. They said the hunt was for the gifted—that we were *dangerous*, that we were—"

*Fuel.* The word fell like a hammer. *Yes. Your Inquisition does not cage the gifted to punish them, child. It cages them to harvest them. Every gifted soul taken is brought north in salt-iron, and their memories are drawn out and poured into the flame by men who call themselves keepers. Hale knows. Hale has always known. The hunt is not a hunt. It is a harvest, and it has been running since before your mother was born.*

The caldera seemed to tilt. Petra sat down hard on the warm stone and did not remember deciding to sit.

All those years in the valley. All those years of hiding, of burning letters, of making invisibility into a personality because visibility meant the wagons, the cages, the salt-iron. They had thought they were hiding from hatred. Hatred would have been almost bearable. This was worse: they had been hiding from a *larder*. Their gift—the thing their mother died for, the thing that made the land answer them outside the wards—was valuable the way a field is valuable, the way a well is valuable. Something to be farmed.

And their mother. Eleven years in the stone. Singing to a candle.

"She wouldn't have wanted this," Petra said. The words came out cracked. "She wrote about the dragon in her letters. She *revered*—she said the flame was a covenant, not a—she never would have—"

*No,* Vess agreed. *She gave freely, as the gifted once all gave freely. That is why her memories burn brighter than any taken by force. Forced memories feed poorly. They smolder. It is why I die faster now than in any age before—the Inquisition takes more souls every year, and feeds me less and less. Fear makes thin fuel.* The great head lowered until the amber eye hung above them both, near enough that Petra could see their own reflection curved in it, tiny and distorted. *So you see the shape of it now. The hunt will not stop while I live, because I am the excuse. And when I die, the hunt will not stop either, because then the border fails, and the things beyond it come down, and men like Hale will find new work.*

"Then what do you want from us?" Orsen asked. His voice was steady in the way Petra had learned meant it cost him everything to keep it so.

The dragon regarded him for a long moment. Then she turned back to Petra.

*What I want,* she said, *is nothing you owe me. But there is a choice ahead of you, and I will not pretend it is a kind one. My flame can be refed. Not by force—from willing hands, freely given, it burns true again. Memories are the currency. Each one given is gone forever from the giver. A taste. A song. A face. A life's worth, if a life's worth is offered.*

Petra looked at the spire. At the faces swimming in the glow. At the brown-inked page drifting among them like a leaf on still water.

Their mother's laughter, preserved. Their mother's voice, singing to a dying light. Everything of her that the pyre hadn't touched, kept safe in stone for eleven years—and kept safe, Petra understood suddenly, coldly, *because the Inquisition had never gotten their hands on her memories to dilute them.*

Grief had been weaponized. Grief had been weaponized for centuries, and they had only just been handed the bill.

Above the caldera rim, far off, a sound reached them: the thin metallic cry of a hawk. Or something shaped like a hawk's cry, repeated twice, answered twice—the signal Orsen had gone gray-faced hearing once before, on the road out of the valley.

He was already turning toward the sound, hand on his knife. "Salt-iron," he said quietly. "That's a cage-train signal. Two days out, maybe less."

Vess's flame guttered, and the whole mountain shuddered.

*Decide quickly, children,* the last dragon said. *I do not think we have until spring.*
