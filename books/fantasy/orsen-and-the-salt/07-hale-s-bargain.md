## 7. Hale's Bargain

Orsen smelled the trap before he saw it.

That was the thing about salt—it remembered. It had soaked into his skin during those years in the Inquisition's service, crusted white in the creases of his knuckles, worked so deep into the old burn scars along his jaw that no amount of washing ever pulled it out again. And now, crouched at the mouth of the caldera's lower path with the wind coming up off the ash flats, he caught it: not the clean mineral bite of the mountain, but the sweeter reek of salt-iron, of cages, of the particular brine they packed around witch-chains to keep a gift from flaring.

He had spent six years learning that smell. He knew it the way a man knows the footsteps of the thing that hunts him.

"Petra." He kept his voice low, though the wind would have eaten anything louder anyway. "Wake up."

She stirred against the warm stone where she'd curled beside him, her map-satchel still clutched to her chest even in sleep—she held it the way other people held children or prayer beads, like something that would break if let go. Her eyes opened all at once, no fog of drowsiness, and he watched her read his face in a heartbeat, the way she read everything: terrain first, threat second, feeling last.

"The wind changed," he said. "Salt-iron. Coming up from the switchbacks below."

She was already moving, rolling her bedroll with hands that didn't shake, because Petra Joubert never shook—not where anyone could see. "How far?"

"A few hours. Maybe less if she pushed her escort through the night."

"She?"

"Hale." He said the name flatly, the way you'd set down something heavy. "It'll be Hale. She doesn't send others for what matters."

Petra looked at him for a long moment. Below them, through a seam in the caldera wall, the light of Vess's failing flame pulsed—a slow, guttering amber, like a lamp running out of oil in an otherwise dark room. It had been brighter yesterday. That was the arithmetic Orsen couldn't stop doing: every hour they hesitated, the fire dimmed, and somewhere in the world the gifted dead grew quieter, their stored memories burning down to nothing.

And somewhere behind them, Inquisitor Hale climbed.

* * *

They did not run. That was the mistake—or the choice; by then Orsen could no longer tell them apart. Running meant abandoning Vess, and Petra would not do it, and he found he could not either, which told him something about himself he wasn't ready to examine. So instead they took the high shelf above the caldera rim—the one place with a view of both the dragon and the approach—and waited, because Petra said, in her maddening cartographer's calm, "If we know exactly when she arrives, we know exactly how long we have."

Orsen knew how long they had. He'd known since the moment he smelled the salt-iron. Hale traveled fast and arrived slow, letting the fear do half her work before she ever showed her face.

She came up the final stretch of trail alone.

That was the performance of it, of course. Orsen counted four shapes in the rocks below—crossbowmen, patient as furniture—and knew there were more he couldn't see. Hale always traveled heavy and walked light. She was a broad woman gone gray at the temples, built like a door, wearing the plain gray wool of her office with no ornament except the small iron sun at her throat. Her face was the face of someone who had once been kind and had decided, deliberately, methodically, to stop.

"Corporal Orsen Vane," she called up, and her voice carried the way voices carry off stone, doubled and strange. "Deserted the Third Inquisitorial Company, autumn of the year the miller's boy burned. Eleven years of service before that. I have your file memorized. Would you like to hear the commendations? There were nine."

"Don't," Orsen said.

"I'm not here for you." She stopped at the base of the shelf, close enough that he could see the wind pulling strands loose from her braid, and made no move toward the crossbows below. "I'm here to offer you your life back."

The words landed in Orsen's chest like a dropped stone. Beside him, Petra had gone very still—that particular stillness, the one he'd learned on the road between the valley and the mountains, the stillness of someone listening with their whole body.

"You can't offer that," he said. "You're not the crown."

"I'm the hand the crown uses when it wants something done quietly." Hale folded her arms. "Full pardon. Reinstatement, if you want it—which you won't, but it's offered. A name scrubbed from the deserters' rolls. Your mother gets the pension she was denied after your 'death.' You walk out of these mountains a free man, Corporal, and no one in the realm will ever say the word *deserter* over your grave."

"And the price."

Hale's eyes went past him. Not to Petra—to the glow seeping up through the caldera seam, the dying pulse of the last dragon-fire in the world.

"The price is you finish what you started," she said. "You were good at it, Orsen. That's why I came myself. Do you remember Marisol Dane? The glassblower's daughter in Corren's Ford. Eight years old. You carried her to the cage yourself because her parents fought you and you were stronger."

Orsen's mouth went dry. He had told Petra about Marisol Dane on the salt roads, three nights ago, in the worst hour of the confession, the one that had nearly ended everything between them before it had properly begun. He had told her because he'd decided that if she was going to love him—and he was beginning to believe she might, in whatever way people like them were allowed to love—he would not do it carrying hidden weight. He had laid every child, every cage, every screaming village night out on the ground between them like a man laying out stolen goods.

Petra had listened. Petra had not forgiven him yet either. But she had stayed.

Now Hale stood below and used the same memory as a hook.

"I remember her," Orsen said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "I remember all of them. That's rather the point of me now."

"Good." Hale nodded, brisk, almost approving. "Then you'll understand what I'm about to tell you, because you've seen the ledgers too."

* * *

What she told them, she told plainly, without theater, and somehow that made it worse.

"There was a war," Hale said. "Before your mother was born, girl—before anyone alive remembers it. The Kessarin incursion. The eastern host came over the Saltspine with fire-wrought engines and they did not stop at borders, because borders meant nothing to them. Two million dead. Whole provinces unmade. You've seen the monuments and thought they were for plague years. They weren't."

"We know the story," Petra said quietly. "Every valley child knows the story. The dragon ended it."

"One dragon," Hale corrected. "One dragon, and the flame she carries isn't hers. It never was. It's compounded—from every gifted soul the old orders ever gave her. Centuries of them. Their memories are the fuel, and the fire is a ward the size of the eastern sky, and while it burns, nothing crosses the Saltspine that shouldn't." She paused, and for one moment something moved under the deliberate flatness of her face—something that might have been exhaustion, or grief, or simply the weight of eleven years of files. "Vess is dying. The flame gutters. And when it goes out—and it will go out within the season, perhaps within the month—the ward dies with it. And the things beyond the Saltspine have had two hundred years to learn patience."

"So the hunt," Orsen said slowly. The shape of it assembled itself in his mind with horrible clarity, all the pieces he'd handled for years without ever being allowed to see the whole. "The cages. The collections. It was never punishment."

"It was harvest," Hale said. "Yes. The gifted don't die in our custody, Corporal. I'd hoped you'd worked that out—it would spare us both some self-loathing. They're taken to the deep vaults, and their memories are taken, and the memories feed the flame. Every child you ever caged is alive, Orsen. Asleep, whole, tended. Only the remembering was harvested." She turned her gaze fully on Petra for the first time. "Your mother's memories are in that fire right now, girl. Have been for sixteen years. You've been sleeping warm inside your mother's sacrifice your entire life and never knew it."

Orsen heard Petra's breath catch—a small sound, quickly mastered, but he heard it. He had learned to hear her.

"That's not mercy," Petra said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, at her sides, were not. "That's farming people."

"It's arithmetic," Hale said. "Two million dead against a few thousand sleeping dreamers. Show me a better sum and I'll resign my office tomorrow." She looked back up at Orsen, and now the iron came into her voice, the voice that had commanded him across a decade of bad nights. "But the arithmetic has changed. The flame is failing faster than the harvest can replenish it. We need a great offering—one soul, close, willing or not, with a gift strong enough to matter. Hers." A nod toward Petra. "Bring her down to the vaults and everything I offered you stands. Refuse—" and here she spread her hands, almost gently, "—and I take her anyway, and you hang as a deserter, and the flame burns down on schedule, and in twenty years your grandchildren learn what a Kessarin engine does to a city. Duty, Corporal. Mercy. Survival. For eleven years they pointed the same direction and you never had to think. Look at them now. Look at where they point."

Orsen looked.

He looked at Hale, who had taught him to read a battlefield and had never once lied to him, and who was telling the truth even now—that was the unforgivable part, that she was honest, that the war was real, that the flame mattered, that the sums were real sums. He looked at Petra, who stood at the edge of the shelf with the wind pulling at her hair and her chin lifted, already composing in her head, he could see it, already mapping the escape route, the fallback, the cost. He looked down through the seam at the guttering amber light, at the last dragon in the world burning down like a candle in an empty church, fueled by the stolen remembering of the sleeping and the dead—including a woman named for the sea, burned in a village square while her child hid in a root cellar and memorized the shape of the flames.

Duty pointed down the mountain, into the vaults, toward the harvest.

Mercy pointed at sleeping children in deep rooms, and at provinces that must never burn again.

Survival pointed everywhere and nowhere, and had never once, in Orsen's experience, been worth following alone.

"Corporal," Hale said, softer now. "History doesn't ask what you felt. It asks whose side you stood on when the sums were paid. I am offering you the winning side."

"No," Orsen said.

Hale blinked. For the first time since the trailhead, something genuine crossed her face—surprise, quick and unguarded, like a crack in a wall.

"You're wrong about the sums," Orsen said. His heart was hammering and his voice was steady and he understood, finally, standing on the ash shelf with his whole life balanced on the edge of the caldera, that these two facts could coexist. "You've been making them wrong for two hundred years. You count the memories as fuel and the people as vessels, and you never once put a value on what a person *is* while they're remembering. Petra's gift isn't a reservoir, Hale. It's not oil. It answers her. It *chose* her. And you want to haul the only living thing the flame has ever chosen down into a vault and bleed it dry by force, because force is the only tool you brought." He stepped forward, to the very lip of the shelf, and the wind took his words and threw them down the mountain. "There's a third option. There always was. You just never trained yourself to see it, because seeing it means admitting the cages were never necessary."

"And what option is that?" Hale asked, and her hand had drifted, casually, toward the signal horn at her belt.

"Ask the dragon," Orsen said. "She's been waiting two hundred years for someone to."

For a moment nobody moved. The crossbows below shifted in their hiding places. The wind moaned through the caldera seam, and beneath it, faint and rhythmic, the failing flame pulsed its slow amber heartbeat.

Then Hale raised the horn to her lips—

—and Petra stepped off the shelf into the open air, and did not fall.

Light took her. Not firelight, not sunlight—something older, rising out of the rock itself, answering her the way it had answered her on the salt roads, the land recognizing its own. She descended the caldera wall in a slow fall of gold, and Orsen heard Hale shout orders, heard the crossbows, heard himself shouting Petra's name and jumping after her into the light—

—and below them, deep in the caldera, the dying flame flared once, bright as dawn, as if it had heard its name called at last.
