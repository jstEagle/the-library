## 8. Memory Is a Currency

The caldera breathed.

Petra felt it before they understood it—a slow tidal pull in the air itself, the way the whole bowl of stone seemed to inhale at dawn and exhale at dusk. Vess lay coiled at its heart like a drowned ship half-buried in black sand, her scales the color of old copper gone green with age, and from between her ribs came the light: a flame no larger than a candle's, guttering in a hollow of her chest as though the world had forgotten to feed it.

*It has not forgotten,* Vess said. Her voice arrived inside Petra's skull the way warmth arrives through a wall—not heard so much as absorbed. *Nothing forgets. It is only taken.*

They had been three days in the caldera now. Three days of watching Orsen pace the rim with his hand never far from his knife, of hauling water up from the sulfur-stained spring, of learning the rhythm of a dying god. Petra sat cross-legged on the warm sand near Vess's jaw, close enough to feel the heat of that small flame against their face, their mother's single preserved page spread carefully on a flat stone between them—the one letter the fire at the mill had missed, rescued by accident or by something older than accident.

"Tell me again," Petra said. "Slowly this time."

Vess's eye was the size of a wagon wheel, gold-shot, filmed at the edges like an egg about to spoil. It rotated with terrible patience until it regarded them fully.

*The flame is memory,* she said. *That has always been its fuel. When your kind first learned to make fire that did not eat wood—fire that ate meaning instead—I burned on the thoughts of the gifted. Every joy surrendered willingly. Every grief laid down like a burden at a shrine.* A plume of smoke curled from her nostrils, thin and gray. *In return, my fire kept the border wars ended. Kept the cold out of the northern passes. Kept the things beneath the salt plains asleep. You have lived your whole life inside the shelter of other people's memories, little cartographer. You simply never knew the price of the roof over your head.*

"And when the hunt began—" Petra started.

*When the hunt began, the Inquisition found a more efficient harvest.* The dragon's great head shifted, and sand hissed away from her neck like a landslide in miniature. *Why wait for gift? Why ask permission? Take the memories whole. Take them screaming. The flame does not care how it is fed. It only cares that it eats.*

Petra looked down at their hands. They had been thinking, since the night at the mill, that they understood what was being asked of them. Feed the dragon. Give up pieces of themselves. They had rehearsed sacrifices in the dark of their bedroll—this friendship abandoned, that summer surrendered—and each imagined loss had felt survivable, a coin paid from a purse that still held others.

"How much," they asked quietly, "is left?"

*You may look,* Vess said. *I will show you what I see. But know that seeing cannot be undone either.*

She exhaled, and the smoke did not rise—it sank, pooling around Petra's knees like fog, and within it images bloomed.

Petra saw themselves as the flame saw them: a figure made of layered light, strata upon strata, like the cross-sections of earth they drew in their maps. At the center, dense and dark and oldest, sat their mother—not the woman, but every moment of her that Petra had kept. The way she'd hummed while grinding grain. The shape of her shadow on the valley wall at evening. The last thing she said before the Inquisition's men took her, which Petra had never told another living soul. Around that core, ring after ring after ring: Orsen's laugh on the pass road. The taste of the first plum of their ninth summer. Their father's hands teaching theirs to hold a stylus. The smell of rain on hot stone. Ten thousand small eternities, stacked like pages.

And around all of it, thinning toward the edges, a faint gray haze where the layers had already begun to blur—three days of the caldera's hunger pulling gently at them even without consent, the way a tide pulls at anything standing too long in shallow water.

"You're already taking some," Petra said. Not accusing. Just noting it, the way they'd note a river changing course.

*I am dying,* Vess said simply. *A dying thing draws breath. Forgive me. I have tried to hold my mouth closed for forty years.*

The smoke dispersed. Petra sat very still, looking at the page on the stone—their mother's handwriting, cramped and hurried, written by candlelight in the years before the burning. *If they come for me, do not run to me. Run past me. There is more map than any of them have ever dreamed.*

Their mother had always talked like that. Like a person leaving instructions for a journey she knew she wouldn't take.

"If I give you a memory," Petra said, "it's gone? Truly gone? Not hidden, not sleeping—gone?"

*Gone as a spent coin is gone. You will know that you once had something there—you will feel the shape of the absence, the way you feel the shape of a missing tooth—but the thing itself will be ash. Others may remember it for you. You will not.*

Petra thought of Orsen, down at the spring, scrubbing rust off his armor with the grim thoroughness of a man punishing himself. If Petra gave away their first sight of him at the gate—that scarred stranger with eyes that recognized too much—would they still trust him afterward? Would trust survive if its foundation were missing? Or would some new trust have to be built on nothing, on faith alone, on the testimony of a person who no longer existed: the one who remembered?

"There's no other way," they said. "No substitute. No trick."

*I have searched for a trick for forty years,* Vess said, and for the first time her voice carried something that was almost humor, dry as bone meal. *I am a very large creature. I have had a great deal of time to think. There is no trick. There is only arithmetic.*

* * *

The first memory Petra gave was small, deliberately small, the way you test ice at the edge of a frozen lake.

They chose the taste of the plum.

They knelt at Vess's jaw, pressed both palms flat against the warm scale of her cheek, and did what the dragon showed them: held the memory in the front of the mind, bright and complete, and then—rather than pushing it away or clinging to it—simply stopped holding on. Let it fall forward into the heat.

It went out of them like water through open fingers. There was a moment of vertigo, of reaching for something that answered with silence, and then the flame flared.

Petra actually heard it this time—a soft rushing roar, the sound a bonfire makes when wind finds it—and light spilled up through the seams of Vess's scales, tracing the great channels of her body like sunrise flooding a canyon. For three heartbeats the entire caldera glowed amber, and the shadows of the rocks wheeled and shrank, and Petra felt—

—they felt *better*. That was the obscene part. The giving didn't hurt. It felt like setting down a pack after a long climb, like the first breath after surfacing. Somewhere in them a plum tasted of sunlight and juice and a nine-year-old's sticky fingers, and they could feel it going, going, and it felt *wonderful*, and that wonderfulness was the most frightening thing they had ever known.

*Yes,* Vess murmured. *That is why the willing ones gave so much, once. It does not feel like losing. It feels like relief. The flame is kind in the taking. Kindness is how it ruins you.*

Petra sat back, breathing hard, probing the place where the plum had been. The absence was smooth-edged already. They knew a fruit had mattered to them once. They could not say why.

"That's one," they whispered.

*One,* Vess agreed. *And I will burn perhaps a day longer for it. Perhaps two, if the wind is kind.*

The mathematics of it settled over Petra like snowfall. One plum. Two days. And Vess needed years—decades—to rebuild enough fire to matter, to end the hunt, to do whatever it was dying dragons needed to do before the war below the salt plains woke again. To save the realm with pocket change would take ten thousand plums. Petra counted what they had. They had a childhood. They had a mother. They had a man at the spring whose face they loved.

They began to understand why the Inquisition harvested instead of asking. Not because asking was slower. Because asking required someone to keep choosing, over and over, and no one could keep choosing forever. Eventually the arithmetic won.

"No," Petra said aloud, to themselves, to the caldera. "I'll choose every time. That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

Vess said nothing, but the great eye watched them with something that might have been pity, or respect, or grief.

* * *

By evening Petra had given four.

The plum. A song their mother used to hum while grinding grain—they gave it and immediately mourned it doubly, because now neither of them knew it, and somewhere in the world a melody ceased entirely to exist. An afternoon of mapping the eastern ridge alone at fifteen, the best solitude of their life, given gladly because solitude was one thing they could afford to lose; they were done being alone. And the smell of rain on hot stone, which hurt worse than expected, because it turned out that smell had been braided through everything else they remembered, and its removal left small holes everywhere, like moths in wool.

Each time, the fire brightened. Each time, Petra felt thinner. Not weaker—thinner. As though they were a drawn map and someone had lifted ink off the lines, leaving the paper intact but the markings fainter. They caught themselves staring at their own hands trying to recall why they'd been proud of them once. They knew they had been proud. The reason was a smooth blank space.

Orsen climbed the slope at dusk carrying bread and the last of the dried fish, and stopped dead when he saw them sitting by the flame. His eyes went narrow, soldier-quick, reading the light on their face.

"You've been feeding her."

"A few small things." Petra heard their own voice and disliked how serene it sounded. Serenity, they realized, was also a symptom. "It doesn't hurt. That's the trap. It feels like relief."

Orsen set the food down slowly and crouched in front of them, taking their chin in his rough hand, turning their face to the last of the daylight. Studying them the way he studied terrain.

"Petra." His voice was low. "How many can you give before there's no one home to stop?"

Petra opened their mouth to answer—and the answer arrived unbidden, unchosen, rising from the dragon's mind into theirs with the gentle inevitability of the tide:

*Hale has entered the mountains,* Vess said. *She carries the cage. And she is not coming for me.*
