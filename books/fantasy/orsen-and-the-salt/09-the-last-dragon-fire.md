## 9. The Last Dragon-Fire

The caldera woke to the smell of iron.

Petra felt it before they heard it—a wrongness in the stone beneath their knees, a tremor like a held breath. They had spent three days at Vess's side, learning the shape of her dying: the way her flame guttered low in the hollow of her chest, a coal wrapped in grey scales, the way she breathed in long, labored pulls as though the air itself had grown too heavy to lift.

Now the air was full of salt and iron and men.

"They're coming up the eastern rim," Orsen said. He stood at the caldera's edge with his back to the wind, and in the grey dawn light Petra could see every scar the salt had left on him—the pale ridges across his knuckles, the burn that pulled his jaw taut when he spoke. He had his sword drawn, but he held it low, the way a man holds a tool he no longer believes in. "Two dozen. Hale's vanguard. She'll have the cage with her."

"The cage." Petra's hands closed around the page in their coat. One page, saved from the fire by accident or by something wiser than accident—their mother's handwriting, slanted and sure, describing the valley in spring. *The river forgets nothing it carries,* she had written. *I am trying to be like the river.* Petra had read it so many times in the dark of the last three nights that they knew it better than their own heartbeat.

Vess stirred behind them. The dragon's eye opened—one vast amber disc, clouded now at the edges like an old man's sight—and fixed on Petra with terrible gentleness.

*You feel them,* she said. Her voice was not sound so much as heat, arriving directly in the blood. *They come for what I hold. For your mother's memory, among others. The Inquisition has fed me for two hundred years, child. Every gifted one they burned, they burned *into* me. That was always the purpose of the pyres.*

"I know," Petra said. "You told me."

*And you have been feeding me since we arrived. Small things. A taste of honey-bread. Your grandmother's song.* The dragon's breath moved over them, warm as a hearth, terrible as a confession. *I can feel where those memories were. You are thinner there. Like cloth worn through.*

Petra looked down at their own hands. They still remembered the taste of honey-bread—or they remembered remembering it, a fact about themselves rather than a possession. It was like standing in a room where a picture had been taken down from the wall; they could see the pale rectangle where it had hung, and nothing else.

"It's not enough," they said quietly. "I can feel it's not enough. You'll gutter out before midwinter."

*Then give me more.*

"No." Orsen turned. His voice cracked like ice underfoot. "No, Petra. Look at yourself. Three days ago you laughed at my terrible singing and now you can't remember why. This is what Hale wants—not to capture you, to *spend* you. You'll give until there's nothing left standing in your own house."

"And if I don't?" Petra rose to face him. Below them, faint and growing, came the ring of boots on scree. "If I keep myself whole and let her die? Then the fire goes out, and whatever war this flame once ended comes back, and Hale wins anyway. Everyone I've ever been, spent either way."

Orsen crossed to them. He took their face in his scarred hands, and his eyes were wet and furious. "There has to be another way."

"There is," said Vess.

Both of them turned. The dragon had raised her head, and the ember in her chest pulsed once, twice, like a heart deciding.

*The memories I hold are not only mine to spend. They are *held*. Given. And a gift may be refused.* Her gaze settled on Petra, and something ancient moved behind the amber. *Your mother's memory lives in me, child. But it did not come to me freely—it was torn from her at the stake. Torn things leave threads. And you carry the other end of every thread she left. Inheritance runs both directions.*

Petra's hand went to their coat. To the page.

"You want me to give you the letter."

*I want you to give me *her*. Not the memory I hold—your memory of her. The inheritance itself. Break the line, and everything the hunt has harvested from your bloodline returns to its source. Me. Whole. All at once.* The dragon's voice dropped to something almost human, almost pleading. *But understand what it costs. Not your memories of her alone. The gift itself. The land will stop answering you. You will be only what you are—and you will never again feel the world lean toward you when you walk.*

Petra stood very still.

Below, the first soldiers crested the far rim, small and black against the burning sky of dawn.

* * *

Orsen saw the Inquisitor before she saw him.

Hale climbed the caldera path with the unhurried stride of a woman who had never once doubted her right to arrive anywhere. Her salt-iron cage rode on a cart behind her, its bars humming, and six hunters flanked her with the coiled, ready silence of men who had done this many times. Behind them, the eastern sky was going the color of a wound.

He stepped out onto the path alone, sword point-down in the scree, hands open.

"Hale."

She stopped. For a moment something flickered across her face—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. Grief's poor cousin.

"Deserter," she said. "I wondered which way you'd run. I told them you'd come here. I told them the broken ones always go looking for the thing that broke them." Her eyes went past him, up the slope, to the small figure standing at the great grey head of the dying dragon. "Is that Joubert's whelp?"

"Petra," Orsen said. "Their name is Petra."

"The mother burned clean in '89," Hale said, and her voice was flat as a ledger. "Best fire I ever lit. Took four hours." She said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse—said it the way a mason reports mortar drying. "The daughter—"

"*Child*, Hale. Not daughter. Not whelp. A person."

"A fuel source." She gestured, and the hunters fanned out around him, cutting him off from the slope. "Stand aside, Orsen. Full pardon. I keep my word—you know that much about me. Your name cleared, your rank restored, a pension. Go home to somewhere green. Forget all this."

Orsen looked at her for a long time. He thought about home—about a farmhouse he barely remembered, a father who'd sold him to the Inquisition at fourteen because the alternative was worse, the barracks where he'd learned to sleep through the screaming. He thought about the children he had helped carry to the cages, how heavy they were, how light. He thought about a voice in a dark tent saying *you know what I am* and meaning *you know what I am too.*

"No," he said.

Hale sighed. "I was afraid you'd say that."

She snapped her fingers, and the hunters moved.

What followed was not a battle; Orsen had been a soldier too long to call it that. It was a negotiation conducted in steel, brief and brutal, on a slope of loose stone above a dying god. He gave ground the way he'd been taught—trading space for time, letting them come to him, using the scree. He broke one man's wrist with the pommel and put another into the rocks and felt his shoulder tear open along an old scar, and through all of it he kept his body between the hunters and the slope, kept turning, kept fighting, because that was the one vow he had left and he meant to keep it with his whole ruined self.

Above him, he heard Petra begin to speak.

* * *

Petra knelt before Vess with the page open in both hands.

The words swam. Their mother's voice rose off the paper the way it always did—*the river forgets nothing it carries*—and Petra understood, finally, what they had been doing all these years. Mapping the unmapped. Hiding the gift. Keeping the valley's secrets and their own. They had thought it was survival. It had been hoarding. They had been a vault with a person inside, guarding a treasure they were too afraid to spend.

Boots on the rim. Shouting. Orsen's roar, cut short by a clash of steel. None of it mattered. All of it mattered. There was no time left to be careful, and careful had never once kept anyone safe.

"Mama," Petra said aloud, to the page, to the dragon, to the ash of a woman four hours in the fire. "You wrote that the river forgets nothing it carries. I think you were wrong. I think the river forgets everything except what it gives away."

They pressed the page to their chest, and reached down the thread.

It was exactly as Vess had promised—not a wall to break but a thread to pull, running from their heart backward through time, through the valley wards and the mill and the compass spinning uselessly at the valley's edge, back to a kitchen where a woman with ink-stained fingers had taught them to draw borders that weren't there. Petra pulled, and the thread pulled back, and everything their mother had ever been came flooding toward them like water finding its level—

—and they let it pass *through*.

That was the trick. That was the mercy of it. They did not lose the memory; they refused to *keep* it. Every recollection of her—the warmth of her hands, the song she hummed while grinding ink, the way she said *petra* like a blessing and a warning both—they held each one up to the light of the thread and let it flow onward, out of them, into the dragon, unspent, ungrieved, given rather than taken. And with the memories went the gift itself, the land's answering lean, the compass-sense, the inheritance entire—flowing out of them like a river finally reaching the sea.

Vess blazed.

Not the guttering coal of the last three days but a furnace, a sunrise, a pillar of gold fire roaring up through the grey scales and out of her throat in a cry that cracked stones on the far rim. Heat washed the caldera. The hunters fell back, shielding their faces. The salt-iron cage on its cart glowed cherry-red and then white and then simply ceased, its nullifying hum swallowed by a sound older than nullification.

Hale stood her ground. Petra would remember that, afterward—that the Inquisitor did not run. She stared up at the blazing dragon with an expression Petra had no name for, some ruin of certainty, and then Vess lowered her great head and breathed out, gently, a single curl of flame that passed over the woman like a parent's hand over a child's hair, taking nothing but the hunt itself. When the light faded, Hale's cage was ash and her warrant was ash and the authority behind them was ash, and she stood empty-handed on the scorched rock, a small grey figure in a world that no longer needed her.

The hunters fled. Orsen leaned on his sword, bleeding, laughing, alive.

And Petra knelt in the firelight feeling—for the first time since childhood—entirely, ordinarily, magnificently *small*. No gift. No inheritance. No duty humming in their blood. Just a person on a mountain, holding a blank piece of paper where a letter used to be, next to a scarred man who had chosen them, beside a dragon made new.

Vess's head swung down. The great amber eye, clear now, bright as a struck match, regarded them both.

*Little cartographer,* she said, warm as a hearth. *You gave away the map of yourself and kept the territory. Do you understand what you've done? The hunt is ended—but the fire I carry now must go somewhere. Two hundred years of the gifted dead are awake in me, and they are asking, all of them, to be brought home.*

Petra looked at the blank page in their hands. Then at Orsen. Then east, toward the valley, toward the realm beyond it—every border on every map suddenly negotiable, every ward dissolving like morning frost.

"Where do we start?" they asked.

Vess spread wings wider than the caldera's mouth.

*Everywhere,* she said. *We start everywhere.*
