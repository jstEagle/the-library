## 10. A Map With No Edges

The ink was made from soot and mountain ash, the way Vess had shown them, ground fine on a slab of black stone until it ran like liquid night. Petra had learned that dragon-fire left a residue unlike any other—finer than lampblack, truer than gall—and that a line drawn with it seemed to remember where it had been. Their mother would have loved it. That was the strange arithmetic of grief now: everything beautiful reminded Petra of her, and nothing hurt the way it used to.

They sat at the long table by the eastern window of a house that had no address, because it stood on no border, because there were no borders anymore to stand upon. Outside, autumn was laying its gold leaf over the foothills where the valley had been, though "the valley" was a word people only used in stories now. The wards had dissolved like sugar in warm water eight months ago, and the land had rushed in to fill the space—not with hunters, as everyone had feared, but with roads, with traders, with children who had never been taught to flinch.

Petra dipped the pen and drew the coastline.

It was the fourth attempt at this map, and the first one that felt honest. The first three had failed for reasons they could not at first name: too much of the old surveying in them, too many straight lines imposed from above, the cartographer's habit of deciding where things should be rather than asking where they were. But a map drawn with gift-ink answered differently. When Petra's hand hovered over a river valley, the ink leaned toward truth the way a compass needle leans toward north—or used to, before compasses became ornaments. The lines found themselves. Coastlines unspooled under the nib like thread pulled from a spool, and Petra followed rather than led.

That was the whole secret of the gift, they understood now. Not power. Attention. Their mother had died for attention—to the land, to the small voices in stone and water—and the crown had called it witchcraft because attention cannot be taxed or conscripted or burned efficiently. Hale had understood it too, in the end. Inquisitor Hale, who had come down from the caldera with ash on her coat and a warrant she never served, who had stood before the assembled hunt in the capital square and said, in a voice like a door closing: *There is nothing left to hunt. The fire is lit. Go home.* Some of them had gone home. Others had stayed to become something else—wardens of the flame, keepers of the memory-vaults where the gifted dead were honored instead of harvested. Hale herself had taken up residence in the caldera's shadow, an old woman learning, slowly and badly, to tend what she had spent her life trying to cage. She wrote to Petra sometimes. Short letters. No apology, because no apology would have been big enough, but each one ended with the same line: *The flame holds. I check it twice a day.*

Petra drew the mountains where Vess slept, and the ink glowed faintly there, warm to the touch even hours later. They had decided not to mark the caldera precisely. Some places deserved to be found only by those who needed finding.

* * *

Orsen came up from the river at midday with his arms full of timber ends and his sleeves rolled past the salt-scars, which he no longer bothered to hide. That was new, still. For years he had worn his scars the way Petra had once worn silence—as armor, as apology, as proof of a sentence already served. Now he let the sun find them. They caught the light like pale rivers running down his forearms, and when children from the settlement downstream asked about them, he told the truth: that he had carried salt-iron for men who caged children, and that he had put it down, and that putting it down was the hardest thing he had ever done until he learned to build instead.

He was building constantly now. It was his version of Petra's mapping—the same instinct, pointed at wood and stone instead of paper. The house had begun as two rooms and refused to stop growing: a porch that wrapped three sides, a workshop with a south-facing window, a loft he swore was for storage and filled instead with books rescued from the Inquisition's burning piles, salvaged by people who had once been ordered to light the fires. He'd framed a second window that morning without being asked, set into the western wall, because he knew—had learned, over months of quiet observation—that Petra liked to watch the light go out of the mountains while they worked.

"Any closer?" he asked, setting the timber down and coming to stand behind their chair. He smelled of sawdust and river water and, underneath it, faintly of brine, the way he always would. Salt got into a man and stayed.

"The coast is done." Petra turned the map so he could see it whole. "Look."

He looked. He was not a man who pretended to understand cartography, and he didn't try; he simply looked the way he looked at everything Petra made, with the particular attention of someone who had once believed he would die without ever seeing anything worth keeping. Then his brow furrowed, and he tapped one scarred finger at the edge of the parchment.

"There's no edge," he said.

"No," Petra said, and smiled. "There isn't."

Every map Petra had ever drawn—in the valley, in secret, on scraps hidden beneath floorboards—had ended somewhere. A border of the known world, a ragged edge where the paper gave out and beyond it the cartographer wrote *here be nothing*, because admitting ignorance was safer than admitting wonder. This map had no such edge. The coastlines ran to the very margins of the great sheet Orsen had stretched on a frame of river birch, and where the paper ended, the ink did not seem to stop so much as continue, as if the world went on whether or not anyone agreed to draw it. Because it did. Because it always had. The valley's forgetting, the crown's borders, the hunts and the wards and the salt-iron cages—all of it had been an argument against a world that refused to be argued out of existence.

"It'll need a frame," Orsen said. "Something good. Oak, maybe. I've got a plank—" He stopped himself, laughed a little. "I'm going to build you a frame for a map with no edges. You see the problem."

"I see you're going to do it anyway," Petra said.

"I am," he admitted, and kissed the top of their head, and went back out into the gold afternoon with his arms full again.

* * *

In the late light, Petra walked the boundary of the property—which was to say, they walked until they felt like stopping, because there was no boundary. The house sat on a rise above the river bend, in country that had belonged to no one and everyone and was now written into no ledger at all. The settlement downstream had offered them a plot, formally, with papers. Orsen had thanked them and declined. "We're done with edges," he'd said, and the elders, most of them former ward-folk who remembered what living inside a line cost, had nodded like people agreeing with a language they'd forgotten they spoke.

Grass came up to Petra's knees. Somewhere near the river, a girl was practicing—Petra could feel it before they heard it, a small bright pressure against the edge of awareness, like a candle held close to the skin. The gifted children born after the dissolution were different. They did not hide. They did not learn to fold themselves small. This one—Maret, the ferryman's daughter—was learning to listen to water, and she practiced loudly, joyfully, the way children practice anything they have never been punished for.

Petra paused and let the feeling wash over them, and thought, not for the first time, of their mother.

She was gone. That was the price, and Petra had paid it with open eyes, kneeling at the caldera's rim with the last preserved page of her letters in both hands while Hale's hunters came up the switchbacks and Vess guttered down to a coal. Feed me, the dragon had said, or feed them. And Petra had chosen—but not the way the choice had been offered. Not their own memories, not the slow thinning Vess had warned of, the self spent coin by coin until nothing remained but the flame. Instead they had given her the inheritance itself: every word of their mother's that survived, the letters half-burned and the one page saved by accident, the voice in the margins, the recipes and warnings and small jokes, the whole preserved country of her. Breaking the inheritance to end the cycle. The fire had taken it all in one breath, and blazed.

And here was the thing Petra had never expected, the thing they still turned over some nights like a stone found in a pocket: it did not feel like loss. Grief, yes—grief forever, grief reorganized but not reduced. But their mother had spent her whole life being kept, hidden, hoarded, burned. To give her to the fire was to give her to the sky. On clear nights, when Vess flew the high passes—and she flew more now, stronger each season, her shadow crossing the moon like a signature—Petra would stand outside and think: *that one's hers.* Not superstition. Just a way of keeping what could not otherwise be kept.

The girl by the river laughed at something the water told her, and the sound carried up the slope, and Petra turned back toward the house.

* * *

Orsen had lit the lamps. Through the western window—the new one—he was visible at the workbench, hunched over the oak plank, sanding it in long patient strokes, building the frame for the map with no edges, because of course he was. The lamplight gilded him. Beyond him, through glass, the mountains went blue and then violet, and high above the highest pass a point of ember-light rose and wheeled, unhurried, alive.

Petra stood in the doorway a long moment with the finished map rolled under one arm, watching the man build a frame for the world.

Then they went inside, unrolled the map across the table, and began—at the margins, where the paper ended and the world did not—to draw what came next.
