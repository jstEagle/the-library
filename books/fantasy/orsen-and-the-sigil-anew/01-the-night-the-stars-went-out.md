## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The child came to Cyrus Hartwell's stall at dusk, which was when frightened children always came, and asked him for a charm against the dark.

Cyrus had been packing up. The evening market in Greyharrow's Lowbridge quarter emptied early these days — the Crown's curfew bells rang at sundown now, and the watchmen had taken to confiscating anything they couldn't tax — but he'd lingered over his inventory the way he always did, touching each little thing he'd made as if it were a bird that might fly off if he stopped paying attention. Luck-charms braided from river-reed. Sleep-knots for colicky infants. A jar of storm-glass beads that fogged pink before rain, which was honest work for a bead, since it did it by itself.

"Please," the child said. She couldn't have been more than eight, all elbows and chapped knuckles, a wool cap pulled down to her eyebrows. "Mam says the stars are going out west of the hills and Da won't come home and I keep having dreams where there's nothing above me at all."

Cyrus set down his inventory ledger. Twenty years of selling charms on street corners teaches a man to hear the shape of a fear underneath the words, and this one had a very particular shape. He knew it intimately. He had worn it himself, once, like a coat cut too close.

"Stars don't go out," he said gently. "They're farther than far. Whatever's happening west of the hills, it isn't your stars."

"Then what is it?"

And there was the question, wasn't it. Cyrus looked at the child — at the way her fingers worried the hem of her cap, at the bruise-colored shadows under her eyes — and felt the old familiar ache behind his sternum, the one that lived where his name used to be. Because he knew what ate light. He knew what it looked like when the sky got hungry. He had put it there himself, or near enough, twenty years ago, on a night he never let himself remember while sober and rarely managed to avoid while drunk.

"First," he said, crouching so their eyes were level, "what's your name?"

"Tansy."

"Tansy. I'm going to sell you a charm, and I want you to understand exactly what you're buying, because I don't sell lies. This—" he took a cord from his tray, a simple braid of reed and river-clay with a single glass bead at its heart, "—is not magic. Magic's been illegal longer than you've been alive, and if anyone tells you different, they're either a fool or a fraud, and I am neither. What this is, is a reminder. You wear it, and every time you touch the bead, you think: *I have been afraid before, and the morning came anyway.* That's all a luck-charm ever was. That's all it needs to be."

Tansy frowned at it. "That's not a real charm?"

"It's the realest kind." He pressed it into her palm and closed her small cold fingers around it. "Two coppers."

She dug in her pocket and came up with one copper and a button shaped like a fish. Cyrus took both. The button went into the tray slot where he kept things he couldn't bear to price.

"Now listen," he said, as she looped the cord over her head. "When you get scared tonight — and you might, dreams are stubborn things — you hold that bead and you breathe out slow, and you count the sounds of Greyharrow instead of counting stars. Cart wheels. Gulls. The bell. The city's louder than the sky, if you let it be."

"Did someone teach you that?"

*Yes,* thought the man who had once been Orsen Vael, who had learned it in a cell with no window, from a woman whose name he also no longer allowed himself. *A long time ago, by someone braver than me.* "An old friend," he said.

Tansy nodded solemnly, the way children do when handed a ritual, and ran off into the blue dusk with her new-old charm bouncing against her chest. Cyrus watched until she turned the corner by the cooper's, and then he stood there a moment longer than he needed to, hand resting on his stall's worn counter, feeling the particular loneliness that comes after being kind.

He should have packed faster.

* * *

The first sign was the gulls.

Greyharrow's gulls were shameless creatures, loud as debt collectors, and at full dark they always rose off the rooftops in wheeling clouds, screaming at the fishing boats coming in. But tonight, as Cyrus latched his shutters, the gulls went silent all at once — not settling, not scattering, just *stopping*, hundreds of them hanging motionless on the wind like scraps of paper nailed to the air.

Cyrus's hands went still on the latch.

He told himself: wind shear. Weather coming down off the Spine. He told himself: the wards along the border are two hundred miles away, older than any kingdom, woven by hands that died before the word *king* meant anything, and they do not fail. He told himself these things in the flat, practiced voice of a man who has spent twenty years telling himself stories, and then he walked out into his narrow cobbled lane and looked up.

The stars over Greyharrow were still there. All of them. The Wain, the Weeper, the pale scatter of the Sisterhood — the whole familiar embroidery of the northern sky, exactly where it should be.

For three more seconds.

He felt it before he saw it. That's the truth he could never make anyone believe afterward, and the reason he never tried: the falling of the wards did not arrive through the eyes. It arrived through the skin, through the teeth, through the water in a man's body — because Cyrus Hartwell, charm-seller of Lowbridge, was made of the same material as the covenant, and what broke two hundred miles away broke in him too.

It felt like a bell the size of the world, struck once.

Every star went out.

Not faded. Not clouded over. *Gone* — snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, the whole sky dropping away like a floor, and Greyharrow plunged into a darkness so total that the lamplighters' flames seemed to shrink from it, guttering down to blue nubs. Somewhere a horse screamed. Somewhere a bell tower began ringing in panic rather than on schedule, all its bells at once, arrhythmic, mad. And under everything, rising out of the ground itself, out of the bedrock and the bones of the city, Cyrus heard — *felt* — the wards dying.

He had touched them only once, long ago. They had sung to him then, vast and slow, a chord sustained across the whole rim of the mortal realm, holding back the hungry dark the way a held breath holds back drowning. Now that chord was tearing, strand by strand, and each strand made a sound like a name being erased.

And Cyrus Hartwell's body answered.

There was no decision in it. That was the horror of it, the part that would haunt him down every mile of the road ahead: his magic moved *before his mind*, the way a hand jerks back from a hot iron. Twenty years of burying the storm — of breathing exercises and river-clay charms and careful, gray, deliberate smallness — and all of it amounted to nothing, because the covenant had known him, and its death-cry reached for the last living thing that shared its language.

Lightning stood up inside his chest.

It went out of him in every direction at once. His shadow burned onto the cobbles behind him, black and enormous and winged with static. Every rain-barrel in the lane flashed to steam. The hair-thin scars along his forearms — old marks he'd told neighbors were from a mill accident — lit up like the veins of a leaf held against the sun, and above his head, impossibly, in a sky with no stars left to reflect, a thunderhead assembled itself out of clear night air, turning, coiling, *waiting*.

Cyrus fell to his knees in his own scorched shadow and did the hardest thing he had done in twenty years.

He swallowed it.

He dragged the storm back down his throat with hands that shook, muscle against instinct, will against the oldest habit of his soul, and the cloud overhead frayed apart into wisps, and the lightning crawled back down into the marrow where he kept it, and he knelt in the dark of Lowbridge gasping like a landed fish while the city screamed around him.

But the dark had seen.

That was the thing about magic. Everyone believed it was a tool, a weapon, a craft — something you picked up and put down. The truth was worse. The truth was that magic was a *witness*. It remembered who had held it. It wrote your name in everything it touched. And somewhere out beyond the edge of the world, in the howling nothing that had just eaten the stars, something ancient and patient had felt a storm answer the falling of the wards — had felt the signature written in that answering bolt — and knew it.

Knew *him*.

Cyrus stayed on his knees until the screaming in the streets resolved into running footsteps, into doors banging, into the watch's whistles shrilling three-note alarm patterns that hadn't been blown since the Purge. Then he stood, on legs that belonged to someone else, and did what he had rehearsed in his mind a thousand times without ever believing he'd need it.

He packed his stall in ninety seconds flat.

* * *

By midnight, half of Greyharrow stood in the streets staring upward, and the other half had decided the end of the world was best observed from indoors with the bar locked. Cyrus moved through the panicked lanes of Lowbridge with a single pack on his shoulder and his hood up, wearing the face he wore to market — mild, forgettable, a man you'd describe as *the charm-seller, you know, gray fellow* — and everywhere around him the city was saying the word he had spent two decades making sure no one said near him.

"*Sorcery.*"

"It's sorcery, I'm telling you, my cousin's wife saw lights on the wardline road—"

"They'll call the hunters. Solace, they say. The witch-hunter. She caught the coven at Marrowfen in a week."

"Maren Solace," someone said, in the hushed tone people used for plague ships, and Cyrus flinched as though struck. He didn't know the name. That was the terrible thing — he *didn't know it*, and it terrified him anyway, because any hunter worth the title would follow the same trail he was already reading in the air: the ozone stink of a storm called on a starless night, the scorch-shadow of a man who had failed, after twenty years of practice, to be nobody.

At the mouth of Cooper's Row, a knot of neighbors had gathered around the town-crier, and Cyrus slowed despite himself — slowed because Tansy was there, small hand fisted in her mother's skirt, the glass bead glinting at her throat.

"—by order of the Crown!" the crier bellowed into the dark. "All persons witnessing unnatural phenomena are commanded to report to the watch! Any person harboring knowledge of sorcerous activity faces the king's justice! The realm endures! The realm endures!"

"The realm endures," the crowd murmured back, ragged, frightened, obedient. Tansy didn't murmur it. She was looking at the empty sky with an expression Cyrus recognized from mirrors — the look of someone doing arithmetic about how much of the world was left. Her free hand crept up and closed around her charm-bead.

*I have been afraid before,* she mouthed, eyes shut, *and the morning came anyway.*

Cyrus turned away before she could open them, because if she opened them and saw his face just then — cracked wide, guilt pouring off him like heat off a forge — she might have asked why the charm-seller was crying, and he had no lie ready that wouldn't poison the gift.

He walked. Out of Lowbridge, past the shrine where an old priestess was desperately relighting candles that kept going out as though the dark were drinking them, up the pilgrim stair toward the east wall, where the road ran out toward the hills and the dead border and whatever was left of the world's oldest promise.

At the gate, a watchman grabbed his arm. "Curfew. Where's your fire, friend? Where's your business?"

"Selling grief-charms to the frightened," Cyrus said. It was even true. "Best night for it in twenty years."

The watchman laughed the ugly laugh of a tired man and waved him through, and Cyrus Hartwell passed out of the last city where anyone knew his false face, and took the east road alone into a country with no stars.

Behind him, far to the west, he could have sworn the darkness was moving. Not spreading the way smoke spreads. Moving the way a tide comes in — patient, total, certain of the shore.

Ahead of him, two hundred miles of dead wards whispered a name into the night.

His name.

The one he'd buried. The one the storm had just dug up and shouted at the sky.

Orsen Vael walked into the dark, and the dark walked to meet him.
