## 4. The First Fragment

The road to Millbrook took three days on foot and cost Cyrus two of the charms he'd carried for luck. He sold neither. He needed them more than coin now — or at least he told himself that, walking with his collar up and his hood low through country gone strange since the stars went out.

The daylight was wrong. That was the thing no one said aloud in the villages he passed through, though everyone thought it. The sun still rose, still crossed the sky, but it rose like a candle behind smoked glass, and by late afternoon the light had a grey, underwater quality, as if the whole world were looking up from the bottom of a pond. Farmers worked their fields anyway. What else could they do? But they worked quickly, and they kept glancing at the horizon as if expecting it to open.

The wards were dead. He could feel it the way a man feels a missing tooth — an absence with edges. For twenty years those wards had hummed along the borders of the mortal realm like a fence of frozen lightning, and Cyrus had built his whole life inside their shadow, small and quiet and careful. Now the fence was down and everything outside it was leaning in.

He passed a shrine to the old covenant on the second day. Someone had smashed it. Not vandals — the work was too thorough, too frightened. Every sigil carved into the stone had been chiseled out with a hammer, every one, even the little decorative knots that meant nothing. Someone had stood here in the dark after the stars went out and taken a hammer to magic itself, stone by stone, because they didn't know what else to do.

Cyrus stood before the ruined shrine for a long moment. *I did this,* he thought. *Twenty years ago I started this, and last week it finished.*

Then he pulled his hood lower and walked on.

* * *

Millbrook wasn't on any map worth owning. It sat in a fold of hills where three old roads died, a village of slate roofs and sheep and one crooked mill, and if you didn't know what you were looking for you'd walk through it in ten minutes and remember nothing. That was the point. Sera Ives had chosen it the way she chose everything: carefully, twice, and with an escape route.

Cyrus knew the way in because he'd helped choose it, back when choosing things together was something they still did. Behind the mill, where the wheel turned slow and green with moss, there was a door that looked like it led to a root cellar. It led instead down a stair cut into the hillside, past a warding knot scratched into the lintel — a hedge-witch's knot, crude and honest, nothing like the crown-work of the great wards — and into a room that smelled of drying herbs, tallow smoke, and secrets.

He stood outside that door for the better part of an hour.

The sun was going down wrong again, the light draining out of the sky like water out of a cracked bowl, and still he stood there, telling himself stories. *She'll slam the door. She'll call the Crown. She'll look at me the way she looked at me the last time, across a table in a safehouse in Corvane, when I lied to her face about her mother's death and she knew — she always knew — and said nothing, just packed her things and left, and that silence was worse than any curse I've ever seen thrown.*

He was Cyrus Hartwell now. Charm-seller. Small magics, harmless ones, the kind that worked mostly because people believed in them. Twenty years of being nobody, and it had almost held. Almost.

The stars went out anyway. The sky fell anyway. And here he was, at his former apprentice's back door, because there was nowhere else left in the world to go.

He knocked.

* * *

The cellar room was fuller than he remembered. Where once there had been four cots and a worktable, now there were twelve, and shelves of jarred remedies, and bundles of dried herbs hanging from beams like sleeping bats, and a chalkboard covered in warding diagrams drawn in a hand he recognized instantly — quick, slanted, impatient with its own elegance. Sera's hand. She'd always resented how good her diagrams looked.

Six women and one young man sat around a scarred table, mending cloaks and grinding valerian and doing all the ordinary work of outlawed magic, which is to say: looking busy and unremarkable while listening hard. They looked up when he came down the stairs. Seven faces, wary and tired and lit by tallow-light.

And at the head of the table, Sera Ives.

She hadn't changed the way he'd feared and hoped. Her hair was iron-grey now at the temples, though she couldn't have been past forty; the underground aged people fast. There was a burn scar on her left forearm he didn't recognize and a steadiness in her eyes he did — the same steadiness she'd had at fifteen, the first time she'd called fire into her palm without flinching, while the other children gasped. She'd never been afraid of power. Only of lies.

"Cyrus Hartwell," she said. Her voice was level. That was bad. Anger he could have worked with. "Charm-seller. Come down from Greyharrow, have we? Heard the city's full of refugees and witch-hunters. Dangerous road for a man who sells luck."

"I need to talk to you."

"You need." She set down the cloak she was mending, folded her hands, and gave him the whole weight of her attention, which had always felt like standing in weather. "Everyone sit," she added to the circle, without looking away from him. "This won't take long. Either he'll say what he came to say, or he'll leave. He's good at leaving."

One of the women snorted. Cyrus felt his ears go hot. Twenty years of composure, undone in a cellar by a woman he'd taught to light candles.

"Sera—"

"Sit down, Cyrus."

He sat. It was that kind of voice.

* * *

They talked in the anteroom off the main cellar, alone, while the circle pretended not to listen through the door, which was made of two planks and a prayer. Sera poured him willow-bark tea like he was a visiting aunt, and the domesticity of it nearly broke him. She remembered how he took it. Of course she did. She remembered everything; that had always been her gift, more than any magic.

"The wards are down," he said. "You know that."

"Everyone knows that. The sky's the color of a drowned man. My people are getting sick — not ill, sick, like the world forgot how to hold them. Two of the hedges in Corvane went silent last week. Their keepers stopped writing mid-word." She sipped her own tea. "So yes. I know the wards are down. Tell me something I don't know."

"The covenant can be reforged."

The cup stopped halfway to her mouth. Then continued. She was better at hiding than he was. Always had been.

"That's a story," she said. "A nice one. Old Mother Tally used to tell it to frighten us — break the covenant and the sky falls, mend the covenant and the stars come home. Fireside stuff. The covenant isn't a thing you reforge, Cyrus. It's not a horseshoe. It was a lattice woven through the bones of the world, and someone tore it out twenty years ago, and everyone who knows anything agrees that whoever did it is either dead or beyond the wards, and either way—"

"It can be reforged. In fragments. The covenant didn't die, Sera. It shattered. Pieces of it survived — bound into objects, places, people. Seven fragments, maybe eight. Gather them to the old wardline at the turning of the season, and it can be rewoven. I know the rite. I—" He stopped. Started again. "I know the rite."

"Do you." She set her cup down with a small precise click. "And how would a charm-seller know the reforging rite of the covenant? A rite, unless every text I've ever smuggled is wrong, that only existed in the royal archives and the memory of the elder mages. Dead men and locked rooms, Cyrus. So either you're lying to me, which would be traditional, or—"

"Sera."

"—or you're something other than what you say you are. Which would also be traditional." She leaned forward. The tallow-light caught the grey in her hair and the steel in her eyes. "Here's what I know. Here's what my people know, because I make it my business to know things. Three nights ago, in Greyharrow, when the stars went out, every hedge and charm-worker within fifty miles felt a storm answer the falling sky. Not rain-storm. Storm-magic. The old kind. The kind that hasn't answered anyone since Orsen Vael died or vanished or whatever the histories decided happened to him. And two days later, Cyrus Hartwell walks into my cellar smelling of ozone and asking about the covenant."

The word hung between them. *Orsen Vael.* He hadn't heard it spoken aloud in twenty years. Hearing it now was like hearing his own heartbeat read out by a stranger.

"I was there," he said quietly. "In Greyharrow. When the sky fell. Something answered through me. I didn't call it."

"No. You never do. That was always your problem." She stood, walked to the shelf, and came back with a bundle of dried sage she had no intention of burning, just something to do with her hands. "You want the fragment. I know you do — don't look surprised, we found a piece of the lattice two years ago, sealed in a loom-weight, humming like a trapped bee. We've kept it hidden. Fed it. It's the most dangerous thing I own and I own it because throwing it in the river felt like murder." She turned to face him fully. "It's yours, if you're right. If the reforging is real. If you can actually do it."

Relief hit him so hard he nearly swayed. "Sera—"

"But." She held up one finger. "There's always a but with you. You get the fragment when you tell me the truth. All of it. What happened that night, twenty years ago. Why the covenant broke. Why you ran. Why my mother died on the wardline and the histories say it was sorcery — *your* sorcery — and you let them say it."

The room went very far away.

* * *

Her mother. Ilse Ives. Ward-keeper of the eastern line, third rank, a broad calm woman who smelled of bread and lye soap and who had once spent an entire summer teaching a terrified eleven-year-old apprentice named Sera to braid warding knots because her formal teacher wouldn't. Ilse had died the night the covenant fell. Everyone on the wardlines had died that night, or so the proclamations said — the loyal keepers, murdered by the traitor Orsen Vael, whose treachery broke the wards and killed the stars' guardians and doomed the realm to a dim and magicless age.

Cyrus had written that proclamation himself. Well — he'd dictated the shape of it, in the last hours, to a royal scrivener with shaking hands, because someone had to control the story and he was the only one left who could. *The traitor Orsen Vael slew the ward-keepers and shattered the covenant.* It was even true, in the way that matters to historians. He had shattered the covenant. The keepers had died in the breaking. Ilse Ives had died thirty feet from him, her warding knots unraveling, her mouth forming words he'd never stayed to hear.

What the proclamation left out was why. What it left out was the kings. What it left out was the sound the covenant made when souls went into it, which was not thunder, which was not screaming, which was worse — it was *satisfaction*, a deep low chord like a cathedral organ finding its note, and the kings standing in the vault below with their lists of names, checking them off.

He had never told anyone. Not the scrivener. Not the few elder mages who'd helped him flee. Not Sera, who had a right to the truth more than anyone alive.

"Sera," he said. "Your mother—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip, and for the first time the steadiness in her voice failed, and underneath it he heard the fifteen-year-old, the girl who'd waited three years for a teacher who never came back. "Don't you dare say her name like you're about to apologize. Apologies are cheap. You've had twenty years of cheap."

"I couldn't tell anyone. If I told the truth—"

"What? They'd have hunted you?" She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "Cyrus. Look around. Look at what you're wearing. Look at what you *are*. They hunted you anyway. You traded the truth for twenty years of selling luck-charms to farmwives, and the truth rotted in you the whole time, and now the sky is falling and you come down my stairs needing the one thing I have, and you think you can pay with *silence*?"

"It's not that simple."

"Nothing with you is ever that simple. That's the whole art of you." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment. When she lowered them she was steady again, and that was worse. "Here's the bargain, and it doesn't move. The fragment for the confession. Everything. That night, start to finish. Why. Who. What you saw. What you did. I'm not the Crown, I don't want a warrant, I want to know what my mother died for. Because right now, Cyrus, I have to consider the possibility that she died for nothing. That the man I loved like a father broke the world and never once told me why. Give me a reason it wasn't nothing."

"There is a reason." His voice came out rough. "There's a reason that would change everything, if you—"

"Then say it."

Silence. The tallow flame bent in a draft from under the door. Somewhere above them the mill wheel turned, slow and green and indifferent.

He opened his mouth. And twenty years rose up in him like water in a well — the vault, the lists of names, the kings' calm voices, the chord of satisfaction, Ilse's unraveling hands, the road east, the false name stitched into a new coat, the first time he signed *Cyrus Hartwell* and his hand shook so badly the ink blotted. If he spoke it, it became real in a new way. It stopped being his secret and became a fact in the world, a fact that could be doubted, twisted, used. And worse — a fact that would land on her like a stone. Whatever the truth was, it would hurt her. It would make her mother's death part of something enormous and terrible, and she would carry that enormity the way he'd carried it, alone, at three in the morning, forever.

He could not do that to her. It was the one kindness he had left, and he clutched it like a drowning man.

"I can't," he said.

Sera closed her eyes. When she opened them, all the love in her had been folded away somewhere, put carefully out of reach, and what remained was the circle-leader, the survivor, the woman who fed dangerous things rather than drown them.

"Then you can't have it," she said. "Get out of my cellar, Cyrus Hartwell."

* * *

He made it four steps toward the stairs before he stopped. Because that was the thing about shame — it didn't push you out the door. It rooted you to the floor.

"They'll kill you," he said, without turning. "If the void keeps spreading, if the Crown keeps burning hedge-workers as scapegoats — Maren Solace is already on the wardline, Sera. I've seen her work. She'll find this place inside a month. The fragment won't save you if you're dead."

"Then it's fortunate," Sera said behind him, "that I've survived twenty years of the Crown without your help." A pause. "Mostly."

"The reforging is real. I swear it on—"

"On what? On your name?" A short, bitter laugh. "Which name?"

He turned. She was standing by the table with her arms folded, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear, and he understood suddenly that this was the weapon, that she had known it would come to this, that she had aimed her grief at him the way he'd once taught her to aim lightning — precisely, and only when necessary.

"You were the best teacher I ever had," she said. "You taught me that magic is honesty. That a lie in the casting twists the spell. That the reason hedge-magic works is that it's *true* — true need, true fear, true hope. You made me swear it. Remember? First lesson. *Never cast from a lie, Sera. The Sigil hears the difference.*" Her voice dropped. "And then you spent twenty years casting from the biggest lie in the world, and now the Sigil has heard it, and the sky is coming down, and you stand in my cellar asking me to trust the liar."

Each word went in like a needle. He had faced witch-hunters with less damage done.

"If I tell you," he said finally, hoarsely, "you'll never forgive me. Whatever I say. You'll wish you'd never asked."

"Probably," Sera agreed. "But I'll know. And knowing is the only thing I've ever wanted from you that you wouldn't give." She unfolded her arms. "The offer stands until dawn. Then I seal the cellar, and you go, and I spend the fragment my own way. Maybe I'm wrong about the reforging. Maybe Mother Tally's fireside story is all it is. But I'd rather be wrong with the truth in my pocket than right with your silence in it."

She gathered the tea cups, ordinary as Sunday, and carried them to the basin, and did not look at him again.

Cyrus climbed the stairs to the mill yard and stood in the drowned grey light, watching the wheel turn. Above the hills, where the stars should have been beginning to show, there was nothing — a sky like a closed eye. Somewhere out in that dark, the void leaned against the broken border of the world, patient as winter. Somewhere behind him, a woman he loved was deciding whether to give the future to a liar.

Dawn was six hours off. He spent five of them walking the perimeter of the village, checking her wards, tightening two knots she'd tied carelessly in the dark, leaving the third exactly as it was because it was hers and she'd earned her mistakes. On the fifth hour he came back down the stairs, and the planked door, and the smell of herbs and tallow, and stood in front of Sera Ives with twenty years in his throat.

"All right," he said. "Ask me what happened that night."

She looked up from the chalkboard, chalk dust on her fingers, hope and dread fighting openly in her face for the first time since he'd arrived.

And before she could speak, before he could draw the breath to begin, the young man burst in from the watch-post above, white-faced, breathless, with news that emptied the room of everything else:

"Witch-hunter," he gasped. "On the north road. Grey coat. Solace."
