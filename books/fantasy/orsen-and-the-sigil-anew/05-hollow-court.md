## 5. Hollow Court

The capital had always smelled of stone and river and money. Now it smelled of fear.

Maren Solace rode through the Ashgate at dawn, past barricades that hadn't existed a week ago, past soldiers in Crown black checking papers with the flat efficiency of men who'd been told to expect enemies and were disappointed to find only merchants and refugees. The city walls rose above her, and above the walls — nothing. No stars. Even in daylight she could feel the absence, like a tooth missing from a familiar jaw.

The wards had fallen six days ago. She'd spent those six days on the road, riding hard from the wardline with a satchel full of evidence and a head full of questions, and every mile closer to Corvane the questions had gotten heavier.

At the gate they took her name, her warrant number, her sword. A clerk with ink-stained fingers wrote it all down without looking up.

"Purpose of visit?"

"I'm here to report to the Office of Inquisition."

"Everyone's reporting to someone." He stamped her paper. "Martial law since yesterday. Curfew at dusk. Don't be out after dusk."

"What happens after dusk?"

He finally looked at her. His eyes were red-rimmed, exhausted. "Nobody knows," he said. "That's the problem."

* * *

The Office of Inquisition occupied a squat granite block in the government quarter, and it was drowning.

Corridors that had been quiet as a tomb when Maren left them were packed with clerks, couriers, junior hunters, and men in the livery of half a dozen ministries all shouting over each other. Reports came in by rider, by pigeon, by semaphore relay from the coast towers: villages gone dark in the west, whole parishes where no one answered a knock, livestock found standing dead-eyed in fields as if something had passed through and taken the *wanting* out of them.

She found her superior, High Hunter Aldous Crane, in his office with the shutters closed and three lamps burning at midday. He looked older than he had two weeks ago. He looked like a man who had read something he wished he hadn't.

"Solace." He didn't stand. "You were at the wardline."

"The night it fell." She set her satchel on his desk. "I have residue samples, trace patterns, wardstone fragments. Whatever broke through wasn't foreign sorcery. It came from inside the lattice. The wards didn't fail — they were *broken*, from the anchor points, deliberately, and the calling signature—"

"I know what the calling signature was." Crane's voice was very quiet. He glanced at the shuttered window as if the walls themselves might be listening. "Close the door."

She did.

When she turned back, he had opened the satchel and was looking at the trace-glass vials without really seeing them. "Storm-calling," he said. "Old pattern. Pre-Covenant War pattern. You ran it against the archives?"

"Against everything we have. There's one match." She kept her voice level. "Orsen Vael."

Crane sat back. For a long moment the only sound was the muffled roar of the corridor outside.

"You understand what you're saying," he said finally. "That name is a dead file. Sealed twenty years. The man was exiled beyond the wards with nothing — no grimoire, no sigil-work, stripped to skin. If he's alive, if he's *here*, and he broke the wards—"

"Then he's the most dangerous man in the realm, and I'm the one who has to bring him in."

"No." Crane stood, crossed to the window, and eased one slat of the shutter open a finger's width. Through the gap Maren could see the palace across the square, its banners hanging limp in windless air. "You misunderstand your orders, Solace. So let me make them plain. Yesterday morning the Regent's council met. They've concluded the wards were broken by agents of Vhessia — foreign sorcery, an act of war. That is the official finding. It went out to every garrison, every newspaper, every pulpit this morning."

"That's not what the evidence says."

"I know what the evidence says. I've seen your preliminary report." He turned from the window. "Here's what you need to understand about the evidence. Nobody upstairs wants it. The realm is frightened, the harvest is failing along the darkened marches, and there are already bread riots in Lowmarket. Fear needs a face, and 'foreign sorcery' gives them one they can point armies at. What do you suppose happens if instead we tell them the truth? That the traitor Orsen Vael — the man whose treason ended the Age of Wonders, the reason their children can't learn a cantrip in school — has come back? That he's walking free somewhere in the borderlands right now?"

Maren said nothing. She was thinking about the child in Greyharrow, the luck-charm, the moment the sky tore open. She was thinking about a calling pattern that matched a dead file exactly.

"They want him dead," Crane said. "Quietly, if possible. Loudly if necessary. Your warrant has been amended." He slid a document across the desk. "Read it."

She read it. Her own name. Her own seal-number. And where the standard language should have read *apprehend and deliver for trial*, it read:

*Deliver the head of Orsen Vael to the Office of the Regent. No trial required. No inquiry authorized.*

"No inquiry authorized," she repeated.

"You're a hunter, Solace. Not an historian." Crane's voice softened, which was worse. "I've known you eight years. You're the best I have. Which is why I'm telling you this plainly, once, in a room with no witnesses: bring me the head, and don't bring me anything else. Burn the rest. Do you hear me?"

She heard him. She took the warrant. She saluted, because her body had been saluting for twenty years and didn't know how to stop.

But walking out through the crowded corridors, past clerks stamping *FOREIGN SORCERY — ACT OF WAR* onto dispatch after dispatch, she caught herself doing arithmetic she didn't want to do.

If the Crown wanted no inquiry — why not?

Inquiries uncovered things. That was rather the point of them.

* * *

The archives beneath the Office were older than the Office itself, and twice as disorganized. Maren had spent three years of her early career cataloguing seized grimoires down there, and she knew the place the way other people knew their mother's kitchen: which stair creaked, which lamp burned cleanest oil, which alcove the senior archivists avoided because the dust never settled right.

Sealed files lived in the Iron Room — a vault of lead-lined cabinets holding documents the Crown had decided the realm was better off not reading. Access required a countersignature from two high officers. Crane would never give one now.

So she didn't ask.

She waited until the night shift changed — the hour when the old archivist, Master Quill, took his supper and his successor spent forty minutes arguing with the ledger before accepting that yes, the numbers were wrong again — and she used the gap. She'd done it twice in her career, both times for legitimate seizures misfiled by idiots. This time her hands shook as she picked the cabinet lock, and she noted the shaking with clinical detachment, the way she'd note a suspect's tremor. Interesting. Diagnostic. Keep going anyway.

The Vael file was where sealed files always were: bottom row, far wall, behind the files marked *PESTILENCE RECORDS 3RD AGE* that nobody ever touched. She pulled it out. It was thin. Thinner than a twenty-year treason case had any right to be.

She carried it to the lamp and read.

The first third was what she expected: the trial record, the confession — coerced, she noticed; the transcript showed three sessions with a Crown questioner whose methods had been formally banned a decade later — the sentence of exile, the stripping of his name from the rolls. Standard. Ugly. Old news.

The second third was correspondence, and it stopped her cold.

Letters between the Royal Wardens and the palace treasury, dated eleven months before the fall of the wards. She read them twice, then a third time, moving her lips like a girl learning letters.

*...the covenant draws at unsustainable rate. The lattice requires feeding at intervals now measured in weeks, not seasons. The old arrangement — the sources Your Majesty authorized — cannot continue without expansion of intake...*

Intake. She knew what the old kings' "intake" had been. Every child in the realm learned it as a cautionary tale: the Blood Tithe, the prisoners and paupers fed into the covenant in the last century of the old kings' reign, before Orsen Vael shattered the lattice and made magic itself contraband. Everyone knew the tithe had been cruel. Everyone agreed it had ended.

These letters said it hadn't ended. These letters said it had been *accelerating*. And these letters said something else, something that made Maren sit down slowly on the cold archive floor with the paper trembling in her hands:

*...projection: at current draw, the wards will fail within two decades regardless of intake. Recommend contingency planning for orderly failure. The Crown should identify in advance which territories can be spared and which must be written off...*

Dated eleven months before the fall.

They had known. Not suspected — *projected*. To the decade. The wards' collapse wasn't a catastrophe. It was a schedule. And the Crown had responded not by warning anyone, not by evacuating the borderlands, not by spending one copper on reinforcement, but by drawing up lists of which villages could be spared and which must be *written off*.

Greyharrow was a border town. The wardline villages were border towns. Every dark village in the west —

She thought of the official finding issued that morning. *Foreign sorcery. Act of war.* An enemy you could point armies at, because the alternative was pointing the finger inward, at the palace, at the treasury, at the men who had watched a scheduled disaster approach and chosen to spend the remaining time preparing the cover story.

And in the middle of it all, one name recurred through the letters like a splinter working toward the surface. Vael. Vael. Vael. The Wardens writing to the treasury: *the traitor's claims regarding the covenant's nature have proven more accurate than comfortable. Recommend continued suppression of all records pertaining to...*

His claims had been accurate. Twenty years ago, the traitor had tried to tell the realm the truth about what the covenant was being fed, and the realm had cut out his tongue, stripped his name, and thrown him past the wards to die.

Maren closed the file. Sat in the dust and lamplight. Felt something shift in her chest that she recognized, distantly, from interrogations — the moment a suspect stops resisting, stops performing, and simply tells you the truth because the performance costs more than the confession.

She had given the law twenty years. Her father had died under a hedge-witch's curse — or so the story went; she'd built her life on that story the way a house is built on a foundation, and she had never once inspected the foundation. She had believed the wards protected the realm. She had believed the Crown protected the wards. She had believed that hunting the unlawful was the same thing as protecting the innocent.

The file lay in her lap, saying otherwise in treasury shorthand.

* * *

She put it back. Lock, cabinet, dust, dark. She walked out through the sleeping archive with her lantern hooded and her face composed, and she did not run, though every nerve in her legs wanted to.

In her quarters — a narrow cell with a cot, a basin, and a window too high to see anything but sky — she sat on the cot and laid out her possessions the way she'd lay out a crime scene. Warrant. Seal. Sword. The amended order: *deliver the head of Orsen Vael. No trial required. No inquiry authorized.*

No inquiry authorized. Because inquiries found files like the one in the Iron Room.

She thought about Crane's tired eyes. About the clerk at the gate saying *nobody knows, that's the problem*. About the dispatches stamped FOREIGN SORCERY, ACT OF WAR, going out to garrisons full of nineteen-year-olds who would march east to fight an enemy that didn't exist while the west went dark village by village, exactly according to schedule.

Her father's face floated up, unbidden. Dead when she was nine. Curse-mark, the village said. Hedge-witch work. She had made a career of that grief, and she suddenly understood — with the same sick clarity as the treasury letters — that she had never once asked who benefited from the story. Who needed a frightened orphan girl to grow up believing magic was murder.

Someone had. Someone always benefited.

Outside, the curfew bell rang. Somewhere below, boots on cobblestones — patrols, doubled since martial law. Above the high window, the sky was clear and utterly starless, and it looked less like night now and more like a lid.

Maren picked up the amended warrant and read her own orders one more time. Deliver the head. No trial. No inquiry.

A good hunter followed her warrant. A good hunter brought back the head and burned the file and slept soundly, because the law was the law and the law was all that stood between the realm and the dark.

But the law, she thought, had known the dark was coming for twenty years and spent the time writing press releases.

She got out paper and ink. She did not write a report. She wrote a list — short, coded, the kind of list a hunter makes when she's planning a hunt but hasn't decided yet which side the quarry is on. Border towns. Archive dates. Names in the treasury correspondence. One line at the bottom, underlined twice:

*Find Vael. Alive.*

Then she fed the draft pages to the lamp and watched them curl, and outside the starless sky pressed down on the capital like a held breath, and somewhere out in the dark borderlands a man with a buried name was running from her warrant — and she was beginning to think he was running from the wrong people entirely.
