## 7. The King Who Fed the Sky

The old man was dying the way a candle dies — not all at once, but in stutters, each flicker smaller than the last.

Sera had found him three days ago in the cellar of a burned-out chapel outside Millbrook, lying on a pallet of rotted vestments with his hands folded on his chest like a man already laid out for burial. He had introduced himself as Elder Corvane, last of the Vael's teachers, and then he had laughed until the laughing turned to coughing and the coughing turned red.

"You came looking for answers," he'd said, "and I'm the only man left who has them, and I'm spending them as fast as my heart will let me."

Now she sat beside him in the dark, holding a candle that guttered every time he spoke, as if even fire were listening. Through the broken ceiling above them she could see the sky, and it was wrong. The stars were gone from west to east like a tide going out, and where they should have been there was nothing — not darkness, which is a thing, but absence, which is a wound.

"He told you he broke it out of pride," Corvane said. "Didn't he. That's what Orsen always said. Pride, temper, ambition. He wore it like a coat so no one would look underneath."

Sera said nothing. Her hands were cold. She thought of Cyrus Hartwell's face in the smugglers' tunnels, storm-light playing across it while the void-touched things shrieked and died, and how he had looked — for one unguarded moment — like a man coming home.

"Ask me," Corvane said. "You've been sitting here two days not asking me. Ask."

"What do you know about Orsen Vael?"

"I taught him." The old man's eyes found her in the candlelight, and they were clear, terribly clear, the clarity of a man past caring about consequences. "Not storms. No one teaches storms. I taught him the covenant. Its structure, its history, its cost." He smiled without any humor in it at all. "Do you know what the covenant costs, girl?"

"Everything," she said. "That's the lesson everyone learns. Magic takes. That's why we hedge-witches work small. Small workings, small prices."

"That is what they *taught* you." He pushed himself up against the wall, and the effort cost him; she saw it in the gray of his lips. "The covenant doesn't take from the caster. It never did. Do you understand me? When your grandmother charmed a fever down, when a farmer's son called rain, the price went elsewhere. It always went elsewhere. Into the lattice. Into the Sigil. And the Sigil has to be fed, or it frays, and when it frays the wards fail, and when the wards fail—" he gestured upward, at the starless wound in the sky, "—that happens."

"Then it takes from everyone equally. Spread thin."

Corvane laughed again, wet and awful. "Oh, child. Oh, you poor, well-taught thing. Nothing about the covenant was ever equal. Who do you think paid to keep it running? Who do you think the kings fed into it?"

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Sera realized she wasn't breathing.

"The prisons," Corvane said. "The debtors' holes. The workhouses. Anyone the Crown could lose without a ledger noting it. A thousand souls a year, drawn up through the ward-stones at midwinter, quiet as smoke. The kings called it the Tithe of the Unseen. There are chambers under the capital, Sera. Under the palace. Rows and rows of stone chairs with iron restraints, and grooves in the floor where nothing was ever spilled, because souls don't spill — they're *poured*." His voice cracked. "I helped build the intake channels. I was young. I believed the arithmetic. One thousand strangers so that ten million could have light and wards and weather. Any reasonable man believes that arithmetic."

"And Orsen?"

"Orsen found the chambers." The old man closed his eyes. "He was twenty-six. They sent him to inspect the deep ward-lines after a tremor — his family name opened doors, his talent made him useful, his conscience made him dangerous and nobody thought to check. He walked into that chamber and he saw the chairs, and he saw a woman strapped into one of them, still alive, still *aware*, waiting for midwinter. She asked him if he was the one who'd come to kill her. She'd been there eleven months. She apologized for the state of the room."

Sera's hands had gone to fists in her lap without her permission.

"He came back up the stairs a different man," Corvane said. "He spent two years gathering proof. Petitioned the king quietly, through channels. You know what happened? They promoted him. Gave him honors. Moved him further from the deep lines. And they filled the chamber again, and the year after that, and the year after that. So on the night of the Long Convergence, when the covenant was at its most open, most malleable—" the old man opened his eyes, and they were burning, "—he put his hands into the living lattice and he broke it. Deliberately. Completely. Knowing exactly what it would cost him, knowing the wards would fail, knowing the sky would go dark, knowing they'd hunt him to the edge of the world and write *traitor* on his grave if they ever found one. Because a broken covenant can be reforged by anyone. But a working covenant can only be owned by whoever holds the feeding-chains. And he refused, do you hear me, he *refused* to let the chains pass to another hand."

The candle went out.

In the sudden dark Sera heard her own heartbeat, and beneath it, faintly, the sound of the old man breathing — slower now, each breath a longer negotiation.

"They'll tell you he doomed the world," Corvane whispered. "They'll be right, in a way. He traded twenty years of borrowed time for a chance at freedom, and the bill has come due. But do not ever let them tell you he did it for himself. He was offered everything. Kingship, effectively. They wanted him to *manage* the Tithe. Keep it humane, keep it efficient, keep the lights on. And he looked at the finest bargain ever struck and he said no." A pause. A breath. "No one says no to that bargain, girl. Almost no one."

"Why are you telling me this?" Sera asked the dark. "Why now?"

"Because you're going to see him again. And you're going to want an answer. And he won't give it, because he'd rather be hated than pitied, and pity is what the truth earns." Something rustled — the old man settling back, or failing to rise again. "So take the truth now, before he can stop you. Take it and carry it. That's all remembrance is, in the end. Someone willing to carry what another person can't."

She lit the candle again with fingers that shook.

Elder Corvane was dead by morning. He had a smile on his face, which she decided to believe was peace and knew, privately, might have been the last laugh of a man who'd finally finished paying his own tithe.

She buried him behind the chapel with her own hands, said the small words hedge-witches say, and then she walked east through the darkening country to find Cyrus Hartwell.

* * *

She found him at a crossroads inn called the Drowned Bell, three days later, sitting alone in a corner with a cup of tea he hadn't touched, watching the window where the sky should have been.

He looked older than he had in the tunnels. Storm-working had cost him something visible — a grayness around the mouth, a new carefulness in how he held his hands, as if they were tools that might go off. Twenty years of hiding, and he'd used his power once, openly, and it had marked him worse than the hiding ever had.

Or maybe, Sera thought, walking toward him, it hadn't marked him at all. Maybe this was just what he looked like without the charm-seller's smile.

"We move north tomorrow," he said, without looking up. "The second fragment's keeper sent word. There's a route through the fens—"

"Sit down, Cyrus."

"It's Orsen, actually."

"No," Sera said, and pulled out the chair across from him, and sat, and made him look at her. "It isn't. Not yet. Right now you're still Cyrus Hartwell, charm-seller, liar, and I need to talk to the liar first, because the other name comes with a debt I'm not sure anyone alive can pay."

Something moved behind his eyes — fear, she thought, real fear, the kind he hadn't shown when the void-creatures came.

"Elder Corvane is dead," she said.

His face did something terrible and brief. Grief, yes, but also — recognition. He'd known the old man was dying. He'd known what the dying man carried.

"You sent me to him."

"I sent you to the only teacher left who remembered the deep structures. I didn't know he'd—" Cyrus stopped. Started again. "Sera."

"Rows of stone chairs." Her voice came out flat and quiet and much colder than she'd intended, or exactly as cold as she'd intended, she couldn't tell anymore. "Under the palace. A thousand souls a year. The Tithe of the Unseen. Prisoners. Debtors. People no one counted." She leaned forward. "Tell me he lied, Cyrus. Tell me the dying man with nothing left to protect spent his last breaths building a beautiful lie. Say it and I'll believe you. I want to believe you. Look me in the eye and say it."

The inn murmured around them. Somewhere a pot banged; someone laughed at some joke that belonged to the old world, the world with stars in it. Cyrus Hartwell looked at her for a long, long moment, and she watched him reach for the smile — she actually watched him reach for it, watched the charm-seller assemble itself piece by piece behind his eyes — and then she watched it fall apart, unfinished, and what was left underneath was just a tired man of nearly fifty who had been carrying something for twenty years.

"She apologized for the state of the room," he said quietly.

Sera's breath caught.

"Her name was Maren Vosk. Debtors' prison, Harrowgate. Eleven months in the chair, waiting for midwinter. She asked if I was the one who'd come to kill her, and when I said no, she apologized for the state of the room, because she'd been trying to scratch a tally on the wall to keep track of the days and she was ashamed of the mess." His voice didn't break. That was the worst part. It stayed level and low and precise, the voice of a man reciting something he'd recited ten thousand times alone in the dark. "I petitioned for two years. I gathered testimony, ledgers, names — I had four hundred names, Sera. Four hundred people poured into the lattice like water into a drain. And the king gave me a medal and moved me to the coastal wards and the next midwinter the chamber filled again. And the next. And I sat at the feast afterward and toasted the covenant's health with men I *knew*, and I smiled, because that's what I do, I smile, and every smile was a chair, every toast was a soul, and I understood that the covenant wasn't broken and needed saving. It worked perfectly. That was the horror of it. It worked *perfectly*, forever, as long as no one ever said no."

"So you said no."

"So I said no." He turned his cup slowly on the table, watching it like it might hold an answer. "I broke it in front of the whole converging lattice so that no one could ever quietly repair it. I broke it loudly, permanently, unforgivably, because a quiet break gets patched and a loud one becomes a question. And the question took twenty years to arrive, but here it is, Sera. Here it is, sitting across from me, wearing your face."

The silence stretched. Outside, the light failed the way it had been failing for weeks — too early, too completely, the sun setting into a sky with nothing in it.

"You could have told me," Sera said finally. "On the road. In the tunnels. Any of the hundred times I asked."

"Yes."

"You let me hate you instead."

"Yes."

"You let me *grieve* you. I mourned you, Orsen. I built a shrine to my dead master and burned candles to a traitor and spent half my life being ashamed of loving you, and you were alive, selling luck-charms to children, and you never once—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway. "You never once gave me the courtesy of the truth."

"No," he agreed. "I didn't."

"Do you know what the worst part is?" She was standing now. She didn't remember standing. "It's a good reason. It's the best reason. It's the kind of reason that should make everything forgivable, and it doesn't, and I hate you for making it not forgive you, because now I have to sit with the fact that being right doesn't absolve you. Breaking the world was still breaking the world. Those twenty years of dark were still twenty years of dark. The villages going silent out west — those are *people*, Orsen. Whatever the kings did, you did this too. Both things are true and you don't get to hand me only one of them."

"I know," he said.

"You don't get to be a martyr. Martyrs are cheap. Martyrs get statues."

"I know," he said again, softer.

"And I don't forgive you." She said it clearly, so it would land, so it would stay landed. "Maybe someday. Maybe when the sky's fixed. But not today, and not because you confessed, and not because Corvane cried telling it. Understanding isn't forgiveness. Don't confuse them. Everyone always confuses them."

He nodded, slowly, and for the first time since she'd known him as Cyrus Hartwell — and for the first time, perhaps, in twenty years — he looked like a man who had been honestly seen and found wanting, and had accepted the verdict.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me." She picked up her pack. "Come on. We have a fragment to find and a sky to fix, and you can spend the whole walk being guilty where I can watch you do it."

But at the door of the inn she stopped, because there was a horse in the yard. A Crown horse. Gray, tall, military-shod, standing at the trough with the easy confidence of an animal whose rider had business here — and tied near it, a second horse, lighter, faster, the kind witch-hunters rode.

Cyrus saw it over her shoulder. She felt him go still behind her, felt the air change the way it changed before a storm, that faint taste of iron and ozone rising off his skin unbidden.

And through the inn's common room, unhurried, pushing open the door from the street, came a woman in a gray traveling coat with a hunter's warrant-case slung at her hip, scanning the room the way a hawk scans a field — patient, thorough, certain the field held what she was looking for.

Her gaze swept the tables, the shadows, the corner where a charm-seller sat frozen over an untouched cup of tea —

and stopped.
