## 8. Maren's Warrant

They brought her in at dawn, wrists bound with iron-thread cord that hissed faintly against her skin — hedge-witch, the soldiers said, caught three miles from the Ashes on the Wardline with a satchel full of charms and a map of the smugglers' tunnels inked in her own hand.

Maren Solace stood at the observation glass and watched the prisoner through it before she went down.

The woman was younger than Maren expected. Late twenties, maybe. Dark hair cut short and practical, a burn scar along one forearm old enough to have gone silver. She sat in the interrogation cell the way soldiers sat waiting for a briefing — spine straight, hands loose, eyes cataloguing the room. Two guards at the door. One table. One lamp. She'd already counted all of it; you could see it in the way her gaze moved, economical, unafraid.

Sera Ives. The name had come up twice in Maren's files over the past week, both times in connection with Greyharrow's underground circles, once in connection with a charm-seller named Cyrus Hartwell who had walked out of a burning wardline and vanished like smoke.

Maren had been chasing smoke for eleven days.

She took the stairs down instead of the lift. She wanted the extra minute to decide what kind of interrogation this would be, and she found she didn't know, and that unsettled her more than anything else this week had.

* * *

"You're the witch-hunter," Sera said when Maren entered. Not a question. "I've read about you. You don't torture people."

"I don't need to."

"No," Sera agreed. "You're good at questions. I could tell from the reports." She tilted her head. "That's not a compliment. It's just an observation."

Maren set the file down on the table but didn't open it. Standard technique: let the prisoner wonder what you already know. But Sera only looked at the file, then back at her, and something almost like amusement crossed her face.

"You're doing the thing where you make me guess. Save it. I'll talk. That's why I let them catch me."

Maren paused with her hand still on the folder. "You let them catch you."

"I stopped running when I heard the patrol coming. I'm tired, witch-hunter. I've been awake for four days moving refugees out of the dark zones west of Greyharrow. Whole villages, gone quiet. Do you know what that looks like? Houses standing, meals half-eaten, no bodies. Just absence." Her voice stayed level, but Maren saw the cost of that levelness in the fine tremor of her bound hands. "So yes. I let your soldiers take me. Ask your questions. I'll answer what I choose to answer, honestly, and we'll both pretend that's how interrogations work."

"Generous of you."

"I'm told I'm generous to a fault." A ghost of a smile. "Ask me about Cyrus Hartwell. That's what you want. Everyone wants that."

Maren sat. Opened the file. Turned it around so Sera could see the sketch inside — the wardline reconstruction, the storm-signature rendered in the Crown's analytic notation, the comparison column beside it.

"Do you know what this is?"

"A storm-calling signature." Sera studied it without flinching. "Old notation. Pre-ban. Where did you get pre-ban records?"

"The sealed archives."

Something moved behind Sera's eyes then — not fear. Recognition. "You pulled Orsen Vael's file."

"I compared the signature at the collapsed wardline to the signature recorded the night the covenant broke. They match. Every harmonic. Which means either Cyrus Hartwell is Orsen Vael, or someone is very deliberately imitating a dead traitor's magic." Maren leaned forward. "Which is it?"

"That's not the right question."

"It's the only question that matters."

"No." Sera shook her head slowly. "It's the question the Crown wants you to ask, because it has a clean answer. Traitor found, traitor killed, story closed. The right question is—" she leaned forward too, matching Maren across the table, "—why does a man who broke the covenant twenty years ago and disappeared come back now, the same week the wards fail? Why would he break something and then return to stand under the wreckage? What did he break it *for*, witch-hunter? Has anyone ever asked that? Has anyone ever asked what the covenant actually was?"

"The covenant was the binding that kept magic lawful," Maren said. "Kept it in Crown hands, licensed, accountable. He shattered it and the sky has been paying for it ever since."

"Is that what they teach at the Academy?" Sera's voice was gentle now, and that gentleness was worse than any mockery. "That magic was ever accountable? That the licensing system protected anyone?" She flexed her bound hands. "I was a hedge-witch before the ban made me a criminal. My mother was one before that. We healed fevers. We charmed rain onto dry fields. And every year we paid the tithe-collectors, and every year someone from our circle was taken to serve the wards, and none of them ever came back. Did you ever wonder where the ward-tithe went? The souls, witch-hunter. The Crown called it conscription. Called it service. Twenty years ago a man looked inside the covenant and saw what the tithe actually fed, and he broke it rather than feed it one more soul."

Maren kept her face still. It was a skill drilled into her at the Academy until it was less a skill than a reflex. But underneath the stillness, something shifted its weight.

"That's treasonous speculation," she said. "There's no evidence—"

"There's evidence everywhere. You just aren't permitted to look at it." Sera sat back. "But you're starting to. Aren't you. You pulled a sealed archive. Witch-hunters can't do that without cause. What cause did you write down?"

Maren didn't answer.

"I thought so." Sera looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw there made her nod slowly, as if confirming a calculation. "He won't tell you himself. He'd die before he told you. So I'm telling you, because you're the one holding the warrant, and warrants are the sharpest thing the Crown owns. Point yours somewhere true."

* * *

Afterward, Maren climbed to her office on the fourth floor of the Hunter's Hall and stood at the window for a long time without seeing the city.

Greyharrow smoked in the distance where the void-touch had reached the eastern districts. Refugees clogged the south roads. Above it all, the sky was wrong — a flat, starless grey even at midday, as if the heavens had been covered with ash-frosted glass. Twenty years ago she'd been nine years old, watching the stars go out from her mother's kitchen window, and her mother had held her and said, *the traitor did this, the traitor stole the light,* and Maren had built her whole life on that sentence. Academy. Commission. Warrant. All of it load-bearing walls erected on a child's grief and a government's sentence.

*What did he break it for?*

She opened her case notes. Eleven days of reconstruction, and the official narrative fit together beautifully — too beautifully. Orsen Vael breaks the covenant, vanishes, returns when the wards fail. Cause and consequence, villain and ruin, a tidy circle. But tidy wasn't evidence. Tidy was what narratives looked like after twenty years of being polished by people with an interest in their shape.

She thought about the signature match. Harmonic identity across two decades meant one caster or a lineage — and there was no lineage. Storm-calling like that died with its caster; the Academy texts were explicit. So Hartwell was Vael, or Hartwell was nothing at all and the signature was forged, and forging a pre-ban storm signature required access to pre-ban archives that only the Crown possessed.

Either way, the trail led somewhere uncomfortable.

A knock. Deputy Corren put his head in — young, ambitious, careful around her since the promotion list had been posted. "Commander wants you. Now."

* * *

Commander Aldous Reeve kept a office like a museum of the law: framed warrants of famous executions, the first witch-hunter's oath in gilt letters, a scale of justice cast in black iron on his desk. He was pouring two cups of tea when Maren entered, which was itself a message. Reeve poured tea for colleagues. Reeve poured tea for people he intended to keep.

"Solace." He handed her a cup. "Eleven days. Fast work."

"The work isn't done."

"It's done enough." He sat back behind the iron scale. "The Council reviewed your findings. Storm signature matches Vael. Prisoner in custody connected to him. The picture is clear." He slid a document across the desk. "Your warrant is amended. No longer capture. Termination. Deliver Orsen Vael's head to the capital within the fortnight, and the Commander's chair opens in the spring. Yours, Solace. Youngest in the Hall's history. They'll carve your name into the oath wall next to the founder's."

Maren looked at the document. The seal was fresh wax, deep red. The language was spare and legal and final. *Termination authorized. Evidence of trial unnecessary given subject's prior conviction.*

No trial. Of course no trial. He'd never had one the first time either — exiled by decree, convicted by proclamation, a legend assembled out of announcements. She had spent her career believing the difference between the Crown and the criminals it hunted was process. Process was the whole point. Process was the wall between justice and appetite.

"Evidence unnecessary," she repeated.

"He was convicted twenty years ago. This is an execution of an existing sentence, not a new judgment." Reeve smiled, warm as banked coals. "You're hesitating. That's not like you."

"I have a prisoner downstairs who claims the official history is false."

"Hedge-witches claim many things. It's why we hunt them."

"She claims the old kings fed souls to the covenant. That the ward-tithe was—"

"Solace." His voice didn't rise. It lowered, which was worse. "Do you know what happens to the realm if that story spreads? There are forty thousand refugees on the south roads who need someone to blame. There are six border provinces deciding whether the Crown can protect them. If the word goes round that the covenant we're desperate to restore was a slaughterhouse — that the men whose names are on the founding monuments fed prisoners into it like coal into a furnace — then there is no Crown. There is no law. There is just everyone with a grudge and everyone with power sorting it out in the streets while the sky eats us." He tapped the warrant. "This isn't about one man's head. It's about whether civilization holds its shape. Sometimes the truth is a luxury, Solace. Right now it's a luxury we cannot afford."

Maren held his gaze. "And if he's innocent of what we say he's guilty of?"

"Then he's still the most dangerous man alive, because the wards fell the week he surfaced, and correlation is enough for a firing squad when the alternative is panic." Reeve rose, signaling the audience was ending. "Take the promotion. Take the head. History will thank you the way it thanks everyone who carries the ugly necessity quietly."

At the door, Maren stopped.

"Commander. One procedural question. The amended warrant cites the sealed royal files as its evidentiary basis. As executing officer, I'm entitled to review the basis. I formally request access to the sealed files on the covenant's collapse. Tonight."

Reeve's teacup paused halfway to his mouth. Something crossed his face — quick, controlled, but not fast enough. Not surprise. *Alarm.*

"That request is irregular."

"So is amending a warrant to skip a trial."

For a moment the office was very quiet. On the desk, the black iron scale caught the lamplight, perfectly balanced, perfectly empty.

"Denied," Reeve said.

"Then I invoke the Founder's Clause." She'd read it a hundred times as a cadet, a dead letter, a curiosity — the first witch-hunter's provision that an officer may appeal a denied evidentiary request directly to the Archive Council, bypassing command. No one had invoked it in sixty years. "Article Nine. My request stands, and my refusal to execute the amended warrant pending review is noted as procedural caution, not disobedience."

"Noted," Reeve said softly, "as disobedience. You understand what you've just done."

Maren understood. She understood it in her chest, in the place where nine-year-old grief had hardened into certainty and was now, with a sound like ice cracking, beginning to give way. She had spent her life inside the law the way a fish lives in water, and she had just felt the water's edge.

"I've requested documents," she said. "Everything else is procedure."

"Everything else," Reeve echoed, and dismissed her with a look she would remember for the rest of her life.

* * *

The Archive Council met at midnight, five archivists in a cold stone room beneath the Hall, and Maren stood before them with her request written in her own hand and her career folded into the paper like a knife into a letter.

Archivist Penn, the eldest, read it twice. "The Founder's Clause. You know no one's used this since the Purification."

"I know."

"You know what's in those files?"

"I know what isn't in them," Maren said. "An answer. I'm ordered to kill a man on the strength of a record I'm forbidden to read. Either the record supports the order, in which case showing me costs nothing, or it doesn't, in which case I'm being sent to commit murder wearing a warrant. Those are the only possibilities, and one of them is intolerable, and I'd like to find out which."

Penn looked at her colleagues. Four faces, four calculations. Then the old woman set the request down and drew a ring of keys from beneath her robes, keys heavy and black, older than the Hall above them.

"The sealed vaults are under the capital. Royal ground. We can open the outer door; the inner door answers only to blood royal, and that hasn't answered to anyone in twenty years." She began walking, and Maren followed, down and down, torchlight giving way to sigil-light giving way to darkness that breathed. "But the outer chamber holds the index. Every sealed file, catalogued. And the index, witch-hunter, is not sealed."

"Why not?"

"Because no king ever imagined anyone would be fool enough to come down here." Penn's voice dropped as the last door loomed — iron, taller than two men, banded in runes worn nearly smooth. "Read carefully, Solace. Indexes are how institutions lie without lying. Everything true is in here somewhere. It's the ordering that deceives."

The lock turned with a sound like a bone breaking.

Inside, shelves climbed into shadow, thousands of folios in the Archive's grey binding, each spine bearing a catalogue number and a date. Maren lifted the master index from its lectern and turned to the entries for the year of the fall.

The files were there. Dozens of them. Ward assessments going back decades. Tithe ledgers. Covenant maintenance records. And at the center of the sequence, a cluster of entries dated the final month before the collapse — seven files, consecutive numbers, all marked with the same notation:

**RECLASSIFIED — CROWN PRIORITY — ACCESS: BLOOD ROYAL ONLY**

Seven files, created in the last weeks before the covenant broke. Before Orsen Vael shattered it. Files that predated the crime they should have documented.

Maren ran her finger down the dates again, slower, as though the paper might change under her touch.

The kings had known. Weeks before the wards failed — before the stars went out, before the exile, before twenty years of a nation's grief — someone in the palace had been preparing for the collapse. Sealing records. Reclassifying truths. Getting ahead of a catastrophe that hadn't happened yet.

Orsen Vael hadn't broken the covenant out of nowhere.

He had interrupted something.

Behind her, Archivist Penn held up a single lantern. "Now you see the shape of it," the old woman said quietly. "The question is what you do with a warrant that points the wrong way."

Maren copied the seven catalogue numbers into her notebook, her hand steady, her pulse loud in her ears.

In the morning, she would request them formally, and be refused, and refuse in turn. In the morning, the machinery of the law would begin grinding against her, and she would find out what she was made of when the water's edge was behind her.

Tonight, she wrote the numbers, closed the index, and thought of a hedge-witch in irons saying *point your warrant somewhere true.*

Somewhere true, then.

Even if it burned the Hall down on the way.
