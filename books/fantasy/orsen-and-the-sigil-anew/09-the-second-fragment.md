## 9. The Second Fragment

The road to the drowned temple was a road that no longer existed.

Cyrus followed it anyway, three days south from the wardline ruins, through country where the covenant's slow death showed itself in small wrongnesses. Rivers ran backward for a hundred yards and then thought better of it. A flock of crows flew in a perfect circle for an hour, neither landing nor leaving. The sun rose in the right place but came up the wrong color, a thin watery yellow like tea brewed twice.

He walked with Sera's map folded in his coat and her words folded tighter under his ribs.

*"You'll pay grief at that temple. Tam won't let you through on charm."*

She had said it the night before they parted — she to the western circles, he to the coast — standing in the doorway of a safehouse that smelled of tallow and dried yarrow, arms crossed, grey-eyed and unforgiving in the way of people who had once loved you enough to stay angry.

"You don't know that," he'd said.

"I know ghosts," she'd said. "I've been one myself, in a way. We don't want apologies. Apologies are for the living, so the living can stop feeling it. What we want is to be remembered *rightly*. And you have spent twenty years remembering him wrongly."

He hadn't answered. There hadn't been an answer that wasn't a lie or a wound, and he was out of practice telling which was which.

Now the coast came up gray and salt-scoured, and the temple rose out of the shallows where the old kings had put it — deliberately, cruelly, so that no pilgrim could ever forget what the sea had been made to swallow.

* * *

It had not always been drowned. That was the first thing people forgot, and the second thing the kings had counted on.

Two hundred years ago it had stood on a headland above the water: the Temple of the First Accord, where the covenant between mortals and the Sigil had first been signed in light. Every child learned its name. Almost no one learned what happened after. When the old dynasties consolidated their grip on magic — when they built the wards and wrote the laws and began, quietly, in cellars beneath palaces, feeding souls into the lattice to keep it cheap — they could not abide a monument to the idea that the covenant had ever belonged to anyone but them.

So they had broken the headland. Drowned the temple whole, altars and archives and all, and told the world it had fallen to the sea's own hunger. An act of god, they said. Best not to ask questions about gods.

The water had kept the truth for two centuries. Now, with the covenant failing and the wards dead, the sea seemed to be losing interest in keeping anyone's secrets. The tide around the temple ran strange — pulled outward in a long, low exhale, as if the ocean were holding its breath — and the drowned ruin stood exposed at its heart like a bone worked loose from flesh.

Cyrus hired no boat. He didn't need one. The water parted for him the way weather always had, reluctantly, resentfully, but parting nonetheless — a corridor of wet sand and stranded fish opening through the shallows as he waded toward the black arches of the ruin.

Storm-calling. His oldest sin and his oldest comfort. Twenty years of strangling it down to charms and sleight-of-hand, and still it answered him like a dog that had never been taught to stop loving the hand that beat it.

He hated how much he loved it. That was the honest word, and he was trying — Sera's accusation ringing in his ears — to be honest today.

* * *

The temple's great doors had rotted to lace, but the arch behind them held, stone carved with the First Words in a script older than any kingdom. Cyrus stepped through into the flooded nave and stopped.

The water inside was only ankle-deep. It should have been chest-high; the floor had settled, the sea had risen. But the water lay flat and still as glass across the whole vast chamber, mirroring a ceiling of stars that did not exist — because above the temple, in the real sky, the stars had gone out, every one, the night the wards fell. Yet here they burned in the reflection, hundreds of them, cold and patient, and Cyrus understood with a lurch that he was not looking at a ceiling.

He was looking at something watching him back.

"Tam," he said. His voice cracked on his brother's name for the first time in twenty years, and he let it crack. "I know you're here."

The stars in the water shivered.

Then the surface broke — not violently, but gently, the way a swimmer surfaces who has decided there is no hurry anymore — and a man stood up out of the mirror-still nave as though stepping out of a bath. He was thirty years old. He would be fifty now, if age touched him. He wore the traveling clothes he'd died in, patched at both elbows, and he had their mother's crooked smile, and he looked at Cyrus with eyes the color of shallow water over sand.

"Well," said Tam Vael. "The storm-caller finally comes to church."

Cyrus's knees nearly went. He had imagined this meeting a thousand times on a thousand sleepless roads — imagined fury, imagined weeping, imagined his brother turning away. He had not imagined this: the mildness. The terrible patience of the dead.

"You're younger than me now," Cyrus heard himself say. Stupid. True.

"Ghosts keep what they had." Tam spread his hands, half-apologetic, half-mocking, a gesture so familiar it hurt worse than anger would have. "You look terrible, Orsen."

"Don't." The name hit him like a slap. "Not yet."

"It's my name for you. You don't get to ration it."

"No," Cyrus admitted. "No, I suppose I don't."

Tam drifted closer — not walking, exactly; the water carried him, or he carried the water — and studied his older brother the way he used to study damaged clockwork, head tilted, looking for the broken gear. "You came for the fragment. Of course you did. Everyone comes to the dead for something. The kings came for souls." He smiled, and the smile had frost in it. "What do you come for, brother? Say it plainly. Lying to a ghost is a waste of breath. We can taste it."

"The fragment," Cyrus said. "Under the main altar. The second shard of the covenant. I need it to reforge—"

"Liar," Tam said pleasantly.

Cyrus closed his mouth.

"Try again."

"...I came for the fragment," Cyrus said slowly, "and I came because—" His throat closed. He forced it open the way he forced doors, brute pressure, no grace. "Because I left you. On the road north, the night of the exile. The hunters were closing and I made the choice that one of us should live, and I chose me, and I told myself afterward it was strategy. It wasn't strategy. It was fear. I ran and I let them take you and I have spent twenty years building a man named Cyrus Hartwell who has no brother, because a man with no brother doesn't have to think about the road north."

The nave was silent except for the faint tick of water finding new paths through old stone.

"That's closer," Tam said quietly.

* * *

They sat — Cyrus on the altar steps, Tam beside him, close enough that the cold coming off the ghost raised gooseflesh along Cyrus's arm — and Tam told him what happened after, and made him listen to all of it.

"They took me to Varrow Keep," Tam said. "You know it?"

"I know the name."

"Everyone knows the name now. Nobody knew it then; that was the point of it." Tam's voice stayed level, conversational, a man describing weather. "There was a cellar under the keep. Old work, pre-ward, the kind of stonework that drinks light. They had a lattice-anchor down there — a crude one, a stolen piece of the Sigil bent into a funnel. The kings had been running soul-tithe for years by then, little tributes, prisoners whose families couldn't bribe, vagrants from cities too poor to matter. Cheap magic. That's what the covenant cost under the old kings, Orsen. It was never free. Somebody paid every day, and it was never the people who benefited."

"I know," Cyrus whispered. "I found out. That's why—"

"I know why. Let me finish. You owe me the finish." Tam turned those water-colored eyes on him. "They asked me where you went. I told them nothing, partly from loyalty, mostly because you'd never told me anything, which I forgave you for then and forgive you for now, because you were trying to keep me clean of it. They fed me to the anchor anyway. Not for information. For arithmetic. The wards needed topping up that winter and I was inventory."

Cyrus made a sound he did not recognize as coming from himself.

"Don't," Tam said, sharp for the first time. "Don't do the thing where you make it about your pain. I died. You grieve. Those are different weights and I will not carry yours on top of mine. Listen. The last thing I felt wasn't fear. It was the Sigil itself, all around me — and it was screaming, Orsen. Do you understand? The covenant was alive, and it was being hurt, every day, for two centuries, and everyone called that peace." He leaned in. "So when you broke it — when you shattered the lattice rather than let them pour one more soul into it — I want you to hear this from your dead brother, because you will never let yourself hear it from the living:

"You were right."

The words landed in Cyrus's chest like a dropped anvil.

"You were right," Tam repeated, merciless in his gentleness, "and it changes nothing, and that is the lesson. Being right didn't save me. Being right got our name burned off every record and our faces onto every warrant. Righteousness is not a currency, brother. It's just a direction. You still have to walk the road — and you left me on the road."

"I know."

"You abandoned me. Truly, actually, in the mud and the rain, with torchlight coming through the trees. And then you spent twenty years paying penance to strangers — charms for frightened children, kindnesses in alleyways — because strangers are easy to be good to. They don't remember you tomorrow." Tam shook his head. "That's not atonement. That's hiding with extra steps."

"What do you want from me?" Cyrus's voice came out raw. "Tell me what you want and I'll do it."

"Now you're asking properly." Tam rose, drifting, the starlight in the water bending toward him. "Here is what I want, and it is the only toll, and you cannot buy your way past it with guilt, because guilt is just another way of keeping yourself the center of the story. I want to be remembered rightly. Not as the brother you failed — that makes me a prop in your tragedy. Not as a saint either; I stole, I lied, I once pushed you in the millpond and swore you fell. Remember me *whole*. Remember that I laughed more than you did. Remember that I was afraid on the road north and I held your hand anyway. Remember that I died screaming and it was not beautiful and it was not for a cause — it was waste, plain waste, and someone should say the word *waste* out loud instead of dressing it up as sacrifice."

"Waste," Cyrus said. The word tasted like blood.

"Yes." Tam nodded slowly. "Say the rest."

"I abandoned my brother on the road north," Cyrus said, each word dragged up like a hook from deep water, "out of fear. He died as fuel for men who called it governance. It was waste. It was not noble. It was my fault, and I hid from it for twenty years, and no charm I ever sold paid it down, because it isn't a debt. It's just true." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "And I remember you laughing, Tam. Gods, I remember you laughing. You laughed the night we left home. You laughed in the rain. You laughed more than anyone deserved to laugh in the year we had."

"There," Tam said softly. "That's the toll paid. Not forgiven. Forgiven isn't mine to give anymore, and honestly, brother — forgiveness was never the door. Remembrance is. Walk through it."

He extended his hand.

In his palm, dripping light instead of water, lay a shard of crystal no bigger than a knife-blade — humming, warm even through the grave-chill of the nave, singing a single clear note that Cyrus felt in his teeth and behind his eyes. The second fragment. The living memory of the First Accord, drowned and guarded and waiting twenty years for a man brave enough to come and grieve.

Cyrus reached for it — and stopped.

"This will burn you, won't it. Holding it. Guarding it all this time."

"Everything burns," Tam said. "Some things are worth the burning." He pressed the shard into Cyrus's hand and closed his brother's fingers over it, and for one moment the touch was almost warm. "One more thing. Free advice, from a ghost who's had two decades to think."

"Anything."

"When you reforge it — and you will, I've watched the void eat the sky and I know you'll die before you let it finish — don't build the covenant back the way it was. Don't hand it to kings. Don't hand it to heroes, either. Heroes are just kings with better stories." The crooked smile, their mother's smile, one last time. "Build it out of ordinary people. Build it out of charm-sellers and hedge-witches and children who want to see sparks. Build it so no one ever has to be inventory again."

"Tam—"

"And Orsen?" The ghost was fading now, unraveling into the starlit water, and his voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the past and from the deep. "When it's done — if there's anything left of you when it's done — come find this pool again. I'll tell you whether you remembered me rightly."

Then the nave was empty, and the false stars went out one by one, and the real dark came flooding back like a tide returning.

* * *

Cyrus climbed out of the ruined temple as the sea exhaled and took its corridor back, wading the last stretch with the fragment against his sternum, warm as a second heartbeat.

On the shore, the world had changed while he grieved.

The sky to the west — the whole western horizon, sea-line to cloud-line — was wrong. Not merely starless now but *thin*, as if the firmament were cloth wearing through, and behind it something vast moved without moving. Along the beach, tide-pools had gone perfectly black and perfectly still, mirrors with nothing in them. A fishing village half a mile down the coast stood with its windows lit and its chimneys smoking and not one person in it, doors open, meals cooling, as though the entire town had simply stepped out of existence mid-evening.

The void wasn't spreading like a storm anymore. Storms had fronts you could outrun. This was spreading like ink through water, like forgetting, like a name dissolving off a page.

And far inland, back the way he'd come, over the hills toward Greyharrow and the western circles where Sera had gone, Cyrus saw a smudge of orange against the dusk that was not sunset — beacon-fires, dozens of them, lighting one after another like a chain of warnings racing east.

Refugee fires. Panic-fires. Something had happened in the west, something bad enough that the outlawed hedge-witches were showing themselves openly to fight it.

Cyrus clutched the fragment to his chest, turned north, and started to run.
