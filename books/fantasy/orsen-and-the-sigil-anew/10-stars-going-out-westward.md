## 10. Stars Going Out Westward

The first village to go dark was called Millbrook, and Sera Ives learned its name from a girl of maybe nine who walked into Greyharrow alone, barefoot, carrying a dead hen under one arm like a doll.

"They stopped," the girl said, when Sera crouched down to her level in the churned mud outside the Northgate. "The stars over our house. One night they were there and the next night there was just — nothing. Like someone cut them out with scissors." She held up the hen. It didn't look slaughtered. It looked *emptied*, feathers intact, body strangely light, as if something had taken the inside out through a hole too small to see.

Sera had seen a lot in six years of running an illegal circle of hedge-witches. She'd seen burns from botched cantrips, seen the fear in people's eyes when soldiers kicked in a door looking for spellwork, seen old women burned on rumors alone. But she had never seen anything that made her stomach drop the way that hen did.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Pell."

"Where are your parents, Pell?"

The girl looked back the way she'd come, west, toward a horizon where the sky had begun to look wrong — not cloudy, not dark, just *absent*, like a page with a word erased. "They told me to walk east," she said. "They said they'd catch up."

They hadn't caught up. By sundown there were two hundred more like her at the gates, then four hundred, then Sera lost count because the count stopped mattering. They came in carts and on foot and carried on the backs of farmers who had carried their children twenty miles without stopping. And every one of them told the same story with variations: the stars went out over their homes first, then the animals went quiet, then the people. Not killed. *Gone quiet.* A whole village sitting at its tables, breathing, blinking, answering questions in flat voices until it stopped answering altogether. A woman described finding her husband milking a cow that had been dead for two days, his hands moving on the teats, his face smooth as a shut door.

"The void," Sera said to her circle that night, in the tannery cellar that had been their meeting place for three years. "It's not leaking anymore. It's *pouring*."

Twelve faces looked back at her by lamplight. Hedge-witches, all of them — herb-workers, charm-menders, a midwife named Odile whose healing touch could knit bone, a former soldier called Brack who could make a shield out of will and sweat if you gave him ten seconds' warning. Criminals, every one, under a law that had outlawed magic for twenty years. They had met in cellars and root-cellars and once, memorably, in a hay barn while soldiers drank themselves stupid thirty feet away.

"We should scatter," said Tam's ghost — no. Sera flinched internally. She still did that sometimes. The voice belonged to Wren, their youngest, seventeen and sharp-tongued. "Scatter and go deep. If it eats villages, we hide in cities until—"

"Until what?" Odile asked. "Until the void gets bored?"

"It's not eating villages," Sera said. "Look at what Pell described. The people weren't taken. They were *hollowed*. That's not hunger. That's harvest."

The word landed in the room like a dropped knife.

She thought about Cyrus Hartwell — Orsen Vael, though he wouldn't say it, though she'd known for weeks now and he still wore the false name like a coat he couldn't take off. He had shattered the covenant twenty years ago. Whatever else that meant, whatever truth he refused to give her, it meant this: the lattice that separated the mortal realm from the raw nothing beyond the wards was broken, and the nothing was coming through, and it moved westward because the wards had been woven strongest in the east, near the capital, where the kings lived.

Of course it moves toward us, she thought bitterly. We were always the cheap side of the wall.

"There's another thing," she said aloud. "My charms are working better."

Wren frowned. "Better how?"

"I hung a warming charm yesterday on a refugee child. It should have lasted a week. It'll last a month. Brack, your shields—" 

"Held against a boar this morning. Shouldn't have. Boar broke a fence post doing it."

Sera nodded slowly. "The covenant is failing, and the magic is coming loose. All that power the kings locked behind their rules and their blood-prices — it's spilling into the world like water from a cracked dam. Which means every hedge-witch in Greyharrow is stronger than she was last month. And every witch-hunter knows it."

A silence. Then Odile, dry as ever: "So we're more dangerous criminals than we've ever been, right when the sky is ending."

"Yes," Sera said. "And here's what I've decided. We stop hiding."

*

They came out into the daylight like moles surfacing.

It wasn't a decision anyone applauded. Wren called it suicide. Brack said nothing but sharpened his knives for an hour. But Sera stood in the tannery cellar with lamplight on her face and told them the truth as she saw it: the refugees flooding the city needed healing, food-charms, warmth, luck, protection — things the Crown's temples charged silver for and delivered slowly, if at all. Greyharrow's garrison had tripled its walls' guard and doubled its taxes on grain in the same week. The city was filling with terrified country folk and the law was responding by squeezing them harder.

"Fear is a currency," Sera said. "The Crown has always spent ours. Time we minted some of our own."

By the third morning they had a stall. An actual stall, in Tanner's Row, between a pie-seller and a farrier — Odile mending bones and fevers with a touch and a whispered word, Wren selling luck-knots for a copper, Brack standing guard with his arms crossed and his shields humming so faintly that only another witch would feel them. Word spread through the refugee camps the way fire spreads through dry grass: *there are witches working openly on Tanner's Row, they don't ask your name, they charge what you can pay.*

On the fifth morning a witch-hunter came.

Sera saw him before he saw her — or let himself be seen, which amounted to the same thing. He was young, earnest, wearing the grey-and-white of the Order of the Vigilant Flame, and he stood at the mouth of Tanner's Row for a long moment, hand resting on the warrant-chain at his belt, watching Odile press her palm to a fevered child's forehead and whisper the old words.

Then he turned around and left.

"He saw us," Wren whispered, when he was gone.

"He saw us," Sera agreed. "And he did the arithmetic. Two hundred witches in this city, maybe more now that the magic's loose. Four hundred witch-hunters in the whole province, half of them pulled to the capital. And a sky falling on everyone equally." She watched the hunter's back recede down the row. "He's not deciding whether to arrest us. He's deciding whether we're worth dying over. Today, we're not. Tomorrow—" She shrugged. "Tomorrow the arithmetic changes. So we work fast."

And they did. The circle swelled from twelve to thirty to sixty. Old witches who hadn't cast since the outlawry came out of hiding, hands shaking, weeping the first time a charm took under their fingers after two decades of silence. Farmers who'd secretly kept their grandmothers' herb-lore taught it openly. Someone chalked the old circle-sign — the interlocked ring that had meant *safe harbor* in the days before the covenant — on doorposts all through the refugee quarter, and no one painted it over.

Sera worked fourteen-hour days and slept badly, and through all of it, one thought circled her like a bird of prey:

*Cyrus Hartwell owes me the truth, and the truth is the only thing that might save any of this.*

*

He came to her on the ninth night, slipping into the back room of the tannery smelling of road dust and rain, and Sera felt her anger rise before he'd even finished closing the door.

"You're alive," she said flatly.

"For now." He set down his pack. He looked older than he had a month ago, which shouldn't have been possible; the lines around his eyes seemed carved deeper, and there was a hollowness under his cheekbones that hadn't been there when he'd sold charms beside her in the market like any other hedge-rat. "The second fragment is real. It's in the drowned temple at Callowmere. There's a guardian."

"What kind of guardian?"

His jaw tightened. "One I owe a debt to."

"Cyrus—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Whatever you're going to say—"

"You're going to walk to a haunted temple, take a piece of the covenant you helped break, and come back, and you still won't tell me why." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated herself for it. "People are *dying out there*. Whole villages gone hollow. Children walking forty miles alone. And you're keeping your little secret like it's a coin you're saving for a rainy day. It's raining, Cyrus. It's raining *nothing*."

For a moment he just looked at her, and she saw something in his face she'd never seen in all the years she'd known him — not the charm-seller's easy warmth, not the fugitive's wariness. Something rawer. Almost pleading.

"Sera. If I tell you what I think happened that night, and I'm wrong, I damn myself. If I'm right—" He exhaled slowly. "If I'm right, then everything the world believes about the fall of the covenant is a lie built on top of twenty years of murders, and the people who committed them are still on their thrones. Either way, the truth won't put the stars back. Only the fragments will. Let me get the fragments."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have that doesn't break something."

She wanted to hit him. She wanted, worse, to believe him. Instead she turned away and started packing supplies onto the table — bread, dried meat, a vial of Odile's fever-tincture, a luck-knot Wren had braided. Practical things. Things that kept hands busy so voices stayed steady.

"Take the river road as far as Thornwick," she said. "After that the void's reached the lowlands. You'll see it. Don't look at it directly longer than you have to — the refugees who stared too long came in hollow-eyed, saying they heard singing." She pushed the bundle across the table. "Come back with the fragment. And Cyrus?" He paused at the door. "One day soon, you're going to owe me more than a name. Start figuring out how to pay."

He was gone before midnight, and she sat alone in the lamplight feeling the weight of everything unsaid settle over her like ash.

That was when the bells began.

*

Not the alarm bells. The *name* bells — the ones rung only when a settlement died.

Sera climbed to the roof of the tannery and looked west, and the sight took her breath and gave it back cold. The darkness had crossed the hills. It wasn't cloud, wasn't storm; it was an absence with edges, a clean black bite taken out of the night sky, and it was wider than it had been two nights ago. Under it, distant fires burned in patterns that meant evacuation, or panic, or both. The bells of three separate villages rang at once, overlapping, and then, one by one, stopped.

Beside her, Wren hugged her arms against the cold. "Thornwick," she said quietly. "Cyrus was headed for Thornwick."

"He'll have gone around. He knows these roads better than the carters do."

"And if he didn't?"

Sera didn't answer. Below them, the refugee quarter glowed with hundreds of small lights — candles in windows, cooking fires, and now, everywhere, the soft blue glow of witch-lights, dozens of them, hanging over doorposts like low stars of their own. The outlawed had lit the city themselves. No temple had authorized it. No magistrate had permitted it. People had simply decided, in the face of a devouring dark, that light was a thing they were allowed to make.

Something loosened in Sera's chest — not hope exactly, but the ground hope grows in.

"Get the circle together," she said. "All of them. Everyone who's ever signed our book, everyone who's ever bought a charm. Wake the midwives and the herb-men and the retired hedge-hags in the hills. Tomorrow we start teaching."

"Teaching what? Most of them barely know a cantrip—"

"Everything we know. Everything their grandmothers knew. Cantrips, wards, the circle-signs, all of it." She watched the black edge of the void creep westward and made her voice steady. "The Crown spent twenty years making sure magic belonged to no one, so no one could stand against them. Well, the sky just fell in, and I'm done asking permission. If the void wants this city, it's going to find sixty witches. Then six hundred." She turned for the stairs. "Then six thousand."

Wren caught her arm at the top of the ladder. "There's a rider," she said, nodding toward the Northgate. "Fast horse, Order colors. Coming straight here."

Sera's stomach clenched. Witch-hunters, finally doing the arithmetic she'd feared. But as the rider clattered into the torchlight below, she saw the horse was lathered and stumbling, ridden half to death, and the rider herself — a woman in grey-and-white, warrant-chain swinging — dismounted before the animal fully stopped.

Maren Solace. Sera had never met her, but every outlaw in the province knew the name and the lean silhouette, and knew that when Maren Solace rode somewhere, someone's life ended.

The witch-hunter looked up at the roofline, found Sera's face in the dark with unnerving precision, and said, in a voice scraped raw by hard riding:

"I need to talk to whoever leads this circle. It's about Orsen Vael." A pause, and then, quieter, almost disbelieving her own words: "I'm not here on a warrant. I'm here because the files say the Crown knew the wards would fail — and I think your charm-seller is the only person alive who can prove it."
