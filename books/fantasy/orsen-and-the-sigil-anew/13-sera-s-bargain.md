## 13. Sera's Bargain

The spymaster's summons arrived folded inside a loaf of bread.

Sera knew what it was before she broke the crust — the baker on Candle Lane had been Crown property since the martial law proclamation, and no one in Greyharrow bought bread from Candle Lane unless they wanted to be found. She turned the loaf over twice, feeling the stiff paper through the flour-dusted skin, and let the circle's youngest member, a girl called Wren with soot still under her nails from the night's ward-work, watch her do it.

"Is it bad?" Wren asked.

"It's an invitation," Sera said. "Those are always worse."

She waited until Wren had gone back to the cellar where the hedge-witches were teaching each other the small crafts — a warming cantrip passed mouth to mouth like a folk song, a charm against nightmares scratched onto slate because paper was evidence now — before she cracked the loaf open and read.

*Madam Ives. His Lordship Corvane requests the pleasure of your company at the Greykeep, tomorrow, the ninth bell. He is given to understand you have knowledge of the fugitive Cyrus Hartwell. He is further given to understand that this knowledge is not for sale but might be, for the right price. He admires pragmatists. Come alone. Come willing. The alternative is that we come to you, and we are less polite.*

No signature. No seal. Just a pressed sprig of ash-leaf, the spymaster's mark, dry as bone.

Sera sat down hard on the flour sack and laughed, once, without any humor in it at all.

Because it was true. She *did* know where Cyrus Hartwell was. She had left him three days ago at the drowned temple road, heading east toward the capital with one fragment of the broken covenant sewn into his coat lining and grief stitched into everything else, and she had told him — she had *promised* him — that she would stay behind, keep the circle safe, keep the tunnels open, keep the hedge-witches fed and hidden while the void ate the western sky.

And then Corvane had gone and found her anyway, because Corvane found everyone eventually. That was his whole talent. Not swords, not spells — the Crown's spymaster couldn't light a candle with magic if his life depended on it, which was precisely why he'd been given the job when the wards fell. In a world where sorcery was outlawed, the man who needed none was the man who could be trusted with anything.

She thought about running. The circle had routes out of the city — smugglers' tunnels, a coal barge, a widow in the marsh country who owed Sera her son's life. They could be gone by dawn.

They could also be followed. And Cyrus was walking east with the future of the world in his coat pocket, and every hunter Corvane sent west chasing ghosts was a hunter not standing between Orsen Vael and the Crown vault.

Sera looked at the ash-leaf a long moment. Then she got up, brushed the flour off her skirts, and went to find Wren again.

"Wake Tamlin," she said. "And the others. We need to talk."

* * *

"We need to talk" turned out to mean: Sera stood in the cellar among eleven exhausted hedge-witches and lied to their faces, and hated herself with a thoroughness that surprised even her.

"Corvane wants me," she said. "He thinks I'll give up Cyrus. I'm going to go and convince him he's right."

The silence that followed had weight. Tamlin, the oldest of them, a stooped ex-lamplighter whose hands shook unless they were holding chalk, spoke first, as he always did.

"You're going to sell him out."

"I'm going to *appear* to sell him out." Sera kept her voice level, teacher-calm, the voice she used for lessons. "Corvane isn't stupid, but he's a buyer. Buyers believe what they've paid for. If I give him something real enough — a name, a route, a piece of the truth — he'll stop hunting the rest of us and start hunting the thing I point him at."

"And the thing you point him at?"

Sera reached into her shirt and drew out the object hanging there on a leather cord, and watched eleven faces go pale in the lamplight.

It was the third fragment of the covenant — or it looked exactly like the third fragment. A sliver of living lattice, veined with pale gold light, humming faintly against her skin like a trapped moth. It had taken her four days and two stolen nights in the archive under Old Chapel Row to make it: wax and bone-ash and a hair-thin wire of true sigil-metal, charmed until its signature matched the genuine article closely enough to fool anyone short of the Sigil itself. Close enough, she prayed, to fool a spymaster who couldn't work magic and would have to rely on instruments and informants. Close enough to buy time.

Cyrus carried the real one. He didn't know she'd made the copy. He didn't know she was holding it now, or what she intended to do with it, and if she had her way he never would.

"He's going east," she said aloud, and felt the lie settle into her chest like a swallowed stone. "Toward the coast. To try to reach the old ferry at Marrow's Landing. That's what I'll tell Corvane. It's plausible — it's *almost* true — and it buys him three days of sending riders the wrong way."

Tamlin's eyes were shrewd above his shaking hands. "And what does Corvane give you for it?"

"My people's safety. An official blind eye. Maybe a pardon, if he's feeling generous, though I won't hold my breath." She tucked the forged fragment back beneath her shirt. "And it gets me inside the Greykeep, which is the one place in this city I've spent two years trying to get into. There are prisoners there. Hedge-folk taken in the sweeps. People who know things about what the kings were doing to the covenant before it broke." She met Tamlin's gaze steadily. "I'm not walking in there to lose, old man. I'm walking in there to trade."

It wasn't entirely a lie. That was the trick of a good bargain — build it out of ninety percent truth so the ten percent goes down smooth.

Wren, at the back, was crying silently, the way children cry when they've learned crying loudly gets people hurt. Sera crossed the cellar and crouched in front of her.

"Hey. Look at me. I'm coming back."

"You don't know that."

"No," Sera admitted. "But I know I'm the best liar in this room, and I know that lying is what's going to save us. So you're going to keep the circle together, and you're going to teach the warming charm to anyone who asks, and you're going to be so boringly careful that when I come back I'll have nothing to fix." She squeezed the girl's hand. "That's the job. Boringly careful. Can you do it?"

Wren wiped her nose with her sleeve and nodded, and Sera stood up feeling approximately nine hundred years old.

That night she wrote two letters. The first went to Cyrus, care of the ferry-master at Marrow's Landing, and said only: *West is burning, east is watching, trust no bread.* He'd understand. He'd taught her half her tradecraft himself, back when she'd been his apprentice and he'd been nobody but a charm-seller with clever hands and sad eyes, and she had loved him then the way you love a teacher — completely, and without ever once suspecting what he was carrying.

The second letter she burned. It had started *Orsen, I'm sorry,* and she couldn't finish it, because sorry implied she'd decided to survive, and she hadn't decided anything yet except the next forty-eight hours.

* * *

The Greykeep had been a fortress once, then a palace, then a courthouse, and now it was all three rotting into one another, and Lord Corvane received her in the part that had been a chapel, because he liked the irony.

Sera had imagined a spider of a man, silk and menace. What she got was broad, grey-templed, comfortable-looking, with the mild face of a provincial magistrate and hands folded on the table before him like a farmer waiting on rain. Two guards at the door. One clerk in the corner, writing everything down. No chains. Tea already poured.

"Madam Ives." He gestured to the chair across from him. "You came alone. Good. I hate crowds; they muddle the accounting."

"Your note promised politeness."

"My note promised I'd be *less impolite* than the alternative, which is a different thing entirely." He poured her tea himself, unhurried. "You lead a circle of unlicensed practitioners. You were apprentice to a fugitive storm-caller. And three days ago you purchased, through intermediaries, quantities of bone-ash and sigil-wire sufficient to counterfeit a covenant fragment. Shall I continue? I have more. I always have more. It's rather my whole occupation."

Sera lifted the tea and drank it slowly. Let him watch her not flinch. "Then you know I'm useful. Men don't waste tea on people they're going to arrest."

"Men waste tea on exactly those people. It softens the confession." But there was a flicker at the corner of his mouth — appreciation, maybe. "Very well. Straight dealing, since you're clearly built for it. The Crown requires Orsen Vael. Dead, per the warrant, though I confess I've developed a professional curiosity about whether alive wouldn't serve better. You know where he is. Name your price."

"The circle walks free. Full pardons, sealed records. The prisoners in your cells below — the hedge-folk from the Ash Street sweep — released to me."

"That's expensive."

"So am I."

Corvane studied her the way she'd seen buyers study a charm at market — turning it over, looking for the flaw, the seam, the place where the maker's hand had slipped. "And in exchange you give me Vael."

"In exchange I give you the truth." She reached into her shirt, slowly, telegraphing every motion to the guards, and laid the forged fragment on the table between them.

The clerk stopped writing. One guard's hand went to his sword. Corvane didn't move at all, which told Sera more about him than any question could have.

It hummed on the wood between them, gold-veined, breathing light. Her four sleepless nights, her stolen wire, her best work. It looked, even to her — and she'd made it — like a piece of God's own scaffolding.

"This is the third fragment," Corvane said quietly. "Which was reported lost."

"It was never lost. It was hidden. By people who understood what the Crown would do with it." She let the anger show, real anger, easy to spend. "You want to reforge the covenant under royal authority. So did the old kings. Ask yourself why the last attempt required feeding it souls, my lord. Ask yourself what your masters intend to feed this one."

Something moved behind his mild eyes — a ledger closing, perhaps, a column balancing. "Where is Vael going?"

"Marrow's Landing. The old ferry. He means to cross to the wardline ruins from the western shore, away from your patrols." Every word of it nearly true, arranged just crookedly enough. "He has two fragments. This makes three. Without the third, the reforging fails, and the void keeps eating westward until it reaches your nice warm city. So." She spread her hands. "Do we have a bargain?"

Corvane picked up the fragment. Held it to the light. Turned it as a buyer turns a charm.

And Sera sat very still, heart hammering, and thought: *it will hold, it will hold, it has to hold* — because if he ran it past a true mage, if he had one instrument in his dungeons sensitive enough to taste the difference between her forgery and the living lattice, then this table became her cell and the circle became kindling and Cyrus would walk east into a trap wearing a false name and carrying the only real hope the world had left.

Corvane set it down. "A pragmatic woman," he said. "I admire pragmatists."

"You mentioned."

"We have a bargain." He slid a paper across the table — writs of pardon, unsigned, pending her information's verification. "You'll remain our guest while my riders confirm the ferry intelligence. For your comfort," he added pleasantly, "the guest quarters are very fine."

*Guest.* Prisoner, in good tailoring.

Sera smiled at him anyway, and took the pen he offered, and signed the confession he placed before her — a full, detailed, damning account of the traitor Cyrus Hartwell, his movements, his intentions, his crimes — in her own neat handwriting, and watched the clerk blot it and seal it, and thought: *every word of this buys him a day. Every day is a mile east.*

She was escorted down through the Greykeep's bones to rooms that were indeed very fine, with a barred window facing west, where the sky over the fallen wardline flickered with the slow dark pulse of the void.

From somewhere far below, through the stone, she could hear voices. Hedge-folk in the cells. Someone singing, very softly, one of the old charms set to a weaving tune — *thread and thimble, wind and wick, what was broken, mend it quick* —

Sera pressed her hand flat against the cold wall and sang the next line under her breath, and began, methodically, to plan how a prisoner robs a prison.

Because here was the thing Corvane's clerks hadn't accounted for, the flaw in the seam of his tidy bargain: the fragment she'd surrendered was wax and bone-ash and prayer. And the moment any of his instruments touched it — the moment any mage in his employ tried to draw so much as a spark through it — it would fail, visibly, catastrophically, and Corvane would know he'd been sold a counterfeit by a pragmatist he'd admired.

She had perhaps three days. Maybe four.

Below her cell window, in the yard, riders were already saddling for the west road, and among them, checking girth straps with a soldier's boredom, stood a lean figure in a courier's cloak that Sera recognized, and her blood went cold — Tamlin's granddaughter, Liss, who had joined the Crown's messenger corps eight months ago for reasons the circle had never fully trusted.

Liss looked up, directly at the barred window, directly at her.

And winked.

Sera stepped back from the window, heart pounding, and allowed herself — for the first time since breaking bread around a spy's letter — the beginning of a smile.
