## 15. Tam's Voice

The wardline at night was worse than the wardline by day.

By day you could pretend it was a scar — old damage, healed over badly, the kind of thing a body learns to live around. By night the broken wards still glowed faintly along their fracture lines, a sick green phosphorescence like rot in timber, and the dark beyond them moved. Not wind. Not cloud. The void had learned to move the way a predator moves: patiently, in long slow coils, tasting the edge of what remained.

Cyrus stood at the crest of the ridge with Sera's circle spread out behind him — eleven hedge-witches and one witch-hunter who was no longer officially anything — and watched the sky eat itself.

The stars were gone from horizon to horizon now. Not dimmed. *Gone*, as if someone had reached up and wiped the glass clean on the far side. In their place hung a darkness with texture to it, a depth that pulled at the eye the way deep water pulls at a swimmer.

"It's faster," Maren said beside him. She'd said little since the vault. She'd said almost nothing since he'd answered her question — *why did you break it?* — with the truth, all of it, in the cold air between the Crown's shelves. But she'd come. That was its own kind of answer. "Three days ago the edge was past the western hills. Now look."

"I'm looking." He'd been looking for twenty years. It didn't help.

Sera came up the ridge with the three fragments wrapped in wool and iron-bark, cradled against her chest like an infant. Behind her, the circle carried lanterns they didn't need; the dying wards gave enough light to be wrong by. "The convergence point is fifty paces down," she said. "Where the original anchor-stone split. I can feel it pulling at the fragments already. They want to go home."

"Then we give them home," Cyrus said.

"And the fourth?" Maren asked. "We have three. The rite needs four. Your elder mage said—"

"The elder mage died before he could say where it was." Sera's voice was flat with exhaustion. They'd ridden two days without real sleep, and everyone's edges were showing. "He said *the last piece went where no king would ever think to look.* Which, given that kings don't look anywhere but inside themselves, tells us nothing useful."

Cyrus knelt at the ridge-top and pressed his palm flat against the earth. Old habit. Grounding. His storm-magic stirred under his skin like a dog hearing its name, eager, and he held it down with the ease of long practice and the weariness of it too. Twenty years of holding it down. Three weeks of letting it out, and already it felt less like a weapon than a limb.

"We do what we can tonight," he said. "We set the anchors. We hold the line while we search. If the void comes before we find the fourth—"

"It won't," said a voice from below the ridge. "Not yet. It's waiting for something."

Every lantern swung toward the sound. Every hand went to whatever passed for a weapon among hedge-witches — knives, iron nails, cupped handfuls of ready spellwork.

Cyrus knew the voice. He had known it since before he could read. And because he knew it, he did not reach for a weapon. He walked down the slope alone, through the green rot-glow of the broken wards, to where a man stood among the fractured anchor-stones.

* * *

Tam Vael looked exactly as he had at nineteen. That was the cruelty of ghosts — they kept the age you failed them at. Lean, dark-haired, with the family crook in his smile and mud on his boots from a road he'd died on twenty years ago. He was translucent at the edges, and through his chest Cyrus could see the fractured stone behind him, glowing.

"You're dead," Cyrus said. Stupid thing to say. His voice cracked on it anyway.

"Took you this long to notice?" Tam tilted his head. "You always were the observant one. Mother used to say you noticed everything except what was in front of you."

Around them, the dark beyond the wards had gone still. Listening. Cyrus felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm — the whole wounded sky holding its breath.

Behind him, footsteps. Sera first, then Maren, then the circle fanning out in the formation they'd practiced, though none of their spells were shaped for this. You couldn't cut a ghost. Sera stopped three paces away, and Cyrus watched her face do the arithmetic — the brother from the stories, the one Orsen Vael abandoned on the exile road, the name in the songs children weren't allowed to sing anymore.

"You're not the only one here," Tam said, and gestured.

They rose out of the ground like frost forming in reverse. Hundreds of them. Then thousands. Figures of pale light standing in the ruins of the wardline as far as the lanterns could reach and far beyond — men, women, children, some in the robes of court mages, most in the rough clothes of prisoners, debtors, the disappeared. Faces Cyrus had spent twenty years not remembering. Faces he remembered anyway, every one, because he'd been the one who found the ledgers, who read the names, who understood what the numbers meant.

"They're the ones," Sera whispered. Her fragments were trembling in their wrappings, humming against each other like struck bells. "The sacrifices. The ones the kings fed to the covenant."

"All nine thousand," Tam said. "Give or take. The covenant kept what it was fed. And when the covenant broke—" he smiled, and there was no crook in it now, only grief worn smooth by two decades, "—we finally got to leave. Except the door out was barred, so we've been standing in the hallway ever since. Waiting for our brother to come home."

"I'm not—" Cyrus started.

"Don't." Tam's voice cracked across the ridge like a whip, and for a moment it wasn't a dead man speaking but the boy who'd once thrown Cyrus into the millpond for lying about a broken window. "Don't you dare stand there and tell me what you're not. I watched you walk away from me on the exile road. I called your name until my voice gave out. So you'll listen now, and you'll hear all of it, and then you can decide what you are."

The circle had gone very quiet. Maren hadn't moved. Her hand rested near her blade, but her eyes were on Cyrus, reading him the way she read crime scenes — every flinch catalogued.

"I listened," Cyrus said quietly. "On the road. I heard you calling."

"Then why didn't you stop?"

"Because if I'd turned around, I'd have taken you with me. And where I was going, they'd have killed you too." His hands were shaking. Twenty years of control, undone by a dead boy in muddy boots. "The kings' hunters were already on the road. I'd shattered the covenant. There was no hiding what I was. If you'd been beside me—"

"You decided for me." Tam stepped closer. The light of him made the broken wards flare. "That's the whole of it, isn't it? Not mercy. Not strategy. You decided. Like you decided to break the covenant without telling a soul. Like you decided to vanish instead of standing trial. Like you decided, just now, that we'd hold the line while you searched for a fragment you have no idea how to find." He shook his head slowly. "You carry everything alone and call it love."

"That's not fair."

"No," Tam agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about any of this is fair. Nine thousand souls eaten by kings who wanted cheap magic isn't fair. A world losing its stars because one man couldn't find a gentler way isn't fair." He spread his hands, and the host of the dead shifted with the gesture, a tide of pale faces. "But fairness left the room a long time ago, brother. What's left is truth. So here's mine."

He reached up, and unbuttoned the collar of his ghost-shirt with fingers that were more memory than matter, the way a man does when he's about to show a scar.

"The night you broke the covenant, you didn't just shatter it. The rite you used — the counter-rite, the one the elder mages swore was impossible — it needed a vessel. Something to hold the pieces apart so they couldn't crawl back together into the kings' hands. Four fragments, four anchors. The first three you scattered — Ives took one, the temple drowned with the second, and the third you hid where only a traitor would think to look, inside the Crown itself. Clever. Cruel. Very you."

"And the fourth," Cyrus said. His mouth had gone dry.

"The rite needed somewhere to put the keystone. The piece that binds the other three. It couldn't be buried — the kings would dig. Couldn't be warded — the kings would unravel it. It had to be somewhere that would run, and hide, and lie, and stay alive." Tam's hand came to rest over his own heart, over the place where a brand would sit on living flesh. "It had to be someone who would never, ever let himself be found."

The world went very small and very loud.

Cyrus felt it before he understood it — a heat between his shoulder blades, low, just above the beltline, a heat he had carried for twenty years and explained a hundred ways. An old burn. A scar from the fall of Greyharrow's granary fire. A mark from a life he'd buried. He'd never once looked at it in a mirror, because looking meant remembering, and remembering meant—

He tore open his shirt and twisted, and Sera caught his arm to hold him steady, and Maren lifted a lantern, and there it was.

A sigil. Burned into his skin in lines finer than any scar, silver-white against the old burn-tissue, and it was moving — slowly, gently, the way the Sigil itself had once moved across the sky. Three arms of it reaching outward, searching. A fourth space, empty, waiting.

The shape of the covenant. Missing exactly one piece.

"No," Cyrus said.

"Yes," said Tam.

"No. He said — the elder mage said *where no king would ever think to look*. I thought he meant—"

"He meant you." Sera's voice was strange, stripped bare. She was staring at the sigil on his back the way she'd stared at her first working spell, at fifteen, in a cellar in Greyharrow. "You absolute *fool*. You've been carrying it for twenty years. Every ward you slipped past, every hunter you dodged — you weren't running from them. You were *carrying* it. If Maren had caught you any time in two decades—"

"Then the kings win," Maren finished quietly. "The warrant was never for arrest. I know that now. They needed him dead before he could speak — or before anyone thought to *look* at him." She met Cyrus's eyes. "I nearly delivered you to them twice. Once in the ash-fields. Once at the vault door."

"But you didn't," Cyrus said.

"But I didn't," she agreed, and something passed between them that neither of them named.

Cyrus's legs gave out. He sat down hard among the fractured stones, in the green light of the dying wards, surrounded by the dead of nine thousand names, and laughed — a short, broken sound with nothing of humor in it. "Twenty years. I hid from myself. I sold luck-charms in a market square with the last piece of the covenant sweating against my spine, and I never once—"

"You couldn't have known," Sera started.

"I could have *looked*." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "One mirror. One moment of honesty in twenty years, and I'd have known. But looking meant remembering that night, and I built my whole life on not remembering, so I wore a scar story instead of the truth, and now—" He dropped his hands. "Now the void is at the door and the keystone has been riding on my back the entire time."

"Not the entire time," Tam said. His voice had gentled, which was somehow worse. "Only since the wards fell. Before that, the sigil slept. It woke when the sky broke — it's been waking *you* too, haven't you noticed? The storms coming easier. The power feeling like home." He crouched in front of his brother, translucent knees bending, and for a moment they were just two boys from a mill town, one of them dead. "The covenant chose well, even if you didn't choose at all. You're the only man alive who both broke it and mourned it. That's who has to reforge it. Someone who knows exactly what it costs."

Above them, the starless dark shuddered.

It was not wind. It was not the void's patient coiling. It was a sound — vast, subsonic, felt in the teeth and the breastbone — and every ghost on the wardline turned toward it as one, nine thousand faces tilting like flowers toward a sun that wasn't there.

"They're coming," Tam said, rising. All the gentleness drained out of him, replaced by something urgent and bright. "Not the void. The *living*. Torches on the south road. A lot of them. Soldiers, and wagons, and—" his face flickered, ghost-quick, seeing things the dead see, "—chains. They have someone in chains."

Sera went white. "How many days did you say we had?"

"None," Tam said. "They're a half-night out. They're bringing a rite of their own, brother. The kings' rite. The one that feeds souls to the lattice." He looked at Cyrus, and then past him, at the circle, at Maren, at the three fragments humming in Sera's arms. "They mean to finish what their masters started. And they're bringing a prisoner whose face I know, whose name I know, who taught half these ghosts' grandchildren their letters—"

"Sera," Cyrus breathed, at the same moment Sera said, "They have *me*?"

And Tam, terrible and clear, said: "No. They have the woman who traded herself at the border house. They have your friend. And they're marching her to this exact spot, because the spymaster read the sealed files too — and he knows the reforging needs a soul equal to the kings' stolen thousands."

The dark beyond the wards leaned closer, listening.

"So," Tam said, and held out his insubstantial hand to his brother, "how fast can you work?"
