## 21. The Charm-Seller's Stall

Greyharrow had learned to live with magic the way a man learns to live with an old scar — carefully, half-noticing it, touching it only to be sure it was still there.

Maren Solace walked the market road on a grey morning in the second spring after the relighting, and she counted the small wonders without meaning to. A woman at the fountain coaxing her laundry dry with a cupped hand and a whispered word. Two boys trading sparks between their fingers like marbles, harmless ones that popped and smelled faintly of rain. A hedge-healer's shingle nailed above a door that had once been a chandler's: *Wounds, fevers, births. No questions.* The Crown hadn't legalized any of it. The Crown had simply stopped being able to stop it, and somewhere in the capital, clerks were still drafting proclamations about it that nobody read.

She had resigned her commission eleven months ago. She still wore the coat out of habit, minus the sigil of office, which she'd kept in a drawer until Sera Ives had told her — with that flat, unimpressed stare that could strip paint — that keeping a witch-hunter's badge as a souvenir was exactly the kind of sentimentality she'd expect from someone who'd once arrested three grandmothers for weather-sense.

So the drawer held nothing now, and Maren carried instead a warrant book full of cases against men who had actually hurt people. Spymaster Corvane had gone to trial in the autumn. It had taken four days. She had testified for two of them. When the verdict came down, she'd felt nothing so clean as satisfaction — just a loosening, like a knot untied after years, leaving her unsure what shape her hands were supposed to make.

The market smelled of bread and wet stone and, underneath everything, the sea. Greyharrow always smelled of the sea. But there was something else this morning, something new, and it took her half the length of the market road to place it because it was so absurdly ordinary:

Charms.

A stall. Small, wedged between a fishmonger and a woman selling apples, draped in faded blue cloth that might once have been a sail. Charms hung from every crossbar on twine and wire — little braided knots, river stones wrapped in copper, feathers bound with red thread, glass beads that caught the light and threw it back slightly wrong, slightly *warm*. A hand-painted sign leaned against the table leg in cheerful, uneven letters:

*SMALL CHARMS. HONEST PRICES. LUCK A SPECIALTY.*

Behind the stall stood a young woman, maybe twenty, maybe younger — hard to say, the way some faces refuse to commit to an age. Dark hair cut blunt at the jaw. Hands quick and sure as she re-strung a bead. And when she looked up at Maren and smiled, easy and open, the smile itself was unremarkable.

It was the eyes that stopped Maren's feet on the cobbles.

Grey. Not the grey of slate or smoke or stormcloud, though they held all those greys in them if you looked too long. These were a specific grey, a grey Maren had last seen across an interrogation table by lamplight, then again across a vault floor lit by dying gold, then one final time at the wardline while the world tore itself a new sky. A grey that had watched her with terrible patience while it explained, in a voice like distant thunder deciding whether to come closer, why the old kings deserved what they got.

Those eyes belonged to Orsen Vael.

These eyes did not know her at all.

* * *

Maren did not believe in coincidence. Twenty years of hunting people who didn't want to be found had beaten belief in coincidence out of her the way a smith beats dents out of a blade. So she did not tell herself it was chance. She told herself it was the covenant, or the reforging, or whatever alchemy of sacrifice Sera still refused to fully describe — and even that was only a kind of naming, not an understanding.

But understanding could wait. First, procedure. Old habits.

She drifted along the row of stalls, pretending interest in the apples, and watched the young woman work. A child bought a knot-charm "for my gran's cough" and the seller pressed an extra feather into her palm and said, *free, for being brave about the price*, and the girl went off glowing. A fisherman haggled over a luck-stone and lost, cheerfully. An old man tried to pay with a story instead of coin and the young woman listened to all of it, nodding, and gave him a charm anyway, and Maren saw her tuck his story away somewhere behind her eyes like it was currency too.

There was no ledger. There was no pattern to what she charged. Maren watched for the better part of an hour and could find none, and that absence of pattern told her more than a pattern would have. This wasn't commerce. It was distribution. Kindness, retail.

And the girl never stumbled. Never paused mid-word searching for something. People with holes in their memory flinch at the edges of them; Maren had interrogated enough of the memory-touched to know. This one moved through her days like water through a channel worn smooth — complete, unhaunted, whole within whatever shape she'd been given.

Except once.

A wagon rumbled past on the cobbles, iron-rimmed wheels, and thunder followed it — just the sound, just the street, but the girl's head came up sharply and her hands went still on the twine and for three heartbeats her face did something complicated and far away, like a person hearing a song from a country they've never visited. Then the wagon passed, and the stillness passed, and she was smiling at her next customer as if nothing in the world had ever been heavy.

Maren bought an apple she didn't want and ate it slowly and thought about Tam Vael's ghost in the drowned temple, demanding remembrance. She thought about a name dissolving into a lattice of light while thousands of ghosts sang. She thought: *the covenant kept its arithmetic. Something had to remain. Something always remains.*

Then she ran out of apple, and courage, and walked over.

* * *

"Morning," the girl said. "Looking for anything particular, or letting luck find you?"

"Letting it find me," Maren said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that.

"Good instinct." The girl gestured at the hanging charms. "Most people grab at luck like it's running from them. Luck hates that. You have to stand still and let it land."

"Is that how it works?"

"That's how *I* work. Whether it's how luck works is anyone's guess." She laughed — a bright, unguarded sound — and started sorting through her stock. "What do you do? You've got the look of someone whose work follows them home."

"I used to enforce the law," Maren said. "Now I mostly investigate it."

The girl's hands paused, just briefly, on a braided cord. "Used to hunt witches?" she asked lightly. Too lightly.

"Once. Long time ago."

"Huh." She resumed sorting. Her tone stayed pleasant, but something under it had shifted — not fear, not anger. Recognition without content. The way you might feel about a word in a language you don't speak. "Well," she said, "then maybe you want this one."

She held up a charm Maren hadn't noticed before, set apart from the others on its own hook: a simple thing, a grey river stone in a cage of copper wire, with one small bead of storm-glass threaded above it. It caught the morning light and held it wrong, warm, the way the other beads did, but deeper. Like looking down a well that went further than wells go.

"What is it?"

"Honestly? I don't know." The girl turned it in her fingers. "I woke up with it. Before I woke up with the stall, before — well, before most things, far as I can tell. It's the only one I've never been able to sell, and I've tried. Every time someone reaches for it, I find a reason. Price is wrong. Wrong day. Wrong person." She shrugged, embarrassed. "Maybe it's waiting."

"For what?"

"No idea. Waiting things rarely send invitations." She weighed it in her palm. "For you, though — for you it feels right. Don't ask me how I know. I just make things and I know things about them, and this one says your name, sort of. Not your actual name. More like..." She frowned, groping. "Like the shape of your name. The weight of it."

Maren's throat was very tight. "How much?"

The girl studied her a moment longer, grey eyes steady, and Maren let herself be studied. Let those eyes look at her and see nothing but a tired woman in an old coat. There was mercy in that, of a strange and enormous kind. All the years Maren had spent putting faces to crimes, and here was the face behind the greatest crime and greatest salvation of the age, looking at her with the blank kindness of a stranger, and asking nothing, owing nothing, remembering nothing.

"For you," the girl said, "one copper."

"That's not honest prices."

"It is today." She pressed the charm into Maren's hand and closed Maren's fingers over it, and her touch was warm, and static prickled faintly between their palms — barely there, the ghost of a ghost of a storm. The girl blinked at the sensation, puzzled, then smiled. "Huh. Tingly. Good omen, probably."

Maren paid the copper. Her hand should have shaken. It didn't, and somehow that was worse.

"Thank you," Maren said.

"You're welcome. Come back if it stops working. Come back even if it doesn't." The girl was already turning to her next customer, a nervous apprentice with coins clutched in both fists. "Luck finds the ones who stand still!"

Maren stood still for one more moment. Just one.

Then she walked away, and did not look back, because she knew — with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life reading people, and had finally learned to read herself — that if she looked back she would say something. *Your name was Orsen. You broke the sky to save us. You erased yourself so that no one would ever own what you gave.* And none of it belonged in this bright small life, and none of it was hers to give, and the girl would only tilt her head and smile politely at a madwoman in a dead man's coat.

Sera would want to know. Of course Sera would want to know; Sera wanted to know everything, wrote everything, was even now assembling the true history into the covenant's memory so that no king could twist it again. Maren owed her this. The circle owed each other everything.

But not today. Today the knowledge sat in Maren's chest like a coal banked under ash, warm and private and hers. Let the histories record the storm-caller. Let the circles sing about the martyr. Here, in a fish market, between a fishmonger and an apple-seller, a young woman with familiar grey eyes sold luck for a copper and gave feathers away free to children, and remembered nothing but kindness.

Some truths were load-bearing. You didn't move those. You built around them, and you were grateful for the shelter.

Maren walked home through the market with the charm in her pocket, and when she took it out that evening and held it to the lamp, the storm-glass bead glowed softly, steadily, like something patient. Like something that had waited a long time and didn't mind waiting a while more.

Outside her window, over the roofs of Greyharrow, the new stars burned — changed, everyone said, rearranged, no constellation where it used to be. Children had already begun inventing names for them.

And far across the city, in a house full of candlelight and open books, Sera Ives dipped her pen and began, at last, the final chapter of the true account — unaware that the ending had just walked into a market stall and sold itself for one copper, and was happy.
