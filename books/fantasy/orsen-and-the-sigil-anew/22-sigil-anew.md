## 22. Sigil Anew

The book had no title page.

That was the first thing Sera decided, sitting in the workroom above the charm-stall on Copperlane with rain ticking against the shutters and a blank codex open before her — bound in grey leather, sewn by her own hands over three sleepless nights, its pages empty as a held breath. A title invited ownership. A title could be claimed, argued over, burned by one king and reprinted by the next with flattering amendments. The covenant's memory would begin where all honest things begin: mid-sentence, mid-life, with no grand opening and no promise of an ending.

It began instead with a list of names.

Not kings. Not heroes. She wrote them small and plain, in ink she'd ground herself from soot and rainwater: every soul the old crowns had fed into the lattice across four hundred years, as many as the sealed files and Tam's ghosts between them could recover. Eleven thousand, two hundred and six. She had wept at nine hundred and stopped counting her tears somewhere past two thousand, because the work was bigger than grief and grief would still be there when the work was done. Each name got a line. Each line got, where anything was known of them, a single fact — *a cooper from Vell's Landing; sang; afraid of deep water* — because the old kings had fed the covenant bodies and called them tribute, and Sera meant to feed it back their lives and call them people.

The covenant took the pages the way dry earth takes rain.

She felt it happen each time she finished one. A faint warmth under her palms, a hum in the floorboards, the candle flames leaning toward the book like listeners. The new covenant was young and hungry for exactly this — not power, not worship, but *witness*. Orsen had unmade himself to give it that appetite. The least she could do was keep it fed on truth.

* * *

Maren Solace came on the fourth evening, dripping, carrying a paper-wrapped parcel that smelled of fried dough, and let herself in without knocking because knocking had never once occurred to her.

"You look terrible," Maren said, setting the parcel on the worktable beside eleven thousand names.

"I've been writing for four days."

"I've been rebuilding a justice system for four months. We're neither of us pretty." She unwrapped the dough, took half without asking, and dropped into the chair across the table. Her eyes went to the codex, and stayed there. "That's it? That's the whole plan? A book?"

"A book in the covenant," Sera said. "Which is everywhere now. In the rain, in the hearth-smoke, in the hedge-charms and the well-water and the little storms children call for fun. You can't burn what's written into weather, Maren. You can't edit a thing that reads itself back to anyone who asks." She turned a page toward her. "A king can lie to his chroniclers. He can't lie to the sky."

Maren chewed. Considered. "And if a king decides he doesn't need to lie? If he just decides magic is his again, the way it was before?"

"He'd have to rewrite the covenant itself. Which means reforging it. Which means—" Sera tapped the list of names, "—this. Souls, freely given, equal to what was stolen. There are no shortcuts left. I made sure of that when I helped write the terms." She had spent a week of the reforging's aftermath doing nothing else, standing at the wardline with the ghosts while the terms settled into the lattice like sediment into clear water: *no bloodline claims it, no crown commands it, no soul is taken, only given.* "Orsen paid the price once. The covenant remembers what it cost him. It will not be cheap again."

"Good," Maren said simply. Then, quieter, in the voice she used maybe twice a year: "Do you miss him?"

Sera looked out the shutter at the rain. Greyharrow had learned to love rain again — real rain, ordinary rain, rain that was only water — but there were evenings like this one when the clouds stacked up green-grey over the harbor and everyone in the city paused, just for a breath, remembering a night when the sky had answered something else.

"Every day," she said. "But I mostly miss the man who wouldn't tell me the truth. The one who sold luck-charms and flinched when I said his name wrong. I got almost no time with the man underneath." She smiled, and it hurt, and she let it. "So I'm making sure nobody else gets hidden like that. Nobody gets rewritten. Not him, not the kings' victims, not even the kings themselves — I wrote their crimes down too, with their names attached. Let them be remembered accurately. It's crueler than hanging."

Maren barked a laugh. "You're terrifying."

"I'm a schoolteacher now, effectively."

"Same thing."

* * *

The writing took the rest of the season.

Sera worked through autumn while the circles grew — openly now, lawfully, though *lawful* was still a generous word in some provinces where magistrates had spent twenty years learning to fear hedge-witches and hadn't yet unlearned it. Maren handled most of that, riding circuit with a warrant book full of arrests she'd made of other witch-hunters, which Sera found deeply funny and Maren found merely correct. The Crown's new council, such as it was, had discovered that a realm full of small magics was ungovernable by fear and grudgingly governable by paperwork. Sera filed paperwork. It was, she reflected, the most subversive act of her life.

Each finished section she read aloud to the codex, and the covenant drank it in:

The night the stars went out, and how a charm-seller named Cyrus Hartwell stood in a market square with storm-light crawling up his arms, seen at last after twenty years of hiding.

The wardline, whispering his true name to the ash.

Sera's own circle, and the bargain she'd offered him — *confess, or don't come to me* — and how he'd refused, and how she'd hated him for it, and how she'd been right to hate him and wrong about everything else.

Tam Vael, drowned temple, remembrance given honestly.

Maren Solace, asking one question in the Crown vault: *why did you break it?* And the answer. All of it. The feeding pens beneath the old capital, the prisoners brought up in carts, the kings' careful arithmetic of souls. She wrote Maren's disobedience in full — the request for sealed files instead of a promotion, the arrest of the spymaster mid-rite, the turning of the warrant — because if the histories taught anything, it should teach that the law is a tool and tools must sometimes be picked up by the handle end.

And then the last chapters, which were hardest: the wardline, the void arriving early, the circle holding it back with raw untrained magic while the sky screamed. The ghosts rising. Tam's voice revealing the final fragment branded into Orsen's own skin since the night he broke the covenant. The rite demanding a soul equal to thousands.

And Orsen Vael, standing before survivors and ghosts and enemies, saying clearly, so the whole listening world could hear: *I am not Cyrus Hartwell. I am Orsen Vael. I broke the covenant, and here is why, and here is what I owe.*

And then the gift. Not his life — his name, his history, every memory that made him *him*, poured into the lattice so that magic would belong to no bloodline and no crown and no legend. So that no one would ever build a shrine to Orsen Vael and start charging admission.

The stars relit changed — softer, closer-seeming, faintly green at the edges — and the world woke up with small magics in its pockets like loose coins.

When she finished that final passage, the codex glowed for a long time, and the rain outside fell upward for the space of three heartbeats, and Sera sat alone in the workroom and cried the way you cry at a funeral that has finally, finally ended.

* * *

Winter came, and with it the child.

Sera saw her first at the market — the same girl from the night the stars went out, taller now, gap-toothed, trailing a mother loaded with baskets. On that terrible night she'd been clutching a luck-charm bought from a grey-eyed stranger, and when the sky fell apart she'd run to him, and he'd knelt, and told her something Sera had never learned the content of. Whatever it was, it had kept. The girl had been one of the first in the new circles' open lessons, a natural, quick and fearless, the kind of student who asked *why* until the teacher ran out of answers.

On a bright cold morning in Deepwinter, Sera was haggling over apples when the girl climbed onto the fountain rim in the middle of the square, held up both hands like a conductor, and called a storm.

A small one. A deliberate one. Clouds gathered over the fountain — a patch of sky perhaps ten feet wide, boiling gently, going grey-green — and then snow began to fall in a slow, glittering column, catching the sunlight, and the girl laughed out loud with her whole body, head thrown back, arms wide, absolutely lit up with the joy of having *asked the sky for something and been answered.*

Nobody panicked. That was the miracle Sera stood still to witness. Two years ago a child doing this would have been dragged inside, hidden, whispered about, possibly reported. Now the market simply adjusted around her — a fishmonger grinned, an old man tipped his hat to the little cloud, someone's dog tried to catch snowflakes. The girl's mother sighed the sigh of a woman whose daughter does this weekly, paid the apple seller, and waited with the patience of long practice for the weather to lose interest.

The storm sparkled itself out. The girl hopped down, flushed and triumphant, and caught Sera watching.

"Did you see?" she demanded. "I made it *sparkle*. Last time it was just regular snow but Master Fen says if you hold the shape loose it catches the light better—"

"I saw," Sera said. "It was the best sparkle in Greyharrow."

The girl beamed, then squinted at her. "You're the history lady. From the circle. Are you really putting everything in the book? Even bad stuff?"

"Especially the bad stuff."

"Why?"

Sera crouched to her level. Up close, the girl's eyes were brown, ordinary, nothing like the grey ones Sera still caught glimpses of in strangers and doorways and mirrors at odd moments — the covenant wearing kindness like a coat, scattered thin through the world, remembering nothing. But the question was a good one, sharp as any Orsen's apprentice had ever asked him, and Sera answered it the way she wished someone had answered her at that age.

"Because someday someone powerful is going to want the story to say something else," she said. "They'll want it neat. They'll want heroes without crimes and crimes without reasons. And when that day comes, the sky itself will remember what actually happened, and say so, and no one — not a king, not a council, not a whole army — can make the sky stop talking." She tapped the girl's sternum lightly. "You're part of the sky now. Every one of you circle kids is. That's the whole point."

The girl considered this with the gravity of an eight-year-old digesting a large idea, then nodded once, decisively, the way Maren nodded at a closed case.

"Okay," she said. "But write down the sparkle part too. History shouldn't be all sad."

"No," Sera agreed, standing, her knees cracking. "It shouldn't."

The girl ran off after her mother, and above the square the last few flakes of her private winter drifted down through sunlight, glittering, harmless, free — falling on cobbles and cart-tops and the awning of a certain charm-stall, where a young woman with familiar grey eyes looked up from her work, watched the snow with a smile she couldn't explain, and felt, for no reason at all, completely and quietly happy.

Sera walked home through the melting sparkle, opened the untitled book, and began a new page.

*Here ends the account of the breaking and the reforging,* she wrote. *It does not end. Nothing true ever does. To whoever reads this — king, commoner, circle-child, stranger — the covenant belongs to you now, and you belong to it. Keep it kind. Keep it awake.*

*Keep watch.*

Outside, over Greyharrow, over the wards that were no longer walls but windows, the changed stars came out one by one — and if you knew how to look, they seemed to lean a little closer than stars used to, listening, remembering, waiting to be asked.
