## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

I have sold a hundred lies in my life, and every one of them was true.

That's the trouble with charms in Vellmark. The Sigil-law says magic is dead, has been dead for sixty years, ever since the border wards went up and the wild realm of Orsen was sealed away like a bad memory. So when a woman buys a luck-knot from my stall and her cow stops dying, that isn't magic. That's coincidence. When a fisherman wears one of my storm-charms and comes home dry through weather that drowned half the quay, that's good seamanship. The charms work because they can't. Say otherwise and you'll hang from the Bellgate with your tongue nailed to the wood, which is a sentence I've seen carried out twice, and both times I made very sure to be selling ribbons elsewhere.

So I lie. Cheerfully. Professionally. *Just knotted thread and river-stone, mistress, nothing but craft.* And underneath the lie, quiet as a mouse under floorboards, lives the thing I actually am: a beast-speaker. Old blood. Grandmother Merrin's blood. The kind of blood the wardens burn for.

The mice talk to me. That's how it started, when I was small — field-mice chattering at the edge of Merrin's garden, telling me where the cat was sleeping, which is the sort of gift that seems charming until you realize a child who hears mice will eventually hear everything else. Horses. Dogs. Once, terribly, a raven, who had opinions about me that were unkind and accurate. I learned early to smile and nod and pretend the animals were just being animals, because the alternative was learning what happens to people like us, and Merrin had already taught me that lesson with her disappearance when I was nine. No body. No trial. Just an empty house and a warden-captain's polite note saying she'd been *relocated for questioning*, and everyone in Vellmark understanding exactly what that meant while pretending they didn't.

Ten years since. I'm twenty-six now, and I run a charm-stall off Tanner's Row with three other liars, and I love them the way you love people you've chosen instead of the ones who got burned.

There's Brannick, who sells love-charms he swears are "just good psychology," delivered with a wink so practiced it could open locks. There's Odile, sharp as a fish-gutting knife, whose protective sigils are drawn so precisely that even the wardens buy them for their own doorways, which is either the best joke or the most dangerous game in the city. And there's little Fen, sixteen, apprentice, mostly employed carrying things and asking questions I've spent a decade teaching him not to ask.

"Tam," Fen said this morning, holding up a knot of red thread, "why does this one work better if you hum while you tie it?"

"It doesn't," I said. "It works better because humming makes you slow down, and slow knots hold."

He looked at me the way he always does — like he could see straight through me, which sometimes I worry he can. Fen has old blood too. I can smell it on him, faintly, the way you smell rain coming. He doesn't know what he is yet. I intend for him never to find out, or at least not until I'm dead and can't be questioned about what I knew.

* * *

The market closed late that day. Harvest-tide, always busy, everyone wanting charms against the coming winter as though thread and stone could argue with frost. By the time I shuttered the stall the lamps were lit and the sky over Vellmark was doing its usual trick: half the stars smothered by the glow of the city, the rest scattered thin above the rooftops like salt spilled on black cloth.

I counted them. Not deliberately — it's a habit Merrin gave me before she went, one of the small superstitions she passed down along with the blood. *Count the stars, Tam. If they're all there, the covenant holds.* I never asked what covenant. She died — vanished — before I was old enough to ask properly, and after that, counting stars felt less like superstition and more like keeping her grave tidy.

That night I counted forty-one.

Then thirty-eight.

Then, between one breath and the next, none.

Not clouded. Not hidden. *Gone*, the way a candle goes when pinched — the whole sky going matte and blind above me, and the dark that came down wasn't night-dark, it was something older, something with weight. Every lamp in Tanner's Row guttered at once. The wards — I couldn't see them, nobody could, they were lines of power woven along the city walls sixty years ago — but I felt them go the way you feel a tooth crack: a high, wrong note through the bones of the world, then silence, then the sick knowledge that something that held is holding no longer.

Fen was still in the shop, sweeping. He looked up at the ceiling as if the answer might be written there.

"Tam," he said. "What was that?"

"Wind," I said, because lying to Fen had become reflex, and hated myself for it even as the words left my mouth. "Go home. Lock your door. Don't open it for anyone wearing grey."

Because that's what came next. Even before the bells started — and oh, they started, every bell in Vellmark at once, the alarm-rhythm, the one that means *not fire, not flood, get inside and stay there* — I saw them. Wardens. Grey cloaks, iron sigils, moving down Tanner's Row in pairs with the calm of soldiers who already know what's coming and have decided not to panic about it. They weren't running toward the walls. They were running toward the low town. Toward the tanner's quarter, the charm-sellers' street, the places where people like me lived our careful little lives.

They knew. Whatever broke tonight, they knew it was coming, and they knew who to collect.

I barred the shop door and told myself I'd wait it out. Told myself the story I always tell: keep your head down, sell your ribbons, survive. But my hands wouldn't stop shaking, and the mice — the few brave ones who'd stayed in my walls rather than fleeing into the dark — were screaming. Not squeaking. *Screaming*, in the small frantic language of their kind, and what they said, translated from whisker-logic into something a human woman could act on, was this:

*It is coming. It is hurt. Do not let the grey men take it.*

Take *what?* I stood in my dark shop listening to mice and understood nothing, and then, at half past the hour, the sky itself answered.

* * *

It came through the roof.

Not gently. A griffin — an actual griffin, wings like sails, forequarters eagle and hindquarters lion, the kind of creature I'd only ever seen in Merrin's forbidden sketchbooks — came down through my tiles in a spiral of broken slate and feathers, and landed in the middle of my shop floor with a sound like a ship dropping anchor.

It was dying. I could see that much without any gift at all: the wound along its flank, deep as my hand, weeping something dark; the way its sides heaved; the way its eyes, gold as coins, had gone dim at the edges. What I needed the gift for came next, and it came whether I was ready or not.

It spoke.

Not in the way birds mimic. In words. In *her* voice — Merrin's voice, the particular rough warmth of it, the accent from the eastern villages that I hadn't heard in seventeen years and recognized in half a heartbeat the way you recognize your own name screamed in the dark.

"Tamsin Eastwick. My daughter's daughter. The stars went out because the covenant broke, and the covenant broke because men broke it, and now the old blood must choose."

I stood there with a broom in my hands, and I want to be honest about what happened next, because this is the part I'll be judged by, whatever judgments come.

My first instinct was terror. Pure, animal, sensible terror. A griffin in my shop was death. A griffin speaking in my grandmother's voice was death twice over, and speaking *to me by name* was the kind of death they write warnings about. The correct move — the move that would have kept me alive, kept Fen alive, kept everything safe and small and survivable — was to run out the back, fetch a warden, say *this creature fell into my shop, I know nothing of it,* and let the grey cloaks do what grey cloaks do.

I thought about it. I want that recorded. I stood for the length of ten heartbeats and genuinely considered handing it over, and the only reason I didn't — the shameful, human, ordinary reason — was that it looked at me. Dim eyes, fading fast, and in them not fear but something worse: trust. It had crossed a broken border and a burning sky and a city full of hunters, and it had come to *me*, to the granddaughter of the woman whose voice it wore, and it was going to die either way, and the only question left was whether it died among friends.

The mice had told me not to let the grey men take it. Merrin's voice had told me the old blood must choose. And outside my shutters, boots were gathering — I could hear them, six pairs at least, moving up Tanner's Row with the unhurried confidence of men who know every door will open for them.

"Choose," the griffin said again, softer now, the voice fraying at the edges. "Tamsin. Little star-counter. Choose."

I put down the broom.

I did the arithmetic every fugitive does — back door, cellar, the drainage tunnel Odile once showed me drunk and swore me to secrecy over — and then I did something no arithmetic supports. I knelt beside it. I put my hands on the wound, and I spoke to it in the way I'd spoken to horses and ravens and frightened children all my life, the way I'd sworn never to speak where anyone could hear.

"I've got you," I said. "Whatever you are, whoever sent you — I've got you. But you have to be quiet, and you have to be quick, because they're coming, and I am done being small."

Its head came up. Just barely. Enough that its beak touched my hair, gentle as an apology, and it breathed against my ear in Merrin's voice one more time:

"The heir of Orsen waits at the old ferry crossing. Bring the star-counter. Bring her *quickly*."

And that is how, at half past midnight on the night the stars went out, I found myself prying up my own floorboards, dragging a wounded griffin the size of a haycart into my cellar, stuffing gold into Fen's coat pocket with instructions to tell anyone who asked that his mistress had gone to visit her aunt in the country, and climbing out through the drainage tunnel into the darkened wilds beyond the walls — carrying, on my back and on my conscience, the last living piece of a covenant I didn't know existed, hunted by men I'd spent my whole life obeying, toward a ferry crossing where someone called *the heir of Orsen* was waiting for me.

I didn't know who the heir was. I didn't know what a covenant was, not really. I didn't know why the wardens wanted the griffin, or what Serl's grey cloaks would do to a charm-seller caught harboring one.

I knew only that the stars had gone out, my grandmother's voice had come home to roost after seventeen years, and for the first time in ten years of careful, cowardly, necessary lying —

I had stopped lying.

God help me, I had stopped.
