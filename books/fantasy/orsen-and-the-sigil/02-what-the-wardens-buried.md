## 2. What the Wardens Buried

The wilds were supposed to be a story.

That's what I'd always believed — the way you believe in the bogeyman, or honest tax collectors. A place beyond the border wards where the old blood ran thick and the trees had opinions. Grandmother used to tell me about it when I was small, before she stopped telling me anything at all: *the realm of Orsen, Tamsin, where every voice has an echo and nothing is ever truly silent.*

I'd assumed she meant it as a warning.

Flying on the back of a wounded griffin through a sky with no stars in it, I revised my assumptions considerably.

Her name was Serevanne — *Serry*, because even dying creatures of legend apparently tolerate nicknames from terrified women clinging to their necks — and she flew badly. One wing dragged. Every few hundred yards her whole body shuddered like a door slamming in wind, and I felt each shudder through my knees where they pressed against feathers that were warm and wrong, warm like fever.

*East,* she said in Grandmother's voice, which was still doing things to my chest I didn't want to examine. *Not for you. For him.*

"For who?"

She didn't answer. Griffins, I was learning, had the conversational generosity of cats.

Below us Vellmark shrank to a smudge of lamplight against the dark, and then not even that. The border wards should have been visible from here — Grandmother's stories said they burned silver on clear nights, a seam of light stitching two worlds together. There was nothing. Just darkness flowing into darkness, the way water flows into water, no boundary at all.

Which meant the thing keeping my city safe from whatever lived out here wasn't broken.

It was *gone*. Removed. Unpicked stitch by stitch by someone with patience and permission.

I thought about the Sigil-law they taught us in school. *Magic is theft,* the primers said. *Magic is the wound the world never healed.* Ten years I'd hidden behind harmless charms — luck-knots and headache-salves and little woven birds that turned their heads if you spoke kindly to them, all of it small enough to pass as craft, all of it a lie I told daily with my whole life.

And all of it, apparently, built on top of a much older lie. Someone had stolen something enormous, and the wardens' answer was to hunt the grandchildren of everyone who'd helped make it.

Serry banked left and my stomach went with her. Below, the trees began — not gradually, but all at once, a black sea of them, and the air changed. It tasted greener. Older. Like biting into a word you didn't know you remembered.

*He waits at the standing stones,* Serry said. Her voice frayed on the last word. *Tell Kaelen — tell him his mother's seal held. Tell him—*

Then the wing gave out entirely.

* * *

We hit the treeline the way grief hits you: no warning, total commitment. I remember branches, and screaming (mine), and the strange softness of moss that broke our fall like it had been waiting to. I remember lying in the dark with my ribs singing and Serry's blood soaking through my sleeve, hot as tea.

And I remember the voice.

"You're either very brave or very stupid, landing a griffin in warden country."

A man stepped out of the dark like he was part of it — tall, grey-eyed, wearing travel-leathers that had been fine once and were now mostly apology. He had a sword he carried like he'd rather not, and a face that looked like it had been exiled along with everything else.

"Kaelen," Serry rasped, before I could say anything. "Kaelen. Your mother's seal held."

He went very still. Then he was on his knees beside us, hands hovering over her wound without touching, the way you hover over something you're afraid will break completely if you admit how much it matters.

"Serry." His voice cracked down the middle. "What did they do to you?"

"Old wound. Old war." She turned her head toward me, one great golden eye catching what little light there was. "Tamsin Eastwick. Granddaughter of Maren. She speaks."

"I don't—" I started, because ten years of habit dies hard. "I sell charms. Little ones. Harmless—"

"She speaks," Serry repeated, with the patience of the dying, "and she lies badly. Kaelen. The Heartstone. They mean to unmake it entirely. She must see what they buried."

Kaelen looked up at me then, and there was something in his face I recognized, because I saw it in my own mirror every morning: the specific exhaustion of someone who has been hiding so long they've forgotten what their real face is for.

"Your grandmother," he said quietly, "helped forge the covenant between my world and yours. Did you know that?"

"I knew she traveled. I knew she came back quiet."

"The covenant wasn't a wall, Tamsin. It was a *handshake*. Two realms agreeing to share one border, one magic, one future. And twenty years ago, certain men in your city decided a handshake was inefficient. That power shared is power halved." He looked back at Serry, and his jaw tightened. "So they buried it. Dug the Heartstone out of its bed, chained it under the wards, and let the wilds start starving. And when anyone with old blood might have objected—"

"The wardens," I said.

"The wardens."

I sat down in the moss, because my legs had opinions of their own. All those years of hiding. All those charms sold with a smile and a lowered gaze. We weren't criminals. We were witnesses. And the men who'd stolen a covenant were hunting us not because we were dangerous, but because we were *proof*.

"Come," Kaelen said, rising. "We have until dawn, perhaps less. They'll have tracked her fall."

"And go where?"

"To what they buried." He offered me his hand — an heir's hand, calloused anyway. "You wanted to know what your grandmother died protecting. Come be disappointed with the rest of us."

Against every instinct that had kept me alive for ten years, I took it.

* * *

Captain Serl did not feel anger. Anger was fuel other men burned; Serl preferred to keep his lamp cold and precise.

The report lay on his field desk: griffin down, three miles past the old wardline. Survivors unknown. He read it twice, then set it down and looked at the man who'd brought it, a young hunter named Vess whose hands were shaking.

"The charm-seller," Serl said. "Eastwick. Confirm she was aboard."

"A witness saw her leave the shop with it, Captain. Carrying her grandmother's satchel."

Maren Eastwick's granddaughter. Of course. The old woman had been dead seventeen years, and her blood was still causing problems. Serl made a small note in the ledger — names mattered; patterns mattered; a girl who hid a gift for a decade was more dangerous than a dozen who flaunted theirs — and closed the book.

"Take six riders. Bring the heir alive if convenient. The girl—" He considered. "The girl is a loose thread. Cut her."

Vess hesitated. "She's just a charm-seller, sir."

"They are never *just* anything." Serl returned to his maps. "That is rather the entire problem."

* * *

We walked through the dark wilds for hours, and the dark wilds walked through us.

It sounds mad, but that's the truth of Orsen's realm: nothing stays outside you for long. The trees murmured in voices almost like language. Small things watched from the undergrowth with eyes like struck matches. Once, a fox trotted alongside us for a full mile, and I heard it — actually *heard* it, a thin ribbon of thought like *hungry, curious, these-two-smell-of-blood-and-ash* — and Kaelen glanced sideways at me when I stumbled.

"You hear them."

"Some. Faintly. It's why I hide it. In Vellmark, hearing a fox is a confession."

"In Orsen, hearing a fox is Tuesday." Something that was almost a smile crossed his face, brief as a fish surfacing. "You've spent ten years apologizing for having ears. I spent eight apologizing for being born. Perhaps we can stop apologizing in the same direction."

I didn't trust him. I want that on the record. But somewhere between the fox and the moment he caught my elbow on a root without being asked — the small, unremarkable courtesy of it — something in my chest unclenched half a degree. Trust, I was learning, doesn't arrive. It leaks.

We reached the standing stones an hour before dawn, and that was where we found Merrin Hale.

Merrin. Who'd sold charms two stalls down from mine for nine years. Who'd covered for me during the inspection of '41 by swearing my luck-knots were plain rope. Who'd waved at me yesterday morning like any other day.

She was hanging from the tallest stone by grey warden cord, and she had been dead for hours, and pinned to her coat was a note in a clerk's neat hand:

*All who harbor the old blood. This one harbored you.*

The sound I made didn't come from anywhere I could name. Kaelen caught me as my knees went, and I beat my fists against his chest because someone had to be hit and he was there, and he took it, both arms around me, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say.

Merrin had hidden me for nine years and never once asked what I was hiding. And they'd strung her up like a trophy for it.

When I finally stepped back, my face was wet and my hands were steady, and I understood — really understood, down in the marrow where fear lives — that this was not a thing I could outrun or out-hide or out-charm. Somewhere out there was a captain named Serl cutting threads, and I was one, and Merrin had been one, and the only way any of us survived was to stop being threads and become something with weight.

"What they buried," I said. My voice came out flat and new. "Show me."

Kaelen studied me for a long moment. "It's a day's walk. And they'll follow."

"Good," I heard myself say. "Let them."

Far off across the black trees, horns began to sound — three notes, cold and deliberate, like a promise being kept.

Kaelen's hand found his sword hilt. "They've found Serry's trail."

"No," I said, watching the darkness where the horns had come from, feeling my grandmother's satchel heavy against my hip. "They've found *us*."
