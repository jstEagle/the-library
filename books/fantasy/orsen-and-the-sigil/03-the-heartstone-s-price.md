## 3. The Heartstone's Price

The Heartstone looked like a cathedral that had lost an argument with a god.

We came over the last ridge at dusk — or what passed for dusk now, under a sky with no stars in it, just a flat grey ceiling like the inside of a pot. Kaelen went still when he saw it, and I felt his grief before he said anything, because apparently exile teaches you to broadcast.

"That was the Hall of Swearing," he said quietly. "I was named there."

What remained of it was a ring of shattered pillars around a sunken pit, and in the pit, a stone the size of a wagon, cracked clean through. It should have glowed. Every story my grandmother ever told me half-asleep by the fire said the Heartstone burned like a captive dawn. Now it was just rock, cold as a dead hearth, and the crack through its middle was wide enough to lose a hand in.

Bramble landed on my shoulder and did not weigh anything at all, which is how I knew how bad off she was. A healthy griffin could have carried me across three counties. This one had been bleeding since the night she fell through my roof, and she'd been flying anyway, because griffins are constitutionally incapable of doing anything sensible.

"We're close," Kaelen said. "The stone will answer blood. Old blood."

"Mine's old enough to be embarrassing," I said. "My grandmother used to say our family kept the covenant's ledgers." He looked at me. "She never told me what that meant until the night everything broke. Then she told me everything, all at once, the way dying people do."

He didn't ask if she'd died. He'd heard the way I said it.

* * *

Serl found us before we found the words to swear.

I smelled the hunters first — not them, but their hounds, and the wrongness underneath: iron and ash and something like burnt sugar, which is what magic smells like when it's been stolen instead of given. Then the torches came down into the pit from every side at once, and there were wardens everywhere, grey-cloaked, patient, and Serl walked out of their middle like she'd been waiting for us to arrive so the appointment could begin.

"Tamsin Eastwick." She said my name the way you read a ledger entry. "Daughter of Maren Ashgrove's line. Do you know what your grandmother signed away?"

"I know what she helped build," I said. "And I know who broke it."

"Nobody broke it." Serl spread her hands, and I'll give her this — she had the voice for it, warm as a market crier's. "It was *collected*. The covenant was a drain, girl. Two realms pouring power into a stone so that neither could use it. Your grandmother's generation called that peace. We call it theft — theft from both sides. The wards are down. The power is loose. And it will pool somewhere. The only question is whether it pools in the hands of someone responsible."

Behind me, Bramble made a sound low in her chest, and Kaelen's hand drifted toward a sword he'd lost two days ago and hadn't stopped reaching for since.

"And here's the part you'll like," Serl went on, stepping closer, boots crunching on covenant-stone. "You're the last of the ledger-keepers. The blood that bound it runs in you. Which means the power doesn't have to be fought over. It can be *claimed*. By you. Legitimately. One signature, one act of will, and you're not a fugitive hiding charms in a market stall. You're the Warden of the Covenant. Legal. Protected. Rich beyond Vellmark's arithmetic."

"And the catch?"

"The wild realm dies." She said it gently, almost kindly. "It's already dying. You've seen it. Let it finish. Seal the border behind it, keep the power here, and no more children burn for having old blood. No more wardens hunting anyone. Because there won't be anything left to hunt. You could save every gifted child in Vellmark, Tamsin. Isn't that what you've been doing all along? Saving them, one charm at a time?"

Here is the thing I don't like admitting, even to myself, even now. For about four heartbeats, I wanted it.

Not the power. Well — some of the power, yes, I'm honest enough to be ashamed. But mostly the *stopping*. Ten years of flinching. Ten years of smiling at customers while listening to their chickens complain about foxes. Ten years of being a lie with a market stall. She was offering me permission to put the lie down forever, and to make the world safe for every child like me, and all it would cost was a realm full of creatures I'd only met through my gift — creatures whose voices I'd heard, who had *names*, who sang to their young in registers no human throat could reach.

Four heartbeats. Then Bramble shifted against my shoulder, feather-light, and pressed her beak to my ear, and spoke — not in my grandmother's voice this time, but in her own, thin and cracked:

"Little ledger-keeper. Whatever you sign, sign it true."

* * *

That's when the hunters moved, because Serl had never intended to let me choose freely. Choices are just theater to people like her; the offer was the leash, and the order came down the moment she judged the leash slipping.

Kaelen shouted. Torches scattered. And Bramble — Bramble opened wings that hadn't carried her properly in days and threw herself off my shoulder into the middle of them, screaming a griffin war-cry that I felt in my teeth, buying us seconds with the only coin she had left.

I will not describe all of it. Some things you carry without looking at them directly. What I will say is that she held six wardens off a stone bridge for long enough, and that when it was done she lay in the dust with her sides going fast and shallow, and her eyes found mine, and they were not afraid.

"The stone," she rasped. "Now. While they regroup. Little keeper — *now*."

Kaelen dragged me toward the cracked Heartstone because I couldn't walk straight. Behind us Serl was shouting orders, and the burnt-sugar smell was thickening, and I understood with terrible clarity that we had perhaps a minute.

"How does it work?" I demanded. "The reforging. Tell me fast."

"Blood and sworn names," Kaelen said. "That's all it ever was. Not a spell. A promise, witnessed by the stone. My blood for Orsen's side. Yours for the human side. And names — true names, sworn aloud, freely given." He drew a knife across his palm without ceremony, the way you'd cut bread. "But Tamsin — a covenant isn't two people. It never was. If it's just us, it's just another pair of hands holding too much power. It has to be *many*. Small voices. Found family. Everyone willing."

So I thought of my stall. Of Marla who sold luck-knots and pretended not to notice my charms worked better than hers. Of old Fenwick and his weather-sense he called "rheumatism." Of every hidden gift in Vellmark, every small voice keeping its head down.

I pressed my bleeding hand against the crack in the Heartstone and I didn't claim anything. I offered.

"Heartstone," I said, "I'm Tamsin Eastwick, granddaughter of Maren, ledger-keeper's line, charm-seller, liar, beast-speaker — all of it true, none of it hidden anymore. I don't take the covenant. I *give* myself to it. And I swear these names into it: Marla Quill, luck-binder. Fenwick Ostler, storm-hearer. Kaelen of Orsen, heir and exile. And every soul in either realm who swears after me — let them be counted, let them be kept, let the power belong to all of them and none of them alone."

Kaelen's hand came down beside mine, blood meeting blood in the crack.

"And I, Kaelen, son of the Hall of Swearing, swear the same," he said. "Two realms. One promise. Many voices."

For a moment, nothing. The stone stayed dead under our hands, and Serl's footsteps were coming fast, and I thought — oh, of course, of course it fails, why would it work for—

Then the crack lit.

Not gold. Not the captive dawn of the stories. It lit the color of a market stall at morning, warm and ordinary and utterly alive, and the light ran up through the broken pillars and out across the darkened wilds like a rumor spreading, and somewhere far away I heard — I *heard*, the way I hear everything — ten thousand small voices answering, one by one, saying their names.

Serl stopped at the edge of the light. I turned to face her with the stone burning at my back, and she looked at me — really looked, for the first time — and I watched her understand that she had lost, and watch her decide, cool as ledger ink, exactly what she was going to do about it.

Her hand slipped into her cloak and came out holding something that glowed with stolen light, a splinter torn from the old wards, and she smiled at me like a woman closing a shop.

"Well then, Warden," she said. "Let's see how many voices you can gather before I start collecting them again."

And she was gone into the dark, taking her splinter with her, while above the ruined hall — faint, then firmer, then blazing — the first stars in nine days began to come back.
