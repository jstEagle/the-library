## 4. A Covenant of Small Voices

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

I know because I was haggling with a fishwife over the price of eels when the sky remembered itself, all at once, like a lamp relit in a window you'd stopped looking at. The whole market went quiet. Then it didn't. People wept into their herring. A child pointed and screamed with joy until his mother shushed him, then everyone was pointing, and nobody shushed anybody, and for one long minute Vellmark stood in the square with its mouth open at a sky that had lied to it for a season.

I bought the eels. It felt important to mark the occasion with something slippery.

"You're crying," said Marla, my fellow charm-seller, my found-sister, the woman who'd shared my stall roof through eleven lean winters without once asking why my charms worked better than hers.

"I'm not."

"You're crying into the eels."

"They're very fresh."

She laughed, and then she wasn't laughing either, and we stood there in the fish-smell and the starlight holding onto each other's sleeves like two people afraid the ground had learned to lie as well as the sky.

Because here is what the crowd didn't know, what only maybe forty people in Vellmark knew that night: the stars hadn't come back on their own. They'd come back because a charm-seller with old blood had pressed her palm to a cracked Heartstone and sworn her name alongside an exiled prince, a dying griffin's last feather, and a list of misfits I'd have crossed the street to avoid a year ago. The covenant was reforged. The border between the human city and the wild realm of Orsen was knitting itself closed — slowly, painfully, like flesh over a wound that had been picked at for a decade.

And Serl was gone.

That part sat in me like a swallowed stone. Warden-Captain Serl, cold as a ledger and twice as balanced, had fled the Heartstone with a splinter of stolen power cupped in her fist like a coal she meant to carry home to warm her hands on later. Kaelen had wanted to pursue. I'd wanted to pursue. But the reforging had taken everything — blood, breath, three days of my life I remember only as white noise and someone singing — and by the time we could stand, her trail was a week cold and leading somewhere none of our maps agreed on.

"She'll surface," Kaelen had said, watching the dark where she'd gone. "People like Serl always surface. Power hoarded is power that itches."

I'd said something brave and dismissive. Privately, every night since, I dreamed of a woman standing in an empty field holding a small bright thing, smiling the way a knife smiles.

* * *

The school opened in spring, which surprised no one more than me.

It started, as most ruinous enterprises do, with a child. A girl named Wren — sharp-elbowed, gap-toothed, the daughter of a tanner — who came to my rebuilt stall to buy a luck-charm and then went pale when the charm *purred* at her. Not hummed. Purred. Like a cat recognizing family.

"Take it back," she whispered, shoving it across the counter. "It knows. Make it stop knowing."

I looked at this child — this terrified, furious, familiar child — and I saw a decade of my own life folded up small and wearing pigtails. Ten years of hiding. Ten years of harmless charms and smaller lies, waiting for the knock on the door.

"It's supposed to know," I said. "That's the whole point of you."

Her mother pulled her away before I could say anything else, but Wren came back. They always come back. Within a month there were four of them, then nine, then a dozen children slipping down my alley at dusk with gifts in their ears and fear in their mouths, and I stood in my kitchen one night surrounded by small voices asking questions no one in Vellmark had been allowed to ask for a generation, and I thought: *Grandmother, you absolute menace, is this what you meant?*

Kaelen found me sitting on the floor amid chalk dust and a hedgehog that talked.

"You're building a school," he said.

"I'm building nothing. I'm being buried alive by children."

"The hedgehog says otherwise."

"The hedgehog is a gossip." I tipped my head back against the cupboard and looked up at him — the exiled heir of Orsen, tall and weathered and still carrying the wilds in the way he moved, like the ground was a courtesy rather than a given. "They'll come for us, you know. Not the wardens — those are done, mostly, scattered. But people. Fear doesn't die just because the stars come back. It just gets embarrassed and goes quiet."

"Then we teach loudly," he said, "before it finds its voice again."

Which is how, against every instinct toward safety I'd spent ten years sharpening, I ended up signing a lease on the old granary by the river with money from the new trade covenant — Orsen silk and timber flowing legally into Vellmark for the first time since my grandmother's era, and me taking a cut to buy desks. Desks! For magic! My grandmother would have laughed herself sick.

* * *

Marla teaches the arithmetic, because even miracle-workers should balance a ledger. Bram, who carried the griffin's body down from the Heartstone on his own back and has never once spoken of it, teaches the practical arts: warding, first aid, how to tell a friendly spirit from a hungry one (rule one: ask). And Kaelen teaches them Orsen — the language, the customs, the long slow history of two realms learning, twice now, to share a border without bleeding on each other.

I teach them the thing I taught myself in secret for ten years: how to listen. How to hold a gift like a bird — firmly enough that it stays, gently enough that it lives.

Wren is my best student and my worst nightmare. Last week she persuaded the river to reverse course for eleven feet purely out of spite toward a boy who'd mocked her braid. The boy apologized. The river preened.

Some evenings I stand at the granary window and watch the border wards shimmer out past the fields — visible now, faint as breath on glass, repaired but scarred, a reminder written in light. The stars sit where stars should sit. On clear nights you can see the wild realm's mountains, closer than they've been in living memory, close enough that sometimes the wind comes off them smelling of green things and carries, if you're gifted, small distant voices saying thank you in languages older than cities.

The griffin's feather hangs above my desk. It's gray now, quiet, spent — but on the first morning of each month, all the charms in the school chime at once, unprompted, and I choose to believe that's him saying hello.

* * *

Kaelen:

I had forgotten what a city sounds like when it is unafraid.

Orsen sings — I grew up inside that song, in halls where the walls themselves were choir. But Vellmark, this city of smoke and stubborn commerce, has a sound of its own now: hammering, argument, the granary full of children reciting ward-rhymes in two tongues, Tamsin Eastwick scolding a hedgehog. Small voices. The covenant was never meant to be a monument or a throne. She understood that before I did. It was always meant to be this — a room full of unlikely heirs, learning to carry what their grandparents buried.

Serl is still out there. I feel it some mornings, the way you feel weather coming: a splinter of stolen power moving through the world, patient, seeking shape. The scouts report nothing. The wards report less. But Tamsin wakes some nights with her hand already curled around a charm, and neither of us says what we both know, which is that a seed does not stay a seed forever.

Let it come, then. We are not hiding anymore.

Tonight I walked her home along the river, past the granary with its windows lit gold, and she stopped and said, "Do you think she ever had a choice? Serl?" And I said, "Everyone has a choice. That's the terrible part." And she nodded and leaned her head briefly against my shoulder, the way you lean on a wall you've decided to trust, and we went on walking.

Behind us, in the school, a child laughed, and every charm in the building chimed like bells.

Somewhere far off, under a sky that finally kept its promises, something small and stolen began, very quietly, to grow.
