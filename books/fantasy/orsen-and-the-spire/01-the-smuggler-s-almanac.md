## 1. The Smuggler's Almanac

There are three rules to moving contraband through the Thornfell passes, and I'd learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

One: never travel the same road twice in a season. Two: never trust a man who whistles while he works. Three: never, under any circumstances, let anyone see you do something impossible.

I was thinking about rule three as I stood on a ledge above the Greywater Gorge and watched a Spire patrol pick its way along the riverbank below us like a string of black ants who'd been told they were important. Six of them. Lanterns hooded, staves slung across their backs, boots crunching on frost-brittle scree. Inquisitors didn't come out this far into the mountains without a reason, and reasons were bad for business.

"They're heading for the ford," Kess whispered beside me. He was flat against the rock, chin on his forearms, watching them with the patient attention he gave everything—patrols, card games, me when he thought I wasn't looking. "If we cross at the low bridge we'll walk right through their lantern light."

"Then we don't cross at the low bridge."

"There is no other bridge."

"There's always another way down. It just might not be a pleasant one." I rolled onto my back and studied the sky instead, because looking at Kess's face right then would have made me say something honest, and honesty was a luxury item we didn't stock. The clouds were doing that thing they did in late autumn—piling up over the high peaks in grey towers, fat with rain nobody had asked for. I could feel them the way you feel a tooth you're trying not to touch. A pressure behind my eyes. An itch under my skin shaped exactly like lightning.

Seven years since I'd touched it. Seven years since I'd let myself so much as hum during a thunderstorm.

"You've got that look," Kess said.

"What look?"

"The one where you're about to suggest something that'll get us killed, but you'll phrase it like it's the sensible option."

"I'm hurt. I phrase things like they're the *charming* option." I sat up and pointed east, past the gorge, to where a goat track threaded down through a stand of black pines toward the river. It was less a path than a rumor of one, and it ended at a stretch of bank that would require us to lead two mules loaded with unlicensed dreamwine across wet stone while the river tried to drown us. "We take the herd trail. Cut above the patrol, drop down past the old kiln, cross below the falls where the water's loud enough to cover the mules complaining."

Kess looked at the route. Then at me. Then back at the route, the way a man looks at a bill he knows he has to pay. "The falls freeze this time of year."

"Parts of them."

"The parts we'd be standing on?"

"Statistically, some of them."

He exhaled through his nose—that long-suffering sound he'd perfected sometime around our second year together—and started repacking the rope without another word. That was the thing about Kess. He complained the way other people breathed: constantly, automatically, and without any actual intention of stopping. He'd follow me off a cliff if I told him the view was worth it, and he'd grumble the whole way down.

I'd never asked him why. Some questions you don't ask because the answer might cost more than the silence.

* * *

The descent went exactly as well as descents go, which is to say we lost a crate of wine, half my dignity, and roughly an hour arguing with a mule named Duchess who had opinions about wet stone that she expressed by simply refusing to exist near it. Kess coaxed her. I threatened her. Between the two of us we got her across the slick shelf beneath the frozen falls, where the water roared down behind a curtain of ice thick as chapel glass, and the spray hung in the air like breath caught mid-sigh.

It was beautiful, is the thing. That was the trap of the Thornfells. They were beautiful the way a knife is beautiful, and every time I forgot that, the mountains reminded me.

Below the falls, the river flattened and spread, and the patrol's lantern light was a smudge somewhere upstream, swallowed by noise and distance. We moved fast and quiet through the shallows, and for a few minutes there was nothing in the world but cold water, mule breath steaming, and Kess's hand steady on the lead rope ahead of me.

This was the part I never said out loud, not even to myself in the dark hours: I loved this. Not the smuggling, exactly—the lying to fences, the counting coin with chapped fingers, the constant arithmetic of risk. But this. The passes at night. The small company of two people and two animals against all that weather and stone. Being no one, going nowhere that mattered, wanted by no one except the people we owed money to.

Restless contentment, Kess called it once. He'd read it in a book. He read everything; it was his one expensive habit, books, and our fence Orla had learned she could shave a cut off his share if she threw a novel into the deal.

Restless contentment. Like a bird that loves its cage because it's warm.

Because here was the truth I kept buried under seven years of careful, deliberate, exhausting ordinariness: I hadn't always been no one. There had been a girl once who could put her hand into a thunderhead the way you'd put your hand into a stream, who could feel the charge gathering over a valley hours before the first drop fell, who had once—only once, and never again—called the storm down and *shaped* it, bent it like green wood, and the price of that single act had been everything. Her name. Her home. Her face in any mirror she passed, because mirrors made her check, reflexively, for the brand that wasn't there anymore but should have been.

Jude Halloran, storm-caller. Dead seven years, officially. Unrecorded, unofficially, which was worse. The Spire didn't execute heretics like me. They erased us. They took your name out of the registries and your history out of the mouths of everyone who knew you, and what was left walked out of the gates with empty pockets and a warning: *do it again and we won't bother with erasure.*

So I became a smuggler. I became careful. I learned to flinch from weather reports and to keep my hands in my pockets when the air went electric, and I built a life so small and so quiet that even I sometimes believed it.

And some nights, standing on ridgelines with storms stacking up over the peaks like armies, I missed it so badly it felt like grief. Like missing a limb that had been amputated for crimes I still wasn't entirely sure I'd committed.

"Jude." Kess's voice, low. "You stopped walking."

I blinked. The river. The ice. Duchess giving me a look of profound mule judgment. "Just checking the sky," I said, which was true, and also a lie, and also the closest thing to prayer I'd managed in years.

"Sky's fine."

"Sky's never fine. Sky's just quiet."

He snorted and kept moving, and I followed, and the itch under my skin settled back down to its usual dull hum, the one I'd lived with so long I'd almost stopped noticing. Almost.

* * *

Orla's warehouse sat in the valley town of Marrowgate like a fat spider in a web of debts, and by the time we reached it—two days later, wine intact minus one crate, dignity unrecovered—I was ready to sleep for a week in a bed that didn't list to port.

Marrowgate was the kind of town that existed because the Spire allowed it to. Slate roofs, coal smoke, a market square where the Spire's writ was nailed to a post like a trophy. Everyone in the valley knew the order ran the lowlands from their white tower a hundred leagues south, and everyone in the valley had also worked out, in that practical mountain way, that the Spire's laws were like the weather: unavoidable, occasionally lethal, and best endured indoors.

We stabled the mules, bribed the stable boy, and found Orla in her counting room doing what she did best, which was looking disappointed at sums.

"Two crates short," she said, before we'd cleared the doorway.

"One crate short, and the crate was lost to a river, which is an act of the gods and therefore not deductible." I dropped onto the stool across from her desk. Orla was seventy if she was a day, built like a rain barrel, with spectacles she wore purely so she could peer over them disapprovingly. She'd been fencing goods in these valleys since before I was born, and she'd outlived three husbands, two tax reforms, and one attempted arrest, the details of which she refused to share and which we all privately suspected involved a shovel.

"The gods," Orla repeated. "The gods drowned your wine."

"Fell off a ledge. Very tragic. Duchess is in mourning."

"Duchess pushed it."

"She's a complicated woman."

Kess laughed from the doorway, and Orla's mouth twitched despite itself, and just like that we were back in the rhythm of it—the haggling, the ledger, the familiar dance where everyone cheated everyone slightly and nobody minded because it meant the world was still turning. She paid us fair-plus-insult, as always. She mentioned, as always, that times were hard and the Spire's new tariffs were squeezing everyone, and that inquisitors had been asking questions in the lowland towns, and that we should keep our heads down.

"Inquisitors?" I asked, keeping my voice at the level of mild professional interest. "What are they hunting? Dreamwine?"

"Who knows. Something about a birth registry. Asking after folk born under—" she waved a hand, "—some moon or other. Black moon, harvest moon, I wasn't listening. They pay for information, though. Which tells you they're serious."

Something cold slid down my spine and settled in my stomach like a swallowed stone. I kept my face bored. I've had a lot of practice keeping my face bored; it's practically a trade skill.

"Born under a black moon," Kess said lightly. "That's half the mountain. Half the *world*, if you count everyone born on an overcast night."

"That's what I said." Orla peered at me over her spectacles, a beat too long. "You feeling all right, Halloran? You've gone grey as the gorge."

"River water," I said. "Cold year."

She let it go, eventually, the way Orla let most things go—reluctantly, and with interest accruing. We collected our pay, and Kess went off to settle our debts with the cooper and buy, inevitably, another book he couldn't afford, and I stood in the yard behind the warehouse while the afternoon went grey overhead and told myself the stone in my gut was nothing. Coincidence. Inquisitors were always asking about something. Birth registries, cargo manifests, the color of a stranger's eyes. It was weather. It was just weather.

Except.

Except that I knew exactly what moon I'd been born under. Every storm-caller did, because the Spire made sure of it—they recorded it, tracked it, watched for it. Children born under a black moon, when the sky went dark at the height of the day, were children the order came looking for. That was how they'd found me, at nine years old, in a fishing village that smelled of salt and tar. That was how the whole machine had started turning.

They weren't asking after dreamwine. They were asking after *me*. Or someone like me. Someone the registries said didn't exist anymore.

The clouds over the western peaks had gone the color of a bruise, and the pressure behind my eyes was back, that old familiar ache, and I stood in Orla's muddy yard with my hands jammed in my pockets and thought: *seven years. Seven years of being no one. Please let me keep being no one.*

The first drops of rain hit the slate roofs around me, soft as footsteps.

That evening, Kess came back to the rented room carrying bread, cheese, a bottle of the good wine he'd sworn he wouldn't buy, and a parcel wrapped in oilcloth that he set on the table with the particular reverence he reserved for printed matter.

"Before you say anything," he began.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to make the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have an entire alphabet of faces, and the one you're making now is the letter that says 'we can't afford books.'" He unwrapped the parcel anyway. "It's payment, actually. Old Tammas Vey finally died, and Orla's clearing his hoard. She's paying us partly in volumes. Your share included this one."

"It's a book, Kess. I can't eat a book."

"It's an *almanac*." He turned it toward me on the table, and I confess my breath caught, just slightly, because smugglers know value when they see it, and this was value. Old. Leather gone soft and dark as chestnuts, corners worn round, the spine cracked in the way of a book that had been opened ten thousand times by hands that loved it. Stamped on the cover in faded gilt: *A Complete Almanac & Prognostication, Being Certain Observations upon Weather, Fortune, and the Turning Years.*

"Weather," I said. My voice came out strange, and I hoped he didn't notice. "You bought me a book about weather."

"He *left* you a book about weather. There's a difference." Kess grinned, tired and pleased with himself, and started in on the bread. "Old Tammas was odd about it, apparently. Wouldn't let anyone touch it. Kept it wrapped up like a relic. Orla says there's writing in the margins, prophecies and such, the usual country nonsense. Storm years, famine years, that sort of thing."

I turned it over in my hands. It was heavier than it looked. Cold, somehow, or maybe that was just the room.

"Prophecies," I said. "Wonderful. Exactly what I need more of in my life."

"Everyone needs a hobby." Kess poured the wine. Outside, the rain picked up, drumming on the roof, and I felt each drop land somewhere under my skin, faint as fingertips tapping on a door.

Later, when Kess was asleep and the candle was guttering, I sat with the almanac in my lap and told myself I'd just skim it. Check the margins for anything valuable. Country nonsense, Orla had said. Storm years and famine years.

I opened it to the current year's page because that's what you do with an almanac—you check today—and the first thing I saw was a date written in ink so old it had gone brown, in a hand that was precise and certain and a century dead:

*Tonight.*

And beneath it, in the same hand, two words that stopped my heart like a hand around it:

*Halloran.*
