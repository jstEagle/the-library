## 2. Ink Older Than Grief

The client's name was Ambrose Fenn, and he was dying the way old men in mountain towns die: slowly, loudly, and with a great deal of complaint about the quality of his physicians. He lived at the top of a house that had been built for a family twice his size, in a room stacked floor to ceiling with books, and he had been paying us to run contraband through Thornfell for six years without once asking what we smuggled or why.

Tonight he wanted to pay me in books instead of coin.

"I'm told you can read," he said, when I came up the stairs and found him propped among his pillows like a king in exile from his own kingdom. His voice had gone thin as winter paper. "Properly, I mean. Not just reading signs and ledgers."

"Signs and ledgers are where all the real knowledge lives," I said. "Everything else is decoration."

He laughed, which turned into coughing, which took long enough that I considered going downstairs and finding him something stronger than the tea steaming on his nightstand. When he surfaced again, his eyes were wet and bright with the particular mischief of men who have decided death is an inconvenience they intend to be rude about.

"You're a hard woman, Halloran."

"Jude," I corrected, out of habit. It wasn't even a lie anymore. Seven years of being Jude had worn the name smooth, like a river stone. "And I'm not hard. I'm expensive."

"And yet here you are, climbing my stairs for free." He gestured at the room around him with a hand that trembled only slightly, which I suspected was pride rather than health. "Look at them. Look at any of them. Take your pick."

I looked. It was like looking at a hoard of dragon-gold and being told to take a coin. There were atlases with hand-tinted coastlines. Herbals with illustrations so precise you could almost smell the plants. A shelf of histories bound in leather gone soft as cloth, spines cracked from use rather than neglect. And behind glass, on the highest shelf, things that made even me—professional cynic, casual blasphemer, woman who had spent seven years pretending magic was a story other people told—go quiet.

"You're not dying," I said. "You're downsizing."

"I'm dying *and* downsizing. Efficiency." He waved at a chair. "Sit. Humor a dead man. Pick one book. Any one. Consider it payment for the last six years of not asking questions."

I sat, because refusing would have cost more than sitting. "You've paid me plenty in coin."

"Coin is for transactions. This is a gift. There's a difference, and the difference matters." His gaze drifted over his shelves with the tenderness most men reserve for lovers or children. "I've spent sixty years collecting these. Do you know what happens to a collection when the collector dies? The vultures come. They sell the atlases to decorators and the herbals to apothecaries and the rest goes into crates, into cellars, into damp. Books want hands, Halloran. Books want to be *read*. That's the whole tragedy of them—they outlive everyone who loved them."

"That's very grim for a man planning to outlive his physicians."

"I plan to spite them all by dying of something interesting." He fixed me with a look that was sharper than it had any right to be. "Pick one."

I got up, mostly to end the conversation, and let my fingers walk along the spines. This was a mistake, because my fingers have never met a book they didn't want to open, and Ambrose knew it. He'd known it since the day we first met, probably. Smugglers learn to read people fast; it keeps you alive. Book-hoarders learn to read people faster; it keeps them entertained.

The atlas was beautiful but useless to me—I knew Thornfell's passes better than any cartographer alive, having bled on most of them. The herbals were lovely but Kess handled our medical kit and would sulk for a week if I brought home a rival authority. The histories—

The histories were dangerous. Anything with the Spire's seal on it was dangerous, and half of Ambrose's collection wore that mark somewhere inside: the tower-and-crown device stamped in gold, the order's motto curled beneath it in letters that always looked to me like a fist closing. *Ex Caelo Ordo.* Order From Heaven. As if heaven had needed their help.

My hand stopped on a spine near the back wall, low down, wedged between a treatise on river law and a book of funeral rites. No gold stamp. No title. Just dark leather gone slick with age, and a smell rising off it when I pulled it free—the smell of a place I'd never been and somehow recognized. Cold stone. Old rain. Ink.

It was heavier than it looked. The pages inside were dense, close-written, in a hand that slanted hard to the right like a person writing against a wind. The ink had faded to brown in places, black in others, and there were marginal notes in at least three different hands, added across decades like sediment layers.

"What is it?" Ambrose asked, watching me too closely.

"No idea. Looks like someone's diary that got ambitious."

"Open the front cover."

I did. On the flyleaf, in that same wind-slanted hand, was written:

*This book is a debt. I write it because I must, and I am sorry for what the knowing will cost its reader.*

Charming. I'd smuggled cursed relics with friendlier preambles.

"It came to me forty years ago," Ambrose said, "from a peddler who claimed it came from Hollowmere. You know Hollowmere?"

"Dead monastery up the north ridge. Burned before my grandmother's time."

"The peddler claimed the fire didn't take everything. Claimed some of the brothers saw it coming and carried what mattered out ahead of the flames." He shrugged, which cost him. "Peddlers claim things. But I've had forty years with this book, and I'll tell you what I know: I've never managed to read it straight through. Not because it's dull. Because every time I come to a certain page, something in the room goes cold, and I find I'd rather be doing anything else. Sixty years of reading, Halloran, and one book has beaten me. I'd like to lose to someone younger."

"This is your idea of a gift?"

"This is my idea of a joke. The gift is that you may keep it." He settled back into his pillows, satisfied, a man who had successfully offloaded both a treasure and a curse in a single evening. "Take it home. Read it or burn it. Either way, it stops haunting my shelves."

* * *

Kess was waiting outside with the mules, because Kess was always waiting outside with the mules; it was less a job than a metaphysical condition. He took one look at the book under my arm and raised an eyebrow.

"Fenn finally went senile?"

"He went generous. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

"That's the second time tonight you've stolen his lines."

"I steal everything worth stealing. It's called trade." I swung up onto my mule. "Come on. I want to look at this thing in proper light."

"Jude." He said it the way he said it when I was about to do something stupid, which meant he said it often. "We've got a full hold and a patrol schedule to dodge. Whatever's in the spooky book can wait until we're over the pass."

"Whatever's in the spooky book has waited a hundred years. It can wait four more hours."

But it couldn't wait four more hours, and I knew it, the way you know a storm is coming before the sky admits it—that pressure behind the ears, that taste of metal. We made camp two miles up the pass in a hollow sheltered from the wind, and while Kess saw to the mules I sat by the fire with the book on my knees and opened it properly for the first time.

The early pages were history, or something wearing history's clothes. A chronicle of the Spire's founding, but wrong—slanted, skeptical, written by someone who had stood inside those walls and seen the machinery behind the incense. The author wrote of wards and bindings, of the great rite that maintained the realm's protections, and of things kept beneath the Spire that the official histories did not mention at all. I read with the detached interest of a woman reading about a country she'd renounced citizenship in.

Then the hand changed.

Not the slant—the slant stayed true—but the pressure. The words came harder, dug deeper, as though the writer had stopped recording and started confessing. The chapter heading, when I reached it, was a single line:

*On the Daughter of the Storm.*

I should have closed it then. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I should have closed it, put it in the fire, and told Ambrose Fenn his joke had landed.

Instead I turned the page.

*In the hundredth year after the binding, a child will be born under a black moon to a daughter of the Halloran line. She will carry the storm unspent. The Spire will mark her, fear her, and cast her out—not because she is heretical, but because she is necessary. Her exile is the forge. Her wandering is the tempering. She will return to them at the appointed hour, and at the appointed hour she will choose.*

*Two endings are written. In the first, she breaks the cages, and the storms go free, and the wards of the realm shatter like ice on a spring river, and the age of the Spire ends in weather.*

*In the second, she kneels, and takes the collar, and the Spire drains her as it drained the spirits before her, and the realm endures another hundred years on borrowed thunder.*

*I have seen both. I do not know which comes to pass. I know only that the choice will be hers alone, offered at the dark moon, and that no power in the world can make it for her.*

There was a date beneath it. Written plainly, no codes, no cipher, in that same digging-deep hand:

*The dark moon of the harvest month, in the hundredth year.*

I did the arithmetic the way you do arithmetic when you already know the answer and are checking only to confirm the shape of the disaster. Hundredth year after the binding. Harvest month. Dark moon.

That was tonight. That was *this* night, the night I was sitting by a fire in the Thornfell passes with a stolen book on my knees and a partner twenty feet away who thought my name was Jude.

Because it was. That was the whole point. That was the entire architecture of my life.

I turned the page with hands that belonged to someone else. There was more—a list of names, generations of them, a family tree drawn in careful strokes. Halloran, Halloran, Halloran, each generation marked with small annotations in the margin. *Died young. Died at sea. Died asking questions.* And at the bottom, the newest entry, the ink barely faded:

*Judith Halloran. Born under the black moon. Taken by the Spire at nine years of age. Branded heretical at seventeen. Exiled. Living.*

*Living,* underlined twice.

The fire crackled. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the pass with a sound I had spent seven years training myself not to hear as anything but wind.

Here is what you need to understand about me: I left the Spire's world behind when I was seventeen years old, and I built a new one out of smuggler's routes and false names and the discipline of never, ever reaching for the thing inside me that used to answer when I called. Seven years of silence. Seven years of being nobody from nowhere, and I had gotten good at it, good enough that some mornings I woke up believing it myself. That was the trick of it. That was the price. You bury a thing deep enough and eventually you can walk over the grave without flinching.

Except someone had dug it up. Someone had stood in a burning monastery a century ago and written down the exact shape of my life—the trial, the brand, the exile, the wandering—and then written *living* underneath it, twice, like a man emphasizing a point in a letter he expected to be argued with.

And he'd known the date. My date. Tonight.

I don't remember standing up. I remember the book falling closed on the ground, and I remember Kess saying my name from across the camp—my false name, my safe name—and I remember the vertigo hitting all at once, cold and total, like plunging into a lake in winter. The stars were in the wrong places. The mountains were in the wrong places. My own hands, held up in front of me, were in the wrong places, because they belonged to a character in a book written before my grandmother was born, and every choice I'd ever made—the running, the hiding, the silence, the name—had been set down in brown ink by a stranger's pen before I existed to make them.

"Jude." Kess was beside me now. I hadn't heard him cross the camp. That was unlike him; Kess moved loud on purpose, so you'd never feel ambushed. "Hey. What is it? What's in the book?"

"Nothing," I said. "A diary. Religious nonsense. Monks."

He looked at me. Kess has a face that lies badly and reads well, and right now it was reading me like a customs officer reading a manifest that doesn't add up. "You're white as milk."

"Cold night."

"It's summer."

"Bad summer." I picked up the book. My hands were steady, which seemed like a betrayal of everything else. "Fenn's dying. He wanted to give away his library before the vultures came. I got the haunted one. Lucky me."

"Throw it in the fire, then."

And there it was—the test, delivered casually by the one person in the world who trusted me completely. Throw it in the fire. Walk away. Be Jude Halloran, smuggler, tomorrow and the day after and the day after that, until the dark moon passed and whatever the prophecy promised failed to arrive, because prophecies fail, because books lie, because a dead monk a century gone had no power over a woman with a mule train and a false name and seven years of practiced silence.

I looked at the fire. The fire looked back, orange and indifferent.

"No," I heard myself say. "It's a gift. Gifts get kept."

Kess watched me tuck it into my pack, and whatever he saw in my face—and he saw plenty, he always saw plenty—he had the grace not to name it. He just handed me his bedroll instead of making me fetch my own, and said, "Sleep. We cross the high pass at dawn, and if the patrol schedule holds, we'll be in the valley town by midweek."

The valley town. Where Ambrose lived. Where the book had come from, forty years ago, by way of a peddler and a burned monastery and a hundred years of patient, terrible patience.

I lay down and did not sleep. Above me the stars wheeled in their ancient, indifferent courses, and somewhere south, past the passes, past the valleys, the Spire stood against the sky doing whatever the Spire did at night—feeding its wards, keeping its secrets, holding its breath. Waiting, maybe. If a dead man could know my date, then living men could know it too. Men with white robes and gold stamps and long memories and a brand they'd seared into my shoulder seventeen years ago with a word that meant *heretic* and might, I understood now with a cold that never quite reached my bones and never quite left them, actually mean *necessary*.

The prophecy had two endings. Break the cages, or kneel and take the collar.

Both of them ended with me at the Spire. Neither of them ended with me here, on a mountainside, being nobody.

Which meant somebody, somewhere, had left out the third ending—the one where Judith Halloran read a dead man's book, weighed her life in her hands, and decided that no one, living or dead, divine or institutional, got to write her last page for her.

I just had to figure out how to survive long enough to write it.

Near dawn, I finally slept, and dreamed of a door I'd sealed shut years ago, standing open, with weather on the other side.
