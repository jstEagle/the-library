## 3. What Kess Doesn't Know

I've known Jude Halloran for six years, and in that time I've learned exactly three things about her with any certainty.

One: she can read a mountain pass the way a priest reads scripture, and she's never once been wrong about where the snow will break. Two: she drinks her tea bitter enough to strip rust, and if you sweeten it she'll drink that too, but she'll look at you like you've personally failed her. Three: she does not talk about her past, and after six years I have stopped asking, because a person who won't tell you where they're from will eventually show you instead — usually at the worst possible moment.

That third thing has been sitting wrong in my chest for two days now.

It started the morning we came down off the high shelf into Grayhollow, when she stopped dead in the middle of the trail and went white as a salt flat. No warning. One minute she was walking point, joking about how the mule had better manners than most of our clients, and the next she was just — gone. Standing there with her eyes fixed on nothing, breathing like she'd surfaced from deep water. When I touched her shoulder she flinched hard enough to nearly take my hand off.

"Jude."

"Fine," she said. "Fine. Just — thought I saw something."

"There's nothing to see. It's a goat track and a dead pine."

"I know what I saw." And then she smiled at me, that quick crooked thing she keeps in her back pocket for exactly these moments, and walked on, and I let her, because that's the arrangement. She doesn't explain; I don't press. Six years of unspoken terms, written nowhere, signed by nobody.

But here's the thing about arrangements: they only work while both parties keep up their end. And Jude Halloran has been keeping hers with all the grace of a man bailing out a leaking boat with his hat.

* * *

Maren Ostwick runs the fence out of the back of her chandler's shop in Grayhollow, behind a wall of tallow and lamp oil that smells like a church having a nervous breakdown. She's seventy if she's a day, sharp as broken glass, and she's been moving goods through Thornfell since before either of us could walk. She also knows everything that moves through this valley, which is why, when I slipped in through her back door that afternoon alone, her eyebrows went up like drawbridges.

"Where's your shadow?" she asked.

"Resting."

"Your shadow doesn't rest. Your shadow once carried a crate of Spire-stamped brandy four miles uphill with a fever. If your shadow is resting, something is wrong with your shadow."

Leave it to Maren to say in ten seconds what I'd spent two days not saying to myself.

"She's tired," I said. "Long run. The pass work took more out of her than usual."

"Mm." Maren didn't believe me. Maren never believes me, which is the foundation of our entire business relationship. She poured two cups of something dark from a pot on her stove and pushed one across the counter at me. "Sit down, Kess. You have the face of a man who wants to ask a favor and hasn't figured out how expensive it's going to be."

"I always have that face."

"Yes, but usually you're lying about it. Today you mean it. That's new."

I sat. The stool creaked under me like it resented the assignment. Outside, Grayhollow went about its evening — cart wheels, a dog losing an argument with a goose, the bell on the temple of the Twin Aspects ringing the hour with its usual air of reluctant obligation. A normal town having a normal evening. Somewhere in it, Jude was sitting in our rented room with a book open in her lap, and I would have bet the mule and both wagons that she hadn't turned a page in an hour.

"The Vellish shipment," I said. "The silk and the blue lotus. We're delivering it tomorrow night, same arrangement as before."

"You're delivering it tonight," Maren said. "The buyer moved the meet. He's spooked — says there were Spire riders asking questions at Ford Ansel two days ago."

My stomach did something complicated. "Inquisitors?"

"Worse. Ledger-men. The ones who count things." She said it lightly, but Maren doesn't use the word *worse* for nothing. "They weren't looking for contraband. They were looking for a person. Asking after travelers born in a certain season, under certain signs. Astrological nonsense, mostly, but they paid well for answers, and this valley is full of people who'd sell their grandmother's teeth for less."

"And you told them nothing."

"I told them I'm a chandler with bad knees and no interest in the sky." She sipped her drink. "Which is true, except for the part about the knees, which are excellent. But Kess — if the Spire's counting heads in my valley, I need to know whether whatever your partner is carrying is going to land on my shop. Because I like you both, and I'd hate to have to pretend otherwise to men with ledgers."

There it was. Maren Ostwick, offering me the exact shape of the question I'd been refusing to ask myself for two days, wrapped in tallow-scented courtesy.

"I don't know what she's carrying," I said. "She doesn't carry things. That's the whole point of her. Things slide off her. She's the least remarkable person in every room she enters, on purpose. It's a skill. I've watched her do it. Taverns full of people forget she was ever there."

"And yet," Maren said, "the Spire is in the valley."

"People get lost in mountains. Pilgrims go missing. It happens every winter."

"Not with ledger-men asking after birth signs, it doesn't." She set down her cup and looked at me with those pale, unhurried eyes. "How much do you owe me, Kess?"

The question landed sideways, the way her questions always do. "For the last shipment? Forty crowns, minus—"

"No. Total. Everything. Since the beginning."

I did the arithmetic without wanting to. It wasn't hard, because I'd done it before, late at night, in the honest dark where a man admits things to himself he won't admit to anyone else. "A hundred and ninety. Give or take."

"A hundred and ninety crowns," Maren repeated. "And do you know why I've never once pressed you for it? Why I let the debt sit there year after year, gathering interest I never charge?"

"Because I'm charming."

"Because you're useful, yes, and because you're careful, and because in six years you have never once brought trouble through my door." She leaned forward. "But I'll tell you the real reason, since we're being honest tonight. The real reason is that I watched you take a beating from the Corvane brothers rather than give up where our goods were cached, and a man who does that doesn't owe money. He owes loyalty. And I collect loyalty the way other people collect rent."

"That's very touching, Maren."

"It's not touching. It's bookkeeping." She tapped the counter once, twice. "So here is your favor, and here is its price. Tonight's delivery goes ahead, and you'll handle it alone, because your partner is in no state to be seen, and we both know it. In exchange, I want you to find out what's wrong with her. Not for me. For you. Because a man who protects someone without knowing what he's protecting them from isn't brave, Kess. He's just early to a funeral."

* * *

I made the delivery alone, and it went clean, which surprised nobody more than me. The buyer was a nervous wool merchant with sweat on his upper lip and a guard who kept checking the treeline, and I smiled my way through the handoff, counted the coin twice, and was back on the road by midnight with nothing heavier in my pockets than the weight of Maren's words, which were heavier than the silk.

*Early to a funeral.* Charming woman.

The walk back gave me time to think, and thinking is a thing I generally avoid on principle, because in my experience it leads directly to trouble. But the trail from the ford to Grayhollow is three hours of switchbacks and moonlight, and a man can only hum so many songs before his own head corners him.

Here's what I know about Jude Halloran, inventory-style, the way I know the contents of a cargo manifest:

She appeared in Grayhollow six years ago with a pack, a knife, and an accent she'd sanded the edges off of. She was good at passes immediately — not learning-good, *already*-good, the kind of good that comes from somewhere. She never gets drunk. She never sleeps with her back to a door. Once, in the low country, a Spire patrol stopped us on the road, and I swear on my mother's grave — wherever that is — the sergeant looked straight at her and then looked away, like his eyes had hit something slippery. She flinched that whole next day. Didn't eat. Kept rubbing her left wrist like it ached.

And seven years — she said it herself once, half-asleep by a fire in the Duskway camps, so quiet I almost missed it. *Seven years since I've heard thunder and meant it.* I asked her what she meant and she said, "Nothing. Go to sleep, Kess," and I did, because that's the arrangement.

Seven years. She came to Grayhollow six years ago. Do the arithmetic. I'd done it before, too, in that same honest dark, and every time I arrived at the same answer and every time I threw it away, because the answer was absurd. Because the alternative was that my partner — the woman who once spent three days nursing a stranger's sick kid through fever, who gives half her cut to the shrine in Grayhollow when she thinks I'm not looking, who is, in defiance of all evidence, the best person I know — was something the stories warn children about.

Storm-caller. Exiled heretic. The kind of name that gets a valley burned.

Except the Spire doesn't exile people anymore. That's the thing. The Spire *erases* them. There haven't been exiled mages walking around free in a generation — the order stopped that practice decades ago, according to every smuggler, priest, and drunk scholar I've ever shared a fire with. Whatever they do with people like that now, they don't do it with a pat on the back and directions to the border.

So either the stories are wrong, or Jude is older than she looks, or—

Or she's something else entirely. Something the stories don't have a name for yet.

I came over the last rise above Grayhollow with the town laid out below me, a handful of lamps in a bowl of dark, and I stood there longer than I needed to, letting the cold work on me, trying to decide which man I wanted to be when I walked down that hill. The sensible one. The one who finishes the job, pays down the debt, keeps the arrangement, asks nothing, learns nothing, stays safe.

Then I thought about her face that morning on the trail. White as a salt flat. Breathing like she'd seen a ghost, or become one.

I went down the hill.

* * *

Our room was above the cooper's yard, two floors of stairs that announced every footstep like a town crier, so I took them slow and quiet out of habit. The lamp was still burning under the door. Past midnight, and the lamp still burning.

She was sitting where I knew she'd be, in the chair by the window, the book open in her lap. Same position as when I'd left. And I knew before I saw her face that she hadn't moved, hadn't read a word, because the candle beside her had burned down a thumb's width and pooled wax on the sill, and the page she was on was the same page she'd been on when I left — I'd noticed it then without knowing why, some old smuggler's instinct for details that matter later.

"Kess." She didn't startle. She never startles. Another entry for the manifest. "Good delivery?"

"Clean. Buyer was twitchy, but his coin spends fine." I hung my coat on the peg. "Maren moved the meet up. Spire riders at Ford Ansel."

Something crossed her face — fast, controlled, but not fast enough, not for someone who'd spent six years learning to read her the way she reads weather. Fear. Real fear, the deep kind, the kind that has a history.

"Riders," she said carefully. "Looking for what?"

"Contraband, officially." I sat down on the edge of my cot, facing her. "Unofficially, Maren says they were asking after a person. Birth signs. Seasons. Black moons, that sort of thing."

The silence stretched. The candle flame leaned and righted itself, though there was no draft. I remember noticing that. I remember filing it away with all the other small wrongnesses, the ones I'd spent six years politely not noticing, and feeling the file drawer groan under the weight.

"Jude." My voice came out lower than I meant it to. "Six years. You've never lied to me. Not once — you just don't tell me things, and I've respected that, because everyone's entitled to a locked room. But if the Spire is in this valley looking for someone, and you already know it's you—"

"I don't know that."

"Don't lie to me. Not tonight. You said you never have."

She closed the book. Slowly. Set her hand flat on the cover like she was holding something down. And for a long moment she just looked at me, and I watched her weigh it — I actually watched her do the arithmetic, the same arithmetic I'd done on the trail, arriving at the same impossible sum.

"Kess," she said finally, and her voice was steady in the way that cost her something, "if I told you what's in this book, you'd have a choice to make. And I don't want you to make it. I want you to keep your hands clean and your ledger short and your life exactly the size it is. That's not me shutting you out. That's the only decent thing I've got left to give anybody."

"That's not your choice to make for me."

"It's the only kind of choice I get to make anymore." She almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Get some sleep. We move at first light — north, the old drover roads. There's somewhere I have to go, and I'd — " she stopped. Started again. "I'd rather not go alone."

North. The old drover roads. Which lead, if you follow them far enough, up past the tree line and into the deep fells, toward the ruins at Hollowmere, where the maps stop pretending and the mountains start keeping secrets.

I lay down on my cot in the dark and listened to her breathe across the room, even and careful, a woman pretending to sleep, and I stared at the ceiling beams and admitted the thing I'd been carrying since the salt-flat morning, the crack running through the middle of everything:

I don't know who she is. Six years, and I don't know who she is, and maybe I never did, and maybe the person I'd have taken a beating for is a story she invented to hide inside.

But I also knew this: whoever she was, whatever she was, she'd said *I'd rather not go alone*, and Jude Halloran did not ask for things. Ever. That request had cost her more than any confession would have.

So. North, then. Into the fells, toward Hollowmere, following a woman made of locked rooms into whatever was waiting there — Spire riders, black moons, storms with her name on them.

I'd settle the debt with Maren on the way out. Loyalty, after all, was the only currency I'd ever been rich in.
