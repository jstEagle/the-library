## 4. The Inquisitor's Ledger

The trouble with small towns is that they talk. The trouble with valley towns is that they talk *fast*, and the trouble with Lowmark specifically was that by the time Kess and I rolled our cart down from the high pass with a load of unlicensed dreamwine and a bad axle, half the town already knew an inquisitor was coming.

The other half knew he'd arrived.

"He's at the Wayfarer's Rest," said Marta Ossel, who sold us eggs and gossip in roughly equal measure and had never once given me a fair price on either. She said it the way people say *there's a wolf on the ridge*—quietly, with her eyes doing all the screaming. "Grey coat. Spire buttons. Been asking questions since before the ferry came in."

"What kind of questions?" Kess asked, easy as anything, because Kess could make even dread sound like small talk.

"Birth records." Marta's gaze flicked to me and away so fast I nearly missed it. Nearly. "Old ones. Church ledgers, midwife rolls. Asking after anyone born—" she lowered her voice like the word itself had a price tag— "*under a black moon.*"

I laughed. It came out about as convincing as a coin purse full of river stones, but Marta had the good grace—or the good fear—not to notice. She pressed two eggs into my hand, told us to keep our heads down, and hurried off toward her stall as if proximity to us might be contagious.

Kess watched her go. Then he looked at me, and his face did that thing it does when he's decided not to ask a question: everything goes carefully blank, like a shuttered window.

"Black moon," he said. "That's a real thing, then?"

"It's a rare thing." I turned back to the cart and pretended to inspect the axle, which was genuinely broken and therefore a convenient place to put my hands where they wouldn't shake. "Eclipse falls on the new moon. Sky goes black instead of red. Happens maybe once in twenty years."

"And people born under one are... what? Lucky?"

"Cursed, depending who you ask." I gave the axle a kick it didn't deserve. "Mostly they're nobody. Mostly it means nothing at all."

*Mostly.* There was the lie, sitting right there in the open, wearing my voice like a stolen coat. Because I'd been born under a black moon—I knew that the way you know your own heartbeat—and for seven years I had built my entire life on the assumption that no one else knew it too. That the record of it had burned with everything else I'd left behind. That I had successfully become a woman with no birthday, no birthplace, and no name worth writing down.

And now some grey-coated functionary of the order that exiled me was in a town of four hundred people asking midwives about eclipses.

I am, historically, a very calm person. It's a professional requirement. Smugglers who panic die in passes; storm-callers who panic die everywhere. But standing there in the mud of Lowmark's market square with an egg cooling in each palm, I felt something cold slide down through me, past the ribs, past the spine, into whatever place a person keeps the things they refuse to think about.

The prophecy book sat wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of my pack. Tonight's date. My name. Two endings, both of them mine.

And now, apparently, a reader on the other side of the page.

* * *

We took the room we always took at the Rest—the corner one with the crooked floor and the window facing the stable yard rather than the street—and I spent the afternoon doing the most dangerous thing I know how to do, which is nothing.

Kess went out to deal with the axle and, more importantly, to be seen dealing with the axle. That's the trick of it: when a town is watching, you give them something boring to watch. A man fixing a wheel is invisible. A woman sitting alone in her room with the curtain drawn is a story waiting to happen.

So I sat. And I listened. The walls of the Wayfarer's Rest are lath and horsehair, and sound travels through them like water through sand—slowed, but arriving eventually.

What arrived, over the course of three hours, was this:

The inquisitor's name was Corrane. Vael Corrane. He had come up from the Spire by the river road with two acolytes and a clerk, and he was paying for information in actual silver, which in Lowmark was less a bribe than a natural disaster. He had already interviewed the ferryman, the parish clerk, and—my stomach dropped at this one—a woman named Bree Halloway, who had been a midwife's apprentice thirty years ago and remembered, God help us all, *everything.*

He was polite. That was the detail that kept snagging in my mind like a splinter. Everyone who described him described the same thing: he was courteous, patient, soft-spoken, thanked people for their time. He bought drinks he didn't drink. He listened the way a well listens—as though whatever fell into him would never come back out.

Inquisitors aren't supposed to be polite. Inquisitors are supposed to be the blunt instrument, the boot in the door, the reason smugglers like me sleep with knives. Politeness meant something worse. Politeness meant he expected to find what he was looking for, and saw no need to hurry about it.

Near dusk, the walls gave me one more gift. The clerk—he had a reedy voice and the nervous habit of repeating himself—was talking to the innkeeper in the common room below.

"—the ledger itself, yes, the old parish ledger, he'll want it copied entire, every entry from the founding forward. He says—" a pause, the sound of someone choosing words carefully— "he says the Spire keeps its own records, but paper rots unevenly. What survives here may not survive there. And vice versa."

"What's he looking for exactly?" the innkeeper asked. "If you don't mind my asking. Folk are starting to wonder."

Another pause. Longer this time.

"He isn't looking for a crime," the clerk said finally. "He's looking for a *birth.*"

I sat very still on the crooked bed and felt the cold thing in my chest spread outward, patient as frost.

A century-old prophecy book, written by a dead hermit in a ruined monastery, had named me. And now the Spire—an institution that did not believe in prophecy, that had branded divination heretical at the same trial where they'd branded me—had sent a polite man with a clerk and a budget to dig through birth ledgers in a valley town I'd chosen precisely because it was forgettable.

Either the two things were coincidence, or they were the same thing. And I had read enough of that book by now, by lamplight, with my heart going like a trapped bird, to know that the author hadn't believed in coincidence either. Every margin note, every cross-reference, every smug little arrow connecting one doom to another—the prophet had treated fate like a ledger. Entries balanced. Debts collected.

Maybe the Spire had learned to keep books too.

* * *

Kess came back after dark smelling of axle grease and the Rest's terrible ale, and found me sitting in the dark with the lamp unlit, which even he could read as unusual.

"You could've lit the lamp," he said. "It's not like the inquisitor can see you through the wall."

"No," I agreed. "But I can hear him through it, and it seemed rude to have more light than he does."

He grunted, dropped onto the bed across from mine—it creaked under him the way everything in this building creaked, the whole structure held together by habit and termites—and started pulling off his boots. "Marta says he's got grey eyes. The inquisitor. Like winter ice, she says." He snorted. "She also says he tipped the ferryman a whole silver for telling him the crossing schedule, which tells you either he's rich, generous, or buying goodwill ahead of something ugly."

"All three," I said. "Spire pays its inquisitors well. Generosity is cheaper than force. And there's always something ugly coming. It's practically their motto."

"You know a lot about the Spire for a woman who claims she's never been within a hundred miles of it."

"I read," I said. "It's in books."

It was, technically. Some of it. The rest was in me, in the scar tissue under my skin where the binding brands had been cut, in the seven-year silence I maintained inside my own chest like a monk maintaining a vow. Kess knew I had secrets. He'd known it since the week we met, when he caught me flinching at thunder and I told him it was an old war injury and he'd looked at me for a long moment and then, mercifully, let it go. Whatever he thought those secrets were, he'd decided they were mine to keep. It was the single most expensive gift anyone had ever given me, and I repaid it daily with interest in lies.

Tonight the interest was due again.

"There's a road out of here north," I said, before I could stop myself. "Past the slate quarry. It's rough, but a cart can manage it if we take the axle slow."

Kess paused, one boot half off. "We just got here. We haven't sold the wine."

"We'll sell it in Harrowdale. Better prices anyway."

"It's three days to Harrowdale."

"Then we'd best leave early."

He got the boot off and set it down, and looked at me across the dark room, and I watched him decide—again, the way he decides every time—not to ask. But this time something flickered behind the decision, a hairline crack in the shuttered window, and his voice when it came was quieter than usual.

"Jude."

"Mm."

"What did you do?"

The question landed harder than any blade could have, because it wasn't accusatory. It was almost gentle. *What did you do*—not *what are you*, not *who are you*, but the assumption, always, that whatever trouble was circling us, it was trouble I'd made. Fair enough. Most of it was.

"Nothing," I said. "Not yet. That's the problem."

He waited. He was good at waiting; it was half of what made him a good partner. The other half was that he could carry a drunk mule uphill without complaining, which in the smuggling trade is rarer than honesty.

"Inquisitors don't come to towns like this for nothing," I said at last, staring at the ceiling. "They come because someone sent them. Someone with authority, and a reason, and—" I stopped, restarted. "There's a book. Back in Thornfell. A client paid me in it instead of coin, an old man with more volumes than sense. It's a prophecy text. Century old, maybe older. And it has..." I flexed my hands in the dark. "It has entries. Dates. Names."

"Whose names?"

"Mine."

The silence that followed had weight. I heard him breathing, slow and deliberate, the way he breathes when he's keeping himself from saying the first three things that occur to him.

"Your smuggling name?" he asked finally. "Because 'Jude Halloran' isn't exactly—"

"My real one."

I felt him go still across the dark. Not shocked—Kess doesn't shock, he processes—but still, the way the forest goes still when something large moves through it.

"The date's in it too," I went on, because apparently once the dam cracked I was going to stand in the flood like an idiot. "Tomorrow's date. Tonight's, now. And two endings written out in full, like the author couldn't decide which one he wanted, so he wrote both and left the choice to—" I laughed, short and bitter. "To me, I suppose. Or to whoever came along and fit the description."

"Which is you."

"Which is me."

Another silence. Outside, somewhere down the street, a door closed—softly, courteously, the way everything in this town seemed to close tonight—and I thought of grey coats and Spire buttons and a well-deep man buying drinks he didn't drink.

"So the inquisitor," Kess said slowly, "isn't here by accident."

"No."

"He's reading the same book."

"Or the same author," I said. "Or the same *orders*. I don't know yet. That's what I intend to find out before he finds me." I turned my head and found his outline against the faint grey of the window. "Which is why we leave north at first light, and why you're going to stop asking questions I can't answer, and why—"

"Jude." His voice was steady. Infuriatingly steady. "You don't have to tell me what you are. You never have, and I've never needed it. But you do have to tell me one thing, right now, tonight, or I'm walking into that common room and introducing myself to the nice inquisitor myself."

My heart stopped, restarted, and ran. "Which is?"

"Am I in danger being near you? Yes or no. Not someday. Now."

I opened my mouth to say *no*, because that's what partners say, because it's kind and it's loyal and it's what I'd been saying for seven years.

But the prophecy had my name in it twice, once for ruin and once for salvation, and neither ending had much room in it for a smuggler with strong shoulders and a crooked smile and a talent for fixing axles. Both endings had room for grief.

"Yes," I said.

It cost me something to say it. I felt it leave me like blood leaving a wound.

Kess was quiet for a long moment. Then he lay back on his bed, put his arms behind his head, and said, "Fine. North at first light, then. Wake me at the grey hour."

"That's it? *Fine?*"

"What do you want, a speech?" I could hear the grin in it, faint and stubborn, the grin that had gotten us out of more trouble than my magic ever had. "You just told me the truth for the first time in seven years. I'm not going to punish you for it by making it dramatic. Sleep, Jude. Tomorrow we run."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the window with the curtain cracked a finger's width and watched the inquisitor's lodging across the yard, where a lamp burned late behind shutters that didn't quite close.

Around midnight, the lamp moved. A silhouette crossed it—tall, unhurried—and paused, and I had the sudden crawling certainty that he was standing at his own window, looking out at the yard, at the stable, at the corner room where a woman sat in the dark pretending to be nobody.

He raised a hand. Just slightly. A small, courteous wave, to no one, to everyone, to the night in general.

Then the lamp went out.

I stayed at the window until the sky went grey, and when Kess stirred I woke him, and we loaded the cart in silence, and I did not look back at the Wayfarer's Rest because I was afraid of what I'd see in its windows.

North, past the slate quarry. Three days to Harrowdale, if Harrowdale was far enough, which I already suspected it wasn't. Somewhere behind us a polite man was copying birth ledgers by lamplight, and somewhere ahead of us lay a ruined monastery where a dead prophet had written my life down like a debt he expected to be collected.

The prophecy had readers on both sides of the page now.

Only one of us, I thought, as the cart groaned northward into the hills, had read the endings.

And only one of us was running.
