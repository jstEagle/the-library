## 5. Seven Years of Silence

Seven years is a long time to rehearse a memory. You sand down the edges. You tell yourself the version that lets you sleep: that you were young, that you were stupid, that you called a storm where you shouldn't have and the Spire did what Spires do. Clean. Simple. A crime, a punishment, a life.

It's a lie I've kept polished like a smuggler's honest face. And tonight—book open on my knees in a cold room above a tavern that smells of tallow and wet wool—I can't keep polishing it anymore.

So let me tell it the way it actually happened. All of it. Even the parts I spent seven years pretending not to remember.

* * *

I was nineteen when they put me in the white room, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that it was very clean for a place where a girl was going to die.

They don't tell you the Spire trials happen underground. The stories say the accused stand before the Archmage in the Hall of Winds, beneath the great oculus where the sky itself witnesses judgment. The truth is a room with no windows two hundred feet below the mountain, lit by mage-lamps so steady they make your eyes ache, because real light flickers and judgment, apparently, should not.

They took my boots first. That's what I remember most clearly at the start—not fear, not the weight of what I'd done, but the indignity of standing barefoot on stone so cold it burned. A woman in grey robes wrote something on a slate and didn't look at me. Two wardens flanked the door with the particular stillness of men who had done this many times and felt nothing.

"Jude Halloran," said the woman with the slate. "Born under the black moon, the ninth of Frostwane."

"Yes."

"You will state your name again for the record."

"I just did."

The wardens shifted. The woman finally looked up, and her expression was the one my mother used to wear when I asked questions at dinner—the look that said the world would be easier if I simply didn't.

"For the record," she repeated.

"Jude Halloran." I said it slowly, like she was simple. "Born under the black moon, the ninth of Frostwane. Also known as the girl whose boots you stole."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. It might have been a smile. It might have been indigestion. In seven years I've decided it doesn't matter.

Then the door opened and the Archmage came in, and everything after that has the strange, slippery quality of a dream you can't quite hold onto—which I told myself for years was just trauma doing its work. Now I'm not so sure. Now, holding a book written before my grandmother drew breath, I'm starting to wonder if the memory slips because someone wanted it to slip.

* * *

Here is what I confessed to, because it was true:

Three days before my arrest, I had stood on the high pasture above our village in a summer storm and *called* it. Not weather-sense, not the small domestic magics the Spire tolerated in rural hedge-witches—reading rain, settling wind, coaxing a late frost off the orchards. I had reached into the heart of a living storm and pulled, and the lightning had come down where I asked it to come down, and for about ninety seconds I had been the most powerful thing on that mountainside.

I hadn't hurt anyone. That was the part nobody cared about. The bolt hit the old lightning-tree on the ridge, split it black, and I stood there laughing with my arms out and rain in my teeth, feeling—for the first time in my life—like the world and I were made of the same material.

My aunt saw the scorch from the valley. My aunt reported me within the hour. Family loyalty in Thornfell runs exactly as deep as the Spire's reward money.

So yes. Guilty. I said so myself, standing barefoot in the white room, chin up, nineteen years old and certain that honesty would count for something. I confessed to the calling. I confessed that I'd known the Edict of Stillness forbade it. I even confessed that I'd do it again, which in hindsight was maybe not the strategic masterstroke I believed it to be.

The Archmage listened to all of it without expression. He was not what I expected. I'd imagined thunder and robes and a voice like an avalanche. Instead there was this narrow, tidy man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles, who looked less like a tyrant than a clerk, and who asked his questions in the mild tone of a man checking figures.

"You called the storm deliberately."

"Yes."

"It answered you."

"It—yes. It answered me."

"How long have you been able to compel lightning, as opposed to merely sensing it?"

That question should have been the first crack. I've replayed it a thousand times. *Compel*, he said, not *call*. And how did he know there was a difference? Most mages couldn't have told you the difference between a storm-caller and a weather-reader if you paid them. But I was nineteen and flattered that anyone was taking me seriously, so I answered.

"Since the winter. Maybe since the autumn. It's hard to explain—it's like the difference between hearing a dog bark and having it come when you whistle."

"And the storm comes when you whistle." He wrote something. "Has anything ever refused you?"

"No."

He stopped writing. For just a moment—half a heartbeat—he looked up at me over those little spectacles, and what was in his face wasn't condemnation or curiosity.

It was satisfaction.

I know how that sounds. Nineteen-year-olds imagine everyone is secretly impressed with them. But I have had seven years and a great many long nights on cold passes to turn that moment over, and I will swear it on my mother's grave: the Archmage of the Spire looked at me, a barefoot girl from a sheep village awaiting sentence for heresy, and he looked *pleased*.

* * *

The trial lasted three days. They call it a trial the way they call the white room a chamber—it has the shape of the thing without any of the substance.

Day one was my confession. Day two was the examination: wardens brought in witnesses from the village, my aunt among them, weeping beautifully and testifying that I'd been unnatural since childhood, that storms followed me, that my mother—who was dead by then and conveniently unable to contradict anyone—had been strange too. Strange blood. Strange line. I sat there listening to my own history get rewritten by people who'd bought bread from my mother, and I learned something that day that has served me better than any magic: grief makes excellent ink. Whatever they write with it stays written.

Day three was the verdict, and here is where my polished lie starts to splinter, because day three is where the fragments live.

The verdict was read aloud by a different clerk—a thin man with a voice like dry paper—and I want to set down exactly what he said, because for seven years I remembered it wrong. Memory is a liar and comfort is its currency. What I remembered him saying was: *"For the crime of storm-calling contrary to the Edict of Stillness, the accused is sentenced to exile."*

What he actually said—and I know because the phrase came back to me last night with the force of a landslide, whole and exact, the way only the worst things return—was:

*"As foreseen, the accused is sentenced to exile."*

As foreseen.

Not *by law*. Not *by the judgment of this council*. As *foreseen*. And no one in that room reacted. No warden blinked. No clerk paused. The Archmage studied his notes. It slid past all of them like a coin dropped in deep water, and I stood there barefoot and nineteen and too terrified to ask the only question that mattered:

Foreseen by whom?

* * *

There were other fragments. Little things. Splinters I worked out of my skin over the years and threw away, one by one, because keeping them hurt and I wanted so badly to be free.

The cell they held me in before sentencing had a window. Cells in the Spire's underlevels shouldn't have windows—you're two hundred feet under a mountain—but mine did, a slit of grey light at ankle height, and through it I could hear voices from the corridor whenever the wardens changed shift. On the second night I heard two of them talking. Young ones, bored, the way people talk when they think the walls are furniture.

"—says the timing has to be right. Black moon cohort, they're calling it—"

"There's only the one this cycle."

"Only one it *needs*, maybe."

And then footsteps, and nothing more. At the time I filed it under nonsense. Prisoners hear nonsense. Fear whispers. I was nineteen and I wanted my life to be a story with a moral, and *the Spire harvested girls born under black moons according to a schedule* does not have a moral. It doesn't even have a shape a sane mind wants to hold.

Another fragment: the sentence itself. Exile sounds merciful until you sit inside it. Banishment from Spire lands, the brand on my wrist—they seared a sigil into the skin that aches when I use even hedge-magic now, a leash disguised as a mark of shame—and a formal edict that any Spire mage was bound to strike me down on sight. That last part never made sense to me. Exile is exile. Why post a kill-order on a girl with no army, no allies, no power they'd just finished stripping? You don't hunt what's already gone.

You hunt what you expect to come back.

And the strangest fragment, the one I've saved for last because it's the one the book cracked open: the escort. They didn't dump me at the border with a warning, the way exiles are supposedly handled. They gave me an escort—two wardens who walked me three days east, past the border stones, deep into the Thornfells, to a specific valley. One of them, the older one, a grey-bearded man with kind eyes and a killer's hands, spoke to me exactly once on that road. We'd stopped at a stream and he crouched beside me while I drank, and he said, quietly, not looking at me:

"Live small, girl. Live dull. However long it takes."

I thought it was kindness. I carried it as kindness for seven years—*live small, live dull*, the advice of a decent man in an indecent job.

But last night, reading a dead prophet's handwriting by candlelight, I understood it differently. Because that warden didn't say it like a warning.

He said it like an instruction.

* * *

Here is the shape of it, now that I can finally bear to look at the whole thing straight on. I'll write it plainly, because if I dress it up I'll lose my nerve:

A century ago, someone wrote a prophecy naming a Halloran girl born under a black moon, on a date that falls tonight, who will either destroy the Spire or save it. Last night I found that prophecy in a dead man's library, and found my own name in it, and found tonight's date, and I have not slept and do not intend to pretend otherwise.

And seven years ago, a tidy man with ink-stained fingers examined a nineteen-year-old storm-caller born under a black moon, heard her confess that storms answer when she whistles and have never once refused, and looked *satisfied*. A clerk read a sentence scripted in advance—"as foreseen"—and no one blinked. Wardens escorted her not to a border but to a specific valley, and instructed her to live small and live dull, and then the Spire posted a standing kill-order to guarantee that she would stay exactly where they'd put her, hunted, hidden, and alive.

You don't exile a weapon. You holster it.

They didn't cast me out seven years ago. They *planted* me. And every year of careful invisibility, every smuggled crate, every night I swallowed my magic and called it survival—that wasn't my freedom. That was their patience.

* * *

Downstairs, the tavern noise swells and fades like tide. Kess is somewhere below, haggling with the fence over the Halvatt silk, lying charmingly about its provenance, and I would give a great deal to be down there with him instead of up here with a book that knows my name better than I do.

Because there's one more fragment. One more splinter I never worked out, because it never fit anything—until tonight.

On the last morning, before the escort walked me east, the Archmage came to my cell himself. Unannounced. Alone, which I later learned was against every rule the Spire has. He stood outside the bars with his spectacles and his ink-stained fingers, and he looked at me for a long time, and then he said:

"Do you know why we don't execute storm-callers, Halloran?"

"I assumed we're rare."

"We are not rare. We are *singular*." He smiled then, small and private, like a man admiring his own garden. "Storms are the oldest power in the world, older than wards, older than oaths, older than the Spire. Everything we build to protect this realm stands on top of them. And once in a generation, sometimes once in a century, a child is born whom the storms treat as kin." He tapped the bars once, gently, almost affectionately. "Kin is valuable, Halloran. Kin is worth waiting for. Go and grow. We'll know where to find you."

I thought it was a threat. I built my whole life around it being a threat—every hidden year, every swallowed storm, every mile of pass and border I put between us.

But threats are for enemies, and you don't threaten your own fields.

Tonight the dark moon rises over Thornfell, and a book written a hundred years ago says this is the night the Halloran girl chooses, and somewhere behind me in the dark, I'd bet my last stolen coin, the Spire's riders are already on the road.

They kept their appointment.

I intend to keep mine.
