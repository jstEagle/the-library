## 6. The Road to Hollowmere

*Jude*

The thing about running from the Spire is that you have to decide, early and firmly, not to think about it too much.

I'd made that decision somewhere around the third mile out of Marrow's Bend, when I'd looked back at the valley town—its chimneys smoking peacefully, its market square already forgetting me—and seen a speck on the eastern ridge road that might have been a rider or might have been a rock with ambitions. Since then I'd kept my eyes forward and my thoughts on logistics, which is the smuggler's answer to every existential crisis: where's the next water, where's the next blind corner, how many hours of dark do we have.

Kess had asked me twice what we were actually doing. Both times I'd said something about a job in the high valleys, which was true in the way that a knife is true—it had an edge and it pointed somewhere, and if you held it wrong it would cost you.

The truth was a dead monastery called Hollowmere, three days into the interior of Thornfell, where a woman named Sera Voss—or whatever her real name was—had written my name down a hundred years before I existed, along with tonight's date, along with two endings for me like she couldn't make up her mind whether I was a fire or a flood.

I hadn't told Kess any of that.

"Your pack's riding crooked," Kess said behind me. He had the mule's lead rope and the particular tone he got when he'd been chewing on a question long enough to swallow it whole.

"It's balanced."

"It's listing left like a drunk leaving a tavern."

"Then the mule's drunk. It happens."

He didn't laugh, which was how I knew we weren't fine. Kess laughs at everything, especially things that aren't funny. It's one of his better qualities and one of his most annoying ones, depending on how much trouble we're in. We were in enough that the silence had weight.

We climbed. The pass above Marrow's Bend wasn't on any Spire map because it wasn't really a pass at all—it was a seam in the mountain that opened up every few decades and let smugglers through, and the mountain seemed content to keep pretending otherwise. Loose scree, then pine, then a shelf of granite with a drop on our right that went down far enough to make your stomach file a complaint. The wind came up the face of it smelling of snow that hadn't fallen yet.

I felt the wind the way I always did now. Like a dog hearing its name in another room.

Seven years since I'd touched it. Seven years of keeping my hands in my pockets, metaphorically speaking, while weather happened around me without my permission or participation. Most days I could pretend I was just a woman who was very, very good at reading clouds. Some days—the day I found my name inked in a dead woman's book, for example—I remembered exactly what I was, and the remembering sat in my chest like a coal that wouldn't cool.

"You're limping," Kess said.

"I'm favoring my left knee because the terrain is garbage."

"You've been favoring it since yesterday."

"And I'll stop favoring it when the terrain improves. Are you going to narrate my body all day, or—"

"Jude." He stopped walking. The mule stopped, because the mule had more sense than both of us. "Talk to me."

There it was. The question underneath all his questions. He'd been carrying it since Marrow's Bend, since the night I'd come back from Old Tam's house with a stack of books instead of coin and a look on my face that apparently scared him, though I'd been trying very hard for neutral.

"We're on a schedule," I said.

"The schedule can wait thirty seconds. The schedule has waited thirty seconds before and survived."

I looked at him. Gray eyes, windburnt, the little scar through his eyebrow from the Corren Ford job that he refused to explain and I refused to stop inventing stories about. My partner. The only person alive whose opinion of me I cared about, which was precisely why I couldn't hand him the truth. Not yet. Maybe not ever. The truth had teeth and I knew where they'd land.

"When we get where we're going," I said, "you'll understand."

"Which is where?"

"A place."

"That's not an answer, Jude, that's a direction with commitment issues."

"Welcome to my entire personality." I started walking again, because if I stood still much longer the coal in my chest would start talking through my mouth, and what it wanted to say was *I found my name in a book written by someone who's been dead longer than we've been alive and I don't know who I am anymore,* and that sentence doesn't fit in a partnership built on cargo manifests and alibis. "Keep up."

Behind me, after a moment, boots on stone. The lead rope creaking. Kess muttering something under his breath that I chose not to hear, because hearing it would have meant answering it, and I was fresh out of answers that weren't lies.

That was the arithmetic of the road, those first days: every mile toward Hollowmere bought us distance from the inquisitors and cost me another coin of trust I'd have to pay back eventually, with interest. I kept spending anyway. That's what liars do when they love somebody.

They spend.

* * *

*Kess*

Here's what I knew about Jude Halloran: she liked her eggs burnt, slept with her back to walls, could read a mountainside faster than most men read their own hands, and lied like other people breathed—which is to say, fluently and without malice, mostly at people who deserved it.

Here's what I didn't know: why she'd gone white as milk over a dead man's books, why she was dragging us three days into the empty teeth of Thornfell toward nothing I'd ever heard of, and why she flinched every time the wind picked up like the wind owed her money.

Partners don't need to know everything. I'm a firm believer in that. Half of staying alive in this trade is not knowing things; knowledge is heavy and gets you hanged. But there's a difference between not knowing and watching the person next to you come apart at the seams and being told it's nothing. One of those is privacy. The other one starts to feel like being managed, and I'd been managed before, by people with ledgers, and I didn't like the taste of it coming off someone I trusted with my life twice a week.

So I watched her instead. That was the work of those days, alongside the climbing: I watched.

She checked the sky more than she used to. Not the way a smuggler checks it—we check it like merchants, for what the weather will cost us. She checked it like it was going to say something. Twice on the second morning I caught her with her head tipped back and her lips moving, and when I asked what she was saying she told me she was counting birds, which was a lie so lazy it almost insulted us both. There weren't any birds. There hadn't been birds since the cold front came down, and Jude Halloran knew weather better than anyone I'd ever met, which meant she knew there weren't birds, which meant she thought I didn't.

That was the part that stuck in me. Not the lie. The assumption I needed one.

We camped that second night in a hollow below a ridge the old-timers called the Shepherd's Shoulder, out of the wind, with a fire small enough to cup in your hands. She took first watch. I pretended to sleep and listened to her not-sleeping, the small sounds of a person sitting very still and listening to the dark like it was reading aloud.

Around midnight she woke me, quiet, hand on my shoulder, and I came up with a knife before my eyes opened because that's the training talking.

"Riders," she whispered. "Below us. On the valley road."

I heard them then. Hooves, spaced careful, the way soldiers ride at night when they're hunting rather than traveling. Four sets, maybe five. Nobody rides the valley road at midnight in formation unless they're looking for somebody, and there are only two somebodies on this mountain.

"Inquisitors?" I asked.

"Or bounty men working for them." Her voice was flat and steady and I noticed, distantly, that her steadiness frightened me more than the riders did. This was a different Jude. This was Jude with the mask fitted properly, and I realized I'd gotten used to her half-mask, the wry one, the one that slipped.

"What's the play?"

"We go dark. No fire, no noise. They'll pass the shoulder and keep climbing toward the high tarns—they're sweeping the obvious routes." She was already kicking dirt over the coals. "We move at first light, north fork instead of south. It adds half a day."

"It adds half a day," I repeated, "and puts us on a route nobody uses because of the washout."

"The washout's crossable."

"The washout killed the Ferro brothers."

"The washout killed the Ferro brothers' judgment. They tried to cross it loaded." She shouldered her pack. In the last gray light of the dying fire I saw her face, and there was something in it I'd never seen before—not fear exactly, but the shape fear makes when it's been living somewhere long enough to furnish the rooms. "Trust me on this one, Kess."

And here's the thing, the thing I've turned over a thousand times since: I did. Right then, in the dark, with armed men on the road below us and my partner lying to me by the hour—I trusted her completely. Not because she'd earned it in that moment. Because seven years of shared roads is its own kind of proof, and because when she said *trust me*, something in me answered before my suspicion could get a word in.

I lay back down and listened to the riders pass below us, close enough that I heard the jingle of Spire-issue tack, the silver bells they put on their bridles so everyone in the valley knows who owns the night. Five horses. A sergeant's cadence in the hoofbeats.

When they were gone, I didn't sleep. I lay there doing sums I didn't like. Who hunts a pair of low-grade smugglers with a Spire patrol? Nobody. We were worth a fine, not a cavalry sweep. Which meant they weren't hunting smugglers. They were hunting something else, and the only unusual thing about either of us lately was—

Was whatever Jude was carrying in that crooked pack, and whatever she'd read in that dead man's book, and whatever lived in the set of her shoulders when the wind rose.

At first light we crossed the Shepherd's Shoulder and took the north fork toward the washout, and I followed her, and I hated myself a little for how easily I did it, and I'd have done it again in a heartbeat.

Love, as near as I can figure, is mostly that. Following someone up a bad road while asking yourself hard questions and deciding the questions can wait.

* * *

*Jude*

The washout was worse than I'd remembered and I'd remembered it honestly, which shows you how bad it was.

Half the hillside had let go some years back and taken the trail with it, leaving a scar of loose shale forty feet across with nothing under it but air and a riverbed a long way down. The Ferro brothers had died trying to cross it with a full load, which was stupid, and Kess invoked their names twice during our approach, which was fair.

"We go one at a time," I said. "Light steps. Don't stop in the middle even if the middle tries to argue."

"And the mule?"

"The mule goes last and hates us forever."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. We got the packs across, then Kess, quick and sure, that particular grace he had on bad ground. Then me, and I won't pretend my knees loved it. Then the mule, who dug in at the lip and gave us a look of profound betrayal before Kess talked him across with the lead rope and a voice gone soft as flour.

On the far side, breathing hard, Kess looked at me and said, "You want to tell me now what's at Hollowmere? Since we're committed to the scenic route?"

I should have lied again. I had a good one ready—a client, a cache, a debt to collect. It was on my tongue, pre-loaded.

What came out instead, because the road had stripped me down to shorter sentences, was: "A grave."

He blinked. "Whose?"

"A woman who wrote a book. A hundred years ago." I busied myself with the mule's girth strap, which needed no help. "She wrote things in it. Things that haven't happened yet. One of them happened four nights ago, in Marrow's Bend, and it was about me. By name."

Silence. Wind in the shale. Somewhere below us the riverbed ran with meltwater, patient as everything else in these mountains.

"Jude," Kess said carefully, "people write nonsense about the future all the time. Half the prophecy trade is old women and confidence men splitting the profits."

"This one had tonight's date. And my mother's name for me—the one nobody's used since I was nine years old." I finally looked at him. "It also had two endings. One where I burn the Spire down. One where I save it. And I need to know which one I'm walking toward, because the Spire sent riders to a valley town asking about a storm-caller born under a black moon, and I was born under a black moon, and I am so tired, Kess."

The words just kept coming, like water finding cracks. I'd meant to ration them.

He didn't laugh. He didn't call it nonsense. He sat down on a rock, took off his cap, and rubbed his scalp the way he did when he was thinking hard, and I braced for every version of *you're sick, you're cursed, you're mad.*

What he said was: "Who else knows you're going there?"

"No one."

"Then we're ahead of them." He put the cap back on. "Half a day on the washout route, you said. So we walk fast, we sleep cold, and you tell me the rest on the move—everything, in order, none of the summary version where you skip the parts that scare you. Those are the parts I need."

I stared at him. "You believe me."

"I believe you're the best judge of danger I've ever met, and you're scared worse than the night the Corrane contract fell through, and that's evidence enough for me." He stood, and picked up the mule's lead, and there it was again—that easy, terrible faith of his, handed over like it cost him nothing. "Also, if a hundred-year-old book says the world ends with you in it, I'd very much like to read the margins."

Something in my chest cracked, painlessly, the way ice does when the thaw finally reaches it.

"Everything," I promised. "In order."

"Liar's oath?"

"Liar's oath."

And we walked north, toward Hollowmere and the dead woman who'd written me, and I told him as much of the truth as I could carry out loud—and kept the rest folded up inside me where it burned.

Because here's what I didn't tell him, the piece I held back even with my hand on the liar's oath: the prophecy's last line. The one I'd read four times by candlelight until the letters swam.

*One road leads to ruin and one to salvation, and the girl will choose—but the author notes, in closing, that she has met the Halloran line before, and they never choose. They are chosen for.*

Somewhere behind us, five riders were turning back from the high tarns, having found nothing, having been meant to find nothing.

And somewhere ahead, in a monastery that had been ash for a century, something was waiting that had been chosen for me before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

I just didn't know yet that it was waiting in the margins.
