## 7. Ash and Marginalia

Hollowmere looked like a place that had died of embarrassment rather than violence. The monastery clung to a shelf of rock above a dead river, its walls slumped like an old man in a chair he'd been told three times to get out of. No scorch marks. No craters. Just centuries of neglect wearing the stone down the way water wears down a lie—slowly, patiently, until there's nothing left worth defending.

"Charming," Kess said behind me. He said it the way he says everything, with that flat smuggler's delivery that means he's either unimpressed or terrified and has decided terror is undignified.

"It's perfect," I said. "Nobody's been here in a hundred years."

"That's usually why nobody's here."

I let him grumble. Grumbling was how Kess prayed. We'd come up the last stretch of road in the dark, off the smuggler paths entirely, following the prophet's map—the one I'd copied out of the almanac by candlelight while Kess slept and pretended not to notice me not sleeping. Three days of cold camps and backtracking twice when we heard hooves on the high trail. Corrane's riders, or bounty mages, or just merchants with bad timing. In Thornfell you learn to assume the worst and be pleasantly surprised maybe once a decade.

The gate was gone. The doors were gone. What remained of the chapel roof had folded inward like a hand closing over something it didn't want anyone to see.

We went in anyway.

* * *

Inside, the air had that particular stillness ruins get—the silence of a room where even the dust has given up on ambition. Moonlight came through the collapsed roof in pale columns, and in those columns hung motes that refused to settle, as if the building were breathing very slowly and didn't want us to know.

Kess did what Kess does: he circled the perimeter, checked sightlines, found the two exits and then found the third one I'd missed, because Kess always finds the third exit. He's the reason I'm alive, though I'd sooner swallow my own tongue than tell him that.

"I'll take the scriptorium," I said.

"You know where the scriptorium is?"

"The map. East wing, past the refectory." I tapped my temple. "I've got a hundred-year-old floor plan in my head now. It's very crowded in here. Mostly prophecy and floor plans."

He gave me a look—the look he's been giving me for three days, the one that says *you're going to tell me eventually, right?*—and I gave him the look back that says *no.* We've developed quite a vocabulary without words. It's the most honest part of our partnership.

The east wing had fared worse than the chapel. The corridor ceiling had come down in one long section, and I crossed the rubble on all fours like something apologetic, feeling grit work into my gloves. Past the collapse, the refectory opened up—a long hall with trestle tables rotted to lace, benches tipped like dominoes mid-fall. And at the far end, past the refectory, a door hanging by one hinge, and beyond it:

Books.

Not many. A score, maybe thirty, huddled in niches carved into the walls, wrapped in oilcloth that had gone stiff as bark. The scriptorium itself was a single round room with a great window blown out onto the valley, and a desk beneath the window, and on the desk—

A ledger. Open. Face down, as if the writer had stepped out to fetch more ink and simply never come back.

My hands were shaking before I touched it. I want to say that was the cold. It wasn't the cold.

* * *

Here's what I knew going in, because I'd read the almanac cover to cover twice on the road, and because some things you read and your body understands them before your mind consents to:

The author signed herself only as *M., of Hollowmere.* A century ago she wrote an almanac of weather and omens, and buried inside the weather, in entries no sane person would flag, she wrote about a storm-caller born under a black moon who would stand at the Spire and either break it or save it. She wrote tonight's date. She wrote my name—not Halloran, the name I wear like a coat, but the other one, the true one, the one the Spire struck from their rolls seven years ago with great ceremony and a hot iron.

What I didn't know was why a woman a hundred years dead had bothered.

The ledger answered the way old documents do: reluctantly, and mostly in the margins.

The main text was meticulous. Rainfall, wind direction, frost dates, the flowering times of stubborn mountain flowers. M. had the handwriting of someone who genuinely loved order—small, upright, economical. But in the margins, squeezed between lines, crammed sideways into corners, sometimes written so faintly I had to tilt the page to catch the light, was another voice entirely. Faster. Angrier. The handwriting of someone writing fast because they might be interrupted, or caught, or worse.

*They do not call it theft,* one margin note read, beside an entry about a drought year. *They call it stewardship.*

Another, beside a note on lightning strikes in the Spire's vicinity: *The towers drink. Count the storms that die within a league of it and count the storms that pass. Then ask yourself what a ward is FOR.*

I sat down on a stool that had been waiting a century for someone, and I read until my eyes burned.

* * *

Let me explain what I thought I knew about the Spire, because understanding what I believed is the only way to understand what it cost to stop believing it.

The Spire protects the realm. That's not propaganda; it's arithmetic. Two hundred years ago, during the Sundering Wars, the mage order raised wards around the valleys and passes, and the wars ended, and the wards held, and everyone from kings to goat-herders has been grateful ever since. The order takes tithe and tribute and the occasional child who shows talent, and in exchange, nothing burns. Storms break against the high wards. Blights fail at the borders. It's a good bargain, or it was presented as one, and I never had cause to audit it.

Until they audited *me.* Until I called a storm at fourteen—accidentally, stupidly, gloriously—and the sky over my village turned green and hail fell in summer and a neighbor's barn lost its roof, and within a month there were gray-cloaked assessors in our kitchen asking my mother questions in soft voices while I sat there knowing, the way children know things, that I had already been sold.

They took me to the Spire. They tested me for two years. And then they tried me, and the charge was heresy—not the storm, understand, but the *manner* of it. My magic, they said, was untethered. Unsanctioned. I drew power from outside the sanctioned channels, which meant outside their control, which in the Spire's grammar is the same word as *evil.* Seven years ago they put the brand on me and walked me to the mountain pass and told me if I ever called a storm again, the wards would find me, and the wards would not be gentle.

And I believed it. That's the humiliating part. I believed every word, because believing it kept the shape of the world intact. Exile was survivable if exile was *just.* If the Spire was arrogant but honest, corrupt in small ways the way all institutions are, then my ruined life was bad luck and bad temper, not design.

The margins in that ledger had other ideas.

*The wards are not a wall,* M. had written, in a hand gone jagged with urgency. *A wall keeps things out. These keep things IN. I have seen the lower vaults—I should not have seen them, and I will pay for seeing them. There are cages under the Spire, and in the cages are storms. Living ones. Old ones. They scream in a register the ear cannot catch but the bone can. Every ward on this continent is strung from their suffering like a fence strung from sinew.*

I read that sentence four times. On the fourth reading my vision blurred and I realized I wasn't blinking.

Storm-spirits. Caged. The thing every mountain child knows as story—the storm-mothers, the thunder-elders, the old powers you leave offerings for at high passes so they'll be gentle with your herd—we'd been taught they *left.* Faded. Went wherever old gods go when people stop needing them. The Spire's own histories said so: *the wild powers withdrew; the order stands vigil in their absence.*

Vigil. Ha. You don't post a vigil over an absence. You post a vigil over a prisoner.

* * *

"Jude."

Kess's voice, low, from the doorway. I hadn't heard him cross the refectory, which meant I'd been far away, which meant I'd been careless, which in my line of work is how you end up in a shallow grave with your boots stolen.

"You've been up here an hour," he said. "You didn't answer the whistle."

"There was a whistle?"

"Twice." He came closer, and in the moonlight his face did the thing it does when he's deciding whether to push. "You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse. I've seen handwriting." I held up the ledger. "Kess, this woman—M.—she was one of them. Spire-trained. Look at the notation system. It's theirs, the formal cipher they use for weather records. She defected. Or she was cast out, or—" I stopped, because the next thought arrived and it was large and cold. "Or she was sent."

"Sent?"

"To watch. To record. And then she saw too much and started recording the wrong things." I turned pages, hunting. "There's a sequence near the end. Dates, names, bloodlines. She tracked families. Mountain families. Families with—"

I found it. Of course I found it. I think some part of me had known it was there since the moment I saw the desk, the way you know a wound is deep before you pull the shirt back.

The entry was dated ninety-eight years before tonight. M.'s tidy hand, and then the margin voice breaking through it like a crack through glaze:

*The order does not wait for storm-callers to be born. That is the lie beneath the lie. They cultivate them. Bloodlines with the old affinity are mapped, watched, married, moved—yes, MOVED, whole households relocated to valleys where the caged storms run close to the surface, where the children breathe it in with the rainwater. A caller raised wild is a wildfire. A caller raised inside the doctrine is a key. They need a key. The cages are failing—I have measured the tremors—and when they fail, the order intends to have a vessel ready. One of their own making. Bred to hold what breaks loose, or to be broken by it. Either outcome serves them. Either outcome proves the wards necessary and the order indispensable.*

Below that, smaller, almost tender, which made it infinitely worse:

*I knew a family in the Thornfell valleys. Good people. The grandmother could taste weather coming in her teeth. The order resettled them twenty years ago—took them from the coast and planted them in a high valley where the spirit roads run near. I warned the grandmother. She laughed at me. Who laughs at a warning from a madwoman? Her granddaughter's daughter will be born under a black moon. I have done the arithmetic. I am so sorry. I am so sorry. There is nothing else in me but being sorry.*

The stool was cold under me. The ruin was cold around me. None of it mattered, because the cold was coming from inside, from a place no coat reaches.

The granddaughter's daughter. Born under a black moon. In a high valley where the spirit roads run near, where a family with weather-taste in their teeth had been *planted* like a crop.

My mother's mother could taste weather in her teeth. My mother used to say it like a party trick, laughing, licking the wind off her finger. And my family lived in a high valley in Thornfell that my grandmother had come to as a girl—from the coast, my mother always said, though she never said why, and I never asked, because children don't ask, and by the time I was old enough to ask, everyone who could answer was dead or scattered.

Seven years. Seven years I'd built my exile into a shape I could carry: *bad luck, bad blood, bad temper, wrong place wrong time.* A story with a villain, sure, but a stupid villain, a bureaucratic villain, the kind of villain that loses paperwork and overreaches and can be dodged by a clever smuggler with good boots.

It wasn't a villain. It was a *gardener.*

I was the crop.

* * *

"Jude." Kess again, closer now, and his hand on my shoulder, warm through the cold. "You're white as chalk. Talk to me."

I couldn't. Not the whole of it. Some truths are too big to say out loud—they come out the size of a house and crush whoever hears them, including you. So I said the piece that fit in my mouth.

"This book predicted me. Before I was born. My name, my trial, tonight—it's all in there, Kess. I told you the almanac had my name in it. This is the rest of it." My voice came out level, which surprised me. Level and strange, like someone else's. "I wasn't born cursed. I was born *planted.* My grandmother was moved to that valley on purpose. My whole life—the village, the testing, the trial, the exile—all of it was cultivation. They weren't punishing me. They were *finishing* me."

Kess was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into the register he uses for frightened horses and frightened partners. "Planted by who? The Spire doesn't do this. The Spire holds the wards. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what they've been told." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "That's the genius of it. You cage the storms, you string the wards from the cages, and then you spend two hundred years being thanked for protection from dangers that only exist because of you. And when the cages start to fail—because everything fails, Kess, everything—you make sure there's a girl bred to soak up the disaster, so you can point at her and say *see, see, the wild powers are monsters, give us more tithe, more children, more power.* Release or ruin. Those were the words in the almanac. I thought they were about a choice I'd make." I pressed my palm flat against the ledger, against a dead woman's furious handwriting. "They're not a choice. They're two products. Two ways the harvest comes in."

Kess crouched down in front of me, forcing me to look at him. His jaw was set the way it gets before a hard run through a patrol line. "Then we burn it," he said simply. "Whatever it is. The plan, the breeding program, the—cages, whatever. We find the edges of it and we burn it."

"You don't even believe in magic plans. You believe in cargo and ledgers."

"I believe in you," he said, like it was nothing, like it was arithmetic, and I had to look away because that sentence hurt worse than everything in the margins combined. He believes in a version of me that doesn't exist—the smuggler, the partner, the woman who is only what she seems. Every hour I don't tell him what I am, that belief gets heavier to carry. Someday it's going to break something, and I already know it'll be him.

"Not yet," I said. "First we finish reading. M. left more. There's a map reference—she mentions the spirit roads running under Thornfell, and a tomb, and—" I flipped toward the back of the ledger, hunting the final pages. "She says if anyone ever reads this, they should go to her grave. She says she left the truth there, the whole truth, the part she couldn't risk writing down."

"And where's the grave?"

I found the page. Found the map sketched in the margin, quick and sure, ridge-lines and a river and a cairn marked with a symbol I recognized with a jolt: the same black-moon sigil that headed the almanac's final entry. Tonight's entry.

"Half a day north," I said. "Past the Ford." Blackmoon Ford. Where the dark moon rises in nine days. Where, if the almanac's divergent endings meant anything, one version of me stood and broke the world.

Kess exhaled slowly. Outside, far off down the valley, thunder muttered—harmless, ordinary, autumn thunder. Except I would never hear ordinary thunder again. Now I'd hear it and wonder if it was one of them. One of the caged. Calling through the wards, the way prisoners tap on walls.

Calling, maybe, the way the margins said they would.

Because there was one more note, the last one, crammed into the bottom corner of the final page in handwriting gone thin and hurried, and I read it twice before I could make myself say it aloud:

*They know their own. The caged storms remember every child of the planted lines. When the Halloran girl walks the spirit roads, they will know her. They will call her daughter.*

"Kess," I said, closing the ledger with hands that finally, finally had stopped shaking, because terror and purpose feel identical in the fingers. "We need to be at that grave by tomorrow night. And after that—" I swallowed. "After that, I have to go underground. Literally. There are roads under these mountains that aren't on any smuggler's map, and something down there has been waiting a hundred years to meet me."

He stared at me. "Meet you."

"Yeah." I stood, and my knees held, and I picked up the ledger like it weighed nothing, like it wasn't the heaviest thing I'd ever carried. "Turns out I'm somebody's daughter. I just don't know yet whether I'm going to thank them—or bury them deeper."
