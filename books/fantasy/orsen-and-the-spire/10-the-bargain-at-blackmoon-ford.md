## 10. The Bargain at Blackmoon Ford

The thing nobody tells you about surrendering is how much paperwork your pride generates on the way down.

I'd spent seven years learning to be small. Small footsteps, small voice, small life folded up neat as contraband in a false-bottomed crate. And now I was going to walk out onto a riverbank and make myself enormous again—big enough for an inquisitor to see from across black water—because Kess was kneeling in the mud with a collar of his own around his throat and Corrane's mages were counting down to something I didn't want to learn the shape of.

I crouched in the reeds above Blackmoon Ford and did the arithmetic one more time, as if the numbers might have changed while I wasn't bullying them.

One partner. One prophecy. One dark moon, four nights out. One binding collar in Corrane's baggage train, iron and silver and old enough to have opinions.

The math only worked one way. It had only ever worked one way, from the moment I'd read my own name in ink older than grief. The book said I would destroy or save the Spire. It never once mentioned that I might do it by *walking in the front door*.

*You're lying to yourself if you think this is a plan,* said the sensible voice in my head, which sounded suspiciously like Kess and had been getting louder all week.

"It's not a plan," I told the reeds. "It's a bargain. Plans survive contact with the enemy. Bargains just have to survive long enough to matter."

The reeds declined to argue. Smart vegetation.

* * *

Blackmoon Ford earned its name honestly. The water ran shallow and quick over pale stone, and when the clouds thinned—which they did tonight, because the sky itself seemed to be holding its breath before the dark moon—the river caught the light and threw it back wrong, silver-black, like a blade laid across the valley's throat.

Corrane had chosen it well. Open ground on both banks. His mages spaced along the ridge with the bored patience of people who had done this a hundred times and enjoyed it maybe twice. Torches burning low and blue—wardfire, the kind that ate wild magic the way moths eat wool.

And in the middle of it all, on his knees in the shallows with two ward-spears crossed behind his neck, was Kess.

They'd made him kneel in the water. Of course they had. Everything about the arrangement was designed to be photographed by memory and replayed forever: the great storm-caller, come to heel; her loyal smuggler, soaked and shivering and *alive*, which was the entire point of the tableau.

He looked terrible. A split lip, a eye going purple, three days of captivity written into the set of his shoulders. But when he lifted his head and saw me step out of the reeds, the first thing that crossed his face wasn't relief.

It was fury.

"You absolute—" he started, and the ward-spears pressed him quiet.

"Good to see you too," I called across the water. "You've been eating well."

"Jude." His voice cracked on the second syllable, and I hated how much I loved hearing it, and hated more what I was about to do with it. "Don't. Whatever you're thinking—don't."

"You know me," I said. "I never think."

Vael Corrane stood at the far bank with his ledger under one arm and his hands folded, patient as winter. Up close—and we were close now, twenty feet of black water between us—he was younger than his reputation. Grey at the temples, though, early, like the job had taxed him in installments. He watched me cross the ford with the expression of a man reading a document he'd already memorized, checking for alterations.

"Halloran," he said. "Jude Halloran. Born under the black moon in the year of the Sundered Bell. You'll forgive the recitation. I find names are load-bearing."

"They're also heavy," I said. "Careful where you set them down."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite amusement. Inquisitors probably weren't issued amusement; it would clash with the robes.

"You came," he said. "I confess I gave it even odds you'd run for the coast."

"I considered it. Then I remembered I don't like boats."

"And your partner?"

"He grows on you," I said. "Like mold."

Kess made a noise that in a healthier context would have been a laugh.

Corrane gestured, and one of his mages brought forward a long case, and opened it, and there it was: the binding collar, coiled on velvet like something sleeping. Iron bands, silver script, and along the inner curve a hairline seam of pale stone that I recognized with a lurch I kept off my face.

Stormglass. They'd built the cage out of the same material the Spire used to hold its prisoners. Which meant someone in that tower had told Corrane exactly what I was—or thought I was—and exactly what would hold me.

Good. Information flowed both directions, if you knew how to bleed it.

"The terms," Corrane said, "are simple. You wear this. You come with us to the Spire. You submit to assessment before the dark moon rite. In exchange, Halloran the smuggler is pardoned—all of it, every crate, every forged seal, every bribe. Your partner walks free with a full amnesty and no memory-tax." Memory-tax: the polite word for what the Spire did to witnesses. "And no one else in this valley burns for knowing you."

"No one else," I repeated. "That's a short list that keeps growing. Who's on it today?"

"A fence named Odile Voss. A ferryman. Two brothers who run the eastern passes." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Cruelty you can hate. Sincerity you just have to survive. "I am not threatening you, Halloran. I am describing consequences. There's a difference, and I've built a career on it."

"I can see why they gave you the nice ledger."

He ignored that, which confirmed he'd been briefed on me thoroughly. Somewhere in the Spire there was a file with my whole life in it, and I was suddenly, coldly certain that file was older than my life.

"Kneel," Corrane said. "We do this properly, or we don't do it at all."

* * *

Here is the trick from my exile years, the one they never taught in any trial chamber because I invented it afterward, alone, in a mountain pass, out of spite:

A binding collar doesn't grip magic. It grips the *shape* of magic—the signature, the pattern, the way a lock doesn't hold a door but holds the idea of a door. For seven years I hadn't suppressed my storms. I'd done something worse, something lonelier: I'd taught myself to fold them flat, to press the whole weather of me down into a shape so small and so dull that even I sometimes forgot it was there.

Everyone assumed exile had burned me out. Let them. A folded storm reads as nothing at all.

But folding isn't the trick. The trick is the crease. When you fold a thing the same way for seven years, the crease becomes part of it—a seam, a fault line, a place where the shape remembers being otherwise. And a collar fitted to a flat, dead signature will sit against that seam like a key cut for the wrong lock, resting in the mechanism, turning nothing.

Until I chose to unfold.

So I knelt in the black water beside Kess, and I let Corrane's mage lift the collar from its velvet, and I kept my storm folded so tight it ached, and I looked at the seam of stormglass and thought, *you're going to fit me like a rumor fits the truth. Close enough to pass. Wrong enough to break.*

The collar closed around my throat with a sound like a book snapping shut.

Cold. Heavy. The silver script warmed immediately, hungry, tasting for lightning and finding—nothing. Flat water. Dead air. A tired smuggler with bad knees and a smuggling record.

The mage stepped back, frowning slightly, and adjusted a dial on his gauntlet, and frowned again.

"She reads clean," he said, puzzled. "Barely a whisper. Are we certain—"

"Certain," Corrane said, and his eyes never left mine, and I understood that he wasn't uncertain at all. He'd expected this. Either I was broken, or I was hiding, and either way the collar would tell him which eventually—or so he believed. "Exile does what exile does. That's rather the tragedy of her, isn't it? The weapon that rusted in its sheath."

*Keep believing that,* I thought, smiling at him with everything I had, which was a smile and a lie and a storm folded flat as paper. *Rust away, inquisitor.*

"Jude—" Kess started.

"Up," I said to him, gently. "You heard the man. Amnesty. Go home, Kess. Sleep somewhere dry. Water your ridiculous plants."

He didn't move. The ward-spears withdrew and he still didn't move, kneeling in the shallows, staring at me like I was a page he couldn't finish reading.

"You're coming back," he said. Not a question. Worse than a question—an instruction, delivered by a man with no authority whatsoever and total moral leverage.

"I'm going to the Spire," I said. "Whether those are the same thing depends on how the week goes."

"Jude."

"Go *home*," I said, sharper than I meant, sharp enough to cut through whatever he was building in his chest. Because if he argued, I'd waver, and if I wavered, Corrane would notice, and if Corrane noticed, the whole architecture of the lie would come down on both our heads. So I gave him the coldest thing I owned. "Seven years, Kess. You never asked what I was running from. Don't start asking now, when asking gets people memory-taxed."

He flinched. I felt it land. I'd aimed it.

Then he stood, slow, dripping, purple-eyed, and looked at me a long moment, and said—quietly, just for me, though the whole riverbank heard it:

"Whatever you're doing, do it *alive*. That's not a request. That's a debt. You owe me one alive Jude Halloran, and I collect."

"Add it to my tab," I said, and turned away before my face could testify against me.

* * *

They marched me east through the night, toward the Spire, toward the dark moon. Corrane rode beside my tether—not dragging me, walking his horse at my pace, which was its own kind of message—and after an hour of silence he said, without preamble:

"You lied to him."

"Which him?"

"Your partner. The pardon is real. The freedom is real. What you told him—that you expect to come back, that this is survivable—you don't believe it. I've sat across from a hundred condemned people, Halloran. I know the sound of a farewell wearing a joke's clothing."

The collar sat cold at my throat, fitting my folded silence perfectly, and I thought about the seam it rested against, and I thought about Kess standing in the shallows collecting a debt I fully intended to pay in the only currency I had left.

"Maybe," I said. "Or maybe you know the sound of a woman who's decided to be very hard to kill."

"That too," Corrane admitted. "It's why I offered terms instead of a pyre. The Spire doesn't want you dead, Halloran. It wants you *assessed*. There's a difference, and I've built a career on it."

"There it is again," I said. "That word. Assessed. Everyone at the Spire uses it like it means something gentle."

Ahead of us, past the black shoulders of Thornfell, the Spire's distant beacon burned against the coming dark moon—a white point of light that had watched over my exile like a scar that never faded. Four nights. The rite. The vessel they thought they'd bred and caught and collared.

I touched the collar's edge, casual as adjusting a scarf, and felt beneath the cold iron the faintest give—the seam, the crease, the folded weather waiting for permission.

Four nights to learn what the Spire kept in its basement. Four nights to decide whether the prophecy got to write my ending, or whether I did.

"Walk faster," Corrane said.

"Walk slower," I said, and smiled into the dark, where no one could see how much it cost me.
