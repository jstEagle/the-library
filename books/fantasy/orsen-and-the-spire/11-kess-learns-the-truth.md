## 11. Kess Learns the Truth

The rope they'd tied my wrists with was good work. I mean that as a compliment. Somebody at Blackmoon Ford had learned knots from a sailor, or a hangman, and there's a family resemblance between those trades I've never been able to shake.

I worked the knot for two hours before it gave, lying on my side in the mud of the riverbank where they'd left me, watching the ford through a screen of reeds until the Spire riders were small enough to be dots and then not even that. Jude went with them walking. That was the part that kept snagging in my chest like a fishhook. Not dragged, not bound—at least not visibly—walking beside Vael Corrane with her chin up like she was boarding a ferry she'd paid for in advance.

Like she'd expected this. Like maybe she'd *arranged* it.

I got my hands free, which took another hour because whoever tied them also believed in backup knots, and then I sat in the mud and did an accounting, because that's what you do when your partner sells herself to the people who've been hunting us for a week. You do an accounting.

One mule, gone. Taken by Corrane's people when they took me. One satchel of contraband—two vials of whisper-sap, a bolt of shadowed silk, six tins of pipeleaf worth more than my life—which was still sitting under the alder where I'd cached it, so the gods were either kind or distracted. One knife, still mine. One partner, traded away for mine.

That last one didn't balance. That was the whole problem. Nothing about Jude Halloran balanced anymore, and had it been balancing for years without me noticing?

* * *

I made camp that night in one of our old spots—a hollow behind a fallen fir off the shepherd's track, big enough for two, which is a cruel size for a campsite when you're one person. I built a fire small enough to hide and sat with my palms out and tried not to think, which has never once worked.

Seven years I'd run passes with her. Seven years of her checking sightlines twice and sleeping facing the door, and I'd filed all of it under *smuggler habits*, because smugglers are careful and paranoid and don't talk about their families, and Jude was the best of us at all three. But there were things I'd filed wrong and I knew it now, knew it the way you know you've been carrying a stone in your boot only after you finally sit down.

She flinched at thunder. Did you know that? I did, somewhere underneath. Thunder would roll down the valley and she'd go still—not scared-still, *listening*-still, like a dog hearing its name from the wrong mouth—and I'd make a joke about the weather and she'd laugh and we'd move on, and I had let us move on for seven straight years.

And she hadn't sung in the rain. Nobody sings in the rain out here, fine, but there was a night in my third year with her—we were soaked through on the Kestrel Stair, lightning walking the ridge like a man pacing a cell—and I'd whooped at it, drunk on altitude and bad brandy, arms out, daring the sky to hit me. And Jude had looked at me with an expression I'd spent years failing to identify and could name now, sitting alone by a fire too small, seven years late: it was grief. Pure, homesick grief. Like I was dancing in front of a locked door.

I'd thought: *odd*. And then I'd thought nothing, because thinking nothing is a skill and I'm very good at it.

The fence in Coldbrook had covered for me three days ago—old Maren, bless her mercenary heart, told Corrane's advance man that the Halloran woman was "a solid buyer, no history," and lied with the flat calm of a woman who charges extra for perjury. She hadn't asked why I needed the lie. She'd just looked at me over her spectacles and said, "Whatever she is, you're going after her, aren't you," and I said, "Obviously," and she said, "Then take the east road, fool, they'll expect the west," and charged me double for the information anyway. Fair.

But Maren's question had cracked something. *Whatever she is.* Because I didn't know what she was. I knew what she liked in her tea and how she cheated at cards and the exact sound she made when something actually surprised her, which was rare enough to be collectible. I knew she was the finest judge of weather I'd ever met, better than any almanac, better than any storm-glass, and I had thought: *mountain sense.* I had thought: *she grew up somewhere high and hard.*

I had thought a great many convenient things.

* * *

Her pack was gone with her, of course. They'd have taken everything—Collar and all, whatever a binding collar looked like; I'd heard Corrane use the word and watched Jude's face do something complicated and terrible. But we had a system, born of a profession where packs get confiscated. Anything you can't afford to lose, you cache. And anything you want found, you leave where only your partner looks.

So in the morning I went back along the ford, upstream, to the split boulder shaped like a broken tooth, and reached into the crack behind it where we'd stashed emergency coin since my second year running with her.

No coin. A book.

It was a fat, ugly thing, bound in cracked leather the color of dried liver, swollen with damp, older than anything else we owned including Maren. Someone had written on the flyleaf in faded brown ink: *Almanac and Prognostication, Being a True Account of Things to Come. Anno Domini—* and then a year I had to count twice, because it put the book a century in the ground.

"A book," I said out loud, to the river, because saying things out loud keeps a man sane and I was running low. "She left me a *book*."

Jude hated books. Not reading—she read like breathing, always had some dog-eared thing in her kit—but books as cargo, books as objects. "Dead weight that bites you," she called them. "Every book is a debt. Somebody wrote it down because they wanted it remembered, and remembering is expensive." I'd laughed. I should have asked her how she knew.

I sat on the cold stone with the river talking to itself below me and opened the cover, and I will tell you honestly that part of me hoped for smuggling codes. Cipher pages, route maps, the names of fences. Something that would let me keep believing she was exactly who I'd decided she was, plus one secret, plus one lie, plus one walk into captivity that she'd explain later with that crooked shrug she did.

Instead I found tonight's date.

Not tonight's—the date of the ford, of the trade, of the dark moon coming. Written a hundred years ago in ink that had turned to rust. And next to the date, in the same hand:

*The Halloran girl comes to the water. The bargain is struck: herself for her heart's own. The collar holds her for a day and no more, for she learned its teeth in the years of her exile.*

I read it four times. My hands weren't steady, which annoyed me, because hands are tools and tools should behave.

*The Halloran girl.* Her name. In a book older than her grandmother, if she'd had a grandmother, which was among the many things I did not know about her.

I turned pages with the care you give a snare. The hand changed sometimes—different nibs, different decades, someone annotating someone—but the spine of it was one voice, a voice that wrote like a man arguing with God and losing on points. Prophecies, mostly. Dates and portents. And threaded through all of it, recurring like a fever, a girl. Born under a black moon. Marked by storms. *The Halloran girl*, again and again, across a hundred years of dead ink, as if the author had known her personally. As if he'd been waiting for her the way you wait for weather.

*Two roads open to her,* said the page nearest today, and beneath it two columns, written out neat as a merchant's ledger.

In the first column: *If she breaks the cages, the realm stands bare. The wards fail. The long war resumes, and this time nothing above the ground survives it.*

In the second: *If the cages hold, the Spire endures, fed and fat, and the Halloran girl ends inside one.*

I closed the book.

Here is the thing about being lied to well: the anger doesn't come all at once. It comes in installments. First came the cold arithmetic—she'd known, she'd known about the collar, she'd walked into it *on schedule*, according to a date some dead man had set before either of us drew breath. Then came the inventory of every conversation I replayed with new ears. Every time I'd asked about her past and she'd given me a true-shaped nothing. Every time I'd said *you can tell me anything* and she'd smiled and said *I know* and told me nothing, and I'd accepted it, because accepting it was comfortable, because her mystery was part of the package, because I had loved the lock more than I'd wanted the door open.

Then came the anger proper. It arrived while I was building up the fire, and it was enormous, and it wanted things. It wanted me to throw the book in the flames. It wanted me to walk west to Coldbrook, drink myself stupid, find new routes, forget her name. It stood there in my chest with its fists up and shouted that I had been a *prop*—seven years of loyalty spent keeping alive a stranger, a weapon somebody else had forged and aimed, and nobody had even had the decency to let me be in on my own usefulness.

I let it shout. I've found anger is like weather: if you stand in it without arguing, it moves on.

And under the shouting, quieter, was the other thing. The thing I kept circling back to like a tongue to a broken tooth.

*Herself for her heart's own.*

Me. I was the price she'd paid herself to cancel. Whatever else was in that book, whatever she'd been hiding since before I met her, the last thing she'd done with her freedom was spend it buying mine. You can be furious at someone and still know exactly what they are. I knew what she was. I'd known for years. I'd just been calling it the wrong word.

* * *

I read the rest of it by firelight, all night, and I won't pretend I understood half. There were pages about the Spire that made my skin crawl—passages about *the caged choir* and *the engines of the high tower drinking the sky*—and passages about Thornfell that read like directions, real ones, marked with symbols I recognized from waymarkers on trails I'd run for years without asking who'd cut them. Trails leading down. Under the mountains.

There was a map folded into the back binding, drawn on paper so old it crackled, showing the passes I knew and then continuing past them, into the rock, along roads with names like *the Weeping Gallery* and *the Long Throat*. Roads that shouldn't exist. Roads somebody had clearly walked.

And in the margin beside the final entry—the entry about the ford, about her, about me—in handwriting fresher than the rest, newer, hers, Jude had written three words. Small. Pressed hard enough to score the paper.

*I'm sorry. Don't.*

Don't follow her. That was the message. That was the whole testament of seven years, boiled down: an apology and a request, and the request was that I let her go be a ledger entry in some century-old madman's arithmetic.

Well.

I fed the fire another branch and thought about it properly, the way you think about a pass in winter: not whether you want to cross, but whether crossing kills you, and if so, whether dying there is worse than living with not having crossed.

Here was what I had. A book full of a dead man's certainty, which might be prophecy or might be the world's longest suicide note. A map to roads under a mountain range that ate experienced climbers for breakfast. An enemy with towers and collars and a head start. No partner. No plan. And one piece of contraband knowledge that Corrane and his whole shining order didn't have, which was that Jude had left me this book *deliberately*, which meant she wanted me to understand—even as she ordered me not to come. Which meant part of her was hoping I'd disobey. You don't leave the map for a man you truly want to stay home. You leave him a letter. She left me a *route*.

Or maybe that was just what I needed to believe. I'm aware of the possibility. I've run contraband through mountains for fifteen years; I know exactly how good I am at believing what profits me.

But here's the other thing, the thing I kept arriving at no matter which direction I argued from: the second column. *If the cages hold, the Halloran girl ends inside one.* I didn't know what the cages were yet—I had guesses, and every guess was worse than the last—but I knew the shape of the sentence. It was the shape of every deal the powerful ever offer. It ended with her in a box and them holding the key, and everyone agreeing afterward that it had been necessary.

Nobody was coming for her. The Spire didn't rescue its own instruments; it shelved them. The prophecy certainly wasn't coming—it had already written both endings and gone quiet, smug as scripture. If anyone was going to stand between Jude Halloran and that second column, it was going to be a smuggler with one knife, a stolen education in ward-marks from a dead man's margins, and a professional relationship with other people's property.

She'd said *don't*.

I banked the fire. I shouldered the pack, and the book rode against my spine like it belonged there, heavy as a debt, warm from the fire.

"Don't," I said to the dark, to the river, to a hundred years of dead certainty and one live woman walking in chains toward a tower that drank the sky. "Yeah. You know I've never once taken delivery instructions from you."

East, then. Maren's road, past the places where the waymarkers carried symbols I now knew the meaning of. Down, eventually, if the map told true. Toward the Spire, which was a sentence I'd never expected to volunteer for, and I laughed into the dark, alone on a mountain with a haunted book, and the laugh came out sounding more like resolve than madness, which I chose to take as a good sign.

Behind me, far to the north, thunder rolled down out of clear stars.

There was no cloud anywhere near it.

I stood very still in the dark and listened to it go, and for the first time in seven years I understood what I was hearing. Not weather. A voice. Saying something over and over in the only language it had left.

It sounded like my name.

I started walking.
