## 12. The Spirit Roads

The map was drawn on the back of a funeral shroud.

I want you to sit with that for a moment, because I certainly did. I'd spent the better part of a day in the prophet's tomb at Hollowmere, reading marginalia by candlelight and having my entire life reframed as somebody else's long game, and when I finally pried loose the stone beneath her sarcophagus—because of course there was a stone beneath the sarcophagus, prophecy authors never make anything easy—what did I find? A folded length of linen, soft with age, inked in iron-gall brown: a map of the Thornfells, except the Thornfells were drawn the way an anatomist draws a body, all skin peeled back to show what's underneath.

Underneath the mountains, it turned out, were roads.

Not mine roads. Not smuggler roads, which I know like I know the backs of my own hands, which is to say intimately and with some regret about the scars. These roads went *down*. They threaded through the roots of Thornfell in great looping arteries, converging somewhere beneath the Spire itself like veins feeding a heart. And along every road, in the prophet's cramped hand, ran the same annotation, repeated like a prayer or a warning:

*They will know your name. Let them say it.*

I'd been telling myself for two days that I had a choice. That I could fold the shroud up, climb out of Hollowmere, walk south to the coast, buy a boat under some name that wasn't Halloran, and spend the rest of my life being nobody in particular, which is a career I'd held for seven years and had gotten reasonably good at. But the truth—the truth I'd been carrying since the night I read my own name in a century-old book—was that the choice had been made for me before I was born. The only question left was whether I'd walk into my cage on my own feet or be dragged.

So. Onward and downward. Into the dark, where things older than grief knew my name.

Gods help me, part of me was *flattered*.

* * *

The entrance was a smuggler's cache, because of course it was. Everything in Thornfell eventually becomes a smuggler's cache; we're like lichen that way. Old Marta's Hole, we called it—a cave behind a waterfall three days' hard climb from Hollowmere, known to maybe six people alive, four of whom I'd trust with contraband and none of whom I'd trust with this.

The waterfall was running thin with autumn. I stood in its cold spray and let it soak me through, partly to wash off Hollowmere's dust and partly because I kept hoping water would feel like water again instead of like a reminder. Seven years I hadn't called a storm. Then one ambush, one bolt of lightning out of a clear sky, one Kess nearly dying—and now the sky followed me around like a dog that remembered who used to feed it. Clouds gathered when I was angry. The wind turned to look at me when I passed. I'd caught myself, twice, talking to weather, and worse, the weather seemed to be listening.

Kess would have said something dry about that. Kess wasn't here, because I'd left him bleeding and furious at Blackmoon Ford with a bag of coin, a lie about meeting again in Vell's Crossing, and the prophecy book, which was the closest thing to a confession I could manage without actually saying the words. He'd find my name in it. He'd understand—or he wouldn't, and he'd hate me, and either way he'd be alive, which was the entire point.

Don't think about him. Down is that way.

Inside Marta's Hole, past the old cache niches with their rotted crate-wood, the cave narrowed to a crawl and then opened without warning into a space that no smuggler had ever found, because no smuggler had ever thought to look for it: a shaft, circular, descending beyond the reach of my lantern light, its walls carved—not eroded, *carved*—with spirals of script in a language I didn't know and yet somehow could almost read, the way you can almost remember a dream.

The prophet's map called this the First Door. It also noted, in the margin, with what I swear looked like grim humor:

*Bring a light you don't love.*

I looked at my lantern. I'd had it six years. It had been through two floods, one avalanche, and the worst winter of my life, and I loved it the way you love anything that has kept you company in the dark.

"Fine," I told it. "Nothing personal."

I set it down at the shaft's lip, took a deep breath, and stepped backward into the dark.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about falling: your body has opinions. My body's opinion was a scream it politely declined to voice, and then the fall stopped—not with impact but with a sensation like stepping from one room into another, a pressure change, a *turning*, and my boots met stone as gently as if the road had reached up and caught me.

I stood very still for a while, doing inventory. Two arms. Two legs. Heart going like a smuggler hearing patrol whistles. All present.

Then I raised my hand and light came.

Not from a lantern—I'd left it topside, as instructed. Light simply arrived, a pale blue-white glow rising off my own skin like heat shimmer, pooling in the hollows of my knuckles, tracing the lines of my palms. Storm-light. I knew it on sight; I'd been born full of it, before the Spire taught me to call it heresy and beat it down to embers. Except now the embers were waking, and they weren't waiting for my permission anymore.

"Well," I said, and my voice came back to me strange, doubled, as if the road itself were repeating it a half-beat late. "That's new."

The spirit road stretched away in both directions, a gallery of black stone wide enough for ten carts, ribbed like the inside of a shell. The spiral script covered everything, floor to vaulted ceiling, and now that I was inside it, now that my skin was shedding light like a lamp, I could read it. Not translate it—there was no translating happening anywhere in my head—but *read* it, the way you read music, the way you read weather. The words were names. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. And they were not written in praise or record.

They were written as bindings. The whole road was a sentence, and the sentence was a cage, and the cage ran beneath the entire mountain range, all the way to the Spire.

I started walking. What else do you do?

* * *

The whispering began an hour in. Or what felt like an hour; time down here moved like syrup, and my body's clock was already filing complaints about the absence of sky.

At first I thought it was the script—that the walls themselves murmured, dead letters stirring as I passed. Then I realized the sound was coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the spaces between the stones, a sound like wind through a keyhole, like breath drawn to speak and held. And threaded through it, over and over, in voices that ranged from a child's murmur to something vast enough to shake grit from the ceiling:

*Jude. Jude Halloran. Jude.*

My name. Not the name I'd worn for seven years—Jen Marsh, freight-handler, unremarkable, forgettable. My real name, the one the Spire had struck from their rolls and the prophecy had written in ink older than grief. They said it softly. They said it the way you say the name of someone you've been waiting for.

I should have run. Any sensible person would have run. But I've never claimed to be sensible, only careful, and careful is a different thing entirely—careful means you assess the threat before deciding which way to jump. So I stopped, in the middle of that endless black gallery, lit by my own skin, and I said to the darkness:

"You know me."

The whispering surged, delighted, a tide coming in. *We know you. We have always known you. We sang to your mother's mother's mother, and she heard us, and the Spire cut her tongue for it.* A pause, a ripple, something like laughter if laughter could bend stone. *You hear us too. You have always heard us. That is why it hurt so much, little storm. That is why the silence nearly killed you.*

Seven years of silence. Seven years of holding my magic down so hard it had become a chronic ache, a toothache in the soul, and I'd assumed that was exile's price, the cost of hiding. But they were telling me it was simpler and crueler than that: a caged thing recognizes a caged thing. I hadn't been suppressing my power all these years. I'd been commiserating.

"I'm not little," I said, mostly to have something to say. "And I'm not a storm. I'm a person. There's a difference."

*Is there?* the dark asked, gentle as snowfall. *Come and see. Come and see what they made you from.*

*Made* me from. I didn't like the sound of that. I liked it even less when the road began to change—the script brightening as I walked, the bindings thinning, the cage-sentence fraying like rope rubbed against stone, until the walls fell away altogether and I stepped out onto—

A shore.

There is no honest way to describe it, so I'll describe it dishonestly, the way dreams deserve: a shore of black glass beneath a sky that was not a sky but a *storm*, an entire horizon-to-horizon wall of cloud and lightning, turning slowly, contained by nothing visible and yet contained, pressed flat against the underside of the world. The sea—if it was a sea—moved without water, a slow rolling of silver light, and along the shore stood shapes.

They were tall. They were made of weather. Some flickered like heat-shimmer, some rolled like fog given intent, one was simply a column of rain falling upward, and all of them, every one, turned toward me as I came down the last stretch of road.

Every instinct I owned said: these are the prisoners. These are what the Spire feeds on. These are the storms they caged a thousand years ago to power their wards, and you are standing in their prison yard, and you are made of the same thing they are.

*Daughter,* they said. All of them at once, and the word came through my bones rather than my ears. *Little daughter of the Halloran line. Little sister of the broken sky. You came. You finally came.*

"I came," I said, "because a dead woman wrote me into a book a hundred years ago and I'd like to have a word with her estate about it." My voice shook. I let it. There didn't seem to be any point pretending down here. "I'm told you can tell me what I am. I'm told you know what the Spire did. So tell me. And while you're at it—" I lifted my glowing hands, and hated how small they looked, "—tell me why everyone keeps calling me *daughter* when as far as I know I had a perfectly ordinary mother who was terrified of thunder."

The nearest shape drifted closer. Up close it resolved into something almost like a face, if faces were made of moving cloud and two points of white fire where eyes might go. When it spoke, it spoke alone, and its voice was the quietest and oldest of all of them.

*Your mother dreamed of us, Jude Halloran. As her mother did, and hers, back to the first woman who stood on a hilltop and answered when we called. Your line is our door. That is what the Spire discovered, and what they feared, and what they decided to farm.*

"Farm," I repeated.

*You were not exiled, daughter. You were planted. Every cruelty they showed you, every year of silence—they were pruning. Shaping. Waiting for the season when the fruit would ripen and walk willingly into their cellar.* The storm-sky above us flickered, and every shape on the shore leaned in, and the whole prison-world seemed to hold its breath. *The dark moon comes in nine days. They mean to open their oldest cage and pour a new spirit into it. They mean for that vessel to be you.*

Nine days. Corrane's deadline. The date in the prophecy, circled twice.

I laughed. I couldn't help it—it came out of me cracked and wild, echoing down the black gallery behind me, and the storm-shapes rippled at the sound like a field of grass in wind. Because of course. Of course. Even my rebellion was on the schedule. Even my fury had been forecast a century in advance.

"Then here's what your prophecy-writing friend didn't put in the margins," I said to all of them, to the turning storm-sky, to the whole buried architecture of my life. "I've spent seven years being nobody, and I got *good* at it. Nobody walks where they aren't expected. Nobody opens the doors they're supposed to open. If the Spire wants me in their cellar on the dark moon—"

The light under my skin flared, and far above, through a thousand feet of mountain, I felt thunder answer.

"—then the dark moon is exactly where I'll be. Just not the way they wrote it."

The shapes on the shore made a sound I will carry to whatever grave I get, which may be nowhere at all: a sound like a storm clearing, like pressure breaking, like ten thousand years of held breath finally released. And the eldest of them extended what might have been a hand, made of rain and white fire, and said:

*Then take our road to the end, daughter. There is one more thing you must see. The grave is not empty. She left you a letter—and a choice the book never dared to write.*

I looked at that hand for a long moment. Then, because my whole life had apparently been authored by other people and I was done being careful about whose fingers I took:

I reached out, and touched the storm.

It didn't hurt. That was the uncanny part. It felt like home—which, given everything I'd just learned about home, should probably have frightened me more than pain ever could.

Together, we walked deeper.
