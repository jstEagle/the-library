## 13. The Author's Grave

The spirit roads spat me out at dawn, if you could call it dawn. Down there beneath Thornfell, light came from somewhere and went to somewhere else, and it never once asked my opinion. I climbed out of a fissure in the mountainside that no map had ever marked, into a valley so narrow the sun only visited it for an hour a day, like a relative who'd rather be anywhere else.

I knew where I was before I knew how I knew it. That was becoming a theme.

Hollowmere's dead monastery sat at the head of the valley, half-swallowed by the mountain it had been built against, as if the rock had decided after four hundred years that it wanted its stone back. The prophet's map—the one inked into the margins of the almanac, the one the storm-spirits had whispered me toward—ended here. The last line of it, in that cramped antique hand, read simply: *She will know the door.*

I did not know the door. I want that on the record. I stood in the cold with my hands jammed in my coat pockets and looked at a ruin full of rubble and bird droppings and thought, *she will know the door, my ass.* But then the wind came down the valley—wind with a shape to it, wind with intent—and it pushed past me up the slope, impatient, the way Kess pushes through a market crowd when he's spotted a buyer he doesn't trust. The spirits didn't speak in words anymore. They'd stopped needing to. They just leaned.

So I followed the wind up through the ruins, past a collapsed cloister where moss had eaten the carvings, past a well so deep that dropping a pebble felt rude, until I came to a low door set into the mountainside itself. Not monastery work. Older. The stone around it was fitted without mortar, tight as a drystone waller's pride, and carved across the lintel was a spiral that I recognized from the almanac's endpapers, from the margins, from the inside of my own eyelids lately.

There was no lock. There was no need for one. The door opened when I touched it, smooth and silent on hinges that should have screamed after centuries, and cold air breathed out at me smelling of stone and old paper and, faintly, impossibly, of rain on hot summer cobbles—a smell from a childhood I'd spent trying not to remember.

"Right," I said to nobody. "Let's meet the family."

* * *

Inside was a tomb, but not the kind people build for the dead they loved. It was the kind they build for the dead they fear.

The chamber ran back into the mountain farther than my eyes could follow, and every surface was covered in writing. Walls, floor, ceiling—ceiling, twenty feet up, which meant somebody had built scaffolding and been very committed—covered in the same cramped hand I'd been reading for weeks. Not copied text. Original. Drafts. Corrections. Whole passages struck through and rewritten, arguments with herself carried on across decades in ink that shifted color as the formulas changed. It wasn't a grave. It was a desk the size of a cathedral.

And in the center, on a plinth of the same seamless stone, lay a sarcophagus with a lid of cloudy glass, and under the glass lay a woman.

She'd been dead a long time. Long enough that she was less a body than an argument her bones were still having with her shroud. But her hands were visible above the wrapping, folded over a book, and even dried to leather those hands looked like mine. Same knuckles. Same old break in the left ring finger, badly set, the kind you get falling out of a tree at nine years old because your cousin dared you.

I have that same finger. My cousin Dara dared me off the Hendricks' wall when I was nine.

I stood there for what felt like a long time doing absolutely nothing, which is a skill I've cultivated, and then I made myself look at the book in her hands instead of her hands, because one of those things I could survive.

It was the almanac. Or rather—it was the almanac the way a mother is the source of a daughter. Thicker. Heavier. Its pages swollen with inserts and errata slips, its spine re-sewn at least twice. This was the master copy. Everything else, including the volume a dying hoarder in Thornfell had paid me with, was a child of this book.

I didn't take it. Some instinct older than sense told me not to touch it yet. Instead I walked the walls, reading, and the woman who wrote it introduced herself the way she apparently introduced herself to everyone: exhaustively, defensively, and in handwriting that got angrier the longer she went.

Her name was Sera Halloran.

*Halloran,* I thought, and laughed, one short ugly bark that echoed off the ceiling. Of course. Of course it was. The prophecy hadn't found me by accident. It hadn't found me by fate either, whatever the storm-spirits liked to whisper. It had found me the way a debt finds a debtor: down the family line, generation to generation, passed along like a cursed ring or a bad knee.

I read. I'm good at reading; it's most of what exile leaves you. And Sera Halloran, dead four hundred years, talked to me all morning.

* * *

Here is what the walls said, stripped of her digressions, her grudges against three separate Archmages, and a truly epic forty-page feud with a rival cartographer:

Sera Halloran had been a storm-caller. One of the last. She had lived in the years after the Sundering Wars, when the Spire was young and hungry and building itself a power supply, and she had discovered what they were doing—not by prophecy, not by gift, but by the deeply unglamorous labor of noticing things. Storms going missing. Weather arriving on schedule like a tax collector. Valleys where the lightning bent wrong.

She had gone looking. She had found the cages.

And then—and this was the part I had to read three times, running my fingers along the letters like I could check them for forgery—she had done nothing.

Not nothing, exactly. Worse than nothing. She had written.

*I could not free them,* the wall said, in ink gone brown as old blood. *I tried. I was young and strong and I called a storm such as has not been seen since, and the wards drank it and grew fat on it, and I understood then that my strength was their strength, that everything I was had been measured and budgeted and entered into their ledger before I ever drew breath. So I did the only thing left to a woman whose enemy owns the future.*

*I wrote a different future.*

I put my hand flat against the wall. The stone was cold enough to burn.

Because here was the thing I'd been circling since Thornfell, the thing I'd kept sliding away from like a drunk avoiding his landlord: prophecies don't come from nowhere. Somebody writes them. And Sera Halloran had written hers knowing exactly what the Spire's readers would do with it—knowing that inquisitors collect prophecy the way magpies collect silver, that they would hunt the Halloran girl, that they would breed and prune and exile and engineer, generation after generation, following her script like sheep following a fence line.

She hadn't predicted me. She'd *built* me. Four hundred years of careful, furious, loving engineering, all of it aimed down the bloodline at a girl who wouldn't be born until the Spire's wards were old and brittle and ready to crack. Every exile, every trial, every branded heretic in my family's history—each one a chisel stroke. My grandmother's silence. My mother's flight. My own seven years of hiding, seven years of never calling a storm, seven years of being small and careful and invisible.

All of it authored. All of it *planned*, by a dead woman with my knuckles, who had decided that her descendants' lives were acceptable currency for one shot at the cages.

And the two endings—the two divergent futures I'd found in the Thornfell copy, destroy or save, the choice that had been sitting on my chest like a stone for weeks? I found them again here, on the far wall, in her final hand, and I finally saw what I'd been too sick with fear to see before.

They weren't two endings. They were two doors of the same cage.

*If she frees them unbound, the storms will raven. The wards fail, the realm bleeds, and the girl dies a destroyer, and the Spire rebuilds its prisons on the ashes and calls it necessity for another thousand years.* That was ending one. Ending two: *if she binds herself to the order, gives her storm to the Archmage, the realm keeps its wards and the cages keep their tenants and the girl dies a savior, honored, gutted, used.*

Save or destroy. Submit or shatter. Those were the options. Those had always been the options. A coin with the same face on both sides, and I'd been flipping it in my head for a month thinking I was making a choice.

Every choice in the prophecy was a cage of another kind. The prophecy itself was the biggest cage of all. Sera Halloran hadn't freed her descendants. She'd built us a maze with two exits, and both exits led where *she* wanted, and she'd had four hundred years to make sure we'd never notice the walls.

I broke something. I'm not proud of it. It was a shelf bracket, carved stone, four centuries old, and I snapped it over my knee like kindling and threw both halves at the sarcophagus, where they bounced off the glass with a sound like a dropped plate.

"You *bitch*," I told her. "You absolute—" I ran out of words, which almost never happens, and settled for kicking the plinth, which hurt my foot and accomplished nothing, and then I stood there breathing hard in the cold while the echo died and the dark came back in around me like water filling a footprint.

The storm-spirits stirred. I felt them lean in—not through the stone, not through the air, but through me, through whatever I was becoming now that I'd let them in past the gate. They weren't alarmed. That was the worst part. They felt like an audience settling in.

"Don't," I said. "Don't you dare feel sorry for me."

They didn't. What they did was worse. They showed me.

* * *

Not words. Memory—hers, Sera's, poured straight down the bloodline like water down a channel, because apparently that's another thing my family inherits along with the knuckles and the temper. I was standing in a war camp four hundred years gone, mud to my ankles, watching a younger Sera argue with a man in Spire grey while between them, in a wagon cage of black iron, something thrashed and flashed and *wept*. A storm-spirit. Small—one of the lesser ones, a squall-thing, barely more than a gust with opinions—and it pressed itself against the bars and reached for Sera with tendrils of static, and it made a sound I have no word for except that I've heard it before, recently, in the dark under Thornfell, in voices that called me *daughter*.

The memory-Sera reached back. The ward-chains flared. And the Archmage's man said, kindly, reasonably, the way all the worst things are said: *"You can't save it, mistress. But you could save what comes after."*

And she believed him. That was the poison at the bottom of the cup. She believed him, and she went home, and she started writing, and she told herself for the rest of her long life that the maze was mercy—that steering her descendants' suffering toward a purpose was better than letting it be pointless. She took the cage the Spire built and she moved into it voluntarily, and she furnished it, and she called it hope.

The memory let go of me and I sat down hard on the floor of a dead woman's monument, next to the pieces of a shelf bracket I'd murdered, and I laughed until it turned into something else, and I let it, because there was no one there to see and no one left alive to lie to.

Seven years I'd spent being careful. Seven years believing my smallness was my own invention, my survival my own cleverness. And the whole time I'd been a sentence in someone else's book, moving through my life on rails she'd laid before my grandmother's grandmother learned to walk. Even my rebellion fit her plan. Even my exile fit. Especially my exile—because a weapon has to be tempered, doesn't it? Has to be quenched. Seven years of silence wasn't my discipline. It was her forge.

Fury is a strange thing when it runs out of places to go. You can't hit a ghost. You can't even properly hate her, because underneath it all—underneath the arrogance and the four hundred years of presumption—she'd been right about the Spire, and she'd been trying to save the storm-things in the cages, and she'd paid for it with everything too. We were the same, was the horrible truth. She'd just gotten to the fury part first and skipped straight past it to scheming, and scheming is what fury looks like when it stops trusting anyone, including the future.

I wasn't going to do that. Whatever I did next, I was going to do it angry and awake and *mine*.

I got up. I wiped my face on my sleeve, which was already ruined, and I looked at the sarcophagus one more time.

"I'm keeping the finger," I told her. "Everything else, we renegotiate."

Then I picked up the master almanac from her hands.

It came free easily, like it had been waiting, and the moment my skin touched the cover the whole chamber changed—the air pressure, the temperature, the *attention* of the place. The writing on the walls began to move. Not the old ink; that stayed put. New lines surfaced through the stone like fish rising in a pond, fresh script in a hand I recognized because it was mine, because it had always been mine, because Sera Halloran had written the last pages of her great work blank and keyed them to blood, waiting four hundred years for a descendant to stand in this room and pick up the pen.

Blank pages. Waiting. The only unwritten thing in the entire edifice.

Except—as the new lines rose and I read them, my stomach dropped through the floor and kept going—they weren't blank anymore. Something had gotten there first. The script surfacing through the stone was in my hand, yes, unmistakably mine—but I hadn't written it. I'd remember writing it. I'd remember writing *this*:

*The daughter stands at the author's grave and understands at last that she was never the reader, never the heroine, never even the prisoner. She is the page. And the storm does not ask the page's permission before it writes.*

Below it, the ink was still rising, still forming, line after line describing me—describing this exact moment, this exact breath—in words I had never spoken and never set down. Words that knew about the shelf bracket. Words that knew what I'd said to her corpse.

Either the prophecy was still writing itself through me, live, using my hand without my leave—

—or something else entirely had picked up the pen.

The lights in the chamber guttered. The air filled with the smell of ozone, and the storm-spirits inside me went suddenly, utterly silent, the way birds go silent before weather. And in the deepest dark at the back of the tomb, where the lamplight couldn't reach and the walls bore no writing at all, something that was not Sera Halloran cleared its throat and said, in a voice like thunder deciding to be polite:

"At last. I've been waiting *ages* for a reader."
