## 14. Council of Storms

I dreamed of weather.

That's not remarkable for me—seven years of exile and I still dreamed of storms the way other people dreamed of their mothers' kitchens. What was remarkable was that this time, the weather dreamed back.

* * *

The dream began as a field I knew: the high meadow above Thornfell where Kess and I had once lost a mule to a crevasse and spent a miserable night laughing about it because the alternative was crying. But in the dream the grass grew silver, and the sky came down to meet it like a lid being lowered onto a pot, and standing in that meadow were shapes that had no business standing anywhere.

They were made of rain. That's the closest my dreaming mind could get to rendering them. Tall columns of falling water that refused to fall, holding themselves upright out of what I can only assume was spite. Lightning moved inside them the way blood moves under skin—not visible, exactly, but present, warming everything it touched. There were nine of them. I counted twice, because counting is what you do when you're terrified and want to feel like you're doing something useful.

"You are smaller than we imagined," said the one at the center. Its voice wasn't sound so much as pressure—the feeling of the air just before it breaks.

"Well," I said, "you're taller than *I* imagined, so we're even."

Silence. Or whatever passes for silence among beings who communicate in barometric shifts. I got the distinct sense I had either offended them or delighted them, and with entities this old, those might be the same thing.

"We watched you sleep," the center one said. "For seven years. You never called us. You never even whispered."

"I was being quiet on purpose."

"Yes." Something rippled through the column of rain—amusement, maybe, or contempt, or the weather simply doing weather things. "We know about purpose. We were built of it."

* * *

Let me back up, because dreams don't care about narrative order but I do.

Three days had passed since the prophet's tomb. Three days of walking out of Hollowmere with my head full of her handwriting, three days of Kess watching me sideways across campfires with the particular patience of a man who has decided not to ask questions he isn't ready to hear answered. Three days of the sky over Thornfell behaving strangely—clouds gathering where no clouds should gather, breaking apart when I looked directly at them, like animals that had learned to be shy around humans.

And every night, the dreams got closer. The first night, thunder. The second, voices in the thunder, too far away to parse. The third night, they stopped pretending to be weather at all.

So there I was, in a silver meadow under a lowered sky, negotiating with nine imprisoned gods who had recently become nine slightly-less-imprisoned gods, thanks to me, and who had opinions about what happened next.

"Your ancestor bound us," the center one said. "Did she tell you that? The prophet. The one whose grave you stood on."

"She told me the Spire caged you. She didn't mention who did the caging."

"The Spire holds the lock," said another spirit, this one shorter, denser, its rain almost sleet. "Your line forged the key. That is why you exist, Halloran girl. Not despite the cages. *Because* of them."

I'd known this, in the way you know a thing you haven't let yourself say out loud. In the tomb, reading Marguerite Halloran's final annotations, I'd felt the shape of it pressing against the inside of my skull. Still, hearing it from the source—that landed differently. Hearing it from beings who remembered it happening.

"So I'm a lockpick," I said. "Born and bred. The Spire wanted a weapon, your captors wanted a key, and nobody thought to ask the key what it wanted."

"Someone is asking now," said the center one. "We are asking. That is the point of this council."

"Councils usually have chairs. And agendas. And fewer apocalypses per capita."

Another ripple. This time I was fairly sure it was laughter. It should have been comforting. It was worse than the anger would have been, somehow—the idea that these ancient, catastrophic things found me funny.

* * *

Here is what they showed me, then. Not with words—with memory, poured into the dream like water into a cup until I overflowed.

They showed me the world before the Spire. A world where storms walked. Where every thunderhead was a person, where lightning was language, where the rains came when they were invited and the droughts broke because someone asked nicely. Nine of them, siblings in the way that mountains are siblings—all born of the same catastrophe, the same first collision of sky and ground, and none of them alike.

They showed me the war. Not the war the Spire teaches—the tidy fable of chaos against order, of mages heroically binding the wild weather that would have drowned the world. The real one. The Spire's founders had been farmers, once. Farmers with magic and a grudge, because the storms wouldn't obey them, wouldn't irrigate on schedule, wouldn't respect property lines. So they built the first cage. And when the other eight came to free their sibling—and they did come, all of them, furious and grieving—they built eight more cages, and called the whole thing a war, and taught their children that the storms had started it.

"They wrote the history before the blood dried," the sleet-spirit said. "Humans do this. We have watched you do it many times."

"Yeah," I said, thinking of a trial seven years ago, of a sentence written before I ever walked into the courtroom. "We really do."

And then they showed me the price of the cages. This was the part I hadn't understood, sitting in the prophet's ruins, reading about wards fed by captive spirits. The wards protecting the realm—the great shield-works that kept out the wastes beyond the mountains, the blight-winds, the things that lived in the empty countries north of the maps—those wards weren't powered by the spirits' presence. They were powered by the spirits' *pain*. Every ward-stone in every Spire outpost drank from nine wells of suffering, had drunk for four hundred years, and if the wells went dry—if the prisoners went free—the wards wouldn't shatter all at once. They would fray. Slowly, at the edges first, like a hem coming undone. And the things beyond the borders would find the loose threads and pull.

"There it is," I said, when the vision released me. "The choice. Free you and unravel the realm, or leave you caged and keep the machine running. The prophecy gave me two endings. Turns out they're both terrible. Very efficient writing."

"No," said the center one.

"No?"

"That is the choice the Spire wants you to see. Two doors, both locked, both theirs." The column of rain leaned toward me, and the pressure of its attention made my dream-teeth ache. "But you did not open our cages, Halloran girl. Do you understand? You cracked them. You are a crack. And a crack is not a door. A crack is a *question*."

* * *

I woke up—or half woke—in the hollow beneath the leaning stone where we'd made camp, and for a moment I couldn't remember which world was which. The real sky through the gap in the rocks was full of clouds that pulsed faintly, rhythmically, like something breathing. Kess sat up across the dead fire, watching me.

"You were talking in your sleep," he said.

"Was I saying anything embarrassing?"

"You were arguing. With somebody. You called them—" he paused, working through it, "—'a bureaucratic nightmare.'"

"Accurate, probably."

He almost smiled. Then didn't. "Jude. Your hair's standing on end. All of it, straight up, like—" he gestured vaguely, "—like you've been struck by lightning and enjoyed it."

I put a hand to my head. He was right. Every hair on my arms was lifted, reaching toward the ceiling of cloud above us, and when I exhaled, my breath fogged silver and hung in the air instead of dissipating.

"Kess," I said carefully, "if I tell you something true tonight, will you promise not to make it weird?"

"It's a little late for that."

* * *

I told him some of it. Not all—I'm not a saint, and some truths need better lighting—but enough. The spirits. The cages. The fact that they'd been talking to me in my sleep for three nights, and that tonight they'd graduated from whispering to convening an entire metaphysical summit in my head, complete with condescension.

Kess took it the way he takes most things: quietly, thoroughly, and with an expression like a man doing long division. When I finished, he poked the dead coals for a while.

"So they're offering you power," he said finally.

"They're offering me *them*. That's different. They want a vessel. Someone to carry all nine of them—memories, grief, four hundred years of captivity, the whole catastrophic archive—out of the wards and into a body, so the realm keeps its shield and they get to stop suffering." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "They picked me because I'm already broken in, apparently. Exile-shaped. Good load-bearing trauma."

"And what do you get?"

"What?"

"In the bargain. What do you get?" He leaned forward, firelight or dreamlight catching his face. "Because I've watched you for seven years, Jude, and you always check the exit before you check the welcome. So ask them what you get. Because if the answer is 'the honor of self-sacrifice,' I want you to walk out of that meadow and not look back."

I stared at him. Of all the things he might have said—*you're insane, this is impossible, I'm leaving*—he'd gone straight to contract negotiation on my behalf.

"I'll ask," I said.

"Do that." He lay back down, pulled his blanket to his chin, and closed his eyes. Then, into the dark: "Tell them if they hurt you, I'll find a way to punch weather."

* * *

The second council convened before I'd fully fallen asleep again, which felt rude, but gods have never been famous for their manners.

"You spoke with your human," the center one said.

"He speaks *with* me, actually. It's a two-way thing. You should try it sometime."

More ripples. More ambiguous weather-emotion. Then the center one said, "You wish to know what you receive."

"Kess's orders."

"Very well." And the meadow changed.

They showed me what carrying them would mean. Not the glory version—the version where I stand on a mountaintop hurling lightning at my enemies while my hair does dramatic things. The truth. Nine lifetimes of memory layered over mine like wet wool. The weight of four hundred years in cages, felt from the inside, forever. The war they remembered—not as history but as wound. Every storm I'd ever carry would carry its own grief, and I would feel all of it, every day, for as long as my body held together.

And my body would hold together for less time than it should. Vessels wear. Even willing ones. Especially willing ones.

"This is your payment," the center one said. "Not power. *Company.* You will never again be alone inside your own skull. Nine of us, and you. We will argue. We have strong opinions about rivers."

"Gods help me."

"Also," said the sleet-spirit, with what I swear was relish, "you will be able to do this."

It gestured—or did whatever a column of rain does instead of gesturing—and the meadow exploded upward. Not violently. *Grandly.* The silver grass rose ten feet, twenty, bending in winds that came from nowhere, and above us the lowered sky opened like a book, and I saw—briefly, unbearably—the whole shape of the realm's wards from the outside, glowing faint and threadbare at the northern edges, and beyond them the dark, and the things in the dark turning slowly toward the light like moths the size of cathedrals.

Then it closed. My heart was going like a drum corps.

"The fraying has begun," the center one said quietly. "Your Archmage knows. That is why the rite is moved forward. That is why he needs a new spirit to drain—he means to patch a failing dam with fresh suffering." A pause, pressure shifting. "The dark moon is eleven days away, Halloran girl. After that, the choice stops being yours. The Spire will choose for you, and the Spire chooses cages. It has always chosen cages."

Eleven days. Corrane's ultimatum, ticking somewhere behind my ribs. The road to the Spire, three hundred miles of bad terrain and worse company. And somewhere ahead of me, an Archmage preparing to drain something new, something living, to feed a lie.

"One more question," I said. "When you say 'carry you'—do I get a vote afterward? Once you're in? Or is this a hostile merger?"

The nine of them did something together then—a slow, synchronized turning, like a compass finding north. And the center one said:

"We have been caged for four hundred years by creatures who decided our shape without asking us. We will not become that creature. Ask us anything, any day, forever. We may say no. You may say no. That is the whole covenant. That is the only covenant worth having."

I stood in the silver grass for a long moment, nine impossible weather-gods waiting on my answer, my partner's voice in my ears—*ask them what you get*—and the honest answer settling over me like the first cold drop of rain.

What I got was company. Terrible, ancient, grieving company. And a body that would burn through faster than it should, and memories that would never let me sleep easy again, and the only version of the future where nobody got caged—including me.

"You know," I said, "most job offers at least pretend the benefits are good."

"We are not most employers."

"No," I said. "You're really not."

I didn't say yes. Not yet. I said, "Eleven days. Let me get to the Spire first. If I'm going to carry nine gods and a war, I want to deliver them somewhere useful."

The meadow began to dissolve around the edges, the silver grass thinning into ordinary darkness, the sky lifting off like a lid removed from a finished meal. But the center one held a moment longer, and its last words followed me down into waking like a stone dropped after a coin:

"The crack is widening, Halloran girl. Whether you choose or not. Choose *fast*."

* * *

I woke with dawn in my eyes and frost on my blanket, though it was midsummer. Kess was already up, saddling the mule, moving with the brisk economy of a man who'd slept badly and decided to outrun it.

Across the valley, the clouds had organized themselves into a spiral, slow and deliberate, centered precisely over our camp. Anyone else would have called it strange weather. I knew a congregation when I saw one.

"Kess," I said, swinging my pack up. "Change of plans. We're not taking the southern pass."

He looked at me, then at the spiral of clouds, then back at me, and I watched him decide—visibly, consciously—not to ask. "Where are we taking?"

"The Spire." I swung up onto the mule. "We've got eleven days, a cargo of gods, and a delivery to make."

Behind us, soft as a held breath, it started to rain.
