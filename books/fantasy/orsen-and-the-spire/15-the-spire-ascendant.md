## 15. The Spire Ascendant

The Spire hires laborers the way a rich man hires mourners: nobody checks your grief, only your back.

I learned that from a quarry foreman in Lowmarsh who'd done two seasons inside as a stone-hauler and come out with his hearing shot and a story for every drink. He told me the intake yard took anyone with hands, a clean bill of health from the parish physicker, and no questions. The last part was the real job requirement. The Spire ran on questions the way mills ran on water, and it ground them fine.

So I didn't ask any. I just became Tam Voss, a widower from the river country with a bad shoulder I exaggerated on the right days, a limp I could turn off like a lamp, and a face I'd been practicing at forgetting. Jude had taught me that trick years ago without meaning to — how to stand so people's eyes slid off you. She was better at it than anyone alive. I used to think she did it because she was shy.

I know better now. Though what I actually know is a book with her name inked in it a hundred years before she was born, and a set of footprints going up into Thornfell, and a hole in my chest shaped exactly like the moment she put that book in my pack and kissed me like a debt being settled.

But we'll get to the book. First, the wall.

* * *

The Spire doesn't look like anything until you're close to it, and then it looks like too much. It stands out of the plain south of Cael Harrow like a needle somebody drove through the world's hand, white stone going up and up until your neck gives out, with the clouds bending around it instead of past. Even the weather negotiates there. That should have been my first clue about everything, but I'd grown up three valleys east of Thornfell, where the sky does what it likes, and I suppose part of me still believed weather was honest.

The intake yard sat in its shadow like a dog sleeping under a gallows. Two hundred of us stood in the cold that first morning while a clerk read names off a slate and mages in grey walked the lines doing something they called *attunement screening*, which meant they passed their palms over you and waited to see if the air flinched.

I flinched plenty. Inside. You can't screen for that yet. Give them time.

"Voss," the clerk said. "Stone-crew nine."

That was the whole of my identity for the next while, and honestly, it was restful. Stone-crew nine hauled foundation blocks for the outer ward-stations, which were being rebuilt along the northern approaches because — and this I overheard on day four, from two masons who thought the wind would keep their secrets — something had *scorched* the old ones. Something had come up out of the mountains in spring and burned wards that were supposed to be older than the realm.

I kept my head down and my ears open and my heart doing a complicated thing I chose not to examine.

Because here's what I'd worked out on the road, walking alone through country that still smelled faintly of ozone if the wind came off Thornfell right: Jude hadn't left me a map or a message or even a lie I could be angry at properly. She'd left me a *book*. And the book said — among other things, things I'd read so many times the pages had gone soft as cloth — that the dark moon at summer's end would be a hinge, and that the Halloran girl would either unmake the Spire or remake it, and that between those endings lay a road through every cage ever built.

She'd also left me a note. One line, tucked in the binding, in handwriting I'd have known in the dark:

*If you're reading this, don't follow me. Follow the work. Someone has to see what's true.*

Jude never asked anyone for anything except once, obliquely, by leaving. So I followed the work. I got myself hired by the machine that made her, and I started writing down everything.

* * *

You want to know what an empire of magic looks like from the inside?

Blisters. It looks like blisters, and bad bread, and a foreman named Osk who counted us in and out each dusk like coins he suspected of being shaved. It looks like the most powerful structure in the known world running, at the level where the work happened, on the same grumbling physics as any other big works project: not enough carts, too many bosses, someone always sick.

That was the thing I understood first and understood deepest, the thing I'd carry out of there if I carried nothing else: the Spire wasn't a temple. It was a *machine*. Temples are built for gods. Machines are built by people who need something done, over and over, forever, and don't especially care what it costs as long as the cost lands somewhere else.

The ward-stations were the visible cost. Each one was a ring of standing stones sunk into the earth at measured intervals, and when they were finished they'd hum — you could feel it through your boots near the half-built ones, a note so low it lived in your teeth. They fed the great wards that wrapped the realm like bandages, keeping out the wild weather and the worse-than-weather, and every child in every valley grew up learning to thank the Spire for the calm skies.

Nobody taught us to ask what paid for the calm.

I did my asking quietly. Stone-crew nine moved all over the grounds, which was luck I hadn't earned, and I got good at looking stupid in useful places. I memorized gate rotations by counting cart-passes. I learned which corridors the grey-robes used and which ones belonged to the servants of the servants. I found the drainage grate behind the tannery that nobody watched, and the stretch of curtain wall where the warding thinned because the masons had quarreled with the enchanters and cut corners out of spite — pettiness, I've decided, is the great solvent of empires, and someday I'm going to write that down somewhere important.

By the third week I had a map forming in my head. Not drawn — drawing got men disappeared, everyone knew that, though "disappeared" mostly turned out to mean reassigned to the deep mines with their wages docked, which was its own kind of lesson about how the Spire valued people. Just held. I'd always had a head for routes; it's why Jude and I worked so well moving contraband. She read the sky, I read the ground, and between us we could get almost anything almost anywhere.

I mapped the Spire the same way. She'd have laughed at me for it. Then she'd have kissed me, and then she'd have left again, and I'd have let her, because that was the arrangement we'd never spoken aloud: I loved a woman made of exits, and I'd made my peace with being the door she came back to.

Except this time she hadn't come back. This time the door was empty. So the map wasn't for smuggling anymore.

It was for getting *her* out. Or getting in after her. I hadn't decided which, and some nights, lying in the laborers' longhouse listening to forty men breathe, I understood with total clarity that those might be the same errand, and that I might be the least qualified person in the realm to run it, and that none of that mattered, because love doesn't check credentials. It just points.

* * *

The discovery came the way discoveries do in big places: sideways, through boredom, via a man named Pell.

Pell was a chandler's boy assigned to our crew for a season, sixteen, all elbows and questions — the exact opposite of survival strategy, which is why I liked him. He'd been seconded to the candle-works below the Archmage's tower, because the Spire burned through candles at a rate that would have bankrupted a duchy, and he came back from his first delivery run vibrating with gossip.

"They've doubled the wax order," he told us around supper, loud enough that Osk cuffed him, quiet enough after that only our end of the bench heard. "For the rite. Dark moon. There's a chamber under the tower — I delivered to the anteroom, me and Wend, and through the door there was this *light*. Not fire-light. It moved wrong. Like water standing up."

"What kind of rite needs a thousand candles?" asked Brannick, our crew's resident cynic, who believed everything was a tax scheme.

"The kind," Pell said, dropping his voice to the register boys use when they've seen something holy or terrible and can't tell the difference, "where they're feeding it to something. Wend says the floor of the anteroom is warm. Through your boots, warm. And there's a smell." He grimaced. "Like the air after lightning."

I stopped chewing.

Ozone. Warm stone. Light that moved like water standing up. I sat there in the longhouse noise with my bowl cooling in my hands and felt the book's weight even though it was thirty leagues away, buried in a smuggler's cache under a false-bottomed crate, because some things you carry whether or not you're carrying them.

*Between the endings lies a road through every cage ever built.*

"Pell," I said, casual as gravel. "This rite. Anyone say what it's *for*?"

He shrugged. "Dark moon comes, they do the rite, wards stay strong. Everyone knows that much. It's the Renewal." He said the word with the flat reverence of someone reciting a prayer he'd never examined, and why would he? I'd said the same word the same way my whole life. The Renewal. The night the Spire re-fed the wards and the realm slept safe. We thanked the mages for it at festivals. There were *cakes*.

"But this year's bigger," Brannick said. "Double candles. Why?"

"That's the thing." Pell leaned in, thrilled to be the center of gravity. "Wend heard the under-chamberlain arguing with a supply mage. This year they're not renewing." His eyes went wide. "They're *adding*. A new binding. A new spirit, straight from capture, and the Archmage herself will preside. First time in a lifetime, Wend says. Maybe longer."

The longhouse went on around me — dice clattering, somebody singing badly, Osk shouting at Pell to stop monopolizing the salt — and I sat inside all of it very still, the way you go still when you realize the floor you've been standing on is thinner than advertised.

A new spirit. Straight from capture.

Which meant somewhere in the Spire's deep places, right now, there was a cage. And something in it. And on the dark moon, the most powerful mage in the realm intended to do to that something what had been done — if the book was true, if the marginalia was true, if the woman I loved was who the book said she was — to every storm that had ever been pressed into service for the realm's calm skies.

And I thought: *they're going to do to someone new what they did to her.* Because that was the shape of it now, wasn't it? The prophecy hadn't been a fortune. It had been a *work order*. Breed the vessel, script the exile, deliver the girl to the door. And the Spire, needing a new battery for its machine, was simply following its own recipe again with fresher ingredients.

Ordinary courage. That's what I decided to call what I did next, later, when I needed a name for it, because *desperation* sounded less flattering and *stupidity* sounded more accurate. I finished my supper. I helped scrub the benches, because Osk noticed who helped. I went to my bunk and lay in the dark and began, methodically, to move my map from my head onto the inside of my own skin of memory, room by room, gate by gate, grate by grate — and for the first time I added the levels I couldn't see, extrapolating downward, toward the warm floors and the standing light and the thing that smelled like lightning.

Forty men breathed around me. Somewhere above us all, clouds bent politely around a needle of white stone, and under the stone, something screamed in frequencies no laborer was ever cleared to hear.

* * *

Three nights before the dark moon, I did the stupidest competent thing of my life.

The candle deliveries gave me my opening — Pell owed me, and I'd spent weeks being the man who fixed his bootlaces and covered his counting errors, and debt is the oldest currency in any works camp. He got me onto the delivery rotation as a carrier. Double hands for double orders. Nobody looked hard at carriers. Carriers were furniture.

The tower stairs went down further than they went up. That's the detail I'll never shake, how far *down* it went, how the Spire had always been less a tower than a taproot. We descended past storerooms, past sealed doors with wards that made my fillings ache, past a landing where the air changed — warmer, wetter, charged, the specific pressure of the hour before a storm breaks. Pell shifted from elbow to elbow beside me, whistling until I elbowed him quiet, and the light from our candle-load threw our shadows huge against walls that drank it wrong.

At the anteroom door we set down the crates. A grey-robe took receipt without looking at us, which was the custom everywhere in the Spire: the invisible and the indispensable, hand in hand. And as we turned to go, as Pell fumbled his tally-slate and bought us ten extra seconds of standing there, I looked past the grey-robe's shoulder through the inner doorway, just once, the way Jude taught me — quick, sidelong, forgettable —

— and saw the chamber.

Not the spirit. Not the cage itself. But the *approach*: a ring of black stone worn smooth as a lakebed, channels cut radiating outward from its center like spokes, and in the channels, light. Moving light. Standing water that wasn't water, crawling slowly inward, toward the middle of the room, toward whatever waited at the hub that I couldn't see from the angle of the door and will spend the rest of my life not quite regretting.

It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen and it was being *piped*. That was the horror of it. Not cruelty — plumbing. The storms of the world, caught and channeled and metered, flowing on schedule to keep the wards fat and the realm calm and the festival cakes coming.

And on the far wall, above the ring, hung a schedule board. Wax chalk on slate. Rite preparations, hour by hour, in a tidy under-chamberlain's hand.

The last line read: *Vessel secured and holding. Inspection at dawn.*

*Vessel.*

I picked up my empty crates and walked up all those stairs on legs made of someone else's patience, and I didn't shake until I was outside, under the bent clouds, in the yard where the wards hummed their tooth-note and two hundred men queued for a supper they'd earned hauling stone for their own cage.

Dawn inspection. Three nights to the dark moon.

Whatever "vessel" the Spire was holding down there in the warm dark — whether it was a captured storm already broken, or something newer, something bred and scripted and exiled seven years ago precisely so it could be collected — it was going to be dragged to that black ring at summer's end and piped.

Unless somebody invisible, indispensable, and completely unqualified got to it first.

I touched the scar on my knuckle where a Thornfell pass had bitten me years ago, the night Jude laughed for a full minute at something I'd said, back when I thought her secrets were small ones.

"Hold on," I told the ground, quietly, in case the dead of the Spire or the caged or anyone else was listening. "I'm coming. I'm slow, but I'm coming."

Above me, for just a heartbeat, the bent clouds flickered — one clean thread of lightning, out of season, out of place, out of every almanac ever written.

Then it was gone, and the queue moved, and I moved with it, ordinary as blisters, straight toward the heart of the machine.
