## 16. Collar and Crown

There are exactly two ways to enter the Spire of Vhalen: invited, or in chains. I'd spent seven years making sure it would be neither. And yet here I was, third option, the one nobody puts on the list—dragged up the pilgrim road between two wardens with a binding collar around my throat that hummed like a hornet's nest dipped in prayer.

I want to say I went with dignity. What I actually did was trip on the fourth step from the bottom and get hauled upright by my armpits like a sack of turnips, which is not the image I'll be leaving out of any songs, because there will be no songs, because I intend to survive this.

The collar deserved a paragraph of its own, so here it is. Cold iron worked through with silver thread, sigils stamped along the band in an alphabet older than the Spire itself. It didn't hurt, precisely. It *waited*. Every time the storm in me stirred—and it never stopped stirring now, not since Hollowmere, not since I'd let it taste open sky—it pressed against something inside the collar, and the collar pressed back, patient as debt collectors. The message was simple: *we know what you are, and we've known for longer than you have.*

That last part was the part that kept me up at night. Well. Kept me up during the day, since they'd decided to travel by night to avoid my weather, which was thoughtful of them.

"Keep moving," said Warden Brask, the one with the jaw like a dropped anvil. He wasn't cruel. That was somehow worse. Cruelty you can hate; competence you just endure.

"I'm moving," I said. "You'll notice I've been moving for three days. Some might call it a pilgrimage."

"Some might call it a hanging offense."

"And yet."

Corrane walked ahead of us, ten paces, always ten paces, close enough to hear everything and far enough to pretend he didn't. He'd barely spoken since Blackmoon Ford, where I had traded myself for Kess and lied through my teeth about cooperating. I'd expected gloating. Inquisitors in the stories always gloat. Corrane instead had looked at me with something adjacent to pity and said, "Thank you," which had been so much more disturbing that I'd spent half the journey trying to decide if he was mocking me or sincere.

Three days later I'd settled on sincere, which was worse than mocking. Fanatics don't gloat. Gloating requires doubt.

The Spire rose ahead of us now, and I'll grant it this: it earned its name. White stone going gray at the base from centuries of weather, climbing out of the valley floor like a spear someone had thrown into the earth and then built a city around the wound. Ward-light crawled up its flanks at dusk—the great lattice that shielded the realm from the Wildings beyond the mountains, or so every child was taught. I had been that child once. I had stood in the novices' courtyard at nine years old and watched those lights and thought: *this is what safety looks like.*

Now I could feel what fed them. Through the collar, muffled but unmistakable, something vast turned over beneath the Spire and sighed. Storm-spirits. Caged ones. The engine under the altar.

They knew I was coming. I could tell because the wind changed.

* * *

They processed me in a chamber below the pilgrims' gate, and I use the word *processed* deliberately, because that's what it felt like: goods entering a warehouse. They took my boots. They took the knife Kess had given me when I turned twenty, the one with his mother's initials scratched under the grip. They took the stub of pencil I'd hidden in my sleeve for three days on the off chance I'd find paper and privacy, which tells you how optimistic I'd been feeling. A clerk with ink-stained fingers recorded all of it in a ledger, including—"one pencil, worn"—with the gravity of a man inventorying crown jewels.

Then a woman came to measure me.

Not torture-me. Measure-me. She had kind eyes and terrible efficiency, and she ran a knotted cord down my arms, my spine, around my skull, murmuring numbers to another clerk. When she reached the old brand on my shoulder blade—the exile mark, burned there seven years ago in this same building—she paused.

"Healing well," she said, as though complimenting a scar I should be proud of.

"It itches," I said. "Do you have anything for that?"

She did not laugh. Nobody in this building laughed. I began to suspect laughter had been confiscated along with my pencil.

They gave me a cell. Of course they did. But not a dungeon cell—a *guest* cell, which is a dungeon that has been gaslit. Clean straw pallet, a basin, a window too high to reach, a door that locked from outside with a sound like a promise being made. On the pallet lay a white robe, folded with corners sharp enough to draw blood.

Ceremonial. For the rite. Three days hence, dark moon, when the Archmage would pour whatever I was into whatever the cage-heart needed, and the wards would drink deep for another century.

I sat on the pallet and did not touch the robe, because touching it felt like agreeing to it.

Through the collar I listened to the thing beneath the Spire breathe.

*Daughter,* it whispered, the way it had whispered in the spirit roads, in my dreams, in the space behind my teeth ever since I'd started listening. *Daughter, they are dressing you for slaughter.*

"Yes," I said aloud, to an empty room, like a madwoman. "I had noticed."

*And still you came.*

I hadn't come. I'd been brought. There was a difference. There was a whole philosophy of difference, and I clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood shaped like a technicality.

But the spirit was right about one thing. Somewhere between Blackmoon Ford and the pilgrim road, between trading myself away and lying to Corrane's face, I had stopped thinking of this as capture. Kess was free. That had been the price and I'd paid it gladly. Everything after that was mine to spend however I chose, and I intended to spend it badly—for the Spire's definition of badly.

The prophecy had given me two endings: destroy the Spire, or save it. It had not given me a third option, but then, the prophet had also failed to mention she was my ancestor, so her editorial standards were clearly negotiable.

I lay back on the straw and started counting the things I knew that the Spire didn't know I knew. It was a short list, but it had good bones.

* * *

On the second day, they let me walk.

This sounds generous. It was logistics. The rite required preparation—fasting, anointing, some ritual scrubbing of the vessel, presumably so the storm-spirits wouldn't object to the packaging—and preparation required corridors. Two wardens, front and back, the collar humming at my throat, and me walking through the beating heart of the order that had exiled me, wearing a novice's gray smock because my own clothes were being "purified," which I suspected meant burned.

I catalogued everything. Old habit. Seven years of smuggling teaches you to read a building the way other people read faces—which doors are guarded, which guards are bored, which corridors bend where the walls say they shouldn't. The Spire was a maze built by people who wanted it read as holy rather than navigable, and I was pleased to discover that holiness makes for terrible architecture. Every sacred geometry in the world is just a floor plan that got a swelled head.

We passed the novices' courtyard. Children, maybe ten of them, drilling cantrips under a tutor's dry voice, their little lightning-fingers sparking and failing and sparking again. One of them—a girl, gap-toothed, ferociously concentrated—caught me looking and stared back with the frank rudeness only children and cats can manage. I winked. Her cantrip misfired spectacularly and set her neighbor's sleeve smoking, and the tutor's voice climbed an octave, and I was marched on before I could do further damage to the Spire's educational outcomes.

It hurt, watching them. That surprised me. I had spent seven years telling myself I felt nothing about this place except the itch of old scars. But there were children in it. Children learning weather the way I had learned it, before anyone told them weather was a weapon or a heresy depending on which side of a trial table you sat.

*They will feed your storm to the wards,* whispered the thing below, unhelpfully, *and these children will grow old never knowing the sky was stolen.*

"Noted," I muttered.

"Speak when spoken to," said the warden behind me.

"I was speaking to the architecture."

He didn't have a response prepared for that, which I counted as a small victory. You take victories where you find them, in chains.

They took me to the anointing chamber next, and this is where the day went wrong—or right; history may quibble—in the form of a supply corridor.

The anointing was oil and cold hands and a liturgy recited at me rather than to me, and afterward a servant was sent to fetch clean linens, and the servant was late, and the junior acolyte supervising me grew impatient and sent a second person, and somewhere in that small administrative failure, the universe—my ancestor's universe, my storms' universe, whatever force arranges near-misses—decided to be cruel.

Because the second person who came through that corridor door, pushing a linen cart, head down, hair tied back wrong for his face, shoulders too broad for the laborer's smock—

Was Kess.

He did not see me. That was the mercy and the agony of it. He was playing his role beautifully, the way he played every role, cart wheels squeaking, eyes fixed on the middle distance, invisible through sheer practiced ordinariness. He had gotten inside. My beautiful, stubborn, impossible fool had gotten himself inside the Spire as a laborer, which meant he had either found a way past Corrane's men after Blackmoon Ford or talked his way through the gates with forged papers and that grin he thinks I don't know is forged too.

And I could not say his name. I could not twitch. I could not do anything but stand between two wardens with oil drying on my collarbones while the man I loved pushed laundry past me at four feet, close enough that I could smell road dust and cheap tallow soap, close enough to count the new scar splitting his eyebrow—he'd been hurt since Ford, since me, since I'd left him standing on a riverbank believing I'd surrendered for real—

His eyes flicked up. Habit. Smugglers check sightlines the way hearts check beats.

Four feet. Gray smock. Binding collar. Me.

For one heartbeat—one, no more—his face did something terrible. Not shock. Shock is loud. This was quiet: pupils tightening, breath stopping mid-draw, the particular stillness of a man watching a door he thought was closed swing open onto everything he feared. His knuckles whitened on the cart's handle. The wheels squeaked to a halt.

"No," I said. Just that. Mouthed more than said, pitched under the wardens' hearing, aimed straight at him: *no, keep walking, don't you dare.*

He held my gaze for the length of a falling leaf. Then he looked down, adjusted his grip, and pushed the cart onward, squeaking, ordinary, gone around the corner with the rest of the linens and every word we'd ever said to each other.

My knees tried to resign. The wardens noticed nothing, or noticed everything and filed it away; with Spire men you never learn which, and both possibilities are bad.

"That one's new," the warden behind me observed, to no one.

"They hire quickly before rites," said the other.

"They hire everyone before rites. Half the city's in these halls carrying something."

If either of them had looked at the laborer's hands—too soft for hauling, rope-burned in the pattern of mooring lines—they might have finished that thought differently. But holiness makes for terrible architecture and worse security, and I had learned long ago that the Spire's greatest ward was the conviction that no one would dare.

Kess had dared. Which meant that whatever I'd planned—whatever half-formed, probably suicidal scheme the spirits and I had been gesturing toward—I now had to plan around a second variable with broad shoulders, poor judgment, and a documented history of refusing to leave me behind.

I should have been furious. I saved the fury for later, because underneath it, treacherously, was something warm and awful: *he came after me.* Betrayal braided with relief braided with dread, all of it pulled tight enough to cut.

*Your heart is loud,* murmured the caged storm below, amused, ancient, insufferable.

"Shut up," I said, and the warden gave me a look, and I smiled at him with all my teeth.

* * *

That night—assuming it was night; my window showed only a slit of stone-colored sky—the collar woke up.

Not the waiting-hum. Something else. I'd been drifting at the edge of sleep when the sigils along the band went hot as a poker, and a voice poured through them, layered, male and female and neither, the voice of the Spire speaking through its own machinery.

*Vessel.* It tasted the word. *You are restless.*

"You could say that." I sat up in the dark. No point pretending sleep. "Lovely accommodations, by the way. The straw is a bit forward."

*The Archmage asks whether you understand what comes.*

"Oh, I understand." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "You're going to open me like a lockbox and pour me into your engine. The question is whether *you* understand what comes. Because I've met the things in your cellar, and they remember why they were locked up, and they've had a hundred years to compose their opening remarks."

Silence. Long enough that I wondered if the collar had gone back to sleep. Then:

*The prophecy names two endings.*

"The prophecy," I said, "was written by my great-great-grandmother, who had a flair for the dramatic and a poor grasp of third options. Ask your Archmage what happens at the rite. Ask him what the spirits do when the vessel starts answering back."

Another silence, and this one had a different texture. Doubt has a texture. I'd heard it in Corrane's pauses on the road south, and I heard it now, threaded through the Spire's iron voice like silver through the collar's iron.

*Rest, vessel.*

"Sweet dreams," I told it, and lay back down, and grinned at the ceiling like a lunatic, because I had frightened a building.

Small victories. You take them where you find them.

But after the grin faded, in the honest dark, I let myself think about the linen cart. About the scar above Kess's eye. About how many hours it would take him to map this place from the servants' corridors, and how many of those hours I had left before the dark moon rose, and how the two sums did not remotely match.

He would come for me. Of course he would. He'd come for me across the Thornfell passes with a bounty on both our heads; he'd come for me through the front gate of the order that exiled me, wearing a smock and pushing laundry. He would come for me at the worst possible moment with the best possible heart, and he would get himself killed, unless—

Unless I moved first. Unless whatever I did at that rite, I did early, loudly, in a way that pulled every warden and ward in the Spire toward me and away from a laborer with an eyebrow scar sneaking through the undercrofts.

Below me, deeper than the foundations, the caged storms turned in their sleep, and their dreaming sounded almost like laughter.

*Soon, daughter,* they said.

"Soon," I agreed, and watched the stone-colored sky go slowly black over the dark moon, and started counting again—hours now, not certainties—and for the first time since Blackmoon Ford, the arithmetic came out on my side.
