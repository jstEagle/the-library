## 17. The Dark Moon Rite

### JUDE

They dressed me in white.

I want that on the record, wherever records go when the person they're about is about to be sacrificed: they dressed me in *white*. Like a bride. Like a lamb. Like something clean being offered up to something hungry, which I suppose was accurate in every particular except cleanliness.

The collar sat snug against my throat, cold as a promise. Corrane had checked it three times on the road up the mountain—his fingers finding the sigils, counting them like a man counting coins he couldn't afford to lose—and each time I'd smiled at him with all the warmth of a frozen lake.

"Comfortable?" he'd asked.

"Cozy," I said. "You should try one."

He hadn't laughed. Inquisitors rarely do. But there had been a flicker at the corner of his mouth, some small muscle remembering what humor felt like before the Spire filed it down to a point, and I'd thought: *there you are.* There was still a man under the ledger. Men under ledgers could be turned. I'd learned that from Kess, who was a whole library of a man pretending to be a pamphlet.

Now they marched me through corridors of white stone toward the rite chamber, and I counted my assets the way I counted contraband in the old days. One collar, tricked with a binding knot I'd been picking at since Blackmoon Ford with a technique I'd invented during seven years of exile and boredom, which is to say: out of spite. One prophecy memorized down to its marginalia. One set of chains, ceremonial, because apparently even human sacrifice gets pageantry. And somewhere in this building, if he'd actually come—and Kess always came, that was his whole tragic architecture—one smuggler with a crowbar and no plan worth the name.

Assets: four. Odds: terrible. Mood: honestly? Better than it had been in years.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about being led to your own destruction: once you've stopped fighting the destination, the walk becomes very peaceful. The dread burns off like morning fog. What's left is just weather. And I had spent seven years learning everything there was to know about weather.

The rite chamber opened around us like the inside of a bell.

It was carved into the mountain's crown—a hollow spire within the Spire, walls ribbed with channels of silver that ran downward like veins, converging on a floor of black glass. Above, where a ceiling should have been, there was only open sky and the dark moon hanging in it, a hole cut out of the night, drinking starlight. Below the glass floor—I could feel it rather than see it, the way you feel a river under ice—something vast turned over in its sleep.

*Hello,* whispered something in the base of my skull, in a voice like wind through a canyon. *Daughter.*

"Not now," I murmured. "We're not alone."

The Archmage stood at the chamber's heart. Serevan Dain. I'd seen his face on wanted broadsheets for seven years—the exile notices never mentioned him by name, but everyone knew whose seal burned the villages that harbored storm-callers. In person he was smaller than legend, gray-robed, silver-bearded, with the mild eyes of a schoolmaster. That was the worst part. He looked like a man who fed birds.

"Halloran," he said, warm as a hearth. "Right on time. You have no idea how rare that is, in a century of prophecy."

"I've got some idea," I said. "Your bookkeeper wrote me a schedule."

They chained me at the center of the black glass, arms spread, wrists in silver manacles bolted to the floor. The channels in the walls began to glow faintly, and beneath my feet the sleeping thing stirred again, closer now, pressing up against the underside of the world.

"Do you know what this room is?" Dain asked, circling me slowly, hands folded behind his back like a lecturer.

"A slaughterhouse," I said.

"A *reservoir*." He smiled. "There were nine storms, once. Nine great spirits of sky and thunder, older than the realm, older than the wards that keep the borderlands from drowning in wild magic. Your ancestors worshipped them. Made bargains with them. Bled for them." He paused at the edge of the glass, looking down through it, fond as a farmer looking at well-water. "And then the founders of the Spire asked themselves a question every institution eventually asks: why share power with gods when you can *own* them?"

My stomach went cold. Not with fear. With recognition—the specific vertigo of a story snapping into place, every loose thread suddenly taut.

"The war," I said slowly. "The Storm War. The one that killed half the borderlands. The one that justified the order, the inquisitors, the exile edicts—"

"There was no war," Dain said pleasantly. "Well. There was a *night* of it. We provoked the spirits into striking Thornfell—killed a few hundred of our own people, regrettable but efficient—and the realm, horrified, handed us everything we asked for. Wards. Prisons. Authority. A mandate." He turned back to me, and his eyes were bright with something I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors after long nights of telling myself comforting lies: the shine of a man who has made his guilt into a foundation and built a cathedral on top of it. "Nine cages need nine keys, Halloran. Nine locks need nine bloodlines bred to fit them. Yours is the last. The Halloran line was our finest work."

The words landed like stones dropped down a well. I waited for the splash and heard none, because the well was deeper than grief.

"Bred," I repeated. My voice came out flat. "My mother. Her mother. The trial—"

"Scripted, yes. Your exile, your isolation, your seven years of anger—we needed the vessel *seasoned*, not broken. Rage feeds the binding. It gives the spirit something to pour itself into." He tilted his head, studying me with genuine scholarly interest. "You've done beautifully. Truly. Every hardship we arranged, you metabolized into exactly the temperament required. You should be proud."

And here is what I did not do: scream, or weep, or curse him. Here is what I did instead, because I am, whatever else is true, my mother's daughter and a smuggler to the bone:

I laughed.

Not much. Just enough. Because he'd made one error, the same error every cage-maker makes since the first cage—he'd told me the lock's design while standing inside the room with me. He'd explained the binding. He'd described the rage, the seasoning, the vessel. And underneath the black glass, the oldest storm in the world had heard every word, and it was not seasoned, and it was not mine, and it was *listening*.

"You're smiling," Dain said, frowning slightly. "That's not in the prophecy."

"No," I agreed. "It really isn't."

Above us, the dark moon reached the apex of its arc. The channels blazed. Somewhere far below, iron doors began to grind open, and the first of the nine prisons exhaled a century of held breath.

Dain raised his arms and began the rite.

### KESS

The laundry chute smelled like six months of other people's mistakes, which, having worked the smuggling trade, Kess considered roughly par for the course.

He came out of it into a linen storeroom two levels above the rite chamber, according to the map he'd scratched into his forearm in lampblack over three days of hauling crates as "Tam Weller, laborer, third shift, no questions." Third shift had been generous with its blindness. Laborers in the Spire learned early that seeing things was a career-ending condition, and so Kess had walked past ward-rooms and armories and one corridor that hummed like a struck tuning fork, and had seen nothing, nothing, nothing.

Except the map. He'd seen the map. It lived behind his eyes now, drawn in the same stubborn ink as the scar tissue of every bad decision he'd ever made, and the newest mark on it was a circle around the chamber directly below, with one word beside it: *Jude.*

She'd left him the book. That was the thing he kept coming back to, turning over like a coin he suspected was shaved. She'd left him the prophecy—the thing she'd clearly have burned if she'd truly meant to vanish—and she'd left it open to her own name. That wasn't an apology. Apologies didn't come with footnotes. That was an *instruction*. Read this. Understand what I am. Then decide.

He'd decided somewhere around mile forty of walking toward the most fortified structure in the known world with a crowbar in his coat.

The problem, as established over three days of hauling crates, was that the Spire did not have doors so much as it had *opinions*. Every threshold between levels was warded, and the wards weren't spells exactly—they were more like rules the building believed in, and the building believed very strongly that Tam Weller belonged in the sublevels and nowhere else. Kess had watched a fellow laborer step one stair too high and get gently, politely, escorted back down by empty air, white-faced and apologizing to nobody.

But the laundry chute had no opinions. The laundry chute, like all infrastructure everywhere, existed in the blind spot of every architect who'd ever lived. And the chute came out forty feet above the rite chamber floor, above a grate, above—

Jude.

His heart did something complicated and painful. She was chained spread-eagle on black glass in white robes, hair loose, head bowed, and the Archmage of the Spire was circling her talking in the easy cadence of a man giving a tour, and the whole room was lit silver from channels in the walls that Kess's gut understood before his mind did: those weren't decorations. Those were *pipes*. This whole beautiful room was plumbing, and the thing being plumbed was chained to the floor wearing his partner's face.

*Don't be stupid,* he told himself. *Be smart. Be patient. Find the ward-anchor, break it quiet, get her out.*

Then the Archmage said, clear across the chamber, in a voice pitched to carry: "There was no war. We started it ourselves."

—and Kess stopped being smart, because the words detonated something in him that had been quietly packed with powder since Hollowmere, since the marginalia, since the moment he'd read *the Halloran girl, bred for release or ruin* and told himself it was mad scribbling. It wasn't madness. It was *minutes*. It was a hundred years of minutes, itemized, and the Spire had signed for the delivery.

Below him, Jude laughed. Small, sharp, familiar—the laugh she used when a fence lowballed her or a patrol got too close, the laugh that meant someone was about to have a bad day. Kess gripped his crowbar and grinned into the dark of the chute, fierce and terrified.

"That's my girl," he breathed. "Make it expensive."

* * *

### JUDE

The rite was a song. That surprised me. I'd expected incantation, grandeur, thunder. Instead Dain simply sang—an old, plain melody, almost a lullaby—and as he sang, the silver channels drank light from the dark moon and poured it down through the glass, and the first prison opened fully, and the storm-spirit rose through the mountain like water finding its level.

I felt it enter the chamber's lattice and stop. Feel it strain against the geometry of the room, against the channels, against the collar at my throat. Felt it *look* at me.

*Daughter,* it said again, and this time the word had weight, had history, had a hundred years of loneliness folded inside it. *They built you from our leavings. They built your grandmother from our leavings. Blood of ours, shaped by their hands.*

"Yeah," I said aloud, and Dain faltered mid-note, because the vessel wasn't supposed to converse. "I got the family history just now. Thanks for asking."

*The collar binds your calling,* the storm said. *But child. Child. Look at what they built the collar from.*

I looked. Inward, down the link the collar forced open between me and the lattice, the way it was designed to—designed to let the spirit flow *into* me, that was the whole mechanism, that was the entire point of breeding me. Flow in, be caged, be milked. The collar regulated the flow. The collar was a valve.

And a valve, any smuggler worth her salt knows, works in both directions.

"Oh," I said softly. "*Oh, you clever bastards.*"

"What?" Dain had stopped singing entirely now. His schoolmaster calm had developed its first crack, a fine hairline running through the mildness. "What did you do?"

"Nothing yet," I said. "But I've been picking at this collar since Ford, and I just realized I didn't need to pick it at all. I needed to understand it. You built a door, Archmage. You just assumed I'd only ever walk through it your way."

I opened the valve.

Not wide. A crack. A smuggler's crack, the width of a knife blade, the width of exactly enough. And the storm-spirit—the first of the nine, the eldest, the one they'd caged deepest and longest—came through it like a whisper through a keyhole, and it did not pour into me the way the rite intended, docile and contained.

It *recognized* me.

Every cell I had lit up with a memory that wasn't mine: open sky before the wards, the taste of rain on mountains that had never been named, the long dark of the cage, and threaded through all of it, impossibly, *tenderness*—the specific tenderness of something ancient regarding something small and new and made partly of itself. The way you regard a child. The way you regard a key.

The collar screamed. Sigils flared white along my throat and blew out one by one like candles in a gale. The manacles shattered—not broke, *shattered*, frost-patterned, flash-frozen from the inside. The channels in the walls flickered, guttered, and for one heartbeat the entire reservoir-lattice hung in perfect, catastrophic balance, waiting to see which way the pressure would resolve.

Dain threw the full force of the Spire at me. I felt it come—wards folding inward, the building itself taking his side, a hundred years of institutional certainty bearing down like an avalanche. Any other day, any other girl, it would have been enough.

But I was not alone in my skin anymore, and the thing sharing it had been waiting a century to hit somebody.

The black glass floor cracked from edge to edge. The dark moon overhead seemed to shudder. And every channel, every pipe, every vein of silver in the Spire of the mage order reversed its flow at once, and the second prison, deep in the roots of the mountain, answered the first with a roar I felt in my teeth:

*WE REMEMBER WHAT YOU DID.*

"Yes," I said, rising off the floor with my feet an inch above the glass and lightning crawling up my white sleeves like ivy, and I have never in my life meant anything the way I meant the next words. "So do I. Let's go collect the rest of the family."

Somewhere above me, faintly, through forty feet of stone, I heard a crowbar hit a grate.

*Kess,* I thought, with the last corner of my mind that was still just a woman and not a weather system. *Oh, you absolute idiot. You beautiful, loyal idiot.*

The ceiling of the rite chamber came down like the sky had been waiting for permission.

* * *

### KESS

Later—much later, when he had time to arrange the memory properly—Kess would describe it as the moment the Spire remembered it was sitting on top of a volcano.

In the moment itself, there was no describing. There was the grate blowing outward past his head, there was falling, there was landing badly on rubble in a room full of silver light going wrong, and there was Jude—white-robed, barefoot, hovering a hand's breadth above a shattered floor with lightning written all over her like annotations in a margin, *her* margins, the ones she'd finally taken back.

Their eyes met across the ruined chamber.

Seven years he'd worked beside her without knowing what she was. Seven years of campfires and close calls and her laughing at his jokes with that crooked mouth, and all of it had been true, and all of it had been a mask, and somehow both facts coexisted in his chest without canceling out, the way a river holds both the rain and the rocks.

"You came," she said. Her voice had harmonics in it now. Two voices, maybe three. It should have frightened him.

"Someone had to bring the crowbar," he said.

The Archmage was screaming orders at wards that no longer answered. The walls of the chamber were peeling open like petals, revealing the machinery beneath, the pipes, the cages, the century of theft laid bare in glowing silver—and beyond the broken walls, Kess could see the Spire stretching upward into darkness, and he could see, far above, through the wound in the roof, the dark moon beginning to move.

And he could see, at the chamber's far entrance, white cloak billowing, Vael Corrane walking into the ruin with his sword drawn and his face gone strange—no longer the inquisitor's certainty, something rawer, something that had just heard the same revelation Kess had and was chewing on it like broken glass.

"Jude," Kess said, jerking his chin at the approaching inquisitor. "Friend or problem?"

Jude looked at Corrane. Corrane looked at the exposed cages, at the pipes, at the truth hanging in the air like smoke. His sword arm trembled.

"I don't know yet," Jude said. The storms in her voice made it sound like a forecast. "Ask him fast."

Above them all, the second cage finished opening, and the mountain began, very quietly, to sing.
