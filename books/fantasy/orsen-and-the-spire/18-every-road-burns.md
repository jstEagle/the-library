## 18. Every Road Burns

The collar came apart the way a lie does—slowly, then all at once.

One moment I was kneeling on the rite-floor with my wrists bound in silver and my name being read out by a man who'd never earned the right to say it; the next, the dark moon's shadow slid across the great oculus above us, and every storm I had ever swallowed woke up at once.

I don't remember standing. I remember the floor remembering it used to be sky.

The marble didn't crack so much as *leave*—a ring of it peeling upward around me like a flower deciding to bloom in reverse—and through that gap came the sound I'd been hearing in my dreams for weeks: wind with a voice, thunder with a memory, rain that knew my mother's name. The binding runes on my wrists glowed white-hot and then simply gave up, which is the most relatable thing any piece of Spire craftsmanship has ever done.

The Archmage stopped mid-intonation. For a heartbeat the whole vast chamber went quiet enough that I could hear the caged spirits far below us, screaming up through nine floors of stone like voices through water.

Then everything happened at once.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about holding a war inside your body: it doesn't wait politely for you to decide what to do with it. It moves. It has opinions. It has been locked in a cellar for three hundred years and it would very much like to speak to the management.

I came off the rite-floor in a column of lightning, or the lightning came off me—I genuinely couldn't tell you which—and the first thing I saw clearly was Vael Corrane, ten feet away, sword drawn, face doing something complicated.

"Inquisitor," I said, because even mid-apocalypse I have manners. "You're blocking my exit."

"You were supposed to kneel." His voice had the flat calm of a man reciting doctrine to keep from screaming. "You were given terms. You *agreed*—"

"I lied," I said. "It's kind of my whole thing. Ask anyone."

He lunged. I'll give him this: he was fast, and he was good, and if I'd been the woman who walked into this building an hour ago he'd have won. But the woman walking out of it was carrying three centuries of imprisoned weather, and when his blade came down the air between us turned to glass and his sword hit it and shattered along its whole length into glittering rain.

He stared at the hilt in his hand. Then at me. Somewhere behind us, the Archmage was shouting words of unbinding and the wards overhead—the great luminous lattice that had held this realm together since before my grandmother's grandmother—flickered like a lamp running dry.

"The wards," Corrane said. Not to me. To himself. "If she's loose, the wards—"

"Are already dying," said a voice from the cage-heart stairwell, and there stood Kess, soot-faced, in a laborer's smock two sizes too big, holding a pry bar like it was a sword and looking like a man who had run nine floors to be exactly this late. "Jude! Told you I'd come."

"You told me you'd stay in Hollowmere," I said.

"I lied." He shrugged. "It's kind of your whole thing. Figured I'd try it."

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to cry. I did neither, because the ceiling chose that moment to come down.

* * *

Let me describe the Spire as it died, because someone should write it down and apparently that someone is me.

It had stood for a thousand years, white stone and silver filigree, the tallest thing in any direction, the place where the realm's weather was negotiated and its mages were made and its inconvenient people were disappeared. Every ward that kept the border storms tame, every charm that kept the plague-winds out of the lowlands, every gentle rain over every farmer's field—it all drew from one source, though almost no one below knew it: the spirits chained in the deep vaults, drained drop by drop, century by century, to keep the lights on upstairs.

And now the drain was open from both ends.

Stone groaned. Windows burst outward in sheets. Through the great oculus poured the actual night sky, black-moon dark, and against it the wards burned like frost on a windowpane—beautiful, thin, going. Down in the vaults the cages were breaking one by one; I could feel each release like a struck bell in my sternum, and each freed spirit came *up*, streaming through the Spire's bones toward the only door left open in the whole structure.

Me.

"Jude." Kess reached me through the smoke, grabbed my arm, immediately regretted it—my skin was throwing sparks like a flint in the dark—and did not let go anyway. That's Kess. "Talk to me. How bad?"

"I'm a jug with a hole in it," I said. "Everything they ever caged down there wants out, and I'm the nearest out."

"So we get you outside."

"It's not—" Lightning crawled up my throat and I tasted ozone and old grief. "Kess, they're not just *in* me. They're talking to me. They've been talking to me since the spirit roads. And what they're saying—" I looked at him. He deserved the truth even now, especially now. "They remember the war. All of it. And some of what they remember, they did."

"Then we figure that out after we're not standing in a collapsing tower."

There was a reason I'd kept this man alive for seven years of bad roads and worse company.

We ran. Or rather, we ran and I floated alternately, because my relationship with gravity had become negotiable. Behind us the rite-floor was a crater full of fleeing acolytes; ahead of us the grand stair had split in half, so I put a hand out and the wind carried us across the gap like a rumor. Below, through shattered floors, I could see the vault levels—cage after cage of storm-light winking out, and with each one the Spire shuddered like a man losing blood.

Corrane followed us. Of course he did. He'd lost his sword, gained a fallen acolyte's blade, and pursued us down the service stairs with the tireless, joyless competence that had made him famous. Twice he nearly had us. Once he actually had Kess, blade at his throat, and shouted up at me:

"Halloran! Surrender and he lives!"

"He was always going to live," I called back, and dropped a thunderclap between them that threw Corrane twenty feet into a wall. "Sorry!" I said, meaning it about forty percent.

But here's the thing about Vael Corrane that I learned that night, the thing I still think about: he got up. Bleeding, half-deaf, doctrine in ruins around him—he got *up*, and he kept coming, and he wasn't coming for me anymore. I could see it in how he moved. He'd stopped looking at where I was and started looking at where the Archmage's voice was coming from, up near the ward-crown, where the old man was trying to hold the lattice together by main force and failing.

Because here was the arithmetic Corrane couldn't avoid: the wards were fed by the cages. The cages were breaking. And the only person in the world who might hold what came out of them was the heretic he'd spent a year hunting.

His entire life was a ledger, and the columns had just refused to add.

* * *

We broke out onto the ward-crown terrace at the top of the Spire into weather that shouldn't have existed indoors, which is to say all of it. Wind from four directions arguing. Rain falling upward. The great lattice overhead burning itself out strand by strand, and beyond it, visible now through the haze, the Thornfell mountains fifty miles west, and beyond *them* the border-storms stirring like dogs smelling dinner.

The Archmage stood at the crown's center, hands raised, sustaining a working older than nations, and he was *losing*. Sweat cut channels through the ash on his face. When he saw me he didn't look surprised. That was somehow the worst part.

"There she is," he said, almost fondly. "The Halloran girl. Do you know how long I waited for you? Your grandmother's line, tended like an orchard. Your exile—do you imagine that was punishment? Exile was *ripening*. A weapon grows strongest in poor soil."

"I read the margins," I said. "Hollowmere. Your prophet kept excellent notes."

"The prophet was a fool who mistook observation for permission." He turned back to the lattice, straining. "You don't understand what you've broken, girl. Those wards are the only thing between this realm and the storms of the old war. Unleash what's in you and everything east of Thornfell drowns. Hold it, and you die, and it unleashes anyway. There is no version of tonight where you win."

"Yeah," I said. "I got that part. I'm working on a third option."

"There is no third option."

"See, everyone keeps saying that." I flexed my hands and lightning pooled in my palms like water. "Everyone's been saying it my whole life. The trial said it. The exile said it. The prophecy said it—two endings, save or destroy, pick one. And I spent a long time thinking those were the only pages in the book." I took a step toward him. "Turns out the book had margins."

He lashed out—not at me, at Kess, a bolt of raw ward-force meant to take my partner off the terrace and settle the negotiation. I caught it. All of it. It went into the storm in my chest and the storm *sang*, and for a moment the Archmage and I stood connected by his own power, and I did the thing I hadn't planned, the thing the spirits had been whispering to me about since the spirit roads.

I showed him.

Not my memories. Theirs. Three hundred years of the vault: the first spirit taken, back when the war was real and the cages seemed like mercy; the second, taken when the war was over and the cages seemed like caution; the tenth, the fiftieth, taken when the war was a story and the cages were just *revenue*. The old war itself, seen from inside it—the Spire's founders provoking it, feeding it, needing an enemy vast enough to justify building a prison-vault under a holy mountain. Generations of Archmages, each one taught the same comforting arithmetic: the realm needs protecting, therefore the cages must be filled, therefore we must never let the realm stop needing protection.

Corrane saw it too. He'd come up the last stairs behind us, blade ready, and the memory hit him the way it hits everyone—like drowning in someone else's country. I watched him stand in the middle of three hundred years of manufactured fear, and I watched the ledger in his eyes finally, finally balance.

"No," he said quietly. It wasn't denial. It was mourning.

"Inquisitor," the Archmage said, without turning, still holding the fraying lattice. "Kill her. Whatever she showed you, it's a weapon's trick. Kill her and we contain this."

"She showed me the founding ledgers," Corrane said. "Vault requisitions. Spirit counts. My own order's hand, in ink eight centuries old." He looked at his blade. At the old man. Back. "I've read those archives my whole life. They were sealed. I thought it was reverence."

"It *was* reverence—"

"It was a *cellar door*." And Vael Corrane, truest believer the Spire ever produced, crossed the terrace and put his sword through his Archmage's back.

* * *

I want to tell you it felt like justice. It didn't. It felt like a door closing on a room where a man had lived his whole life, and the man inside had just realized the room was a cell, and the key had arrived one lifetime too late to matter.

The Archmage slid down the crown-stone, the great working dying with him, and the last strands of the ward-lattice went out overhead like a festival ending. Silence, for one impossible breath—the first true silence the Spire had known in a thousand years.

Then the sky remembered it was empty, and started filling.

Corrane knelt beside the man he'd served, and didn't look at me, and said, in the voice of a man reading his own sentence: "The wards are gone. Everything you're holding—if you let go, the border storms come through within a season. Maybe less."

"I know."

"And if you hold it?"

"I know that too."

He finally looked up. Ash in his beard, blood on his hands that wasn't his, and something in his face I'd spent a year assuming he didn't have: grief, freely offered, at his own expense. "Then whatever you're about to do down there," he said, nodding at the cage-heart stairwell, the deep vault, the broken bells of the spirit cages, "do it fast. I'll hold the terrace. Someone should stand guard over the Spire's corpse, and I'm the only one left who knows all its doors."

"Why?" I asked. "Why help me?"

"Because you showed me the truth instead of killing me when you had a dozen chances." He rose, picked up his ruined blade, and set it point-down on the stone like a grave marker. "That's more mercy than my order ever taught me. Consider it a debt. My order taught me debts most of all."

Kess tugged my sleeve. "Jude. The vault. What's down there?"

"The heart," I said. "The first cage. The oldest one. And the thing the prophecy never wrote an ending for, because the prophet couldn't see past her own blood." I looked at my partner—at the soot and the pry bar and the seven years of loyalty he'd carried without once asking what I was—and I lied to him one last time, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

"Wait here. This part I do alone."

"Jude—"

"Guard the stairs," I said. "Both of you. However long it takes."

And I stepped off the edge of the terrace into the dark, and the storm carried me down, down through the dying spine of the Spire, toward the heart of the cage that had been waiting for me before I was born—

—and far below, in the oldest dark, something ancient felt me coming, and began, softly, to sing my name.
