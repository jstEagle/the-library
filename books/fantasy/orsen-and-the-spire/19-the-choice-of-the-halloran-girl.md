## 19. The Choice of the Halloran Girl

The heart of the Spire was not what I had imagined in seven years of not imagining it.

I had imagined a vault, maybe. A dungeon with better masonry. Something with chains and iron and the smell of old cruelty. What I found instead was a garden.

A round chamber at the Spire's root, its ceiling lost somewhere above in a haze of gold light, and rising from the floor—growing from it, the way a tree grows from soil—were cages. Not iron. Glass, or something like glass, blown into shapes like seed pods, each one taller than a man, each one holding a storm.

They were beautiful. That's the thing I couldn't get past, standing there with Corrane's blood still on my knuckles and the whole tower shaking around me. They were beautiful, and they were screaming.

I could hear them now the way I'd never been able to hear anything before. Since the rite had cracked open whatever the Archmage had spent nineteen years nailing shut inside me, the world came in loud. Every ward in the building sang its dying note. Every spirit in every pod turned toward me like flowers toward a window.

*Daughter,* they said. They'd been saying it since the spirit roads. *Daughter, daughter, daughter.* A hundred voices layered over each other, some of them crackling like dry brush, some rolling low as thunder over water, one of them—a small one, near the top—whistling thin and high, the way wind does through a keyhole when the house is empty.

"I'm not your daughter," I said out loud, because apparently I was the kind of person who argued with weather now. "I'm the girl you got written into a book. There's a difference."

The storms didn't answer. Storms never do. They just kept screaming, and the pods kept glowing that sickly harvest-gold, and above me, through however many floors of collapsing mage-order architecture, I could hear Kess doing something brave and stupid, because that was the only mode he had.

I'd left him behind at Blackmoon Ford. I'd left him a book and a lie and a goodbye I hadn't said properly, because if I'd said it properly I would have meant it, and meaning it would have stopped me from walking into the Spire in chains. And then he'd shown up anyway—infiltrated the place dressed as a laborer, mapped its wards, gotten himself nearly killed twice that I knew of—and I'd felt, in the middle of everything, in the middle of the Archmage's death-rite and Corrane's blade turning and the wards coming down like a curtain rod snapping, something so furious and so fond that I thought: *well. That settles it. Whatever I choose, I choose it knowing he'll be angry at me for choosing it.*

That's love, I think. Or at least it's the version I've got.

* * *

Let me back up, because the last hour happened fast and I want to remember it slowly.

The rite chamber was three levels up. The Archmage—Serevan, his name was Serevan, though by the end he barely answered to it—had me bound to a dais with collars keyed to my own magic, which is a special kind of cruelty: your power turned into your leash. He'd been monologuing. Of course he'd been monologuing. Nineteen years of planning, a century of institutional rot, and when it finally came to the moment, he wanted an audience.

"The war was ours," he'd told me, almost gently, while the dark moon climbed past the skylight and the pods below began to pulse. "There was no invasion. No storm-demon horde. We made them up, or our founders did, to justify the caging. Fear builds better foundations than stone. And every generation we needed the story fresher, so we needed—" and here he'd looked at me with something I refused to call tenderness—"someone new. Someone who could walk among the spirits and be believed. The Halloran line was bred for it. Your grandmother's grandmother was the first attempt. You are simply the first success."

"You exiled me," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Seven years ago. You branded me heretical."

"We released you," he said. "Into the wild, to grow. Exile is just a greenhouse with bad weather."

I want you to understand what that did to me. Seven years. Seven years of cold passes and cheap whiskey and learning to sleep without lightning under my skin, telling myself the story of the wronged woman, the unjustly cast-out woman, and the whole time I'd been livestock on a long pasture. Even my suffering had been scheduled.

Corrane had been there. Standing guard at the rite with his ledger and his sincere gray eyes, believing every word, because Vael Corrane was the rarest and most dangerous kind of fanatic: the kind who was right about the facts and wrong about everything else. And then the storm-memory hit him—the spirits, freed enough by the failing rite to pour their recollection into anyone standing too close—and I watched him see it. The war that never was. The villages. The centuries of caged screaming dressed up as salvation.

He turned his blade on the Archmage before I even asked. I didn't ask. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever book gets written about this night: I did not ask him to. He chose. People can do that. It took me twenty-six years and a prophecy to learn it, but people can do that.

Serevan died badly, mid-incantation, his great working half-finished, and a half-finished working to drain a hundred storm-spirits into a vessel that then walked away—that doesn't just stop. It *unravels*. Which is how I ended up descending into the Spire's root while the tower came apart around me, following the pull in my chest like a fish on a line.

Which brings us back to the garden.

* * *

Here's the problem, laid out plainly, because I laid it out plainly to myself while the ceiling groaned:

Option one: free the spirits. Open every pod, let them loose unbound into the world. The prophecy's first ending. *And the Halloran girl shall break the vessels, and the storms shall return to the sky, and the realm shall know freedom.* What the prophecy did not put in its pretty marginalia was that those storms had been caged for four hundred years, and caged things come out mad. The wards protecting the realm from the actual wild weather beyond the Thornfells were fed by these same spirits. Free them all at once, unshaped, furious, and the wards shatter, and the storms pour through the gap, and the borderlands drown. Maybe the whole realm. Freedom, delivered like a landslide.

Option two: leave them caged. Preserve the order, patch the wards, keep the machine running. The prophecy's second ending. *Or she shall kneel, and the Spire shall stand, and the age of quiet shall continue.* Except the machine required a vessel, required breeding girls and branding them heretics and calling it husbandry, and I had already spent seven years paying interest on that loan. Also the machine was currently on fire. So option two was less a choice than a eulogy.

And option three—

There was no option three. That was the whole point of the prophecy. I'd read it in Hollowmere's ashes, every annotation, every crossed-out line. The prophet—my ancestor, my blood, the woman whose grave I'd stood on two nights ago and cursed—had written two roads and only two. She'd been very thorough about the cage she built out of ink. Two endings, both hers, both designed to funnel the Halloran girl exactly here, exactly now, to this room, to this choice between ruin and kneeling.

But the prophet had made one mistake, and it was the same mistake the Archmage made, and it's the mistake everyone makes about storms.

She assumed the storm stays outside the house.

* * *

I walked to the nearest pod and put my hand against the glass.

It was warm. Warmer than glass should be, warm like a forehead, warm like fever. Inside, the storm pressed against my palm—not attacking, not testing. *Recognizing.* Like a dog pressing its head into a hand. Like something that has waited so long for touch that it has forgotten how to do anything else with it.

"Hey," I said. My voice cracked on the second word. "Hey. I know. I know."

Four hundred years. Some of them had been in these pods for four hundred years, harvested from the wild during the fake war, told they were demons, told their nature was a crime. I had done seven years of exile and thought myself an expert on the subject. I was a tourist.

Above me, something large gave way several floors up. Dust sifted down through the golden light. Somewhere in the wreck of the upper tower, Kess was alive—I was sure of it the way you're sure of your own heartbeat, which is to say I decided to be sure and called it knowledge—and every minute I stood here philosophizing was a minute the collapse worked its way down toward him.

"Here's my problem," I told the storm in the pod. It flickered, listening, or maybe just flickering. "If I open all of you at once, you come out the way you are right now. Furious. Four centuries of furious. And there are people down in the valley—stupid, ordinary people, the fence who shorted me on coin, the innkeeper who watered the whiskey, kids, farmers—who didn't cage anybody. They'll be under you when you land."

The storm flared. Not in anger. In *agreement*, which was worse, because it meant the storm knew. It knew what it was now, and it hated itself for it, and it would still come out of that pod like a hammer, because that's what four hundred years does.

"So I can't open the door," I said. "But I can't leave it shut. And I can't kneel, because I've met the people who'd have me kneel, and I've read the family book, and I am *done* being written by dead women and live archmages."

I looked at my hand against the glass. At the faint light passing through my palm, storm-light and skin-light, no longer clearly separate.

"There's a third road," I said. "You won't like it. Neither will I."

Because here's what I'd learned in the spirit roads, walking beneath Thornfell with the whispers saying *daughter*: the storms didn't want a jailer or a liberator. They wanted a *sky*. They'd been trying to tell me that the whole time, in their thunder-language, and I'd been too human to hear it. They didn't need to be released *into* the world. They needed somewhere to *be*—something vast enough to hold all that fury and grief and whistling loneliness without breaking. The sky used to be that. The sky had failed them, or the Spire had failed on the sky's behalf, same thing.

But there was another vessel in the room. One bred for it across five generations. One already half-open since the rite cracked her bindings. One who had spent seven years practicing emptiness, holding herself hollow so no one would notice the lightning missing, and who understood—better than any vessel ever built—exactly how much room there is inside a person who has learned to make herself small.

I could carry them. All of them. Into myself, shaped by choice instead of force, held by will instead of glass. The wards would keep their feed—not the pods, but me; I'd become the well the Spire drank from, except I'd be a well that could walk away, a well with opinions. The realm's protections would hold, frayed but holding, because I would hold them. And the storms wouldn't come out unbound and mad. They'd come home to a body that had chosen them.

The cost was not subtle. I want to be honest about that, because everyone in this story has lied to me and I refuse to join the club. I would not be Jude-with-a-little-extra. I would be Jude-and-storm, a shared country, and borders shift in countries like that. Some days I might be mostly me. Some days mostly weather. I might forget the taste of whiskey. I might forget Kess's laugh, or worse, keep it and lose the feeling that went with it. The prophecy's author had never written this ending because I don't think she could bear to imagine it: a granddaughter who solved the puzzle by dissolving the puzzler.

But she also never wrote me choosing anything. Both her endings had the Halloran girl as material—breaker of vessels or kneeling vessel, an instrument either way. This one was mine. Ugly, unwritten, mine.

"Well," I said to the storm, and to the hundred others gone silent and attentive behind it, the whole garden leaning in. "Anybody object?"

The small one near the top whistled. It sounded, impossibly, like laughter.

"Yeah," I said. "Me neither."

* * *

Breaking the pods was easy. That surprised me. After everything—the years, the book, the breeding program, the rite—you'd think the final act would cost effort. But the Archmage's half-finished working had already loosened every seal in the room, and more than that: the pods weren't locked. They'd never been locked, not really. They were held shut by the spirits' own despair, the way a fist stays closed longest when it's forgotten it's a hand. All I did was remind them.

Glass burst upward in a hundred gold blossoms. And the storms came out.

Not as hammers. As *water finding a channel*—and the channel was me.

I won't describe it accurately because accuracy isn't possible, but I'll try: it was like being born, if being born remembered everything. Four hundred years poured into the space where Jude Halloran used to fit, and Jude Halloran expanded—or didn't expand, exactly, but *deepened*, the way a valley deepens when the river cuts it, keeping her shape by letting the shape erode into something truer. I felt every cage. I felt the founder's hands on the first captured storm, the lies told in the founding councils, the villages burned to feed the fear that fed the order. I felt the small storm's four hundred years of whistling through its own keyhole. I felt my grandmother's grandmother, the first attempt, the one who hadn't survived the breeding, and I grieved her with borrowed memory and it was still my grief.

And underneath all of it, threaded through it, holding it the way a riverbank holds a river: me. Wry, stubborn, smuggler-grade me. *This far and no farther,* I told the storms, the way you'd tell a flood. *You get the valleys I give you. You get the whiskey memories and the bad jokes and the pass at dawn. You get Kess.*

Oh, Kess. I gave them Kess like a treasure. Here, I said, pouring it into the shared country of myself: this is what his face does when he thinks he's being sly. This is the debt he never told me about, paying off a fence in Thornfell to cover my tracks. This is him reading my name in a dead woman's book and closing it and going after me anyway, because that's who he is, that's the whole of who he is, loyalty with a limp and a smart mouth.

The storms took him in like rain takes a seed. *Him,* they said, wondering. *Daughter-of-him.*

"Not quite," I said, laughing, crying, both of it happening in lightning. "But close enough."

The wards caught. I felt them catch—felt the Spire's dying lattice find me at its root and drink, and I let it, a sip not a drain, my terms now, my rate. Above me the collapse slowed. Didn't stop—the order was finished regardless, its machinery broken, its secret out—but the tower would fall as ruins, not as a weapon. The realm's protections frayed and held. Frayed and held. I would spend the rest of my existence being the stitch in that fabric, and I said yes to that too, eyes open, nobody's instrument.

Then I went to find Kess, because I had promised myself one thing before the storms finished moving in: I would say the goodbye properly this time. Face to face. While I was still mostly me.

* * *

I found him in the wreckage of the rite chamber, hauling rubble off a trapped acolyte who'd probably earned it and probably hadn't—who knows, who cares, that was always Kess's arithmetic: dig first, judge later. He saw me and his face did the thing, the not-sly thing, relief and fury fighting for the same real estate.

"You're supposed to be gone," he said. "You traded yourself. You left me a *book*, Jude, you absolute—"

"I know."

"—cowardly, self-sacrificing, *infuriating*—"

"I know, Kess."

"—and now the tower's falling and you're *glowing*, why are you glowing, what did you *do*?"

What did I do. I opened my mouth to explain—to say *I'm carrying a hundred storms now, I'm the third ending, I'm leaving, I'm sorry, I love you, I wrote none of this and all of it*—and what came out instead was light. Just a little. A leak at the seams. His face when he saw it: that's the picture I'm keeping, the one I'll hold in the shared country on the days the weather wins. Not horror. Grief, yes, arriving early for a funeral I hadn't admitted to yet. But under the grief, steady as bedrock: *there she is.*

"Jude," he said carefully, the way you talk to someone standing on ice. "Talk to me."

"Can't stay long," I said. "Weather to attend to."

"Don't." His voice broke on it. "Don't do the wry thing. Not now. Not for this."

So I didn't. I crossed the rubble and I held his face in my hands—my hands, still hands, still mine, sparks jumping between my fingers and his jaw like the world's smallest thunderstorm—and I said the goodbye properly, the whole ugly truth of it, the carrying and the cost and the road I was taking out of the world's maps. He argued. Of course he argued. He had three good points and I countered none of them, because being right wasn't the currency anymore; being *chosen* was, and I had chosen, and he knew it, and it broke him and healed him in the same breath, the way only true things do.

When I stepped back, the light was winning. I could feel the small storm whistling near the top of me, and the big ones rolling, and all of it settling into its valleys, learning the country.

"Find me," Kess said. Not a question. An order, from a man with no authority whatsoever, and the most leverage of anyone alive.

"I'll send weather," I said.

And I walked out of the Spire through walls that had stopped being walls, into the dark before dawn, wearing the storm like a coat that wore me back—while somewhere behind me, in the ruins, a man with a limp started digging through the rubble of the only life he'd known, looking for a book, a body, a footprint, anything to prove the woman he loved had walked out of legend and into the mountains.

He'd find the footprints. He was always going to find the footprints.

But that's his chapter to tell.
