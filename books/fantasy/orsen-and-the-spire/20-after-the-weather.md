## 20. After the Weather

The Spire had always looked like it was holding itself up out of spite. Now it looked like it had lost the argument.

I stood in the shadow of the outer wall—what was left of the outer wall—and tried to think of something clever to say about it, because that's what I do. That's what I've always done when the world gets too big for me. You crack a joke at the funeral so you don't have to be the one crying into the casserole.

Nothing came.

The tower's crown was gone. Not toppled—*gone*, sheared off clean as a whittled stick, and the stump of it smoked gently in the morning light like a candle someone had pinched between wet fingers. The great wards that had hung over the valley for three hundred years, shimmering like heat-haze made visible, were fraying. I could see them now without any mage-sight at all, which seemed like a bad sign. Threads of pale light unraveling off the mountains, drifting apart like cobwebs in wind. But holding. Barely, grudgingly, the way an old rope holds after half its strands have snapped—still bearing weight, just no longer pretending it could bear much more.

The realm wasn't ending. I want to be clear about that, because in the first hour after the sky stopped screaming, everyone in the valley town below kept asking me if it was, as though the smuggler who'd once sold them watered-down brandy would know. The realm wasn't ending. It was just *changed*. The weather came different now. Storms rolled through on their own schedule, uninvited, unbound, and the Spire's remaining mages ran around with their instruments trying to predict them and failing, because you can't put a leash on something and then act surprised it doesn't heel.

I'd come up the mountain with a labor crew. That was the joke of it—the Spire, seat of the mage order, arbiter of heresy, breaker of my partner's life, hiring common hands from the valley town to haul rubble at two bits a day. Nobody recognized me. Why would they? I was one more broad-shouldered fool with a barrow, and the mages were too busy staring at their cracked scrying-glasses to look at anything with feet.

Jude would have found that funny. Jude found most things funny right up until they killed you.

I tipped the first barrow of broken wardstone over the edge of the work site and told myself I was there for the coin. Two bits a day plus bread was two bits a day plus bread, and Kess Marchetti had never met honest wages he didn't like. Then I told myself I was there for answers, which was closer. Then, somewhere around noon of the third day, standing in a courtyard full of scorched flagstones with my gloves full of somebody's melted door hinges, I admitted what I'd known since the moment I read her name in that prophecy book in a waystation outside Blackmoon Ford:

I was there for her.

And I knew—I knew the way you know a rigged dice game the second you see the cup—exactly what I'd find.

* * *

Let me back up. Because if I'm going to tell this properly, I have to start with the night the sky tore open, and I was three hundred miles away doing the stupidest thing I've ever done, which is saying something given my line of work.

After the ford, after Jude traded herself to Corrane's chains and slipped loose somewhere between the river and the Spire—after all that, I did the only thing left to me. I went to the Spire too. Not as a laborer then. As a nobody with forged papers and a strong back, because the Spire always needs strong backs and never looks at them. I spent eleven days hauling grain sacks and wardstone in the underlevels, learning corridors, counting guards, telling myself I was going to get her out.

I hadn't worked out how yet. Details. I'm a details man but even I need more than eleven days.

Then the dark moon rose, and the rite began, and every bell in the Spire rang wrong—a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it in my ears—and the whole mountain started to shake like a dog shedding water. I remember running. I remember the storm coming down *inside*, through the corridors, lightning walking the walls like it owned them, which by that point it did. I remember the spirit-cage heart chamber, or the doorway of it anyway, before the heat drove me back—remember seeing a woman made of white fire standing in a cage of black iron, and the iron coming apart around her like frost off a window.

I remember thinking, with the part of my brain that never shuts up even at the end of the world: *that's my partner. She looks good.*

Then the roof of the world came off, and I woke up three days later in a valley farmhouse with my eyebrows burned off and a farmer's wife feeding me broth and asking if I'd seen her cat.

That's the version I gave people. It's even mostly true. What I left out was the last thing I saw before the flash—took me weeks to dig it out of my memory, the way you dig a splinter, slow and wincing. In the heart chamber, in the white fire, she'd turned. Looked straight at where I stood in the doorway, through smoke and lightning and three hundred years of prophecy, and she'd smiled.

Not a goodbye smile. A *gotcha* smile. Like she'd known I wouldn't stay gone. Like she was already writing the next chapter and I was in it whether I liked it or not.

I hated her for that. For about four seconds. Then I loved her for it for the rest of my life, probably.

* * *

The labor crew worked the outer ring, and the outer ring told me nothing except that the Spire employed far more people than it fed and paid far fewer than it promised. So I started volunteering for inner jobs. Rubble detail in the collapsed east archive. Cart duty down the spirit roads' sealed entrances—sealed now, fused shut with glass, whatever had happened down there having happened thoroughly. Guard escort for the assessors, gray-robed survivors of the old order picking through the wreckage of their own power like gulls on a gutted fish.

They talked. Mages talk when they're frightened, and these ones were very frightened, because their order had ruled the realm for centuries and in one night a smuggler woman had taken the lid off everything. They argued in the courtyards about whether the Archmage had died in the rite—nobody could produce a body—or fled, or been, according to one whispering acolyte I overheard near the west stair, "struck down by the inquisitor Vael Corrane himself."

Corrane. The name landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. I'd heard enough about him from Jude's sleep-talk, from the waystation gossip, from the wanted-broadsheets with his severe inked face on them. The fanatic. The true believer. The man who'd hunted her across half the realm because his book said she'd end the world.

The whisperer said Corrane had turned his blade on the Archmage in the middle of the collapse. Said he'd seen the storms' memories himself—whatever that meant—and come out of them a different man. Said he now walked the ruins unarmed, helping pull survivors out, saying nothing to anyone, wearing the expression of a man doing penance arithmetic he'd never finish.

I found him on the ninth day.

He was in the shattered cloister, alone, stacking bodies. Not many left to stack by then—the living had been carried out weeks ago—but there were corners the crews avoided, superstitious, and Corrane worked those corners himself. He'd taken off the inquisitor's coat. He was just a big tired man in a gray shirt lifting the dead like they weighed something, which they did, all of them.

We looked at each other across the cloister for a long moment. He knew what I was. I could see it land—Kess, the partner, the smuggler who'd come sniffing through the ruins for six days straight asking questions dressed as small talk.

"You're looking for her," he said. Not accusing. Just tired.

"I'm looking for a grave," I said. "People deserve graves."

"She isn't in one." He set down the body he was carrying—gently, I'll give him that, gently—and wiped his hands on his thighs. "I was here. At the end. Closer than anyone." His jaw worked. "She took them into herself. All of them. Every storm the Spire caged for three hundred years, every spirit, every war they remembered. There wasn't room left over for—" He gestured vaguely at the air where a person might be. "There wasn't a person left standing afterward. Just weather, walking away."

"You saw her walk away."

"I saw lightning make footprints." He said it flatly, like a report. Like a man reporting exactly what he saw and not one thing more, because that was the discipline he'd built his whole life on and it was all he had left. "Fused glass, out past the south wall. Footprints burned into it. Heading for Thornfell." He met my eyes. "I followed them to the tree line. They kept going. I didn't."

"Why not?"

Something moved behind his face—grief, maybe, or shame, or both sharing a coat. "Because whatever walks out of that kind of fire isn't done becoming what it's going to be. And because I spent a year hunting a woman based on what a book said she'd be." He picked the body back up. "I'm retired from reading other people's endings."

I should have hit him. Part of me wants to tell you I did. But he carried her enemies' dead with his own hands, and he'd handed me the only true thing anyone in that ruin knew, so I turned around and walked out of the cloister with my throat closed up tight as a knot.

* * *

The south wall. Dusk, tenth day.

The assessors had mapped the damage by then; I'd stolen a look at their charts. The blast pattern radiated outward from the heart chamber in rings, each ring stranger than the last. Closest in: stone fused to glass. Farther out: stone simply *gone*, unmade, as though it had been asked politely to stop existing. And out past the south wall, in a field of slagged granite where the spirit-cage's outer anchors had torn free, there was a line of prints.

I'd told myself I'd know them when I saw them. I told myself a lot of things that week.

They were small. That undid me first—before the scorch, before the impossible physics of it, just the plain fact that her boots had always been a little too big for her and she'd refused, for seven years, on principle, to buy ones that fit. The prints were burned into the glass-dark stone maybe half an inch deep, edges crisp, each one ringed with a faint halo of discoloration like heat-shadow. Step. Step. Step. Perfectly even spacing. Whatever had walked here hadn't stumbled, hadn't hurried, hadn't hesitated.

It had walked out of the ruin of everything the Spire ever built, at a stroll, heading home.

I knelt beside the first print and put my hand in it. The stone was warm. Tenth day, autumn nights cold enough to see your breath, and the stone was warm.

I sat down in the slag field and did the ugly crying, the kind with snot in it, the kind you'd kill a man over if he mentioned it later. Mourning without a grave is a strange animal. Grief needs somewhere to go—a headstone, an urn, a jacket left hanging on a chair. I had none of it. No body, no blood, not so much as a boot. I had a line of warm holes in the ground pointing at a mountain range, and a prophecy book in my pack with her name in it, and a smile through white fire that said *wait for me* without saying wait for me, because Jude Halloran never promised anybody anything she couldn't weasel out of later.

So I mourned the way you mourn fog. Big, shapeless, everywhere, and gone the moment you tried to hold it.

A while later—it might have been an hour, it might have been most of the night—I got up, and I took out my knife, and I cut a mark into the glass-stone beside the first footprint. Just a smuggler's sign, the one we scratch on fence posts and bridge pilings: *road open, safe passage, friend passed this way.* Stupid. Sentimental. She'd never see it. Probably.

I carved it anyway. Then another, at the tree line. Then, over the following months—for I stayed, you understand, I stayed and ran the passes again like always—one at every crossroads from the Spire road to the Thornfell high country. If some part of her came walking home someday, half storm and half the stubbornest woman I ever knew, I wanted her to find the roads marked. Wanted her to know somebody'd kept the lantern lit.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the first winter of the new weather, and it came sideways and loud. The fraying wards held over the valley towns—they say the spirits themselves shore them up now, out of respect, or guilt, or some third thing nobody's got a word for—but the high passes answered to no one. Storms walked where they liked. Caravans hired guides or died. Business, as my dear departed fence used to say, has never been better for the foolish and the faithful.

I ran the passes alone. I'd always half-known I would, eventually. Partners are a luxury for men whose partners stay bought.

Except.

On the ridge above Hollowmere, the dead monastery where she'd learned the truth about herself, I stopped to shake the rain out of my hat, and the rain stopped. Not eased. *Stopped*, mid-fall, all around me, hanging in the air like beads on a string, and the wind went quiet, and the whole grey afternoon held its breath.

I stood very still and said, feeling like an idiot: "Jude?"

Far off down the valley, one single thunderclap. Clear sky. One clap, like a knock. Like a hand rapped twice on a door: *still here. keep talking.*

I laughed until my ribs hurt, standing in a pocket of suspended rain on a haunted mountain, and then I talked to her for an hour about nothing—the price of tin, the state of the passes, the assessor who'd tried to shortchange me, the cat I'd finally found for the farmer's wife—and the storm held off around me the whole time, polite as a friend waiting outside a shop.

When I finally shouldered my pack and headed down, the rain resumed, gentle, falling behind me in a line. Following, maybe. Or seeing me home.

I don't know what she is now. Neither does she, I'd bet the whole caravan on it. But the passes are mine, and the stories are mine to carry, and every traveler who hires Kess Marchetti hears about the woman made of weather who broke the Spire and walked into Thornfell, and every one of them laughs, and every one of them pays extra for the high route anyway, just in case.

And on the highest ridges, when the clouds stack up dark and the air goes electric, I say her name out loud, into the wind—

—and lately, more and more often, the lightning says it back.
