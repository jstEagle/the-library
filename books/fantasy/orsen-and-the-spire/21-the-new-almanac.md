## 21. The New Almanac

The trouble with running the passes alone, I've decided, is that there's nobody to lie to but yourself.

Kess Halloran—no relation, don't ask—smuggler of record in the Thornfell valleys, keeper of three routes and one very tired mule named Duchess. That's me. Six months since the Spire cracked open like an egg and rained lightning on everything I thought was true, and here I am, back at it: hauling contraband through snow-choked cols, bribing border wardens who now work for nobody because there's no Spire left to pay them, and telling stories to travelers by firelight because apparently that's what grief does when it can't find a grave.

The stories are good ones, at least. I should know. I invented most of them.

"There was a woman," I tell a caravan of wool merchants huddled against the wind at Blackmoon Ford, "made out of weather. Not a mage—the mages all ran or burned—but weather itself wearing boots. She walked into the Spire with chains on her wrists and walked out of every map ever drawn."

"Did she die?" asks the youngest merchant, a girl with chapped lips and the kind of eyes that used to get people burned.

"Nobody knows," I say, which is true, and also the only honest sentence in my whole repertoire. "The wards are still up. Frayed, but holding. Somebody's holding them."

I don't tell them the rest. I don't tell them about the collar she slipped like it was a rumor, or the bounty mages who found out what a seven-year silence sounds like when it breaks, or the way Vael Corrane—inquisitor, fanatic, dead man walking—turned his blade on his own Archmage because a storm showed him the truth he'd spent his life refusing to read. I especially don't tell them that I loved her, or that she never once said it back in so many words, or that the last thing she did for me was leave me a book with my heart already broken inside it, pre-marked, like a scholar's footnote to her own betrayal.

Some cargo you don't unload. You just carry it.

* * *

Duchess hates winter. Duchess hates summer. Duchess has strong opinions about autumn and considers spring a personal insult. What I'm saying is that the mule and I understand each other completely, which is more than I can say for anyone else these days.

We run the high route in late autumn because the low routes have tolls now—new tolls, valley-council tolls, the kind of small bureaucratic evil that grows in the cracks when empires fall. The Thornfells don't care who governs the valleys. The Thornfells care about wind and rock and whether you're fool enough to cross them in the wrong month. I've been fool enough exactly enough times to know the difference between brave and stupid, and mostly I pick stupid anyway, because brave requires believing you'll come out the other side as the same person.

Six months ago I came out the other side of Hollowmere with a prophecy book under my arm and her name in my mouth and nothing else.

I'd gone after her. Let the record show. I sold everything that wasn't bolted down—including things that were—and hired on at the Spire as a laborer, hauling stone for the dark moon rite, mapping wards with charcoal on my forearms where the overseers wouldn't look. I got within two corridors of her. I know this because afterward, when the walls came down and the storms went through the halls like judgment day had finally kept its appointment, I found the corridor where they'd held her, and the scorch marks on the floor spelled a pattern I recognized from the margins of her book. She'd been writing. Even in chains, even collared, she'd been leaving notes for whoever came looking.

I came looking. I was too late by one corridor and one choice, and the choice wasn't mine to make anyway. It never was. That was the whole point of her, in the end—that the prophecy wrote two endings and she refused both, took a third road that nobody had inked, and paid for the penmanship with everything she had.

The realm's wards frayed that night. Everyone felt it—the long shudder under the world, dogs howling from the coast to the passes, old mages weeping in their towers without knowing why. But they held. They hold still. And every smuggler, farmer, and child born since then owes their unbroken sky to a woman who is either dead, transformed, or walking somewhere past the edge of every map I own.

I choose to believe the third one. It's not optimism. It's stubbornness, which is the same thing with better posture.

* * *

In the valley town—Thornhollow, they call it now; it had another name before, but names change faster than towns do—there's a tavern called the Broken Ward, because of course there is. Every town within a hundred miles of the Spire has a Broken Ward or a Fallen Spire or a Storm's Rest, and half of them claim the woman made of weather drank there once. She didn't. She never drank anywhere. I knew her for nine years and I saw her drunk exactly zero times, which in the smuggling trade is practically sainthood.

But I drink there, and I tell the stories, and I've noticed something lately that I don't love: the stories are getting away from me.

Last week a carter told *me* the tale—my own tale, third-hand, mangled—about how the storm-woman fought a hundred inquisitors on the Spire steps and turned them all to rain. There were no inquisitors on the steps. There were maybe six, and Corrane handled most of them himself before the Archmage's guards cut him down, and I will carry the image of that man choosing the right side one breath too late until I die. But the carter's version had a hundred, and thunder, and the woman laughing.

She wasn't laughing. I've imagined that night ten thousand times and she was never laughing. She was doing the thing she did with her jaw when she was terrified and refusing to show it, the thing I used to tease her about, the thing I would give a year of my life to see again even once, terrified jaw and all.

"You were there, weren't you?" the carter said, squinting at me. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a man who's carrying something heavy and calling it luggage."

I bought him a drink for that. Accurate diagnosis, terrible bedside manner.

Here's what I actually know, ledgered plain, because lying in my own head stopped working around midwinter:

One. Jude Halloran existed. Not the name in the prophecy book—I mean the person. She liked her tea bitter and her jokes dry and she slept with a knife under her pillow and her back to the wall, always, even after seven years of no one hunting us. She counted coins twice and trusted people never, except me, which was either wisdom or the worst decision of her life, and she made it daily.

Two. She was bred for this. The Spire caged storm-spirits under Thornfell and engineered her bloodline across generations to be either the cage-breaker or the new lock, and they exiled her at nineteen not as punishment but as seasoning. Everything she suffered was a recipe step. When I learned that, I understood why she'd lied to me for nine years—not about being a storm-caller, though yes, that too, but about being afraid. She thought fear was the honest part. She thought everything else was performance.

Three. She freed the spirits. All of them. And rather than let them loose unbound—rather than shatter the wards and drown the realm in the war the Spire had started to justify its own prisons—she took them into herself. Into her body. Whatever walks away from that isn't dead, exactly. But it isn't Jude Halloran drinking bitter tea, either. Or maybe it is. Maybe it's Jude Halloran plus a thousand years of remembered war, choosing, every single day, who she is now.

Four. On the highest ridge of the Thornfells, when I speak her name, lightning answers.

That last one isn't a story. That last one is the reason I keep climbing.

* * *

It started in spring, purely by accident, the way most true things happen.

I was running the high col above Hollowmere—yes, I go back; call it pilgrimage, call it stupidity, I've stopped distinguishing—and a storm front was rolling in off the western peaks, the big gray kind that makes even Duchess humble. And I don't know why, maybe altitude, maybe loneliness, maybe the particular madness of standing in a place where someone you loved became something else, but I said her name out loud. Just said it. "Jude." Like calling a partner across a campfire.

Lightning hit the ridge line forty yards east of me. Clean strike, no thunder preceding it, out of a cloud that hadn't looked ready. Duchess screamed and tried to become a smaller mule. I stood there with my hair standing on end and my heart going like a smuggler caught at a checkpoint, and I laughed until I cried, which took a while, because there was a lot stored up.

I've tested it eleven times since. Eleven for eleven. Never close enough to hurt me—always off to the side, always precise, always *answering*, the way a person answers when you say their name across a crowded room and they turn, and look, and decide whether to come over.

She decides not to come over. But she turns. I'd bet the passes on it.

* * *

Autumn again. The almanac problem.

Before she left—if "left" covers taking a thousand storms into your body and walking out of the world—she left me the book. The prophecy. The Halloran almanac, her ancestor's hand, a century of dates and names and two endings written for a girl who hadn't been born yet. I've read it so many times the spine's gone soft. I've read the marginalia until I could recite it: the cages beneath Thornfell, the breeding, the *the Halloran girl was made for release or ruin*, the cold arithmetic of a prophet who loved his descendant enough to write her future and not enough to leave her alone.

And here's the thing I figured out somewhere around the fourth reading, the thing that keeps me climbing ridges and saying her name to the sky:

The book ends. The prophecy stops. Last date, last entry, the night of the dark moon rite. After that—nothing. A century of foresight and it just... stops, like a road washed out, like the author reached the edge of what he could see and found her standing there, blocking the view.

Because she went off the page. Because the third path wasn't in him to foresee. Whatever she is now, whatever she's doing out past the maps, she's writing it herself, in weather instead of ink. An almanac of one. Rain and quiet, entry by entry.

So I do the only sensible thing a man can do when the woman he loves becomes a force of nature: I keep a ledger of her entries.

Silly? Absolutely. I mark them in the back of her book, in pencil, in my terrible hand. *Third of Frostmonth, high col above Hollowmere, answered on first call.* *Ninth, valley road near Thornhollow, distant strike, south-southeast, felt like acknowledgment.* *Twelfth, no answer, overcast, don't read into it.* Some nights I sit by the fire with a mug of bitter tea—I drink it bitter now, don't ask me why, it's not sentiment, it's just how I take it—and I add a line, and it's the closest thing to prayer I've ever managed, and I was raised by a chapel-keeper, so I know prayer when I'm doing it.

The entries are getting farther apart. Or farther away. Hard to tell distance from frequency when the other party is atmospheric. But they haven't stopped. Eleven calls, eleven answers, and last week a twelfth, and this one was different, and that's what I need to write down properly, because I've been circling it for six days and Duchess is tired of watching me pace.

* * *

The twelfth time was two days ago, on the highest ridge in the Thornfells—the one the old maps call the Crown, the one where the wind comes through so clean it feels like the world inhaling. I climbed it for no reason I'll admit to. Routine survey of a smuggling route, I told myself. In late autumn. Alone. Sure.

Storm front rolling in, big and gray and patient. I stood on the crown of the mountains with the book in my satchel and said her name.

"Jude."

The lightning came—but not the sharp single strike. This time the whole cloud lit from inside, a slow white bloom, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, or waking up. And the thunder, when it came, didn't crack. It rolled. Long and low and shaped almost—almost—like a word. I stood there in the wind with tears freezing on my face trying to hear it, telling myself I was imagining it, knowing I wasn't.

Then, at the edge of the ridge, where the rock drops away into cloud, I saw footprints.

Scorched footprints. Fused glass-bright in the stone, each print steaming faintly in the cold, trailing north along the ridge crest toward country no map I own bothers to draw. Fresh. Hours old, maybe less. Heading away from everything, the way she always did, except—

Except the stride was wrong. Shorter than hers. Careful. Like someone relearning a body, measuring it, testing each step. Like a woman made of weather practicing being a person again, one footprint at a time.

Or like someone following the same trail I've been reading for months, and finding, at the end of it, a man with a soft-spined book and a pocket full of pencil marks and absolutely nothing left to lose.

I didn't follow them that day. The light was going, and Duchess had opinions, and some part of me—the part that spent nine years learning her silences—said *not yet, not like this, she's not ready to be found and you're not ready to find her.* So I stood on the Crown until dark, and I said her name one more time, softly, not asking anything, just saying it the way you'd say it across a campfire, and the clouds overhead glowed once, gentle as a lamp turned down low.

Goodnight, I thought. Wherever you're walking, walk well.

Then I went home and sharpened my knife and repacked my kit and told Duchess, who pretended not to listen, that in the morning we were going north. Past the maps. Off the page. Into whatever almanac she's writing, rain and quiet, entry by entry—and if she wants to stay a legend, fine, legends don't need partners. But if any part of her is still counting coins twice and sleeping with her back to the wall and drinking her tea bitter, then somebody ought to be there to hold the other end of the silence.

The footprints led north. The storm front is moving north. And tomorrow, so am I.

* * *

At dawn the passes were white and empty and wide open, the way roads are when nobody's written them yet, and I swear the eastern sky flickered once—just once, quick as a wink—as if to say: *well? Keep up.*

Duchess sighed. I swung up into the saddle.

"Halloran," I said to the horizon, testing the name like a coin, and waited for the answer.

It came from ahead of us, not behind—far north, past the edge of every map, a single bright thread of lightning stitching earth to sky, like a door left open.

Like an invitation.
