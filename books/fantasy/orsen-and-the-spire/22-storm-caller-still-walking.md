## 22. Storm-Caller, Still Walking

The first thing I learned about being weather is that weather doesn't hurry.

I used to think that was the whole problem with it—storms arrived when they pleased, ruined what they liked, and left without apologizing to anyone. Seven years I'd spent being the opposite of that: punctual, quiet, small, a woman who folded herself into the shape of whatever room she was in. And then I'd spent one terrible night at the Spire being entirely storm, all thunder and no judgment, and learned that swinging between extremes is just another cage with wider bars.

So now I practice the middle thing. The hard thing.

This morning, for instance, I woke on a hillside in a country that doesn't appear on any map I've ever smuggled across, watched the sun come up through clouds I had personally arranged into something decorative, and decided—not because anyone asked me, not because a book told me to, but because I felt like it—that today I would be mostly woman.

It's a strange kind of freedom, choosing yourself daily. Nobody warns you how tiring it is. There's no prophecy for it. Believe me, I checked.

* * *

Let me back up, because I've gotten ahead of myself, and one of the things the storms taught me—screamed at me, really, in the language of pressure and light—is that a story told too fast hits like hail.

After the Spire, after the heart-cage and the choice and the burning, I walked north. Not because of anything so dramatic as exile or destiny. I walked north because Kess was asleep in the ruins with a broken arm and an expression I couldn't bear to look at yet, and because if I stayed, I would have tried to explain, and there were no words in me that weren't lightning. Every feeling I had come out as weather in those first days. I cried once—a real cry, grief-shaped—and flattened a stand of pines two valleys over. I laughed at something stupid and set three haystacks alight in a village whose name I still don't know. I sent them rain to put it out. I sent them an apology made of a perfect afternoon. It's not the same as saying sorry. Nothing is. But I'm getting better at the other kinds.

North, then. Up past Thornfell, past the passes where Kess and I used to run contraband under the noses of Spire patrols, past the last surveyor's cairn and the last shepherd who'd ever heard of the mage order. Past the edge of everything the maps bothered with.

The storms came with me. Of course they did. They live in me now—all of them, the ones I freed from the Spire's cages, the old ones, the furious ones, the ones who remember a war older than the realm. When the Archmage's rite broke and the wards came down, every spirit in that black iron heart had a choice: scatter into a sky that had forgotten them, or go somewhere warm. I was warm. I was, apparently, built warm—the Halloran girl, bred across generations by a prophet who thought she was writing a rescue and a family that didn't know it was writing a weapon. They poured themselves into me like rain into cracked ground, and I did not shatter, though I want it noted for the record that I considered it.

They talk to me still. Not in words, mostly. In weather-moods. A restlessness in my left shoulder means the eastern spirit wants to run. A heaviness behind my eyes means the deep one—the oldest, the one they called Mother-of-Squalls before the Spire took her name away—is dreaming about the sea. Some mornings I wake up homesick for places I've never been, and I've learned to let that be theirs instead of mine. We're learning to share a house, the storms and I. It's like living with very large, very old cats who occasionally knock continents off tables.

But here is the thing I did not expect, the thing no prophecy wrote in either of its tidy endings: most days, we get along fine.

* * *

The valley I've settled in—for now; I'm never settled, only camped—doesn't have a name in any tongue I speak. The people here call it something soft that sounds like water over stones. There are maybe forty households, terraced up the slopes, growing a grain I've learned to like and keeping goats with the personal gravity of minor nobility. They found me in their second autumn, half-frozen above the tree line, and instead of running or worshipping me—which, believe me, both have happened, and worship is far worse—they fed me soup and asked if I could do anything useful.

I said I knew weather.

The matriarch, a tiny iron woman named Sefa with hands like tree roots, looked me up and down and said, "Good. Our harvest festival has been rained out four years running."

That's the job, these days. That's the whole grand legend of the Storm-Caller: I keep the rain off the festival. I nudge the hail east when the barley's heavy. I hold fog on the high pasture so the wolves can't see the lambs. Once a season I climb to the ridge and let the storms out of me for an hour, all of them at once, a great rolling tantrum against nothing, because they need it and so do I, and afterward the whole valley smells washed and everyone complains cheerfully about the noise.

Sefa pays me in bread, goat cheese, and complete indifference to my history, which is the most valuable currency I've ever earned.

Last week her grandson, a boy of about eight with a permanent scowl, asked me if I was really the woman from the stories—the one who burned the Spire, the one made of lightning. The stories have traveled farther than I have, which figures. Stories travel light. I carry the storms; the storms' reputation travels free.

"I'm the one," I said.

"Did it hurt?"

"Every part of it."

He considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate. "Would you do it again?"

And here's where I'll tell you the truth, since this account is the only almanac I keep now and I promised myself I wouldn't lie in it. I thought about the heart-cage. About the spirits singing in their iron, about the Archmage's calm face as he explained that cages were mercy, that order required prisons, that some things must be held down for the good of everything above them. I thought about a trial seven years ago where my sentence was written before I walked in. About a prophecy that gave me two endings and called them choices.

"Yeah," I said. "Same way. Every time."

He nodded, satisfied, and went back to tormenting his goat. Children ask better questions than inquisitors. Vael Corrane never once asked me if I'd do it again—he only ever asked what I was. As if I were a lock to pick instead of a person to meet.

I wonder sometimes what happened to him. He turned his blade on the Archmage in the end; the storm showed him the war, the whole rotten founding lie of the Spire, and he chose, late and bloody, to be a man instead of an instrument. Last I saw him he was standing in the falling ceiling of the Rite Hall, looking at his own hands like they belonged to a stranger. I hope he found out who they belong to. Everyone should get the chance.

* * *

Kess writes. Of course he does. He was always the one who kept the ledgers.

His letters come by ways I won't describe, because some of my old smuggling routes are still the fastest roads in the world and I'm not giving them up to whoever's reading this. They arrive folded around river stones, which is his idea of a joke and also, knowing Kess, a practical weight for mountain wind. His handwriting has gotten worse, which I choose to read as happiness.

He tells me about the passes. The Spire stands broken but not fallen—it's a school again, or trying to be, run by a council that includes, unbelievably, two former inquisitors and a hedge-witch who once tried to arrest him. The wards are fraying at the edges and the realm is learning, slowly, clumsily, to hold itself together without stolen power, the way a body learns to walk on a healing leg. Some nights the fraying shows. Some nights the old wards flicker and every candle in three counties gutters, and people look up at the sky like it might answer.

It does, sometimes. I send it a little steadiness. No one knows. That's rather the point.

He tells me about the fence in Lowmark who retired to grow actual legal turnips and hates it. He tells me about the new patrol routes, the new tolls, the small honest corruptions of a world rebuilding. He tells me, in the postscripts, in handwriting even worse than the body of the letter, that the ridge above Hollowmere is good walking in spring. That the monastery ruins bloom with something white. That if a certain person ever wanted company on a certain road, she knows where to leave word.

I haven't gone. Not yet. Don't mistake me—I will. But there's a thing I have to finish first, and it isn't a quest or a debt or a destiny. It's smaller than that and harder.

I'm learning to be alone without being hidden.

Seven years as a smuggler, I told myself I liked the quiet. What I actually liked was being unreadable—no name, no history, no handle for anyone to grab. Then the prophecy grabbed me anyway, and the Spire, and the storms, and I learned that hiding is just a slower way of being caught. So I stayed north. I stayed until the silence stopped being armor and started being actual silence. Until I could sit on this hillside with forty strangers' lives depending on my weather and feel—this is the embarrassing part, write it down, Jude, you promised no lies—feel *known*. Sefa knows I burn hay when I laugh. The boy knows I flinch at iron bells. The storms know every scar. And none of it is a leash.

Turns out you can be seen and still be free. Nobody put that in the book either. The book was mostly useless, honestly. Great villain, though.

* * *

Tonight there's work. A cold front is coming down off the northern ice with bad intentions, and the valley's late planting is still in the ground, green and hopeful and utterly defenseless. Twenty years ago the Spire's wards would have turned it aside without anyone noticing—that was the bargain nobody knew they'd made, comfort bought with prisoners. Now the front comes through like weather used to, honest and indifferent, and it's mine to meet.

I climb the ridge at dusk, the way I always do, past the white flowers that Kess says bloom at Hollowmere (they bloom here too; the storms carried seeds in my hair, I think, little stowaways), and I stand where the wind can get a good grip on me.

"All right," I say to the sky, which is to say to myself, which is to say to the eleven ancient furious grieving magnificent things that share my ribs. "Let's show them how it's done."

We rise. Not rage—we're done with rage as a default setting; rage is just fear that found a job. We rise like a tide deciding to be gentle. I take the front's weight onto us, spread it thin, comb its killing cold into a long soft snowfall that will sit on the fields like a blanket instead of a blade. The eastern spirit runs the wind in long joyful circles. The deep one hums under it all, low and tidal, and for once her dream is a good one—I can feel the sea in it, warm shallows, a coast neither of us has names for.

Below, lights come on in the valley windows, one by one. Someone starts a song. The snow falls the way I asked it to, and the asking costs me almost nothing now, the way breathing costs almost nothing, the way it should have cost all along.

This is the almanac I keep. Not ink. Weather. An entry for every day: what I chose to be, and whether I meant it. Today's entry reads: *mostly woman, partly storm, fully present.* Tomorrow's is blank, which used to frighten me more than any prophecy. Now it just looks like morning.

I stay on the ridge until the stars come out, and then I do the last thing, the ritual within the ritual. I face south, toward Thornfell, toward a ridge where a man with a bad arm walks the high passes telling travelers stories about a woman made of weather, and I say his name into the wind.

The lightning answers. It always answers. One clean strike on the far southern horizon, bright as a signature.

Somewhere down there, I'd bet the whole valley's barley, Kess stops mid-story, grins at the sky, and tells whatever poor traveler is listening: *"There she is."*

Yes, love. Here I am.

Still walking.
