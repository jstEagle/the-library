## 1. The Ledger of Small Doors

The candle had burned down to the width of a thumbnail when Jude Sandoval began the third shelf.

He liked this hour best — the hour after the wardens made their rounds and before the first gray suggestion of dawn, when the Guildhouse gate held its breath and the inventory was his alone. Nineteen years he had kept the ledgers of the Impossible Things, and in nineteen years he had never once missed an entry, never smudged a figure, never let a single crate pass the threshold uncounted. Pellam said it was a sickness. Jude preferred to think of it as a kind of devotion.

He dipped his pen, uncorked the first bottle on the shelf, and held it to the light.

*Item 4,417: one lullaby, bottled, Vessian style — no. Correction.* He crossed out the last word before the ink dried. There was no such place as Vessia. The ledgers were very clear about that. *One lullaby, bottled, origin unknown, provenance: the Salt Road caravans, spring consignment.* The liquid inside was pale gold and moved against the glass the way honey moves — slowly, reluctantly, as if it would rather be sleeping. When he tilted it near his ear he could just catch it: a woman's voice, worn smooth by repetition, humming three notes that rose like stairs and came down again.

Jude did not hum along. That was rule one of the keeper's trade, inked into the front page of every ledger: *Never sing back.* A lullaby heard twice is a lullaby learned, and a lullaby learned will follow you home and tuck itself into your dreams until you forget which thoughts are yours. He had seen it happen to a caravan guard once, a big man from the river country who'd spent a week weeping for a mother he'd never had. The Guild had re-bottled him eventually. Mostly.

He stoppered the bottle, logged it, and set it back in its cradle of straw.

*Item 4,418: one shadow, stolen, donor deceased, condition: restless.*

This one lived in a long box of black glass, and even through the glass Jude could feel it pressing — that faint cool pressure at the base of the skull, like someone standing just behind your left shoulder. Shadows were difficult stock. They kept the shape of their griefs. This one had belonged to a duelist, according to the intake slip, a man who had sold it to pay a debt and then, shadowless, had found he could not remember why he'd ever been afraid of anything, and had walked off a cliff with a smile on his face. The shadow, meanwhile, still flinched at the sound of steel.

Jude checked the seals, confirmed the wax unbroken, noted the temperature of the vault wall against his palm — cold meant content, warm meant trouble — and wrote *condition stable* in his small, exact hand.

His hand was the only imprecise thing about him, and only because of the scar across the knuckle, from the year he'd tried to inventory a door without asking its permission. He'd been young then. He knew better now. Everything in the gatehouse had permission, or ought to.

*Item 4,419.*

The door stood at the end of the third shelf where all the small doors stood, propped upright in its frame like a painting of a doorway rather than a real one — knee-high, oak, iron hinges gone green with age. Its brass plate read TUESDAY in letters that had been re-engraved so many times they'd nearly worn through the metal.

Jude crouched before it and knocked politely, twice.

From inside came the smell of cut grass and rain on hot stone, and a sound like a distant market bell, and something that might have been laughter or might have been wind through laundry lines. Tuesday was one of the Guild's oldest holdings. Nobody remembered who had bought it, or from whom, or what had become of the original day. It opened onto a street that existed nowhere on any map — a narrow cobbled lane under a sky the color of pewter, where it was always mid-morning and always about to rain and never actually raining, where a baker's stall stood stocked with loaves no one could carry back through the door because bread, like everything else on the far side, refused to be owned.

Jude had walked that street exactly once, in his first year, before Pellam caught him and boxed his ears. "It's not a place, boy, it's a *holding*," the old keeper had said. "You don't visit stock. You count it." And Jude, who had grown up hungry in the dockside warrens where nothing was ever yours and everything could be taken, had understood immediately and completely. Things you counted stayed counted. Things you loved got lost. The Guild's whole empire rested on that simple arithmetic, and Jude Sandoval was very good at arithmetic.

He logged the door: *seals intact, hinge oil applied, no temporal drift detected.* Then he paused, pen hovering, because there was always a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — when he looked at the little door and felt something that was not in any ledger. Not longing, exactly. Something adjacent to longing. The feeling of standing outside a lit window in winter.

He wrote *no anomalies* and moved on.

* * *

By the time he reached the fifth shelf the candle had been replaced twice, and the gatehouse around him had settled into its nighttime voice: the tick of the great lock cooling, the whisper of the wards in the rafters, the soft industrial breathing of the vaults below, where the large holdings slept — the winter that had been purchased from a failing duchy, the staircase that went up longer than it went down, the well that answered questions but charged by the word. Above him, through the slatted window, the moon was going down over the rooftops of Coriander Street, and the city beyond the wall was doing what cities do at this hour: forgetting.

That was the trade, when you boiled it down. Everyone thought the Guild dealt in marvels, and it did, but marvels were only the packaging. What the Guild actually bought and sold was *forgetting*. A kingdom too poor to keep its ghosts paid the Guild to hold them. A widow who could not bear her husband's laugh sold it by the bottle. Cities with inconvenient pasts leased those pasts to the vaults and woke up clean. And on the other side of the scale, the buyers: collectors, kings, the sentimental and the monstrous, all of them purchasing pieces of other people's yesterdays.

Jude had never much wondered where it all came from. That was above his station. His station was the threshold — the gate, the ledger, the count. Nothing crossed without being written; nothing written was ever lost. In nineteen years he had come to think of himself as a kind of hinge himself, and he took a plain, stubborn pride in it. Other men had wives, children, graves to tend. Jude had 11,206 entries, current through last quarter, and every one of them correct.

Mostly correct.

There was the matter of the seventh shelf, which he was approaching now, and which always slowed his pen whether he willed it or not. The restricted stock. Holdings whose intake slips were written in the Auditor's own cipher, whose provenance columns read only *acquired per contract*, whose contracts themselves lived in the deep archive behind a door Jude had keys to open but not, strictly speaking, to read behind. He counted them the way he counted everything — weight, seal, temperature, number — and he did not ask what they were. A bottle that hummed when the moon was full. A crate that was colder inside than the room it sat in. A long, low chest bound in gray leather that he had never once seen open, and which gave off, on certain nights, a smell he had no name for: not rot, not flowers, something like the air after a crowd has stopped singing.

*Item 6,001 through 6,014: verified. Seals intact. No anomalies.*

No anomalies. He had written that phrase so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone. It was almost a prayer. *No anomalies, no anomalies.* The world outside the gate was full of them — wars, plagues, the slow rot of borders — but here, inside the count, everything held still and behaved.

Pellam shuffled in from the inner office with two cups of chicory, moving with the careful gait of a man whose knees had been old since before Jude was born. He was seventy if he was a day, bald as an egg, with forearms like dock rope and a face full of seams. He set one cup on the counting table without being asked, which was as close as either of them ever came to affection.

"Third shelf done?" Pellam asked.

"Done. Fifth started. The Tuesday door's drifting again — half a bell late on its chime."

"Old days wander. Can't blame them." Pellam lowered himself onto his stool with a grunt and pulled the duplicate ledger toward him, though his eyes weren't what they'd been and everyone knew the second column was really Jude's work checked against itself. "Moonset soon. You'll want the outer gate walked before the light comes."

"I'll want the outer gate walked now," Jude said. "I don't like how the wards sounded at midnight."

"They always sound like that in autumn."

"They sounded like that in autumn nineteen years ago too, and you told me then it was nothing, and I've been telling myself it's nothing ever since." Jude sipped his chicory. It was terrible. It was perfect. "Humor me."

Pellam looked at him for a moment with an expression Jude couldn't parse — something old and tired and almost sorry — and then shrugged and hauled himself up. "Walk the gate, then. I'll mind the books."

That was the shape of their nights: Jude walking the perimeter with his lantern, touching each ward-stone in sequence, listening; Pellam minding the books, which meant mostly minding the fire and occasionally writing his name in the margin so the ledger would know a human hand had passed over it. The ledgers liked that. Everything in the Guildhouse liked to be acknowledged. That was the first thing they taught you: *the impossible is only impossible until it's ignored, and then it becomes dangerous.*

The outer gate was a thing of black iron and older stone, tall enough that the top of it disappeared into the dark of the arch, wide enough for the Guild's wagons, and locked with a lock the size of a man's head. Beyond it lay the courtyard, then the wall, then Coriander Street running downhill into the sleeping city. Jude touched the first ward-stone. Warm. Content. The second. The third.

At the fourth stone he stopped.

It wasn't warm. It was *listening*. He couldn't have said how he knew — nineteen years of nights had taught him the difference the way a sailor's hands know weather — but the stone under his palm had gone taut and attentive, like a dog that has heard a whistle no one else can hear. And beneath it, so faint he might have invented it, the gate itself was making a sound. Not the cooling tick of iron. A hum. Low, sustained, patient. The hum of a struck string deciding whether to become music.

"Pellam," Jude called, without turning around. "Come hear this."

But even as he said it, the hum shifted — resolved, settled, became something almost like anticipation — and Jude found himself looking past the gate, past the courtyard, down the long slope of Coriander Street to where the lamplight ran out and the dark began. The city was quiet. The city was always quiet. But somewhere down there, in the forgotten places where the map wore thin, something was coming toward the gate, and the gate — his gate, his immaculate, obedient, well-counted gate — knew it, and was glad.

"Nothing," he told himself, and wrote it in the margin of his mind the way Pellam wrote in the margins of ledgers: *no anomalies.* "Nothing. Finish the count."

He finished the count. He logged items 4,420 through 4,436, oiled the Tuesday door's hinges a second time because it seemed to ask, banked the fires, and blew out the candle at the precise minute the almanac prescribed. Then he sat in the keeper's chair with the great ledger open on his knees and the key ring heavy at his belt, and he waited for dawn the way he had waited for dawn four thousand-odd times before: alone, exact, and perfectly content, if content is the right word for a man who has never once looked directly at the thing he refuses to see.

The knock came at moonset.

Three blows on the outer gate, spaced like a heartbeat — and not a traveler's knock, not a fist on wood, but something deliberate and strange, a rhythm Jude's ears did not know and his bones, apparently, did. The wards sang. Every bottle on the seventh shelf hummed at once. And from the far side of the iron, thin as thread, a child's voice spoke a language that has no living speakers, that appears in no ledger in the Guild's keeping —

— and Jude Sandoval, who had never once failed to log an anomaly, sat frozen in the keeper's chair and understood every word.
