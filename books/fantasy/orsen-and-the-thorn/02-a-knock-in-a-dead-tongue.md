## 2. A Knock in a Dead Tongue

Moonset came late to the gatehouse, the way it always did in the last week of autumn, dragging its pale hem across the rooftops of the Lower Ward as though reluctant to leave. Jude Sandoval stood at his window with a cup of cold chicory and watched the light go, because watching light go was, in its way, part of the inventory.

Everything at the Guild of Impossible Things was inventory. He had learned that in his first year under Pellam's crooked tutelage, when he'd tried to give a bottled lullaby back to a weeping mother who could no longer afford it and Pellam had taken him aside with the gentleness of a man defusing something explosive.

"Nothing here is yours to give, boy," Pellam had said. "You keep the ledgers. The ledgers keep everything else."

Nineteen years later, Jude kept them still.

He dressed by feel — the gatehouse was never fully dark, but neither was it ever fully lit; the lamps burned at exactly the wattage prescribed by Regulation Eleven, which was to say barely — and went down into the counting room where the night's work waited. The third of November. Quarterly reconciliation. Every impossible thing in the gatehouse's keeping had to be counted, weighed where weighing applied, and entered in the master ledger in his own small, upright hand.

He began, as he always began, with the low shelves.

Bottled lullabies first: forty-one this quarter, down from forty-six. Three had been sold to the Duchess of Marle, who collected her own childhood the way other women collected porcelain. Two had expired — lullabies did that, going sour in their glass until they sang only of abandonment — and those he wrapped in grey felt and set aside for the incinerator, feeling the familiar small ache he refused to examine too closely. One had simply gone missing, which was worse than expiration, because things that went missing from the Guild were not lost. They were *somewhere*, and somewhere was a problem.

He wrote *41* in the ledger. His pen made no sound on the page, which was one of the small mercies of ledger-paper; it drank ink the way sand drank rain.

Then the shadow.

It hung in its cage of smoked glass like a moth pinned mid-flight, and it was restless tonight. Shadows usually slept during reconciliation — they were shy of being counted — but this one pressed itself against the glass and spread its fingers there, five long smudges, as if trying the lock. It had been confiscated from a card-sharp in the Salt Quarter who had wagered it and lost, and in four years it had never once been awake at moonset.

Jude crouched before the cage and looked at it, and it looked at him, or did whatever shadows did instead of looking.

"You're counted," he told it quietly. "You're safe. Go back to sleep."

It didn't. But it stopped pressing, which was nearly obedience, and he noted in the margin — *restless, cause unknown* — in the tiny script he reserved for anomalies. There were eleven such marginal notes in nineteen years. He knew each one the way a priest knows his sins.

The door that opened onto Tuesday was next. It stood in its frame of white ash at the back of the counting room, and through it, as always, came the smell of cut grass and someone else's laundry and a church bell that rang on no day the calendar admitted to. He checked the hinges, oiled the latch with the tallow-and-ironwood compound specified in the maintenance codex, and did not, tonight, look through it for longer than regulation allowed. Some nights he lingered. Tuesday was a kind place. On Tuesdays, in whatever street lay through that frame, it was always late afternoon forever, and someone was always about to call him in to supper.

He closed the ledger entry and permitted himself thirty seconds of looking anyway. Then he shut the little brass cover over the door's face, as the codex required, and heard — faintly, muffled — the bell ring once, as if annoyed.

The rest of the inventory took until the small hours. A jar of borrowed courage, three-quarters full. A wedding vow kept in amber, still warm. Sixty feet of river in a lead pipe, coiled like rope, murmuring in its sleep. He counted them all, weighed what could be weighed, and wrote, and wrote, and wrote, and the gatehouse settled around him into its usual silence, which was not silence at all but the sound of two hundred impossible things breathing slowly in the dark.

It was nearly moonset — truly moonset now, the last grey rind of it slipping behind the granary roofs — when he heard the knock.

Three knocks. Soft. Unhurried. The knock of someone who knows they will be answered.

Jude froze with his pen above the ledger. No one knocked at the gatehouse. That was not how the Guild worked. Clients were summoned by letter that arrived before it was sent; couriers used the postern; wardens had keys. In nineteen years, the great iron-banded gate at the front of the house had been knocked upon exactly twice, and both times it had been Pellam testing whether Jude would open it without checking the visitor's credentials first.

He had failed both times. He would not fail now.

He took up the lamp, took up the visitor's registry, and crossed the flagstones to the gate, his footsteps loud in a way he disliked. Through the viewing slit — iron, a hand's width tall, angled so that whoever stood outside saw only darkness — he looked out into the lane.

A child stood at the gate.

A boy, perhaps eight years old, perhaps ten — it was hard to tell in the last thin light, hard to tell even after the light left entirely, because the boy seemed to hold the dusk around himself like a coat. He was small and thin and barefoot, though the frost had come early this year and lay silvered on the cobbles, and he did not shiver. His hair was dark and uncombed. His clothes were wrong — not ragged, which Jude would have understood, but *wrong*: a tunic cut in a style Jude had never seen, dyed a deep river-blue, fastened with bone buttons carved into the shapes of fish. No tailor in the city worked like that. No tailor in any city Jude had inventoried worked like that.

And in his right hand, held against his chest the way children hold things they have been told are precious, was a key.

Brass. Long as a forearm. Shaped — Jude's breath caught, and he hated himself for catching it — shaped like a thorn. A single curved barb of metal, elegant and cruel, with a stem that ended in a grip wound round with faded red thread.

The boy looked directly at the viewing slit. Directly at Jude, though nothing should have been visible through it from outside. His eyes were the colour of the tunic, river-blue, and very old, and very tired, and eight years old all at once.

Then he spoke.

The language was nothing Jude had ever heard. He was certain of that immediately and completely, the way one is certain of a face one has never seen. It was not the trade-tongue, nor High Cantorish, nor the clicking dialects of the Reef Kings, nor the vowel-heavy speech of the southern deserts, nor anything from the Guild's own catalogue of two hundred and six recorded languages, a catalogue Jude had helped compile and could recite from memory. The sounds were soft and layered, full of breath, words that seemed to arrive already half-sung. The boy said perhaps twenty of them, his voice clear and carrying, pitched as though he had said these exact words many times, to many doors, and expected them to work.

They should have meant nothing.

Jude understood every word.

That was the thought that arrived second, a heartbeat behind the sheer animal shock of comprehension, and it arrived the way ice arrives in a drink — suddenly, everywhere, changing the whole temperature of the moment. Because he *did* understand. Not guessed, not inferred from tone. Understood, plainly and effortlessly, the way he understood his own name, and the understanding sat in him without origin, without translation, as though the language had been waiting in some sealed room of his mind for a key of its own.

*Gatekeeper,* the boy had said. *I am the last one. I have come to be let in.*

Jude's hand was shaking. He watched it shake, distantly, as though it belonged to the shadow in the cage.

There was no entry for this. He looked down at the visitor's registry in his hands — the proper procedure, the *only* procedure — and found his pen hovering over a form designed for names, nations, purposes of visit, items declared. He knew no nation that spoke this tongue. He knew no name to write. The boy had not given one.

*Open the gate,* the child said, in the language with no country. *Please. It is almost moonset, and I must be inside before the moon is gone.*

"Why?" Jude asked — aloud, foolishly, in the trade-tongue, his voice cracking on the single syllable. "Why before moonset? Who told you—"

The boy tilted his head, listening to the trade-tongue the way one listens to a familiar song played badly, and answered in his own.

*You speak it wrongly,* he said, not unkindly, *but you speak it. You have always spoken it. You only forgot.*

"I don't forget things." The words came out harder than Jude intended, edged with a fear he could not name. "That is my entire profession. I do not forget."

*No,* the boy agreed, with the terrible gentle gravity of children and the very old. *You keep.*

And then the gate began to hum.

It started below hearing — a vibration in the flagstones, in the iron bands, in Jude's own back teeth — and rose, slow and enormous, like a cathedral organ waking after centuries. The great gate, which had not moved for anyone without the proper seals in nineteen years, trembled on its hinges. Frost leapt off the cobbles in a fine glittering mist. Down the counting room, behind its brass cover, the door that opened onto Tuesday rang its bell — once, twice, urgently — and the shadow in its cage threw itself against the glass, and sixty feet of sleeping river churned in its lead pipe with a sound like distant surf, and every impossible thing in the house turned, in whatever way it could turn, toward the gate.

Toward the boy.

Jude stood in the middle of it with his lamp guttering and his ledger clutched to his chest like a shield, and he understood, with the clarity that comes only at the edge of catastrophe, that the house knew the child. That the iron and the ash-wood and the bottled rivers recognized him — recognized the thorn-shaped key, recognized the language falling soft and sung from his mouth — the way a dog recognizes the footstep of someone long dead.

*The gate remembers me,* the boy said. He put his palm flat against the iron, and the humming deepened into something almost like speech, almost like a name being repeated over and over in joy. *It sang when I was born. Before. It sang for my mother's mother's mother. Open it, gatekeeper. Please. I am so tired, and they are coming, and I cannot let them find me standing in the open.*

"Who's coming?" Jude whispered.

The boy lifted his face toward the east, toward the hills beyond the city, and for a moment his river-blue eyes were full of a grief so vast and so old that Jude's knees nearly buckled under the weight of witnessing it. Grief did not belong on a child's face. Grief like that belonged on monuments.

*The thing that carries what we threw away,* the boy said. *The Thorn. It is coming to bring me home, and home is a grave.*

The moon's last sliver slipped behind the granary roof. Darkness took the lane whole.

And Jude Sandoval — keeper of ledgers, servant of the Guild, a man who had followed every rule for nineteen years precisely because the rules were the walls that kept the world's impossibilities safely numbered and shelved — reached for the gate's bar.

His fingers touched the cold iron.

Somewhere far away, down the dark eastern roads, something began to walk faster.

* * *

He did not open it. Later, he would turn that moment over a thousand times — the bar in his hand, the humming gate, the child's patient blue eyes — and marvel that he had managed, in the end, to obey. But the rules were walls, and walls held, and so instead of lifting the bar he did the only sanctioned thing available to him: he retreated to the counting room, barred the inner door, and opened the emergency register, the slim black volume reserved for visitors whose existence constituted a breach.

There was no form for what he needed to record. He invented one, hands steadying themselves through sheer professional habit:

*Visitor: male child, 8–10 years. Nation of origin: unknown. Language: unknown (unrecorded). Items declared: one (1) key, brass, thorn-form, provenance unknown. Purpose of visit: entry. Notes: subject claims prior relationship with premises. Premises appear to confirm.*

His pen hovered. Then, in the tiny script he reserved for anomalies — the twelfth note in nineteen years — he added:

*Understood him. Do not know why. Gate hummed. All inventory responded. Recommend immediate report to Magister Corvane.*

Writing Corvane's name made his stomach drop, though he could not have said why. The auditor was months overdue for inspection; reports sent to him vanished into the Guild's central archive without reply. Still. Procedure was procedure. Walls were walls.

When he returned to the viewing slit, the lane was empty.

Frost lay unbroken on the cobbles where the boy had stood — except that it didn't, quite. In the centre of the lane, perfectly preserved, lay the impression of two small bare feet, and the frost around them had not melted but *bloomed*, crystallized into patterns Jude had never seen: interlocking curves, fish and rivers and stars, a design too intricate for accident, covering the whole width of the street.

And on the topmost bar of the gate, hooked there as neatly as if hung by a careful hand, rested a single object.

Brass. Long as a finger. Shaped like a thorn.

Jude reached up and took it, and the moment his skin met the metal the humming — which had softened to almost nothing — surged once through the whole house, every shelf and cage and bottle singing a single held note, and then fell silent, all together, like an orchestra waiting.

In the silence, very clearly, in the language with no country, Jude heard — inside his own skull, in his own voice, in words he did not know he knew —

*Thank you, keeper. Keep it safe. I will come back for it, and for you.*

The counting room clock struck four. Behind its brass cover, the door to Tuesday rang its bell, softly, over and over, like something weeping.

Jude stood in the dark with a dead nation's key burning cold in his fist, and reached for the ledger to write down the one word the boy had never said aloud, the word that had been sitting in his understanding since the gate first hummed, the word he had been refusing to read.

He wrote it. His hand did not shake now. It was past shaking.

*Vessia.*
