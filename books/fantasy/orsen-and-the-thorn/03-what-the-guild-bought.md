## 3. What the Guild Bought

The boy slept in the coal cellar, beneath the sacks of ash the gatehouse never burned because burning them would mean admitting they were something other than ash. Jude had checked twice that they were only ash. He had held a handful to the lamp and watched it crumble gray and ordinary, and he had told himself this was diligence, and not fear.

The boy was still there when he came down with bread and watered wine, curled against the far wall where the cold came through least, one hand closed around the brass key even in sleep. The key was shaped like a thorn — a long curved spike of metal, barbed near the head, warm to the touch in a way that metal had no business being. Jude had tried to set it on the inventory shelf while the boy washed his face. It had been back in the boy's fist within the hour. Neither of them had discussed it.

"Eat," Jude said, in the trade-tongue, and then, because it seemed suddenly obscene not to, he said it again in the dead language. *Sella.* Eat.

The boy's eyes opened — gray-green, watchful, too old for eight or nine years of anything — and he ate without taking his gaze from Jude's face, as if Jude were a page he was reading ahead in.

"I'm going upstairs," Jude said. "I have work. You stay here. If anyone knocks, you're a chimney sweep's son waiting on his father."

The boy answered him in Vessian, three soft syllables, and patted the ground beside him once, an invitation or a blessing, Jude couldn't tell which. Then he lay back down and was asleep again before Jude reached the third stair, sleeping the way children sleep who have run very far and know the running isn't finished.

* * *

The restricted ledgers lived behind the iron door at the end of the counting room, and the iron door answered to two keys and a spoken word, and Jude had both keys and had known the word since he was nineteen years old, when Pellam had taught it to him over a cup of plum brandy and said, *Now you can never un-know it, lad, so mind what you go looking for.*

He had gone looking for things before. Bottled lullabies whose provenance pages had gone missing. A stolen shadow that kept trying to sign its own name in the margin. The door that opened onto Tuesday, which required quarterly maintenance and an apology. But always small searches, tidy ones, searches with a beginning and an end. He had never stood before the iron door at moonset with a child hidden in his coal cellar and asked the question that could not be un-asked:

*What language is that?*

The lamp guttered as he spoke the word. The lock turned the way heavy honest locks turn, reluctantly, like an old man rising from a chair he intends to stay in.

Inside, the restricted collection ran forty feet deep, shelved floor to vaulted ceiling, and every ledger in it was bound in leather the color of dried blood because the Guild bought its bindings from the same tannery it bought everything else from, and the tannery, like everything else, had been chosen for durability rather than beauty. Contracts. Deeds. Covenants. Acquisitions. Two hundred years of the Guild doing business with the impossible, recorded in a hand so uniform it might have been one scribe working without sleep since the founding.

Jude knew the catalog by heart — that had been his pride, once. He found the shelf he wanted without hunting: **ACQUISITIONS, TERRITORIAL**, and within it, the volumes arranged by century. He pulled the oldest, blew the dust from its clasp, and carried it to the reading table under the one lamp that burned without oil, because some rooms in the Guild were lit by arrangement rather than fuel.

The volume fell open almost before he touched it. Not to the first page. To a page two-thirds in, as if the book had been waiting, as if the book had opinions about what he should read.

The header read: **VESSEA.**

Jude did not know the word. That was the first wrong thing. He knew every entry in the territorial acquisitions — he had memorized them at twenty-two as a kind of devotion, the way other men memorized scripture. There was no Vessa. No Vessia. And yet here it was, in ink gone brown as old blood, occupying eleven full pages, more than any nation in the book, more than the Salt Principalities, more than the drowned duchies, more than all of them together.

**Contract of Acquisition, Final and Irrevocable.**

**Parties:** The Guild of Impossible Things, buyer. Aldric Vane, ninth of that name, King of Vessia, seller.

**Consideration:** As follows.

He read the consideration four times. His lips moved. The lamp-light seemed to pull away from the page, leaving the words lit from somewhere else entirely.

*The Guild shall receive: the borders of Vessia entire, with all roads, rivers, bridges, milestones, and boundary stones therein; the past of Vessia entire, with all deeds, songs, grievances, marriages, debts, recipes, grudges, lullabies, and names of the dead; the future of Vessia entire, with all children not yet born, all harvests not yet sown, all oaths not yet sworn.*

*In exchange, the King of Vessia shall receive: one hundred years of life beyond his natural span, and the guarantee that his name shall be remembered when all else is forgotten.*

*The people of Vessia, being party to the contract by right of citizenship, are deemed assets of the seller and transfer with the sale.*

Deemed assets. Transfer with the sale.

Jude sat very still. Somewhere above him, the gate hummed — it had been humming since the boy arrived, a low note below hearing that he felt in his teeth rather than his ears, and he had told himself it was the wind, though the wind had died two hours before the knock.

He turned the page. There was a map. He knew maps; he filed them; he loved them the way lonely men love the idea of elsewhere. This map showed the eastern reaches he had walked himself on Guild business — the Vale Road, the twin towns of Harrow's Bend, the river country where the ferryman charged in secrets instead of coin. Except the map showed none of those names. It showed a country shaped like a cupped hand, rivers running where he remembered roads, cities marked where he remembered empty moorland, and across the whole of it, in the old scribe's immaculate hand:

*Herein stored: one (1) nation, complete. Condition upon receipt: grieving. Do not open until claimed.*

Do not open until claimed.

His finger rested on the word *claimed*, and the paper was warm under his fingertip, warm the way the thorn-key was warm, and he snatched his hand back as if scalded.

There were marginalia. Two centuries of marginalia, in different hands — auditors' hands, keepers' hands, Pellam's hand, he realized with a lurch, recognizing the small hooked letters his mentor used for private notes. He read them in order, and they told a story the main text did not.

*Auditor Ferren, year 12:* The asset shows signs of leakage. Villagers along the former border report dreams in an unknown tongue. Recommend reinforcement of the forgetting.

*Auditor Corvane's predecessor, year 61:* Leakage continues. A woman in Harrow's Bend sang a song she has no source for. Song confiscated. Woman fined for unlicensed memory.

*Pellam, undated, in pencil, faint as breath:* We did not buy a country. We bought a wound and called it merchandise. God forgive the arithmetic.

And at the bottom of the final page, in fresh ink, in a hand Jude knew better than his own because he saw it every quarter-year across an inspection table:

*Anomaly anticipated. The heir will come to a gate. Keepers will be tested. — C.*

Jude closed the book.

The sound of it closing was very loud in the stone room, louder than it should have been, and under it, faint but unmistakable, he heard the coal cellar's direction — the boy, asleep, murmuring in the dead language, and the murmur sounded less like dreaming now and more like recitation, like a child practicing lines for something.

He thought about the nineteen years. That was the terrible thing, afterward, how clearly he could see them: the young man who had signed the keeper's oath believing the Guild was a sanctuary for the world's misfit wonders, a place where a stolen shadow could be safe and a bottled lullaby could wait for someone who needed it. Nineteen years of meticulous inventories, of quarterly audits passed clean, of pride in his own precision — and all of it built, the way a beautiful house is built over a pit, on this. On eleven pages. On a king who sold his people's past so his own name would outlive them. On a Guild that had taken delivery of a nation and shelved it between the Salt Principalities and the drowned duchies and called it storage.

*Condition upon receipt: grieving.*

He had inventoried griefs before. Small ones. A widow's last words to her husband, purchased by her daughter and never collected. He had kept them dusted. He had thought that was kindness.

He put the volume back exactly where it had stood, squared its spine with the others, and stood a moment in the dark with his hand flat on the leather, the way you'd rest a hand on a coffin you weren't sure you had the right to close.

Then he went down to the coal cellar, because there was nothing else in the world to do.

* * *

The boy was awake. He was sitting up with his knees drawn in, and he was looking at the ceiling as if he could see through it, up through the floors, up to the gate itself, which was humming now loud enough that the sacks of ash trembled faintly against each other.

"You found it," the boy said in Vessian, and Jude understood, and understanding no longer surprised him, which frightened him more than anything else that night.

"Found what?"

"What my country cost." The boy looked at him. In the lamplight his eyes were the exact gray-green of the sea off a coast Jude had never visited and somehow missed. "You smell like the ledgers. Paper and old glue." He held up the thorn-key. It was glowing now, very softly, the way coals glow after the fire seems dead. "It knows you've read it. Things wake up when they're read."

"Orsen," Jude said — the boy had given the name at the gate, the only word of it Jude had caught plainly — "what does the key open?"

Orsen turned the key over in his small fingers, and for a moment his face did something terrible and adult: it became patient. It became the face of someone who has already grieved this and is only waiting for everyone else to catch up.

"A door," he said. "Out of the echo. My mother put it in my hand and told me to run until I found a man who counted things." He smiled then, briefly, and it broke Jude's heart along a seam he hadn't known was there. "She said counters would be the last honest people left."

The gate above them changed its note. The hum deepened, gathered, and became — Jude felt it in his sternum before his ears confirmed it — a rhythm. Regular. Deliberate.

Like knocking.

Three slow strikes from a gate that had stood shut and silent for nineteen years, and Orsen was on his feet with the key raised like a knife, and somewhere in the counting room above, the lamp that burned without oil went out.

"They found me," the boy whispered, in the language older than any kingdom, and Jude, keeper of the ledgers, liar for less than an hour, reached out and pulled the child behind him into the dark.
