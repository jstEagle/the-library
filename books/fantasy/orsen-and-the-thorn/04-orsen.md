## 4. Orsen

The first thing Orsen remembered was a harvest festival, and the last thing he remembered was one too, and he could not tell if they were the same festival or two hundred years apart.

He was sitting on a cold floor when he understood this, in a room that smelled of dust and lamp oil and something older underneath — old paper, old stone, the smell of a place that kept things instead of living in them. The tall man with the tired eyes had carried him here. He had said his name was Jude. He had said *you're safe now*, and Orsen had wanted to believe him so badly that for a moment he almost did.

Then the memories came again.

They came like weather. That was the only way he could think of it — not like remembering, which was supposed to be his, chosen and small, but like weather rolling over a field, darkening everything it touched whether the field consented or not.

* * *

The festival came first, because it always came first.

Lanterns strung between the branches of pear trees, hundreds of them, each one a folded paper boat with a candle inside, and the whole orchard glowing like the sky had come down to rest among the fruit. Music from a reed instrument he knew the name of before he knew he knew it — the *vessalin*, three pipes bound with red cord, played by blowing across all of them at once so the melody sounded like wind deciding to sing. His mother's hand around his, her palm rough from threshing work, her rings clicking — no, wait, she didn't have rings, she had chapped knuckles and flour under her nails, and the woman with the rings was someone else's mother entirely, someone who lived in a city of white bridges —

Orsen pressed his fists against his eyes until he saw sparks.

*Whose memory is this?*

That was the question, and it had teeth. Because the festival felt more real than anything he owned. He could taste the festival bread — sweetened with pear mash, torn and shared, everyone breaking off a piece of everyone else's loaf so that by evening every family had eaten from every other family's table. He could feel the wax of a lantern dripping onto his knuckles as he carried it to the highest branch he could reach. He could hear his name being called — not *Orsen*, something else, a name that fit him like a coat cut for a taller boy —

And then, underneath it, fainter, like a song heard through a wall: another festival. A smaller one. A kitchen with a crooked window, a woman humming off-key, a cake with lopsided frosting because he'd insisted on doing the letters himself. *Happy birthday, Orsen.* Her voice. His mother's voice. The true one? The only one?

He couldn't find the seam between them. That was the worst part. Everyone assumed a person's life was one long cloth, but his was two cloths stitched together by someone careless, and every time he ran his fingers along the seam looking for where he ended and the other began, the stitches dissolved and both cloths slid apart and he fell between them.

* * *

"You have to eat something."

Jude's voice, from somewhere above him. Orsen realized he was curled on the floor with his cheek against the cold boards, though he didn't remember lying down. There was a bowl of soup near his hand, steam still rising off it. Barley and onion. He knew barley and onion.

He also knew forty ways to season river eel, and had never seen an eel in his life.

"I'm not hungry," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle, and he hated that, hated being small, hated that grief this size needed a bigger body and there wasn't time to grow one.

Jude crouched down. Up close his eyes were the color of tea left too long, and they were kind in a careful way, the way a man is careful with a door he isn't sure is locked. "You've been sitting on that floor for an hour," Jude said. "Talking."

"Was I?"

"In the old language." Jude said it slowly, watching him. "I understood some of it. I don't know how. Do you know what you were saying?"

Orsen shook his head. Then stopped. Then nodded, very slightly, because that wasn't quite true either. "I was counting," he whispered. "Names. I think I was counting names. There are so many of them, mister Jude. They keep coming and they want me to say them out loud, all of them, and if I say them—" He didn't finish, because he didn't know what happened if he said them, only that the wanting was enormous, tidal, a whole country's worth of unfinished goodbyes pressing against the back of his teeth.

"Say what?" Jude asked quietly. "What happens if you say them?"

*They get remembered,* said something inside him, in a voice that was not a child's voice. *And then we can finally rest.*

But he said: "I don't know."

* * *

Sleep was the enemy. He learned that the second night.

When he was awake, the memories arrived one at a time, and he could sometimes hold them at arm's length, look at them, decide *not mine, put it down*. But sleep took his arms away. In sleep the memories walked right up to him and put themselves on, like coats, like skins, and he woke up mid-life in the middle of someone else's morning.

He woke once as a girl named Sefa who had climbed the white bridge railings to dive for coins, and lay gasping on the pallet, hands patting his own body — flat chest, thin arms, a boy, a boy, *Orsen, you're Orsen* — until his heart slowed.

He woke once as an old man dying in a bed of lavender, contented, surrounded by grandchildren, and the contentment was the cruelest part, because Orsen lay there aching with a peace that belonged to someone else while his own fear sat outside the door of the dream, waiting its turn.

And he woke once in the middle of the burning.

There was no fire. That was the thing no one would ever believe. Vessia — and he knew the word now, the word had been given to him along with everything else, *Vessia, Vessia, my country, my country* — Vessia had not burned. It had been *unburned*. It had been unwritten. He stood in the middle of a street he knew the way you know your own street, past the baker's with the blue shutters, past the fountain shaped like a leaping fish, and the world was going white at the edges, not flame-white but page-white, blankness eating inward like frost creeping across a window. People didn't scream. They looked down at their hands with expressions of mild confusion, and then they looked up at their neighbors, and then they simply weren't, and the space where they'd been didn't even echo. A woman dropped a basket of apples and the apples hit the cobbles without sound. The fountain kept flowing. The fish kept leaping. And the whiteness came up the street toward him, patient as tide, and in the last moment before it reached him he heard — everywhere, from every window, from under the ground, from the sky itself — a sound like a vast book closing.

He woke screaming, and Jude was there, holding him, saying his name over and over, *Orsen, Orsen, you're Orsen, you're here, you're with me* — and the strange thing, the thing Orsen held onto afterward like a rope, was that hearing his own name spoken in Jude's ordinary tired voice pulled him back faster than anything else. Not magic. Just a man, saying a boy's name, meaning it.

"How many people?" Jude asked, later, when Orsen's breathing had settled. He was sitting very straight, the way people sit when they're afraid of the answer. "In the burning. How many were there?"

Orsen tried to count and the number wouldn't stay still. It moved like a school of fish. "More than the stars you can see," he said finally. "Less than the stars you can't." He rubbed his eyes. "Mister Jude, am I sick? Is that what this is? Mama said if I got sick she'd—"

He stopped.

*Mama.* Which mama. The word split in half as soon as he touched it, and behind each half stretched a whole life, a whole kitchen, a whole set of arms, and he could not — he *could not* —

He started crying without deciding to, the ugly silent kind, shoulders shaking, and Jude gathered him in awkwardly, like a man who hadn't held a child in years and wasn't sure of the mechanics anymore, and Orsen clung to the rough wool of his sleeve and thought: *this one. This smell, this wool, this room. This is mine. Hold onto this.*

It wasn't much of an anchor. But it was the only one he had.

* * *

On the third day, the key spoke to him.

It hung on a cord around his neck, brass worn smooth, shaped like a thorn — curved, barbed, beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful. He'd had it since before he could remember having it, which should have meant nothing, except that nothing about his remembering could be trusted anymore.

He was sitting by the narrow window, watching rain write its grey sentences down the glass, when he noticed the key was warm. Then he noticed it was humming. Not a sound in the air — a sound in his chest, in his teeth, a note so low it was less a sound than a decision.

And the memories changed.

Until then they had come as fragments, weather, scattered storms. Now they came *organized*. A map unfolded behind his eyes: the city of white bridges, the capital, called — *Avelen*, the name arriving with the taste of river water. The royal road running east. The mountain passes where the border guards had once waved travelers through with salt-bread and jokes. And at the center of the map, beneath the palace, a door. He had never seen it, and he knew it perfectly: black wood, iron hinges forged from melted plow-blades, a lock shaped like a rose with too many petals.

The door out. The words arrived with the knowledge, calm and absolute, the way you know your own heartbeat: *the door out of the stored country.*

"The key wants to go home," Orsen said aloud, surprised to hear himself speaking the old tongue, more surprised when he understood himself perfectly.

Behind him, Jude went very still. Orsen turned. The tall man was standing in the doorway with a ledger under his arm — he seemed to always have a ledger under his arm, like other men carried swords — and his face had gone the color of the paper he kept.

"What did you say?" Jude asked.

"The key knows the way." Orsen held it up. It pulsed against his palm like a second heart, warm, insistent, patient the way only very old things are patient. "It's been waiting a long time, mister Jude. It says—" He tilted his head, listening to something no one else could hear. "It says the Guild has been keeping us. All of us. In a room. Like winter coats in summer."

Jude set the ledger down very carefully, as though it might bite.

"Orsen," he said, and his voice had a crack in it now too, matching the one in Orsen's. "Does the key say what happens if we open that door?"

Orsen listened. The key hummed. Somewhere far away — farther than the city, farther than the mountains, farther than the edges of any map — something enormous turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

"It says," Orsen said, "that someone is already coming to stop us."

And down in the gatehouse below, faint but unmistakable, the great iron bell of the Guild of Impossible Things began to ring.
