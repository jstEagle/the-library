## 5. The Auditor Arrives

The knock came at dawn, and it was wrong.

Jude knew every sound the gatehouse made. He had spent nineteen years learning them the way a musician learns an instrument: the groan of the eastern hinge in damp weather, the tick of the inventory clock that ran four minutes fast no matter how often he corrected it, the particular hush of bottled lullabies settling on their shelves when the temperature dropped. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its goods quiet, but not silent — everything stored there remembered being used, and remembering things made noise if you knew how to listen.

This knock was none of those sounds. It was three strikes, evenly spaced, delivered with the patience of someone who had never once been kept waiting and did not intend to begin.

Jude set down his pen mid-entry — *one jar, moth-wing static, grade B, slightly weeping* — and stood very still.

Above him, through the floorboards, he heard Orsen wake.

The boy had been with him four days now, hidden in the loft above the counting room where Jude stored mislabeled doors, the ones whose destinations had drifted over the decades until they opened onto nothing useful or nothing at all. It was the safest place in the gatehouse because it was the least interesting. No auditor had inspected the mislabeled stock in eleven years. Orsen slept among doors that went nowhere, curled under Jude's own coat, and each morning he woke speaking Vessian to the dark, and each morning the dark seemed to lean closer to listen.

"Stay quiet," Jude breathed toward the ceiling, though the boy could not have heard him. "Whatever happens, stay quiet."

He smoothed his coat, checked that the restricted ledger was locked in its drawer beneath the false bottom of his desk — a hiding place he had built himself, which meant it was a hiding place an auditor would find in under a minute, which meant it would have to do — and opened the door.

Magister Corvane stood on the threshold like a sentence already written.

He was smaller than Jude remembered, or perhaps Jude had grown. Grey-robed, grey-eyed, with the neat silver beard of a man who trimmed it by calendar rather than mirror. Around his neck, on a chain of black iron, hung the Auditor's Seal: a small brass disc stamped with the Guild's mark — a key crossed with a quill — that could open any lock the Guild had ever sold, bought, or imagined. He carried no luggage. Auditors never did. They traveled light because wherever they arrived, the weight was already there, waiting to be counted.

"Keeper Sandoval." His voice was dry paper. "You look well."

"Magister." Jude bowed, nineteen years of habit folding his spine into the correct angle. "This is unexpected."

"Unexpected things are my trade." Corvane stepped past him without invitation, and the gatehouse changed around him. Jude felt it happen — felt the bottles on their shelves go still, felt the stolen shadow in its crate press itself flat against the wood as if hoping to be mistaken for darkness. Even the impossible things feared an audit. Perhaps especially them.

"I'm inspecting the border houses," Corvane said, walking the length of the counting room with his hands clasped behind his back, his gaze moving across the shelves like a hand across skin. "There has been an anomaly in the ledgers. A resonance. Something old has begun to hum, Keeper, and the humming is coming from *here*."

*

Jude poured tea because his hands needed something lawful to do.

Corvane sat at the counting table without touching the cup, and began, methodically, to destroy Jude's life.

"The quarterly manifest," he said. "Read me the last seven entries."

Jude read them. His voice held. Nineteen years of reciting inventory to superiors who never listened had taught him how to make numbers sound like sleep.

"Again. Slower."

He read them again. On the fourth entry — *one door, salvaged, destination lapsed* — Corvane raised one finger, and Jude stopped as if the finger were a blade at his throat.

"That door," Corvane said. "Where is it stored?"

"The loft. With the other mislabeled stock."

"Mm." Corvane drank his tea at last, one measured sip. "And what else is stored in the loft?"

"Nothing, Magister. Dust and driftwood."

It came out smooth. That was the terrible part. Jude had braced for his voice to crack, for sweat to betray him, for some visible seam to split along nineteen years of honest service — and instead the lie slid out of him clean and easy as a coin across a counter, and somewhere beneath his terror a small cold voice observed: *you are good at this.*

Corvane looked at him for a long moment. Grey eyes, patient as arithmetic.

"Nineteen years," the auditor said. "I checked. You have never filed a late report, never misplaced so much as a moth's wing of static, never taken a holiday longer than two days. You are, by every measure this Guild possesses, its most reliable keeper." He set down the cup. "Which is precisely why I am troubled, Keeper Sandoval. Reliable men do not lie about lofts."

The words landed like ice water. Jude kept his face still through sheer animal discipline. "I don't understand, Magister."

"No," Corvane agreed mildly. "You don't. That is the design." He rose, and his robes whispered against the floor like pages turning. "Walk with me. Show me your house. And Keeper — I will know everything in it before I leave. Not because I suspect you." A thin smile, the kind that never reached the eyes. "Because it is Tuesday, and Tuesdays are for counting."

* * *

They walked the gatehouse together, and it was the longest morning of Jude's life.

Corvane audited the way other men prayed: completely, without hurry, and with total conviction that something was listening. He lifted each bottle of lullaby and held it to his ear; one of them, a harvest song from a country Jude had never heard of, made him pause a half-second too long before setting it down. He examined the stolen shadow, now docile in its crate of salt-dark glass, and noted its serial number without writing anything down — auditors wrote nothing down, because auditors *were* the ledger, in a way Jude had never wanted to think about too closely. He tested the door that opened onto Tuesday, stepped halfway through, stood in the mild grey light of an endless weekday morning, and returned looking faintly refreshed.

Through all of it, Jude performed. He narrated provenance. He quoted acquisition dates. He unlocked and relocked and answered questions with the flat competence of a man who had nothing anywhere in his life but inventory. And underneath the performance, beneath the recitation, his mind ran the same loop again and again like a cart in a groove:

*The boy is twelve feet above us. The boy is humming in his sleep. The boy is a country that was erased, and erased countries leave holes, and holes resonate, and Magister Corvane can hear a bottle of static weep from across a room.*

Four days ago, hiding Orsen had felt like a simple kindness. Now it felt like holding a lit candle inside a powder store and calling the flame a secret.

In the vault corridor, Corvane stopped.

He did not announce it. He simply stopped walking, mid-stride, the way a compass needle stops when it finds north, and turned his head toward the sealed crates at the corridor's end — the deep storage, the oldest stock, the crates that had not been opened in a century and were warded in three dead alphabets.

"How long," Corvane said quietly, "have those crates been humming?"

"They don't hum, Magister."

"Don't they." The auditor moved toward them, and Jude's heart climbed into his throat and stayed there. Corvane laid his palm flat against the nearest crate — black oak, banded in tarnished silver, stamped with a seal Jude had always assumed was decorative: a thorn wound around a star. "Interesting. The resonance is stronger here. Stronger than the report suggested." He withdrew his hand and looked at Jude with something almost like sympathy. "Do you know what's in these, Keeper?"

"Unlabeled legacy stock. Pre-dating my tenure."

"Pre-dating everyone's tenure." Corvane studied the seal. "These came in two hundred years ago, in a single shipment, under a contract so restricted that only three living members of the Guild are cleared to read it. I am one of them. You are not." He turned. "So you will forgive me, Keeper Sandoval, if I find your gatehouse — your immaculate, obedient, perfectly-kept gatehouse — suddenly very interesting indeed."

Above them, faint as breath, something in the loft shifted.

Jude heard it. He was certain — *certain* — Corvane heard it too. A floorboard. A small weight turning over in sleep. A child murmuring in a language that predated the floorboards, the gatehouse, the Guild, possibly the ground.

Corvane's eyes went up. Held. Came down.

"A rat?" Jude offered.

"In your loft?"

"We are not immune to rats, Magister. We smuggle doors. Rats come with territory."

For one suspended second, Jude watched consideration move behind the grey eyes like weather behind glass — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that whatever happened next would happen regardless of whether he lied well. Corvane had not come for rats. Corvane had come because the Guild's instruments had detected a nation-sized absence beginning to fill itself in, and the filling had started here, at a border gatehouse run by the most reliable keeper in the organization. The question was never whether Corvane would find Orsen.

The question was what Corvane would do when he did — and whether Jude would be standing beside the boy when it happened, or beside the auditor.

"Rats," Corvane repeated. He smiled his thin smile. "Yes. We should speak of rats, Keeper. Specifically, of what happens to houses that harbor them."

* * *

The inspection ended at dusk, the way inspections do — not concluded but withdrawn, like a tide that intends to come back.

Corvane stood at the gate, drawing his hood up against the evening, and paused with one hand on the iron.

"You have served honorably," he said, not turning around. "Nineteen years. I want you to hear me clearly, because what I say next is not a threat. It is an accounting." He looked back over his shoulder, and in the last light his eyes were the color of the ledger's margins, the color of everything written and nothing lived. "Something has entered the world recently that should not be in it. An unregistered presence. Old, resonant, and — this is the part that concerns the Guild — *awake*. When I find it, and I will find it, there will be a disposition hearing. Assets of this nature are either bound into the ledgers or destroyed. There is no third column."

"And if the keeper who harbored it—"

"—will be charged with concealment, yes. Unless." Corvane let the word sit between them, round and heavy as a coin. "Unless the keeper comes forward first. Voluntary disclosure is weighed differently, Keeper Sandoval. The Guild forgives its instruments. It does not forgive its furniture." He released the gate. "Three days. I will be at the Wayhouse in Marrow Cross. If a certain rat should wish to be registered, bring it to me, and I will see that your nineteen years count for something."

Then he was gone, down the road, a grey shape diminishing into the grey evening, and the gatehouse exhaled around Jude like a held breath finally spent.

He stood in the doorway a long time. Then he locked the gate, climbed the stairs to the loft, and pushed open the door.

Orsen sat up among the dead doors, blinking, the thorn-shaped key clutched in one fist even in sleep. He looked at Jude's face and whatever he saw there made him pull the coat tighter around his shoulders.

"He knows," Jude said. His voice sounded strange to him — lighter and more frightened at once, like a man discovering a window in a wall he'd believed solid. "Not you, not yet. But the shape of you. The hum of you. He gave me three days to hand you over."

The boy said something soft in Vessian. Jude didn't understand the words — not yet, though he was starting to catch the music of them, the rise and fall like water finding its level — but he understood the tone. It was a question. *What will you do?*

Jude looked at his hands. Nineteen years of entries in them, nineteen years of doors opened and closed exactly as authorized, and now, for the first time since he'd signed the keeper's oath, he had done something unauthorized — and instead of shame he felt the strangest sensation, bright and terrifying, climbing up through his chest like dawn through a keyhole.

He had lied to Magister Corvane's face. And the world had not ended. The lie had *held*. Somewhere in the great ledger of his life, an entry had been altered, and no lightning had fallen, and the tea had still been warm.

"I'm going to make tea," Jude heard himself say. "And then I'm going to open a drawer I've never opened, and read a contract I'm not cleared to read. Because if they're going to charge me with concealing you, boy, I intend to know exactly what it is I'm concealing."

Below them, in the deepest drawer of the desk, behind the false bottom, the restricted ledger waited — and as Jude descended the stairs, he could have sworn he felt it waiting, the way the crates in the vault waited, the way the whole erased country waited:

patiently, and awake.
