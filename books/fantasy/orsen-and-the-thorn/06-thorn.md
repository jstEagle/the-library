## 6. Thorn

It was born in the place where forgetting goes.

Not a grave — graves are tended, marked, visited by the guilty on certain Sundays. The place where forgetting goes is colder than that. It is a cellar beneath a cellar, a drawer at the bottom of a locked cabinet in a house no one can find twice. Vessia had been folded into such a place two hundred years ago, and everything the folding could not hold — the grief of mothers who woke one morning unable to name their children's fathers, the shame of soldiers who had signed the surrender, the rage of a king who sold his country and wept while he counted the coin — all of it pooled there, in the dark, and waited.

And because grief is patient, and shame more patient still, and rage most patient of all, the pool learned to stand up.

The Thorn did not remember being born. It remembered only what it was made of: every discarded sorrow of a nation that no longer existed. It wore them like a coat cut for a taller man. Its body was assembled from the things the world had chosen not to keep — the smell of rain on Vessian rooftops, the weight of a harvest scythe, the last word of a lullaby whose melody survived but whose language did not. It had hands. It had chosen to have hands, because its work required them.

Its work was simple. Somewhere in the world above, a boy had been born carrying the last living piece of Vessia. The Thorn intended to take that piece back, or end it, or hold it — even the Thorn did not know which, and this uncertainty was the closest thing it had to a heart.

Tonight it walked the city of Corvale, where the Guild kept its ledgers, and the city did not see it.

Cities never saw it. That was the mercy of being made from forgotten things: you could stand in a lamplighter's shadow and be less visible than the shadow itself. The Thorn moved through Corvale the way water moves through cloth, and it listened with ears grown from a thousand eavesdropped confessions, and it hunted.

* * *

The first name on its list was a chandler named Bessick Hallow.

The Thorn knew him. Not his face — faces were the least reliable thing about the living, they changed and aged and lied — but his trade. In the year 1189 of the old reckoning, Bessick Hallow's great-grandfather had shipped forty barrels of Vessian tallow downriver, candles rendered from fat that had burned in Vessian temples, and he had known exactly what he was carrying, and he had charged triple for it because the war made scarcity profitable. The Guild had recorded the transaction in a ledger bound in grey leather. The Thorn had read that ledger. It read all of them, in the hours when it was not walking, because memory was the only country it still had citizenship in.

Bessick Hallow lived above his shop on Candlemaker's Row, behind a door painted green, and he was old now, and he slept badly, though he could not have said why. Some part of him had always known. The Thorn understood this about its victims better than they understood themselves: guilt keeps, like tallow. It renders down over generations until what remains burns with a clean, terrible light.

The Thorn came up the stairs without sound. It stood over the bed and let the old man feel it before he saw it — the way a room grows cold when something ancient enters — and Bessick Hallow opened his eyes and looked up into a face that was almost a face, features arranged the way a child arranges stones into a person, and he did not scream. The Thorn respected him for that.

"You," Hallow whispered. "I dreamed you. Forty years I've dreamed you."

"I know," said the Thorn. Its voice was many voices pressed flat, a choir heard through a wall. "You dreamed me because your grandfather's coin never left your bloodline. Money remembers where it has been."

"Are you death?"

"I am what comes after death when no one mourns." The Thorn knelt beside the bed, and its near-face was gentle, insofar as it could be gentle. "Tell me what you know of the boy."

Hallow's breath rattled. "There's no boy."

"There is a boy who speaks the old tongue. There is a boy with a key. He passed through this district three nights ago, carried in a keeper's arms, and every candle in your shop guttered as he went by, because wax knows its homeland. You saw him. You told no one. Why?"

Because the old man had wept, the Thorn knew, though Hallow had not yet admitted it. Because the sight of the child had cracked open a vault in him his family had sealed six generations ago, and out of it had come a word he did not know he knew — *veshala*, homecoming — spoken aloud into an empty shop at midnight, terrifying him more than any intruder ever had.

"Say it," the Thorn said softly. "Say the word you said that night."

Hallow's lips trembled. "*Veshala,*" he breathed, and the syllables came out whole, unbroken, in an accent dead two centuries, and tears ran sideways into his pillow. "God forgive me. What is happening to me? What is that language?"

"It is yours," said the Thorn. "It was taken from you before you were born, and it has been waiting in your blood for a reason to come home." It reached out and touched the old man's forehead, two fingers, light as ash settling. "Sleep now. When you wake, you will remember Vessia, and you will grieve her properly, and grieving is the only inheritance anyone in your line ever earned honestly."

"And then?" Hallow whispered. "Will you kill me?"

The Thorn considered. Killing was its craft, but tonight the craft felt heavy, the way a scythe feels heavy in wet grass. "No," it said. "You will do more damage alive. A man who weeps in public for a country that never existed makes people ask questions. Questions are knives too." It rose. "But hear me, chandler. If the Guild comes asking whether you've seen the boy — and they will come — remember that you owe them nothing. Your grandfather's triple price bought your silence, not your loyalty."

It went down the stairs and out through the green door, and behind it the old man lay in the dark, weeping for a homeland he had never seen, and the Thorn carried his grief away with it, adding it to the coat, which was growing heavier by the day.

* * *

The second name was harder.

Her name had been Maren Quill, and she had not traded in goods at all. She had traded in songs. Two hundred years ago, when the Guild's agents moved through dying Vessia buying up whatever could be carried — borders, pasts, futures, the whole improbable inventory of a nation — Maren Quill's grandmother had been a collector of music, and she had paid a copper apiece for Vessian lullabies from mothers too starving to refuse, and she had written them down in notation, stripped of their words (words she couldn't read anyway), and sold them to the Guild as *cultural salvage, lot 44*. The lullabies still existed. They sat in a vault beneath Corvale, catalogued and preserved and utterly silent, the way a butterfly exists in a case.

Maren Quill herself was a music teacher now, gray-haired, beloved by her students, and she taught those same melodies without knowing their origin, humming them to children as if they were hers.

The Thorn found her in her schoolroom after dark, grading exercises by lamplight, and it stood in the doorway for a long time before she noticed it, because some part of the Thorn did not want to do what it had come to do.

She noticed eventually. Teachers notice silence; it is the sound of mischief or grief. She looked up, and she saw the impossible shape in her doorway, and she set down her pen with deliberate calm.

"You're not here for the children," she said.

"No."

"For me, then." She studied it. Most people flinched from the Thorn's face; Maren Quill looked at it the way one looks at a painting that disturbs but compels. "You have her cheekbones," she said suddenly, wonderingly. "My grandmother's. The woman in the miniature upstairs. How do you have my grandmother's face?"

"Because your grandmother is part of what I am," the Thorn said. "She collected forty-one lullabies from dying mothers and sold them by weight. I am made of what she threw away when she wrote down only the notes."

Maren Quill went very still. "Those songs are mine," she said. "I've sung them my whole life. My mother sang them. Where did they come from?"

"From a country called Vessia," the Thorn said, and watched the name land in her like a stone dropped down a well — a long fall, a long waiting, and then no splash at all, because the well had been dug so deep the water had forgotten how to receive anything. "Do you know it?"

"I—" Her brow furrowed. "I know the name. Everyone knows the name. It's on the maps. It's—"

"It is on no maps," the Thorn said gently. "Try to picture it. Try to picture its flag, its capital, its king. Try."

She tried. The Thorn watched her try, watched the effort move through her face like weather, and watched it fail, and watched the failure frighten her more than the assassin in her schoolroom ever could. "There's nothing there," she whispered. "How can there be nothing there? I know the *word*—"

"The word is all the Guild left loose. A name with no country attached, like a bell with no clapper. But you have the clapper, Maren Quill. You've been ringing it for thirty years in this room. Every child you taught those melodies to has been singing Vessian mourning songs without knowing it, and the songs have been doing what songs do." It stepped closer. "They have been keeping the door soft."

"What door?" Her voice had gone thin. "What are you talking about? Who are you?"

"I am the grief of two million people, given legs," the Thorn said. "And I am looking for a boy who carries the last of their blood, and I am going to find him, and what happens then is older than either of us. But first—" it extended one hand, palm up, an oddly courtly gesture "—sing."

"Sing?"

"The one in three-four time. The one about the river taking the bridge. Sing it as you sing it to the little ones."

Maren Quill was trembling now, but she was a teacher, and teachers obey the strange commands of the night because curiosity is stronger than fear. She sang. Four lines, soft, the melody worn smooth as river glass by decades of small voices.

And the Thorn wept.

It had not known it could. The weeping was terrible to hear — not human weeping but the sound of a reservoir cracking, two hundred years of held-back mourning finding one hairline fissure — and Maren Quill stopped singing and covered her mouth with both hands, and the lamplight bent toward the Thorn as if leaning in to listen.

"That song," the Thorn said, when it could speak, "was written by a woman named Ilseva Dorn the night before her city burned without fire. She sang it to her daughter. The daughter grew up and had a daughter, and that granddaughter was the mother your grandmother paid a single copper. One copper, Maren Quill. For the last song a mother ever gave her child." It wiped its near-face with the back of its hand, a gesture it had copied from watching soldiers, long ago. "Sing it again. And then teach it with the words, from now on. I will give you the words. They are the only payment I have."

And it taught her. There in the lamplit schoolroom, the assassin of a dead nation leaned over a music teacher's desk and wrote out, in a script that predated every alphabet she knew, the lyrics of forty-one lullabies, and its handwriting shook, and hers shook as she copied them, and outside the window the city slept on top of its stolen centuries like a child sleeping on a chest of letters it has never opened.

When dawn grayed the windows, the Thorn stood to go.

"The boy," Maren Quill said quietly. "You're going to kill him, aren't you. Whatever you are."

"I don't know," the Thorn said, and the honesty of it seemed to surprise them both. "He is the heir. He is also a child. Both of these things are true, and I am made entirely of things that were true and were not allowed to matter, so I have a certain sympathy for truths in trouble." At the door it paused. "If a keeper comes to you — a tall man, careful eyes, ink on his fingers — tell him what I told you. Tell him the songs want their words back. He'll understand before he wants to."

* * *

*

By the third night, the Thorn could feel the boy the way a compass feels north.

It was not direction exactly. It was a pull in the substance of it, in the grief-stuff of its making, a lean toward something. Vessia's last blood was somewhere in the eastern quarters of the city, behind walls the Guild thought were secret, and every hour the pull strengthened, because the boy was changing — the Thorn could taste it on the air. The inherited memories were rising in him faster now. Soon there would be very little boy left and a great deal of country, and the Thorn needed to reach him before the Guild did, because the Guild had plans for countries, and the Thorn had been inside those plans for two hundred years and knew what the inside of a plan smelled like.

Preservative. Salt and paper and the particular chemical sweetness of a thing kept from decaying because decay would mean change and change would mean loss of value.

The Thorn had been a valuable thing once. It knew.

It crossed the rooftops of the tanners' quarter as the moon set, moving through the gaps between chimneys, and below it the city exhaled its night-smells — smoke, wet wool, horse, the river — and threaded through all of it, faint as a hair in milk, the scent it had followed across half the world: woodsmoke and bread and a flowering shrub that grew only in Vessian churchyards, blooming now in the lungs of a nine-year-old boy.

The safehouse was ahead. The Thorn felt the wards before it saw the building — Guild work, ledger-magic, names written in circles to make the air forget how to admit strangers. Clever. Cruel, in its way: wards built from forgetting, guarding the last boy who remembered.

The Thorn stood on the rooftop opposite and did not attack.

This was the part it had not explained to itself. It had killed traders and collectors and brokers of stolen homelands for two hundred years — no, it corrected itself, it had *unmade* them, killing implied a life ended, and what the Thorn did was closer to returning things to the pool — and it had never once hesitated, because hesitation requires a future, and the Thorn had none. But tonight it crouched on the cold slates and looked down at the warded windows and found that its hands, its chosen hands, its working hands, had curled into fists for no tactical reason whatsoever.

Somewhere below, the boy was dreaming. The Thorn could feel the dreams leaking through the ward like light under a door: a harvest festival, a mother's voice, a city burning without fire. The same dreams the Thorn itself was made of. For two hundred years those memories had belonged to the pool alone. Now they were waking up in a child, and the child was frightened, and the Thorn knew exactly how frightened, because it had been that frightened once, in its first hour of standing up out of the dark, wearing its coat of sorrows, discovering that grief alone does not teach you how to breathe.

*I am going to kill him,* it told itself, in the choir-voice, in the flat pressed tone. *Or save him. Or carry him home. The words are interchangeable.*

Below, in the warded house, a lamp went out.

And the Thorn went rigid, because the lamp had not gone out the way lamps do. It had gone out *in answer* — snuffed by a draft that came from nowhere, a draft shaped like recognition. Something in that house had sensed it on the roof. Something young, and old, and speaking — though no voice crossed the night — in a language the Thorn had not heard spoken aloud since the world agreed to lose it.

Then, faintly, through stone and ward and sleeping street, the boy's voice reached it anyway, the way a bell reaches through water:

*"Thorn of my grandfather's shame. Come down. I know why you weep."*

The Thorn stood on the rooftop a long moment, the wind moving through it as though it were already the mist it sometimes feared it was.

Then it stepped off the edge, and fell toward the house like a decision finally made.
