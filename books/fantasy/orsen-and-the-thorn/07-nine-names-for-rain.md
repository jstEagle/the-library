## 7. Nine Names for Rain

The vaults beneath the Guild of Impossible Things were older than the building above them, older than the street, older, some said, than the city's founding charter — though Jude had always assumed that was the kind of lie keepers told apprentices to make inventory feel like priesthood. He had walked these corridors for nineteen years with a lamp and a ledger and never once wondered what slept behind the sealed doors. He had catalogued their contents by weight and number and moved on.

Now he was running through them with a child's hand in his, and every door they passed was waking up.

"This way," Jude breathed, pulling Orsen left at the junction where Corridor Nine met the Long Stair. "The wardens will check the upper floors first. Corvane thinks in floors. He thinks in *offices*."

Orsen didn't answer. He hadn't spoken since Pellam's whistle had sent them down through the trapdoor behind the gatehouse stove, but his grip on Jude's fingers was fierce and certain, and when the lamps along the corridor guttered as they passed — guttered, Jude noticed, *toward* the boy, like flowers turning to sun — he did not flinch.

The storage vaults of the Guild were arranged not by category but by *difficulty*. The shallow galleries near the stairs held the simple contraband: bottled lullabies racked like wine, a stolen shadow folded into a cedar chest, crates of Tuesday stamped with customs seals from countries that had never existed on any map Jude had filed. Deeper ran the corridors of the genuinely dangerous — the annexes where memory was kept in bulk, where names hung in glass like preserved fruit, where doors stood in frames with no wall around them, breathing.

And deeper still, past all of it, past the iron gate marked **RESTRICTED — DO NOT FILE**, lay the wing that appeared on no ledger Jude had ever been given. He had known it was there. Every keeper knew. It was simply one of those things a loyal man did not look at, the way one did not count another man's coins or ask about the scar under a friend's collar. Nineteen years of willful blindness, and now his feet carried him straight toward it because there was nowhere else left to be blind *to*.

He stopped at the iron gate, breathing hard. The lock was old work — pre-Guild, maybe pre-kingdom — and it should have taken him an hour and a set of picks he didn't have.

Orsen reached up and touched it.

"**Selu veth. Anselu.**"

*Open, please. I'm home.*

Jude understood it the way he understood rain — without translation, without effort, the words landing somewhere below language. And the lock, impossibly, *heard*. He watched the mechanism turn over inside itself like a sleeper rolling toward a familiar voice, and then the gate swung inward on hinges that had not moved, by the dust, in two hundred years.

"You shouldn't be able to do that," Jude said. His voice came out strange. Reverent. Frightened.

Orsen looked up at him. In the lamplight his eyes were the grey-green of deep water, and very old, and also seven years old, both at once. "**Kessu ilan?**" *Why are you afraid?*

"I don't know what you just said."

"Yes you do." The boy said it in Common, his first words in hours, and they were flat and tired and terribly adult. "You've known everything I say since the gate. You just keep pretending you don't, because if you admit it, then you have to decide what side you're on."

Jude had no answer for that. He took the lamp and went through the gate, and the boy followed, and behind them the iron door closed itself softly, like someone drawing a blanket over a sleeping room.

* * *

The wing beyond was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

Jude had expected chaos — the unfiled overflow of two centuries, crates stacked to the dark. Instead he found order so perfect it hurt: long aisles of shelving receding beyond the lamp's reach, each bay numbered in a script he almost recognized, each crate sealed not with wax but with *silence*. That was the only word for it. When he laid his palm against the nearest crate, he felt nothing — no grain of wood, no weight of contents, just a smooth absence, like pressing a hand against the part of a dream you're not allowed to remember.

The labels were Vessian.

He couldn't read them. No one could; the language had died with the country, which was the entire point of the contract, which was the entire point of the Guild. But he knew the letterforms the way you know a face from a portrait painted before you were born — from the restricted ledgers upstairs, from the contract itself with its clause after clause of beautiful, terrible script.

"**Oren selu,**" Orsen said quietly, walking the aisle, trailing his fingers along the crates. *Sleep well.* A courtesy. A goodnight to children, to guests, to the dead.

The crate under his hand went warm.

Jude stepped back. The seal didn't break — nothing spilled, nothing escaped — but the silence *softened*, the way a held breath softens when the body finally believes it may exhale. And from inside came a sound: faint, muffled, unmistakable. Singing. A woman's voice, humming something slow and sweet and sad, the way mothers hum when they think no one is listening.

Orsen pressed his ear to the wood and smiled, and it was the first true smile Jude had seen on him since the gate.

"**Mama selu.**" *Goodnight, Mama.*

"That's not your mother," Jude said, before he could stop himself. "That's a relic. That's inventory. That is a *crate*—"

"It's somebody's mother." Orsen lifted his head. "There are thousands of them in here, keeper. Thousands of mothers, thousands of harvest songs, thousands of grandmothers who knew nine different names for rain and which one meant the river would rise. You people didn't steal treasure. You stole *Tuesday evenings*. You stole the boring parts. The parts that made a country a country." He turned back to the shelves, and his voice dropped. "They've been saying goodnight to each other in the dark for two hundred years. Nobody ever answered."

Jude stood in the aisle with his lamp and his ledger-hand and felt something crack cleanly through the middle of his nineteen years of service.

"*Nobody ever answered,*" he repeated.

"They couldn't hear us. We couldn't hear them. That was the bargain." Orsen shrugged, small shoulders under a coat too big for him. "Sealed is sealed. Except—" he tapped the crate, gently, the way you'd tap a shy horse, "—except me. I'm not sealed. I'm the receipt."

* * *

They went deeper, because deeper was the only direction left, and because Orsen wanted to — no, Jude corrected himself, watching the boy walk the aisles like a landlord walking his fields — because Orsen *remembered* wanting to.

And the vault woke as he passed.

A crate of festival lanterns rustled its paper skin when he spoke a word of greeting. A shelf of clay whistles — nine of them, tuned to nine rains — shivered and hummed a chord no instrument should have remembered. In one bay a single child's shoe sat alone in its case, and when Orsen paused before it, the whole aisle filled with the smell of cut grass and hot bread, sudden and violent as grief, and Jude had to put a hand on the shelving to stay upright.

"**Ilu kessa varn?**" Orsen asked the shoe. *Did you run far?*

The smell of grass deepened. Somewhere in the case, faintly, a sound like laughter.

"Stop," Jude whispered. "Orsen. Stop talking."

"Why?"

"Because every word you say, they wake up a little more. And things that wake up in this building get *filed*." He grabbed the boy's shoulder. "Corvane has wards on this whole sub-level. If enough of this wing comes alive at once, he'll feel it from the reading room. He'll know exactly where we are."

Orsen considered this with the unnerving seriousness of a child who has already carried more weight than most adults ever lift. Then he nodded once and pressed his lips together — and immediately, visibly, began to suffer. Because the boy was full of a dead country's evening conversations and had just been asked to hold his tongue in the one place on earth that could finally hear him.

Jude looked at him for a long moment.

Then he did the thing he would later understand was the true beginning of his treason, more than the lying, more than the flight, more than anything that happened at the water. He crouched down in the aisle of the forbidden wing, lamp between them, and said:

"Teach me one."

Orsen blinked. "*What?*"

"A word. One word. If we can't talk freely, teach me to talk quietly. Teach me—" he gestured helplessly at the listening dark, "—teach me how to say goodnight properly."

Something changed in the boy's face. Jude would spend years trying to describe it afterward and never quite manage it. It was the look of someone who has been carrying a heavy thing alone for a very long time, and turns to find another pair of hands already open.

"**Selu,**" Orsen said. "That's goodnight. But it's also thank-you, and it's also rest-well, and it's also goodbye-if-it's-forever. It's a lot of word."

"Selu," Jude repeated.

Down the aisle, three crates sighed — a soft, collective exhalation, like a congregation settling into pews. The lamp flame leaned toward the boy.

"Again," Orsen said. "Slower. Your mouth doesn't believe you yet."

"Selu."

"Better. Now — **varn**. Rain. But there are nine kinds, and nine words, and you have to learn them in order or they'll know you're a stranger."

"There are nine words for rain."

"Of course there are. There were nine rains in Vessia. The thin rain that means planting. The loud rain that means the river's angry. The rain that falls upward off the gorge in spring — that one has the prettiest name." Orsen counted them on his fingers, solemn as a clerk. "**Varn-sel. Varn-du. Varn-kesh. Varn-ilan. Varn-mora. Varn-teth. Varn-su. Varn-halla. Varn-ossen.**" He looked up. "That last one's the upward rain. Ossen means *returning*. Things that go away and come back."

Jude wrote nothing down. He didn't need to. The words went into him somewhere below the ledger-hand, below the keeper, below the nineteen years — and stayed.

"Varn-ossen," he said carefully.

Every crate in the aisle glowed — faintly, briefly, warmly, the way coals breathe when stirred. Not waking. Just... acknowledging. A stranger who had bothered to learn the word for returning, in a building built on making sure nothing ever returned.

"Oh," Jude said softly. "Oh, they *like* that."

"They like anyone who learns it." Orsen took his hand again. "Come on. There's a door at the end of the wing. A real one. I can feel it thinking."

* * *

They walked and Jude learned, and the learning was the strangest tenderness he had ever known.

He learned **kessu** — *why*, but also *the question you ask a fire before lighting it*. He learned **ilan** — *home*, but only when spoken while touching the ground. He learned that Vessian had no word for *inventory*, which made Orsen laugh for the first time, a bright startled sound in the cold aisles, until Jude realized the boy was laughing at him and found, to his astonishment, that he didn't mind.

He learned that the language had no separate word for *grief* and *love*. They were the same word — **mora** — and the difference was only which way you were facing.

"That can't be right," Jude said.

"Why not?"

"Because then every time you loved something you'd be grieving it in advance."

Orsen stopped walking. He looked up at Jude with the deep-water eyes, and for a moment the inherited centuries were very close to the surface, close enough that the boy seemed lit from behind.

"Now you understand my country," he said.

They heard the wardens before they saw them — boots above, muffled through stone, and worse, the particular hum of ledger-magic being worked downward through the floors, methodical as a searchlight. Corvane's signature was all over it: precise, patient, thorough. Jude pulled Orsen into the shadow of a standing door — one of the frameless ones, breathing slowly in its alcove — and clamped a gentle hand over the boy's mouth before he could greet it.

Through the gap between the doorframe and the wall, Jude watched lamplight swing down the far aisle. Heard a warden murmur: "...sub-level nine showing thermal anomalies across four bays... Magister says sweep the restricted wing by dawn..."

By dawn. Three hours, perhaps less.

The boots faded. Jude let out his breath and looked down at Orsen, who was staring past him at the breathing door with an expression of absolute recognition.

"**Anselu, varn-ossen,**" the boy whispered against Jude's palm. *Hello, returning one.*

The door exhaled. And in the space of that breath, Jude felt — not heard, *felt* — the whole forbidden wing shift its attention, thousands of sealed silences turning toward the alcove like faces toward a window, waiting to see whether the door would answer.

It opened onto darkness and the smell of night rain.

Real rain. Falling down, ordinary, blessedly unmagical — onto mud, onto stones, onto whatever waited outside the Guild's walls and maps and ledgers.

Orsen tugged his hand. "**Kessu ilan?**" *Why are you afraid?* — the same question as before, but softer now, and Jude found he had an answer.

"I'm not," he said, and was surprised to find it true. "Not of the dark. Come on. Lesson continues outside."

They stepped through together — and behind them, down the long aisle, every crate in the forbidden wing began, very softly, to sing.

It was not a lullaby. It was a call. And somewhere above them, in the reading room, Jude knew the Magister Auditor had just looked up from his desk.
