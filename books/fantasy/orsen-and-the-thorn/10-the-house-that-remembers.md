## 10. The House That Remembers

The safehouse was a house that remembered things.

Orsen felt it the moment they came through its door — a narrow townhouse wedged between a shuttered chandlery and a wall so old it had begun to lean, as if tired of standing. The house had opinions. It creaked in places that were not load-bearing. It breathed through gaps in the window frames with a sound like someone counting under their breath. And when he walked across the floorboards of the second-floor room, they sang.

Not creaked. *Sang.*

Low notes, half-remembered, in a scale that did not belong to this city. He stopped mid-step and stood very still on the third board from the window, and the note hung in the air around his ankle like a moth.

"Do you hear that?" he asked.

Jude was setting down the lamp. He looked tired in a way Orsen had never seen before — not the tiredness of the body but of the ledger-keeper's face, which had always been so certain of itself. "Hear what?"

"The floor." Orsen knelt and pressed his palm flat against the wood. Warmth came up through it, faint as sun through winter glass. "It knows a song."

Jude watched him for a long moment with an expression Orsen couldn't read — something between fear and wonder, the way people look at weather that has turned strange. "This house belonged to a merchant family," he said carefully. "Before the Guild took it over. They traded... east."

*East,* thought Orsen, and the word opened inside him like a door.

He didn't say anything else. But that night, lying on a straw mattress under a blanket that smelled of dust and camphor, he put his hand against the floor again, and the boards hummed their small song until he slept.

* * *

In the dream, there was no question about whose life it was.

That was the first thing he understood, and understanding it felt like coming home after a long walk in the rain. In the dream he was not confused. In the dream everything fit.

He woke into a morning in a city called Vessa-sel-Anh, which needed no translation because he was dreaming in the language that named it. Light came through shutters in long gold bars, striped across a floor of pale stone worn smooth by two hundred years of feet. He could hear his mother below, singing while she worked — the harvest song, the one about the river giving back what it borrowed — and her voice moved through the house the way light did, filling every corner without hurry.

He got up. His legs were longer in the dream. His hands were bigger. There was a callus on his right thumb from a tool he knew how to name: a *serrin*, a grafting knife, because it was late summer and he was apprenticed now to the orchard-keepers of the terraces above the city, and every morning before lessons he carried cuttings down in wet cloth.

He went downstairs. His mother stood at the basin with her sleeves pushed up, her dark hair pinned carelessly, flour on one forearm where she'd been baking earlier even though she was cutting plums now, and when she saw him she said, "You're up before the bell. Who are you, and what have you done with my son?"

And he laughed — laughed with a boy's voice, a voice that hadn't broken yet, a voice that was *his*, no argument about it — and said something back in the old tongue, some joke about bells and lazy apprentices, and she flicked plum juice at him and missed on purpose.

Outside, the city was awake. He could smell bread and river water and the particular green smell of the terraces after night watering. A neighbor's dog trotted past him with the solemnity of a creature on official business. Two old men argued cheerfully outside the shrine of the Nine Names — arguing about rain, always about rain, because there were nine words for rain in Vessia and each one meant something different, meant *coming* or *staying* or *forgiving*, and the whole country treated the difference as a matter of theology.

"Good morrow, little orchard-man," called the baker's wife, whose name was Sennet, who had burned her left hand years ago and kept it wrapped in silk because scars deserved dignity. "Tell your mother I owe her for the sugar."

"I'll tell her," he said. "She'll pretend she's forgotten."

"She'll pretend," Sennet agreed, delighted, "because pretending is how we love each other here."

He walked up through the terraces with his cuttings. The sun climbed. Somewhere below, the river ran silver and unhurried, giving back what it borrowed. He grafted a branch of late plum onto older wood with his serrin, wrapping the join in waxed cord, and his teacher — a stooped woman named Ilva who smelled of smoke and honey — watched his hands and said, "You do that like you've done it in another life," and everyone laughed, because in Vessia that was just a thing you said to a child who learned quickly.

The day went on. It went on and on, golden and specific, full of names — Sennet, Ilva, the dog was called Brindle, the shrine-keeper was Old Maren who had nine opinions about rain and shared them freely — and every hour the dream grew more solid under his feet, more real than real, until he stopped noticing it was a dream at all.

Why would he notice? You don't notice the world being the world.

His mother called him in at dusk. She called him by a name.

"*Sefan.* Wash your hands."

And he turned toward her voice, happy, obedient, entirely himself —

— and somewhere very far away, in a place that was not a place, a boy named Orsen heard that name and felt, for one terrible instant, like a coat hanging on a hook in someone else's hallway.

But then his mother smiled, and the feeling drowned, and the dream closed over him like warm water.

* * *

He woke speaking.

He didn't know he was speaking at first. He only knew that his mouth was moving and that the sounds coming out of it were easy, fluent, *right* — the way walking is right, the way breathing is right — and that the dim room around him was wrong, all wrong, the angles wrong, the smells wrong, the light gray where it should be gold.

"—the bell hasn't rung, Mama, why are the shutters—" 

He stopped.

A man sat in the chair by the door. Not his father. Not anyone. A thin man with ink-stained fingers and a keeper's careful face, watching him with eyes that were trying very hard not to look afraid.

Jude. The name surfaced slowly, like something rising through deep water. *Jude.* Yes. Jude. The gatehouse. The ledgers. The flight through the vaults where the crates had sung to him. The road east. The house that remembered.

But those were — those were the *other* memories. The shallow ones. The paper ones.

"Mama?" he said again, and heard how young his own voice sounded, and hated it. "Mama, where—" He swung his legs off the mattress and stood, and the floorboards under his bare feet were silent, and their silence was the loudest thing he had ever heard. "Where is the terrace? Where is the — the shrine, Old Maren, the dog, the dog's name was—"

*Brindle.* The dog's name was Brindle. He could smell the dog's warm fur. He could feel the silk wrap on Sennet's burned hand. He could see the exact gold angle of morning light through his mother's shutters, could hear the nine names for rain waiting in his mouth like coins in a pocket.

He could not remember his mother's face.

Not the dream-mother — hers was perfect, burned in forever, flour on her forearm, laughter in her throat. The other one. The real one. The woman who had — who must have — who had carried him out of a burning country, or sent him away, or died holding him, or — he reached for her and found nothing. No face. No voice. Not even a shape. Just the fact of her, the way you know there's a wall behind you in the dark without seeing it.

There had been a woman. There had been arms. There had been a word she used to call him home at dusk.

He could not find any of it.

"No," he said, and the word came out in the old tongue, and he tried again and made it come out in the trade-tongue, clumsy now, heavy in his mouth like stones instead of water. "No. No, wait. Her name. What was her name?"

Jude rose from the chair. "Orsen."

"That's not — that's not what she called me." His hands were shaking. He looked at them and they were a child's hands, small, uncallused, no mark of the serrin anywhere. When had that happened? When had the orchard let go of him? "She called me — she called me—"

*Sefan.*

The name rose in him sweet and complete, and he almost said it, almost let it fill his mouth, because it was such a good name, such a *worn* name, polished by use, loved by use — and then he caught himself, caught the trap of it, the beautiful soft trap.

If he said *Sefan*, he would be Sefan. He understood that suddenly, completely, the way you understand a stair is missing in the dark. Every day the dream gave him more — the streets, the songs, the grafting knife, the golden hours — and every day it took a little payment in return. Yesterday he had still known the color of the blanket on his real bed, wherever that bed was. This morning he reached for it and found a hole shaped like a blanket.

The dream was not a memory. He saw that now. It was a *room*. A room built out of everything Vessia had been, and it wanted him in it, wanted him furnished into it, wanted a boy to stand in the golden light and be Sefan forever so that the light would have someone to fall on.

"It's taking my mother," he said aloud, in the dead language, because grief spoke that language better.

Jude crossed the room and knelt down in front of him — knelt, the keeper of ledgers, the man of inventories and precise entries, kneeling on singing floorboards to be at a child's eye level. "Orsen. Look at me. Look at *me*."

He looked. It cost him something to look away from the gold light behind his eyes.

"You're Orsen," Jude said. "Say it."

"Orsen." It sounded foreign in his own mouth. Paper-thin. A name from a ledger.

"Say it again."

"Orsen." Better. Barely.

"Three weeks ago," Jude said, "you knocked on my gate at moonset. Do you remember?"

He grasped for it. The dream pulled at him, offered him instead a dusk in Vessa-sel-Anh, his mother's voice, the smell of plums — *no* — he held on to the question like a rail in floodwater. "I — knocked. On a gate. It was cold. My feet were cold. There was a key." He touched his chest, where the brass key hung on its cord, thorn-shaped, biting gently into his skin through his shirt when he lay on it. "The key was cold too."

"You spoke a language I'd never heard," Jude said, "and I understood every word. You asked if the door was real. Remember what you told me after? You said—" and here Jude's voice did something strange, softened and roughened at once, "—you said, *'Everything else burned without fire.'*"

Burned without fire. Yes. He remembered saying it. He remembered the feeling of it — the whole country going out like a lamp, not destroyed but *unlit*, folded away, and him falling through the fold—

"And then," Jude went on, quietly, relentlessly, "I made you tea that tasted terrible, because I am a keeper and not a cook, and you drank all of it anyway to be polite, and you made a face the entire time, and I thought—" he paused, and Orsen saw the man searching for the true sentence and deciding, for once, to say it — "I thought, *this child has crossed the end of the world alone, and he is still polite. Whatever else happens, I will not fail him.*"

Orsen's eyes were hot. "You didn't make a face at the tea?"

"I made many faces at the tea. Internally. Where they belong."

Something cracked in Orsen's chest — not breaking, opening — and a laugh came out of him, wet and surprised, in his own voice, in the trade-tongue, and the dream's grip loosened just enough to hurt.

"Keep talking," he said quickly, before the gold light could close back over. "Please. Tell me the rest. Tell me the small things."

So Jude talked. He sat on the floor like it was nothing, like keepers sat on floors every day, and he gave Orsen back his three weeks piece by piece, the way you'd return scattered pages to a book. The night in the vaults when Orsen had spoken to a sealed crate and the crate had answered, unlocking itself with a sigh, spilling out ribbons the color of dusk. The morning Jude had tried to teach him the trade-word for *bread* and Orsen had taught him six Vessian words for it instead, and Jude had written them all in the margin of his inventory book, in a keeper's hand, like they were items worth keeping. The game they'd invented on the road east, naming things the old tongue had no word for yet — lampposts, boots, boredom — making up new words together, laughing in hedgerows, building a language that belonged to neither country and therefore, somehow, to both.

"You called boredom *'shelveth'*," Jude said. "Shelf-wind. Wind blowing through empty shelves."

"Because that's what it feels like."

"It does," Jude agreed gravely. "I have been a keeper for nineteen years. I know shelf-wind intimately."

And Orsen laughed again, and held on to the laugh, and held on to the man's ink-stained hands and the terrible tea and the word *shelveth* and the key biting his chest, these few bright coins of a life that was actually his, small change against the dream's vast golden fortune — but his, minted in his own name.

"Stay," he said, when Jude finally moved to rise. "Don't go downstairs yet. If you stop talking, I'll—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Jude saw it, and sat back down, and stayed until the gray light through the window turned to actual morning, telling him small true things about a small true life.

* * *

But dreams are patient, and countries are patient, and the house remembered.

That evening, helping Jude carry water up from the cistern, Orsen passed the mirror in the landing — a cheap thing, spotted with age — and stopped.

For half a heartbeat, the face looking back at him was older. Longer. A boy of twelve or thirteen, orchard-tanned, with a small white scar at the hairline from a fall off the terraces. *Sefan's* face, superimposed on his own like breath on glass.

Then it was gone, and only Orsen looked out, pale city-child, unknown parentage, three weeks old to the world.

He stood very still. Downstairs, Jude was lighting the lamp, and the house was breathing through its window-gaps, counting under its breath, and far to the west — Orsen could feel it now, a pressure behind his sternum like weather — something was coming through the forgotten alleys of the city, wearing the shape of everything Vessia had thrown away.

"Jude," he called down, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him. "How long do we stay here?"

A pause. The scratch of a match. Then Jude's voice, carefully level, in the tone of a man reading bad news in a ledger:

"Not long, I think. Someone knocked at the chandlery next door an hour ago. And whatever it was—" the match flared, and his shadow jumped huge against the stairwell wall, "—the house stopped singing."
