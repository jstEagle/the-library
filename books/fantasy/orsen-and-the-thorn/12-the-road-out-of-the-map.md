## 12. The Road Out of the Map

The road east had been a cart track when they found it, rutted and apologetic, the kind of road that existed only because no one had bothered to unmake it. By the second morning it was something else entirely.

Jude noticed it first in his feet.

He was not a man accustomed to noticing his feet. Nineteen years of inventory had taught him to notice everything — the weight of a crate, the temperature of a seal, the exact shade of wax that meant a door had been opened and closed properly — but he had never once thought about how a road felt from above, through boot leather, through bone. Now he could not stop thinking about it. The ruts were gone. Somewhere in the night, without any hand laying stone or pulling weed, the track had straightened beneath them, and the surface had gone smooth as poured honey, and it sloped — almost imperceptibly — toward the center of the way, so that walking on it felt less like travel and more like being carried.

"Processional," Orsen said.

Jude looked down at the boy. Orsen was walking with his hands behind his back, the way an old man walks a garden he has tended for forty years, and his voice, when he spoke, came out doubled — a child's voice with something vast underneath it, like a bell rung inside a cathedral.

"What did you say?"

"The road." Orsen pointed ahead with his chin. "It's a processional way. They built them so the dead could be walked home standing up. You don't want the dead to stumble." He frowned then, as if hearing himself, and pressed both palms against his eyes. "I don't know why I said that. I don't know what a processional way is."

"You do," Jude said quietly. "Part of you does."

They walked on. Behind them, if Jude turned to look — and he made himself turn only once, because looking back felt like an invitation — the road resumed its old crooked self at exactly the point where Vessia's memory ended and the world's indifference began. Ahead of them, it ran true as a plumb line toward mountains that had been, until yesterday, hills.

* * *

Orsen's POV:

There were two boys walking east, and Orsen could feel both of them inside his own skin.

One of them was seven years old and had a blister coming up on his left heel and was afraid of the Thorn the way children are afraid of things under beds — completely, but also vaguely, the fear shapeless as fog. The other boy had never seen a bed. The other boy had slept in harvest fields with his cousins' warm bodies stacked around him like firewood, had eaten honey-bread at the Festival of the First Turning, had stood on his mother's feet while she danced and let her carry him across the courtyard on the soles of her sandals.

The other boy remembered a city burning without fire.

That was the part Orsen could not make fit anywhere. He understood burning — everyone understood burning; you learned it from candles and hearths and the cautionary songs about touching stoves. But the city in his head had burned the way a name burns when someone decides to stop saying it. There had been no smoke. There had been no ash. There had been a morning when the harbor was full of ships and an afternoon when the harbor was full of water that had never touched a hull, and the people had not screamed, because screaming requires someone left to hear it, and by the time the erasure reached them there was no one left outside Vessia who could have heard anything at all.

"Jude," he said, because saying Jude's name helped. It was a rope thrown from one boy to the other. "Tell me something true."

Jude glanced down at him. The keeper had gotten good at this game in the four days since the safehouse, since Pellam — Orsen didn't let himself think the word *since Pellam*, he put the word away in a box and sat on the box — since then, anyway, Jude had learned that the boy needed small true things the way other travelers needed water.

"Something true," Jude said. "All right. The Guild pays its gatekeepers on the first of every month, and for nineteen years I have been paid on the first of every month, and I have never once spent the money on anything I wanted."

"That's sad."

"It's true, though. You asked for true, not happy." A pause. The road hummed faintly under their boots, a sound like a held note. "Here's another. When you knocked on my gate, I knew your language before you spoke it. Not the words. The *shape* of it. Like recognizing a song from the room it used to be played in." He said it carefully, watching the road rather than the boy. "I've been trying not to think about why."

Orsen considered this. Inherited memory moved in him now like weather — fronts rolling through, sometimes a soft rain of images, sometimes a storm that took his whole body and made him stand still in the middle of the road while a harvest festival happened behind his eyes. But between the fronts there were clear hours, and in those hours he was only himself, and being only himself had started to feel like a place he visited rather than a place he lived.

"Why do you keep asking me for true things?" Jude said suddenly. It wasn't quite a question. It was a man opening a door he'd been leaning against.

"Because my own head isn't true anymore," Orsen said. "It's got two countries in it. Yours is smaller. I can hold all of yours at once."

Jude laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed, but real. "Two countries," he repeated. "Gods. You're seven."

"I'm also two hundred," Orsen said, "on my mother's side."

And then the front rolled in again, and he stopped seeing the road, and he was standing in a temple colonnade watching priests carry a bronze door on their shoulders through falling petals, and somewhere very far away a man named Jude kept hold of his hand so he wouldn't wander off the edge of the world.

* * *

Jude's POV:

The village had no name on any map Jude had ever inventoried, which was itself remarkable, because Jude had inventoried maps the way other men inventoried coin. He knew the eastern territories the way a jeweler knows flaws: the market towns, the toll bridges, the villages that paid their levies in wool and the ones that paid in silence. This village should not have existed. It sat in a fold of country that the surveyors had marked as poor grazing land, and it should have been six hovels and a goat.

It was forty houses with white walls and blue shutters, arranged around a well in the pattern of a wheel.

"They're singing," Orsen whispered.

They were. Jude heard it before he properly heard it — a sound rising from the open doors and the fields beyond, men and women and children, all of them humming the same melody, unhurried, in unison, the way a congregation hums when it knows a hymn so deeply it has forgotten the words are separate from the tune. Except these people were scattered — in doorways, in gardens, driving a cart — and none of them appeared to be listening to one another, and yet every voice was in perfect time.

No one taught them that song. Jude was certain of it the way he was certain of ledger arithmetic. The melody had a structure he recognized without knowing from where, a rising phrase that bent back on itself like —

Like a key turning.

His skin went cold. He knew that phrase. He had heard it in the vaults, in the sealed crates, in the weeks before flight, when Orsen's voice had unlocked Vessian relics like old friends answering an old friend's call. The song the villagers were humming was a Vessian working-song. It was the sound of a stored country bleeding back through the fabric of the map.

"We shouldn't stop," Jude said.

"You're already stopping," Orsen said, which was true, which annoyed him.

An old woman was drawing water at the well. She saw them on the road — a tall thin man in a gatekeeper's coat gone gray at the seams, and a dark-haired child holding his hand — and she did not startle, did not reach for a hoe or call out a warning. She simply looked at Orsen for a long moment, and the humming around them faltered, house by house, field by field, like candles going out in a draft, and then every person in the village turned to look at the boy at the same time.

Jude stepped half in front of him. Old habit. You shielded the assets. Then he caught himself — *he is not an asset* — and hated how long it had taken.

The old woman set down her bucket. She crossed the packed earth of the square, slowly, joints complaining, and when she reached them she knelt. Knelt. Forty houses' worth of villagers watched their elder kneel in the dust before a seven-year-old, and began, softly, to weep.

"Vessarai," she said to Orsen. And then, in the trade-tongue, in a voice rusted from disuse: "Little king. We kept the roads clean. All these years, we kept them clean, and we didn't know why."

"I'm not —" Orsen started, and stopped, because the woman had taken his free hand and pressed it against her cheek, and whatever he had been about to deny died in his throat.

Jude stood in the wheel-patterned square of a village that did not exist and felt awe move through him like cold water — and under the awe, urgent as a second heartbeat, mounting dread. Because he understood now what the old woman's words meant. *We kept the roads clean.* The erasure had taken names, taken borders, taken two centuries of memory from the world. But it had not taken the body's knowledge. Hands that had swept processional ways had handed the sweeping down, grandmother to daughter, as a habit without a reason. Songs had survived as tunes without words. Roads had survived as instincts without destinations.

Vessia had not been destroyed. Vessia had been *stored* — he'd read the clause, he knew — and a stored thing presses outward on its container, always, the way water presses on a dam.

And here was the heir, walking east along the pressure line, and every mile the container grew thinner.

"How far?" he asked the old woman.

She rose, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand, and pointed up the processional way to where the false hills stood against the sky. "To the door? No one goes past the pass. The pass is where the forgetting gets thick." Her gaze slid to Orsen and softened and feared all at once. "But the road remembers for us, little king. Follow the road. It's been waiting longer than we have."

Orsen's POV:

Above the pass, the world stopped pretending.

Orsen felt the change come gradually and then all at once, the way sleep comes. For a morning they climbed through ordinary country — pine, scree, a hawk riding a thermal with the patience of a landlord — and then the trees began to be wrong. Not wrong in themselves; each tree was a fine, healthy pine. Wrong in their *arrangement*. They stood in rows. Not planted rows — ceremonial ones, flanking the road at intervals of exactly thirty paces, and between them grew flowers that had no business blooming at altitude, blue star-shaped blossoms that Orsen's other childhood knew by name: *selvane*, the door-flower, planted wherever a threshold wanted honoring.

"Jude," he said. "Look at the flowers."

"I see them." Jude crouched, touched one blossom, and drew his fingers back as though it had hummed against his skin. Perhaps it had. "They're catalogued," he said, half to himself, in the flat voice he used when the ledgers had said something terrible. "Blue starflower. Common ornamental. Grows in hedgerows throughout the eastern provinces." He looked up, and his face was pale under its windburn. "Except they don't. I've processed eleven thousand crates of eastern goods, Orsen. I have never once seen this flower. The ledgers say it exists everywhere and I have never seen it anywhere. That's not an error. That's a *cover story*. Someone wrote this flower into the world's paperwork so no one would ask why it only grows here."

The road crested the pass, and Vessia lay below them.

Not the drowned valley — that was farther east, Orsen knew, the way he knew his own heartbeat, the stored heart waiting in open water. This was the approach, the outer court, the country's echo made solid. A wide green vale where the processional way descended in long switchbacks toward a river that ran silver and *straight*, refusing to meander, a river that flowed like a road because once it had been treated like one. Terraces cut the hillsides in patterns too regular for nature and too organic for engineering. Far off, a shape that might have been a ruined tower stood wrapped in selvane vines, blue stars climbing its stones like ivy writing a signature.

And the air — the air was the worst and best of it. It smelled like bread baking and rain on hot stone and something else, something underneath, a scent with no trade-tongue name, and breathing it was like breathing after a long illness. Orsen's chest hurt with it. His eyes filled without permission.

*I know this place,* he thought, and then, with the vertigo of the truly divided: *Which of me knows this place?*

"Orsen." Jude's hand on his shoulder, steady, warm, human, nineteen years of gatehouse and grief and stubbornness in one grip. "Talk to me. Which boy is standing here?"

Orsen thought about it honestly. He took inventory of himself the way Jude took inventory of crates.

"The one with the blister," he said. "Mostly. The other one is —" He searched. "The other one is homesick. But it's a strange homesickness. It's like missing a house you never lived in because your mother sang about it." He wiped his face with his sleeve, fiercely, a seven-year-old gesture, and clung to it. "I'm mostly me, Jude. But the me-part is getting smaller. Every mile east, it's getting smaller. Like the country is pouring into me faster than I can pour out."

Jude was quiet for a moment. Below them the silver river caught the afternoon light and threw it back in long bars, and somewhere down in the green vale, faint but unmistakable, a bell rang once — though there was no tower near enough to ring it, and no hand near enough to pull.

"When we get to the door," Jude said at last, "you won't have to hold it anymore. Whatever happens. Whatever we choose. You won't have to be the container." He said it like a vow, and Orsen, who had learned in four hard days to read this man's silences better than most people read books, believed him.

They started down the switchbacks together, boy and keeper, the road carrying them as it had carried the dead home standing up, two hundred years ago, in a country that was no longer supposed to exist.

Behind them, at the crest of the pass, the blue flowers closed their stars one by one, like eyes.

Ahead, in the vale, the bell rang again. And again. And Orsen realized with a lurch that it was not ringing for evening, or for prayer, or for the dead.

It was ringing an alarm — in a language only two living people could understand, and one of them was seven years old, and the other had just understood, from the rhythm alone, that the message was simple and terrible:

*Someone else has reached the door.*
