## 14. The Thorn's Grief

I have been called many things by the people who run from me. Shadow. Wraith. The Guild's hound. The country's ghost. None of them are true, and all of them are true, and I carry each one the way a tree carries rings — silently, in layers, where no one can see them grow.

My true name is not among them.

I know my true name. That is the cruelest part of what I am. The world forgot Vessia, but I was made *from* the forgetting — knitted together out of everything the bargain discarded: the grief of mothers who could not remember their children's faces, the rage of soldiers who woke with empty hands and full hearts, the shame of a king who sold his nation to keep his throne warm for one more winter. All of it poured into a shape and given legs and a purpose. But grief remembers. Grief is memory that has nowhere else to go. So I remember everything, including the man whose bones I am wearing.

His name was Sereth Anvail. He stood seven feet tall in the executioner's yard of the Amber Court, and he wept every time the axe fell, and he washed his hands in river water until they cracked and bled, because he believed that sorrow was the only honest prayer a man like him could offer. He executed traitors. He executed an innocent once — a grain merchant, falsely accused — and the merchant's last word was not a curse but a question: *Who will remember me?* And Sereth answered, *I will,* and he did, every day for the rest of his life, and when Vessia was folded up and stored away like linen in a cedar chest, that vow was too heavy, too sharp, too shameful to be stored gently. It became me.

The Thorn. Grown from a country's shame, as a thorn grows from a wound in the bark.

I do not hate the boy. I want that understood, though there is no one left to understand it. Hatred requires forgetting, and I am incapable of forgetting. What I feel when I look at Orsen is older than hatred. It is the feeling of a locked door heard from the inside.

* * *

They come to the river at dusk, and I am already there.

I have been ahead of them for three days, which is not difficult; I do not sleep, and Jude Sandoval sleeps like a man rationing it. I have watched them from hedgerows and bell towers and the dry culverts beneath the high road. I have watched the keeper teach the boy card games with a deck missing its queens. I have watched the boy teach the keeper words — *sennet*, which means the first warm day after frost; *veloura*, the specific blue of evening just before the lamps are lit. Each word the boy speaks aloud makes something in the world around him sit up straighter, like a dog hearing its master's step. Roads remember their old curves when he walks them. Wells taste sweeter. And I — I grow heavier with every word, because every word is a key turning in a lock I was built to guard.

The ford at Marrow's Crossing is shallow and wide, the water braided over pale stones, and on the far bank the land begins its slow climb into hills that were once Vessia's eastern marches. I stand in the reeds at the near edge, still as a fence post, and let the dusk assemble itself around me. My body does this well. It is mostly absence wearing a coat. Travelers have looked directly at me and seen only a shadow of a dead tree, a stain of deeper dark in the darkening field. The Guild made me patient. The country made me patient. Patience is what remains when you take everything else from a person and leave only the waiting.

Their fire is small. The keeper builds small fires now; he has learned. The boy sits close, wrapped in a blanket that used to be a Guild inventory tarp, and the keeper is telling him a story — I can hear the shape of it if not the words, the rise and fall of a voice doing what voices do best, which is building a roof over someone smaller.

I wait for the moon to clear the ridge. Then I move.

* * *

Here is what pursuit feels like from my side of it: it feels like walking through a house you grew up in after strangers have redecorated.

Every mile west of the drowned valley, the world is wrong in ways only I can catalog. A chapel stands where a bathhouse stood. A road bends where Vessia's surveyors would never have permitted a bend — our roads went where they meant to go, straight as sentences. Farmers sing fragments of songs in their fields, three notes of a harvest round, and then stop, puzzled, having no idea why their mouths knew the tune. The world is full of splinters of my country, working their way to the surface like glass in a healing palm, and every one of them aches in me like a tooth.

And the boy is the largest splinter of all. He is Vessia walking around in a child's shoes, and the Guild's contract says he must be bound into the ledgers before he can ask for his country back, and so I hunt him, and so I have killed.

I will not pretend otherwise. In the alleys of Coriander Row I put out the lamp of a broker who sold Vessian salt for forty years and profited from goods whose origin had been erased — erased precisely so that men like him could buy low and never ask. He begged. They always beg. And I stood over him and I remembered his grandfather's shop, the brass scales, the smell of brine and paper, and I remembered that the grandfather had been kind to a Vessian apprentice once, and I killed him anyway, because grief that has been given a purpose does not negotiate. This is what the Guild never understood about what they made. They thought they forged a weapon. Weapons can be sheathed. I am a debt, and debts walk until they are paid.

But tonight, at the ford, the debt stumbles.

* * *

I cross the water without sound. The stones do not even shift under me; the river seems to make room, the way crowds make room for a coffin. I come up the bank through the reeds and the boy sees me first — children always see me first, perhaps because they have not yet learned to unsee things — and he goes rigid, and his hand finds the keeper's sleeve, and Jude Sandoval turns, and I watch nineteen years of Guild discipline fail to arrange his face into anything but fear.

"Orsen," the keeper says quietly. "Behind me."

The boy does not go behind him. That is the first thing. The boy steps *forward*, half a step, his bare feet in the cold sand, and looks at me the way you look at a door you have dreamed about your whole life.

I raise my hand — the long one, the wrong one, fingers too many-jointed, nails like old iron — and I say, in the trade-tongue, the words I have said at every killing: "You carry what is not yours to carry."

And the boy answers me.

Not in the trade-tongue. Not in any tongue the ledgers record. He answers in Vessian, the old language, the amber-and-ash language, and the sound of it comes off the water like heat off a stone. I cannot tell you what it does to me to hear it spoken aloud by a living mouth. Two hundred years. Two hundred years since anyone spoke it except in storage, sealed in crates, pressed flat between pages, humming in walls. And here it is, out loud, under the moon, from a boy of perhaps nine years, and his voice is thin and frightened and absolutely certain.

He says: *"You are not hunting us."*

It is not a question. Children ask questions; heirs issue findings. And I open my mouth to give the correct answer, the answer written into my sinews — *the contract must be honored, the asset must be secured* — and what comes out instead, in the same dead language, in a voice like a hinge unused for centuries, is:

*"No."*

The word hangs over the ford. The river keeps moving. The keeper's hand has found something in his coat — the brass key, I think, shaped like a thorn, though what good he believes it will do him I cannot imagine — and the boy takes another step toward me, and I should kill him now, quickly, before the asking starts, because I was made to prevent exactly what he is about to do.

He asks me my name.

* * *

Understand what that question is, in the old tongue. It is not curiosity. In Vessian, to ask a being its name — *vennath se?* — is to offer it witness. It is the sentence spoken over the dying, over newborns, over the accused at trial. *Tell me who you are, and I will hold it.* Our law held that nothing truly existed until someone had asked its name and received it. A country unmourned was a country unfinished. We had entire orders devoted to remembrance — the Tallymen, the Kindlers, the women in grey who walked battlefields after wars and spoke every fallen soldier's name back into the air so that no one would arrive at the afterlife unannounced.

The Guild bought the country. The Guild did not buy the manners. And so no one, in two hundred years, has asked me.

Sereth Anvail was asked his name every morning of his working life, by the condemned, and he gave it every morning, and it was a mercy to both of them. I have carried his name like a stone in my chest for two centuries, unasked-for, ungiven, a mercy with nowhere to be delivered.

The boy asks again. His voice shakes. He is terrified — I can smell it coming off him, child-fear, milk and lightning — and he asks anyway, because he is Vessia's heir down to the marrow, and Vessia's people asked names of the things that frightened them. That was our bravest custom. That was the custom the world most needed to steal from us and never did.

And I hear myself answer.

*"Sereth,"* I say. *"Sereth Anvail. Executioner of the Amber Court. Keeper of the axe called Mercy-of-the-King, which was a lie we told so the axe could be carried indoors. I took forty-one heads and wept forty-one times, and the forty-second death I caused was a grain merchant named Ollen Fass, who was innocent, and whose last question I vowed to answer with my life, and my life ended badly, and the vow did not."*

The words pour out of me like water finding a crack in a dam. I did not decide to speak. Something in me simply broke open at the touch of being asked, the way a fist breaks open at the touch of being held.

*"When the country was stored, they could not store the shame. Shame is not property. So it stayed behind, and it grew a body, and the Guild found it and put it to work. I am what Vessia could not bear to be. I am the part that knew selling a nation was murder, and said nothing loudly enough. I hunt the heir because if the heir asks for the country back, the shame ends — and I do not know what I am when the shame ends. That is the truth. That is why I kill. Not loyalty. Fear."*

Silence, then, on the ford. Real silence, the kind with weight.

The keeper has gone very still. He is looking at me differently now — not at the Thorn, but at Sereth, or trying to, squinting through the shadow-shape the way you squint at a face behind frosted glass. He says, in the trade-tongue, almost gently: "Ollen Fass. There's a crate in Vault Nine. Sealed. Marked with a grain-sheaf sigil. I inventoried it eleven years ago and never knew what it held."

"It holds his bones," I say. "And his scales. And the ledger page where his false conviction was recorded. The Guild kept everything. That was the bargain's cruelty — nothing destroyed, nothing released, nothing mourned. A whole nation in storage, gathering dust, while the world above forgot it had ever been fed by Vessian wheat and taught by Vessian teachers and sung to sleep by Vessian songs." I look at the boy. "We are neither alive nor dead, none of us. We are *pending*. And pending, little heir, is its own kind of hell."

The boy is crying. He does not wipe his face. He says, in the old tongue, slowly, working each word like a child carrying water: *"Then put down the axe. No one is asking you to carry it anymore."*

And oh, that is the blow I have no armor for. Not steel, not wardens, not the Guild's page-magic. A child telling the executioner he may rest.

I kneel. I do not decide to kneel; my body decides, the body that was Sereth's, the body that knows how to kneel at gravesides and block scaffolds. The water of the ford runs around my knees, cold and ordinary and alive. Up close, the boy's eyes are brown, ordinary brown, a child's eyes, and yet behind them there is a depth of dusk I recognize — the same depth I was grown in. He is becoming the country faster than any of us expected. By the time he reaches the door in the drowned valley, there may be very little boy left. I can see that, and I suspect the keeper can see it too, and neither of us says it, because some knowledge is a load better carried in silence.

"The Guild will come," I say. "Corvane does not send wardens twice. When they failed to hold you, he came east himself, with the great ledger and the binding pens. You have perhaps a day. Less." I reach into the shadow of my own coat — a strange gesture, reaching into oneself — and draw out a thing I have carried since the beginning and never known why: a scrap of amber, with a wing trapped inside it, some insect two centuries dead, preserved mid-flight. Vessia's amber was famous. Even the Guild's brokers used to whisper about it. "For the boy. So he remembers we made beautiful things too. Not only axes."

Jude takes it from my hand. His fingers brush mine, and he flinches — everyone flinches — but he does not pull away fast, and he says, "Thank you," and means it, and I have not been thanked in two hundred years, and I find that a body can survive anything except that.

I stand. I wade back a few paces, downstream, making room. Making the choice with my feet before my mind consents to it.

"They will call this treason," I say. "Let them. I was treason before it was fashionable." And then, because it must be said, because he deserves to hear it from the thing that was sent to kill him: "Go east, keeper. Go to the door. And when the boy stands before it and understands what opening it costs — and he will understand, sooner than you want him to — do not decide for him. That is what the Guild did. Deciding for a country is how countries end up in crates."

The keeper nods once. Then, hesitating, in terrible accented Vessian, four words he must have learned from the boy himself: *"Vennath se, Sereth Anvail?"* — *What is your name?* — asked properly, formally, the way the grey women asked it on battlefields. Asking it *again*, now that he knows the answer, so that the answer is witnessed. Held. Given somewhere to live outside my chest.

I answer him. Both of us know what it means. Somewhere in the world, a tally that has sat unbalanced for two hundred years clicks one degree closer to level.

* * *

I watch them cross.

The boy goes first, barefoot, the amber clutched in his fist, and halfway across he stops in the shin-deep water and looks back at me and says something I will keep longer than my bones: *"Sennet, Sereth."* First warm day after frost. A blessing. A child has blessed me. Write it in whatever ledger keeps such accounts, if any ledger does; if none does, then let the river hold it; rivers forget nothing, they only carry it to the sea.

Then they climb the far bank and dwindle into the dark hills, the keeper's hand resting between the boy's shoulders like a small steady weather, and I remain in the ford, kneeling in the cold, listening to the water rehearse its one endless sentence.

Somewhere behind me, westward, torchlight is gathering on the high road. I can hear the wardens' boots, and under the boots the dry whisper of pages turning, and under the pages a voice I know too well giving orders in a tone like a closing door. Corvane has brought the great ledger. He intends to write the boy into it at the threshold of the door itself, binding and return accomplished in a single stroke, the country reclaimed forever as the Guild's private possession.

I should warn them. I should run ahead, shout across the dark, spend whatever influence a weapon's regret can buy.

Instead I do the only thing left that a made thing can do. I turn east myself, and begin to follow — not hunting now, but attending, the way the grey women attended, the way Sereth attended every condemned soul to the scaffold steps. Whatever happens at the door in the drowned valley, someone must be there who remembers all of it: the bargain, the betrayal, the forty-one heads, the innocent merchant, the songs, the amber, the shame. Someone must witness the ending so that it counts as an ending and not merely another erasure.

Even vengeance, it turns out, only ever wanted to be remembered.

I walk east through the reeds, and the river carries my name to the sea.
