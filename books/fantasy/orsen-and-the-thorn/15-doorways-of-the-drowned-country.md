## 15. Doorways of the Drowned Country

The valley had a name once. Orsen knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat — without wanting to, without asking.

*Selvane,* said the memory, arriving like weather. *The Valley of Selvane, where the river bent twice before it reached the sea, and the reeds grew tall enough to hide a child who did not want to be found.*

He stood at the valley's rim with Jude beside him, both of them looking down at water where there should have been fields. The drowned country spread out below them, gray-green and still, broken here and there by the tips of things that refused to stay hidden: a chimney, a bell-tower's crown, the carved head of some guardian beast rising from the flood like a swimmer too tired to go on.

"That wasn't on any map," Jude said quietly. He had his ledger-satchel slung across his chest, and his hand kept drifting to it the way other men touched amulets. "I've crossed this province four times. There was a road down there. Farms. A town called—"

"Maravel," Orsen said.

Jude went very still.

"The town was called Maravel," Orsen repeated, and heard the words come out in two languages at once, one layered over the other like paint over older paint. "It had eleven bridges. The eleventh was only for funerals. Everyone walked it in silence so the dead wouldn't learn the way back."

"Where did you—" Jude started, then stopped himself. Orsen watched him swallow the question. They had stopped asking *where do you get these things* three days ago, somewhere past the humming villages, somewhere past the night Orsen woke up weeping for a harvest he had never seen. Now Jude only asked *what do you need*, and *are you still in there*, and those were better questions anyway.

Because Orsen didn't know where the memories came from anymore. That was the truth he carried like a stone in his chest. At first they had come like visitors — a festival's drumbeat surfacing while he ate, his mother's voice singing half a lullaby before sleep. Visitors knocked. These didn't knock anymore. They simply *arrived*, faster now, crowding in behind his eyes before his own thoughts could finish forming, until some mornings he opened his mouth and wasn't sure which boy's voice would come out.

"I know the way," he said instead. "Down through the reeds. There's a causeway."

"There's water, Orsen. All the way down."

"There's a causeway *under* the water." He heard himself say it and felt the rightness of it settle into his bones like a key finding its lock. "It only shows itself to people Maravel wants to see it. That's not a story. That's just — that's how it is." He frowned, because he could feel the seam of it, the place where inherited knowledge met the boy who had arrived at a gatehouse speaking a dead tongue. "I don't remember learning that. But I've always known it. Both things are true."

Jude looked at him for a long moment. In the weeks since the gatehouse, since Pellam's blood on the inventory floor, since the road east that kept straightening beneath their feet into old processional ways, Jude had developed a way of listening that Orsen had never seen from an adult — as if the adult were the one being taught.

"Then we walk," Jude said. "And if the water doesn't want us, we turn around and find another way."

"It'll want us." Orsen started down the slope. "It's been waiting two hundred years."

* * *

The reeds rose around them taller than Orsen's head, and they were wrong in a way that made his skin prickle: they smelled of harvest. Not rot, not river-mud, but cut grain and woodsmoke and the sweet dust of threshing floors. Reeds shouldn't smell like that. Drowned things shouldn't smell like anything but drowning.

*They planted the reeds after,* said a memory, sliding in uninvited. *After the storing. Grandmother said the whole valley smelled like bread for a year, as if the country couldn't decide whether it had died or been put away for winter.*

Orsen stumbled. Jude caught his elbow without comment.

"How many is that today?" Jude asked softly.

"I don't—" Orsen counted, the way Jude had taught him, the way you counted coins or breaths. "Nine. Since dawn. No — ten. I forgot the one about the grandmother."

"You forgot because it isn't yours to carry alone." Jude crouched so they were eye to eye, there among the impossible reeds. His face had grown thinner on the road; the keeper's meticulousness had worn down to something rawer, kinder. "Listen to me. When they come, you say them out loud. To me. Every one. That's what I'm for now. I keep things. It turns out I can keep memories too."

"They're not mine," Orsen whispered. It was the fear underneath all the other fears, the one he hadn't been able to say since the safehouse, since the morning he'd woken unable to recall his own mother's face — his real mother, the one from before, whose name he still knew but whose face had gone soft and water-blurred. "Jude, what if there's no me left? What if I walk down to that door and by the time we reach it, I'm not Orsen anymore? What if I'm just Vessia wearing a boy?"

Jude's jaw tightened. For a moment he looked like the man from the ledgers, precise and frightened of errors. Then he reached out and took Orsen's hand, and his palm was warm and calloused from nineteen years of turning pages.

"Then I'll stand at that door and tell you about the night you knocked on my gate," Jude said. "I'll tell you that your hand was too small to reach the bell-pull and you used the knocker twice, and that when I asked your name you gave me a word I didn't know yet, and that I understood it anyway, which should have terrified me more than it did. I'll tell you about the crates in the vault that sang when you spoke. About the rain we named nine ways. I will keep *you*, Orsen. That's a promise from a keeper, and keepers' promises are the only kind the Guild never figured out how to break."

Orsen nodded, because his throat was too tight for words, and they went on down into the reeds.

* * *

The water began at their ankles and it was warm.

That was the first impossible thing. The second was that it stayed at their ankles — no matter the dips in the drowned road beneath, no matter the places where the flood must have been deep enough to drown a church, the water held at ankle-height, patient as a servant, as if the valley itself had decided exactly how much of itself it would permit them to wade through.

Ahead of them, the sunken causeway showed itself in flashes: pale stones beneath the green water, running straight toward the bell-tower crown in the middle distance. Here and there a lantern-post still stood above the surface, its iron curled into the shape of a heron, and as they passed each one, the heron's head turned — slowly, courteously — to watch them go.

"They're counting us," Orsen murmured.

"What?"

"The posts. They always counted travelers on the funeral bridge. Eleven herons, eleven bridges—" He stopped. Put his hand over his mouth. Too late; the memory was already pouring through, wide and bright and terrible.

*A wedding on the ninth bridge. White ribbons on the railings, and the bride's brother drunk and singing, and the river below carrying petals. Music from every doorway in Maravel. Someone's grandmother dancing with someone's grandson, and the fiddler playing until his fingers bled, and nobody minding, because it was Maravel and Maravel loved loudly—*

"—and it loved loudly," Orsen finished aloud, gasping, standing knee-deep in warm water with tears running down his face that weren't entirely his grief. "There was a wedding. I wasn't at the wedding. I was never at the wedding. But I can hear the fiddle, Jude, I can *hear* it—"

"I hear you." Jude held his shoulders. "Say it all. Don't fight it."

So Orsen said it. He described the ribbons and the drunk brother and the fiddler's bleeding fingers while the heron-posts turned their heads and the warm water lapped at them, and somewhere in the middle of telling it, the grief changed shape — became less like drowning and more like mourning, which is a thing a person can survive. When he finished, the valley seemed quieter. Attentive.

"Better?" Jude asked.

"No." Orsen wiped his face. "But fuller. Like — like I'm less full of holes."

They waded on. And the memories kept coming, faster now, as though the valley knew its heir was near and could not help calling out everything it had saved: the names of streets (*Coppersmith Row, the Street of Nine Lanterns, Weepers' Lane, which wasn't sad at all, it was where the midwives lived*), the taste of honeyed eel at the autumn market, the exact blue of the king's banners, the sound of the great bell in Maravel's tower that rang only when the country needed to gather. With every step the border between *Orsen* and *Vessia* wore thinner, a shoreline eroding, and he felt himself becoming less a boy walking through a memory and more a memory learning to walk.

Once, he caught himself giving Jude directions in the old tongue and translating only afterward. Once, he answered a question Jude hadn't asked yet. Once — and this one frightened them both — he looked at his own reflection in the still water and saw, for half a heartbeat, an adult's face looking back: a woman's face, calm and crowned, with eyes like the deep end of the sky.

*The last queen,* came the thought, gentle as snowfall. *Who signed nothing. Who hid me in a boat and told me to forget her face, and I have failed her, and I am sorry, and I am coming home.*

"Orsen." Jude's voice, sharp with worry. "Whose face was that?"

"A queen's." His own voice sounded far away. "Jude, I think she was my mother. I think maybe all of them were my mother. I think a country might be a mother, when you're its last child." He looked down at his small hands in the green water. "I can't hold all of it. It keeps coming in and nothing goes out. I'm going to overflow."

"Then we hurry," Jude said, and there was something in his face Orsen had learned to read on the road — the keeper's terror of losing an entry, translated at last into the terror of losing a person. "Tell me where."

"Straight out. Past the tower. The door stands in the open water beyond it, where the valley is deepest. You'll know it when you see it because—" Because, said the memory, arriving complete: *because it is the only door in Vessia that was never attached to a house, and a door with no house is a promise with no one left to keep it.* "—because it doesn't belong to anything. It just stands there. Waiting to be kept."

* * *

They passed the bell-tower as the light went long and gold.

Up close, the drowned tower was greater than it had seemed from the rim — its crown breaking the surface like an island, its walls descending into green depths where windows glowed faintly, impossibly, with a light that was not sunlight and not lamplight but something stored. Preserved. Two hundred years of held breath, shining upward through the water.

Orsen stopped and laid his palm against the wet stone, and the whole valley exhaled.

He felt it happen — felt the country notice him the way a sleeper notices a hand on their forehead. The water warmed by a degree. The heron-posts all turned at once. Far below, in the drowned streets of Maravel, lights kindled in window after window after window, street after street, a whole city waking beneath the flood to look up at the small dark figure touching its tower.

"Jude," Orsen breathed. "They're awake. They're all awake. They've been asleep for two hundred years and they're *awake*—"

And the memories came then like a breached dam, like a choir given permission, thousands of voices where before there had been dozens — harvest songs and funeral silences and the names of every street and every bridge and every heron-post, the king's dying bargain and the queen's unsigned refusal and the smell of bread that lasted a year, all of it pouring into the small vessel of a nine-year-old boy, and Orsen cried out and would have fallen except that Jude caught him, held him up in the warm ankle-deep water, shouting his name, *Orsen, ORSEN, stay with me, stay with me—*

And inside the flood, Orsen did something he had never thought to do before.

He made room.

Not by pushing the memories away — he understood now, with a knowledge older than his own life, that they could not be pushed, only received. But he set them side by side instead of stacked. He let the wedding and the burning-without-fire and the lullaby and the gatehouse knocker all exist in the same country at once, the way a valley holds a river without complaint. He stopped trying to be a wall against Vessia and became, instead, its harbor.

The flood settled. Not gone — never gone — but flowing now instead of crashing.

When he opened his eyes, Jude was kneeling in the water holding him, and past Jude's shoulder, out where the valley deepened toward its heart, something stood reflected in the gold light of evening.

A door.

It stood alone in the open water, half a mile out, where no house stood and no wall ran and no reason for a door had existed for two centuries. It was tall and dark and plain, oak gone silver with waiting, and it stood upright upon the flood as if the water were a floor, and it had no frame, no building, no country around it — only itself, and the brass keyhole at its center, shaped unmistakably like a thorn.

The thorn-key hung at Orsen's neck on its cord. It had grown warm. It had been growing warmer for days, and now it pulled against the cord like a compass needle finding north, like a child finding its mother's hand in a crowd.

"We're here," Jude whispered. "Saints above. It's real."

Orsen looked at the door, and the door — he was almost sure of it — looked back.

And then the light on the water changed. Not sunset; something colder. Far behind them, back along the drowned causeway, the heron-posts were turning their heads away from Orsen and toward the shore they'd left — every iron bird at once, staring at something coming down through the reeds. The warm water around their ankles cooled by degrees, and far off, at the valley's rim, Orsen heard a sound he had hoped never to hear again: the dry rustle of pages turning, many pages, moving in wind that had no source.

Ledger-magic. Wardens. And a voice carrying flat and clear across the water, the voice of a man who had once offered mercy dressed as partnership:

"Keeper Sandoval. Heir of Vessia. The Guild has come to collect its property."

Orsen's hand closed around the thorn-key, and the drowned city below lit every window it had.
