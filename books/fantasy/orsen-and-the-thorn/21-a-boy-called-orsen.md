## 21. A Boy Called Orsen

The dreams stopped being someone else's on the first morning of autumn.

I woke in the narrow bed above the gatehouse — my bed now, though it had been Jude's for nineteen years before he gave it to me — and for a long moment I lay still, listening to the way the light came through the shutters. It came in slats. It fell across the floor in stripes. And I knew, with a certainty that required no memory at all, that this was *my* room, in *my* house, and that the smell of bread rising from the kitchen below belonged to no one's past but mine.

For two years, waking had been the hardest part of every day. Waking was when the borders inside me blurred. I would open my eyes and not know whether I was a boy called Orsen or a girl named Sefa who had drowned in the Vessia river flood, or an old man named Toma who had kept bees on the terraces above the capital and died believing his country would outlive him. I would open my eyes and grieve for people I had never met as if their funerals were happening that morning, because in some sense they were — two hundred years of held breath, all of it exhaling into me.

But that morning, the dead were quiet.

Not gone. That is the thing I want written down somewhere, in a ledger if necessary, in ink that will last: *not gone.* When we opened the door and let Vessia end, everyone expected the memories to leave me the way water leaves a broken cup. Corvane had warned of it. The wardens had whispered about it. Even Jude, gentle Jude, had braced himself for a boy who might wake up hollow, emptied of everything he'd carried, holding nothing but his own small life.

Instead the memories settled. They sank, the way silt sinks after a flood, and what remained standing above them was me.

I got up. I dressed. I went downstairs, and Maren — the cook, broad and loud and unimpressed by history — pushed a heel of bread and a dish of honey at me and said, "You look less haunted today, little archivist," and I said, "I'm not little," and she said, "You're nine," and I ate my breakfast and did not argue, because she was right, and because arguing with Maren was one of the few pleasures of my new life that required no inherited knowledge whatsoever.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things still stood, though it was not the same Guild that had bought a country and shelved it like a jar of preserves.

After the door opened — after the light and the names and the sigh heard in every language — the ledgers had needed rewriting from the first page. Not corrected. Rewritten. There is a difference, and I learned it early, because it became my work. Correction means making the record match what you wish had happened. Rewriting means telling the truth even when the truth indicts you. The old ledgers were full of corrections: entries where Vessia's purchase was recorded as an *acquisition of unclaimed territory*, where the dying king's signature was described as a *voluntary bequest*, where the word *people* appeared nowhere at all, as if a country could be bought without anyone living in it.

My job was to go through those pages and write what actually happened in the margins. In the old tongue when the old tongue fit better. In plain trade-speak when clarity mattered more. Sometimes both, side by side, so that any future reader could see the seam between the lie and the truth and know exactly where it ran.

They called me the first honest archivist. I did not feel honest. I felt like a boy doing penance for sins committed before he was born. But Pellam had told me once — one of the last things he told me, there in the safehouse with the Thorn coming up the stairs — that guilt inherited is still guilt, and the only honorable thing to do with it is carry it openly. So I carried it. I sat in the archive hall every day with my ink and my straightedge and my magnifying lens, and I wrote *this was theft* beside entries that had stood unchallenged for two centuries, and slowly, page by page, the Guild's great memory stopped being a vault of stolen things and started being something closer to a confession.

Jude found me there on the morning the dreams went quiet. He always knew where I was; he had a keeper's instinct for the location of lost things, and I suppose I still qualified.

"You slept," he said. It wasn't a question. He had learned to read my face the way other people read weather.

"I slept." I turned the page I was working on so he could see it: a shipping manifest from the year the border closed, listing crates of Vessian lullabies bound for a buyer in the southern ports. In the margin, in my careful hand, I had written the name of the woman who had sung them, because I knew her name now — not as a haunting, but as a fact, the way I knew my own. Ilset Vane. She had three children. She had hummed while she worked. Her voice had been low and slightly rough, like a door that needed oiling, and when she sang the rain-songs her youngest would fall asleep against her hip.

"Ilset Vane," Jude read aloud, testing it. "You sound different when you say these names."

"How?"

"Lighter. Before, when you spoke them, it was like watching someone hold up a weight to show you how heavy it was. Now it's like—" He searched for it. Jude was a man of precise words; it cost him something to reach for an imprecise one. "Now it's like introducing a friend."

I looked down at the manifest, at Ilset Vane's name sitting in the margin like a small lit window. "That's what they are now," I said. "Friends. Or — neighbors. People I grew up next to." I tried to explain it, because he asked, and because Jude always asked, and because explaining it helped me believe it was real. "When Vessia was stored, all those lives were pressed flat. Packed away. Every time one surfaced in me, it came up screaming, because it had been folded wrong for two hundred years. But now they've been *let out*. They ended. And things that are allowed to end can rest." I touched the edge of the page. "Ilset Vane isn't trapped in my head anymore. She's just someone I remember."

"And you remember her without becoming her."

"Without drowning in her." I smiled, and found that the smile was entirely mine. "I still know the rain has nine names. I still know how the processional roads were laid, stone by stone, and why the capital's gates faced east. But knowing isn't the same as grieving anymore. Knowing is just — knowing. Like knowing the trade-routes of the salt coast. It sits in me instead of sitting on me."

Jude sat down across the table from me, which he almost never did during working hours; the archive was my domain and he respected it the way he respected all borders, which was carefully and only with permission. "There's something I've been meaning to ask you," he said. "And I keep not asking it, because I'm afraid of the answer."

"The name," I said.

He blinked. "How—"

"Because you flinch every time you sign something near me. Because you start sentences with 'the boy' and stop yourself. Because you're Jude Sandoval, and you spent your whole life keeping other people's names in order, and you can't stand the fact that yours is missing from every ledger in this building." I closed the manifest gently. "Ask me."

He took a breath. "Do you want to keep it? The name Orsen? It was never registered. It was never in any book. After everything — after the inheritance, after the door — you could choose anything. A Vessian name. A new one entirely. No one would question it. After what this Guild did to your country, no one on earth would question it."

I thought about it. I had thought about it for two years, honestly, in the quiet corners of the nights when the memories were still too loud, and then more recently in the quieter corners of the nights when they began to settle. And here is what I had come to understand, the understanding arriving slow as dawn but arriving all the same:

The name Orsen was the only thing in my life that had never belonged to anyone else.

My face carried Vessia's bloodlines. My mind carried Vessia's dead. My hands knew crafts I had never been taught, my mouth knew songs I had never heard sung aloud. Everything about me was an inheritance, a bequest, a burden passed down a chain of custody two centuries long. But *Orsen* — the boy who knocked on a gate at moonset speaking a dead language, the boy Jude took in, the boy who learned to laugh again over Maren's burnt honey-cakes — that boy had been made in the space of a few weeks, out of fear and flight and one man's stubborn kindness. That boy was not inherited. That boy was *chosen*, in the only way a child ever chooses anything: by surviving into himself.

"Keep it," I said. "Register it. Put it in the ledger in your own handwriting, because I want it entered honestly, by someone who knows what honesty costs." I held his eyes. "Orsen. Son of no recorded house. Heir of a country that chose to end rather than be caged. First archivist of the reformed Guild. That's what I want it to say."

Jude's hand shook when he wrote it. I watched him press the nib down and draw each letter with the care of a man engraving stone, and when he finished he sat back and looked at the entry for a long time, and I saw his throat move.

"There," he said quietly. "In the book. Where it can't be taken back."

"That's the point of books," I said, "isn't it? Keeper."

* * *

Winter came, and with it the first class of students.

It had been Corvane's idea, in the end — Corvane, who walked into exile and then, six months later, walked back out of it, thinner and greyer and carrying a trunk of documents he had rescued from the old regime's private vaults. He presented them to the new council of the Guild with neither apology nor excuse, only the statement that stolen things should be returned to the record even when returning them ruined the thief, and the council, to its credit or its desperation, accepted both the documents and the man. He taught contract law now, in a small cold classroom, to apprentices who had no idea he had once been the most feared auditor in the trade. He said he preferred it that way. He said fear made a poor foundation for learning. I believed him, mostly because he flinched whenever I passed his doorway, and shame, I had learned, was its own kind of honest archivist.

But the language classes were mine.

We met in the archive hall on the last afternoon of each week, because the archive was where the language lived now — in manifests and marginalia, in songbooks and shipping lists, in the thousands of documents the old Guild had catalogued as *inventory* and that I was recataloguing, entry by entry, as *voices*. Twelve students the first term. Then twenty. Then forty, and children from outside the Guild too, tradesmen's daughters and dockworkers' sons, drawn by the rumor that a boy their own age could teach them words that had died and been buried and dug back up.

On the first day of winter term, I stood before them with a slate in my hand and forty faces looking up at me, and I made a decision I had been circling for months.

I did not teach them Vessia as a ruin.

Every other teacher in the world, if handed a dead country, would have taught it as a ruin. They would have begun with the ending — the bargain, the erasure, the burning city without fire — because endings are dramatic and drama holds a classroom. I knew the temptation personally. For two years the ending had been the loudest thing in me, the part that screamed, the part that woke me at night with ash in my teeth.

But Ilset Vane had not been an ending. Ilset Vane had hummed while she worked. Old Toma had talked to his bees. The girl Sefa had cheated at dice and laughed with her whole body and once swum the flooded river on a dare, and none of those things had anything to do with how Vessia died, and all of them were Vessia.

So I wrote on the slate, in the old tongue, the first sentence every child in Vessia had learned before the country was ever taken:

*"The rain has nine names, and you will learn them all before you learn to fear the storm."*

Then I turned around and taught them the nine names for rain. Mist-rain, the kind that doesn't wet you but finds you anyway. The hard brief rain of the high terraces. The rain that comes before the harvest festival, that smells like cut grain. The rain the mothers used to call the sky's lullaby. I taught them the words the way I had learned them — not from grief, but from use — and I watched forty faces do what faces do when a dead language comes alive in their mouths: they stumbled, they laughed, they tried again, and somewhere around the fifth name the words stopped being relics and started being theirs.

A girl in the front row — a cooper's daughter with ink-stained fingers — raised her hand. "Is it true," she asked, "that you have all of them inside you? All the people?"

I considered lying, or softening it, and decided against both, because I had promised myself that this classroom would be the one place in the world with no margins full of euphemism.

"I did," I said. "All of them. It nearly broke me, and it broke other things too, and I won't pretend otherwise. But they're not inside me anymore. They're *finished*. They lived, and they were loved, and they ended, and now what's left of them is—" I gestured at the shelves, the manifests, the slate with its nine rains. "This. Us. What we choose to carry forward." I tapped the slate. "A country isn't its borders. I know that better than anyone alive. A country is a set of stories that people agree to keep telling. So we're going to keep telling them. Not because we owe the dead a debt. Because the stories are good ones."

The girl nodded slowly, the way children nod when they have understood something larger than they can yet say. Then she picked up her chalk and copied the nine names onto her board in careful, crooked letters, and I turned back to my slate, and for a moment — just a moment — I felt it again: not the haunting, not the weight, but something underneath it, older and gentler. The sense of a harvest festival at dusk, of lamps being lit along a processional road, of a whole people leaning in to hear what would happen next.

Listening. Approving. Letting go.

* * *

That evening I climbed the gatehouse stairs to the top of the wall, where Jude was doing the inventory he still insisted on performing by hand, lantern in hand, checking each bottled lullaby and stolen shadow and impossible door against his list. Some habits survive the fall of empires.

"Class went well?" he asked without looking up.

"Forty students. By spring it'll be sixty." I leaned on the parapet beside him. Below us, the yard of the Guild of Impossible Things lay quiet in the blue snow-light, and beyond the wall the city went about its evening — carts and lamplighters and the smell of a hundred suppers, none of them knowing or needing to know that the language of a vanished nation was being spoken thirty feet above their heads, and would be spoken tomorrow, and the day after, by children who had never seen its shores.

"Sixty," Jude murmured. "When I started here, the Guild measured its worth in what it could lock away." He lifted the lantern so its light fell on the open ledger in his hands — the great ledger itself, the master volume, the one whose final page had been written on the night the door opened. "Look at this, if you want. I've been saving it for you."

I took the book. The last page bore the line I had read a hundred times since, the line half the city had read by now, copied and recopied until it had worn smooth as a psalm:

*Vessia was here, and was loved.*

But below it, in fresher ink, in Jude's careful hand, there was a second line I had never seen. Small. Almost shy. As if it had been waiting for the right reader.

*I, Jude Sandoval, keeper, certify that the heir of Vessia, Orsen, has been entered in this ledger freely, under his own name, and that this entry is true.*

Below that, a blank space waited. The size of a signature.

I looked up. Jude was watching me with the expression he wore when he opened a door he wasn't sure should be opened — the old terror and the strange exhilaration, side by side, exactly as they had been on the night a child knocked at his gate.

"It needs a counter-signature," he said. "Every honest entry does. Archivist's prerogative." He held out the pen.

I took it. And as I bent toward the page, I heard — faint, beneath the wind, beneath the city, beneath everything — the ninth name for rain, falling softly on a country that no longer existed, on a people who were finally, at long last, allowed to rest.

I signed my name.
