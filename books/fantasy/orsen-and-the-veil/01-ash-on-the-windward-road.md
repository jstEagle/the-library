## 1. Ash on the Windward Road

The rain caught up with Rosalind Halloran two miles out of Millbrook, and she let it. Three weeks of dust and salt air and lying to customs men with a smile, and the first honest thing to touch her skin was cold water running down the back of her collar. She tilted her face into it and kept walking.

The windward road climbed out of the river valley in long switchbacks, hedged on both sides by blackthorn gone feral. From the top of the third bend you could see the whole of the Hollow: her master's cottage with its turf roof, the herb garden fenced in woven hazel, the beehives set where the morning sun would find them first. She had been looking forward to that view for nineteen days. She had imagined the smoke rising from the chimney, the kettle already on, Master Aldous pretending he hadn't been waiting at the window since noon.

There was no smoke.

She stopped walking before she understood why. The body knew first — some old animal arithmetic of wrongness — and her feet simply quit, and then her eyes did their work, and there was nothing left to do but see it.

The cottage was burned down to its ribs.

Not all of it. The stone hearth-wall still stood, and one corner of the east room, and the doorframe with its carved lintel that Aldous had bought secondhand from a chapel being torn down in Corleigh. Everything else was char and collapse. The turf roof had burned through and fallen inward, so the cottage looked like a thing that had been eaten from above. Rain hissed off the hot places still buried under the ash, and thin threads of steam rose through the wet, as if the ruin were breathing.

Rosalind's pack slid off her shoulder and hit the mud. She didn't remember letting go of it.

She ran. The switchbacks, the gate — the gate was standing, which was somehow worse than if it had burned too, because someone had nailed a sheet of paper to it, and paper doesn't survive fire unless hands put it there after. She tore past it without reading. Later. Everything could wait until later except this.

"Aldous!" Her voice cracked apart on his name. "Master Aldous!"

The garden gate hung open. The bees were gone — not dead, just gone, every hive abandoned, and that frightened her more than the ash did. Bees know things. Her master used to say that: *a hive will tell you the truth about a house before the house does.*

She found him at the edge of what had been the kitchen garden, laid out straight beneath a wool blanket that someone had tucked carefully around his shoulders. Carefully. That detail arrived in her mind and refused to leave. Someone had carried him out of the fire, or out of whatever happened before the fire, and laid him down, and covered him, and tucked the blanket in like a mother settling a child.

He looked small. He had never once looked small to her. Twelve years ago he'd been a giant who filled a doorway and a silence both, the crook-backed stranger who'd paid a tavern keeper in Corleigh three coppers to feed the orphan girl nobody wanted, and then taught her letters on slate, and herbs in the garden, and how to walk a border road at night without leaving sign. He'd been the only person alive who knew what she was and hadn't crossed himself about it.

Now he was a shape under a blanket, and the rain was flattening his white hair against his forehead, and his eyes were closed, and his hands were folded, and none of it was sleep.

Rosalind knelt in the mud beside him. She didn't wail. She didn't tear her hair. What came out of her instead was very quiet and very steady, a sound like water finding a crack in stone, and she stayed that way — kneeling, silent, shaking — while the rain came down harder and the light went out of the afternoon.

* * *

It was full dark when she finally moved, and when she moved, everything in her had changed temperature. Grief was still there, but grief is a soft thing, and something had wrapped itself around it and hardened. She recognized the feeling from her smuggling years: the moment on a dark road when fear stops being panic and becomes procedure. Check the road. Count your exits. Move.

She went into the ruin.

The floorboards were treacherous, half of them fallen into the cellar, but she knew the house blind. Kitchen. East room. The little study behind the hearth where Aldous kept his books — and where the fire had burned hottest, she saw now, hotter than an accident burns. This wasn't a spark off the hearth. The study had been fired deliberately, thoroughly, and the rest of the cottage had simply been unlucky enough to be attached to it.

Whoever came wanted the books. Whoever came got most of them; the study was a drift of scorched paper and broken spines. But Aldous had spent forty years being a careful man in a careless world, and Rosalind had spent twelve years learning his habits without knowing she was learning them.

She pried up the flagstone behind where his desk had stood.

The iron box was there, and inside the box, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as the day he'd hidden it: the grimoire. Not the show-piece herbal on the parlor shelf that any witch-hunter would burn with satisfaction — the real one, the marginalia book, the one whose margins grew denser every year, written in Aldous's crabbed hand and, underneath that, in another hand entirely, older, in ink that had gone brown as dried blood. She'd seen it exactly twice, both times by accident, both times followed by a look from her master that had made her decide never to mention it.

She took it. And because taking one secret makes you brave enough to take others, she went to the bedroom corner, to the loose board under the bed frame, and found the purse — eleven crowns and a handful of silver, a smuggler's emergency fund, and Aldous had known she'd know where it was.

That was when she remembered the key.

* * *

It had happened nine days ago, the night before she'd left for the coast run. She'd been loading the mule in the yard, and Aldous had come out barefoot in the cold, which was unlike him, and pressed something into her palm and closed her fingers over it, and held them shut with both of his old hands.

"Brass," he'd said. "Don't sell it. I know you, Rosalind Halloran, you'll price it in your head before I finish talking. Don't."

"Then what am I supposed to do with it?"

"Wear it. Forget it. If you can." His grip had tightened, and his eyes had done something she'd never seen them do — they'd gone somewhere else, somewhere years away, and come back afraid. "If anything happens to me while you're gone — anything — don't bury me, don't mourn me, don't even think about me longer than a night. Take the box under the flagstone and go north. Go north and keep going until you find a man who knows what the key opens."

"What does it open?"

And he'd smiled, that infuriating half-smile, the teacher's smile, the one that meant the lesson was yours to earn. "Something that isn't there," he'd said. "Go on now. You'll miss the tide."

She'd laughed at him. She'd actually laughed, and kissed his stubbled cheek, and ridden out whistling.

Now she stood in the ashes of his life with the key in her fist, and for the first time she looked at it properly, by the light of a shuttered lantern, the way he'd taught her to look at anything that mattered: slowly, and twice.

It was brass, palm-length, heavy for its size, unadorned except for a single line of engraving along the shaft — characters in no alphabet she knew, though they scratched at her memory like a word on the tip of the tongue. The bow of the key was shaped like nothing she'd ever seen on a lock: not a loop or a ring but a narrow arch, a doorway in miniature, with a hairline crack down the middle of it as if the door were meant to open too.

*A door.* Something in her chest turned over. *A key shaped like a door, for something that isn't there.*

North, then. North past the hill country, past the ford at Graywater, into the borderlands where the maps went vague and the roads went by names the crown didn't collect taxes from. She knew those roads. Smugglers' roads, hedge-witch roads, the veins of the country that official cartography pretended were empty. Somewhere up there was a man who knew what her dead master's key was for.

*Dead.* The word landed again, fresh each time, a stone dropped down a well that never reached bottom.

* * *

Near dawn the rain stopped, and Rosalind sat in the doorway of the ruined cottage — the one intact corner, under the chapel lintel — and read the proclamation she'd torn from the gate.

It was good vellum, crown-issue, the kind with the wax seal stamped in real sealing wax rather than tallow. The royal arms of Veyland, the lion and the crossed keys. She read it once for content and twice for tone, because tone tells you what a document is afraid of.

*By order of the Crown: Let it be known throughout the marches and shires of Veyland that the Purging of Gifts has commenced. All persons possessed of uncanny arts — hedge-craft, charm-working, sight, spell, or any art deriving from the old kingdom — are hereby commanded to surrender themselves to crown authority within thirty days of this proclamation. Those who surrender shall be examined and, if found penitent, shown mercy. Those who harbor, conceal, or abet the gifted shall share their punishment. There is no middle road. Signed under seal, in the third year of King Merrick's reign.*

*Any art deriving from the old kingdom.* She read that phrase four times. Every proclamation has boilerplate, but this wasn't boilerplate; it was the spine of the whole edict. Not sorcery generally. Not devilry. One specific, vanished place, named nowhere on the page and everywhere between the lines.

The old kingdom. Thornfell, the peddlers called it, when they told fireside stories they mostly didn't believe. The kingdom that wasn't on any map. The kingdom that, according to her master's occasional mutterings over his cups — and Aldous drank rarely, but when he drank, geography came out of him that matched no atlas she'd ever stolen or sold — had been *unmade*. Not conquered. Conquering leaves ruins and tax rolls. Thornfell had been drawn out of existence, erased by treaty and ink, and the erasure had taken the memory of it with it, so that most people couldn't have told you what they'd forgotten.

Except the gifted could. That was the thing Aldous had never said outright but had circled, obliquely, for twelve years: hedge-craft didn't come from books or demons or bloodlines, whatever the priests claimed. It came from *there*. It leaked through, like water through a wall. Every charm-worker in Veyland was a crack in the erasure, and King Merrick had decided to caulk the cracks.

With fire, apparently.

Rosalind folded the proclamation along its original creases and put it in her coat, over her heart, where she kept things she intended to hate for a long time. Then she took stock, because stock-taking was survival and survival was the only prayer she knew.

One mule, hobbled in the blackthorn, untouched — the hunters hadn't bothered with livestock, or hadn't had time. Eleven crowns and change. A grimoire with margins full of a dead language. A brass key shaped like a door. A dead man's last instruction: *go north.*

And one more thing, which she only discovered when she shrugged her pack on and felt the weight of it wrong. She opened the flap and found the bundle she'd carried home from the coast run — the silk and saffron she'd meant to trade to Aldous for winter stores — and on top of it, wrapped in waxed linen, a jar of honey from the Millbrook fair, because he liked it stirred into his tea, and she had bought it without letting herself think about why.

She sat down on the doorstep again and held the jar and let herself have five minutes. She counted them out honestly, the way Aldous had taught her to count breaths in the herb garden when the nightmares came, and when the five minutes were spent she wiped her face with her sleeve, stood up, and sealed the jar into the bottom of the pack where it wouldn't break.

"You'd laugh at me," she said aloud, to the ash, to the blanket-shaped silence in the garden. "You'd say, *girl, sentiment is ballast, throw it overboard.*" Her voice held. That surprised her. "But you also taught me never to travel lighter than my reasons. So I'm carrying it. And I'm carrying you. And whoever nailed that paper to your gate is going to learn what this family does with debts."

The east was graying. She hobbled the mule, loaded him, checked the windward road both ways the way she'd check a river crossing — and froze.

Down at the second switchback, maybe half a mile off, movement. Two riders, unhurried, in the gray not-light. Not reeves, not toll-men. They wore the traveling cloaks of crown service, and one of them led a spare horse with something slung across its saddle that was the right size and shape to be a cage.

They were riding toward the Hollow. Toward the smoke that was no longer smoking. Either following the same report that had brought the fire, or following *her* — because a runner who comes home always comes home, that was the first law of the roads, and someone patient enough to burn a cottage might well be patient enough to watch it.

Rosalind didn't run. Running leaves sign, and sign leaves stories. She walked the mule west instead, off the windward road entirely, down the sheep track through the blackthorn that only she and Aldous and the foxes used, moving at the pace of a woman with nowhere particular to be. Behind her, faint on the wind, she heard the riders reach the gate. Heard one of them dismount. Heard, very clearly, a voice say:

"She'll run for the border. They always run for the border."

Rosalind smiled, and there was nothing warm in it.

"No," she said, to herself, to the mule's ear, to the burned man she was carrying north in a jar of honey and a book of margins. "I'm going to find out what you died hiding. And then, old man — then we'll talk about borders."

The track bent, and the Hollow dropped away behind her, and the hills ahead went up gray and endless into the north.
