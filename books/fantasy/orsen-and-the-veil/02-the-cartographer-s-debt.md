## 2. The Cartographer's Debt

The trouble with drinking yourself blind in a border town is that the border towns are the first to know when something has gone wrong in the capital. The news arrives with the mail coaches, and the mail coaches arrive whether you want them or not.

Orsen Vael had been in Harrowgate for eleven months, which was ten months longer than he'd planned. He'd come down from the capital with a letter of dismissal folded in his coat like a knife he kept checking was still there, and a purse that should have lasted six months if he'd been careful. He had not been careful. Careful, it turned out, was a skill that left a man the same day his name was struck from the rolls of the Royal Survey.

So now he sat in the taproom of the Drowned Bell, which was the second-worst tavern in Harrowgate, because the worst one had thrown him out in the spring, and he drank the local rotgut — they called it *stonebreaker*, which told you everything — and he waited for the day to end so another could begin.

He still wore the ring. That was the pathetic part. A plain band of pewter with the Survey's compass rose stamped into it, the mark of a King's Cartographer. He'd been ordered to surrender it. He'd told the clerk he'd lost it. Some nights he took it off and set it on the table and looked at it for a long time, and some nights he even put it away in his pocket, but by morning it was always back on his finger, warm from his hand, like a thing that had grown there.

Eleven months ago he had been Orsen Vael, youngest senior surveyor in the history of the Royal Cartographic Office, the man who drew the new borders of Veyland after the Reconciliation. His maps hung in the council chamber. Schoolchildren traced his coastlines with their fingers. And then—

Then the surveyor-general had called him in on a gray morning and asked him, very gently, what he remembered about Thornfell.

And Orsen had said, "Thornfell?"

Because that was the correct answer. Because there was no such place. Because he had checked the archives himself, twice, and found nothing: no treaties, no wars, no trade ledgers, no gravestones. The space on the map where a kingdom should have been was simply mountain waste and forest, unclaimed, uninteresting, empty. He had drawn those mountains himself. He had drawn them *well*.

It was only later — much later, at three in the morning, sober for once — that he understood the question hadn't been *what do you remember about Thornfell*. It had been *what do you remember about drawing Thornfell out*.

Someone in the palace knew. Someone always knows. And rather than hang him or buy him, they'd dismissed him — quietly, without scandal, without charges — the way you dismiss a man whose continued presence is more dangerous than his absence. *We have no further need of your services.* Six words. They might as well have written them on his headstone.

He'd spent the first month telling himself he'd imagined it. He'd spent the next ten trying to drink the memory of the surveyor-general's face out of his skull. Neither worked.

* * *

The girl came in near dusk, shaking rain off a hooded cloak, and Orsen noticed her the way you notice anything anymore: not at all, then all at once.

She wasn't from Harrowgate. She moved wrong for a town — quick, watchful, hugging the walls the way travelers do when they're carrying something worth stealing. She went to the bar, spoke low to Marta the landlady, and Marta shook her head and pointed, of all the cursed things, directly at Orsen.

The girl looked at him. Looked at Marta. Looked back.

Then she crossed the room and sat down across from him without asking, which in Harrowgate was either an invitation to robbery or a conversation, and given that she was maybe nineteen and built like a whip, he assumed robbery.

"You're the one who buys odd things," she said. "Marta says you used to be somebody."

"Marta says a great many things."

"About maps." She leaned forward. Her hood slipped back and he saw a young woman with dark hair cut short and practical, a smudge of soot at her jaw she hadn't noticed, and eyes that were doing arithmetic on him faster than he could follow. "Marta says you pay good coin for old instruments, documents, survey marks. Anything with official seals."

"I pay for distraction," Orsen said. "You're offering none. Good evening."

She didn't move. Instead she reached into her cloak and set something on the table between them, and the whole character of the room changed.

It was a key. Brass, old, longer than a man's thumb, and shaped wrong — not the flat toothed profile of any lock Orsen had ever catalogued, but a column wrapped in fine ridged spiraling, like a screw carved from a single piece of metal, ending in a head stamped with a device he knew better than his own name.

A compass rose. But not the Survey's rose. This one had eight points instead of four, and between the points, where the Survey's stamp left blank metal, this one bore letters worn nearly smooth. He tilted it toward the candle without touching it.

*T-H-O-R-N-F-E-L-L.*

The taproom noise fell away. Orsen became aware of his own heartbeat, of the sweat starting cold along his spine, of the fact that he had not touched stonebreaker in eleven months that had ever tasted like anything, and that right now he wanted nothing in the world except to be somewhere else.

"Where did you get this?" His voice came out steady, which surprised him.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously." He made himself look up from the key. "Girl. Listen to me. Where did you get this?"

Something flickered across her face — grief, fresh and raw, the kind that hasn't learned to sit down yet. "My master's cottage was burned two days ago. Crown men. There was a proclamation nailed to his gate. All gifted persons to be surrendered for examination." She said it flatly, recitationally, the way people say things they've repeated to themselves until the words go smooth. "He gave me this before I ran. Pressed it into my hand through the window, actually, which tells you how much time we had."

"Your master."

"A hedge-teacher. Old Halloran. He taught charms to farmwives and midwives and never hurt anyone in his life." Her chin came up. "I'm selling it because I need to eat and travel light, and because whatever it opens, I can't open it, and dead men don't need keys."

*Old Halloran.* The name snagged somewhere in Orsen's memory, in the same drawer as the surveyor-general's face. He'd seen that name once, years ago, in a list he'd been ordered to burn. A list of names that had survived the Unmapping. He'd assumed the list itself was a test — that anyone who asked about it would be next through the door. He'd burned it. He'd burned it and gone home and slept.

"And you brought it here," he said. "To a border town. To sell."

"Border towns are where things get sold."

"Border towns are where things get *noticed*." He glanced past her shoulder, at the taproom, at the two men by the fire wearing traveling cloaks too fine for their boots, at the way one of them had turned his head toward their table the moment the girl sat down. Crown men traveled in pairs. Crown men in the provinces lately traveled in fours. "There will be proclamations in this town by now. There may be someone already asking after a runaway apprentice who carries her master's effects."

Her eyes narrowed. "You know what this is."

"I know what it isn't." He lowered his voice until she had to lean in to hear it, and hated himself for how much he wanted her to lean in, how starved he was for this — for the work, for the shape of the problem, for anything that wasn't the bottom of a cup. "That key opens nothing in Veyland. No lock in any inventory I ever kept — and I kept all of them. It's not a key to a door. It's a key to a *boundary*. Do you understand the difference?"

"No."

"Neither did I, for years." He turned the key slowly with one finger, not lifting it from the table, keeping it low. The spirals caught the candlelight and seemed, briefly, to move — though that was the drink, surely, that was the drink. "There was a kingdom here once. Before Veyland drew its borders where they are. Before my maps. It was called Thornfell, and it does not appear in any record, any ledger, any grave, because it was not conquered. It was *unmade*. Removed. Drawn out of existence by treaty and ink and — other things. Things I helped draw." His mouth was dry. "This key belonged to that kingdom. Which means whoever held it last was either a collector who doesn't exist, or someone from a country that doesn't exist."

The girl stared at him. "That's madness."

"Yes," Orsen said. "It usually is, at first."

* * *

They talked for two hours, there in the corner of the Drowned Bell, and Orsen drank less in those two hours than he had in any week of the last eleven, because his hands wanted to be doing something else.

He got the story out of her in pieces. Rosalind Halloran — no relation to the old man by blood; taken in as a child; raised on hedge-charms and smugglers' roads. She'd run goods over the border passes since she was twelve and knew the mountains between Veyland and the waste better than any crown surveyor alive, which was a sentence that made Orsen laugh, bitterly, because he had *been* a crown surveyor, and he knew exactly how well they knew those mountains.

"And what do you want with me?" she asked eventually. "You've told me what the key is. You haven't told me why you care."

Because he couldn't say *redemption*, because the word was too large for his mouth. So he said what was true underneath it.

"Because I'm the man who erased it." He set his hands flat on the table. "Twelve years ago I sat in a survey office in the capital and drew the borders that closed around Thornfell like a fist. I was told the region was disputed waste. I was told the previous surveys were corrupted. I redrew every road, every river, every village boundary in that quarter of the map, and when I was done, the space where Thornfell had been was legally, cartographically, officially *nothing*. And I was praised for it. Commended. Given a medal I later sold for drink." He heard his own voice go flat and hard. "I don't know what happened to the people. I've spent eleven months telling myself there were no people, because the records say there were no people, because *I am one of the reasons the records say that*."

Rosalind was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters.

"My master," she said finally, "had a way of talking about the roads. He used to say the smugglers' paths weren't paths at all — that they were older than smuggling, older than the border, that they followed lines nobody surveyed. I thought it was an old man's romance." She looked down at the brass key between them. "Two days ago, when the cottage was burning, I ran the windward road, the one that isn't on any map. And the fog came down around me like a wall, and I walked through it, and when I came out the other side the crown riders who'd been behind me were just... gone. Not lost. *Gone*, like they'd never been on the road at all."

Orsen felt the floor tilt under him, very slightly, the way it did when he'd drunk too much — except he'd barely touched his cup.

"The roads are threads," he said slowly. "If the Veil — if the concealment — was woven across the whole territory, then anything that predates the Unmapping might be caught in the weave. Paths. Boundaries. *Keys*." He looked at her, really looked, and for the first time in eleven months the drink wasn't loud in his head. Something else was. Something that sounded like a surveyor-general asking gently, *what do you remember about Thornfell?* — and like an old man pressing a brass key through a burning window. "Your master didn't give you a keepsake, Miss Halloran. He gave you a thread. And I think he gave it to you knowing exactly who you'd have to find to pull it."

"Why would he—"

"Because everyone who remembers is either dead, hiding, or hunting you. And I—" Orsen stopped. Started again. "I am the only man in Veyland who knows what was done, because I did part of it. If there's a way to undo an erasure, the method lives in the mind of the man who made it. That's not pride. That's just... cartography. Every line on a map can be redrawn. That's the whole terror of the profession."

"That sounds like a confession."

"It is one." He reached across the table and closed his hand around the key — and the moment his skin touched the brass, the eight-pointed rose seemed to warm against his palm, faintly, like a sleeping animal. He snatched his hand back. Rosalind had seen it. Of course she had.

"So," she said. "What does it open?"

Orsen looked at the key lying on the scarred wood between them, at the letters worn smooth by centuries of a country's hands, and felt eleven months of stonebreaker curdle into something sharper. Fear, yes. But under the fear, disgraceful and undeniable: hope.

"There's a survey station," he said. "Abandoned. Crown property, decommissioned after the Reconciliation, in the high forest north of here. Officially it was closed because of wolf activity." He met her eyes. "Officially, nothing about it exists. Just like the kingdom it was built to study. Your key fits its inner door — I'd stake my ruined name on it. And inside, if the crown didn't burn the records like they burned everything else..."

He trailed off. By the fire, one of the two fine-cloaked men had risen and was speaking quietly to the barmaid, showing her something. A paper. A drawn likeness, maybe. Marta's eyes came up across the room and found Orsen's, and what passed between them in that look was eleven months of free lodging bought with coin he shouldn't have had, and a warning.

"They're asking after someone," Orsen said quietly. "Possibly you. Possibly both of us, if that key is what I think it is. We can settle terms on the road." He stood, steadier than he had any right to be, and pushed the key back across the table to her. "Take it. Hide it. And Miss Halloran — decide quickly. Because the men by that fire are going to finish their drinks, and I have a strong suspicion that whatever your master started when he pressed this into your hand, it ends at that survey station. One way or another."

Rosalind looked at the key. Then at the hunters by the fire. Then at him — at the pewter ring on his finger, at the ruin of a man who had just confessed to unmaking a country, and who was, despite everything, already reaching for his coat.

"Well," she said. "You'd better keep up, then. I know the roads that aren't on your maps."

Outside, the rain was turning to snow, and somewhere down the street a bell began to ring — not the tavern bell, not the chapel bell, but the sharp triple-strike of the crown watch, the signal every border child knew meant *a hunt has begun.*

Orsen pulled his collar up and followed the girl into the dark, and for the first time in eleven months, he was afraid of something other than himself.
