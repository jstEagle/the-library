## 3. Witch-Hunters at the Ford

The river came on them an hour past dawn, running high and brown with snowmelt, loud enough that they heard it before they saw it. Rosalind smelled it first — cold stone and wet iron — and something in her chest loosened. Water meant a crossing. A crossing meant distance between her and the smoke still rising behind them where Halloran's cottage had been.

Orsen stopped at the tree line and squinted at the far bank like a man reading a letter he didn't trust. "That's the Graywater," he said. "There's a ford. Half a mile down. Used to be marked on the survey charts."

"Used to be?"

"The crown unmarked half the fords after the Unmapping." He said it flatly, the way a man says a thing he's said so often it has worn smooth. "Easier to hunt people when they can only cross where you let them."

Rosalind shifted the grimoire higher under her cloak. It was heavier than a book had any right to be, as if the leather itself were ashamed of what it carried. Two days since the burning. Two days of walking north with a stranger who drank from a flask at every rest stop and talked about maps the way other men talked about God. She still wasn't sure why she'd let him come. Because he knew what the key opened. Because he was the only person alive who hadn't looked at her like a criminal when she told him what she was.

Because, if she was honest, she was tired of being alone with the ash.

"Ford it is," she said.

* * *

They found the ford, and they found it occupied.

Rosalind saw the horses first — six of them, gray and dun, standing hip-shot in the shallows while their riders watered them. Then the riders themselves: chain mail under travel cloaks, the sunburst-and-sword of the crown stamped into their breastplates, and at their center a tall figure in black carrying not a sword but a censer, its smoke trailing thin and sweet even across the water.

Witch-hunters. Not a patrol. A full hunting party, this far from the capital, this fast.

"They're not looking for smugglers," Orsen breathed beside her. He'd gone very still, the flask forgotten in his hand. "Smugglers get questioned. That's a pyre detail."

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry. She knew the shape of those men. Every child raised on the wrong side of a hedge-charm knew it — the way you knew the shape of wolves without ever having seen one. Her master had drilled it into her years ago: *If you ever see the sunburst on the road, you are already dead. You just haven't stopped moving yet.*

Except now she had nowhere to move to. The ford was the only crossing for twenty miles, and it belonged to them.

"There," Orsen whispered. A tracker had dismounted and was crouched at the near edge of the ford, doing something deliberate with his hands. Reading sign. Their sign — two sets of boot prints along a riverbank that hadn't seen rain in a week.

"We go back," Rosalind said. "We find another—"

"With what? The Graywater runs off the mountains. There is no other crossing." His voice cracked on the last word, and she realized with a strange distant clarity that Orsen Veyl, royal cartographer, disgraced, drunk, was afraid. Genuinely, bone-deep afraid. It should have frightened her more. Instead it steadied her, the way a struck flint steadies a candle.

The tracker stood. Turned toward the tree line. Raised one arm.

*You are already dead,* said Halloran's voice in her memory. *You just haven't stopped moving yet.*

"No," Rosalind said aloud, and did not know until the word left her whether she was answering him or the hunters or the river itself.

She stepped out of the trees.

* * *

Later — much later, in the years when people would ask her about it, because people always asked — she would try to explain what happened at the ford, and she would never quite manage it. Not because she forgot. Because the truth sounded either too small or too large, depending on who was listening.

Here is the small version: she walked down to the water's edge with her hood up and her heart hammering like a fist on a door, and when the hunters turned and shouted and began to spur their horses into the shallows, she knelt and pressed both palms flat against the current and spoke the words her master had taught her over a rain barrel when she was nine years old. Words for calling water. Hedge-craft. Kitchen magic. The kind of gift the crown called *lesser* right up until the day it burned you for it.

Here is the larger version:

The Graywater answered.

Not all of it — she wasn't a storm, she wasn't a sea-witch out of the old stories. But the river was already high, already swollen with meltwater and looking, Rosalind thought wildly, like a dog straining at a rope. All she did was whisper to it. *Come. Come here. Come now.* And the rope snapped.

The water rose the way a living thing rises — a shoulder of brown flood lifting out of the channel, curling, gathering itself, and then coming down the ford like a falling wall. Horses screamed. Men went off their saddles like dolls swept off a table. The censer-bearer in black dropped his smoke into the water and it died with a hiss, and Rosalind felt that hiss somewhere behind her sternum, felt the sweet censer-smell snuffed out, and understood with a clarity that would never leave her: whatever that smoke was for, it had been meant for her.

The flood took the ford and kept going. It took the far bank. It took a birch tree at the roots and rolled it downstream like a twig. It was too much — she could feel it pulling at her through her palms, greedy, delighted, wanting more than she had to give — and she wrenched her hands back and gasped, "*Enough,*" the way you'd speak to a horse bolting under you, and the river, grumbling, obeyed.

Then there was only the ordinary sound of water, and six riderless horses stampeding south, and men crawling out of the shallows on the far bank, coughing, alive, furious, and cut off.

Rosalind knelt in the mud with her arms shaking and her breath coming in sobs she couldn't stop, staring at her own hands as if they belonged to someone else.

* * *

"Get up."

Orsen had hold of her elbow, hauling her upright. His face was gray. "Get up, girl, they'll have a bowman—"

"I killed—" she started, and then couldn't finish it, because she hadn't; the men were moving, swearing, dragging each other onto the bank, and the flood had been rage, not murder. But it might have been murder. It had wanted, for three long seconds, to be murder, and she had been the one holding the leash.

"You didn't kill anyone. You bought us a river." Orsen pulled her upstream along the bank, away from the ford, toward a place where the willows grew thick to the waterline. "There's a footbridge. Old survey bridge, timber, probably rotten, but it'll take us if we go one at a time. Move."

She moved. Her legs worked because he made them work, one hand clamped around her arm, and it was only halfway to the bridge that she realized he hadn't let go of the flask, and that he'd thrown it away. She glanced back once and saw it glinting in the mud, abandoned, and something about that — more than the flood, more than the shouting hunters — told her how bad it had been.

At the bridge he made her cross first. The timbers groaned but held. On the far side she turned and watched him come, thin and stooped and forty pounds too light for his coat, walking a rotten beam over a flooded river with the concentration of a man defusing something, and when he stepped off onto solid ground he sat down abruptly in the grass and put his head between his knees.

Rosalind stood over him, dripping, shaking, waiting for it. For him to look at her the way the village children had looked at the tinker's daughter, the one they'd taken last spring. For the flinch. For the crossed fingers, the muttered charm against evil, the slow backing away.

Orsen lifted his head. He looked at her — really looked, the way he'd looked at the burned map in his saddlebag, the way he looked at anything true — and said:

"How long have you been able to do that?"

"That's it?" Her voice came out ragged. "That's all you have to say?"

"What do you want me to say?" He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "That I'm frightened? I am. That was the most dangerous thing I've ever seen a person do, and I once watched a king erase a country. But I'm not going to pretend I didn't see it." He got to his feet, slowly, joints cracking. "You called a mountain river like it was a kitchen kettle. Rosalind. Do you understand what you are?"

"A witch." The word tasted like ash. "Like the proclamation says. Like my master died for."

"No." He said it sharply, and she blinked. "A hedge-witch boils fevers and charms rain onto turnip fields. What you did takes training. Years of it. That old man didn't just hide you — he built you. Brick by brick, and never let you see the blueprint." He was staring past her now, upriver, toward the mountains, and his expression had changed into something she hadn't seen on him before. Not shame. Not drink. Calculation, bright and hungry. "He was preparing you for something."

"He was preparing me to survive," Rosalind said. "That's all anyone's ever done for me, and look how well it worked."

But even as she said it, her hand found the brass key through her shirt, and it was warm. Warmer than her skin. It had been warm since the ford, since the moment the water rose, and she decided — deliberately, the way you decide to keep walking on a broken ankle — not to think about that yet.

* * *

They put six miles between themselves and the Graywater before Orsen would allow a rest, and even then it was in a hollow under a rock overhang, no fire, sharing hard bread and harder silence. Rosalind's hands had finally stopped shaking. Her mind hadn't. It kept replaying it: the wall of water, the delight in it, the way it had *known* her, the way she had known it — not like a tool, not like a weapon. Like a cousin. Like something she'd been separated from at birth.

All her life she had hidden it. Charms woven small and sideways, disguised as luck, as knack, as a smuggler's good instincts. Never in front of anyone. Never in daylight. Never where the smoke of a censer could find it.

And today she had stood in front of six armed men of the crown and torn a river out of its bed, and the world had not ended. The world had, in fact, continued, with her in it, wet and alive and — she searched herself carefully, the way you probe a bruise — and *unafraid*. Not of the hunters. Not anymore. Something had burned off in the flood along with the censer-smoke, some residue of fear she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for skin.

They could take her name. They'd taken her master. They could burn every cottage she'd ever loved.

They could not make her small again. She had tried being small for twenty-three years and it had gotten a good man killed.

"You're smiling," Orsen said. He sounded wary.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's unsettling."

Rosalind looked at him across the hollow — this ruined man with his ruined maps and his thrown-away flask, the only witness to what she'd done, the only person in Veyland who had seen her gift and reached for her hand instead of a rope. "My master used to say the gift isn't the danger," she said. "The hiding is. He said a dammed river doesn't get calmer, it gets *hungrier*, and one day the whole valley finds out at once." She flexed her fingers, remembering the pull of the current, the terrible joy of it. "Today the valley found out."

"And tomorrow the crown finds out," Orsen said quietly. "Six riders lost a witch at the Graywater. That report travels faster than we do. By week's end there won't be six of them, Rosalind. There'll be sixty. And they won't be watering horses at a ford — they'll know your face, your direction, and what you can do." He paused. "Which means we need to reach the key's lock before they reach us. No more rivers. No more roads. We go the way the maps forgot."

Rosalind pulled the key from beneath her shirt and held it up between them. In the gray light of the hollow it seemed to glow faintly, though she told herself it was only the brass catching what little sky the overhang allowed. Warm. Still warm. Warmer than it had been yesterday, and she had begun, despite herself, to wonder if it wasn't warming because they were getting close — or because something on the other side of the lock knew they were coming.

"Then tell me," she said. "No more drinking, no more *used to be marked on the charts*. Tell me what my master died protecting. Tell me what this opens."

Orsen looked at the key for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had the flatness of a man stepping off a ledge he'd been circling for thirty years.

"It opens a country," he said. "One that I helped bury."

Far to the south, faint on the wind, Rosalind thought she heard horns — the hunters regrouping, or perhaps only the river lying. Either way, they would be moving by dark.

She closed her hand around the key, warm as a heartbeat, and listened to the cartographer begin his confession.
