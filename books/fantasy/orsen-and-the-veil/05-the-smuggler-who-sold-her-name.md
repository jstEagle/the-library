## 5. The Smuggler Who Sold Her Name

The trouble with knowing a man for eleven years is that you stop looking at him.

I'd known Dain Ferro since I was nine and he was twelve, both of us running contraband through the reed-beds of the Marish for a woman named Sefa who paid in copper and never asked questions. I knew the way he whistled when he lied — always the same three notes, off-key on purpose, like a lock being picked. I knew he slept sitting up on boats because of a childhood spent in flooded cellars. I knew his laugh had a crack in it, and that he covered the crack by laughing louder.

So when he stepped out from behind the waystone at Colder's Rest with his hands raised and that cracked laugh already going, every part of me that wasn't exhausted wanted to run to him.

Every part of me that was exhausted reached for the knife instead.

"Easy," he said. "Easy, Roz. If I wanted you dead you'd have been dead three miles back, before the road bent."

Orsen shifted beside me. He'd been doing that all morning — shifting, flinching, one hand drifting toward the short blade he carried like a man who'd bought it yesterday and hadn't yet made peace with it. A cartographer's hands. Ink-stained even now, under the grime. Not a fighting man's hands.

"Dain," I said. "You're a long way from the Marish."

"I'm a long way from everything." He came forward slowly, palms still out, and I took inventory the way I always did: boots good, coat new — too new, army wool, dyed brown but not well enough — satchel heavy on the left hip. Fed. Rested. Someone had fed him recently, and someone with money.

"You heard about Master Ellery," I said. Watched his face. It did what faces do; it went grave in the right places, at the right speed. Too right. Too smooth. Grief performed by a man who'd rehearsed it.

"The cottage?" His voice dropped. "Roz, I'm sorry. I heard three days ago, out past Harrow Fen. I came as fast as—"

"Three days ago." The words sat in my mouth like river stones. Three days ago the ash was still warm. Three days ago I was sleeping in a ditch with a grimoire against my ribs and a brass key knotted into my hair, listening for hooves.

"And how did you hear, exactly? Out past Harrow Fen, where nothing happens and nobody talks?"

He blinked. "Word travels."

"Word travels *fast* when someone carries it."

*Whistle*, I thought. *Give me the whistle.* But he didn't whistle. He just stood there in the gray light with the fog coming off the fens behind him, and his face kept doing its careful work, and I understood that I was going to have to do this alone, this arithmetic, this terrible sum, without any tell to help me — because Dain Ferro had learned new tricks since the last time I'd watched him lie.

Orsen cleared his throat. "Friend of yours?"

"Oldest one I've got," I said, and hated how true it sounded.

* * *

We let him walk with us because refusing would have been louder than accepting.

That's the smuggler's first law, older than any of us: suspicion is a torch you carry in the dark. It shows you the road, and it shows everyone where you are. So I laughed at his jokes — he had good ones, he always had good ones — and I let him take the second watch, and I told him a trimmed version of the truth: master dead, crown hunting the gifted, heading north because north was away.

I didn't mention the key. I didn't mention Orsen's name. I didn't mention the grimoire, or the shrine we were making for, or anything that could be sold.

Because that was the thought I couldn't put down, the one that gnawed while I chewed trail bread and smiled: *everything can be sold.* That was the trade we were both in. Smuggling is just selling things to people who aren't supposed to have them, and Dain had always been better at the selling part than I was. I was the one who found the paths. He was the one who found the buyers.

And somewhere between Harrow Fen and here, someone had bought *him.*

* * *

It came together on the second night, at a fire so small we called it a candle.

Dain was asleep — or performing sleep, breathing in the slow theatrical rhythm of a man who wants to be believed unconscious. Orsen sat across the coals from me, mending a strap on his satchel with clumsy, patient fingers.

"Your friend," he said quietly. "How well do you know him?"

"Well enough to know he's lying."

Orsen's needle stopped. "Then why is he here?"

"Because turning him away tells him what matters. Because if he's crown-bought, sending him off sends him straight to the nearest garrison with everything he's seen. And because—" I stopped. The honest answer sat in my chest like a swallowed coal. *Because he pulled me out of the Marish flood when I was eleven. Because he gave me half his bread for two winters. Because loyalty is a debt you don't get to audit.*

"Because he's mine," I said finally. "My people. What's left of them."

Orsen looked at me across the fire with an expression I couldn't read — pity, maybe, or recognition. A man who'd served a crown that erased a whole kingdom probably knew something about loving things that turn out to be lies.

"He said something today," Orsen said. "At the ford. When you were checking the water. He asked me which road we meant to take north. Casually. The way a man asks about weather."

"What did you tell him?"

"Nothing. I'm not sure he noticed I noticed." The needle went back to work. "But Rosalind — the witch-hunters at the ford. They knew the crossing. They were waiting at the one place a smuggler would cross, off every official map. How does a crown captain learn a smuggler's ford?"

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the night.

"They don't," I said. "Unless a smuggler tells them."

* * *

I didn't sleep after that. I lay with my back to the fire and my eyes open and I made myself look at it, all of it, laid out like goods on a trading cloth.

Master Ellery's cottage burned on a market day, when the roads were full and no one would mark riders. The proclamation was nailed up within hours — not days, *hours* — which meant the hunters knew the cottage before they came, knew what it was, knew whose it was. And who knew? Who in the whole wet green world knew that Ellery kept books behind the false wall, that he taught hedge-craft to a smuggler girl, that his cottage held more than herbs and ink?

Dain had visited twice that autumn. Sat at Ellery's table. Ate his soup. Laughed his cracked laugh while the old man pretended not to be charmed.

And the ford. The ford was ours — mine and Dain's, a secret we'd found together when I was fourteen, wading the shallows at dawn, calling it *our door* the way children call things. No map had it. No crown soldier could have it. Unless the man who'd helped me find it handed it over like a coin.

I turned over, very slowly, and looked at him across the dying fire.

He'd rolled toward the warmth in his sleep — real sleep now, or a better performance — and the firelight caught the new coat, the army wool, the dye gone wrong at the collar. And there, half-tucked under the coat's edge where he thought no one would ever look because no one ever looks at a man they love: a paper. Folded small. Sealed with plain wax, the kind garrisons use when they're issuing orders to civilians.

I knew that seal. Every runner on the windward roads knew that seal. Pardon papers. Crown pardon, conditional, bearer's name left blank until the ink dried on whatever he traded for it.

My name. My route. My master's grave.

Eleven years, and it fit in a folded square of paper smaller than my palm.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about betrayal: it doesn't feel like a knife. It feels like a door you've walked through a thousand times, suddenly locked, and you standing there like a fool with your hand still raised to knock.

I got up. I walked into the dark, far enough that the fire was a red coin behind me, and I stood among the birches and shook. Not crying — I want to be clear about that, though no one will ever read this and know the difference. Shaking. The way a bow shakes, drawn.

Because some traitor-cold part of me was already working. Already calculating. *If I kill him, the pardon dies with him, but the information doesn't — he'll have reported the route already, or written it down. If I send him away, he goes to the garrison and reports everything anyway, plus a grievance. If I keep him close—*

Keep him close.

Keep your enemy where your eyes are. Feed him the road you want sold. Let the old love sit in your chest like a stone and say nothing, say nothing, say nothing, because grief is a luxury and survival is a craft, and Master Ellery — who is ash, who is a blackened gate with a proclamation nailed to it — Master Ellery would have done the arithmetic too. He'd have done it faster.

I thought about the key knotted into my hair. About the thing Orsen said it opened, the country that officially did not exist, the spell running out thread by thread. About how many people between here and the mountains might be bought the same way Dain was bought — cheaply, with a pardon and a warm coat, because fear makes everyone a merchant.

I walked back to the fire.

Dain's eyes were open. Just barely, just a gleam, watching me come out of the trees.

"Bad dreams?" he murmured.

"Wolves," I said. "Thought I heard wolves."

He smiled and closed his eyes. Three notes of a whistle, almost — a hum, really, sleepy and content. Off-key on purpose.

I lay down with my back to him and stared at the dark and made my vow, quiet as breath: *you will walk every mile with us, Dain Ferro, and you will hand your masters exactly what I choose to feed them, and when this is done I will decide what we are — friends, or a story I used to know.*

Behind me, his breathing slowed into real sleep, and I lay awake counting the things I'd never again be able to unknow.

* * *

We broke camp at gray light. Dain led us to the north fork, cheerful, certain, pointing out landmarks he shouldn't have known were landmarks anymore — and I nodded along, and memorized each one, and began, silently, to build him a false map of our intentions, one generous lie at a time.

By midmorning the trees thickened toward the Deepwood, and somewhere ahead, under moss and root and a generation of forgetting, the first keystone waited.

So did, I suspected, whoever Dain had promised it to.

I checked the knife at my belt, smiled at my oldest friend, and walked into the green dark with a traitor at my shoulder and a dead man's key against my scalp — trusting no one, loving no one, carrying the whole cold weight of what we'd been, so that what was coming wouldn't take me by surprise.
