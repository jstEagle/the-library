## 6. Keystone in the Deepwood

**Rosalind**

The Deepwood did not appear on any map Orsen carried, and he carried maps of places that had been burned, flooded, and in one case deliberately erased by royal decree. When Rosalind pointed this out — a whole forest missing from a man who claimed to have drawn half the kingdom's borders — he only shrugged and said, "That's how you know it's important."

They had been walking for three days since the survey station. Three days of Orsen navigating by brass key and grimoire marginalia, holding the little key up like a dowsing rod while it tugged faintly toward the northeast, and three days of Rosalind pretending she wasn't watching him do it. The key had belonged to her master. Aldous had pressed it into her palm with his last unburned hand, and every time Orsen touched it now, something in her chest pulled tight as a snare line.

"You could ask," Orsen said, without turning around.

"I don't know what you mean."

"You've been staring at my hand for a mile."

"I've been staring at the trees," Rosalind said. "Because they're moving."

They were. Not the way trees move in wind — there was no wind down here under the canopy, no wind at all, just a green stillness so complete that their own footsteps sounded obscene. The trees moved the way a crowd moves: slowly, with intent, closing gaps behind them. The path they'd walked an hour ago was gone. Not overgrown. *Gone*, as if the forest had never permitted it.

Orsen stopped and looked back at the seamless wall of trunks. "Right," he said. "That's new."

"It's not new." Rosalind came up beside him. Her master's grimoire was heavy against her ribs where she'd strapped it under her coat, and she found herself touching it through the leather, the way you'd touch a wound to be sure it was still yours. "Aldous wrote about this place. He called it the Swallowed Shrine. He said the wood eats roads because it doesn't want to be found by people who are looking."

"Then how do we find it?"

"We don't look." She closed her eyes. This was the part she had never told anyone, not even Dain — especially not Dain — the small domestic magic Aldous had taught her between lessons on lockwork and ledger-forgery. Hedge-craft, he'd called it. Nothing. A nothing-gift, useful for charms and cantrips and flooding fords when witch-hunters were close enough to smell your fear. But hedge-craft had rules, and the first rule was that you didn't take from a living thing without asking.

She put her palm flat against the nearest trunk. The bark was warm. That was wrong, she knew it was wrong, bark should be cool and indifferent, but under her hand the tree felt like a sleeping animal, vast and slow-breathing.

*We're not here to take,* she thought at it, not in words exactly but in the shape of wanting. *We're here because something was left. Something that wants to be given.*

The forest considered this. She felt it considering, the way you feel a landlord reading your forged lease — a long, weighing silence in which anything might happen.

Then the trees opened.

Not dramatically. A gap simply appeared ahead of them, maybe ten feet wide, floored in moss the color of old coins, and beyond it Rosalind could see stone — grey, lichened, half-swallowed by roots, but unmistakably worked by hands.

Orsen exhaled slowly. "I've crossed this forest four times," he said. "Surveying. It took us a week each time and we never saw so much as a boundary stone."

"Maybe you were looking," Rosalind said, and stepped through.

* * *

The shrine had been a tower once, or the base of one; the rest had gone into the ground centuries ago, and the forest had grown over the ruin like skin over a scar. What remained was a single round chamber open to the sky, its walls furred with moss, its floor a mosaic of white and black river stones worn smooth by feet long dead. In the center stood a pedestal, and on the pedestal —

Rosalind's breath caught.

It was a keystone. She knew it the way you know your own name: a wedge of pale stone the size of a loaf of bread, carved all over with script that hurt to look at directly, letters that seemed to rearrange themselves when she wasn't focusing. Light moved inside it. Not reflected light — there was barely any sun under that canopy — but a slow gold pulse, like something breathing in its sleep.

"That's it," Orsen whispered. He had gone very still, the way he went still around anything official, anything with authority, and Rosalind realized with a jolt that he was afraid of it. "That's a keystone of the Veil. I helped draw the borders that cut Thornfell out of the world, and I have spent eleven years telling myself it was paperwork, it was cartography, it was lines on vellum — and there it is. There's the line." His voice cracked on the last word. "It's real. God help me, it was always real."

"Stay there," Rosalind said.

"Why?"

"Because I asked the forest to let us in, and the forest said yes, and I don't think the forest speaks for whatever's guarding that stone." She approached the pedestal slowly, hands visible, empty, the way you'd approach a strange dog or a nervous nobleman. Twenty feet away, ten. The gold pulse quickened. Five feet.

The dead rose.

They came up out of the mosaic floor without disturbing a single stone — three figures, tall and gaunt, armored in corroded bronze over bodies that were more root than bone. Their faces had been carved, not born: serene, eyeless, patient. They did not draw weapons. They simply stood between her and the keystone, and the temperature of the chamber dropped until her breath fogged, and a voice that came from everywhere and nowhere — from the moss, the stones, the standing dead themselves — spoke:

***Why do you come?***

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry. Behind her she heard Orsen shift, heard the whisper of him reaching for the knife he mostly used for sharpening pencils, and she put out one hand to stop him without looking back.

"To carry it," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Someone has to. Before the fire fails."

***Many have come.*** The wight in the center tilted its carved face. ***Soldiers came with axes. Priests came with chains. A king's men came with instruments to measure what cannot be measured. All of them wanted to possess it. None were answered.***

"I'm not here to possess it."

***No?*** The voice was amused, in the way winter is amused. ***You carry a book of stolen craft. You carry a key you did not make. You come with a man who drew the borders of erasure. Tell me, hedge-witch: if we gave you this stone, what would you build with it?***

And that was the question, wasn't it. Rosalind stood in the cold with the dead looking through her, and she made herself think honestly, because she suspected the thing could taste a lie the way she could taste copper.

"Aldous raised me," she said finally. "He never told me why. He taught me locks and ledgers and hedge-charms, and he never told me why, and then the crown burned him in his own doorway and nailed a proclamation to his gate, and I ran, and I'm still running, and I don't know what I'd build." She swallowed. "But I know what I wouldn't. I wouldn't build a wall. Walls are what got everyone burned."

Silence. The wights did not move. The gold light pulsed, slower now, almost thoughtful.

***The last keeper said nearly the same thing,*** the voice said. ***Before the end. She said: a kingdom is not a wall around a people. It is a door they agree to share.*** The center wight extended one root-wrapped hand, palm up, an invitation or a surrender. ***We have waited a generation for someone to say it again. Take what is offered, hedge-witch. And see what you are carrying.***

Rosalind reached out and lifted the keystone from its pedestal.

It weighed nothing. That was the first shock — a stone that size should have taken both arms, and instead it sat in her palms like a bird, warm, alive, pulsing. The second shock came immediately after, because the moment her skin met the stone, the world folded.

* * *

She was standing in a market square.

Sunlight — real sunlight, high and honeyed — fell on stalls hung with green banners stitched with a white thorn-crown. The air smelled of bread and hot metal and river water. People everywhere: a woman arguing cheerfully over the price of eels, children chasing a dog between the barrels, a bell ringing somewhere above tiled roofs, and on every face the easy, unguarded expression of people who have never once wondered whether their country will exist tomorrow.

Thornfell. Living Thornfell. Not ruins, not ash — a city going about a Tuesday.

An old man at a fruit stall caught her eye and grinned. "First time at the Thornsday market, girl? You've the look of someone lost. Here—" he pressed an apple into her hand, red as a coal, warm from his pocket— "no one stands in Corrowmarket hungry. House rule."

"I—" Rosalind's voice failed. She held the apple. She could feel its weight, its slight give under her thumb, the wax of its skin. Somewhere a mother was calling a child home to supper in an accent Rosalind had never heard and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face from a dream. *This is what he lost.* Aldous. This was the Tuesday he had carried alone for thirty years, in a cottage at the edge of a kingdom that had never heard of him, teaching a smuggler's orphan to pick locks because there was nothing else left to teach.

The vision rippled. The market dimmed at the edges like paper near flame.

"No," she heard herself say. "Wait. Please—"

***It is memory,*** the wight-voice said gently, from very far away. ***Memory is all that feeds the fire now. See what dies with him, hedge-witch, if you fail.***

The apple was still in her hand when the market square dissolved — and it was still in her hand when she came back to herself, kneeling on the mosaic floor of the swallowed shrine, tears running freely down her face, the keystone glowing steady and gold against her chest.

Behind her, Orsen said, very quietly: "What did you see?"

Rosalind wiped her face with her sleeve. She got up. She did not answer him, partly because she couldn't yet, and partly because the forest had begun, all around them, to scream.

---

**Orsen**

The trees screamed.

There was no better word for it. The sound came from everywhere at once — a groaning, creaking roar, as if the entire Deepwood had been holding its breath for a century and had just remembered it had lungs. Birds exploded out of the canopy in a black sheet. The moss underfoot shivered.

Rosalind was already moving, keystone clutched to her chest, apple — an apple, where had she gotten an *apple* — jammed into her belt. "Run," she said. "Now. It let us in; it doesn't intend to let us out the same way."

"The guardian—"

"The guardian's done with us. The forest isn't." She grabbed his sleeve and hauled, and Orsen ran, which at forty-three, after three days of hard road and one too many years of drink, was less running than controlled falling forward. Behind them the shrine chamber was filling with roots — he glanced back once and wished he hadn't; the walls were *moving*, the whole ruin folding shut like a fist, the mosaic floor vanishing under a tide of green. Burying itself again. Going back to sleep for another hundred years, or until the next person came who could answer its question.

They burst out of the treeline into grey afternoon light and kept running, downhill, along a streambed, until Rosalind finally staggered to a halt against a boulder, gasping, laughing — actually laughing, a wild breathless sound that alarmed him more than the screaming had.

"It gave me an apple," she said.

"I saw."

"It grew it. Out of a memory. Orsen—" she held it up, and her hands were shaking, and her eyes were wet and furious and lit from inside. "Do you understand what that means? The Veil isn't failing because it's weak. It's failing because it's *starving*. Everything in there — the shrine, the wights, the whole forest — it's all been living on stored memory for a generation. One apple cost that place a year of life. Maybe five." She looked down at the keystone, and her laugh died. "How many more of these are there?"

"Three," Orsen said. "By the old survey records. Buried, hidden, or lost — the crown's archives listed them as 'assets pending recovery' for about six months before someone decided it was safer to pretend the file didn't exist." He hesitated. "Rosalind. What did you see in there? When you touched it. Your face—"

She was quiet for a long moment. The stream muttered past. Somewhere behind them, the Deepwood had gone silent again, satisfied, sealed.

"A market," she said at last. "Full of people. Someone gave me an apple and called it house rule — no one goes hungry in Corrowmarket. I never heard of Corrowmarket. I've never heard of any of it. And I wanted it so badly I couldn't breathe." She turned to look at him, and Orsen, who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of meeting no one's eyes, found he could not look away. "You drew the borders that killed this. So tell me honestly, cartographer. When you erased Thornfell — did you know it was a *place*? Did you know it had markets? Did you know it had a house rule about apples?"

The question went into him like a blade between ribs, which he supposed he had earned.

"I knew it was coordinates," he said quietly. "I was twenty-six years old and the king's warrant had my name on it and the alternative to drawing those lines was the gallows, and I told myself coordinates weren't people. I've been telling myself that for eleven years." He looked at the keystone glowing against her chest, at the gold light playing across her dirt-streaked face. "Seeing it just now — the stone, the shrine, all of it real — I think some part of me hoped I'd been right. That it really was just lines on vellum. That I'd only murdered a map."

"And now?"

"Now I know I need to find the other keystones faster than whoever else is looking for them." He started downslope, toward the fading light and the long road east. "Because the crown burned your master within a week of the Purging proclamation, Rosalind. That's not panic. That's planning. Someone in Veyland has known about the Veil all along — and they're not hunting gifted folk to protect the realm."

"They're hunting us to keep it dead," Rosalind said, falling into step beside him.

"Or worse." Orsen pulled his coat tighter as the mountain wind came down off the peaks ahead, carrying a smell he hadn't smelled since childhood and had never expected to smell again: snowmelt, and far beneath it, faint as a dying ember, smoke. "Worse would be if they're not trying to keep the Veil down. Worse would be if they're trying to bring what's behind it *up* — on their terms, with their army standing on the threshold."

Rosalind stopped. Ahead of them, where the foothills began their climb toward the high passes, the evening sky flickered.

Just once. Just for a heartbeat — the whole western horizon stuttering like a candle in a draft, mountains blinking out and back, out and back — and then it was solid again, ordinary, blue-grey, innocent.

Neither of them said anything. But Rosalind's hand found the keystone at her chest, and Orsen noticed that the gold light inside it had begun, very faintly, to gutter — like a fire remembering it was dying.

"We need to move faster," she said.

They walked into the dark, and behind them, deep in the Deepwood, the swallowed shrine slept on — dreaming, Orsen thought, of apples and Tuesdays, waiting to see if anyone would ever come home.
