## 7. The Guttering

The mountain had no name on any map Orsen had ever drawn, which was how he knew they were close.

He'd spent eleven years as the crown's cartographer learning every trick of erasure. He knew what a removed thing looked like: the smooth blank space where a river should bend, the road that ended in nothing for no reason, the surveyor's note in an old margin that said *here be Thornfell* and then a line struck through it so hard it tore the vellum. Erasure left scars if you knew how to read them. The mountains above Graywater were one long scar.

They climbed for three days out of the Deepwood, and Rosalind did not speak to him for two of them.

He deserved it. He'd told her everything in the survey station — the Unmapping, the borders he'd inked with his own hand, the king of Thornfell who refused to kneel and so was unmade along with his country. He'd expected her to spit at him. Instead she'd looked at him with something worse than hatred: recognition. She'd said, *You drew my master's home off the earth,* and then she'd rolled into her blanket with her back to the fire, and she hadn't forgiven him since.

Dain, by contrast, was cheerful company. Too cheerful. Orsen didn't trust the man's ease — smugglers who smiled that much were either very good or very bought — but Rosalind kept him close, watched him constantly, and said nothing about why. So Orsen said nothing either, and marked it down in the ledger he kept behind his eyes, the one where he recorded debts. Dain's page was already crowded.

On the third morning the trees gave out. They came up over the shoulder of the ridge into a world of grey stone and thin wind, and Orsen stopped so suddenly that Dain walked into his back.

"What," Dain said. "What is that."

Orsen didn't answer. He was looking east, where the range dropped away into a valley that should have held a river town called Marrow's Ford — he'd mapped its market square himself, twenty years ago, before the Purging, back when Veyland still pretended its neighbors existed. The valley was there. The river was there. And above it, the sky was wrong.

It flickered.

Not like lightning. Like a lamp running low — the light over the valley guttered, dimmed, brightened, dimmed again, and each time it dimmed, the valley beneath it went *less*. Not darker. Less. The rooftops thinned to suggestions. The river flattened to a blue line drawn by a hesitant hand. For half a heartbeat Orsen saw the valley as it must look from outside the Veil: empty grassland, unbroken hills, no town at all, never any town.

Then the light steadied, and Marrow's Ford snapped back into being, smoke rising from its chimneys, and Orsen discovered he was on his knees in the scree without remembering falling.

"Orsen." Rosalind crouched beside him. Her hand hovered near his shoulder but didn't land. "What did you see?"

"You saw it too?" His voice came out scraped.

"I saw the light do something strange." Her eyes were narrow, fixed on the valley. "You saw more."

"I saw it stop existing." He got a boot under himself and stood, legs unsteady. "That's what the Veil is, Rosalind. I only understood it in pieces before — I helped build the cage, but I never saw the bars move. It isn't a wall around Thornfell. It's a *hold* on existence itself. Everything inside it exists because something insists it does. And whatever does the insisting—"

"—is dying," Rosalind finished.

* * *

They made camp early, in the lee of a granite spine, because none of them could stomach walking with the sky doing that. Orsen built a fire small enough to hide and sat hunched over it with his old survey journal open across his knees, doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

The Veil had stood for thirty years. He'd assumed it would stand forever, the way you assume the sun. But the flickers had a rhythm now that he'd started timing against his pulse, and the rhythm was shortening. In the Deepwood they'd seen nothing. Yesterday, one flicker at dusk. Today, four since noon, and the last one had lasted long enough that Dain had sworn aloud in three languages.

"The dragon," Rosalind said. She was sitting across the fire, the grimoire open on her knees, though she wasn't reading it. She'd been carrying it like a child carries a talisman since the cottage burned. "Your story said the Veil was woven from the king's dying curse and sustained by fire. There's one fire left."

"Serevanth." Orsen said the name carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "Last dragon of Thornfell. If the histories are right, he's older than the kingdom. When the Unmapping happened, the court mages thought the Veil would burn out in a decade. It didn't. Everyone assumed the spell was simply stronger than they'd estimated." He turned a page without seeing it. "Nobody considered that something might be *feeding* it."

"And now the feeding is failing."

"Now the feeding is failing." He closed the journal. "Rosalind, I need you to understand the mathematics of this, because I don't think you do yet. A concealment spell fails gradually and then catastrophically. First it flickers. Then whole regions go dark permanently — not destroyed, just *uninsisted-upon*, which amounts to the same thing for anyone standing there. Then the whole structure goes at once, all at once, everywhere, and everything inside it—" He stopped. Started again. "Thornfell has maybe forty thousand people left inside it. Descendants. Survivors. People who've lived their whole lives under a sky that blinks."

Rosalind was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere below them, faint as memory, Marrow's Ford flickered again — once, twice — and steadied.

"My master knew," she said finally. "All those years, teaching me hedge-charms nobody uses, making me memorize roads that aren't on maps, pressing a brass key into my hand when the witch-hunters came. He knew the Veil was failing and he was building me into the answer." Her jaw tightened. "And he never told me. Not once. Not even at the end."

"He was Unmapped," Orsen said quietly. "People from inside the Veil learn early that truth is a thing the crown burns."

"That's not an excuse."

"No," Orsen agreed. "It's just the shape of the wound."

* * *

Night came down hard and cold, the way it does above the treeline, and the flickers got worse.

Orsen couldn't sleep. He lay in his bedroll watching the stars perform their slow wrongness — because that was the other thing, the thing that had finally frightened him properly. When the valley light guttered, the stars above it guttered too. Constellations he'd navigated by for thirty years stuttered like candles in a draft. The Hunter lost his belt. The Sisters blinked out and returned one short. The sky itself was part of the Veil's claim, and the claim was lapsing.

Around midnight, Rosalind sat up.

She didn't say anything. She just sat with her blanket around her shoulders, looking east, and after a moment Orsen sat up too, and they watched together as the horizon over the high pass began to pulse. Dim. Bright. Dim. Each dimming lasted longer than the last. Each return was weaker.

"It's like watching someone breathe," Rosalind said. "Someone far away. Someone you can't reach."

"Yes," Orsen said. Because that was exactly what it was, and he hated that she'd found the truer description first.

"How long?" she asked. "Truly. Your best guess."

Orsen did the arithmetic one more time, honestly, because she deserved honesty even if she hadn't forgiven him. "If the intervals keep halving — and failure curves like this always accelerate — we have days. Not weeks. Maybe five. Maybe fewer."

Rosalind nodded slowly, absorbing it, and Orsen watched her do what she apparently always did with terror: pack it down into something usable. He'd seen it at the ford, when the witch-hunters nearly had them and she'd raised her hands and pulled the river out of its banks like a woman drawing a curtain. Terror went in; resolve came out. He wondered what it cost her. He suspected the answer was everything, paid silently, forever.

"Then we move faster," she said. "We leave before dawn."

"We'll kill ourselves on that scree in the dark."

"Better us than forty thousand people who never chose any of this." She looked at him across the dead fire, and her face was half-lit by the pulsing horizon, gold and shadow alternating, gold and shadow. "You owe them, Orsen. You know it. That's why you're here."

It landed like a blow because it was true. He looked away, at the flickering sky, and let the shame sit where it belonged.

"Yes," he said. "I know it."

Behind them, softly, Dain's bedroll rustled. Orsen noted — filed it, the way he filed everything now — that Dain had been awake through all of it, and that the man's eyes, when they caught the pulsing light, weren't fixed on the horizon at all.

They were fixed on Rosalind.

* * *

They broke camp in the black hour, and the mountain tried to kill them for it.

The scree field below the granite spine was loose shale over ice, and they crossed it by starlight that kept misbehaving. Twice the Hunter's belt vanished mid-stride and Orsen lost his bearing entirely, compass spinning uselessly — of course the compass spun; north itself was part of what the Veil insisted upon, and near the thin places, insistence wavered. He navigated instead by the one thing that never flickered: the deep, faint warmth in the rock underfoot, rising from somewhere east, from the caldera at the heart of old Thornfell. The dragon's heat, bleeding up through thirty years of stone. Following it felt like following a heartbeat through a wall.

Near dawn they gained the col — the high saddle between two peaks — and the world opened up, and all three of them stopped at once.

Below them, spread eastward beyond the pass, lay the borderlands of the Veil itself. Orsen had drawn this region once, from rumor and secondhand report, and had labeled it with the crown's official designation: *unproductive terrain, unsurveyed.* What he saw now was a country. Green valleys stitched with stone walls. A road, ancient and well-made, running toward mountains that glowed at their roots with ember-light. And over all of it, vast as weather, the shimmer — the visible edge of the Veil, like heat-haze standing on end, like the surface of water seen from beneath.

It was beautiful. It was also dying.

Even as they watched, the shimmer guttered. A whole western valley — farms, a village, a white church tower — dimmed to a sketch, held there for three heartbeats, four, five, and came back. Somewhere in that village, Orsen thought, people had just lived through another night of wondering if morning would find them. Children born under this sky had never seen a star hold steady.

"Gods," Dain breathed. He'd gone pale under his tan. "That's what you're chasing? That's what she—" He caught himself, shut his mouth, but the sentence had already escaped, and Orsen heard the shape of what it had been going to be. *That's what she sold.* Or: *that's what I told them you were chasing.*

Rosalind heard it too. Orsen watched her hear it — watched her file it away beside the ford and the burned cottage and every other piece of the ledger she was keeping on her oldest friend. She said only: "Keep moving. We cross the col before full light."

But as they descended toward the pass, the sky delivered its argument anyway.

It came without warning. One moment the shimmer stood whole across the eastern horizon; the next, a hole opened in it — a gap miles wide, high up, through which Orsen saw *nothing*. Not darkness. Nothing. The eye refused it. His mind slid off the absence like a nail off glass, and beside him Rosalind made a sound he'd never heard from her, small and involuntary, the sound of someone seeing the underside of the world.

Through the gap, for one terrible instant, the land below was simply not there. No valleys. No walls. No road. Only the bare assertion of empty hills, and the ember-glow at the mountain roots burning on alone, faithful, the last lit window in a house whose walls had failed.

Then the Veil surged closed, gasping, and the country reassembled itself — and Orsen realized he was shouting, and didn't remember starting.

"MOVE!" He grabbed Rosalind's arm. "That's the collapse front — it's not uniform, it's tearing from the edges inward — if we're standing in the open when a gap like that comes down—"

They ran. All three of them, packs banging, down the switchbacks of the col with the sky pulsing overhead like a failing heart, dimming and surging, dimming and surging, each surge weaker. Orsen ran on legs that remembered being twenty, lungs full of knives, and thought with strange clarity: *I drew the borders that did this. I inked the lines that cut this country out of the world, and now the world is finishing the job.*

At the bottom of the col, in the shelter of an overhang, they collapsed together, gasping, and looked back up.

The gap had closed. The shimmer stood. But it stood thinner — visibly, horribly thinner — and through it, steady now, unmistakable, the ember-light at the roots of the eastern mountains pulsed in time with the Veil's failing rhythm. Slow. Slower. A great fire banked down to coals, and the coals going grey.

"There," Rosalind said hoarsely, pointing. "That glow. That's him, isn't it. That's the dragon."

"That's Serevanth," Orsen said. "Or what's left of his fire." He fumbled out his journal with shaking hands and began, compulsively, to sketch the pass, the shimmer, the glow — because drawing was the only prayer he knew. "Two keystones, your master's book says, and both of them feed the flame. We have one shrine in the Deepwood behind us and a salt mine ahead, and days, Rosalind. Days."

"Then we don't sleep again until—" She stopped.

Orsen followed her gaze. Dain was standing apart from them at the mouth of the overhang, looking back up the col the way they'd come — south, toward Veyland, toward the lowlands, toward the roads a man might take if he had news to sell and buyers waiting. He stood there a long moment. Then he turned back, smiling his easy smile, and said, "Right. Salt mine. Lead the way."

Rosalind smiled back at him.

Orsen, watching, felt the ledger behind his eyes open to a fresh page, and wrote the date at the top of it.
