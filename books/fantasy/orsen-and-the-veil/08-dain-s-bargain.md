## 8. Dain's Bargain

The rain had been following them for two days, and Dain had been telling himself stories the whole way.

That was his gift, if he'd had one — not hedge-craft, not the little charms Rosalind could pull out of her sleeve like coins, but the older magic of a man lying to himself in a warm voice. He was good at it. He'd had practice.

*You're protecting her,* he told himself, watching her ride ahead through the drizzle, Orsen's grey mare plodding behind hers. *You're keeping her close where you can see her. You're the only reason she's still breathing.*

It almost worked. It usually did.

They were three days out of Graywater Pass, following an old drover's track that wound up into the hills like a scar that hadn't finished healing. Dain rode at the rear, which he'd insisted on — *someone has to watch our backtrail* — and which let him look at Rosalind without her looking back. She'd changed since the cottage burned. Not just the grief; grief he knew how to read on a face, knew how to use. This was something harder. She sat her horse like a woman who had already decided what she was willing to lose, and that frightened him more than any witch-hunter ever had.

He'd sold her name for less than she'd have paid for it herself. That was the joke of it. The hunters' captain hadn't even haggled. A pardon, a purse, and a promise not to ask questions about the other names on Dain's list, and he'd given up twenty years of friendship across a tavern table like a man paying off a debt of six coppers.

*But you didn't know they'd burn the cottage.* That one he told himself most often, and it was even true, and it changed nothing, because a man who sells a knife doesn't get to choose what the buyer cuts.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a shepherd's bothy, its roof half fallen, its walls thick enough to hold back the wind. Rosalind built no fire. She'd stopped building fires the night after the ford, when she flooded a river crossing with a word and a gesture and saw the witch-hunters' horses screaming in the water. Now she cooked over coals raked into a pit so shallow it might have been an accident of the ground.

Orsen sat apart, as always, mending a strap on his saddlebag with the concentration of a man performing surgery. He was growing on Dain, which annoyed him. He'd expected the disgraced cartographer to be either a coward or a fanatic, and instead he was neither — just a tired man carrying guilt the way other men carried a bad back, quietly and everywhere.

"So," Dain said, dropping down beside Rosalind and offering her the last strip of dried venison. "Graywater Pass tomorrow."

She took the meat without looking up. "Tomorrow. Why?"

"No reason. Just mapping it out loud." He grinned. "Old habit. Smugglers narrate their routes so the route stays real. You remember."

"I remember." She chewed, watching the dark beyond the broken doorway. Then, quieter: "Do you ever miss it? The roads. Before all this."

"Every day." And that was true too. That was the worst part — how much of what he said was true. Lies built entirely from truth held up longest. "I miss the salt road in autumn. I miss Halloran's terrible coffee. I miss—" He stopped himself before *you*, because that door opened onto a room he didn't want to stand in tonight.

She glanced at him then, and for one unguarded moment her eyes were nineteen again, both of them seventeen and rich with somebody else's cargo, laughing on a rooftop in Marrowgate while the watch whistled below. Then the moment closed, the way doors do, and she said, "Sleep, Dain. Long climb tomorrow."

He slept badly. He dreamed of the cottage burning and woke once to find Orsen awake too, watching him from across the dead fire-pit with eyes that missed nothing. Cartographers, Dain thought. They spend their lives noticing what's out of place. He turned his back and willed himself back under.

* * *

The hunters caught him alone at midday, and later — much later — he would understand that this was not luck.

Rosalind and Orsen had gone ahead to scout the pass's eastern fork, leaving Dain with the horses at a stream crossing. He was watering the bay mare when the bracken moved, and four figures rose out of it like the hill itself deciding to stand up. Grey cloaks. Iron-banded staves. The crown's white hand stitched at every shoulder, and above them all, unhurried, the captain.

Ilse Vane. He'd met her once, in a room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, when he'd given her the first name. She looked exactly as he remembered: plain as a fencepost, calm as deep water, with a scar splitting one eyebrow and eyes that weighed a man like a butcher weighing meat.

"Dain Ferro," she said. No question in it.

"Captain." He kept his hands loose at his sides, kept his voice easy. Twenty years of talking his way past toll-keepers and tax-men lived in that voice. "This is a lonely stretch of road for four of the crown's finest."

"We're not here for you." She walked past him, looked at the horses, looked at the stream, looked at the track climbing toward the pass. Reading it. Reading *him*. "We're here for the road you're standing on."

"I don't follow."

"You followed well enough in Marrowgate." She turned back to him, and there it was — not menace, worse, patience. "Halloran's apprentice. The burned cottage. You gave us the name, we gave you the paper. The paper's good for one name. I'm told you're traveling with two more now. The old mapmaker. And the girl."

Dain felt the bottom drop out of his stomach and land somewhere around his boots. "I don't know what—"

"Save it." Vane drew something from inside her cloak: not a warrant, not a blade. A folded square of paper, grease-stained, familiar. His own handwriting. His own list — the names he'd copied out that night in Marrowgate, the ledger of everyone he'd ever run goods with, written in the desperate arithmetic of a man buying his life cheap. "Your master list. Recovered from your rooms. Your pardon covers the names you gave us. It does not cover the ones you didn't." Her thumb moved down the page and stopped. "There are eleven names here, Ferro. You gave us three."

The rain had stopped. Dain became aware of everything at once — the water talking over stones, the bay mare shifting her weight, the enormous silence of the hills, and underneath it all the small, clear knowledge that he had maybe thirty seconds before these four people stopped needing him alive.

"Eleven names," Vane said again. "Eight outstanding. The crown pays eight pardons' worth of gold for eight names, or one rope for one liar. Choose quickly. My lieutenant is not a patient man."

Behind her, the lieutenant smiled. Dain would remember that smile later, in the bad hours. He'd seen men look at cargo that way.

And here is the thing Dain would never be able to explain honestly, not to Rosalind, not to a priest, not to himself at three in the morning — although he would try, God help him, he would try until the trying wore grooves in him: in that moment, with eight names between himself and a rope, he genuinely believed he was doing the merciful thing.

Because he told himself: *they'll take her alive.* The proclamation wanted the gifted surrendered, not killed. There were forms. There were cells, trials, collars — the crown liked its witches collared and useful. If he gave Vane the route, the hunters would take Rosalind cleanly, at Graywater Pass, before she walked into whatever doom she was walking toward. Before she fed herself to some dragon or Veil or old ghost of a dead kingdom. Before Orsen's maps got her killed in a hole in the mountain. He'd heard enough of their talk by fires to know where they were bound. The caldera. Old Thornfell's grave.

*A clean arrest beats a dirty death,* he told himself. *A collar beats a pyre. I'm saving her from herself.*

It was beautiful, the story. It fit his hands perfectly. It was even ten percent true, which for Dain Ferro was practically sainthood.

"Graywater Pass," he heard himself say. His voice came out steady, and he marveled at it, the way a drowning man might marvel at the calm blue surface of the water above him. "Eastern fork. Tomorrow, near dusk — they scout slow, the old man's horse is done. They'll camp at the drovers' shelter below the scree. She carries two keystones wrapped in oilcloth in her left saddlebag, and a grimoire against her ribs, and she can flood a river with a word so don't come at her from the water side." He swallowed. "Come at dusk. She sleeps light but the old man doesn't. Take her quiet and she won't hurt anyone. She's not—" His voice cracked, finally, one hairline fracture, and he hated himself for it. "She's not what the proclamations say. Don't let them hurt her."

Vane watched him the whole time without blinking. When he finished, she folded the list away and nodded once, like a merchant closing a fair bargain.

"The eastern fork at dusk," she said. "And the destination?"

"They're making for the old capital. Under the mountain. The caldera." He couldn't stop now; stopping meant thinking. "There's a dragon. Or the bones of one. Something about a Veil. I don't understand it and I don't want to."

For the first time, something moved behind Ilse Vane's eyes — not surprise exactly. Recognition. As if he'd named a place she'd already dreamed of. It passed in a blink.

"Go home, Ferro," she said. "South. If I find you north of Graywater again, the pardon becomes kindling." She turned to her lieutenant. "Backtrack to the river road. We take the pass from the west rim — higher ground. Move."

And then they were gone, folding back into the bracken the way they'd come, and Dain stood alone in the loud silence with two horses and a pardon that suddenly weighed as much as an anchor.

* * *

He caught up with them an hour before dusk, waving, grinning, full of a story about a straying horse and a bog hole. Rosalind barely glanced at him. Orsen watched him a moment too long, the way he did, and said nothing, and Dain filed that look away under *later, deal with later.*

They climbed. The pass narrowed. The light went long and copper-colored, and the shadows of the scree fields stretched out across the track like fingers reaching for the valley floor.

Dain rode at the rear and told himself the story one more time, polishing it, perfecting it. *They'll take her alive. There are cells. Collars. She'll live. Better a collar than a dragon's grave. I'm saving her. I'm the only one saving her.*

But the story had developed cracks, and through the cracks he could hear the truth moving around in the dark like something with too many legs:

He'd given them the route, the hour, the camp, the saddlebag, the grimoire. He'd handed a woman who trusted him — who had shared roofs and roads and stolen bread and twenty years of almost-something — to the people who burned her master in his bed. He'd dressed it up as mercy because mercy was the only coat that fit over what he'd done, and even then the seams showed.

And underneath, quieter, in the cellar of himself where he kept the things he actually knew: he'd done it because he was afraid. Because Rosalind was becoming someone he couldn't follow — someone with a key and a quest and a dead kingdom waiting for her like a bride — and if he couldn't keep her, he'd rather the crown had her safe in a collar than free in a world that was leaving him behind.

*Love,* he called it. He'd call it love for the rest of his life, probably. It had just enough of love's shape to pass, the way forged coin passes in bad light.

Below the scree, the drovers' shelter came into view, smokeless, dark, exactly where he'd promised it would be. Above, on the western rim, the last sunlight caught something that wasn't rock — a flicker of grey, iron-banded, gone.

Dain's mouth went dry.

*Rosalind.* The name rose in his throat like a stone coming up. One shout. One warning, thrown ahead of the hunters, and everything he'd done today became something else — a trap he'd walked into and then walked out of, a betrayal interrupted, forgivable, survivable. One word and he was still her friend.

One word.

The shelter grew closer. The light died off the rim. Somewhere above them, patient as winter, Ilse Vane waited for dusk.

Dain opened his mouth.

And found he had absolutely nothing to say.
