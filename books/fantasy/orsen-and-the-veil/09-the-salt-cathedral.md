## 9. The Salt Cathedral

The mine swallowed the last of the daylight a mile before we reached it.

I'd known salt country my whole life without knowing it — the white scars on the hillsides south of Graywater, the spoil heaps like burial mounds, the way the wind tasted faintly of something old and mineral. Smugglers ran brandy through these hills twice a season. I had walked past the mouth of the Delve-Mother Mine a dozen times and never once looked at it twice.

Now I understood why. You weren't meant to look at it twice.

Orsen stopped at the treeline while I went forward alone, as the letter in the grimoire's margin had instructed. *Come to the salt when the moon is dark. Come alone. Come willing.* The moon was dark tonight, a hole in the sky where the stars around it seemed too bright, and the mountain above us flickered — I'd seen it flicker three times since the Guttering, valleys going thin as smoke and snapping back — so *willing* was not a word I was in any mood to parse.

The mine mouth was timbered with beams gone silver-grey, older than any timber had a right to be. No cart tracks led in. No slag. Just a shaft descending into a darkness that didn't smell of dust or rats but of clean cold salt, like the inside of a preserved thing.

I went down.

The ladder rungs were iron worn smooth by generations of hands, and I counted them because counting kept the fear in its box — forty rungs, sixty, ninety — until my boots found floor and the darkness opened out around me like a held breath.

Then someone struck flint, and the cathedral caught fire with light.

Salt. Walls of salt, veined blue and rose and bone-white, rising in pillars that some patient hand had carved into columns, arches, vaulting overhead into a ceiling that glittered like frost. Benches of translucent stone faced an altar that was not an altar at all but a block of raw salt with something wrapped in oilcloth resting on it, no bigger than a loaf of bread. Lanterns hung from hooks cut into the walls, dozens of them, unlit, waiting for a congregation.

And there was a congregation. Perhaps thirty people stood among the benches, silent, watching me. Farmers' hands. Miners' shoulders. A woman with a baby slung at her hip. An old man leaning on a stick. They wore rough wool and road mud, and every one of them wore, at throat or wrist or pinned inside a collar, a small token of blue glass.

Thornfell's color. The color from the vision the first keystone had shown me — banners against a green sky, a king's crown of antler-bone, a river running silver through a city that no longer existed.

"You came down alone," said the old man with the stick. His voice carried in the salt air the way sound carries in a chapel. "Good. That's obedience, at least. Whether it's wisdom we'll see."

"I'm looking for the keystone," I said. My voice sounded small under all that vaulting. "And answers."

"Everyone who comes here is looking for both." He limped forward, one step at a time, and up close his eyes were pale as the salt around us. "Most are looking for them in the wrong order. Name yourself."

"Rosalind Halloran. Apprentice to Aldous Merrin, hedge-witch, deceased. Carrier of the first keystone." I touched the satchel at my hip where the shard of green stone lay warm as a sleeping bird. "Student of Orsen Vael, formerly royal cartographer to the crown of Veyland."

At the name *Vael* a murmur went through them, low and ugly. The woman with the baby pulled her child closer. The old man raised one hand and the murmur died.

"He told you what he did?" the elder asked me.

"He confessed. In a survey station in the Deepwood. He drew the borders that unmapped Thornfell. He's spent thirty years drinking himself toward death over it, and for the last month he's been climbing mountains and bleeding for a chance to undo it. Hate him if you want. I've done my share. But he's the only man alive who knows how the Veil was woven, and you'll need him before this is finished."

Silence. The lanterns swayed slightly on their hooks though there was no draft underground.

"The Unmapped do not forgive easily," the elder said. "We are the ones your maps forgot. My grandmother crossed out of Thornfell the night it burned behind her. She watched the borders close like a wound sealing, and she spent the rest of her life teaching children the names of streets that were no longer anywhere." He studied me with those pale eyes. "You carry Merrin's key. Then you know already that he was one of us."

"I learned two nights ago," I said. "From his handwriting. He never told me while he lived."

"No. He wouldn't. Telling makes it real, and real things can be hunted." The old man turned, stick tapping, and gestured me toward the altar. "Come. You've come far enough to see what we keep."

* * *

Up close, the oilcloth bundle was nothing much. I unwrapped it with fingers that wanted to shake and made them not, and there it lay: a second keystone, twin to the first, a wedge of green stone shot through with veins that glowed faintly, faintly, like embers refusing to die. When my skin touched it, the warmth climbed my arm and settled behind my breastbone, and somewhere impossibly far away — miles, maybe more — I felt something vast turn its attention toward me.

The dragon. Feeling the keystones come together.

"It knows you now," the elder said quietly. "Serevanth. Last of the fire-drakes, oldest living thing under the sky, and dying. Every day his flame gutters lower, and every day the Veil thins with it. You saw the flickering on your way here. Valleys blinking. Roads that exist at dawn and don't exist by dusk. That is a kingdom's death rattle, girl, and it's getting faster."

"Then we hurry," I said. "Two keystones. We go to the caldera, we find the dragon, we restore—"

"Restore." He said the word the way a surgeon says *amputate*. "Sit down, Rosalind Halloran."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down anyway." It wasn't unkind. It was the voice of a man who had delivered this particular news before and would deliver it again after me. I sat on a salt bench that was cold through my trousers.

He lowered himself beside me, stick across his knees, and spoke to me the way you speak to someone at a graveside.

"Merrin taught you craft. Hedge-charms, floodings, small workings. So you know the first law: nothing is given freely by the world. Every working draws from somewhere. A charm takes heat from a hearth. A flooding takes the water's own weight and turns it. Small magic, small prices." He paused. "Now scale it. What does it cost to hold an entire kingdom outside of existence? To fold a nation — its land, its people, its dead — into a pocket between the world's attention? What sustains that?"

"A life," I said. My master's marginalia had already shown me the shape of it, and I had been refusing to read the words straight-on for two days. "A life freely given."

"One life, freely given, fed into the dragon's fire. Serevanth burns it slow — a willing soul is fuel that lasts decades, a lifetime, longer. That was the bargain the first time. There was a woman, at the end, when Thornfell's armies were broken and the king was dead. She walked into the fire of her own will, and the Veil rose, and our people were hidden, and she has been burning ever since. Thirty years of being consumed, slowly, so that we could survive." His voice didn't waver. "She is nearly ash now. That's why the sky flickers."

I looked at the keystone in my hands. Warm. Patient. Like something waiting to be fed.

"And when she's gone?"

"Then either another walks into the fire, or everything under the Veil dies screaming as it's crushed back into the world. The city. Whatever remains of the Unmapped who stayed behind. And the collapse won't be gentle — a Veil that falls tears as it goes. Everyone within fifty miles of the caldera dies with it. Villages. Towns. The borderlands of Veyland itself." He spread his hands. "That is the truth you asked for. Restoration isn't a gift. It's a furnace, and it wants feeding."

The cathedral was very quiet. Somewhere above us, muffled through a hundred feet of salt, I thought I heard wind.

"There has to be another way," I said. "There's always another way. Magic is a craft, and crafts have techniques—"

"Every generation, someone comes down that ladder saying that." The elder almost smiled. "Merrin said it, forty years ago, sitting on that same bench. He searched his whole life for a technique. He found you instead. He found a gifted child with smuggler's roads in her blood and no family to mourn her, and he raised her next to the answer, hoping she'd never have to read the last page." The pale eyes held mine. "He hoped you'd find a third option, Rosalind. He also knew you might not. And he left you the key anyway, because a chance with a terrible price is worth more than certainty with none."

My hands had closed around the keystone without my deciding to. Behind my breastbone, the distant vastness stirred again — the dragon, feeling both shards together, feeling *me*.

"If I refuse," I said slowly, "the Veil falls and kills thousands. If I accept, someone walks into a fire. Those are the choices."

"Those are the choices as they stand," the elder agreed. "We've kept the faith a generation on the hope that the choices might change. We teach our children the old names. We light the lanterns on the dark of every moon. We wait for someone clever enough — or desperate enough — to break the arithmetic." He rose, joints cracking, and looked down at me with something that was not quite pity and not quite faith. "Are you clever enough, smuggler? Or only desperate?"

I stood. The keystone went into the satchel beside its twin, and the two stones touched, and the warmth behind my breastbone flared into something almost like a heartbeat.

"I don't know yet," I said honestly. "But I know I'm not walking into that fire today, and I know I'm not letting fifty miles of people die either. Which means somewhere between those two facts, there's a door. I've spent my life finding doors in walls other people called solid. Give me time."

The elder studied me a long moment. Then, slowly, he bowed — a real bow, deep, the bow of a man greeting something he'd waited a generation to greet.

"Then take the blessing of the Unmapped," he said, "and take warning besides: you're not the only one hunting the caldera. Three days ago, crown riders passed the north ridge. Witch-hunters, moving fast, asking questions about a woman and a disgraced mapmaker." His face tightened. "They have someone with them who knows our roads. Someone who used to run them."

Dain. The name landed in my chest like a dropped stone. He was still traveling with us — or had been, until the pass, until the ambush, until Orsen—

Orsen. Who was in their hands.

"They took my friend," I said. "Three days ago. At Graywater Pass."

"Then you know better than anyone what they'll make him tell them," the elder said quietly. "And you know exactly where you're all headed — the same place, drawn by the same dying fire. Go up the ladder, Rosalind Halloran. The moon is dark, the hunters are riding, and the dragon is running out of time to be saved by anyone at all."

Behind my ribs, the two keystones beat together like a single heart learning to count.

I climbed toward the dark of the mine mouth, and the cold night air, and the mountains that flickered between existence and erasure — carrying the price of survival in a satchel at my hip, and no idea yet whose life would pay it.
