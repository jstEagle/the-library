## 10. Ambush at Graywater Pass

**Rosalind**

The pass had a smell to it, and Rosalind had learned to trust her nose over any map Orsen could draw.

Graywater Pass smelled of wet stone and old snowmelt, of pine rot and the iron tang of the river that gave it its name — a river that ran gray because it carried the mountain's ground slate down in suspension, fine as flour. It was the kind of place smugglers loved and hated equally: narrow enough to defend, open enough to be seen from three ridgelines if you were careless. Dain had walked them into it with the easy confidence of a man who'd crossed here a hundred times, pointing out where the old toll-station had stood, where you could ford the river without losing your boots, where the crown's patrols never bothered to look because there was nothing worth taxing.

That was the thing about Dain. He made carelessness sound like wisdom.

They were two days out of the salt cathedral, and Rosalind still felt the weight of Elder Maren's words sitting on her chest like a stone. *A life freely given.* She hadn't told Orsen yet. She didn't know why she was keeping it from him — or maybe she did, and the reason had teeth. If she said it aloud, he would start looking for ways around it, and she wasn't sure she wanted to be talked out of something before she'd even decided to do it.

The keystones rode against her ribs in a double satchel, one warm as a sleeping bird, one cold as well-water. The first sang to her sometimes when she slept. She tried not to think about that either.

"Water ahead," Dain called back, cheerful as a market-day bell. "We cross by noon, we're through the pass by dark, and after that it's shepherd country all the way to the caldera road."

"You've been very helpful," Orsen said flatly. He'd been saying things like that since the deepwood — not hostile exactly, but weighted, each sentence set down carefully like a card he suspected was marked. He hadn't liked Dain from the first hour. Rosalind couldn't decide whether that was Orsen's cartographer's instinct reading terrain wrong, or reading it right.

"He's been helpful," Rosalind said. "Which is more than I expected, so let's not sour it."

Dain grinned at her over his shoulder, that old grin from the windward road, from the nights they'd run lantern-oil past the excise men and split the take on a flat rock under the stars. Fifteen years of friendship lived in that grin. That was what made it unbearable to look at directly.

Because she knew. Had known since the second night, when he'd asked — too casually, while sharpening a knife that didn't need it — which way they meant to go after the mountains. When he'd mentioned, apropos of nothing, that the witch-hunters' new captain was said to be thorough. When she'd found the scrap of birchbark in his coat pocket, folded small, marked with charcoal in a cipher she'd taught him herself when they were nineteen: *R. HALLORAN. GRIMOIRE CONFIRMED. TWO OBJECTS OF INTEREST.*

She had stood there in the dark with that bark in her hand and her whole history in her throat, and she had put it back.

Why? She'd been asking herself that for days. Because she wanted to be wrong. Because fifteen years was a country she didn't want burned. Because some cold, practical part of her — the part her master had trained, the part that survived the fire at the cottage — whispered that a known traitor was more useful than an unknown enemy. Let him lead them. Let him think he was leading them. Every step Dain chose was a step she could anticipate.

So she walked into Graywater Pass knowing, and she let herself be known into it, and the only thing she allowed herself was this: she checked the ridgelines. Twice as often as a smuggler should.

It was on the fourth check, somewhere past the burnt toll-station, that she saw the crows lift off the eastern ridge all at once, like a handful of black grain thrown into the sky.

Crows didn't do that for wolves. Wolves came up the slope. Something had come *down* it.

"Dain," she said quietly. "How many men does a pardon buy?"

He turned. For half a heartbeat his face did something complicated — and then it did nothing at all, smooth as river stone, and that smoothness was answer enough.

"Ros—"

"Down," she said, and threw herself sideways as the first crossbow bolt went through the space where her head had been and buried itself in a pine trunk with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Then the pass was screaming.

They came off both ridges at once — she'd been watching the east and the west had waited for her eyes to commit, which meant someone up there understood how a person scans terrain, which meant this wasn't a patrol stumbling onto them. This was planned. Drawn. *Mapped.* Black-and-gray figures in the crown's hunting leathers, moving with the drilled economy of men who had done this to other people many times, and the crossbows kept coming, and the river was thirty yards away and might as well have been thirty miles.

Orsen was shouting her name. Dain was shouting something else — she caught *surrender, Rosalind, surrender and they'll only* — and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Dain believed that. That he had probably been promised it. That in whatever story Dain told himself on the road behind closed eyes, handing her over gently was a kindness, was mercy, was love even, was better than her dying in a crossfire he'd arranged.

A bolt tore through her sleeve. Another grazed her thigh, hot as a brand, and she went down on one knee among the stones and felt something in her rise that she had spent her whole life keeping folded small.

Her gift. Her hedge-craft. The little charms, the finding-spells, the weather-sense — the things that got Master Aldous nailed to his own doorpost in proclamation and burned to ash inside his own cottage.

At the ford, weeks ago, she had flooded a river to save them, and it had taken everything and left her shaking. This was not that. This was not a charm. The rage came up through her like water through cracked ground, and the two keystones against her ribs answered it, humming, and the gray river stopped running gray and started running *bright*, and every stone within twenty paces of her lifted an inch off the earth and hung there trembling.

The crossbowmen stopped. Even at a distance, even through the smoke of their own volley, she saw them feel it — the pressure, the wrongness, the air going thick as tallow. One of them made a sound she'd remember later: not a battle cry, not fear exactly. Recognition. The sound of a man realizing the stories were undersold.

*Rosalind,* something in her said, in her master's voice, in her own voice, *you could end them. Right now. Every one. And no one would ever hunt a gifted child again.*

And God help her, some starving corner of her wanted to say yes.

* * *

**Orsen**

Orsen had spent eleven years learning how to be small. A disgraced cartographer in a border town learns to drink quietly, walk with his shoulders rounded, keep his eyes on the mud. It had kept him alive through the purges of the Guild, through the night they burned the master-atlas room with three archivists locked inside, through every year since. Smallness was a craft, and he had been good at it.

Watching Rosalind Halloran stand up out of the gravel with the stones rising around her like a congregation, Orsen forgot every lesson he had ever learned about being small.

The bolts hung in the air near her — not stopped, he realized dizzily, *redirected*, curving away like iron filings refusing a magnet. The river had gone bright and was running uphill in places, threads of silver water climbing the bank and hanging there. And Rosalind stood in the middle of it with blood on her leg and her hair whipping in a wind that existed nowhere else, and she looked less like a smuggler than like the margin-notes come alive — all those furious annotations Aldous had inked in the grimoire's edges, the ones Orsen had pretended not to understand.

*Here is what they feared,* Orsen thought. *Here is what we drew the maps to bury.*

"Halloran!" The voice cut across the pass like a whip-crack, and Orsen knew before he turned that it belonged to the woman on the eastern ridge, because it was the only voice present that wasn't afraid. "Captain Ilse Vane, Crown Office of Inquiry! You are ordered to surrender yourself and all objects of interest!"

Objects of interest. As if the keystones were tax goods. As if Rosalind were contraband.

And then Vane came down the slope herself — descending the scree at a pace no sensible soldier would attempt, cloak snapping, sword drawn but pointed at the ground, walking straight through the zone of lifted stones as though the air there were ordinary air — and Orsen saw what Rosalind could not see from the center of her own fury, because he had spent a lifetime reading terrain and Vane moved like terrain that had been surveyed.

The stones didn't touch her. Not wouldn't. *Couldn't.* They curved around her the same way the crossbow bolts curved around Rosalind, and Captain Ilse Vane walked through a hedge-witch's unbound fury untouched, and she was smiling — not cruelly. Knowingly.

*Oh,* Orsen thought. *Oh, you clever, damned woman.*

"She's gifted!" he shouted, and his voice broke on it. "Rosalind — the captain — she's one of *us*!"

He saw it land. Saw Rosalind's eyes find Vane across fifty feet of suspended stone and bright water, and saw the exact moment the fury in her face turned into something worse: betrayal. Not personal — cosmic. The hunt's captain, gifted, serving the crown that burned cottages and nailed proclamations to gates. Serving it *well*, apparently. Wearing its leash like a sash.

Vane heard him too. Something flickered across her face — quick, quickly buried — and Orsen understood he had just done something irreversible. You couldn't un-tell a secret like that. Not to a woman like her. Not in front of her own hunters.

"Take the mapmaker," Vane said, and her voice was steady but her sword-hand wasn't, quite. "Let the girl run. We only need the objects."

*Only need the objects.* Which meant she didn't want Rosalind dead, or — colder thought — didn't want her dead *here*, in front of witnesses, with stones still hanging in the air like a miracle mid-sentence. Which meant Vane was managing something, and managing meant agenda, and agenda meant leverage Orsen didn't yet understand.

The hunters surged forward. There were perhaps twenty; the pass funneled them; and Rosalind, standing at the center of her held breath of power, had to choose — Orsen saw the choice arrive in her body, in the tremor that ran through the floating stones. Hold them all, hold the pass, hold *him*, and burn out the way she'd burned out at the ford. Or drop it and run.

"Don't," he called to her. "Don't you dare spend yourself on me. Run, girl. *Run.*"

"I'm not leaving—"

"That's an order from the man who unmapped a kingdom!" It came out ragged, furious, true. "I have cost enough countries. I will not cost you too. GO."

And because she was who she was — because underneath the fury and the grief and the terrible new power she was still, at bottom, the woman who had gone back into a burning cottage for a book — she hesitated. One heartbeat. Two.

It was long enough.

A hunter reached him — big, methodical, professional — and put him down into the gravel with a knee between his shoulder blades, and the last thing Orsen saw clearly was Rosalind turning toward the river, toward the far wall of the pass, the satchel clutched to her chest, and Dain standing between her and escape with his hands raised and his mouth moving — pleading, gesturing, *pointing* — and whether he was pointing her toward safety or toward the hunters' net, Orsen couldn't tell from the ground, with a boot on his neck and the world tilting.

The stones fell. All at once, like a held note released. The river remembered which way was downhill. And Rosalind went up the western scree alone, small and fast and impossibly alive, and vanished into the pines with both keystones and no one at all.

Then a hood came over Orsen's head, and the pass went dark.

* * *

**Rosalind**

She ran until the pass was a bruise of sound behind her, and then she ran until it was nothing at all, and then she climbed a lightning-split pine above the tree line and watched the road below through the gap of her fingers, because the part of her that survived fires always, always counted exits twice.

They brought Orsen out an hour later, hooded, bound, walking. Alive. She catalogued that the way she'd once catalogued cargo: alive, walking, unmarked. Vane rode beside him on a dun mare, and even at this distance Rosalind could read the geometry of it — the captain keeping her prisoner close, not as trophy but as *possession*. Something to be protected from her own men? Something to be traded?

*She's one of us.*

His last words rang in her skull like a dropped bell. She turned them over as the column snaked down toward the valley, and the more she turned them, the more they explained. The way Vane had walked through her working untouched. The way the ambush had been shaped — precise, surgical, designed to take the mapmaker and scare the witch rather than kill either. A gifted woman hiding inside the hunt, wearing its colors as camouflage, surviving the purge by being its best blade. Rosalind knew that arithmetic. Had done versions of it herself, all her life: hide the gift, laugh at the right jokes, spit on the right rumors.

But Vane had gone further than hiding. Vane had *hunted*. Had burned, maybe. Had signed whatever needed signing. And now she had Orsen, and the crown's warrant, and every reason in the world to squeeze him until the caldera road fell out of his mouth like a stone from a boot.

Rage came again, and this time Rosalind didn't fight it, because fighting it had nearly killed her an hour ago. She let it sit. She studied it, the way her master had taught her to study anything dangerous — at arm's length, respectfully, noting its habits.

*I could have killed them all,* she admitted to the pine-scented silence. *Every one. And part of me wanted to.*

The keystone against her ribs pulsed once, warm, like a hand on a fevered forehead. Warning, or comfort. She genuinely couldn't tell anymore.

Below, the column disappeared into the valley mist, taking her cartographer, her confession-keeper, her impossible, cowardly, brave old man — the only person alive who knew what the Veil was and how it had been drawn. They would interrogate him. He would resist; he was stubborn as bad weather. But everyone talked eventually, and Vane was patient, and Dain —

Dain.

She had seen him at the end, hands up, mouth moving, pointing. Pointing west, toward the scree she'd climbed. Toward her route. And the hunters had swept east instead.

She sat with that for a long time, knees drawn up, watching the mist eat the road. Maybe he'd been trying to misdirect them. Maybe he'd been pointing and missing. Maybe mercy and betrayal were the same gesture viewed from different ridgelines, and she would die never knowing which one Dain had made.

It didn't matter. What mattered was this: Orsen was in the hands of a gifted captain who served the crown, and Rosalind had two keystones, a stolen horse's chance, and a destination that was now, thanks to Dain's first betrayal, almost certainly known to the enemy. The caldera. The dragon. The deadline burning down like a candle in a draft.

They would expect her to run. To abandon Orsen, flee north, save herself.

So that settled it.

Rosalind slid down from the pine, tore a strip from her hem to bind her grazed thigh, and turned south — toward the valley, toward the hunters' campfires she could already imagine kindling at dusk, toward the woman who wore the hunt like armor and the man who wore friendship like a mask and the old man in chains who had once, with clean lines and obedient ink, erased a kingdom.

She had lost the pass. She had not yet lost everything.

By dark she'd have a plan. By morning, a stolen horse.

And somewhere ahead of her, in a tent lit by a single lamp, Captain Ilse Vane was about to learn what happened when you took the one person whose guilt was heavier than your warrant.

Rosalind smiled, and there was nothing kind in it, and she hated how good it felt.
