## 11. The Captain's Mirror

The cell smelled of wet stone and old blood, and Ilse Vane had stood in enough of them to know the difference between a man who was afraid and a man who was calculating which fear to show her.

Orsen Vale was the second kind. He sat on the bench with his wrists chained to a ring in the wall — standard procedure, though she'd have preferred the chair — and he watched her come down the steps with the flat patience of a man who had already decided how much of himself he was willing to lose.

She let the door bang shut behind her. Let the lantern swing once, twice, throwing his shadow long across the floor. Small theater. It worked on smugglers and hedge-witches and boys caught with stolen bread. She doubted it would work on him, but ritual had its own value: it told the prisoner who held the keys, and it told her who she was supposed to be tonight.

"Orsen Vale," she said. "Formerly Royal Cartographer to the Crown of Veyland. Dismissed eleven years ago for reasons that were never entered into the record." She set the lantern on the table between them and sat, unhurried, arranging her coat. "Dismissed quietly. Which is interesting, because quiet dismissals usually mean someone wanted you gone without questions. And here you are, eleven years later, walking the border roads with a smuggler girl and two keystones of the Veil in her pack."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Orsen said.

"Of course not."

She opened the folder she'd brought. Not because she needed it — she had memorized every page — but because the sound of paper made men lean forward. They always wanted to know what was written about them. Vanity survived everything, even chains.

"Let me save us both the evening," she said. "You're going to tell me where she's going. The Halloran girl. You'll do it eventually; they all do. The only question is whether you do it before or after I stop being polite, and whether what's left of you afterward is worth the trouble of feeding."

Orsen looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, bitter, more breath than sound.

"You rehearsed that," he said. "The whole speech. You've given it before."

"Fifty-one times this year."

"And how many of those fifty-one talked?"

"Forty-four."

His eyes flickered. Recalculating. Good. She wanted him off balance — not broken, never that, broken men lied to make the pain stop and their lies were worthless — just unsettled enough to forget that he was supposed to be the one with secrets.

"Here's my problem, Vale," she went on. "My orders say bring in the gifted and anyone aiding them. My orders also say, in a sealed annex I wasn't supposed to read but did, that the keystones are to be delivered not to the Archive, not to the King's surveyors, but to the Office of Purification. Do you know what the Office of Purification does with objects like those?"

Something moved behind his face. He knew. Of course he knew. A court cartographer who'd helped draw Thornfell out of the world would know exactly what the crown's cleansers did with relics of the Unmapping.

"They burn them," he said quietly.

"They unmake them. There's a difference, and it matters." She leaned back. "Burned things leave ash. Unmade things leave nothing — no record, no memory, no trace that they ever existed at all. Sound familiar?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the guardroom floor. The lantern hissed.

"You were there," Ilse said. "At the Unmapping. You drew the borders that erased a kingdom. So let me ask you something, cartographer, and think carefully before you answer, because I'll know if you lie: when the Veil falls — when the last dragon dies and whatever spell holds Thornfell's ghost together finally gutters out — what happens to everyone standing inside it?"

Orsen's chains clinked as his hands tightened.

"That's not public knowledge," he said.

"No. It isn't. That's rather my point."

* * *

He tried to hold out for another hour. She let him. It cost her nothing; she had water brought, she asked small questions, she built the shape of him out of answers he didn't know he was giving. Where he'd been living — a border town called Harrow's Ford, a room above a chandler's. How long since he'd held a commission — eleven years, three months. Whether he drank — the tremor in his hands answered that one before his mouth could.

And all the while she felt it, the way she always felt it, low under her sternum: the hum. His gift. Faint, dusty, half-starved from disuse, but unmistakable. The gift of true measure — the sense that let him see distances and boundaries as plainly as other men saw faces. It reached out from him even now, even chained, brushing the walls of the cell, measuring the room, measuring *her*, and he didn't even seem to know he was doing it.

Ilse Vane had spent nine years hunting people like herself. She knew exactly what the hum felt like from the inside.

"You still use it," she said, during a lull. "Your gift."

Orsen went very still.

"The measuring," she said. "You can't help it. You've been mapping this cell since I walked in. Corners, sightlines, thickness of the door. Am I wrong?"

"You're a witch-hunter," he said. "Why would you care what I—"

"Because I want to know if you're honest about anything." She stood, crossed to him, and crouched so they were eye to eye. Close enough that he couldn't look anywhere else. "One question, cartographer. Answer it truly and we can keep talking like civilized people. When you drew the borders at the Unmapping — when you signed your name to lines that erased a kingdom and every soul in it — did you know what the lines would do? Did you know the Veil would take them? All of them?"

His jaw worked. She watched him weigh it — the old reflex, the instinct to deflect, to lawyer, to survive — and then something else won. Exhaustion, maybe. Or the particular honesty of a man who has been drinking himself blind for eleven years over the same question.

"Yes," he said. "I knew."

"What did they tell you it would be? Officially."

"A boundary revision. A redrawing. Thornfell's king refused to kneel after the war, so the crown dissolved the marcher lordships and folded the territory into Veyland. On paper." His voice had gone flat, reciting. "They told me the maps were administrative. That the old kingdom would fade from common use, nothing more. Nobody said anything about a Veil. Nobody said anything about—" he stopped.

"About the kingdom taking its people with it when it went."

"Yes."

"Did you ask?"

"At the end? Yes." He laughed again, that terrible dry sound. "By then the ink was dry and the king's seal was on it and my name was in the colophon. Do you understand what that means, Captain? Every copy of every map. My name. Forever. In the document that killed a country."

Ilse rose and stepped back. Her heart was doing something she refused to name.

* * *

She should have taken the confession and left. That was procedure. Extract, verify, report. She had done it fifty-one times this year and forty-four of those prisoners had talked and none of them had ever seen her hands shake, because her hands did not shake.

But she stayed by the door a moment longer, and in that moment Orsen looked up at her — really looked, with the measuring gift and the ordinary eyes both — and said:

"You're gifted."

The cell went silent as a held breath.

"It's faint," he went on, and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible precision of a man whose whole life had been precision. "Suppressed. Bound somehow — a discipline, or a charm, something you wear. But it's there. I'd guess perception. Sight of some kind." He tilted his head. "How long have you been hunting your own kind, Captain Vane?"

Her hand was on her knife before she'd decided to move it. Old training. She made herself take it away.

"Careful," she said.

"Or what? You'll report me?" He almost smiled. "To whom? The Office of Purification? Tell me, Captain — when they test the hunters, as they do every spring, what do they find when they test *you*?"

The lantern flame bent, though there was no draft.

Nine years. Nine years of wearing the cold-iron band under her collar, the one that pressed her gift flat and left a scar like a brand around her throat that she told people came from a smuggler's garrote. Nine years of standing in testing lines holding her breath while the examiners' probes swept the room, knowing the band would hold, knowing it had to hold, knowing that the day it failed was the day she traded one cell for another — or worse, traded her sword for a pyre. Nine years of hunting girls like Rosalind Halloran so that no one would ever think to hunt her.

And this ruined, drunken, disgraced mapmaker had seen through it in an hour, from a bench, in chains.

"How," she said. Her voice came out lower than she intended.

"Your suppression charm leaks. Badly. Whoever sold it to you took your coin and gave you tin." He shrugged as far as the chains allowed. "Also, you flinched when I said 'unmake.' People only flinch at words that could apply to them."

Ilse sat down across from him again. Slowly. Deliberately. Because if she didn't give her body something to do, it was going to do something unforgivable.

"You understand what you've just done," she said. "You've handed me the best reason I will ever have to put a blade in you and write it up as an escape attempt. No inquiry. No questions. A dead traitor-cartographer tells no tales."

"Yes," Orsen said. "And yet you haven't. Which tells me you're not going to. Which tells me something else, too." He leaned forward, chains drawing taut, and for the first time since she'd entered the cell he looked less like a prisoner than a man laying cards on a table. "It tells me you've been asking yourself the same question I have. What is the crown actually going to do with the Veil? Because you read the annex. You know it's not containment anymore. Containment was the story for the first ten years. This year the language changed. I'd bet my remaining years you've noticed."

She said nothing.

"Purification doesn't mean destroy anymore, does it, Captain," he said softly. "Not for the Veil itself. For the keystones. They don't want the spell dead. They want it *owned*. A kingdom that exists only where the crown permits, visible or invisible at royal pleasure. A weapon shaped like a country. And the Office of Purification doesn't need the dragon alive for that. They need it *broken*. Tamed. Collared. And everyone who knows how the Veil was woven — every exile, every keeper, every gifted soul who might reweave it otherwise — burned or bound first." He sat back. "Starting, I imagine, with any hunter who might object."

The lantern hissed. Ilse discovered her own hands were flat on the table, braced, as if the room had tilted.

She thought of the annex. She thought of the new orders that had come down in autumn, the ones with the changed phrasing, *acquisition and control of Veil-relevant assets*, and how she had read them three times and told herself it was bureaucratic drift. She thought of the testing lines each spring, and the year the examiners had started asking hunters to submit to *voluntary secondary screening*, and how she had volunteered first, loudly, so no one would wonder why she hesitated.

She thought of the girl at Graywater Pass, cornered, outnumbered, who had looked at Ilse across the smoke with an expression Ilse recognized from her own mirror — rage and terror braided into something that could go either way, toward the hunt or against it.

Complicity, her mother used to say, is just cowardice that's learned to march.

"Say your piece," Ilse said at last. "Whatever bargain you've been building toward all night. Say it."

* * *

Orsen took a breath, and Ilse watched him assemble the offer with the same care he must once have given to coastlines and mountain ranges — line by line, checking each measurement twice.

"The Halloran girl is going to the dragon," he said. "Serevanth. Under the caldera, in the old capital. She has two keystones and her master's grimoire, and she means to restore the Veil before it fails. If she fails, the collapse kills everyone within miles — including, incidentally, every hunter the crown has on these roads. Your included."

"I know."

"Then you know the crown's plan is worse. They'll reach the dragon after us, with collars and extraction teams, and they will try to take a dying god's fire by force, and either they'll fail and the Veil comes down on all our heads, or they'll succeed and every border village in Veyland becomes a hostage inside a spell the king can switch on and off." He held her gaze. "Help me get to the dragon first. Help me reach Rosalind. And I will bury the annex. I will bury the changed orders, the acquisition language, all of it. No report reaches the Office of Purification with your name anywhere near it. As far as the record shows, Captain Ilse Vane lost her prisoner in the pass and spent the winter searching. Diligently. Heartbreakingly."

"And when it's done? When your smuggler girl works her miracle or dies trying?"

"Then you can arrest us both," Orsen said. "Honestly. I'll even wait. But you'll do it knowing what the crown meant to do, and you'll have chosen which country you serve — the one on the maps, or the one that makes them."

Ilse was quiet for a long time.

She thought about the mirror in her quarters at the garrison, the one she avoided looking at in the mornings while she fastened the cold-iron band beneath her collar. She thought about how many springs she had volunteered first. She thought about a girl's eyes across the smoke at Graywater Pass, and about the word *unmade*, and about her mother's voice saying *cowardice that's learned to march*.

Fifty-two, she thought. This makes fifty-two. And the first one I've wanted to fail.

"If you're lying to me," she said, rising, "if this is some cartographer's trick, some measured little trap—"

"Captain." Orsen smiled, and for one moment, behind the ruin of him, she glimpsed the man whose name was written in the colophon of a killing document — young, brilliant, certain he was drawing lines on paper. "Everything I've ever done badly, I did with perfect measurements. I'm tired of being precise. Take me or trust me. Either way, we ride at dawn."

Ilse unlocked his chains. The iron fell loud in the dark, and outside, far to the north, over the mountains where the sky sometimes flickered like a guttering lamp, the night seemed — for just a heartbeat — to blink.
