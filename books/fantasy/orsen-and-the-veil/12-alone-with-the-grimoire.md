## 12. Alone with the Grimoire

The shepherd's hut sat in a fold of hillside like something the mountain had tried to swallow and given up on. Its turf roof was half-collapsed at one corner, its single window a black square stuffed with heather against the wind. Whoever had lived here had been gone long enough for the hearth to grow a skin of moss.

Rosalind checked it anyway — corners first, then under the cot, then the loose flagstone by the door that every smuggler knew was never as loose as it looked. Empty. Cold. She set her pack down inside and stood a moment in the doorway, listening to the wind comb the high grass, before she let herself breathe.

Orsen was two ridges back with the horses, minding the fire they'd agreed not to light until full dark. Two days since Graywater Pass. Two days since she'd run with both keystones wrapped in wool against her ribs while hunters' horns tore the dusk apart behind her, and Orsen's voice — *go, go, don't look back* — had been the last thing she heard from him.

She hadn't looked back. That was the discipline of smuggling: you didn't look back, because looking back was how you died, and dying meant you couldn't come back later. So she'd gone, and now she sat down on the cot's rotten frame with the grimoire in her lap and told herself that going back for him was exactly what she intended to do.

Just not yet. Not without knowing what she was carrying.

She unwrapped the book from its oilcloth. It was the only thing of Master Aldous's she'd saved besides the brass key, and it was ugly — a slab of salt-stained leather, spine re-sewn twice with gut cord, corners gnawed by some animal that had clearly regretted it. He'd called it his *ledger*, which was a joke between them, because to anyone else it would have looked like an accountant's ruin: columns of figures, cargo manifests, tide tables, all in his cramped left-slanting hand.

But Rosalind had been his apprentice for eleven years, and she knew the ledger was a mask the way the Veil was a mask. The real text lived in the margins.

She turned to the last page he'd worked on and held it near the window's grey light.

*The ford will flood if you ask the water nicely,* he'd written once, beside a sketch of the very crossing where she'd drowned three witch-hunters' pursuit. *It always wanted to. Remember that about rivers, and about most things.*

She'd read that note a hundred times on the road and smiled at it every time. Tonight it made her chest hurt. Everything in this book was him talking to her across his own death, patient as ever, and she was only now beginning to understand that he'd been talking to her like this for years — not teaching hedge-craft, but building something.

She started at the beginning.

Not the first page; Aldous had taught her that no honest book starts at its own first page. She went instead to the flyleaf, where the oldest writing lived, and there — as she'd known it would be, because she'd checked every night since the burning and every night found herself unable to make herself read it — was the line she'd been avoiding.

It wasn't in Veylandic script. It was in the old hand, the one from the shrine in the Deepwood, the one carved around the keystone's cradle. She'd seen enough of it now to pick out letters, and Aldous had drilled her on the sounds of it years ago, calling it "the smugglers' cant" when she asked what language it was.

*Born under the Veil,* the flyleaf said. *I remember the sun rising over Thornfell.*

Rosalind read it four times. Then she set the book face-down on her knee and looked at the mossy hearth for a long while.

He remembered the sun rising over Thornfell. Which meant he'd been alive when Thornfell still rose. Which meant —

No. That couldn't be. Thornfell had been unmapped before the crown's oldest records began; even Orsen, who'd drawn the borders that erased it, spoke of it like a man speaking of a fever dream. Nobody alive remembered it. The exiles in the salt mine were children of children of the Unmapped, keeping a faith they'd inherited secondhand.

But Aldous had never lied to her. He'd hidden things — God, he'd hidden everything — but he'd never lied.

*You're reading it wrong,* she told herself, and picked the book back up, and read it right, and it said the same thing.

* * *

She worked through the night by stubs of tallow candle she found in a wall niche, and the marginalia opened to her like a lock finally given the right key.

Once she accepted that the flyleaf was true, everything else rearranged itself. The columns of figures weren't figures. They were dates — dates in a calendar Veyland didn't use, counting forward from a year that had never officially existed. The cargo manifests were lists of names. She ran her finger down one and felt the hair rise on her arms: *Maren Ostwick, gifted, safe passage north. Tam Broadfoot and daughters, three, gifted, safe passage north.* Decades of names. Decades of people Aldous had moved along the smugglers' roads, out of Veyland, toward — toward what? Toward the mountains. Toward the caldera. Toward the dragon whose fire was failing even then.

He hadn't been a hedge-witch who took in an orphan. He'd been a keeper. A relay station in a network older than the crown, running the last survivors of an erased nation along roads that were themselves part of the spell.

She found the page that proved the roads were the spell around midnight, and had to put the candle down and press both palms flat against the cold floor until the shaking stopped.

Aldous had sketched the smugglers' routes — her routes, the ones she'd run since she was nine years old, the windward road, the salt road, the graywater trail — and over them, in the old hand, he'd written their true names. *The Windward Road is the Veil's east thread. The Salt Road runs its spine. Where the threads cross, the keystones may be moved.* Every route she knew by heart, every shortcut Dain had shown her, every ford and pass — they were stitches. She hadn't been running contraband all these years. She'd been walking the seams of a garment that kept a dead country wrapped and warm.

And the crown had just declared the Purging of Gifts, and burned her master's cottage, and nailed a proclamation to his gate, and she understood suddenly, wholly, why. It wasn't fear of hedge-witches. It was that the crown knew the Veil existed, knew it was failing, and wanted every person who could touch it gathered up or ash before it failed in a way nobody could control.

They weren't hunting witches. They were hunting thread.

* * *

Near dawn she found the pages about herself.

She'd been dreading them without knowing it, the way you dread the last room in a house you're searching. They came late in the book, in fresher ink, in the years after she'd arrived — a scrawny, silent child pulled half-frozen from a river flood, delivered to his door by a woman Aldous paid in silver and never named.

*The girl has the mark,* he wrote. *Faint, but there. The river gave her back, which the old texts say means the Veil gave her back. I have prayed to be wrong. I am not often granted prayers.*

Rosalind's hand went, without her deciding it, to the base of her throat, where a birthmark sat like a thumbprint of shadow. All her life she'd assumed it was nothing. Aldous had once touched it, when she was small, and gone very quiet, and she'd thought he was being tender.

*I did not choose her,* the margin continued. *Let that be written plainly, so that whoever reads this after me does not mistake me for a kind man. The Veil chose her, or the river did, and I chose only not to stand in the way. But I raised her deliberately. I taught her the roads because the roads must be known by someone with the mark. I taught her the craft because the craft is the thread-work wearing work clothes. And I taught her to survive alone because I suspected — correctly, it seems — that I would not live long enough to finish.*

There was more, much more, whole passages crossed out and rewritten, and beneath them, in the firmest hand in the entire book, the sentence he had clearly struggled hardest to write:

*If you are reading this, Rosalind, then I am ash and you are carrying my ledger, which means you already know I never told you the truth straight out. Forgive me. A door that announces itself is kicked in. You were safer not knowing you were a door.*

She read that line until the candle guttered and the words swam.

*A door.* Not a weapon, not a queen, not the heir to anything so grand as a throne. A door. Something that stood between two countries and belonged fully to neither, and could be opened from either side, and could be barred — and could be broken.

She thought of the vision the keystone had shown her in the Deepwood: living Thornfell, market bells, a king laughing, ordinary people under an ordinary sun. She'd ached for it like a homesickness for a home she'd never had. Now she understood the ache had been installed in her on purpose, or inherited, or grown into her like the mark — and understanding it didn't shrink it. If anything it grew larger, because now it had a name. It wasn't longing. It was inheritance. The Unmapped had passed down their country the only way they could: inside their children, folded small, waiting.

Her master had been born under the Veil. He'd spent his whole life carrying a nation in a ledger disguised as accounts, moving its remnant along its own stitched roads, and when the end came he'd pressed a brass key into her hand and burned rather than give up one name.

And she had spent eleven years thinking she was an orphan learning tricks.

* * *

Full daylight came grey through the heather-choked window. Rosalind got up, stiff and cold, and built a small fire in the mossy hearth because she needed one, needed the sound of it, and put the kettle on because Aldous had always said no truth should be faced without tea.

While it heated, she laid out what she knew, the way she'd lay out a cargo manifest, because order was the only prayer she trusted.

One: the Veil was real, and failing, and the smugglers' roads were its threads.

Two: the keystones were its anchors, and she carried two of them, and there were others.

Three: Serevanth, the last dragon, was dying, and when his fire went out the Veil would fall — violently, Orsen said, taking everything within miles of every thin place with it.

Four: restoring Thornfell outright required a life freely given to the dragon's fire. The elder in the salt cathedral had said it plainly, and Rosalind had carried those words like a stone in her boot ever since.

Five: her master had raised her for this. Not for the sacrifice — she searched the margins again, desperately, and found no instruction toward it, no ritual of giving, nothing that suggested he'd intended her death. What he'd intended, she realized slowly, turning the pages, was choice. Every lesson, every drill, every hard winter he'd made her survive alone in the hills — he'd been building someone capable of standing at the hinge of history and *deciding*, rather than being decided for.

*You were safer not knowing you were a door.* But doors don't get to stay shut forever. Someone knocks.

The kettle sang. She poured the tea and drank it scalding, sitting on the floor with her back against the cot, the grimoire open on her knees, and for the first time since the cottage burned she let herself cry — not the sharp silent tears of Graywater Pass, but the slow, ugly, private kind, grief and fury and something underneath both that she refused, for now, to name, because naming it sounded too much like hope.

She cried for Aldous, who had been born under a sun that officially had never risen, and died under a sky that officially contained no such country. She cried for the girl from the river, who'd been handed a destiny before she could walk and never told. She cried because she was tired, bone-tired, hunted, alone on a cold mountain with a dead man's book and a living man's debt, and because none of it mattered, and all of it did.

Then she wiped her face, because Aldous had also taught her that grief was weather, not climate, and weather passes.

* * *

She spent the rest of the day doing what Aldous would have done: working.

She copied the true names of the roads onto a scrap of hide, in case the book was taken from her. She mapped the keystone sites she knew against the thread-crossings and found they matched perfectly — the Deepwood shrine sat where the windward and salt roads crossed; the salt cathedral sat on the spine itself. The pattern was beautiful and terrible, a whole geography of concealment, and somewhere in it was the caldera, the center, the knot where all threads ran home.

And somewhere in it, too, was the answer to the question the elder had posed. Because Aldous's marginalia kept circling one phrase, appearing in a dozen hands' widths of text across thirty years, always the same five words:

*The Veil takes willing memory.*

Not lives. Memory. She found it first beside a recipe for a forgetting-charm, then beside a sketch of the caldera, then — most tellingly — in the final entry of the book, written in a shakier hand than the rest, dated three days before the burning:

*The old rite demands a life because the old kings thought in terms of walls and prices. But the Veil was never a wall. It is a remembering, sustained. Ask what remembers, and what wishes to be remembered, and whether a kingdom's love for itself might be fuel enough. I am too old to test it. Perhaps she is not.*

Perhaps she is not.

Rosalind closed the book and held it against her chest and stared into the fire for a long time.

It might be nothing. An old man's speculation, a theory never tested, the kind of elegant idea that dies beautifully the moment it meets reality. The dragon might need blood and flame, not sentiment. The elder had spoken with the weight of generations behind her words.

But Aldous had never written a careless word in his life, and he had chosen those five words for his final entry, knowing the book might be all of him that survived.

*Ask what remembers, and what wishes to be remembered.*

Outside, the light was going gold and long. Orsen would be bringing the horses up within the hour, and she would have to decide what to tell him — how much of this to share with a man who had helped erase the very country her master came from, a man currently in a hunter's cage because he'd stayed behind to buy her time. She owed him rescue. She did not yet know if she owed him trust.

She was still weighing it when she heard the hoofbeats — and then heard something else under them, wrong, too many hooves, too fast, coming up the windward side of the hill where no friend would ride at a gallop.

Rosalind snuffed the fire with a thought, swept the grimoire and keystones into her pack, and was through the back of the hut and into the heather before the first rider crested the ridge.

Below her, on the track, she saw them clearly in the low sun: six riders in crown grey, and at their head, riding with his hands bound to his saddle, head hanging, alive — Orsen.

And riding beside him, pointing up at the shepherd's hut, smiling like a man greeting family, was Dain.
