## 13. The Traitor's Confession

Dain had been following her for three days before he worked up the nerve to show himself.

Three days of trailing a woman he'd known since she was eleven years old and all knees and elbows, hauling contraband brandy down the Windward Road, learning the smugglers' paths faster than any boy twice her age. Three days of watching her move through high country with Orsen the cartographer at her shoulder, both of them bent under packs, both of them checking their backtrail like professionals. Three days of rehearsing what he'd say.

None of the rehearsals survived contact with his own stomach. Every time he got as far as *Rosalind, I need to tell you something*, the words curdled into *Rosalind, I sold your master to the witch-hunters for a pardon and thirty crowns* and he'd stop walking and dry-heave behind a boulder until the retching passed.

He was doing exactly that, on the morning of the fourth day, when a voice behind him said:

"You've been chewing the same strip of jerky since Graywater Pass. Either it's terrible or you're not eating it."

Dain turned around slowly, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand, feeling every hour of sleeplessness on his face. Rosalind stood ten feet away on the sheep track, arms crossed, pack still on. She'd left Orsen somewhere below — smart, that. She'd known someone was back there, and she'd come alone, which meant she'd wanted to handle whatever she found without witnesses.

She'd always been the smartest person Dain knew. It was one of about four hundred things he was going to miss about her.

"Hallo, Roz," he said.

"Don't." Her voice was flat as slate. "Don't 'hallo' me. You've been on our trail since the pass. I counted your fires. You built them in hollows like I taught you, which is how I knew it was you, because nobody else alive is stupid enough to think hiding smoke in a hollow works above the treeline."

"It usually works."

"It never worked. I let you believe it worked because you were proud of it." She came closer. Up close he could see she'd been crying recently — the raw rims of her eyes, the salt tracks the wind hadn't finished erasing. He'd put those tears there, some of them. Most of them. All of them. "Why are you here, Dain?"

And there it was. The door he'd walked three days to reach, standing open, and him unable to step through.

"I heard you went east," he said. "After the pass. I heard they took Orsen."

"They did."

"And you got him back?"

"That's my business."

"Is he—"

"Why are you here, Dain?" she asked again, quieter this time, and quiet was worse. Quiet meant she wasn't performing anger. Quiet meant the question was real.

Dain looked past her at the mountains, at the thin smear of woodsmoke rising from the shepherd's hut where she'd been hiding, at the enormous indifferent sky where the Veil flickered sometimes now — whole valleys blinking out like guttering candles, the world hiccupping on its own erasure. Everyone on these roads had seen it by now. Everyone knew something was ending.

"Sit down," he said. "Please. This is going to take a while, and if you're standing over me I'm going to lose my nerve again, and I've lost it about forty times already."

She didn't sit. But she didn't leave either, so Dain lowered himself onto a rock and put his face in his hands, because looking at her while he said it was more than he could manage.

"The night your master's cottage burned," he said, "I was in Colder's Ford. I'd gone there to move a shipment of Veylandish steel through the tollhouse — legitimate work, well, legitimate for us — and Captain Vane's hunters were in the taproom of the Drowned Ewe. They weren't hunting anyone specific yet. They were asking questions. Who moved goods on the Windward Road. Who kept strange hours. Who'd ever been seen doing anything the crown would call gifted."

He heard Rosalind go very still.

"And you told them," she said.

"I told them about Master Aldous." His voice cracked on the old man's name. "Not on purpose. Not — no. That's a lie. On purpose. A hunter bought me ale and asked me directly if I knew any gifted folk, and I thought, if I don't answer, he'll mark me as uncooperative, and if I mark as uncooperative they'll start pulling my shipments, asking about my cargo, and I panicked. I gave them Aldous's name the way you'd give a cutpurse your purse — fast, so it'd be over. I told myself it wouldn't matter. Old man, hedge-wizard, lives alone, what were they going to do, ask him questions? And then—" His throat closed. He forced through it. "Then the cottage burned, and the proclamation went up, and your name was on the list, Roz. Your name was on the list and I put it there. Because after Aldous I owed them more. Because once you've paid a debt in other people's blood, the collectors keep coming back."

The silence that followed was the longest of Dain's life. Longer than the silence in the hold of a listing ship. Longer than the silence after his father died. He made himself look up.

Rosalind hadn't moved. She stood with her arms crossed and her eyes fixed on him with an expression he couldn't read, except that it wasn't surprise. That was the worst part. She wasn't surprised.

"How long have you known?" he whispered.

"Since Graywater Pass." She uncrossed her arms. "Vane's lieutenant called you 'our friend on the roads' when they had Orsen tied up. Said you'd given them the route. Said you'd told them where we'd cross. I didn't want to believe it, so I spent two days proving it to myself instead of running, which may yet get me killed. The ford, Dain. You told them about the ford."

"I told them everything." He laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and broken. "Everything except the keystones, because I don't know where those are, thank all the dead gods for small mercies. But the route, the crossings, the survey station, the hut — if they ask, I'll tell them. That's what I am now. That's what I made myself."

"Why?" It came out of her almost gently, which was somehow crueler than screaming. "We grew up together. You carried me across the Mirefen when I broke my ankle. You taught me cards. You're the reason I know the Windward Road at all. Why?"

Because he was a coward. Because he loved his own skin. Because loyalty was easy when it cost nothing and he'd discovered, the first time it cost something, what he actually was.

"Because I was afraid," he said. "That's the whole of it. No grand reason. No grievance. You'll want there to be a reason and there isn't one. They offered me a pardon and thirty crowns and I was afraid of what refusing would cost, and I've been afraid ever since, and fear turns out to be a debt that compounds."

Rosalind finally sat. Not near him — six feet away, on a stone, her hands laced tight in her lap, and Dain understood the distance for what it was: a border drawn between them, permanent as anything on Orsen's maps.

"Aldous raised me," she said. "You knew that."

"I know."

"He found me stealing apples off his tree when I was nine. Instead of thrashing me he asked if I could read. Then he fed me. Then he taught me everything — the craft, the letters, the hedge-charms, all of it. He was the only person who ever chose me, Dain. My own blood didn't. The road didn't. He did. And you sold him for thirty crowns and a signature."

"I know."

"Do you?" She leaned forward. "Do you actually know it, or do you just wish you did? Because I've watched you talk your way out of things your whole life, and I keep waiting for the turn. For the part where you explain how it's really fine."

"There's no part where it's really fine." Dain dug into his coat and drew out the paper he'd been carrying for three days, folded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. He held it out. "This is the pardon. Vane signed it. It names me as a crown informant. It has my description on it, my haunts, my routes. It's the single most valuable document I own and I've been trying to work up the courage to burn it for three days and I can't, because burning it means admitting what I am. So you burn it. Or keep it. I don't care anymore. I just can't be the one holding it."

She took it. Unfolded it. Read it without expression.

Then she struck flint to her knife's tang, touched flame to the corner, and held it until fire climbed the page and licked her fingers before she dropped the last black scrap into the dirt and ground it under her heel.

"There," she said. "Now neither of us is holding it."

Dain's eyes were burning. He scrubbed his sleeve across his face, hating himself for it, hating everything.

"Here's what I came to do," he said. "It's the only thing I could think of that even begins to pay it down, and I know it doesn't, I know nothing pays it down, but listen anyway. Vane thinks I'm still hers. She expects a report on your route east. She expects me at the Drowned Ewe within the fortnight with word of where you're going and who's helping you. If I don't come, she assumes you killed me or bought me, and she doubles her patrols on every eastern road and hunts you blind but hard."

"Or?"

"Or I come to her with a report that's true in every particular except the one that matters. I tell her you're heading for the Salt Cathedral — which you were, I saw the marks on your map roll, I'm not guessing. I tell her the route. I give her real details, real campsites, real timings, things she can verify, things her scouts will confirm, so she believes me. And then—" He swallowed. "And then I lead her. Personally. I offer to guide her column along the high trail above the Gullet gorge, the one only smugglers know, the shortcut to the mine country. And at the narrows, where the trail runs along a ledge with a four-hundred-foot drop, I take her column down the wrong fork. The dead fork. The one that used to be a smugglers' escape route before the rockfall, the one with no bottom."

Rosalind stared at him. "There's no way out of the dead fork."

"No."

"Dain."

"It might not kill everyone," he said quickly. "It's dark, it's broken ground, some of them will live, hunters are tough, they'll climb out eventually. But it'll cost them days. Days you need. And Vane will be so furious she'll tear the pardon up herself, and the men who crawl out will remember I led them into a hole in the world, and—" He spread his hands. "You see the shape of it."

"I see the shape of it. You die in that gorge."

"Probably."

"Probably isn't no."

"Probably is the best odds I've earned." He made himself hold her gaze. "Rosalind Halloran, I have betrayed everyone I ever claimed to love, including you, including a kind old man who once gave an apple-thieving brat a job out of pure kindness, and I cannot undo one hour of it. But I can choose, right now, today, with my eyes open, whose side the last of me lands on. Let me land on yours. It's all I've got. It isn't much, but it's true, and I have been lying so long that being true feels like setting down a weight I forgot I was carrying."

Rosalind looked away. When she spoke again, her voice had gone rough.

"If I say no, will you do it anyway?"

"Yes."

"If I say yes, does that mean I forgive you?"

"No."

"Good." She nodded slowly. "Because I don't. Maybe someday, maybe not, maybe not even then. What you did to Aldous — I don't know if there's forgiveness shaped for that. But I'm not going to waste the one decent thing you've ever done with your life by refusing it out of spite." She stood, and for a moment her face softened into something that looked, briefly, like the girl who'd crossed the Mirefen on his back, laughing at the leeches. Then it hardened again, and the moment was gone. "When do you leave?"

"Tonight. Vane's forward camp is at Hollowmere. Two days' walk if I push. I'll tell her I trailed you to the hut, that you're resting a week with a bad ankle, that the cathedral route goes through the Gullet." He rose too, joints aching. "She'll want to move fast. The Veil's failing everywhere; the crown's terrified of what comes out if it falls. She won't wait."

"Orsen will want to argue with the plan," Rosalind said. "He'll say there's another way. There isn't, is there?"

"Not one that buys you more than a day."

They stood facing each other on the empty sheep track, two people who had shared a childhood and could share nothing now, and Dain found he didn't want the last words to be about gorges and columns and death. 

"Your master," he said. "Aldous. Was he good to you? At the end, I mean. Before."

Rosalind's jaw tightened. "He pressed a brass key into my hand while his house burned and told me to run. So yes. To the very end."

Dain nodded. It was more than he deserved to hear.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the night I gave them his name, I went home and drank until I couldn't stand, and I told myself I'd warn him in the morning. And in the morning I told myself I'd warn him the next morning. And then the cottage burned, and I learned the thing I'll carry into whatever comes after: there is no morning brave enough. There's only the choice you make while you're still afraid. I made the wrong one. I'm making a different one now, while I'm still afraid. That's all confession is, I think. Just a braver coward, at the end."

Rosalind reached out — he flinched before he could stop himself — and straightened the collar of his coat, the way she used to when they were kids heading into a toll-town, making each other look respectable before the customs men saw them.

"Walk safe, Dain," she said.

"Walk safe, Roz."

He turned east toward Hollowmere and didn't look back, because if he looked back he'd lose his nerve for the fortieth-and-first time, and this time there was no forty-second.

Behind him he heard her boots on the track, climbing toward the hut, toward Orsen, toward the dragon's dying fire and the door at the end of the world.

Two days later, in the command tent at Hollowmere, Dain stood before Captain Ilse Vane with a report that was true in every particular except the one that mattered, and watched her eyes narrow — not at his words, but at something behind them — as she said:

"Before we discuss the girl, informant... tell me why you smell of guilt and mountain ash. And why your hands haven't stopped shaking since you walked in."

* * *

Far to the east, Rosalind felt the pardon's ashes settle in her chest like snow, and began to walk.
