## 14. Fire Under the Mountain

**Rosalind**

The heat came first.

Not warmth — heat, a pressure against the skin like standing too near an open forge, though the mountain air around us stayed cold enough to fog our breath. Orsen felt it too. He stopped on the scree slope and pressed his palm flat against a boulder, and when he pulled it away his fingers came back clean but trembling.

"It's under us," he said. "Under all of this."

We had followed the dragon's dying fire for six days — through the high passes where the Veil thinned so badly that valleys blinked in and out of being, through shepherd country where Rosalind-the-smuggler's old routes had dissolved into geography that no longer agreed with itself. The grimoire rode against my ribs in its oilskin wrap, and both keystones rode in my pack, and every mile north they grew heavier. Not in weight. In *pull*. Like two compass needles that had finally found their north and wanted nothing else.

Now we stood at the throat of the caldera.

Old Thornfell's capital had been built inside a dead volcano — I knew that from Orsen's maps, or rather from the maps he'd burned and redrawn until the truth of them fit in his head alone. What the maps hadn't shown me was the scale. The caldera rim stretched wider than any city wall I'd ever smuggled goods past, a broken crown of black rock five hundred feet above us, and the floor below was filled with fog. Not cloud-fog. Ash-fog, grey and fine as flour, stirred by nothing.

"Is it alive down there?" Dain asked. His voice had been quiet for three days now, ever since the confession I still hadn't forgiven. "The city. Is anything—"

"Nothing's alive," Orsen said. "That's the point."

He led us down a goat track that switchbacked along the inner wall, and with every turn the heat pressed harder and the ash-fog rose to meet us, and then the fog parted the way a curtain parts, and I saw the streets.

They were intact.

Every cobbstone laid true, every gutter cut straight, every doorway standing open onto rooms that still held their furniture under a skin of grey. A fountain stood at the first crossroads we reached, dry, its basin full of ash drifted like snow, and carved into its rim was a motto in a script I didn't know and yet somehow could almost read: *From fire, the door.* Lanterns still hung from iron brackets along the main street, their glass unbroken, waiting for someone to light them. A child's wooden horse lay on its side in a doorway, one wheel still turning slowly in a draft I couldn't feel.

"Nobody looted it," Dain whispered. "A whole capital, and nobody ever came back for the silver."

"You can't loot what you can't find," Orsen said. "And you can't find what was drawn out of the world." He walked ahead of us with his shoulders hunched, and I realized he wasn't looking at the treasure. He was checking the street names against something in his memory, and flinching at each one. "I drew this out. Every lane. My lines are why nobody ever came back."

I touched his arm. He didn't shrug it off. That was new, and neither of us remarked on it.

The banners were worse than the silver.

They hung from the guildhalls along the processional way — dozens of them, silk gone stiff and grey, but the colors still ghosting through the ash: green and white, a tree with roots and branches both aflame. The same device was cut into the cobbles, stamped into the shutters, stitched into a child's coat lying folded on a step. And beneath every banner, in the doorways, in the gutters, in the shadow of the fountain —

Bones.

Not scattered. Not gnawed or dragged. Laid out. Whole families side by side on the floors of their own homes, hands folded, faces turned toward the doors, as if they had lain down together on the last day and simply waited for the ash. Some of the skulls wore crowns of woven flowers, long since turned to brown lace. Near the guildhall steps, an entire row of them — old men, by the size — faced the mountain's heart with their arms crossed over their chests like soldiers at rest.

"They didn't run," I said. My voice came out strange, flat, too loud in the silence. "When the Veil closed. When the king refused to kneel and Veyland's armies came. They just... lay down."

"The Veil was never only a shield," Orsen said quietly. "It was a promise. *What is hidden cannot be conquered.* They believed it would hold long enough for a return. They dressed for it." He stopped at the guildhall steps and looked up at the burnt-tree banner hanging there, and for a moment he looked older than the mountain. "I've imagined this city for twenty years. Every night. I told myself I was remembering it to punish myself. But I think part of me hoped it would be rubble. Rubble I could bear. This—" his hand made a small helpless gesture at the open doors, the folded hands, the child's horse — "this is a house with everyone still home."

Dain had stopped walking. He stood in the middle of the processional way with his arms wrapped around himself, and when I looked back at him his face was the color of the ash.

"I sold your name," he said, not to me — to the street, to the bones, to the air. "For a pardon. And these people sold *everything*, and got less than nothing, and they're still here, still waiting, and I—" He bent double and was sick against a cobbstone, and no one said anything, because there was nothing to say that the city hadn't already said better.

We walked on.

**Orsen**

I had drawn this city four hundred times.

In the border-town taverns, on the backs of debt notices, in the margins of other men's maps where no one would look closely — always the same streets, always from memory, always wrong in some small way I couldn't identify. I told myself for two decades that the errors were decay, that memory was a leaky vessel. Walking the processional way now, I understood the truth: I had never misremembered Thornfell. I had *refused* to remember it correctly, because correct memory meant the fountain, and the fountain meant the square beyond it, and the square meant the palace, and the palace meant the king whose refusal I had helped erase.

The errors had been mercy. Self-administered, insufficient, and now spent.

The heat guided us. That was the terrible elegance of it — Serevanth's fading fire radiated up through the caldera floor itself, and where it ran strongest, the ash lay thinnest, so the dragon's own dying body marked the road to him. We followed the warm cobbles east through districts I had once labeled in a clerk's hand: *Foundry Quarter. Chandlers' Row. The King's Lea.* Each name struck me like a small calibrated blow. I had written those labels. I had been paid by the crown, in good Veylander silver, to write those labels on maps of a country that officially did not exist, and I had taken the money, and I had told myself a cartographer draws what is commanded because the alternative is a shallow grave beside the survey road.

The alternative had always been a lie. There had been a third option, the one I never took: refuse, flee, vanish, and let another hand draw the lines. It would have changed nothing except my soul. But souls are not nothing. I had learned that too late, and the learning had cost Rosalind her master, and might yet cost her everything else.

She walked ahead of me with the pack straps creaking, and I watched the way she moved through the dead city — not reverent, exactly. Careful. She stepped over the threshold-bones without looking down, but her eyes tracked every banner, every carved flame, every scrap of embroidery, cataloguing them the way she used to catalog guard rotations on a smuggling route. She was reading the city. And the city, I thought, was reading her back.

At the Chandlers' Row well, she stopped.

"Orsen." Her voice was careful too. "This house. Look at the door."

The lintel was carved with the burning tree, and beneath it, smaller, worn nearly smooth: a spiral. The same spiral was branded into the brass key her master had died pressing into her hand. The same spiral, if I was honest with myself, curled through the marginalia of his grimoire on nearly every page I'd glimpsed.

"Your master was born here," I said. It wasn't a question. I don't know why I said it gently; gentleness had never been our currency.

"He never said." She touched the spiral with one finger, and where her finger passed, the ash on the wood slid away as if the grain remembered being clean. "He taught me hedge-charms and called them kitchen magic. He taught me the roads and called them shortcuts. He raised me next door to a kingdom he swore didn't exist anymore, and he never once—" Her jaw tightened. "Never once told me I was standing in its doorway."

"Because telling you would have made you a target," I said. "The same reason I never told anyone what I'd drawn. We were both cowards, Rosalind. His cowardice just happened to look like love."

She looked at me then, and I braced for the anger I deserved, but what crossed her face was worse — recognition. She nodded once, slowly, and turned back to the door, and I understood that I had just handed her a piece of herself and she was deciding whether to keep it or throw it back at me.

The heat pulsed under our feet. Stronger now. Closer.

* * *

**Rosalind**

The palace sat at the caldera's heart like a ship at the bottom of a grey sea.

Its towers rose through the ash-fog, black stone shot through with veins of something that glowed faintly, dully, the way coals glow an hour after the fire dies — and I understood with a lurch that the veins weren't decoration. The whole palace was built around the dragon's fire the way a lantern is built around a candle. Serevanth slept — or whatever dragons do instead of sleeping — beneath it, and the building above him was his lamp-glass.

The great gates stood open. Of course they did. Everything in this city stood open, waiting.

Inside, the throne room's roof had partially fallen, letting a column of grey daylight down onto the dais, and the banners here were grander — silk thick as sailcloth, the burning tree worked in thread that still held a ghost of gold. Bones on the dais steps, arranged with terrible care: a king's household, lying in state for twenty years for a coronation that never came. On the throne itself, no skeleton. Just a folded cloak, green and white, and atop it a crown of black iron set with uncut stone.

"He left it," Orsen breathed. "The last king took the Veil with him into death, but he left the crown for whoever came after."

"Maybe he left it for no one," I said. "Maybe leaving things behind is just what dying people do."

But I didn't believe it, and neither did he, and we both stood there a moment too long looking at the iron crown like it was a lit fuse.

Then the floor shuddered.

Not an earthquake. A breath. The whole caldera inhaled — the ash-fog swirled inward toward the throne, toward the dais, toward a seam in the far wall I hadn't noticed until the light behind it flickered — and the heat under our feet surged so hard I staggered, and from somewhere deep beneath the palace came a sound I will hear until I die.

A heartbeat. Slow, vast, arrhythmic. The sound of a fire trying to remember how to burn.

"That's him," Orsen whispered. "That's the Guttering. We're standing on top of it."

The seam in the wall split — not broken, *opened*, stone folding aside like a curtain drawn by a tired hand — and stairs descended into a red glow that pulsed with each slow beat. Warm air rolled up past us, smelling of sulfur and, impossibly, of woodsmoke and bread, of hearths, of a thousand fires that had once been lit in this city by living hands.

Dain hung back at the top of the stairs. I didn't ask him to come. Whatever he was now — traitor, penitent, both — the thing below wasn't his to face.

Orsen and I went down together.

The stair wound deep, the walls veined with the same dull coal-light, and with every turn the heartbeat grew louder until it wasn't a sound anymore but a presence, a pressure in the chest, and then the stair opened out and there he was.

Serevanth.

I had imagined dragons from smugglers' tales: vast, winged, coiled gold. The reality was worse and stranger. He lay in a cavern of black glass, and he was enormous — his skull alone was the size of a cottage — but he was *thin*, the great wings folded tight against ribs that showed through the hide like barrel staves, and his scales had gone the grey of banked ash. Only along his throat and breast ran the last veins of fire, pulsing faintly with each heartbeat, dimming a little more between each beat, like a lamp running out of oil.

His eye opened.

It found us across the cavern without effort, ancient and amber and unbearably tired, and when he spoke, his voice was the caldera breathing.

"*Two keystones. A cartographer's shame. And a hedge-witch who reads doors.*" The amber eye shifted to me, and I felt seen in a way no witch-hunter had ever managed — seen all the way down, past the smuggler and the apprentice, to whatever my master had been building all along. "*You are late. But you are here. Come closer, keeper of keys, and I will tell you what your kingdom asks of you — and then you will wish, very much, that you had never learned to read.*"

Behind us, far above, faint through the stone, came a sound I almost missed: boots. Many boots, descending the outer stair.

Orsen heard it too. Our eyes met in the coal-light, and I saw my own dread reflected there.

The hunters had found the mountain.
