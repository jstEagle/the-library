## 15. The Last Dragon

The heat found them before the light did.

Rosalind felt it through her boots first — a faint warmth rising out of the ash-covered street like the memory of a hearth — and then she saw it: a glow beneath the palace doors, gold shot through with grey, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart. The doors themselves were bronze, taller than three men, worked over with dragons in relief so worn by centuries of ashfall that they seemed to be swimming up out of metal rather than carved into it.

She pressed her palm flat against the bronze. Warm. Not fire-warm. *Alive*-warm.

"Orsen," she said quietly.

He came to stand beside her, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Above them the Veil flickered — she could see it now even without looking up, feel it in her teeth, a stutter in the world like a lamp running dry. Every few breaths the ruined street around them went thin at the edges, translucent, and she glimpsed through it the other place: the same street under Veyland's sky, choked with pine and silence, no palace at all, just hills. Then it would solidify again, and the dead city would breathe back into being around them.

"How long has he been down there?" she asked.

"Longer than me," Orsen said. "Longer than the Unmapping. He was old when Thornfell fell." He set his hand on the door beside hers. "I drew the borders that buried this city, Rosalind. Whatever's behind that door has every reason to hate what I am."

"You keep saying that like it's supposed to stop me."

"It stopped *me* for twenty years."

She looked at him. His face was grey in the glow, and older than his years, and honest in a way that still surprised her — honesty had come late to Orsen, bought at the price of everything else. She put her shoulder to the door.

It opened without resistance. It had been waiting.

* * *

The throne hall of Old Thornfell was vast enough to hold weather. Ash lay across the floor in drifts, soft as snow, and their footsteps made no sound in it. Banners hung from rafters lost in darkness above, their colors gone to shadow but their shapes intact — stags, rivers, a crowned mountain. Along both walls stood rows of pillars carved with names, thousands of names, and Rosalind understood without being told that these were not kings. They were everyone. A country that wrote all its people into its walls.

And at the far end, coiled around a broken throne like a mountain that had decided to sit down, was the dragon.

He was dying. There was no gentler way to see it. Serevanth's scales had been silver once — she could tell from the few near his heart that still caught light — but most had gone the color of old lead, dull and pitted, and between them his skin showed dry and cracked as drought-earth. His wings were folded tight against his flanks, and where membrane should have shimmered it had gone brittle, veined with dark lines like a leaf in autumn. His head rested on the dais steps, eyes closed, and his breathing was so slow and so deep that the whole hall moved with it — ash drifting up on the inhale, settling on the exhale, a tide of dust keeping time with the last heartbeat of a kingdom.

But it was the fire that broke her. Because there wasn't any. Where a dragon's throat should have burned, there was only a faint ember-glow, deep down, like a coal buried under too much ash — and around him, arranged in a wide circle on the floor, were two shallow basins of black stone, each cradling a keystone.

Her keystones. The ones from the Deepwood shrine and the salt cathedral, which had been cold and heavy in her pack since Graywater Pass, since Orsen was taken, since Dain—

They were here. Sitting in their basins. Glowing faintly, each one, a thread of pale light running from stone to stone to dragon, and Rosalind's hand flew to her pack.

Empty. Of course empty. She laughed, or something close to it, and the sound skittered off into the dark of the hall.

"He took them," Orsen murmured. "Or they came home on their own. With the Veil this thin, I don't think the difference matters anymore."

The dragon's eye opened.

It was the size of a shield, and it did not gleam the way stories promised. It was clouded, half-lidded, the pupil a vertical slit gone soft at the edges — an old eye, a tired eye, an eye that had watched a country die and then been made to hold the corpse warm for a hundred years. It found Rosalind across forty yards of ash, and it looked at her the way her master used to look at her when she'd gotten a charm right on the first try: recognition, and grief, and something underneath both that she couldn't name yet.

"*Halloran,*" the dragon said.

His voice didn't boom. That was the worst part. It was a dry whisper, vast but frayed, like wind through a burnt forest, and the hall carried it gently, the way you carry something you're afraid will break.

"My master's name," Rosalind said. Her voice sounded very small.

"He was born four streets from where you stand." The great head shifted; ash cascaded from his jaw. "Aldous Halloran. He crossed out of the Veil as a boy, before the last of the gates closed. One of the Unmapped. He spent his life walking back toward me, and when his legs would no longer make the journey, he sent the thing he loved best instead."

Rosalind's throat tightened. "He never told me. Any of it. Twenty years and he never once—"

"No. He wouldn't." Something moved in the clouded eye that might have been amusement. "He was Thornfell-born. We do not hand our children burdens. We wait until they are strong enough to choose them." The slit pupil narrowed slightly. "You have the look of him when he was young. Stubborn. Frightened. Doing both at once."

"Where is he?" she asked, though she already knew. "Is he—"

"Ash returns to ash, little hedge-witch. But his name is in those pillars, if you care to find it. They all are." The dragon exhaled, a long grey sigh, and the ember in his throat guttered visibly. Rosalind flinched. "Forgive me. Time is short, and shorter than it was yesterday. Come closer. Both of you. If we are to speak of endings, you should hear them clearly."

* * *

They crossed the hall side by side, two small figures wading through ash, and climbed the dais until they stood close enough to touch him. Up close the scale of him was almost impossible — his foreleg alone was bigger than her master's cottage had been — and up close she could smell him: hot stone and old smoke and, beneath it, something sweet and fading, like the last coals of a bonfire at dawn.

"I am sustained by two things," Serevanth said. "The keystones, which hold the shape of the Veil. And my fire, which holds its life. The stones I can manage. The fire is leaving me." He lifted his head, slowly, joints cracking like felled trees, and turned so that both of them stood in the light of his throat. "Look. Look honestly. You have honest eyes, cartographer. Use them."

Orsen looked. So did Rosalind. And there, deep in the dragon's throat, was the ember — no bigger than a fist now, she realized, no bigger than a human heart — guttering, dimming, the light coming and going like breath in a fever.

"When it goes out," the dragon said, "the Veil does not fade politely. It tears. Everything held inside it releases at once — the city, the dead, the stored weight of a century of erasure — and the land for fifty miles around remembers all of it simultaneously. Your Veyland borderlands will not survive that remembering. Neither will the people in them."

"The Guttering," Orsen said hoarsely. "We saw it in the mountains. Valleys blinking—"

"Blinking is the mercy. Blinking is the warning." The dragon settled his head back onto the steps, and the effort of even that much showed in the tremor that ran through him. "There is one way to renew the fire. One way only, and it cannot be cheated, stolen, or forced. The fire must be fed a willing life. Freely given. A soul that walks into the flame knowing exactly what it surrenders, and gives it anyway."

The words landed in the silent hall like stones into still water.

Rosalind heard herself ask the question from very far away. "Whose life?"

"Any life freely given burns bright enough. But a gifted life — a life bound to the Veil by blood and craft — burns longest." The clouded eye fixed on her. "Mine is nearly spent. I cannot feed myself. The keystones cannot love. It must be a person. And of all persons living, the Veil answers most readily to—" He paused, and there was something almost gentle in the vast ruined voice. "You know who. You have known since the salt cathedral. Since the elder told you the price and you carried it here anyway, in your pack, next to your bread."

She had known. She had known since Elder Maren had looked at her across the salt-dark and said *a life freely given*, and Rosalind had nodded and kept walking, telling herself there would be another answer further down the road. There wasn't. This was the end of the road. This was the answer.

Beside her, Orsen had gone white. "No," he said. "No. There has to be another—"

"There is not," Serevanth said simply. "I did not make this law. I have merely carried it longer than anyone should carry anything."

"Then I'll do it," Orsen said. "My life. My debt. I drew the borders that killed this country — it should be my—"

"It should be nothing of the kind." The dragon's voice hardened, and for one moment the ember in his throat flared, throwing their shadows huge up the pillar-walls. "Guilt is not willingness, cartographer. A life offered to pay a debt is a purchase, not a gift, and the fire knows the difference. It would choke on you. Besides—" the hardness softened again, wearily — "you are needed alive. Someone must draw what comes next. Whoever she chooses to be."

Rosalind barely heard him. She was looking at the ember. A fist-sized coal, guttering, holding up the sky over two countries, and the only food it would take was a person who chose it.

Nobody should have to decide this alone. That was the thought that rose in her, clear and cold, cutting through the roar in her ears. Nobody should ever have to stand in front of a dying god and weigh their own heartbeat against ten thousand strangers'. Her master had known that — had raised her deliberately, taught her deliberately, walked her down this road step by step so that she would arrive here *prepared*, and even he hadn't been able to make the choice for her, because there was no making it for someone. That was the whole horror of it. That was the whole point.

"Rosalind." Orsen's hand was on her arm. "We don't have to decide tonight. We can think. There's time—"

"There isn't," the dragon said, "and lying to her helps no one. The ember fails within days. Perhaps hours, if the wind turns."

She pulled her arm free and walked to the edge of the dais and stood looking out over the hall — the pillars of names, the banners, the ashdrifts like unmarked graves — and she thought about Veyland. Not the crown, not the witch-hunters, not Ilse Vane and her righteous hunters. The people. Farmers at the ford. The border towns full of nobody-special, the ones who'd never signed any proclamation, who'd go on burning hedge-cottages because they were frightened and told to. If the Veil tore, they died screaming in a remembering. If it lived, it lived on her.

Or on someone. That was the thing she kept circling. *On someone.* Why her? Because she was strongest? Because she was convenient? Because a dead man had aimed his daughter at this moment like an arrow twenty years in the drawing?

"I'm not going to decide standing here," she said finally, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Not tonight, not in five minutes, not because a dragon asked nicely. If I give my life, I give it awake. On my own terms. When I've thought it through with my own mind and not just my fear."

Serevanth regarded her for a long moment. Then, impossibly, the great head dipped — a nod, or the ghost of one.

"Good," he whispered. "That is the correct answer. A rushed gift is worth nothing, and I would rather die honestly than be saved by panic." The ember pulsed, dimmed, pulsed again. "Rest here tonight. The hall is safe — the Veil still holds, barely. Eat. Sleep if you can. And tomorrow, when your hunters arrive—"

Rosalind turned. "What?"

The clouded eye slid toward the great bronze doors, and something ancient and tired moved through the dragon's voice.

"They are coming, little hedge-witch. I can feel their hearts through the Veil — a captain with a hidden flame, and a prisoner who walks willingly beside her chains. They will be here by tomorrow's dusk." The slit pupil narrowed. "So whatever you decide, decide it soon. Because the choice you carry is about to become everyone's problem — and I am far too old, and far too empty, to fight for it."

Above them, through the broken roof, the sky flickered.

Once. Twice.

And did not come back all the way.
