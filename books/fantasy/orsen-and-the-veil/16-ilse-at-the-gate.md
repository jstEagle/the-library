## 16. Ilse at the Gate

The mountain had been breathing wrong for three days.

Ilse Vane felt it through her boots before she saw anything — a tremor in the stone, faint and arrhythmic, like a heart stumbling in its sleep. Her horse felt it too. It kept shying left, toward the caldera's rim, then flinching back as if the ground had bitten it.

"Captain," Lieutenant Corvus said, riding up beside her. He was a lean man with a voice like dry paper and the particular patience of someone who enjoyed other people's suffering at a distance. "Scouts report the pass ahead is clear. No sign of the fugitives."

"They'll be here." She didn't look at him. She'd stopped looking at him somewhere past Graywater Pass, after Orsen had been dragged from his hiding place behind a fallen survey marker, after she'd seen the old cartographer's eyes go to *her* — not to the hunters, not to the crown's colors, but to her hands, as if he already knew what lived under her skin.

She'd told herself it was nothing. A guilty man seeing guilt everywhere.

Three days of wrong-breathing mountains had made that harder to believe.

*You are a hunter of the crown,* she recited inwardly, the way she'd recited it every morning for eleven years. *You hunt what the crown fears. You serve so you will never be hunted.* The catechism held her together the way a corset holds a body: tightly, painfully, and only as long as no one looks too closely at what's underneath.

Behind her, thirty hunters rode in good order. Ahead, the road climbed through ash-grey scree toward a gate that should not exist, because the country beyond it did not exist either. Somewhere between those two facts, Ilse Vane had been living a lie so long it had grown roots.

They crested the final ridge at dusk.

And the world opened.

* * *

There was no gate. That was the first thing. There was only a gap in the mountainside where the rock simply stopped being rock — a seam of air that shimmered like heat off a forge, and beyond it, impossibly, a city.

Not ruins. Not bones. A city.

Towers of pale stone rose out of the caldera floor, their spires unbroken, their banners still hanging from the walls — faded to ghosts of blue and silver, but hanging, moving faintly in a wind that shouldn't reach this deep into a dead volcano. Streets ran in clean lines between rooftops. A great palace crowned the far side, its windows catching the last red light like a hundred open eyes.

Under all of it lay ash, thick as snowfall, softening every edge. Ash on the roofs. Ash in the streets. Ash drifted against doorways as if the whole city had gone to sleep mid-winter and never woken.

No one moved in it. Nothing moved in it. And yet Ilse could not shake the feeling that the city was not dead but *holding its breath* — that somewhere beneath the ash, a heart was beating very slowly, very faintly, and had been for twenty years.

"Saints above," someone whispered behind her.

"Silence in the ranks," Corvus snapped, but his own voice had gone thin.

Ilse dismounted. Her legs felt strange, as if she were stepping off a ship onto land that might still be moving. She walked to the edge of the shimmering seam and stopped an arm's length from it. This close, she could feel it — a pressure against her skin, gentle and enormous, like standing at the edge of deep water.

Her gift stirred.

It always stirred near strong workings — a warmth in her palms, a taste of copper at the back of her throat. She had spent eleven years learning to smother it, to keep her hands still when other hunters' charms prickled, to stand in circles of detection-spells and think about ledgers, about grain prices, about anything dull enough to keep her blood flat. She had gotten so good at it that sometimes she forgot it was there at all.

Here, it woke like a dog hearing its name.

The Veil pressed against her, and her gift pressed back, and for one vertiginous moment Ilse understood — truly understood, in her body rather than her mind — what Rosalind Halloran must have felt her whole life. What it cost to hold something like this inside you while the world sharpened knives.

"Captain?" Corvus had come up beside her. "Orders?"

She made herself look away from the city. Made her voice come out flat and official. "We go in. Halloran will have reached the dragon or be close to it. We take the keystones, we take her, and we take the cartographer back for trial."

"And if she resists?"

"She won't." Ilse started forward. "She's one girl with hedge-charms."

But even as she said it, she remembered the flooded ford at Graywater — no, that had been the river crossing weeks ago; she remembered the reports, rather, read by lamplight, of a river rising against armed men at a girl's word. One girl with hedge-charms who had drowned a ford and vanished with two keystones and a disgraced royal cartographer.

One girl who was walking into the heart of a kingdom the crown had murdered, carrying the keys to its resurrection.

Ilse stepped through the seam.

The sensation was like passing through a curtain of warm water. For half a heartbeat she was nowhere — no mountain, no crown, no name — and then she stood on Thornfell soil, and the ash came up soft around her boots, and the banners overhead shifted in their long slow wind.

Her gift sang.

* * *

They moved down the great avenue in formation, boots crunching ash, and Ilse let the silence do its work on her.

Every window they passed was dark, but the glass was intact. Every door stood closed, but the ironwork on it was beautiful — wrought into vines and birds, into letters in a script Ilse didn't know and yet somehow almost recognized, the way you almost recognize a face from a dream. A child's wooden hoop leaned against a doorstep, waiting for a child twenty years gone. In a baker's window, loaves had turned to stone under the ash, still in their rows, still shaped by hands that had expected tomorrow.

This was not a battlefield. Battlefields were torn. This was a held breath. This was a city that had died so completely and so gently that it hadn't even had time to fall down.

*The Unmapping.* Orsen had said the word once, in the wagon, before they'd bound his wrists — not to her, exactly, more to the dark, the way a man says a thing he's carried too long. *You can't kill a kingdom, Captain. So we made it never have existed. I drew the borders. I drew them well.*

He had drawn them well. And here was what well-drawn borders looked like from the inside: a whole nation turned into a tomb so tidy that even grief couldn't find purchase in it.

"Halloran's trail leads toward the palace," a scout reported, jogging back along the avenue. "Fresh prints in the ash. Two sets. And—" he hesitated, "—there's light, Captain. Deep in. Under the palace. Like firelight, but wrong-colored."

Wrong-colored. Ilse thought of the sky-flicker reports from the northern valleys, the ones the crown had classified and buried: whole landscapes blinking out of existence for a heartbeat at a time. The Veil, failing. The last dragon, guttering.

The crown's orders sat in her breast pocket, sealed with wax and ribbon. *Recover the keystones. Eliminate the bearer if necessary. The Veil must fall entire.* She had read them a dozen times and each time the words had seemed reasonable, necessary, the kind of hard arithmetic rulers must do. *A dead kingdom cannot be allowed to rise. The borderlands would burn.*

Standing now in the ash of those borderlands — because that's what this was, wasn't it? Veyland's maps had simply drawn a line across a living place and called the living place nothing — the arithmetic curdled in her stomach.

"Captain." Corvus again, falling into step beside her. His voice had dropped to something private. "A word."

She slowed. Let the column draw ahead.

"The men are rattled," he said quietly. "This place... it gets in the head. Best we move fast, take the witch, burn what needs burning, and get out. And Captain—" his eyes flicked to her hands, casual, deliberate, "—you've been flexing your fingers since the ridge. Nervous habit?"

The bottom dropped out of her stomach.

He knew. He had known for how long? Weeks? Since Graywater? Had he seen it in the way she'd handled Orsen, in the way she'd ridden ahead of the detection-charms instead of behind them? Was that why he'd volunteered to lead the vanguard, why he'd kept her close, why he watched her now with that dry-paper patience?

"I get stiff in the cold," she said.

"Mm." He smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Of course. Cold. Well. The crown pays me to notice things, Captain, same as it pays you. We'll speak again after the witch is taken. I'd hate for anything to... complicate your command."

He touched two fingers to his brow in a parody of a salute and walked back up the column, leaving Ilse standing in the ash with her heart hammering and her gift roaring in her palms like a caged animal demanding to be let out.

*He'll report me. After. Whatever happens with Halloran, he'll write it down, seal it, send it south, and I'll die in a cellar under the Bastion with my name struck from the rolls — unmapped, just like this city. Just like everyone the crown decides was never real.*

Unless she delivered Halloran's head first. Unless she gave the crown such a victory that no one ever audited her hands.

That had always been the bargain, hadn't it? That was the whole architecture of her life. Serve the knife so the knife passes over you. Hunt the gifted so no one hunts the gifted girl wearing the captain's coat.

She looked down the avenue, toward the wrong-colored light pulsing under the palace, and realized she was no longer certain which side of the knife she was standing on.

* * *

They found the entrance to the under-halls in the palace's root: a stair descending beneath the throne room, its walls carved with the same vine-and-bird script, its air warm and smelling of hot metal and old smoke. The wrong-colored light welled up from below, pulsing now in a rhythm Ilse's body recognized before her mind did — slow, labored, struggling.

A heartbeat. They were walking down into a heartbeat.

At the bottom of the stair, the corridor opened into a vast cavern, and Ilse stopped so abruptly that the hunter behind her walked into her back.

The dragon filled the far end of the cavern like a mountain range that had decided to lie down. Grey-scaled, vast, ancient, its wings folded tight against its flanks, its head resting low on the stone. Its eyes were open — molten gold, dimming — and before it, lit by its fading inner fire, stood two figures.

Rosalind Halloran. Small, ash-streaked, hollow-eyed, standing with her spine straight as a pikestaff. Beside her, freed of his bonds — someone had cut him loose, or he'd charmed the ropes, or the mountain itself had — stood Orsen, the disgraced cartographer, holding a brass key like a man holding a live coal.

Between them and the dragon, on a plinth of black stone, two keystones glowed with a light like captured dawn.

"There she is," Corvus breathed beside her, and there was naked hunger in his voice. "Captain — the witch, the stones, the traitor. All of it. One stroke."

Ilse didn't answer. She was watching Rosalind Halloran watch *them*, and what she saw in the girl's face undid something in her. Not fear. Not defiance. Recognition. The girl looked at Ilse the way Ilse had once looked at herself in a river, years ago, the night before she took the crown's oath — looked at her and knew.

"You're one of us," Halloran said. Not loudly. The cavern carried it anyway. "You've been hiding it for years. I can see it from here. Your hands are shaking."

"Ranks ready!" Corvus barked, and thirty crossbows came up along the line.

"Corvus." Ilse heard her own voice come out level. Cold. Command-voice. "Hold."

"Captain, the orders—"

"I know the orders." She stepped forward, out of the line, alone, into the space between the crossbows and the girl. The dragon's dim gold eye tracked her. The keystones pulsed. Somewhere above them, the whole mountain shuddered, and fine ash sifted down through the wrong-colored light like grey snow falling indoors.

She looked at the dragon. At the dying fire in it. At the city overhead that had done nothing, ever, except exist where a map said it shouldn't.

Then she looked at her own hands. Steady now. Steadier than they'd been in eleven years.

"Eleven years," she said, and didn't know until the words were out that she meant to say them aloud. "I've worn the coat eleven years. Do you know what the oath costs, Lieutenant? Do you know what I've burned? What I've signed? Every warrant, every pyre, every family I pulled apart — I told myself it bought my survival. That the knife would pass over me if I held it for them." She turned, slowly, to face the line of hunters, to face Corvus and his dry-paper smile. "It doesn't pass over. It never passes over. It just waits until you're useful enough to matter and compromised enough to bury."

"Captain Vane." Corvus's voice had gone carefully pleasant. "You are relieved of command, effective immediately. Hunters — seize the captain."

No one moved.

Thirty hunters stood in the dragon's dying light, crossbows raised, looking between their lieutenant and their captain, and Ilse watched the arithmetic happen behind their eyes — the same arithmetic she'd done her whole life, serve-or-burn — and understood that whatever happened next would happen in the space of a breath.

So she spent the breath first.

Her gift came up through her palms like sunrise. Eleven years of smothering it, and it came out not as a weapon but as *truth* — a wave of raw, undeniable knowing that washed down the line of hunters and showed each of them, in one blinding instant, exactly what they were. Every hidden charm. Every suppressed flicker. Every hunter in that line who had ever quieted a candle with a thought, or warmed a frozen comrade with a touch, or lied awake wondering why the detection-spells never found *them*. The crown's witch-hunters, shown themselves.

Gasps. Dropped crossbows. One young hunter fell to his knees in the ash.

And Corvus — Corvus went for his sword.

Ilse was faster. Eleven years of hiding had made her fast in ways no drill-yard taught. Her blade cleared its sheath and met his mid-draw, knocked it wide, and laid its edge against his throat before his second breath.

"No," she said quietly. "You don't get to make me the monster in this story, Corvus. I've been the monster. I know the part. You'd have sold me the way you'd sell anyone — a sealed letter, a cellar, a name struck from the rolls. But not tonight. Tonight you're going to order your men to stand down, and then you're going to sit in the ash and think about what this city did to deserve us."

"This is treason," he whispered. The blade bit a bead of red at his throat. "The crown will—"

"The crown," Ilse said, "drew a line across a living country and called it nothing. The crown can learn what nothing looks like from the inside." She raised her voice, and it rang off the cavern walls, off the dragon's ancient scales, off twenty years of held breath. "Hunters! Lower your arms. Anyone who wants to walk out of this mountain and report me, walk now, and may you sleep well afterward. Anyone who has ever hidden what they are — stay."

The silence stretched. Ash sifted down. The dragon's gold eye burned low and patient, watching, the way old things watch.

One by one — hesitantly, then in a rush, like a dam going — the hunters lowered their crossbows. Half of them. Maybe more. Some stood uncertain, caught between oaths and truths. A handful at the back turned and fled up the stair, boots hammering, and Ilse let them go. Witnesses. Runners. Fine. The crown would know soon enough what had happened under the mountain.

Let it come.

She released Corvus with a shove. He stumbled back, hand pressed to his throat, eyes wide with a hatred that was mostly fear.

"Bind him," she said. "Gag him. He talks to no one until this is finished."

Halloran was staring at her. So was Orsen. The cartographer's face had gone strange — not grateful, Ilse noticed. Appraising. As if he were re-drawing her, line by line, onto some new map.

"Why?" Halloran asked simply.

Ilse looked past her, at the keystones glowing on their plinth, at the guttering colossus behind them, at the seam of shimmering air far above through which the world outside waited — the world she had spent eleven years protecting from exactly this truth.

"Because I'm tired," she said, "of hunting myself."

She sheathed her sword. Behind her, the dragon exhaled — a long, rattling sigh of smoke and embers, and the wrong-colored light pulsed once, twice, weaker.

And in that pulse, Ilse saw the truth of it plain: whatever these people had come here to decide, the decision was already bleeding out. The fire was nearly gone.

"How long?" she asked Halloran.

The girl's jaw tightened. "Hours. Maybe less."

"Then whatever you're going to do," Ilse said, drawing her blade again — not at the girl, but turning to face the stair, the dark, the crown that would already be marching — "you'd better do it fast. Because I promise you, Captain Halloran: we are not the only ones who found this mountain."

Far above, through the seam in the rock, the first horns of the crown's relief column echoed down off the ridgeline.
