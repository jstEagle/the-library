## 17. The Price Named

The ropes came off his wrists and Orsen's hands screamed.

He had forgotten what blood returning to flesh felt like — a thousand small knives waking at once. He flexed his fingers anyway, because a cartographer's hands were the only part of himself he had never entirely hated, and because Ilse Vane had cut him loose with her own knife and he wanted very badly not to shake in front of her.

"Walk," she said. "Don't run. My lieutenant is dead, my hunters are confused, and I am one bad decision away from being exactly what the crown says I am. Don't make me regret you."

They went down through the ash streets of Old Thornfell together, past houses with their doors still latched against a night that had ended forty years ago, past a fountain where a stone child poured water that no longer fell anywhere. The dragon's heat pulsed under everything like a buried heart. Somewhere ahead, in the palace ruin, Rosalind was waiting with two keystones and a decision that could unmake a country.

Orsen's legs were unsteady from three days in a cell, but his mind had never been clearer. That was the terrible thing about captivity: it gave you time to think, and thinking was the one discipline he had never managed to abandon, even drunk, even disgraced, even now.

He had done the arithmetic in the dark. It did not come out kind.

* * *

Rosalind stood at the edge of the ritual circle the exiles' elders had chalked into the palace floor generations ago, waiting for a generation that never came. The chalk lines were faded but intact, and when she saw Orsen she crossed the distance between them fast — too fast for someone who had spent three days believing him broken.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"Ilse Vane hit me when I wouldn't tell her where you'd gone." He touched his split lip. "Then she apologized. Then she killed her own lieutenant for me. I'm still deciding how I feel about all of it."

"Later." Her hands hovered near his face without quite touching, the way she always did when she wasn't sure she was allowed. "Are you whole?"

"I'm whole."

She exhaled, and for one moment he let himself stand in the warmth of it — the relief on her face, the fact that she had looked for him first before looking at the keystones, before looking at the dying god of fire beneath them. He catalogued it the way he catalogued everything, because he knew what he had come here to do, and he wanted one clean memory of her before he did it.

"Serevanth told you the terms," he said.

Her face closed like a gate. "You've been talking to Ilse Vane."

"She told me what the crown knows. Which is more than you know, because you've been reading a dead man's marginalia while I spent ten years drawing the borders you're about to burn down." He kept his voice level. Cartographer's voice. Surveyor's voice. The voice that had once made kings listen and then made them stop listening forever. "Rosalind. Before you open that circle, I need you to look at a map."

"There's no map. There's a door—"

"There is *always* a map." He pulled a stub of charcoal from his coat — they hadn't even taken that; they hadn't thought a pencil was dangerous — and knelt, right there on the ancient flagstones, and began to draw.

He drew fast, from memory, the way he drew everything: the Veil's boundary as the old surveys had defined it, the mountain spine, the river valleys, and then east of it, pressed up against the line like water against a dam, the borderlands of Veyland. Graywater. The Salt Marches. The windward road Rosalind had grown up smuggling along. Forty years of farms and fords and market towns that existed only because Thornfell had stopped existing.

"When Thornfell was unmapped," he said, "the land didn't vanish. Only the kingdom did. And land abhors a vacuum worse than nature abhors anything else. Veyland moved in. Three hundred thousand people live in the borderlands now, Rosalind. Farmers who were granted those valleys by a crown that stole them from a ghost. Towns built on roads that used to be Thornfell's royal highways. When the Veil falls — or when it's rewritten — every deed, every grant, every border stone becomes a lie overnight."

"So we make new truths. We've been making them the whole way here."

"With ink? With goodwill?" He stood, charcoal-black fingers shaking now, finally shaking. "With a *dragon*? Rosalind, think about what happens the morning after your door opens. Half of Veyland's borderlands wake up inside a resurrected nation they never agreed to join. The other half wake up next door to one. The crown will call it an invasion — a dead kingdom crawling out of its grave to take back stolen land. They'll march. And the people who live on that line — smugglers, farmers, the salt-miners who kept your faith alive for a generation — they'll be the ones burning."

"They're already burning. You watched the proclamation get nailed to my master's gate same as I read it."

"The Purging is cruelty. What you're proposing is *war*. There's a difference, and the difference has a body count, and I have drawn enough casualty maps to know how they fill in." He heard his own voice rising and forced it down. "I'm not asking you to abandon them. I'm asking you to slow down. One night. Talk to Serevanth again. Ask him if there's a version of this that doesn't put an army between two peoples by sunrise."

"He's dying, Orsen. You saw the sky gutter. There is no slowing down. There's tonight or there's nothing."

"Then maybe nothing is the honest answer!"

The word cracked off the ash walls and came back at him, and he saw it land on her like a blow. Good, some cold part of him said. Let it hurt. Truth should hurt. But the rest of him felt sick, because he knew — he had known since the cell, since Ilse's knife — that what he was saying was only half truth, and half truth was the specialty of men like him.

Rosalind picked up the charcoal stub he'd dropped. She looked at his map of the borderlands for a long moment. Then, deliberately, she drew a single line through it.

"That's Dain's road," she said quietly. "The one he sold to the hunters. The one Ilse marched down to capture you. You're standing in the middle of the war you're so afraid of, Orsen. It found us anyway. Doing nothing doesn't keep anyone safe. It just decides who gets to be safe."

"The borderlands aren't Dain's road. They're three hundred thousand people who didn't choose any of this."

"And my master didn't choose to burn in his own cottage. And the miners under the salt cathedral didn't choose to spend their lives guarding a keystone for a country that forgot them. Someone always pays, Orsen. The only question is whether we choose who honestly or let the crown choose for us." She set the charcoal down between them like a blade laid on a table. "You helped draw them out of existence. Ten years ago. You told me yourself — you drew the lines and the lines became true. So tell me why the same hand can't draw something kinder."

And there it was. The thing underneath the argument, the thing he had carried up the mountain in Ilse's custody like a second set of chains.

"Because I don't trust my hand anymore," he said. "That's the truth you want? Fine. I sat in a cell for three days and I did the arithmetic and I couldn't make it come out clean either way, and do you know what I realized? It doesn't matter which way it comes out. Whatever I choose, I'll be choosing it to feel less like the man who erased a kingdom. Not because it's right. Because I need it to be right." His voice failed and he hated it. "You said I want absolution. You're right. I do. And absolution is the most dangerous thing in this room, because everyone in it wants some. You want your master back. The elder wants her exile ended. Serevanth wants his fire. Ilse wants her soul. And none of us is asking the only question that matters, which is: what does the world need, as opposed to what do we need to stop feeling?"

For a moment neither of them spoke. Underfoot, faint but unmistakable, the dragon's heat dimmed — a stutter in the floor's warmth, like a lamp running out of oil. Above, through the broken roof, the stars flickered. Actually flickered, the way candlelight does, though stars have no business doing such a thing.

Rosalind looked up at them, and when she looked back at Orsen her eyes were wet and furious.

"You want to know what the world needs?" she said. "It needs people willing to be wrong for each other. That's all a country is. That's all it ever was. My master taught me that — no. He didn't teach me. He *showed* me, for fifteen years, feeding a stranger's daughter and teaching her letters, and I never asked why, because I thought love without explanation was just how kindness worked. It wasn't. It was a man spending his whole life building a door for a girl he hoped would walk through it." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears. "You built walls, Orsen. Lines on paper that decided who lived on which side of existence. I'm not going to stand here and pretend I don't understand why — you were young and the crown held the pen above your hand. But you don't get to use your guilt as a veto. Your shame doesn't outrank their lives."

"No," he said. "But it might outrank my judgment. That's what I'm telling you. That's the whole of it."

"Then go."

The word came out flat. Final. A border drawn between two people on a floor that had seen kingdoms end.

"If you truly believe your hands can't be trusted," she said, "then leave them out of it. Walk down the mountain. Draw your maps of whatever survives. But don't stand in this circle tonight and don't stand beside me tomorrow, because I cannot spend my last free hours wondering whether the man next to me is helping or hesitating."

"Rosalind—"

"*Go.*"

He went.

* * *

He made it as far as the fountain with the stone child before his legs gave out, and he sat on its rim in the ash-dark with his head in his charcoal-stained hands, and he did not leave.

That was the humiliating truth of it. He had delivered the speech of his life — the careful, cruel, half-honest arithmetic of a man who had learned that maps decide who dies — and she had seen through it in four sentences, and he had walked away, and now he sat twenty yards from the door of the palace like a dog outside a lit window.

Because she was right. That was the unbearable part. Not about the borderlands — the borderlands were real, the famine was real, the war was real, and some cold surveyor's corner of his mind would keep doing that arithmetic until he died. But she was right about him. He had come up that mountain wanting to save three hundred thousand strangers, and somewhere on the switchbacks the reasons had gotten tangled, the way rivers tangle in a delta, until he couldn't tell the altruism from the absolution. He had wanted to stand in front of her and be the man who said no, the prudent man, the man who had learned from his mistakes — instead of the man who made them.

A shadow fell across the fountain.

Ilse Vane stood there with her helmet under her arm and her lieutenant's blood, he noticed, still dark on her cuff. She looked at him sitting in the ruins of his argument, and something almost gentle passed over her scarred face.

"She threw you out," Ilse said.

"I walked."

"Mm." She sat down on the fountain rim beside him, uninvited, the way soldiers sit. For a while she said nothing, and the stars overhead flickered again, longer this time, and both of them watched the sky blink like an eye trying to stay open.

"I spent six years hiding behind the hunt's banner," she said at last. "Telling myself I was protecting gifted children by finding them first. Some nights I even believed it." She turned the helmet in her hands. "You know what I learned? Self-deception doesn't sound like lying. It sounds like prudence. It sounds like *someone has to be realistic here*. I'd bet my commission that's what you sounded like in there."

"It's what I sounded like."

"And?"

Orsen looked up at the guttering stars, at the dead city holding its breath around them, at the faint orange pulse beneath the flagstones that was a dragon's heart deciding whether to stop.

"And I think," he said slowly, "that I've been drawing other people's borders so long I've forgotten I'm allowed to cross one."

He stood. His legs held. Ilse watched him with an expression that was not quite approval — approval required certainty, and neither of them had any — but was perhaps its cousin.

"For what it's worth," she said, rising too, settling her sword-belt, "when you go back in there, she's going to be furious."

"I know."

"And the ritual might kill you all."

"I know."

"And the crown will send more than me, next time. Whole companies. Maybe the witch-finders themselves."

"I know that too." He started toward the palace door, then stopped, and looked back at her. "Why are you telling me this?"

Ilse Vane smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was an honest one, and after the last ten years he found honesty nearly unendurable in its beauty.

"Because in about an hour," she said, "every hunter I couldn't turn is going to come down that pass with fire in their hands, and someone is going to have to stand at the gate and hold them off long enough for a girl and a dragon to finish what they're starting. And I promised myself, when I killed Lieutenant Corvane, that the next thing I chose would be chosen *cleanly*." She drew her sword an inch and let it slide home. "Go make your amends, cartographer. I'll mind the door."

Behind him, deep under the ash and the bones and the forty silent years, the dragon's heartbeat stuttered again — and this time it did not resume.

Above the broken roof, half the stars went out.

Orsen ran.
