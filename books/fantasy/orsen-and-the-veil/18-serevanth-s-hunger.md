## 18. Serevanth's Hunger

The dragon's fire was going out.

Rosalind felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the air of the caldera, like the moment before a candle gutters, when the flame seems to lean away from its own light. She stood in the shattered colonnade of the old palace with the grimoire open against her forearm and watched Serevanth breathe, and each breath came slower than the last, and each breath carried less fire.

Two days since Orsen had walked out of the palace ruins with his jaw set and his eyes anywhere but hers. Two days since she had shouted words at his back that she could not take back and was not sure she wanted to. The city had not cared. Ash drifted through the broken windows of the Hall of Maps where he'd slept — where they'd both slept, once, on bedrolls beside a brazier that no longer mattered — and the banners of Thornfell hung gray and heavy above streets where no one had walked for a generation.

Now it was only her and the dragon and the two keystones, warm as living things against her ribs.

"Tell me again," she said. Her voice sounded small under the vaulted ceiling, where painted kings and queens looked down through centuries of soot. "Tell me exactly what happens if you die."

Serevanth's head lay along the floor of what had been the throne room, his neck stretched across sixty feet of cracked marble, his chin resting on the steps where a king's boots would have stood. His scales had gone the color of old iron. When he exhaled, the plume that rose from his nostrils was thin as smoke from a doused fire — gray, not gold, and cold enough that frost feathered across the stone near his mouth.

*The Veil is my fire,* he said. His voice arrived inside her skull rather than her ears, vast and slow, like something moving under deep water. *My fire is nearly spent. What remains will not fade gently.*

"You said that already."

*I say it because you keep asking as though the answer might change.* One ancient eye opened — amber shot through with threads of black, like a jewel cracking from within. *When I die, the Veil does not slip away like fog at morning. It tears. Everything held behind it for a hundred years comes back all at once, all at the same place, and the land must remember itself violently or not at all.*

"And the people in Veyland."

*The borderlands will be inside the tear.* He closed the eye again. *The villages. Graywater. The shepherd valleys. Perhaps the ford where you flooded the hunters' crossing, little witch. Perhaps the town where your cartographer drinks himself blind. The Unmapping pushed Thornfell out of the world; my death will pull it back, and the world will have to make room, and it will make room by breaking whatever lies in the way.*

Rosalind looked down at the grimoire. Master Ellery's handwriting marched up the margins in tight, patient lines — the same hand that had corrected her charcoal sketches of hedge-charms, that had drawn little stars beside the passages he thought she'd understand someday. *The Veil can be fed,* one note read. *Fire wants fuel. A life freely given burns longest.* And beneath it, in ink so fresh it might have been written yesterday: *I am sorry, Rosalind. I am sorry. I am sorry.*

He had known. All those years teaching her to bind a splint and charm a fever down and read the smugglers' roads like scripture — he had been raising a sacrifice and calling it an apprenticeship.

She had stopped being angry about it somewhere on the mountain road. Anger required a person to be angry at, and Master Ellery was ash in a burned cottage three kingdoms away. What remained was simpler and worse: the arithmetic. One life, freely given, against thousands taken without consent. There was no clever way around it. She had spent the whole night looking for one, turning the grimoire's pages until her fingers were gray with ash, and there was no clever way around it.

There was only the door Orsen had wanted — reweave the Veil into a threshold, draw power from memory instead of death — and she did not know how to do that, and she did not have time to learn, and she was alone.

"I need to write a letter," she said aloud, to no one, because saying it made it real, and making it real was the only courage she had left.

*

She wrote it in the guardhouse just inside the caldera's rim, a squat stone room where the wind couldn't reach and the last of the daylight came through arrow slits in dusty bars. She'd found paper in a captain's desk — actual paper, dry, untouched by a century of rot, because nothing inside the Veil decayed quite the way it should — and a stub of charcoal that had once belonged to some long-dead officer of a kingdom that officially did not exist.

The first line took her an hour.

*Dain —*

She crossed it out. Dain was south somewhere, leading Ilse Vane's hunters astray with a plan that would probably kill him, and whatever else he deserved, he didn't deserve a letter written by a dead woman. That would be cruelty dressed as absolution. Let him live with what he'd done while she lived too.

*Orsen —*

That one she kept. She sat with the charcoal hovering and thought about the night at the survey station, when he had told her about the Unmapping with his hands flat on the table like a man surrendering to something. *I drew the borders,* he'd said. *I was good at it. That's the worst part. They gave me a medal.* And she had hated him a little, and trusted him anyway, because hating someone and trusting them are not opposites; sometimes they're the same rope.

*Orsen —*

*If you're reading this, then either you came back or someone brought you this letter, and either way I suppose you'll want to argue with me, so let me save us both the trouble.*

*You were right that restoring Thornfell the old way would break Veyland. You were right that I want to save everyone and that wanting to save everyone is how countries get people killed. You were right about almost everything except the part where you walked away instead of staying to be right next to me.*

*Here is what I know that you don't. My master spent twenty years preparing me for this and never told me, and I could spend this letter being furious about that, but the truth is I'm not. He taught me every road, every charm, every way of seeing the world that wasn't on any map. He taught me that a wall is just a door that's forgotten what it's for. If there's another way to feed the fire — a way that isn't one life traded for thousands — I think it has to come from what he taught me. I think it has to come from the key.*

*If I fail, the Veil tears and the borderlands burn, and none of this letter will matter, so I've decided not to fail. That's not bravery. I've learned the difference these past weeks. Bravery is being afraid and doing it anyway. This is just arithmetic with better handwriting.*

*You owe Thornfell a map. Draw me one worth the debt.*

*Rosalind*

She read it twice. Then she folded it, and unfolded it, and added one more line at the bottom in smaller letters, the kind of thing you only write when you believe no one will ever see it:

*P.S. — I forgive you. Not fully. But enough.*

Then she sealed it with a smear of pine pitch from the torch bracket, because she had no wax and no seal and no right to ceremony, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where the keystones pressed against it from the other side.

*

Night came down over the caldera like a lid.

Rosalind climbed the great stair to the throne room, carrying a lantern she didn't need — the keystone in her pocket gave off a faint warmth that had begun, in the last day, to glow. Both of them did now. She'd taken them out and laid them side by side on the marble, and they pulsed in slow alternation, like two hearts learning to beat together, and between their pulses she could feel the shape of the Veil stretched overhead like a second sky.

Serevanth was worse.

The frost had spread from his mouth down his neck in ferns of white, and where it touched his scales they had gone dull as river stones. His breathing had slowed to one long draw and release every half-minute, and in the spaces between breaths the whole city seemed to hold still, waiting.

*Little witch.* The voice was thinner now. She could feel the effort of it, like watching someone lift a weight that had once been nothing. *You have decided.*

"Yes."

*To give yourself.*

"To try something first." She knelt beside his jaw, close enough to feel the cold coming off him, which was wrong, all wrong, a dragon should radiate heat like a forge, and the wrongness of it made her throat tight. "Master Ellery's notes say the fire wants a life freely given. But he also wrote — here, look —" she held the grimoire up to the keystone-light, "— that the Veil was never meant to be a wall. That was Thornfell's mistake. They built it in fear, dying, and fear makes walls. He thought a Veil built in hope might be built differently. Might be *fed* differently."

*And if you are wrong?*

"Then I'm standing next to a dying dragon when the sky tears open, and honestly, Ser, I won't know the difference for very long."

Something moved through the vast dark of him that might have been laughter. It hurt to watch, like watching a mountain try to smile.

*Your cartographer argued for letting me die,* Serevanth said. *Cleanly. Quickly. He called it mercy.*

"He argues for whatever hurts him least."

*And you?*

Rosalind looked at her hands. They were steady. She'd expected them to shake and they didn't, and somehow that was the most frightening thing that had happened all day. "I argue for the door," she said. "Even if I have to build it out of myself."

The dragon was quiet for a long time. Outside, wind moved through the dead streets, stirring ash off the rooftops of a capital that had been erased so thoroughly that even its conquerors' grandchildren didn't know its name. Somewhere beyond the caldera rim, beyond the flickering false sky, Veyland went on sleeping — shepherds, farmers, children, the whole unnumbered living weight of a country that had never asked to be saved or damned by anyone's arithmetic.

*There is a thing I have not told you,* Serevanth said at last. *Because telling it changes nothing, and because I am old, and old creatures hoard even their truths.*

"Tell it anyway."

*The keystones are not keys.* His eye opened again, and this time the black threads in the amber had widened, and behind them she saw something that made her breath catch: a reflection of the throne room, but alive — banners bright, fires burning, a court in session, a kingdom mid-breath. *They are seeds. Your master knew. The exiles beneath the salt knew. The Veil was grown, not built, and what is grown can be regrown — but a seed needs soil, and water, and the gardener must put her hands in the ground.*

"What does that mean?" Her voice cracked on the second word. "Ser. What does that *mean*?"

*It means the choice may not be what either of us believed.* The amber eye dimmed, guttered, steadied. *It means that when the moment comes — and it is coming soon, child, I can feel the last of my fire counting itself like coins — you must not ask what you are willing to lose. Ask what you are willing to plant.*

The lantern flame bent sideways though there was no wind.

And then, faint and far, from the direction of the caldera gate — the sound Rosalind had been listening for since sunset and dreading since dawn: boot heels on old stone. Many of them. Moving fast.

Someone was coming, and they weren't being quiet about it.
