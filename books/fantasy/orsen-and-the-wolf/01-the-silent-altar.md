## 1. The Silent Altar

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the mountain smelled of wet stone and pine rot, which Wendell Zhou took as a good omen. In Thornfell, you learned to read omens in small things—the way mist pooled in a hollow, the absence of crows, the particular silence of a pass that meant no one had walked it recently. Silence was currency. Silence was survival. Thirteen years of it had made Wendell rich in ways no magistrate could tax.

They moved along the old drover's road with the salt mule's lead rope looped twice around one fist, boots finding the quiet places between loose stones. The mule—Bramble, ill-tempered and indispensable—plodded behind with the resigned air of an animal that had made this crossing more times than its owner cared to count. Forty pounds of Greyhollow salt rode in oilcloth sacks beneath the panniers, disguised as dried fish. Salt was cheap in the lowlands and worth its weight in silver above the snowline, where the church's tithe-collectors had priced it beyond the reach of shepherds who needed it to keep their flocks alive.

Wendell did not think of themselves as a criminal. They thought of themselves as a gap in the world—a person shaped precisely like the space between rules, narrow enough to slip through anything. It was a comfortable way to live, if you didn't look at it too closely. Most nights they didn't.

The road bent north around the shoulder of Mount Ceren, and Wendell felt the change before they saw it: a thinning in the air, the way a room feels different after someone has died in it. Bramble slowed. Ears back.

"No," Wendell said quietly, to the mule, to the mountain, to whatever was listening. "Not this morning."

But the road gave them no choice. The drover's path had washed out below in the spring floods, and the only crossing left was the high track—and the high track passed the altar.

It came into view as the light did, grey on grey: a ring of standing stones on a shelf of rock above the treeline, seven of them once, five still upright, all furred with moss so thick and green it looked like the mountain itself had grown fur over the wound. The altar stone lay in the center, split down the middle by frost or by time or by something less patient than either. Lichen climbed its flanks in pale continents. A rowan tree had rooted in the crack and grown sideways, stubborn and crooked, its berries gone over to rust.

Thirteen years ago, Wendell had knelt on that stone with blood on their palms and sworn an oath that was supposed to mean something.

*Orsen of the Mountain, Keeper of the High Passes, Hearer of the Small Voices—I am yours, and my road is your road, and what I carry, I carry for you.*

They had been twenty-two years old and full of a mercenary's simple faith: that gods kept their bargains, that oaths were load-bearing walls, that the company's patron would protect them because protection was what patrons were *for*. The whole company had sworn that day—all forty-one of them, kneeling in a ring while the wind came off the glacier and the old priest with the burned face spoke words in a language older than the church's calendar. Wendell could still feel the oath-marks if they pressed their fingertips to their inner wrists: two faint lines, cool as riverbed stones, that had not warmed or shifted since the night Orsen fell silent.

Because that was the thing about the mountain god. One winter night thirteen years ago, every prayer in Thornfell had simply stopped being answered. No thunder, no sign, no explanation. The shrines went cold. The priests of the old rites grew old and then died without successors. And the new gods' church swept up from the lowlands like water filling a dry channel, building chapels where cairns had stood, renaming passes, posting tithes, teaching the mountain people that the voices they'd heard all their lives had been demons all along.

The other sworn companies had scattered or converted or died. Wendell had done none of those things. Wendell had done something worse, or better, depending on how you counted: they had simply *stopped speaking*. No prayers. No curses in the god's name. Not even thanks. If you never called out, nothing could answer, and if nothing answered, nothing could mark you, and if nothing marked you, the church's inquisitors—who had burned three oath-keepers in Greyhollow's market square in the first year alone—would have no reason to learn your name.

Invisible. That was the whole art of survival, distilled. Don't be seen, don't be heard, don't be *known*. Carry your salt, pay your bribes, keep your head down, and let the dead god sleep untroubled in whatever dark place gods go when they stop answering.

Wendell would have walked past the altar now, same as they had four times a year for thirteen years, eyes forward, breath even, heart locked down tight as a strongbox.

But the prayer slipped out anyway.

Later—they would think about this later, for years, turning it over like a coin worn smooth—they would try to find the moment it began. Whether it was the smell of the rowan berries, or the light coming up gold behind Ceren's eastern ridge, or the sight of the split altar stone wearing its green shroud like something buried alive. Whether some cracked bell in their chest had finally rung on its own, thirteen years of silence being simply more than a body could hold. Or whether—this was the thought that frightened them most, the one they kept in the deepest drawer—it wasn't Wendell who started it at all.

Whatever it was, the words came out on a breath, half-swallowed, barely a sound:

*"Old man. Are you there?"*

The kind of prayer that isn't a prayer. The kind you say to a grave.

The mountain answered.

Not with thunder. Not with any voice. What happened was smaller than that and infinitely worse. The moss on the altar stone—every frond of it, across the whole ruined ring—turned toward Wendell at once, the way wheat turns toward sun, a soft green ripple moving against the wind. The rowan tree shivered though there was no breeze. And inside Wendell's wrists, the two faint oath-lines, cold for thirteen years, warmed.

Warmed like hands. Like someone taking hold from the other side of a door.

Bramble screamed—not a whinny but a raw, human-sounding scream—and wrenched free of the lead rope, bolting back down the track with the salt sacks bouncing wild. Wendell didn't chase her. Wendell couldn't move at all. They stood on the high track with their arms hanging at their sides and their heart going like a siege drum, watching the moss settle back into stillness, watching the rowan go quiet, feeling the warmth in their wrists fade by slow degrees like a fire banked rather than put out.

"No," Wendell said aloud, and their voice sounded wrong in the wide morning air, too loud, too *present*, a thing that could be heard. "No, no, I didn't mean it. I take it back. You hear me? I take it back."

The mountain said nothing. Of course it didn't. Thirteen years of nothing; one morning of moss turning like a head lifting; and now nothing again. It should have been a comfort. It was the opposite. Because Wendell had spent thirteen years believing the silence was permanent, structural, a law of the world like gravity—and laws didn't flicker. Laws didn't lean toward you when you whispered.

Something had heard. Something had *answered*, in the only language it had left.

And the worst part—the part that made Wendell's hands shake as they retrieved the dropped lead rope and stood listening to Bramble crash away downslope—was the relief. Underneath the fear, underneath the animal certainty that they had just broken the one rule that had kept them alive, there was a hot, shameful gladness, like a child hearing a parent's footsteps after hiding too long. *You're there. You're still there. You didn't leave us, you were taken, you were—*

Stop. Wendell pressed their wrists hard against their thighs until the warmth hurt. Thinking like that got people burned. Thinking like that was how the three oath-keepers in Greyhollow had ended up as ash and rumor. The church didn't need proof of heresy; it needed only the *hope* of it, the smell of it on a person, and hope was exactly what had just bloomed in Wendell's chest like blood in water.

They looked at the altar one last time. The moss lay flat and innocent. The rowan stood crooked and ordinary against the brightening sky. Everything was as it had been, except everything wasn't, and Wendell understood—with the clarity that comes in the half-second after you've stepped somewhere you can't step back from—that invisibility was over. Whatever had turned toward them on that mountainside had *seen* them. And things that see you do not forget.

"Thirteen years," Wendell told the empty air, hoarse. "Thirteen years I kept my mouth shut for both of us. You couldn't have managed one more?"

The wind came off the glacier and pushed past them downhill, following Bramble, and Wendell stood alone on the high track with no mule, no cargo, and no silence left to hide in.

* * *

They caught Bramble two miles down, standing trembling in a stream shallow with the salt sacks soaked through, and by the time Wendell had calmed her and re-rigged the panniers the sun was fully up, which was its own problem. Daylight on the high tracks meant shepherds, and shepherds meant talk, and talk was the last thing Wendell could afford. They took the long way instead, dropping below the treeline onto a smuggler's cut so steep Bramble had to be led nose-first, and reached the Greyhollow back-gate near dusk with wet boots, aching shoulders, and a mind that would not stop circling the same wound.

Greyhollow sat in the throat of the valley like a stone in a gullet: slate roofs, one crooked bell tower flying the new gods' sun-and-chain banner, and a market square that still bore faint scorch shadows on its cobbles if you knew where to look. Wendell knew where to look. Wendell made a point of never looking.

They delivered the salt through the cellar door of the Drowned Bell, where Maren Voss counted it by lamplight with her sleeves rolled and her abacus clicking fast, the way she did when the numbers were bad.

"You're late," she said, not looking up.

"Road washed out. Took the high track." Wendell set the last sack on the scale and watched the needle swing. Their voice was steady. They had practiced it being steady for two miles. "Half the salt's damp. Take it out of my share."

Maren's eyes came up then, sharp and grey as the town's slate. She was fifty, built like a barrel stave, with a smuggler's memory and a moneylender's soul, and she had been Wendell's handler for nine years without ever once asking a question she didn't already know the answer to. That was why Wendell trusted her—or something adjacent to trust, the professional version, the kind that had a price attached and everyone knew the figure.

"You took the high track," Maren repeated. Something in her voice had changed. The abacus had gone still.

"The low road's under six feet of meltwater. Yes."

"And you passed—" She stopped herself. Clicked one bead across. Clicked it back. "Never mind. Here's your purse. Here's the next manifest. And Wendell." She slid a folded paper across the table instead of the purse, and her face, when Wendell looked at it, was doing something Wendell had never seen it do in nine years. It was afraid. "Before you take the next job, you need to read what's nailed to the chapel door."

Wendell unfolded the paper. It was printed on good stock, church stock, the kind that cost real money, with the sun-and-chain seal stamped fat and black at the bottom:

*By order of the Diocese of the Lowland Reach: Let it be known that the faithful are commanded to report any person who claims to hear voices, receive signs, or commune with the silenced powers. Such communion is demonry. Informants will be compensated. The reward for verified report is fifty crowns.*

Fifty crowns. Wendell read the number twice. Fifty crowns was two years of smuggling salt. Fifty crowns was a new life in a town with no name. Fifty crowns was what a friend cost, if you had friends, and what a handler cost, if you had handlers.

"When did this go up?" Wendell asked.

"Six days ago." Maren's jaw worked. "There's more. A woman in Millbrook swears her well water turned sweet after she prayed at the old cairn. A shepherd on the Ceren slopes says he heard singing under the ice. The church is calling it mass hysteria. They're sending someone. An inquisitor." She said the word the way you'd say *landslide*—not naming a person, naming a force. "Wendell. What happened on that mountain?"

The lamplight hissed. Somewhere overhead, floorboards creaked as the tavern filled with the evening trade, ordinary people drinking ordinary beer, and Wendell felt the warmth in their wrists like a brand and knew—knew with sick, absolute certainty—that they were not the only one who had noticed the mountain leaning toward them. Six days ago the notices went up. Six days ago, the shepherd heard singing under the ice.

The silence hadn't just broken for Wendell. It was breaking everywhere, all at once, like ice going out on a spring river.

"I passed the altar," Wendell heard themselves say, because Maren had earned one true sentence in nine years, and because saying it out loud made it smaller, made it survivable. "The old one. Where we swore. And I—" Their voice caught. They hated it for catching. "I said something. Just a word. Just a question. And the moss turned. Maren, the moss *turned toward me*."

For a long moment Maren didn't move. Then she did something worse than shouting, worse than denouncing, worse than reaching for the paper and the fifty crowns. She reached across the table and gripped Wendell's wrist—gripped it right over the oath-mark, where the skin was still warm—and held on like a drowning woman holding driftwood.

"Listen to me," she whispered. "Whatever you heard down in yourself today—bury it. Bury it deeper than the salt. Because I'm telling you as the only friend you've got: the church isn't hunting voices anymore. They're hunting the *first* ones. The ones that can prove it started. And somebody, Wendell—somebody has already sold word that a sworn keeper crossed the high track this morning."

The cellar door banged open above them. Boots on the stairs—heavy boots, several pairs, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the building they were entering.

Maren let go of Wendell's wrist as if it had burned her.

"They're early," she said.
