## 2. Salt and Silver

Greyhollow smelled of wet stone and tallow, the way it always did this close to the border, where the mountains gave up their last argument and the lowlands began in earnest. Wendell came down off the pass at dusk with the mule's panniers riding heavy and the salt wrapped in oiled wool against the damp, and they did not look back at the ridge where the ruined altar sat under its shroud of moss.

They had not looked back the whole way down.

That was its own kind of prayer, maybe. The not-looking. Thirteen years of practice had taught Wendell that silence was a craft like any other—you honed it, you maintained your tools, you did not let the edge go dull. Silence was how a sworn mercenary of a dead god survived in a country where the new gods' church rang bells from every steeple and called the old rites superstition, heresy, worse. You kept your head down. You moved goods that wanted moving. You never, ever prayed.

Except once, apparently, on a mountain trail with no one to hear, when the moss on Orsen's altar had been green as spring and something old and vast had turned over in its sleep and said—

*You are still mine.*

Wendell's hands were steady on the reins. They had made sure of that for two days now. Steady hands, level breathing, the flat gray face of a smuggler who thought about nothing but tolls and weather. Inside, somewhere beneath the breastbone, was a splinter of cold that would not come out.

*Heard by more than them.* They didn't know yet that it was true. But they feared it, the way you fear a sound in the dark before you've identified it—generally, comprehensively, without evidence.

The mule clattered over the border stones. The toll-warden's lantern swung up, and Wendell produced the papers Maren had forged—wool merchant, out of Highfell, nothing worth a second glance—and the warden waved them through with the bored efficiency of a man who had checked ten thousand carts and found nothing in any of them. Greyhollow's gates stood open for the evening market. Somewhere a bell was ringing vespers, six clean bronze notes that fell over the rooftops like a net.

Wendell counted the notes out of habit. Six for the six new gods. Light, Law, Tide, Seed, Forge, and the Veiled One whose name nobody said aloud. When they'd been a child in the high valleys, there had been one bell, cracked and green with age, rung only at midwinter, and everyone had known whose it was.

They led the mule through the market square without stopping. Salt sellers shouted their prices from stalls that would be illegal in three days, when the church's tax wagons came through. A crier stood on the fountain steps reading proclamations in a voice polished smooth by repetition, and Wendell caught only the tail of it—"—by order of the Diocese of the Borderlands, any soul who hears voices, sees visions, or receives signs is bound by law to report to the nearest vestry, where honest confession will be met with mercy and silver—"

Mercy and silver. Wendell almost laughed. In the old days the gods had offered neither, and people had loved them anyway.

* * *

Maren kept her counting house behind the tannery, up a stair that smelled so thoroughly of cured hides that no watchman had voluntarily climbed it in twenty years. She was fifty, sharp-faced, with ink stains on her fingers she made no effort to hide, because an honest forger advertised her honesty. She looked up when Wendell came in, and Wendell saw—before anything else, before the greeting, before the tea she always poured—the way her eyes flicked to the shutters.

She'd barred them. Maren never barred her shutters; she said it made the room feel like a coffin.

"You're late," she said.

"Snow on the Kestrel." Wendell set the sample packet of salt on her desk, unwrapped it. Fine white crystals, dry as bone. "Grade you asked for. The rest is at the stable under the wool."

"Mm." She weighed a pinch between finger and thumb, tasted it, nodded. Then she poured the tea after all, two cups, and pushed one across, and did not drink from her own. That was the third wrong thing. Wendell set their cup down untouched.

"Say it," they said.

Maren exhaled through her nose. She took off her spectacles, cleaned them on her sleeve, put them back on—all the little rituals of a woman arranging her face. "There are riders in town," she said. "Church riders. Not the usual tithe-collectors. These wear gray and silver, and they carry letters sealed with the Veiled One's knot, which means they answer to the Inquisition and not to the local vestry, which means they can do things the local vestry can't."

"Inquisition doesn't come to Greyhollow. There's nothing here worth burning."

"There is now." Maren slid a paper across the desk. It was a printed broadsheet, fresh ink, the church's wheel-and-eye stamped at the top. Wendell read it twice because the first time their eyes refused the words.

*REWARD. By order of the Holy Office. Any person having knowledge of individuals who claim communion with the silent gods—who hear voices, receive dreams, bear unexplained marks, or exhibit other signs of illicit devotion—shall report such knowledge to the Office under seal of confession. Reward of forty crowns for information leading to apprehension. Anonymity guaranteed. Mercy promised to the repentant.*

Forty crowns. Wendell did the arithmetic without wanting to. Forty crowns was two years of smuggling salt. Forty crowns was what a handler might owe, say, to certain creditors in Lowmarket who did not forgive debts, who had ways of collecting that left handlers unable to hold a pen.

"How many copies of that are nailed up?" Wendell asked.

"All of them. Every post from here to the coast." Maren folded her hands. "And it gets better. The riders aren't just posting paper. They're asking questions. At the tolls, at the inns, at the shrine-houses. Asking whether anyone's reported hearing voices lately. Asking specifically—" and here her voice dropped, went careful, the voice she used when the words themselves were contraband, "—asking whether anyone passing through mentioned the old altars. Whether anyone stopped at one. Whether anyone was seen *praying* at one."

The tea steamed between them. Outside, faintly, the vespers bell finished its sixth note.

"I don't pray," Wendell said.

"No," Maren agreed. "You don't. You're the most silent man I've ever moved goods for, and I've moved goods for men who communicated entirely in blinks. Which is why I'm telling you this instead of selling you, and I want you to remember that I'm telling you this, Wendell, because—" She stopped. Started again. "Because a shepherd came down off the high pass two days ago. Tended flocks above the tree line, up where the old shrines are. And he swears—he's told the riders, he's told the vestry, he'll tell anyone who buys him a beer—that on the night of the last storm, every bell in his head rang at once, and he heard a voice come off the mountain like a landslide learning to talk. He says the whole pass heard it. He says the rocks heard it."

Wendell said nothing. Their heartbeat was very loud and very slow, like footsteps on a stair.

"He couldn't say what it said. Just that it was *old*. Old and glad, he said, like something waking up glad." Maren's eyes were fixed on Wendell's face, reading it the way she read forged seals, looking for the flaw. "The riders have been up that pass since yesterday morning. There's a shrine up there, isn't there? On the high trail. The one everyone says is just a ruin."

"It's just a ruin," Wendell said.

"Wendell."

"It's a ruin, Maren. Moss on it. Sheep shelter under it. Nobody's prayed there in thirteen years, because there's been nothing to pray to." They heard their own voice go flat and hard and knew it convinced her exactly as much as it deserved to. "Whatever your shepherd heard, it wasn't me. I don't pray. Praying is how you end up on a broadsheet."

Maren held their gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded slowly, and some of the tension went out of her shoulders, though not all of it, and certainly not the part that mattered. "Good," she said. "Keep being a man who doesn't pray. Because I'm going to tell you something else, and then you're going to finish your tea, take your coin, and be out of Greyhollow before the gray riders make their evening rounds—and you're never going to come through my door again."

"That's dramatic."

"I'm a dramatic woman. I forge documents for a living; drama is the trade." She leaned forward. "One of the riders talked. Riders talk; they're young and they think stable boys are furniture. This one told the farrier that the Office has a list. Names. People flagged over the years—one here, one there—for oddness. For standing too long at ruins. For marks that might be oath-marks under their sleeves. The list is short. Maybe thirty names across the whole march. And the rider said the strangest thing about it, the thing that made the farrier mention it to me, which makes me mention it to you." Her voice had gone very quiet. "He said the list wasn't written recently. He said the list was *old*. Sealed away in the diocesan vaults for years, and only now, this month, dug out and dusted off. As if somebody knew, ahead of time, that the silence was going to break. As if they were waiting for it."

* * *

Wendell drank the tea after all. It had gone bitter with sitting, but bitterness was familiar, and familiar things steadied the hands.

They took their payment in mixed coin, heavy enough to be real, light enough to run with. Maren walked them to the top of the stairs and stopped there, one hand on the rail, and for a moment she looked older than fifty—she looked like a woman watching a ship leave a harbor she already knew was mined.

"Wendell," she said. "Whatever answered you on that mountain—and I'm not saying it did, and you're not saying it did—but whatever it was, if it exists: stop talking to it. Whatever it offers you, however it calls you, however much of your life you gave it once. Silence is the only wall these people can't climb. Don't give them a door."

"The answer wasn't mine to refuse," Wendell said quietly. "It spoke first."

"Then don't speak second." Maren's jaw tightened. "I mean it. I've seen what happens to the ones who answer. Thirty years ago, when I was young and stupid and the church was less organized, I watched them take a woman out of Harrow's Ford for hearing voices. They were gentle about it. That was the worst part. They were so gentle, and she went so quietly, smiling, like she was going to meet someone. And nobody ever saw her again, and the church said she'd been 'restored to grace,' and her children grew up and lit candles to the six and never asked where their mother went." She stepped back into the dark of the stairwell. "Go. Out the tanner's gate, not the square. And Wendell—if anyone asks, you were never in Greyhollow. I was never your handler. We were never anything."

"We were friends," Wendell said.

Maren's face did something complicated and brief, like weather over water. "That's exactly why you were never here," she said, and closed the door.

* * *

The tanner's gate let out onto the river path, and the river path ran dark and slick along the Greywater toward the south road. Wendell led the mule by hand, hooves muffled in sacking, breath smoking in the cold. Overhead the clouds had torn open and the stars came through hard and clear, and among them rode the thirteen moons of Thornfell—or rather twelve of them did, the old calendar's scattered family of lights, waxing and waning on their separate schedules like a quarrelsome household. The thirteenth moon, the dark one, the one that was more idea than object, hung nowhere and everywhere, as it always did. Wendell had never given it a thought in their life. Tonight they counted all twelve visible ones twice, and could not have said why, except that counting was better than thinking, and thinking was better than remembering the exact timbre of a voice like a landslide learning to talk.

*You are still mine.*

The words had a weight now they hadn't had on the mountain. On the mountain, in the shock of it, the voice had seemed almost warm—a hearth after thirteen years of cold road. Now, walking away from a friend's barred shutters, past broadsheets offering forty crowns for the crime of listening, the warmth curdled into something else. Ownership. *Mine.* Not a greeting. A claim, reasserted, after more than a decade of neglect, by a god who had let his sworn servants rot in silence while the bells of the six multiplied across the country like mold.

Or—worse, a small voice suggested—not a god reclaiming what was his, but a prisoner beating on the walls of his cell, and Wendell the one fool in earshot.

The river bent. The south road climbed away toward the passes, and Wendell meant to take it, meant to put twenty miles between themselves and Greyhollow before dawn, and they had gotten perhaps half a mile along the bank when the mule stopped.

Not balked. Stopped. Ears forward, nostrils wide, staring back the way they'd come—at the town, at the dark bulk of the tannery, at something Wendell could not see.

Wendell stood still. The river muttered over its stones. Somewhere behind them, faint with distance, a dog began to bark—not the idle barking of dogs at cats, but the sustained, urgent, rising chorus of dogs that had found something. It came from the direction of the market square. Then a second chorus joined it from the east gate. Then a third, from the river path itself, downstream, closing.

And under the barking, thin and clear across the water, a human voice calling orders in the clipped cadence Wendell had heard only once before, years ago, from a distance, at a burning in Harrow's Ford.

Gray riders. Evening rounds. Except rounds didn't come with three packs of dogs and a coordinated sweep, and rounds didn't start within an hour of a salt merchant climbing a tannery stair.

Wendell's mind moved fast and cold, laying the map over the dark: the river path was open ground, moonlit, death. The south road funneled through the toll station where the riders had spent the day asking questions. East lay the cliffs and the old quarry tracks—steep, bad going for a mule, but bad going for horses too, and bad going for dogs.

They turned the mule east.

As they did, as they pressed into the shadow of the first cliff-wall and began the long scramble up the quarry track with the mule's breath sawing and the dog-song swelling below, Wendell looked back once—just once, breaking their own oldest rule—and saw, on the ridgeline above Greyhollow, black against the stars, a shape that was not a rider and not a horse.

It stood on four legs. It was very large. And it was not looking at the town, or at the torchlight bobbing along the river path, or at anything the dogs had found.

It was looking at Wendell.

Then the clouds closed, and the ridgeline was empty, and Wendell told themselves—climbing, sweating, heart slamming against the splinter of cold beneath their breastbone—they told themselves it had been a shadow, a trick of starlight, a big dog gone feral, anything at all.

They did not believe it. And far below, faint but unmistakable on the night wind, they heard the gray riders find Maren's door.
