## 3. The Wolf on the Pass

The thing about being followed is that you usually don't know it until it's too late to matter.

Wendell Zhou had survived thirteen years in Thornfell by knowing things before they mattered—knowing which passes the toll-keepers watched, knowing which shrines still held warmth and which were only stone, knowing when a prayer was safe and when it would get you hanged. So when the feeling arrived on the second morning out of Greyhollow, that prickle at the base of the skull like frost forming, they did not panic. They counted.

They counted their steps for an hour. The feeling stayed.

They stopped to retie a bootlace that didn't need tying and let their eyes drift along the treeline without turning their head. Pine, pine, scree, pine. A jay scolded somewhere upslope. Nothing else.

They walked on.

By midday they had climbed above the salt road into the high passes proper, where the pines gave way to wind-bent birch and the air thinned enough to taste of iron. This was country Wendell knew the way other people knew their own hands: the switchback below Ceren's shoulder, the cairn-field where pilgrims used to stack stones for a god who no longer answered, the narrow shelf above the Whitefall drop where one misstep meant a long time to think about your mistakes. They had run salt and silver and worse through these passes since they were nineteen years old, invisible as weather.

And something was walking behind them, patient as weather too.

It wasn't a person. Wendell was certain of that much by the third hour, because a person leaves sign—a broken twig at knee height, a bootprint in the soft ground under the birches—and what Wendell found instead, when they finally allowed themselves to look properly, was a track the size of a dinner plate pressed into the mud beside the trail. Four toes. Claws blunted from use rather than sharpened. Deep, deliberate, placed with care.

A wolf. But no wolf made tracks like this. Wolves traveled in packs, and packs left churned earth and scattered prints and the musk of a dozen bodies. This was a single line of enormous prints, evenly spaced, following the exact line of Wendell's own path—not paralleling it, not cutting across it, but *following* it, the way a shadow follows.

Wendell stood over the print for a long moment, breath fogging, and felt the old unease from the altar come crawling back up their spine. *Something answered.* Three nights ago they had knelt in the moss-choked ruin above the salt road and a prayer had slipped out of them like blood from a reopened wound, and something had answered, and now the world had changed shape around them. Maren's warning rang in their ears—*stop praying, you fool, people are listening*—but this wasn't listening. This was *following*. And whatever followed a man who'd been answered by a silent god was either hunting him or guarding him, and Wendell genuinely could not tell which frightened them more.

They spoke to the empty trail, because there was no one left to hear and because talking steadied their hands.

"If you're going to eat me," they said, "do it before the next climb. I'm heavier going up."

Nothing answered. But somewhere behind them, upslope among the birches, something exhaled—a long, low sound that might have been wind and might have been amusement—and Wendell decided, with the particular discipline of a person who has survived by not asking questions, to keep walking.

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow beneath the old waystone, a leaning slab of granite carved with spirals so worn they were nearly smooth, one of a hundred such stones the old faith had raised along the passes before the new gods came with their bells and their ledgers. Wendell built a small fire, small enough to smother in seconds, boiled tea over it, and sat with their back to the stone and their knife loose in its sheath and their crossbow across their knees.

The eyes came at full dark.

They caught the firelight maybe two hundred paces up the slope, two points of green set wide apart, and they were wrong in a way Wendell couldn't immediately name. Too high off the ground. Too far apart. When the head shifted—and it did shift, deliberately, letting the light catch it, the way a person steps forward to be seen—the shape behind the eyes resolved into something the size of a yearling calf, grey as slate, still as the mountain itself.

Wendell did not reach for the crossbow. That was instinct, not courage; they understood even as their hand stayed flat on their thigh that anything that size could close two hundred paces before they finished lifting the weapon, and that shooting it would accomplish nothing except telling it they were afraid.

"Big for these hills," Wendell said aloud. Their voice came out level, which surprised them.

The wolf said nothing. Of course it said nothing. It sat down on its haunches with a gravity that looked almost ceremonial, wrapped its tail over its forepaws, and watched the fire with the patience of something that had been watching for a very long time and intended to go on watching for a very long more.

Wendell drank their tea. They ate half a heel of bread. They banked the fire and lay down with the knife against their forearm, and they slept, which was the strangest part—they slept deeply and dreamlessly for the first time since the altar, and woke at grey dawn feeling more rested than they had any right to be with a monster sitting watch over them.

The wolf was gone. Its prints ringed the camp in a perfect circle, two hundred paces out, never once coming closer.

Wendell looked at that circle for a long time, then broke camp and walked north, and did not look back, and heard—softly, deliberately, so that they would hear it—one enormous paw placed in the mud behind them.

* * *

Two more days. Two more circles of prints around two more camps. The wolf kept its distance on the trail but never lost them, and once, crossing a scree field above the Whitefall, Wendell glanced back and saw it simply *sitting*, halfway up the opposite slope, outlined against the sky like a carving on a tomb. Not hunting. Hunting animals did not sit. Hunting animals did not wait for you to finish your tea.

Wendell began, against every instinct that had kept them alive, to talk to it.

At first it was practical—"I'm taking the high shelf tomorrow, if you're going to follow me, don't startle me on the narrows"—and then it was less practical, because the passes were quiet and quiet had always been dangerous for Wendell, quiet was when prayers leaked out. They told the wolf about the salt routes. About the cairn-field, and how pilgrims used to stack stones for Orsen and leave them balanced impossibly, seven and eight high, and how the stacks had all fallen the same winter the god went silent. About the oath, told in the flat voice of someone reciting a ledger entry: sworn at nineteen, marked with ash and iron, bound to a mountain god who had promised protection in exchange for service and who had then, thirteen years ago, stopped existing—or stopped answering, which for all practical purposes was the same thing.

"You're probably his," Wendell said one evening, watching the green eyes across the fire. "Everyone says the old gods' beasts died with them. Maybe you didn't get the word." They turned the bread heel in their fingers. "Or maybe you're the church's new trick. Some hound they've bred to sniff out anyone the old powers touch. Lure them into feeling safe, then lead the inquisitors right to them." They laughed, short and without humor. "That would be clever. That's the kind of clever that gets people like me killed."

The wolf tilted its head. And here was the thing Wendell would turn over for years afterward, the detail that refused to fit any honest shape: the tilt was *questioning*. Not the mechanical head-cock of a dog parsing a tone of voice, but the specific, weighted pause of a listener deciding whether to correct you.

"I'm not going to find out which you are tonight," Wendell said, and rolled into their blanket.

That night the circle of prints was drawn closer than usual. Fifty paces. Close enough that in the morning Wendell could see, in the frost, where the great paws had paused at each point of the compass, facing outward. Facing away from Wendell. Watching the dark beyond the firelight, not the fire itself.

Guarding, or herding. Omen or threat. Wendell still could not say, and the not-knowing had begun to feel less like danger and more like a door left open in a house they'd thought was empty.

* * *

Kestrel Notch came on the fifth day, and Wendell walked into it tired, which was how they had let it happen.

The notch was a throat of rock between two shoulders of Mount Ceren, the only easy crossing for thirty miles, and everyone who worked the passes knew it was the only place worth ambushing anyone. Wendell knew it better than most. They also knew the ambush season had ended weeks ago—too cold for the lowland crews to hold a position, too early for snow to drive the desperate down from the heights—and so they had let five days of strange company soften the edge of their attention, and they came around the first bend of the notch thinking about tea, and the net hit them from above.

It was an old smuggler's trick, which stung worse than the impact: weighted lines dropped from the ledge overhead, meant to tangle legs and drag a runner down. Wendell went to one knee in the scree, rolled, came up with the knife already cutting at the mesh—and stopped, because there were four of them coming down the trail, and four was too many, and three of them had crossbows.

"Well," said the leader, a broad man with a beard full of frost and a church-forged sword he had clearly not paid church prices for, "the salt rat comes home. Zhou, isn't it? Heard you were running the high route this month." He smiled with too many teeth. "Word travels. Word's worth money, these days."

"Desmond Hale," Wendell said. "You used to run for the Carrow syndicate. What happened, they stop feeding you?"

"The Carrow syndicate discovered God," Hale said pleasantly. "We all did, lately. New management. New rules." He gestured with the sword, lazy, enjoying himself. "Here's mine: the church pays silver for anyone the old powers have touched. Pays better than salt ever did. And you—" his eyes went to Wendell's sleeve, where the oath-marks lay hidden under wool, "—you've got the look of someone who's been *touched*. So we're going to walk you down to Greyhollow, nice and slow, and collect."

"And if I'd rather not?"

"Then we take the bounty for the pelt instead of the prisoner. Either way, Zhou, you've stopped being invisible."

There it was. The word Wendell had spent thirteen years refusing to think. They measured the distances—twenty feet to Hale, thirty to the crossbows, the notch walls sheer on both sides, the scree loose underfoot—and found nothing there but arithmetic that ended badly. Their hand drifted toward the crossbow slung at their back, and the nearest bowman tracked the movement and grinned.

And then the top of the western wall came alive.

Wendell saw it happen in pieces, the way you see lightning: a grey shape detaching from the skyline where no shape should have been standing, falling fifteen feet onto the ledge above the crossbowmen, landing without a sound. Then the sound arrived after all—a roar that was not a wolf's sound at all, that was deeper and older and structured like language, like a single enormous syllable of some dead tongue—and the two nearest men simply sat down in the scree, dropping their weapons, their faces gone the color of tallow.

The third fired. The bolt skipped off the wolf's shoulder as if it had struck the mountain itself, and the wolf crossed the distance between them in two strides that Wendell's eyes refused to itemize, and then it did not kill him. It put one vast paw on his chest and pressed him into the scree and held him there, pinned, effortless, its jaws an inch from his throat, and it *looked at him*—looked into him—with those green eyes, and whatever it communicated in that look made the man begin, quietly, to weep.

Hale ran. Wendell would give him that; he was fast for a big man, and he got ten strides down the trail toward the south mouth of the notch before the wolf lifted its head and made a sound—not loud, barely more than a breath, a low resonant note like a finger run round the rim of a glass—and Hale's legs stopped working. He went down hard, scrambled, tried again, and his body simply would not obey, and he lay there in the scree shaking while the wolf walked past him with terrible unhurried grace and planted itself squarely in his path.

Silence, then. Wind through the notch. The fourth man had fled at the first roar and was a distant figure dwindling down the northern trail, and good luck to him.

Wendell stood very still with the knife in their hand and their heart slamming against their ribs, and looked at the wolf, and the wolf looked back.

"That," Wendell said finally, "was not a wolf."

The wolf sat down. Wrapped its tail over its paws. Waited.

"No animal stops a man with a *word*." Wendell's voice was steady but their hands were not, and they sheathed the knife mostly to hide them. "No animal takes a crossbow bolt like a hillside. No animal draws a perfect guard-circle around my camp for five nights and never once comes closer than it means to." They took a step closer. The wolf did not move. Up close it was bigger still, its coat the grey of granite in rain, its chest deep as a barrel, and there was something about its face—something in the set of the brow and the patience of the mouth—that was not canine at all. Something almost human, worn like a mask over the skull beneath.

"They say the old gods' servants were beasts that learned to speak," Wendell said quietly. "Before the church burned them. They say Orsen's last servant was a wolf the size of a horse, and that when the god went silent, the servant went up the mountain and was never seen again." They swallowed. "Thirteen years ago."

The green eyes held theirs, and in them Wendell saw—clearly, unmistakably, the way you see a face in fire—recognition. Grief. And underneath the grief, something that looked unbearably like hope, aimed at them like a drawn bow.

"You know me," Wendell whispered. "You knew me before today."

Hale moaned faintly in the scree. The wolf flicked one ear toward him, dismissive, and did not look away from Wendell.

Wendell thought of the altar, the moss, the prayer that had slipped out like blood. *Something answered.* They had told themselves it was wind. They had told themselves it was exhaustion, imagination, the flaring of a dying ember in a dead hearth. But embers did not follow you for five days through the high passes. Embers did not guard your sleep in a perfect circle. Embers did not look at you like they had been waiting thirteen years for you to walk this trail.

"Take me to him," Wendell said. The words came out before they decided to say them, the way prayers do. "Whatever you are—wherever he is—if there's anything left of him to find—take me there."

The wolf rose. It came to Wendell—not lunging, not looming, but walking the way a companion walks—and stood close enough that its breath stirred their hair, warm and smelling of cold stone and pine. Then it turned its head north, up the notch, toward the high white shoulder of Mount Ceren and the country beyond that no map bothered to draw, and looked back at Wendell with an expression of such plain, patient expectation that it could only be called an answer.

Behind them, Hale found his legs at last and fled south, and Wendell let him go. Let the word travel. Let the whole church of the new gods hear that the salt rat of Thornfell had been seen walking into the high country with a beast out of the old stories at his side. Invisibility was over. It had been over since the altar, and some tight knot in Wendell's chest that had been wound for thirteen years chose this moment, absurdly, to loosen.

They shouldered their pack. The wolf fell into step beside them—not behind, not ahead, *beside*—and together they climbed out of Kestrel Notch into the falling snow, heading for the one place in Thornfell Wendell had sworn never to go again.

The cave shrine below Ceren's crown, where they had sworn their oath at nineteen, and where the mountain, they now understood, had been waiting all along to tell them what it remembered.
