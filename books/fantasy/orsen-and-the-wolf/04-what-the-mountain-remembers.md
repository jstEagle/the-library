## 4. What the Mountain Remembers

The storm found Wendell an hour past dusk, rolling down off the high shoulders of Mount Ceren like something poured from a jug—first the smell of it, wet stone and ozone, then the wind that shoved them sideways on the switchback trail, then the rain itself, cold and needling as thrown gravel.

Wendell had meant to push through to the shepherd's hut at Cold Ford. They had meant to be small and quick and gone by morning, another gray shape moving through Thornfell's gray country, noticed by no one. But the storm had other plans, and so, apparently, did the mountain.

The cave mouth opened in the cliffside barely twenty paces off the trail, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging birch. Wendell would have missed it entirely if not for the light—a thin, sickly green glow seeping through the birch stems like moss given fire.

They knew that glow. Every smuggler in Thornfell knew it, and every one of them gave cave shrines a wide berth, because old shrines were old trouble: church tithes, church questions, sometimes church fire. You didn't shelter where the old gods had been worshipped. You walked past with your eyes down and your mouth shut, the way you'd survived thirteen years of doing.

But the wind hit them again, a fist of rain and gravel, and somewhere below in the dark a slope groaned—the sound of scree beginning to move, a landslide deciding whether to take the trail with it.

"Damn it," Wendell said, to no one, to the storm, to whatever had answered their prayer three nights ago and hadn't stopped listening since.

They ducked under the birch curtain and into the mountain.

* * *

Inside, the shrine was smaller than the glow suggested. A single chamber, maybe ten paces across, its ceiling low enough that Wendell had to stoop. The walls were granite veined with quartz, and the quartz was what glowed—that faint green phosphorescence the old-timers called *godlight*, though the priests of the new gods called it heresy-light and chiseled it out wherever they found it. Here they'd never come. The chamber was too far up, too poor, too forgotten.

In the center of the floor stood the altar: a slab of black stone on two uprights, carved over every surface with spirals and mountain-goats and running stags, worn smooth in places by centuries of hands. Moss had claimed it too, softening the edges, but someone—long ago—had tried to defend it. Wendell could see the ghost-marks of chisel strikes, aborted, shallow. Whoever came to desecrate this place had stopped halfway. Maybe the godlight got into their eyes. Maybe their nerve failed.

A niche in the back wall held the remains of an offering bowl, dry as bone.

Wendell stood dripping in the entrance for a long moment, weighing the odds like any good smuggler weighs a suspicious cargo. Then the landslide below let go with a long rumbling roar, and the decision made itself.

They built a fire near the entrance, small and shielded, fed with birch bark and the stubble of dead heath they scraped from the cracks. They wrung out their cloak, ate half a heel of hard bread, and told themselves the shaking in their hands was just the cold.

It wasn't the cold.

Three nights ago, at the ruined altar in the low pass, Wendell had let slip a prayer—one stupid, unguarded sentence, *Orsen, if you're there*—and something had answered. Not words. Worse than words. A pressure behind the eyes, a warmth like a hand laid flat against the back of the skull, and a single sensation that Wendell still couldn't name except as *recognition*. Like being picked out of a crowd by someone who had known you as a child.

Since then, the world had tilted. Maren's warning in Greyhollow rang in their ears: *the church is paying for reports of voices, Wendell. Stop praying. Stop being interesting.* And the wolf—the enormous grey wolf that had watched them through two days of passes and torn apart four bandits at Kestrel Notch with an intelligence no beast should own—that wolf was out there now, somewhere in the storm, and Wendell had stopped pretending to themselves that they hoped it had moved on.

They banked the fire. They lay down on the stone with their back to the altar, because facing it felt worse, and pulled the damp cloak over their shoulders.

Sleep took them fast and deep, the way it does after fear and exertion, and the dream was waiting.

* * *

It began with snow.

Not falling snow—snow already fallen, lying blue-white and endless under a sky crowded with more stars than should fit. Wendell stood—or rather, *was*, because this was no body they recognized—on a ridge above a valley filled with fires. Hundreds of fires. Thousands. The whole valley floor glittered like a spilled jewelry box, and rising from each fire was smoke, and woven through each thread of smoke was a voice.

Prayers. That was the first understanding, arriving whole and certain the way things arrive in dreams: those are prayers. The valley was full of people, and every one of them was praying upward, toward the ridge, toward—

Toward *him*. Toward the thing whose memory Wendell was standing inside.

Orsen looked down at his own hands and found them vast—mountain-vast, granite-veined, snow lying in the creases of knuckles the size of barn roofs. He could feel the prayers landing on him like warm rain. He could feel each one individually, the way a man feels individual drops in a shower: the shepherd praying his ewes through lambing, the widow praying her son home from the lowland wars, the child praying purely and selfishly for a knife with a white handle. Thousands of them, all at once, all of them *his*, and none of them heavy. That was the miracle of it, the thing mortals never understood. Prayer was not a burden. Prayer was food, and warmth, and company. Thirteen years of silence later, Wendell understood in Orsen's bones exactly what had been lost.

*I was not alone,* the memory said, in the way memories say things without saying anything.

Others came up the ridge to meet him. Wendell saw them as Orsen saw them, and the seeing hurt—not because they were terrible, but because they were beautiful, in the way that a drawn blade is beautiful. There was a woman-shaped presence crowned in river-water, her hair flowing upstream. There was a tall figure wrapped in birch leaves whose footsteps left forests. There was a smaller one, quick and bright, all wings and teeth, a god of carrion birds and sudden fortune. And there was one who walked last, hooded, whose face Orsen's memory slid away from like rain off oiled cloth.

Brothers. Sisters. The old gods of Thornfell, in the years before the new ones crossed the sea.

They spoke together on the ridge, and their voices were weather. And beneath the weather, beneath the easy affection of ancient kin, Orsen's memory carried a tension Wendell recognized from a hundred mercenary camps: the strain of allies who have won together and not yet decided how to divide the spoils.

Because the new gods had come. Ships out of the east, carrying gods of book and bell and blood-price, gods who wanted worship the way conquerors want tribute—all of it, everywhere, forever. And the old gods had met them, and fought them, and lost ground, and finally gathered on this ridge to decide what desperate thing must be done.

The hooded one spoke last. Its voice was the only one Orsen's memory refused to carry clearly—a smear of sound, a hole in the shape of the dream. But Wendell felt the plan arrive anyway, felt it settle into Orsen's mind like a splinter working toward the heart:

*Take your heart out of your chest and hide it.*

That was the betrayal. Not a knife in the dark. An argument, delivered gently, by family.

*They cannot kill you while your heart is hidden,* the hooded one said—or said something; the words were smudged but the meaning burned through clean. *Hide it where even we cannot find it. Let us tell the new gods you are dead. Let them grow fat and careless. And when the turn is right, we dig it up and you rise whole, and Thornfell is ours again.*

And Orsen—who trusted, who had spent ten thousand years trusting these voices the way a mountain trusts its roots—Orsen opened his own chest.

Wendell felt it. That was the horror of the dream: there was no distance in it, no watching-from-outside. They felt granite ribs part like doors. They felt the heart come free—not flesh, nothing so small—a burning weight the size of a hill's core, dense with thirteen centuries of stored prayer, every whispered hope ever spoken on these slopes packed into one radiant mass. It did not hurt coming out. That was somehow worse. It felt like setting down a pack carried so long the shoulders had grown around it.

The others buried it. Wendell watched through Orsen's eyes as the river-goddess cut a channel with a thought and the birch-god grew roots to line it, and together they sank the heart into a vault beneath Mount Ceren itself and sealed it with chains forged from something worse than iron—from *prayer-iron*, metal quenched in ten thousand broken vows, the only substance that could bind divinity. And the hooded one set the final seal, and smiled—Wendell never saw the smile, but felt it, felt its shape in the dark—and said:

*Now sleep, brother. We will wake you when it is time.*

Then the dream tore.

Not faded—tore, like cloth ripped along a seam, and through the rip Wendell saw the truth the memory had been hiding: the others turning away from the sealed vault, the hooded one giving orders in its smeared voice, the new gods' bells ringing across the valley, and the old gods *fleeing*—no, not fleeing, being *hunted*, hunted by both armies at once, because the hooded one had sold them all. Sold Orsen's sleeping heart to the new gods as proof of surrender, sold his siblings' hiding places as payment, bought itself a place in the new order while everything it had sworn kinship to was chained or burned or buried.

The new gods had not stolen Orsen's heart.

*Their own family had.* And then told the church where it lay, and let the church build its chains of prayer-iron around it, and let the mountain god sleep silent for thirteen years while his worshippers forgot his name.

The last image before waking was the wolf—young then, ordinary-sized, a lean grey shape crouched in the snow beside the sealed vault, the only soul who had stayed. Staying when everyone else ran. Watching over a buried heart that no one was ever supposed to find.

*Good dog,* the memory murmured, with something that was almost Orsen's laugh, almost his grief. *Wait for me.*

* * *

Interlude — the wolf

Rain. The wolf tasted it before it fell—iron and stone-dust on the wind, the mountain's sweat—and had been in the lee of a granite fin for an hour when the first drops came.

He did not feel the cold. Whatever he was now, it kept its heat close. He felt other things instead, things with edges: the wet wool-and-steel smell of the human in the shrine, threaded through with woodsmoke and the sour note fear leaves in sweat. The green murmur of the godlight in the quartz, which sang to him the way old songs sing to the old. And beneath everything, through forty feet of rock, a slow pulse.

The heart. Still beating. Still waiting. Thirteen years of chains and it had never once stopped, and some nights—he could not have said why, in the wordless way of beasts—he hated it for that.

The human slept. The wolf circled the shrine twice, laid his jaw across his forepaws where the birch curtain broke the wind, and watched the doorway. Rain ran off his ruff in beads. Somewhere below, the landslide settled into silence.

He dreamed, briefly, of snow and stars and hands the size of hills, and woke with a whine caught in his throat, and put his nose back toward the door, and waited.

* * *

Wendell came awake all at once, the way soldiers do, hand already finding the knife under their cheek.

Dawn light filtered grey through the birch curtain. The fire was ash. Everything was ordinary—stone walls, mossy altar, the offering bowl in its niche—and everything was changed beyond repair, because Wendell now carried knowledge in their skull that had not been there when they closed their eyes, and it sat in them like swallowed coals.

They knew where the heart was buried. Not vaguely, not as rumor: they knew the vault's location the way they knew the layout of their own childhood house. Beneath Mount Ceren, third gallery past the drowned stair, behind a seal of prayer-iron shaped like a closed eye. They could walk there. Some traitorous part of them was already planning the route.

They knew the old gods had been imprisoned, not departed—chained or scattered or bound into sleeping shapes by their own kin, sold to the new gods' church in exchange for toleration. They knew the silence of thirteen years had never been abandonment. It had been a cage, and Wendell had lived all that time comfortably inside its shadow, smuggling salt past the jailers, and called it freedom.

And they knew—this was the part that made them press their palms flat against the cold stone until the granular bite of it grounded them—that the seal was failing. The dream had shown them the thirteenth moon, swollen and wrong-colored, and shown them the eye-shaped seal cracking along its lid like ice on a spring pond. When it broke, the heart would call out, and everything in Thornfell that had ever held a scrap of divinity would hear it and come running. Old gods. New gods. The church. Everyone.

Everyone except, apparently, one enormous grey wolf who had once been asked to wait.

"No," Wendell said aloud, to the empty shrine. Their voice cracked on it. "No, no, no."

Because here was the arithmetic they could not escape: the answer at the ruined altar, the recognition, the warmth at the back of the skull. They had assumed Orsen had reached for them. Now, with the dream's knowledge sitting cold in their gut, they understood it might be the reverse. The heart, sensing its seal weakening, had reached outward—for anyone bound to it by oath. For the nearest living thread of the old covenant.

And it had found Wendell. Smuggler, survivor, keeper of a thirteen-year vow of silence. The mountain god's stolen heart had chosen them as its compass needle, and it had poured its location into their memory like wine into a cup, and cups do not get a vote.

Wendell sat with their back against the mossy altar for a long time, watching dust motes drift through the godlight, doing the sums.

Stay invisible: impossible. The church was already paying for reports of voices; Maren had already looked at them too long; a wolf the size of a yearling ox had already been seen at their side through two mountain passes. Invisibility was a currency they'd spent their last coins of.

Run: possible, for a while. South past Greyhollow, over the border, into country where the old gods had never been worshipped and a smuggler could vanish. But the heart's knowledge would come with them, and the seal would break regardless, and the war would start regardless, and they would spend the rest of their life knowing they could have—what? Done what? They didn't even know yet. Return the heart and reignite the old war? Destroy it and snuff out every divine power in Thornfell, including whatever fragile thread still connected them to Orsen? They turned the choices over and found no bottom in either.

Go to the vault: that was the path the dream wanted. The path the coal in their chest warmed toward, treacherously, like a cat seeking the fire.

Outside the birch curtain, a branch snapped.

Wendell was on their feet with the knife out before the sound finished, pressed to the wall beside the entrance, breathing shallow. Through the gap in the stems: grey. A shoulder, a flank, the great head lowering under the wet birch leaves.

The wolf stopped at the edge of the firelight's cold remains and looked in at them—not at the shrine, not at the altar, at *them*—with pale amber eyes that held absolutely nothing animal in them. No hunger. No caution. Something older and wearier, the look of a guard at the end of a very long watch.

"You know," Wendell said quietly. It wasn't a question. "You've known the whole time. Where it's buried. What I am to it."

The wolf's ears tipped forward. It did not move.

Thirteen years, Wendell had kept their head down and their mouth shut and their prayers unsaid, and it had worked, it had *worked*, and now in a single night the mountain had handed them a secret worth a war and posted a wolf at their door like a debt collector. Somewhere south, Maren was probably counting church silver. Somewhere east, someone in a cassock was writing their description on a reward notice. And somewhere beneath their feet, under forty feet of granite and chains of broken promises, a heart was beating faster.

The wolf turned and walked a few paces up the trail, then stopped, and looked back over its shoulder. Waiting. The gesture was so plainly, insultingly human that Wendell almost laughed.

"I haven't agreed to anything," they told it, sheathing the knife and shouldering their pack. "Understand that. I'm going because staying is worse."

The wolf held their gaze a moment longer—amber, patient, unbearably sad—and then turned up the trail toward Mount Ceren, and Wendell, cursing softly under their breath, followed.
