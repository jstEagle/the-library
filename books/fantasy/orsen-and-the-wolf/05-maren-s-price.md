## 5. Maren's Price

The rain found Greyhollow before Wendell did, sliding down the mountain in grey sheets that turned the switchback roads into rivers of mud and slate. By the time they reached the town gate, their boots were soaked through, their oath-cloak heavy as a burial shroud, and the wolf—of course—was nowhere to be seen.

It had been three days since Kestrel Notch. Three days since the bandits and the impossible animal that had moved through them like a thought given teeth. Wendell had tried to lose the wolf twice on the high passes, doubling back through scree fields and fording a freezing river at a place where the current ran white and mean. Each time, by morning, it was there again: a grey shape on a ridgeline, patient as weather, watching with eyes the color of old copper.

Wendell had stopped trying to shake it. That frightened them more than anything else—the ease with which they had surrendered to being followed.

Greyhollow huddled under its eaves like a man pretending to sleep. Smoke rose crooked from a hundred chimneys, bent sideways by wind off the Ceren massif. The town smelled of wet wool, tanneries, and the particular staleness of a place that lived off what passed through it rather than what grew within it. Smugglers' towns were all the same that way. They fed on movement, and when movement stopped, they starved quietly and blamed the mountains.

Wendell came in through the tannery gate, as always, where the guards took one look at the stink on their cloak and waved them past without a second glance. Nobody looked closely at anyone who smelled like rendered fat and old blood. Invisibility had a smell, and Wendell wore it like armor.

Maren kept her counting house above the Salt Cellar, down the alley where the gutters ran brown and the walls wept moisture year-round. Wendell climbed the outside stairs slowly, listening to the wood complain beneath their weight, and told themselves the tightness in their chest was just exhaustion from the pass.

They knew better. Thirteen years of smuggling had taught them to read a street the way a hunter read spoor, and Greyhollow's streets were wrong tonight. Too quiet. The Salt Cellar's usual crowd of carters and fence-menders was absent. A pair of men stood under the awning across the alley who had no business standing anywhere in this rain—they wore oilcloaks cut too well, boots too clean, and they held themselves with the particular stillness of soldiers pretending to be civilians.

Church men. Or paid to look like it.

Wendell climbed anyway. Because Maren had sent word—a boy, a scrap of paper, *come tonight, come alone*—and because thirteen years ago, on a ruined altar choked with moss, they had learned that oaths outlive the people who swear them. Maren had been the one to teach them the salt roads. Whatever she wanted, she deserved to ask it to their face.

The door at the top of the stairs was unlocked. That was wrong too. Maren locked her door against her own mother.

Inside, the counting house was dim, lit by a single lamp turned low. Maren sat behind her desk with a ledger open before her, and she looked—Wendell realized with a slow, cold drop of the stomach—*old*. Not tired-old. Old the way a building looks when the rot has finally reached the beams. Her hands, usually quick as a pickpocket's, lay flat on the ledger as if holding it down against a wind.

"Wendell." Her voice was steady. That was somehow worse than trembling would have been. "Sit."

"I'd rather stand." Wendell stayed near the door, back to the wall, where they could see the window and hear the stairs. "There are two men across the alley wearing church money on their backs. And your door's open. So let's not pretend this is a social call."

Maren flinched. It was small—the twitch of an eyelid, a tightening at the corner of her mouth—but Wendell had spent thirteen years reading people for a living, and Maren had taught them most of what they knew about it.

"You saw them," she said.

"Everyone in this town could see them. They're standing like they're being paid by the hour."

"They are." Maren closed the ledger. Her hand lingered on the cover, flat, the way you'd rest a palm on the chest of something dying. "Wendell, I need you to listen to all of it before you decide what you're going to do. Because I've watched you decide things for thirteen years, and you decide fast, and you decide final, and I—" She stopped. Started again. "I sold you."

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Wendell heard them hit bottom, but for a long moment nothing came back up.

"Say that again," Wendell said quietly, "slower."

"The night you left Greyhollow after the delivery. The night you—" Maren's throat worked. "The night you told me something answered you. At the altar. You told me that much, at least, before you went up the mountain. You didn't think I noticed how you said it. You've been invisible for thirteen years, Wendell, but you've never been able to hide a thing from me. You were scared. You were scared and you were *hoping*, both at once, and I've seen that look exactly once before in my life—in a mirror, the winter my husband died."

"Maren."

"Debts," she said, and now the steadiness cracked, and underneath it her voice was raw and fast, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this confession a hundred times in the dark and still couldn't make it clean. "You know I carry debts. You've known for years—I never hid that either. The Salt Cellar, the warehouse notes, the interest running like water through a cracked cistern. What you don't know is who holds the paper. The church bought my debts two years ago. All of them. Quietly, through three intermediaries, so I wouldn't even know whose hand was around my throat until they chose to squeeze."

"And they squeezed."

"They squeezed." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as old bone. "A week after you left. A man in grey robes sat where you're sitting and put a piece of paper on my desk and said, *We understand one of your couriers has begun hearing voices.* They already knew, Wendell. Do you understand? They didn't need me to tell them. Someone talked at the altar site—a shepherd, a pilgrim, someone—and the report moved down the chain faster than you move down a mountain. All they needed from me was confirmation. Details. Your routes. Your habits. Where you'd run if you were frightened." She lifted her eyes, and they were wet and steady and full of a misery that looked almost like relief, the way a confession does when it's finally out. "And I gave it. Every road you know. Every cache. Every tunnel. I gave them thirteen years of you, itemized, and they forgave four hundred crowns of debt, and I have hated myself every hour since, and none of it—not one hour of it—changes what I did."

The rain filled the silence. It drummed on the roof, ran in the gutters, hissed against the window glass. Wendell became aware, distantly, that their hands had gone very still at their sides—the stillness of a body deciding whether to fight or flee while the mind catches up.

"The tunnels," Wendell said. Their own voice sounded far away. "You gave them the tunnels."

"I gave them the ones I know." Maren pushed back her chair and stood, and her legs shook, and she stood anyway. "Which is why I'm telling you the rest. There's a way out of this town that isn't in any ledger of mine, because I never knew it existed until you showed it to me eleven years ago, drunk, after the Harrow job, laughing about how you'd found it as a kid. Under the tannery. The old drainage gallery from before the church paved the low quarter. You said no one else alive knew it. You made me swear I'd forget it." Her mouth twisted. "I forgot everything else I promised you. I kept that one. It's the only thing I have left to give you, and it isn't even mine to give."

"Why?" The word came out of Wendell harder than they intended. "Why tell me at all? Why not just let them take me? You'd be safe. Clean ledger, clean conscience—or whatever passes for one in this business. Why warn me?"

"Because handing you over and warning you are the same act, Wendell, and I know it." Maren came around the desk, and Wendell did not step back, though every instinct screamed to. She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Wendell could see the lamplight trembling in her eyes. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I want you to understand that. I'm not going to insult you by asking. I'm fifty-eight years old, I buried a husband and a son, I've spent thirty years selling other people's secrets to keep my own neck whole, and last month I sold the only person who ever treated me like something other than a tool. And then I sat in this room for six days knowing those men across the alley were waiting for my signal, and I wrote that note to you instead, and I don't even know if that makes it better or worse. Maybe worse. Maybe warning you is just the last thing I'm selling myself so I can sleep tonight." She drew a breath that shuddered going in and came out level. "But I'll tell you one true thing, since I owe you at least one. They're not taking you alive to question you. The order came down from someone high—an inquisitor named Vael. They want the answering stopped. Permanently. Whatever's woken up in the old gods, the church means to smother it in its cradle, and you're the first spark they've found."

*The first spark.* Wendell turned the phrase over and felt the shape of it, cold and smooth. First implied second. First implied more.

"The dreams," Wendell said. "The cave shrine. The memories that aren't mine."

Something flickered across Maren's face—fear, old and instinctive, the fear of a woman who had survived thirty years in Greyhollow by never touching anything holy. "Don't tell me," she said quickly. "Don't. If I don't know it, I can't sell it. That's the only rule that's ever kept me alive, and I broke it once for you already." She pressed something into Wendell's hand—small, wrapped in oilcloth. A purse, heavy with coin, and folded inside it a scrap of paper bearing a name and a village: *Sela Venn, drowned chapel at Mirrow Ford.* "An oracle. Half-mad, fully blind, lives in a flooded church and answers questions nobody should ask. If anyone knows what's waking up and why the church is afraid enough to kill couriers over it, it's her. Go north, not south. South is the toll roads, and they have your face on every post between here and the capital by now."

"And you?" Wendell asked. "When they realize I slipped the net?"

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest thing Wendell had seen in thirteen years of sad things. "I'm an old woman with forgiven debts and a talent for looking harmless. They won't waste an inquisitor on me. And if they do—" she shrugged, a small, tired motion, "—then I'll have paid what I owed. Not to the church. To you. There's a difference, whatever the ledgers say."

Below, in the alley, a whistle sounded—two short notes, one long. The signal. Maren's shoulders sagged as if the sound had cut strings she'd been holding herself up with.

"That's them moving," she whispered. "Back stair's blocked—they posted a man in the yard an hour ago. Tannery, Wendell. The drainage gallery. Go *now*, and don't stop walking until Mirrow Ford, and if you meet a wolf on the road—" she caught herself, frowned, "—why did I say that?"

"Because you know everything," Wendell said, "and sell most of it."

For one moment, neither of them moved. Then Maren reached out and gripped Wendell's forearm, hard, the grip of a woman hauling on a rope, and Wendell let her, and neither of them said anything that would matter later, because there was nothing left to say that hadn't already been priced and sold.

"Go," Maren said.

Wendell went.

* * *

The tannery at night was a cathedral of stink. Wendell moved through the curing sheds by memory, past racks of hides hanging like flayed saints, past the lime pits breathing their chemical fog into the dark. Behind them, muffled by walls and rain, they heard boots in the alley and voices giving quiet orders. Searching the counting house. Finding, perhaps, an empty room, a dead lamp, a ledger closed on nothing.

The gallery entrance was where an eleven-years-drunk version of Wendell remembered it: behind the third lime pit, a grate rusted to lace, and beyond it a crawl of black air that smelled of the mountain's deep stone. Wendell pried the grate loose with the blade of their knife, wedged it open with a shim of oak, and slid into the dark feet-first.

The drainage gallery ran under the low quarter for half a mile, a brick throat built by masons dead two hundred years, back when Greyhollow had worshipped different gods and needed different plumbing. Water ran shin-deep along a central channel. Rats protested Wendell's passage in indignant squeaks. Twice the ceiling dipped low enough that Wendell had to go flat and wriggle, cloak snagging, lungs tight, while the dark pressed in like a hand.

And in the dark, unbidden, the memory rose: moss on the altar, the taste of blood from a split thumb, a voice like stone shifting underground saying *sworn and sealed*. Thirteen years of silence, and now the whole world was waking up, and the price of waking seemed to be paid by everyone who had ever loved the sleeping.

Wendell crawled on, and hated, and grieved, and did not stop.

The gallery ended at a sluice gate above the millrace, exactly as remembered. Wendell shouldered it open into the rain, dropped eight feet into the willow-choked bank below, and came up gasping with the millpond's overflow soaking them to the ribs. North lay the river path, dark and empty, running toward Mirrow Ford and a blind oracle in a drowned chapel.

Wendell paused once, on the bank, and looked back at Greyhollow—its crooked smoke, its weeping walls, the single lit window of Maren's counting house glowing yellow through the rain like a coal in ash. Loyalty had a market price. Tonight Wendell had learned theirs: four hundred crowns, forgiven. Cheaper than a good horse.

Something moved on the ridge above the town. A grey shape, still as a gravestone, outlined against the last light of the storm clouds.

Watching. Waiting. As if it had known exactly which door Wendell would come out of—as if it had been waiting at this exit all along.

Wendell's hand found the knife at their belt and did not draw it.

"Well," they said aloud, to the rain, to the wolf, to the god who might be listening again. "You already know the way north. Lead on, then."

The wolf turned, unhurried, and started down the river path toward Mirrow Ford—and Wendell, soaked, betrayed, and hunted by an inquisitor whose name they now carried like a brand, followed it into the dark.
