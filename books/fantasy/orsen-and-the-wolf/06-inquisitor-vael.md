## 6. Inquisitor Vael

The interrogation room in the Greyhollow chapterhouse had once been a salt cellar. Sera Vael could still smell it—brine soaked so deep into the timber that no amount of incense would ever drive it out, and beneath the brine, the older smell of fear that every room like this acquired, the way a butcher's block acquired blood. She had asked for the room specifically. Not for the smell. For the low ceiling, the single lamp, the chair bolted to the floor at the exact distance from the table where a seated person's shoulders began to ache after an hour. Discomfort loosened tongues faster than pain ever did. Pain made people lie beautifully. Discomfort just made them tired.

Maren Cotter had been sitting in the chair for two hours.

She was fifty-some years old, with smuggler's forearms and a merchant's smile, and she had spent those two hours doing what her kind always did: negotiating. With Vael, with herself, with the room. She'd started with outrage—"I'm a loyal subject of the Concord, I've paid my tithes"—then moved to bargaining—"there are others who heard what I heard, ask the ferryman, ask the toll-keeper at Kestrel bridge"—and now she had arrived, as they all did, at the flat gray country of exhaustion where the truth lived.

"Heard voices," Maren said. Her voice was sandpaper. "That's what you want me to say. That I heard voices."

"I want you to say what happened," Vael said. "In your own words. The ones you'd use if no one were paying you."

Maren's laugh was short and bitter. "No one pays for words like mine."

"Someone did," Vael said quietly. "You sold word of it to the church. That's why you're here instead of on your boat."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, the chapterhouse bell marked the hour—one soft bronze note, the new gods' hour of reflection, when every bell in Thornfell rang and every believer was meant to stop and consider their smallness before the divine. Vael did not stop. She had stopped believing in stopping, years ago, in a burned village whose name she kept in the same locked drawer of her mind where she kept her sister's face.

Maren looked at her hands. They were good hands, work-worn, honest-looking. Liars often had honest-looking hands; Vael had learned not to trust them.

"It was three nights past," Maren said finally. "Wendell Zhou came down off the high road with a load of grey salt, same as they do every month, same route, same face. You could set a clock by them. That's what I liked about them. That's what I *sold* about them." She swallowed. "They came into my back room to settle accounts, and they were wrong-footed. Shaken. Said they'd passed the old altar up at Ceren's foot—the mossy one, the one even the goats won't touch—and said a prayer. Didn't mean to. Said it slipped out of them like water through a cracked jug."

"And?"

"And something answered." Maren's voice dropped, and Vael watched the fear come into her face properly now, not performed, not negotiated—the real thing, the animal thing. "Not with words. Wendell wouldn't say how. But they went white as milk and told me the silence was over. Thirteen years of nothing from the old gods, and now this. And then they begged me—begged me, Inquisitor—to tell no one. And I looked at them, at this person I've traded with for six years, this person who's never cheated me, never raised their voice, never given me a reason in God's green—"

"In whose name?" Vael asked.

Maren blinked. "What?"

"You said 'in God's green.' Which god? There are nine in the Concord. Ten, counting the Sleeper, whom we don't count."

"The—whichever. The one that matters." Maren's jaw tightened. "That's the trouble with your church, isn't it? Nine gods and none of them listening. People pray to the Nine and get liturgy back. Then one smuggler mutters at a dead altar and gets an *answer*, and suddenly there's coin on the table for anyone who's heard voices." She leaned forward as far as the bolted chair allowed. "You want to know what I know, Inquisitor? I know you're not hunting Wendell because they're dangerous. I know you're hunting them because they're *proof*. Proof the old gods aren't dead. Proof everything your bells and your tithes and your nine shining faces are built on is a fence around an empty field."

Vael let the words sit. It cost her something to let them sit. Everything cost her something these days; she had learned to pay without flinching.

"You're right," she said.

Maren faltered. She had prepared for denial, for zealotry, for the whole liturgical armor. She had not prepared for agreement.

"The field isn't empty," Vael continued. "It never was. The old gods are waking, Mistress Cotter, and when they wake fully, they will want back what was taken from them—and what was taken from them was taken by fire, by my church, by people who believed exactly as sincerely as you believe nothing. There will be a war. Not a war of armies—a war of *answers*. Every prayer in Thornfell will become a battlefield, because every soul will have to choose which voice to kneel to. Do you understand what that means? It means Greyhollow burns either way. It means your boat, your salt, your six years of quiet trade—all of it ends, one way or another. The only question left is whether it ends quickly."

"So you'll kill one smuggler to save the world." Maren spat, weakly, toward the floor. "Zealots always dress it up as arithmetic."

"I don't intend to kill them." Vael rose, took the lamp from the table, and held it so its light fell across the map she'd unrolled there—Thornfell rendered in the cartographer's careful ink, the mountains hatched like sleeping animals. "I intend to find them before the old gods finish waking. Because whatever answered them at that altar knows things. And knowledge, in a war like this one, is the only mercy worth having." She rolled the map closed. "You're free to go, Mistress Cotter. Your debt to the church is paid. Consider what you bought with it."

Maren stood on legs gone unsteady. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"They'll forgive me," she said. "That's the worst part. Wendell forgives everyone. It's how they survive." A pause. "Don't mistake it for weakness. Forgiveness is the only weapon they've got, and they aim it better than most men aim knives."

Then she was gone, and the salt smell settled back over the room like snowfall.

* * *

The toll records arrived at dusk, carried by a lay brother with ink-stained fingers and the apologetic posture of a man who brought bad news professionally.

Vael spread them across the chapterhouse table under the good lamps: eleven years of ledgers from the Kestrel bridge toll-house, the ferry crossings at Greyhollow, the mountain gate at Ceren's Foot. The church kept such records everywhere—not for taxes, though the taxes were real, but because movement was confession. Where a person walked, week after week, year after year, told you what they worshipped, whom they feared, what they were building. The Nine taught that the soul revealed itself in patterns. Vael had simply extended the theology to ledgers.

"Wendell Zhou," she said to Brother Anselm, her record-keeper, a thin young man with a stammer he only lost when reading numbers. "Eleven years. Show me the pattern."

Anselm laid the pages out in sequence, and the pattern emerged the way images emerge in a developing plate—slowly, then all at once.

"Same circuit," he said. "Every month, near enough. Greyhollow to Ceren's Foot by the high road, over Kestrel Notch, down to the river towns, back. Never varies more than three days. Salt going out, iron coming back, sometimes cloth." He tapped the columns. "But look here, Sister. Look at the dates against the holy calendar."

Vael looked. And felt the cold move up her spine, the particular cold of a theory becoming a fact.

"Every crossing of Kestrel Notch falls within a day of a feast of the Nine," she said. "Every one. Eleven years."

"Yes, Sister. And every delivery to Ceren's Foot falls within a day of—" He hesitated, because saying it aloud gave it weight. "—of the old observances. The ones from before. Hearthnight. The Long Quiet. The moon-counts."

"He wasn't avoiding the old holy days," Vael said slowly. "He was keeping them. Under cover of commerce. Eleven years of smuggling goods on roads and prayers on a calendar." She straightened. "Who else crosses Kestrel Notch on the old observances?"

Anselm pulled the secondary lists. Cross-referenced. His stammer returned. "S-seventeen names, Sister. Recurring. Some dead now, some moved away. But four still active. One of them is—" he turned the page around so she could see— "Brannic Hale. Formerly of the mercenary company called the Oathbound. Disbanded thirteen years ago."

"Thirteen years ago," Vael repeated. "When the old gods went silent."

"Yes, Sister."

Vael walked to the window. Below, Greyhollow lay in its bowl of lamplight and woodsmoke, ordinary, drowsy, unaware. Somewhere out there, a smuggler with a calendar full of dead feasts was running through the high passes with an answered prayer in their head and a bounty on their name. Somewhere behind them, the seal her church had built—or inherited, the histories were maddeningly unclear—was wearing thin. She could feel it the way sailors feel weather: not evidence yet, but pressure. The chapterhouse bells had rung flat twice this week. The shrine-lamps guttered in windless rooms. In the river towns, they said, a drowned chapel had begun to glow at night, and the faithful who prayed there reported that the Nine answered too slowly, as if distracted—as if looking over their shoulders at something older stirring behind them.

"Sister?" Anselm said. "Your orders?"

She thought of her sister, and put the thought back in its drawer. She did that perhaps forty times a day. Lira, aged nineteen, fevered in a village the old gods' last war had cut off from any healer, praying to whatever remained within reach of a dying girl's voice—and getting, as far as anyone knew, nothing. Or worse than nothing. Vael had been twenty-two. She had joined the church within the year, and she had never once told the story, because stories like that were currency and she refused to spend hers on sympathy.

Here was what she believed, and believed with the whole cold engine of her mind: the old gods had fought a war once, over pride, over territory, over whatever gods fight over, and the war had eaten villages like hers like a fire eats a forest. The new gods had ended it—or buried it—and the burial had held for thirteen years of peace. Now the grave was opening. And everyone who wanted to dig it open faster—the smugglers, the oracle-cults, the wolf-cultists in the high valleys, whoever had answered Wendell Zhou at that ruined altar—they all spoke the language of restoration, of return, of the old true ways. No one ever spoke the language of what the old ways had *cost*.

Peace was not the absence of war. Peace was a wall, and walls required wardens, and wardens required dirty hands, and hers had been dirty for twenty years and would stay dirty until the wall stood on its own or fell on everyone.

"We ride at first light," she said. "Kestrel Notch. If Zhou crossed there on the observance days, they'll cross again. And Anselm—" he looked up— "send word ahead to the garrison at Ceren's Foot. Quietly. I want the mountain gates watched, but I want no one touched. If the old gods are answering prayers again, then every frightened peasant who kneels at a mossy altar is a door, and I need doors, not corpses. We are not butchers. Say that part twice."

He said it twice.

She turned back to the map, to the hatched mountains, to the small inked notch where a wolf-shaped shadow of a rumor had begun to gather—travelers on the high passes speaking of a grey beast that walked beside lone travelers and vanished, that killed bandits and spared merchants, that watched. She had dismissed the reports as mountain folklore. She was done dismissing things.

Four names on the observance list. One smuggler with an answered prayer. One wolf out of legend. One seal wearing thin beneath a mountain whose name, in the oldest tongue, had meant *the heart that holds*.

Vael blew out the lamps except one, and sat down to write her report to the Archon, and found that her pen hesitated over the page for the first time in her career—because the report required her to state, plainly, what she intended to do if she found Wendell Zhou before the seal broke, and the truth was that she did not yet know. Kill the messenger, and lose the message. Spare the messenger, and risk the message being a declaration of war written in a dead god's handwriting.

In the end she wrote: *"I will bring back the answer itself."*

And below it, in smaller script, the sentence she would deny under oath if ever questioned:

*"Whatever it costs."*

Outside, the bells rang the midnight hour, and for the third time that week, the note came out flat—dull, strained, like a voice forced to sing while something enormous pressed down on it from underneath the world.

Vael listened until the sound died, then packed her saddlebags by candlelight, and did not sleep.
