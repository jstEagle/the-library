## 7. Thirteen Turns

The drowned chapel sat in the flooded quarry three days north of Kestrel Notch, and it had been sinking for two hundred years without ever quite managing to die.

Wendell had heard of it the way smugglers heard of most things—in fragments, over cheap wine, from people who were drunk enough to talk and sober enough to mean it. *The Oracle of Stillwater*, they called her. She lived where the water had swallowed a chapel of the new gods whole, back when the new gods were young and their architects built too greedily on old ground. The bell tower still stood above the surface like a drowning man's last finger. Everything else was below.

Wendell came at dusk, when the water turned the color of iron, and left the wolf on the ridge above.

That had been the arrangement since the Notch—not spoken, never agreed to, but understood. The wolf followed at a distance that varied with Wendell's mood: closer when the road grew dangerous, farther when Wendell's nerves frayed. Tonight Wendell's nerves were rope ends, and the distance was generous.

"You stay," Wendell said, not turning around. "Whatever she is down there, she won't talk with you breathing down my neck."

The wolf sat in the bracken, grey against grey, amber eyes reflecting the last light. It did not blink.

Wendell descended.

* * *

The path into the quarry switchbacked through spoil heaps gone soft with moss, and at the bottom the water began—first as seepage, then as shallow sheets that mirrored the darkening sky, then as true depth. Someone had laid stepping stones across the flooded nave, each one crowned with a stub of candle-glass. Most were unlit. Three burned with small yellow flames that did not gutter in the wind off the water, which should have been Wendell's first warning.

The chapel doors still hung on their hinges below the waterline, warped open like a mouth mid-sentence. Inside, pews rose from the flood in black rows, and above them the vaulted ceiling curved down into reflection until it seemed the water held a second chapel, inverted, deeper than the first.

On the altar—the altar of the new gods, cracked clean through—sat an old woman.

She was mending a net. That was all. Her hands moved with the economy of someone who had done one thing ten thousand times, drawing the shuttle through mesh, and she did not look up as Wendell picked a careful way across the stones.

"You're late," she said.

"I didn't know I was expected."

"Nobody ever does." She tied off a knot and finally raised her head. Her eyes were pale as riverstone, and they went through Wendell the way water goes through cloth. "Thirteen years ago you swore on a broken altar and got no answer, and you told yourself that was fine. Silence was safe. You built a life in the silence, little smuggler, a nice dry life of salt and lies. And then the mountain spoke." She clicked her tongue. "And now look at you. Wet boots. Bad color. Sleeping in caves."

Wendell stopped three stones away. "People have died for less than what I'm carrying."

"People have died for exactly what you're carrying," the oracle said. "That's rather the point of it."

The candle-flames leaned though there was no wind. Somewhere under the water, something shifted—a long settling, like a body turning over in sleep.

"Are you hers?" Wendell asked. "Old gods' woman? New?"

"I was a novitiate of the Radiant Choir before the flood took this place. I prayed to Serevan for deliverance while the water rose." She smiled without warmth. "He didn't answer. A fisherwoman of the old rites pulled me out with her bare hands. So draw your own conclusions about whose side I'm on, or don't. It won't change what I tell you."

"Then tell me."

She set down the net. Folded her hands. And when she spoke again, her voice had changed—flattened, widened, as if something larger were speaking through a very small door.

"The mountain god Orsen fell silent thirteen years past. You believe this was abandonment. The world believes this was death. Both are wrong. Orsen was *taken*."

Wendell's mouth went dry. In the cave shrine, in the dream that had not been a dream, they had seen fragments—a theft, chains, buried light. But fragments were not words, and words made it real.

"Taken by whom?"

"By its siblings. The old gods who survived the first war by hiding beneath it. They could not kill Orsen—mountains do not die easily—but they feared what it carried." The oracle's stone-pale eyes fixed on Wendell. "Its heart. Not the meat-and-muscle heart of a beast, little smuggler. The *heart*. The thing that let it answer prayers. The thing that let it make oaths binding. Divinity itself, drawn out and made separate, so it could be stolen and hidden and hoarded."

"They hid it," Wendell said slowly, "and sealed Orsen away so it couldn't go looking."

"And so no one else could either. They wrapped the location in layers of forgetting and scattered the key." She paused, and the pause had teeth. "You've been dreaming."

It wasn't a question. Wendell's hands curled at their sides. "I dream of a vault under Mount Ceren. Chains of prayer-iron. I dream of a betrayal at a table of stone, and faces I can't hold onto, and—" They stopped.

"And?"

"And I dream from *inside*," Wendell said quietly. "Not watching. Standing there. Like it's my memory."

The oracle nodded once, unsurprised, and Wendell felt the floor of the world tilt.

"No," they said. "No. I'm nobody. I'm a salt-runner. I swore an oath on a dead altar because everyone in my company did and it cost nothing—"

"It cost everything. That's how oaths work." She leaned forward. "When Orsen was sealed, it knew the seal would not hold forever. Nothing holds forever; even mountains erode. So it hid its secret the only way left to it. It pressed the memory of the vault's location into the souls of every soul sworn to it. Every mercenary who spoke the words on every broken altar. A thousand living locks. The church calls them 'those who hear voices.' The old gods call them keys."

"A thousand keys," Wendell said. "Then why me? Why hunt *me*?"

"Because a lock is only useful if it can be opened. Thirteen years the keys slept, useless, because Orsen's silence kept them dormant." The candles flared. "Three weeks ago, something answered your prayer. Something woke. And now the keys are turning, one by one, all across Thornfell—and every power that wants the heart, old or new, can feel them turning."

The water lapped against the stones. Very far away, or perhaps not far away at all, Wendell thought they heard the wolf breathe.

"What happens if they find all the keys?"

"Then someone walks into Mount Ceren and takes a god's heart out of the ground, and Thornfell burns the way it burned the first time, before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath." She said it flatly, a ledger entry, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "But that is not the clock you need to fear."

"There's another clock."

"There is always another clock." The oracle rose, joints cracking, and stepped lightly across the flooded altar as though the water beneath her were solid. "Orsen was imprisoned beneath the thirteenth moon's cycle—do you know the old reckoning? Not the thirteenth moon of a year. The thirteenth moon of the *world*. The hidden turn, the dark wheel, the one the new church scrubbed from every calendar. It turns once every few centuries, slow as a glacier, and it completes its turn soon. Days, perhaps. A fortnight at the outside."

"When it completes—"

"The seal breaks. On its own. Whether the heart is found or not, whether a single key has turned or all thousand. Orsen wakes in its prison with its strength returned but not its freedom, and it will *scream*, little smuggler, and every old god will hear it and know the war they buried is standing up out of the grave. The new gods will feel it too. And they will come to Thornfell to finish what their grandparents started, and they will not be gentle about finding the heart first."

Wendell stared at her. "So it doesn't matter what I do. The war comes either way."

"Oh, it matters." The oracle smiled, and there was something almost kind in it, and almost cruel, and both at once. "If the seal breaks and the heart is still hidden—if the keys are still scattered, sleeping, unturned—then the gods arrive to a locked room. They will tear the mountains apart looking, yes. But tearing takes time. Time is the only weapon mortals have ever had against gods, and you would have some of it. But if the heart is *found*—if some clever key leads them straight to it—" She spread her hands. "Then the war starts tomorrow, fully armed, and Thornfell is the battlefield."

"So the best thing I can do," Wendell said, and the words came out of them like splinters, "is nothing. Stay ignorant. Stay invisible. Let my own memory stay shut."

"That would be wisest."

"And I can't. Because the church is already reading toll records, and Maren sold my name, and the dreams are getting louder, and—" Wendell laughed, short and ugly. "Because I already half-know. Don't I? The vault under Ceren. The prayer-iron. I've known since the cave, and every night the knowing gets clearer, like a bruise coming up through skin."

The oracle regarded them for a long moment. Then she reached beneath the altar stone and brought out something small, wrapped in oilcloth, and held it out.

Wendell didn't take it. "What is it?"

"Open it and see."

They took it. The oilcloth was stiff with age. Inside lay a coin—or what had once been a coin—blackened, melted half out of shape, stamped with a mountain sigil worn nearly smooth. Wendell knew the sigil. Everyone who'd ever sworn to Orsen wore it, inked or scarred or branded, somewhere on their body. Wendell's own mark sat under their left collarbone and had been cold for thirteen years.

"This is an oath-token," Wendell said. "Company-issue. Where did—"

"It washed up from the water three days after your prayer was answered. First thing the chapel has given me in forty years." Her voice dropped. "There were others, little smuggler. Dozens. All over Thornfell, tokens going cold and then burning hot, dreams rising like floodwater. Your company. The Company of the Broken Peak. They're waking up too."

The bottom dropped out of Wendell's stomach. "Most of them are dead."

"Some are." The oracle's eyes were pitiless. "Some renounced their marks and think distance will save them. And some are alive, and dreaming the same dreams, and desperate, and frightened—and frightened men with a god's treasure in their heads are more dangerous than any inquisitor. You want allies, I can see it. Go find them, then. But understand what you're carrying to them. Every key you wake is a key the hunters can follow."

Above, on the ridge, faint as a held breath, the wolf began to howl—one long note, low, aimed at nothing Wendell could see. The candle flames bent toward the sound.

"What breaks the seal?" Wendell asked suddenly. "The thirteenth turn. Is there any way to stop it? Delay it? Anything?"

The oracle looked at them for a long time. Water dripped somewhere in the dark of the nave, patient as a second heartbeat.

"There are old stories," she said at last. "Stories about what a mortal might do with a god's heart, once found. Return it, and you crown a king. Destroy it, and you blind every god in Thornfell at once—old and new alike, their miracles and their monsters, all of it, gone quiet forever. Some say the destruction would take the oath-sworn with it." She tilted her head. "Would you burn, little smuggler, to keep the fire from spreading?"

"I don't know," Wendell said honestly. "Ask me when I'm holding it."

"Honesty. From an oath-swearer." She almost smiled. "Get out of my chapel. The stones are sinking faster tonight; they do that when I talk too long."

Wendell turned to go, then stopped, one foot on the first stepping stone. "One more thing. The wolf that's been following me. Do you know what it is?"

For the first time, the oracle hesitated. She looked up, through the broken ceiling, toward the ridge where the howl had faded, and something in her riverstone face flickered—recognition, or grief, or both.

"I know what it isn't," she said. "It isn't hunting you. Beyond that, ask it yourself. It's waited longer than you have."

* * *

Wendell climbed out of the quarry with the melted token in their fist and their thoughts in ruins.

A thousand keys. A clock ticking in a calendar that had been erased. Their own skull, a locked room full of somebody else's treasure. And somewhere behind them, a company of ghosts waking up one by one, each of them now a door the church could kick in.

The wolf was waiting at the top of the ridge, sitting exactly where Wendell had left it, as though the intervening hours had been a moment's absence. Its amber eyes dropped to the token in Wendell's hand.

Wendell held it up between two fingers. "You knew. Didn't you. What I am. What's inside my head." The wolf's ears tipped forward. It made no move, gave no sound, offered nothing but that steady, patient attention that was somehow worse than speech. "Everyone keeps telling me to ask you questions. The bandits' corpses, practically. Now a madwoman in a flooded church. Well, I'm asking. What are you?"

The wolf stood. Stretched, unhurried, joints popping softly in the cold. Then it turned and walked three paces along the ridgeline and stopped, and looked back over its shoulder—not at Wendell's face, but southward. Toward the low country. Toward Greyhollow, and the roads beyond, and the old campgrounds where the Company of the Broken Peak had once pitched its banners.

Toward the surviving keys.

"You want me to find them," Wendell said. "Before Vael does. Before anyone does."

The wolf held its gaze south, amber eyes catching the risen moon, and Wendell felt the mark beneath their collarbone warm against their skin like a coal being breathed on—and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that whatever happened next would not wait for them to be ready.

They tightened their cloak, checked the knife at their hip, and followed the wolf south into the dark.

Behind them, far below, the last of the drowned chapel's candles went out, one by one, as the stones sank another hand's width into the waiting water.
