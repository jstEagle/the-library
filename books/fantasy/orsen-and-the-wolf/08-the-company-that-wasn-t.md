## 8. The Company That Wasn't

The company had been called the Grey Oath, once, and its banner—a wolf's head split by a sword—had hung over a hundred battlefields before Orsen went quiet and the oaths went sour.

Wendell found what was left of it in a river town called Ferrow's Bend, three days' hard walk south of the drowned chapel, following a rumor that cost them their last silver. The rumor was half right. There was a company in Ferrow's Bend. It just wasn't called anything anymore.

They found it in the yard of a tannery gone to rot, where six men drilled with wooden staves in the grey morning light, and a seventh sat on a barrel watching them with the patience of a man who had nothing better to wait for. The banner above the gate was the old one, but the wolf's head had been painted over—whitewashed, then painted again with a sunburst, the mark of the Church of the Radiant Choir. Someone had done it badly. The wolf's ears still showed through, like something buried under snow.

Wendell stood at the gate a long time before they walked in.

Thirteen years ago there had been forty of them. Forty sworn spears of the Grey Oath, bound by blood and salt to the mountain god who kept the high passes safe and asked nothing for it but remembrance. Wendell had been nineteen and stupid with grief when they'd sworn—fresh from burying a mother the new gods' priests wouldn't consecrate because she'd died unshriven—and Orsen's altar had been the only door left open. *You will not be alone,* the god had said, or the mountain had said it through the god; after thirteen years of silence Wendell could no longer separate the voice from the echo. *While you keep my roads, I keep your name.*

Now the roads were lawless, the altars were moss-choked, and the man on the barrel was Brannic Hale, who had been sergeant of the second spear when Wendell was a raw recruit, and who looked up at them now with eyes that took a full three seconds to remember their face.

"Wendell Zhou." He said it like he was testing whether the name still held weight. "Thought you were dead."

"Worked hard at it."

He laughed, and it was almost the old laugh, and for one dangerous moment Wendell let themselves believe this might be easy. Then Brannic's gaze dropped to the oath-marks on Wendell's forearms—the faded grey lines that every sworn soldier carried—and his laugh died in his throat.

"You're marked," he said quietly. "Still marked."

"Never burned off."

Brannic looked down at his own arms. Bare. Clean. The skin where his marks had been was smooth as river stone, and Wendell knew without asking what that smoothness had cost. Unswearing was not a ceremony the old gods offered gently. You carved the marks out yourself, or you paid a hedge-priest of the Choir to do it with blessed iron, and either way you screamed, and either way the scar never quite matched the rest of your skin.

"The others?" Wendell asked, though the empty yard already answered.

"Dace died at Marlow Ford, year two of the silence. Fever." Brannic ticked names off against his knee, each one landing like a stone in still water. "Osk and Tammera took the Choir's pardon and farm outside Vail. Renounced. Halloran—" He stopped. Started again. "Halloran hanged himself off the Kestrel bridge. Left a note that just said *he isn't listening anymore.* The rest scattered. Some renounced, some just... stopped saying the name." He spread his hands. "This is it. Six men drilling with sticks because if we don't drill at something we'll drink ourselves into the river. We take caravan guard work now. Honest work." His mouth twisted around the word. "For the Choir's toll-houses, mostly. They pay well."

"They pay in coin that says their god's face on it," Wendell said.

"Coin spends the same." Brannic stood, and up close Wendell could see how much of him was new: the belly softening under the mail, the grey coming in at the temples, the fine tremor in his left hand that he tucked against his thigh when he noticed Wendell noticing. "So. Thirteen years of nothing from you, and now you walk into my yard looking like a man who's been sleeping in ditches, with fresh oath-marks burning on your arms like the silence never happened. And behind you—" his eyes flicked past Wendell's shoulder, to the tree line, and narrowed, "—there's a wolf the size of a pony sitting in the shadows like it's holding the door."

"He's with me."

"That's not an answer, Zhou. That's a problem wearing an answer's clothes."

* * *

They talked in the tannery's cold hearth-room, over a fire Brannic built small, the way men build fires when they've learned fuel is a thing that runs out. The six drill-men drifted in and settled along the walls, and Wendell recognized faces under the weathering—Pietr, who'd been the company cook; young Aldous, who wasn't young anymore; a woman named Sefa whose scarred jaw Wendell remembered from the pass campaigns. All of them bare-armed. All of them unsworn. All of them watching Wendell's marks the way starving men watch bread.

Wendell told them everything.

Not all of it—they kept the heart's location locked behind their teeth even from themselves, a door in their own memory that the oracle had sealed with a word—but enough. The answering voice at the ruined altar. The church's rewards for anyone who heard voices. Maren's betrayal. The wolf. The dreams of theft and burial and betrayal among the gods themselves. The thirteenth moon, turning toward its final quarter, and the seal that would break whether anyone was ready or not.

When they finished, the hearth-room was silent except for the fire's small eating sounds.

"So the old gods are coming back," Pietr said finally. "And the new ones are going to fight them. And it'll be a war like the last one, which none of us are old enough to remember but all of us grew up on the stories of. Fields of salt. Rivers running wrong for a generation."

"That's the shape of it."

"And you came here," Brannic said slowly, "to what? Raise the Grey Oath one more time? Forty spears against a war between gods?"

"I came here because you swore the same oath I did. Because when Orsen fell silent, we were told to hold faith until the mountain spoke again. The mountain is speaking. Through me, maybe, but speaking." Wendell leaned forward. "I need people who remember what we promised. People who can watch my back while I find what the god lost. That's all. I'm not asking you to fight heaven. I'm asking you to help me stop heaven from fighting."

Sefa snorted. Aldous looked at his boots. Pietr studied the fire like it owed him money.

It was Brannic who spoke, and when he did, his voice had changed—lower, careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice he intended to test first.

"There were eleven of us who unswore. Did you know that? Eleven, in the first two years, when it became clear the god wasn't coming back. Carving out the marks ourselves because no hedge-priest would touch old-rite ink." He turned his forearm over, showed the pale scar-ghosts. "I did mine with a boat-hook blade and a jug of grain spirit. Took four nights. You know why I did it, Zhou?"

"Because he stopped listening."

"Because *you* stopped listening." Brannic's eyes came up, and there was something in them Wendell had never seen there before—not anger, exactly. Hunger wearing anger's coat. "We were told to hold faith. We held. And then the company started dying—fever, bandits, a bridge collapse that should have killed one man and killed five—and the god said *nothing.* Not a warning. Not a comfort. Nothing. And I sat where you're sitting and I thought: what am I keeping faith *with?* A memory? A habit?" He shook his head. "No. I unswore. And I'll tell you the truth, since you gave us yours: the night the marks came out, I slept better than I had in years."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the wolf made a single low sound—not a growl, more like a bell struck once underwater—and everyone in the room flinched except Wendell.

"But," Brannic said.

Everyone waited on the *but.*

"But you're marked, and the marks are burning fresh, and that means the god's power is moving again, which means whatever you're carrying is worth more than every toll-house in Thornfell." He leaned forward, mirroring Wendell, close enough that Wendell could smell the grain spirit on his breath at midmorning. "The Choir pays fifty crowns for word of a voice-hearer. What do you think they'd pay for the person the old god talks *through?* What do you think the old god's own faithful would pay, the ones hiding in the hills, the ones who never unswore, the ones praying into the dark for thirteen years waiting for exactly this?"

"I'm not for sale, Brannic."

"Everything's for sale. I run a company now; I know the market." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the mess-tent, the one that had made recruits trust him, and Wendell hated how well it still worked. "But I'm not selling you. I'm offering to buy *in.* Take me with you. Me and whoever of these fools volunteers—which will be all of them, because they're bored and broke and I tell them stories about the old days until they bleed nostalgia. I'll put my spears between you and the Choir's hunters. I know how Inquisitors work; I've guarded their caravans, I've drunk their wine, I know which ones travel heavy and which ones travel smart. And in exchange—"

"In exchange for what?"

"For a share." His voice dropped to almost a whisper, and the whole room leaned in. "Whatever the god lost. Whatever's buried under whatever mountain. When you find it—when the old power comes back into the world—I want my piece. My portion of the divine. Enough to matter. Enough that no one ever carves my marks out again, because I'll never need marks to be safe."

The words sat in the air like smoke.

Wendell thought of the dream: the heart in its chains of prayer-iron, and the other gods circling it, and how each of their faces had worn the same expression Brannic wore now. They had all wanted a piece. That was how the war started, the first time. Not with hatred. With hunger.

"That's not treasure," Wendell said quietly. "What's buried down there isn't coin. It's the thing the other gods went to war over. It doesn't share. It *consumes.*"

"Then it consumes me rich." Brannic spread his hands, magnanimous, terrible. "My risk. My soul. My choice. Those are the terms, Zhou. Take them or walk out my gate and take your chances alone with the Inquisitor who's already bought your route from your handler. Oh yes—" he saw Wendell's face change, and nodded, unsurprised, almost gentle. "Word travels. Greyhollow to Ferrow's Bend in nine days, if the rider's good. A woman named Vael. They say she doesn't sleep. They say she reconstructed your whole road from toll ledgers and ferry receipts like she was reading a novel." He stood, joints cracking, and offered his hand across the fire. "So. Alone, with her riders closing from the north? Or with me, and mine, and a chance?"

Wendell looked at the hand.

Thirteen years ago, in another life, they would have taken it without thinking. Sergeant Brannic Hale, who taught them how to hold a shield line, who carried them bleeding down the Kestrel switchbacks when they took a bolt in the thigh, who sang the old marching songs in a voice like gravel and honey. The hand that had pulled them out of the river at Marlow Ford.

The same hand. Different man behind it. Or maybe the same man, and it had always been this way underneath, and the oath had only been holding the lid on.

Outside, through the cracked shutter, Wendell could see the wolf standing at the yard's edge, grey against grey, watching the window with eyes the color of deep water. It did not growl. It did not move. It simply watched, the way a judge watches.

Wendell took the hand.

"One share," they said. "Promised. But hear me, Brannic—if it comes down to the heart or the world, I choose the world, and I'll put you down myself if you reach for it when I say leave it."

Brannic's grip tightened, brief and hard, and his smile never moved.

"Fair terms," he said. "Company, form up! We ride within the hour. Dust off the old gear—we're going into the mountains, boys and girls, back to the high passes." He turned to Wendell, and for just a moment, under the hunger, under the calculation, Wendell saw something else flicker—something that might have been the man from Marlow Ford, surfacing like a drowning swimmer. "For the Grey Oath," he said softly.

"For the Grey Oath," the six echoed, and some of them even sounded like they meant it.

* * *

They rode north by afternoon, eight horses and one wolf that ran the ridgelines parallel to the road, always visible, always distant. Brannic watched it through a stolen spyglass for most of the first hour and finally lowered the glass with a frown.

"It's not hunting us," he said. "It's *escorting* us. Like it knows the route."

"It does."

"How?"

Wendell watched the wolf pause on a granite shoulder, silhouetted against the climbing moon—the thirteenth moon, fat now, two turns from full—and felt the door in their own memory shift in its frame, patient as a held breath.

"Same way I do," they said.

By dusk the first shrine appeared beside the road—an old one, moss-eaten, its wolf-carving barely legible—and Wendell saw several of the company make signs against it, quick and guilty, the Choir's sunburst traced on their breasts. But Sefa, riding rear guard, slowed as she passed it. Her hand came up. Hovered. Almost touched the stone wolf's muzzle.

She rode on without touching it. But she looked back twice.

That night, camped under the leaning crags of the Ceren foothills, Wendell woke near midnight to the sound of voices. Brannic sat apart from the sleeping fire, talking low with Pietr, heads together, and though the wind stole the words, Wendell caught the shape of them—the cadence of men dividing something in advance. Shares. Portions. Pieces.

On the ridge above the camp, the wolf lifted its head and looked at Wendell across the dark, and its eyes held no surprise at all.

Two days later, at the mouth of the pass below Mount Ceren, they found the first sign nailed to a waystone: white parchment, sunburst seal, neat black letters offering sanctuary, pardon, and fifty crowns for information regarding *the voice-bearer known as Wendell Zhou.*

Below it, fresher, written in charcoal by a shaking hand, someone had scrawled a single line:

*SHE IS ALREADY ON THE MOUNTAIN.*
