## 9. Hunted Through Thornfell

The first arrow came out of the dawn mist and buried itself in the pine trunk a hand's width from Wendell's ear.

They didn't stop to look. Thirteen years on the smuggler's roads had taught them the arithmetic of survival: every second spent looking was a second the archer spent aiming. They threw themselves down the scree slope instead, boots skidding on frost-slick stone, arms windmilling, letting gravity do what fear could not—move them faster than thought.

Behind them, above them, horns. Three notes, then silence. Vael's riders signaling position. Wendell had heard those horns in another life, in another company, when they had been the one riding down deserters through passes like these. The sound had never changed. It still sounded like the sky closing.

*Left,* said something in their mind—not a voice, not quite. A pressure. A certainty.

Wendell went left without deciding to.

The slope ended in a drop they hadn't known was there, thirty feet of granite falling away into a stream-choked ravine. Any other morning they would have stopped, scouted, found the goat-track that wound down in switchbacks. This morning they jumped for the pine growing out of the cliff face, caught it, felt bark tear skin off their palms, and dropped the last twenty feet into cold water that punched the breath out of them.

The horn sounded again—closer now, confused. The riders had lost the line of the trail.

Wendell lay in the shallows, chest heaving, and looked up through the branches at the grey shape pacing along the rim above them. The wolf. It had led them left. It had known about the drop before they did.

"You're going to get me killed," Wendell rasped at it.

The wolf looked down at them with eyes the color of old ice, and if a wolf could be unimpressed, this one managed it.

* * *

Sera Vael stood at the notch where the trail split and let her riders fan out around her, and she did not allow herself the luxury of frustration.

Three days. Three days of toll records and shepherd interviews and reading the mountains the way her mother had taught her to read scripture—patiently, line by line, trusting that meaning was there for anyone humble enough to keep looking. Wendell Zhou was good. Better than good; the smuggler moved through Thornfell like water finding cracks, leaving almost nothing. Almost.

But almost was a word, and words were Vael's country.

"Blood on the rock," she said, crouching by the pine at the cliff's edge. Bark stripped, fresh. Sap still wet. "They came down fast. Desperate." She touched the torn wood, then held her fingers up to the light. "And here—" a smear on the lowest branch, dark against the bark. "They hurt themselves. That matters."

"How, Inquisitor?" asked Brother Callen, young and eager, his horse stamping in the cold.

"A person who is merely fleeing makes clean choices or dies making dirty ones. This—" she nodded at the jump, the torn hands, "—is someone being *driven*. Not by us. We were behind them, not above them. Something else pushed them off that cliff." She straightened, scanning the ridgeline, the mist, the places where shadow pooled like standing water. "Something they trust enough to obey."

"The wolf," Callen said. "The shepherds keep mentioning a wolf."

"The shepherds keep mentioning a wolf that walks the high passes in winter, alone, that no trap holds and no hunter shoots." Vael mounted, and her voice stayed level, but something beneath it tightened. "In the old days, they'd have called that a servant of Orsen. In the old days, they'd have been right to."

She believed what she had always believed: that the old gods were embers in a house full of dry timber, and that mercy meant drowning them now, in the dark, before they could catch. Every prayer answered since the thaw was a door opening onto a war that had killed one in three people in Thornfell the last time it burned. Her sister had died in the last one. Not to gods—to men carrying gods' names like knives.

She would not watch it again. Whatever it cost.

"Ride for the Kestrel road," she said. "Zhou will run for the drowned chapel. People who learn terrible things run to people who explain them. It's the oldest pattern there is." She turned her horse east, into the wind. "We'll be there first."

* * *

By the second night, Wendell understood that they were dying slowly and that the wolf knew it too.

Not from wounds—the palms had scabbed, the ankle wrenched in the ravine had settled into a dull complaint. From hunger, mostly, and cold, and the particular exhaustion of sleeping in snatches with one ear open, which was no sleep at all. They had eaten nothing but a handful of frozen bilberries and half a raw trout since Greyhollow. Their thoughts had begun to move like honey in January.

They were climbing toward the high shrine caves above Lake Merren when the wolf finally made its move.

It had been pacing them all day, visible and invisible, sometimes a silhouette on a ridge, sometimes nothing at all—and Wendell had stopped flinching at it somewhere past noon, which frightened them more than the flinching had. Now, as the light failed and the temperature fell like a blade, the wolf stepped directly into their path and sat down. Waiting.

"I don't speak wolf," Wendell said.

The wolf stood, walked ten paces along a trail Wendell had dismissed as a drainage gully, and looked back.

The gully turned out to be a path. The path climbed to a shelf of rock behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, and inside the shelf was a den—old, lined with generations of shed underfur pressed into the stone like felt, warm in the way only deep rock is warm. There was even a cache: a marmot, frozen, and beside it something that made Wendell's throat close—a clay jar, sealed with pitch, holding smoked fish and a strip of dried apple. Human food. Kept fresh. Kept *for* someone.

For thirteen years? Wendell didn't ask. They ate, wrapped themselves in the oath-cloak, and slept six hours without dreaming for the first time since the altar.

When they woke, the wolf was watching them from the mouth of the den, and the moonlight made its fur silver, and Wendell heard themselves say, quietly, "Thank you"—and realized, with a strange falling sensation, that they meant it, and that the wolf knew they meant it, and that neither of them found this strange anymore.

* * *

Vael lost them at the lake, and it cost her a rider.

Not dead—Dain's horse had gone down in a snow-bridge collapse over a hidden stream, and the man had crawled out with a broken collarbone and a story about how the bridge hadn't looked broken until his horse stepped on it. As if the mountain had decided. As if the mountain were *choosing sides*.

Vael sat her horse on the lakeshore in the blue hour before dawn and studied the far slopes, and admitted to herself what she had been refusing to admit for two days.

They weren't tracking a smuggler anymore. They were tracking something the mountain itself was hiding. Streams froze and unfroze to erase hoofprints. Rockslides closed trails behind their quarry and opened others ahead. Twice her scouts had sworn they saw their man clearly—once on a ridgeline, once crossing a scree field—and both times, by the time the company arrived, there was nothing but wind and the enormous indifferent silence of Thornfell.

Except it wasn't indifferent. That was the thing she couldn't put in a report. The mountain was paying attention.

"Inquisitor." Callen reined up beside her, breath steaming. "The oracle's chapel. If Zhou's making for it, we should push hard—we can beat them there."

"Yes," Vael said. And then, because she was honest with herself if no one else: "And when we take them, Callen, I want you to remember this morning. Remember that the ground opened under Dain and not under me. Whatever answers prayers in these hills has an opinion about what we're doing. I want you to decide, before we reach that chapel, whether your faith can survive being *disliked* by something old."

Callen's face went carefully blank, the way young men's faces do when they've been asked a question they'll spend years answering.

Vael turned east. Behind them, on the highest ridge, something grey watched them go, and did not follow, because following was not its work. Its work was elsewhere—was with the exhausted figure limping up the pilgrim stair toward the drowned chapel, teaching it, hour by hour, mile by mile, to trust.

* * *

Brannic caught up with them on the third day, and Wendell nearly put a knife in him before recognizing the beard.

He came up the trail from the south with two pack-mules and a face full of apology, hands wide and empty, calling out "Zhou! Zhou, in the Lady's name, hold!"—and Wendell, who had not slept properly in four days, who had been shot at and drowned and hunted across half a mountain range, felt something in their chest crack sideways between relief and rage.

"You sold me out?" they said. "No—you *found* me. Maren sold me out. You just—" They gestured at him, at the mules, at the sheer audacity of his existence. "You followed me across Thornfell."

"To help." Brannic set down the lead rope and spread his hands. "I heard what happened in Greyhollow. I heard what the church is paying. And I thought—there's maybe five of us left who swore the oath, Zhou. Five. And one of them's being hunted by the whole resurgent church, and nobody lifts a hand? I couldn't live with it." His voice roughened. "We were a company once. We were *brothers*."

"We were mercenaries," Wendell said. "Brothers is what we called it so we could sleep at night."

But they took his dried meat, and they let him walk with them toward the drowned chapel, because exhaustion makes companions of devils, and because some small starved part of them wanted it to be true—that the oath still meant something, that they were not alone.

The wolf watched Brannic the entire day with flat yellow attention, and would not come within fifty yards of him, and when Wendell asked why, the answer came as pressure and distaste, wordless and absolute, like a hand pushing against the back of their neck.

That night, camped under an overhang while sleet ticked against the rocks, Wendell woke to voices pitched low. Brannic, sitting up, talking to himself—or praying, though to whom, with the old gods stirring and the new ones listening, Wendell couldn't guess. Then they caught the words, and wished they hadn't.

"*...power enough to buy back everything. Everything they took from us, Zhou. You'll see. When we find it, you'll understand why I came.*"

Wendell lay still in the dark and did not sleep again, and the wolf's eyes glowed faintly at the edge of the firelight, watching Brannic the way a lighthouse watches a reef.

* * *

The drowned chapel announced itself by smell: cold water and stone rot and, underneath, something older—incense burned so long ago it had become part of the walls. It sat in a fold of the mountain where a spring had risen and swallowed it knee-deep centuries ago, and the oracle lived in the belfry, above the waterline, among pigeons and the ghosts of hymns.

Wendell went in alone. The wolf refused the threshold entirely, hackles up, and stood in the falling snow outside like a sentinel, facing back down the trail they'd come by.

Inside, the oracle was younger than Wendell expected and stranger—an androgynous figure with riverwater eyes, sitting in a rotted pew with their feet in the flood, as if the water were a fire they warmed themself at.

"You brought it with you," the oracle said, before Wendell spoke. "The knowing. It leaks. I could smell it on the trail from here." They tilted their head. "Thirteen turns, oath-bearer. The thirteenth moon is more than half spent. When it completes its turning, the seal breaks—heart or no heart, found or unfound. The prison opens either way. The only question left in the world is what's standing next to the door when it does."

"And the heart. Where—"

"Is locked in your skull." The oracle smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was an honest one. "Orsen hid its own secret in the only vault the new gods couldn't breach: a mortal memory, bound by oath. You dreamed the theft. You know the mountain. You know the vault under Ceren. You've known since the cave shrine, and you've been running from the knowledge ever since, because knowing makes you real, and real things can be killed."

Outside, faintly, carried on the wind off the snowfield, a horn sounded. Three notes. Then a fourth.

The oracle's smile faded. "Your wolf has been trying to tell you all day," they said. "Riders. Many. Coming up the valley faster than you came down it." They drew their feet from the water. "There's a way out the north side, through the ossuary, onto the Weeping Stair. It's a bad road. It's also the only road."

Wendell was already moving.

They burst from the chapel into snow and failing light, and the wolf was waiting, body low, ears flat—and beyond it, down the valley, torches. A dozen. Two dozen. A thin bright chain of them winding up the pilgrim road, closing like a noose drawn slow.

And behind Wendell, from inside the chapel, Brannic's voice, rising with something that was not fear and was worse than fear:

"There it is," he breathed. "There's the army of the new gods, and there's the heart's location sitting in your head, and there's me, Zhou. There's *me*, and I'm the only friend you've got left. So you're going to tell me where that vault is. Right now. Before they take you, or after—but you're going to tell me."

The wolf growled, low and endless, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

Above them, between the peaks, the thirteenth moon rose swollen and wrong-colored, three-quarters turned, and everywhere along the ridgelines of Thornfell, in shrines and hollows and drowned chapels, old altars began, very softly, to bleed light.
