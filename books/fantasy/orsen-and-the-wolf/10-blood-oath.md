## 10. Blood Oath

The Weeping Stair had earned its name honestly.

Wendell had crossed it twice in their life, both times in summer, both times with dry boots and a full purse, and both times they had thought the waterfalls that sheeted down the cliff face merely picturesque—a thousand small streams tumbling from the snowfields above, threading through moss and stone, falling a hundred feet into the gorge below. Now, in the last week of autumn, with the temperature dropping toward ice and Vael's riders somewhere behind them on the high road, the Stair was something else entirely: a wall of wet black rock rising out of the mist, slick as oiled glass, carved with steps so old that no one remembered who had cut them or why.

The wolf stopped at the base of the cliff and looked up.

Wendell looked too, and felt their stomach drop. The steps climbed the face of the falls themselves—cut directly into the stone where the water ran, so that anyone ascending would climb inside the waterfall, boots finding purchase on stone that wept constantly under their feet. In summer it would be cold but possible. In late autumn, with the spray already stiffening to rime at the edges—

"We're not climbing that," Wendell said aloud. They'd been talking to the wolf for days now. It seemed rude not to.

The wolf turned its head and gave them a look that was unmistakably *yes, we are*.

Behind them, down the valley, a horn sounded. Thin and clear, the church's hunting call, three notes rising. Wendell had learned its grammar over nine days of flight: one note for sighting, two for regrouping, three for *we have them boxed*. Three notes meant Vael's outriders had reached the mouth of the gorge behind them. And ahead, Wendell knew from years of running contraband through these mountains, the gorge narrowed to a dead end within two miles—the Broken Bowl, a rockfall basin that smugglers avoided precisely because there was no way out of it except back the way you came.

Which left the Stair.

"Of course," Wendell muttered. "Of course it's the Stair."

They climbed.

It was worse than they'd feared. The first thirty steps were merely treacherous, the stone greased with runoff, each foothold a negotiation between boot leather and wet granite. By the fiftieth step the spray was hitting them sideways, driven by wind funneling up the gorge, and the cold went through Wendell's coat as if the coat were made of rumor. Their fingers went numb around the handholds. Twice their foot skated off a ledge entirely and their whole weight swung out over the void before some desperate grip caught them—and both times, a grey shape flowed past them on the rock, impossibly sure-footed, pressing close enough that Wendell could feel warmth radiating off its fur, as if the wolf were less bothered by the cold than any living thing had a right to be.

Halfway up, Wendell made the mistake of looking down.

The gorge floor was a hundred feet below, a churn of white water and black boulders. On the trail at its mouth, small as toys, riders were dismounting. Red-and-white tabards. The colors of the Radiant Concord, bright even through the mist. One of them pointed upward.

"They've seen us," Wendell gasped. "Wolf—they've *seen* us—"

The wolf did not slow. If anything it climbed faster, and Wendell, hauling themselves up the wet stone after it, understood with a strange clarity that the wolf was not fleeing. It was herding them upward. Toward something.

Toward the top, where the Stair ended—not in open ground, as Wendell had hoped, but in a shelf of stone barely wider than a wagon, backed by an overhang of cliff, fronted by nothing but air. A ledge. A trap with a view.

And standing on the shelf, half-swallowed by moss and lichen, was a shrine.

* * *

Wendell knew old shrines. They'd spent thirteen years walking past them—the little cairns and carved niches where the mountain folk had once left offerings to gods who never answered. Most were anonymous, worn smooth by weather, their dedications eroded to suggestion. This one was not worn. This one had been *hidden*, tucked beneath the overhang where the rain couldn't reach, and the carving above its shallow basin was crisp as the day it was cut:

A mountain, crowned with antlers. Beneath it, in the old high script that Wendell had only ever seen scratched into oath-stones:

**ORSEN WHO HOLDS. ORSEN WHO HEARS. ORSEN WHO REMEMBERS.**

The wolf sat down before the basin and waited.

"No," Wendell said. Water ran down their face; whether it was spray or sweat, they couldn't have said. "No, I know what you're asking. I'm not doing that. I don't even know what *that* is, not really—I saw it once, when I was nineteen, and I swore I'd never—"

Down the cliff face, faint through the roar of the falls, came the ring of iron-shod boots on stone. The riders were climbing.

The wolf looked at them. And this time, for the first time since Kestrel Notch, since the cave shrine, since all the long days of watching and saving and silently judging, the wolf made a sound—not a growl, not a bark, but a low, resonant note that came up through the stone itself, through Wendell's boots, through the bones of their legs, and landed somewhere behind their sternum like a key turning in a lock.

*I am running out of time,* the sound said, without words. *We are running out of time.*

"I know," Wendell whispered. "I know you are. I dreamed it—the seal, the moons, all of it. Thirteen turns and then—" They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "And then everything wakes up and starts killing everything else. I *know*."

The boots rang closer. Wendell counted the climbers by sound: four, maybe five, spread across the lower steps. Vael herself wouldn't be among them—she kept her crossbowmen back and sent her blades forward—but her blades were quite capable of killing them on this shelf without her supervision.

"But this—" Wendell gestured at the basin, at the knife-shaped groove beside it, at the wolf. "This is the old rite. The blood rite. My grandmother told me stories about it. You don't just swear an oath with blood—you *give* yourself. All the way down. There's no taking it back. There's no being invisible anymore. Whoever swears becomes—" They faltered. "*Known.* To the god. To whatever's left of the god. Forever."

The wolf's ears flattened. Its eyes—amber, patient, unbearably sad—held theirs.

*You have been invisible your whole life,* the stone-sound said gently. *Has it kept you safe?*

Wendell thought about Maren's face in the lamplight of Greyhollow, tear-streaked and resolute, selling them out to cover her debts. About Brannic's handshake, warm and calculating. About the toll records, the wanted notices, the church's reward for anyone who heard voices. About thirteen years of silence purchased at the price of every prayer they'd swallowed, every altar they'd walked past, every night they'd lain awake wondering if the god they'd sworn to at nineteen had ever known their name.

"No," Wendell said. "It hasn't."

Below, the first rider's helm broke the lip of the falls, forty steps down, water streaming off the steel.

Wendell drew their belt knife.

It wasn't a ceremonial blade. It was a smuggler's knife, short and ugly, notched from cutting rope and opening crates of contraband salt. But it was also, Wendell realized as they turned it in the grey light, the same knife they'd carried at nineteen, re-hafted twice but the same steel, because some part of them had never been able to throw away anything from the day they'd knelt at the ruined altar and sworn to a god who didn't answer. The oath-blade. Brannic had called it that once, drunk, laughing, and Wendell had let him believe he was joking.

They weren't joking now.

"All right," Wendell said. "All right. But we do it properly. If I'm going to stop being nobody, I'm going to do it right." They knelt at the basin, knees cracking with cold, and pulled the wolf's foreleg toward them. The wolf offered it without resistance, without hesitation, as if it had been waiting thirteen years—or longer—for exactly this.

Wendell set the blade against the pad of the great paw.

"For what it's worth," they said, "I think you're more than a beast. I've thought it since Kestrel Notch. Maybe since the pass. So this isn't me binding an animal. Understand? Whatever you are—whatever's left of what you were—I'm naming you *companion*. Equal under the rite. That's the old law. Companion, not servant. Not beast."

The wolf exhaled, long and slow, and the sound of it was almost human.

*Companion,* the stone agreed.

Wendell cut.

The wolf didn't flinch. Blood welled dark and shockingly red against the grey fur, and Wendell caught it in the basin—three drops, then five, then seven, the old odd numbers, the numbers of holding—and then drew the blade across their own palm, deep enough to matter, and let their own blood fall into the pool until the two mingled past telling apart.

Then they spoke the words. The ones from nineteen years ago, the ones they'd sworn they'd forgotten and hadn't, not one syllable:

*"By blood given freely, by name spoken truly, by path shared to the ending—Orsen who holds, hear me. Orsen who hears, keep me. Orsen who remembers, remember me. What is mine is yours. What is yours is mine. From this breath to the last breath. Companion to companion. So sworn, so sealed, so done."*

They pressed their bleeding palm into the basin, into the mingled blood, and the world came apart.

* * *

Pain first. Pain beyond anything Wendell had a name for—not the pain of flesh but the pain of *geography*, of mountains being torn up by the roots, of a heart ripped from a chest the size of a range. They were falling and burning and drowning all at once, and underneath the pain, vast and slow and grieving, moved something enormous: a consciousness like winter, like starlight, like the pressure at the bottom of a glacier lake.

*Thirteen years,* it grieved. *Thirteen years in the dark, and the dark has a shape, and the shape is my own body walking without me.*

Wendell saw it all in the space between two heartbeats.

A council of gods beneath the roots of Mount Ceren, seven shapes of light arguing around a table of living stone. Orsen standing apart, mountain-shouldered, refusing. The theft—not a battle but a *betrayal*, hands they trusted closing around their heart while they slept the godsleep, chains of prayer-iron forged from ten thousand faithful whispers. The burial. The sealing. The long silence mistaken by every mortal in Thornfell for abandonment.

And threaded through all of it, unbearable, intimate, the wolf's memory: fourteen years of four-legged flight, of sleeping in snow, of watching a young mercenary kneel at a ruined altar and swear an oath to a silent god, and wanting—with a hunger that had nothing divine in it at all, that was purely and terribly *personal*—to answer.

*I heard you,* the grief said. *Every prayer you didn't pray. Every time you walked past and kept your mouth shut to stay safe. I heard the silence you made instead of prayers, and it was the loudest thing in my prison.*

"Stop," Wendell gasped aloud, on the shelf, in the rain, blood still pressed to stone. "Stop, it's too much, I can't—"

*Yes,* said the bond, calm as bedrock. *You can. That is what the rite means. That is what you swore.*

The pain crested—and then, instead of breaking them, it *settled*, the way a flood settles into a riverbed, finding channels that had apparently been waiting in Wendell all along. Channels cut by nineteen-year-old vows. By thirteen years of unspoken loyalty. By every mile they'd walked these mountains knowing them better than any priest or soldier ever would.

When Wendell opened their eyes, they were still kneeling at the basin. The rain still fell. But they could feel the mountain now—not metaphorically, not in poetry, but the way a man feels his own hand. The weight of the snowpack above. The slow grind of the deep rock. The thirteenth moon, invisible behind cloud, dragging its final turn across the sky like a key scraping in a rusted lock.

And they could feel the wolf—no. They could feel *Orsen*. Small and mortal-fleshed and kneeling beside them, bleeding from the paw, burning with a patience that had lasted fourteen years and was finally, finally almost spent.

"You're dying," Wendell said. They didn't know how they knew. They knew completely. "The mortal body. It can't hold what's left of you much longer. That's why the rite. That's why *now*."

The wolf looked at them, and did not deny it.

Boots scraped on stone. Wendell turned, rising, and found the first rider pulling himself onto the shelf—Vael's man, sword drawn, water streaming from him, his face set in the grim efficiency of someone who had chased one target across nine days of mountain and intended to finish it.

He looked at Wendell. He looked at the wolf. He looked at the blood in the basin, and the old script above it, and something flickered across his face—the instinctive unease of a new-god's soldier confronted with old-rite blood, still wet, still smoking faintly with power that his church insisted was dead.

"Mercenary Zhou," he said. "Inquisitor Vael orders you taken alive. The creature can be put down."

Wendell flexed their cut hand. Blood dripped from their fingers onto the shrine stone, and where it fell, the moss *grew*, green and sudden, spreading in a handprint shape.

"No," Wendell said, and their voice came out doubled, layered, their own rough timbre underlaid with something deep and tectonic. "I don't think she does. And I don't think you want to try."

The soldier hesitated—which was, Wendell understood now, feeling the fear move through the man like current through wire, the correct instinct. Below them on the Stair, the other riders had stopped climbing. Through the bond, Wendell felt what they felt: the mountain itself shifting its attention toward them, the whole Weeping Stair suddenly aware, suddenly *awake*, waterfalls pausing mid-fall like held breath.

The soldier took one step backward. Then another. Then he turned and went down the cliff as fast as a man could go without simply jumping.

Wendell stood alone on the shelf with their companion, breathing hard, watching the mist swallow the retreating riders. Somewhere far below, a horn sounded again—one note. Sighting withdrawn. Falling back to report.

They would report to Vael. And Vael, who was not a fool, would understand exactly what had happened on this shelf, and she would come back with everything she had.

Wendell looked down at their palm. The cut had already closed—not healed, exactly, but *sealed*, the skin drawn together in a thin silver line shaped like a mountain crowned with antlers. An oath-mark. The first of many, if the old stories were true. They would burn when the god burned. They would break when the god broke.

They had stopped being a bystander. There was no invisible now. There was no staying out of it. There was only the road ahead, and the moon counting down its last turn, and the vault beneath Mount Ceren where a stolen heart hung in chains of prayer-iron, waiting for someone to decide what divinity was worth.

"Well," Wendell said to the wolf—to Orsen—to their companion, in the doubled voice that was theirs alone again, mostly. "I suppose we'd better get to Ceren before she does."

The wolf rose, shook the water from its coat, and started along the ridge toward the east—toward the heart of the mountain, and the heart of the god, and whatever waited for them both at the end of the thirteenth turn.

Wendell followed. For the first time in thirteen years, they followed *knowing*—and the mountain, under their boots, hummed like a struck bell.
