## 11. The Heart Beneath

The dream came again on the third night of the climb, and this time it did not release Wendell gently.

They stood in a corridor of black stone, and the walls were warm. Not sun-warm—*body*-warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, the way a wound is warm. Somewhere below them something beat, slow and vast, and each pulse moved through the stone like a tide moving through a harbor. Wendell knew the corridor. They had never been here, and they knew it the way you know your own hands: by having carried it all their life without looking down.

*A theft,* the memory said, in Orsen's voice that was not a voice but a weight behind the ribs. *A betrayal. A burial.*

They woke with frost on their eyelashes and the wolf's breath fogging inches from their face, grey eyes watching them with an attention no animal should own.

"I know where it is," Wendell said. Their voice cracked on the cold air. "Under Ceren. It's under Ceren."

The wolf made no sound. But it stood, and turned its head toward the mountain's white shoulder rising against the stars, and waited for them to follow.

* * *

Mount Ceren had been a pilgrim mountain once. Wendell remembered the stories from childhood—the processions in summer, the bells, the thousands of candles guttering in the wind along the switchback paths. The new gods' church had burned the shrines in the first year of their ascendancy, and after Orsen went silent, even the smugglers stopped using the passes. The mountain became a place the world agreed to forget.

Which was why it still held what it held.

They climbed through three days of worsening weather, Wendell following a map drawn from inside their own skull. That was the worst part—not the cold, not the wolf's silent vigilance, but the certainty. Every fork in the trail, every cairn, every dead shrine: Wendell knew which way to go before they arrived, as if they were walking a road they'd already walked in someone else's skin. At the fourth shrine—a hollowed alcove with a stone basin full of old rain—the knowledge rose so sharply they had to stop and press a hand against the rock.

*Here we rested, the last night. Here I told them where to bury me.*

"Whose voice is that?" Wendell whispered.

The wolf sat beside them, close enough that its fur brushed their cloak, and said nothing at all.

Brannic caught up with them on the fifth day, coming up the eastern approach with two pack mules and the easy grin he'd worn since the reunion at Hollowford. He looked better than he had any right to look—clean-shaven again, boots oiled, the oath-marks on his forearms freshly inked over, darkened deliberately, worn like jewelry.

"You look like death," he said cheerfully, dropping his pack. "Good death. Productive death."

"There are things living up here that shouldn't be."

"Then it's good we brought iron." He patted one of the mules' loads, and metal clinked beneath the sacking. "You sleep any more of those dreams?"

Wendell hesitated. "Every night."

"And?"

"And I know the way now." They looked at Brannic—at the darkened marks, the too-eager eyes—and felt the first cold thread of doubt wind through the warmth of having an ally. "Brannic. What do you think is down there?"

He smiled wider. "Salvation," he said. "What else?"

* * *

The entrance was not a door. It was a wound.

Wendell found it exactly where the dream-memory placed it: a seam in the mountainside above the treeline, hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, wide enough for one person sideways. The rock around it was wrong—too smooth, fused, as though the mountain had melted shut around something and then been cut open again. When Wendell pressed their palm against the stone, the pulse came through it clearly for the first time. Not imagined. Not dreamed.

*Beat.*

The whole mountain was beating.

"It's alive," Brannic breathed behind them. His grin had gone strange. Reverent. "Thirteen years and it never stopped."

"It was never buried here," Wendell said quietly, working the memory open like a knot. "It was *imprisoned.* The other gods didn't hide Orsen's heart—they chained it. Prayer-iron. Because a heart alone, without a body, without a mind—" They stopped. The rest of the sentence was in the dream, and the dream's ending was a sound like gods screaming.

"Without a mind to leash it," Brannic finished softly. He'd come up beside them, and his breath smelled of the pepper-spirit he'd been drinking when he thought Wendell slept. "Yes. I had that part of the dream too."

Wendell went very still. "You said the dreams skipped you. You said you remembered nothing."

"I lied." Brannic said it easily, the way he confessed everything, the charm never dropping. "I've dreamed of this place since Orsen went quiet. Thirteen years, Wendell. Do you know what that does to a man? Waking every morning with a god's heartbeat in his teeth and no idea where it comes from?" He shouldered past toward the seam, dragging a lantern off the mule. "I'm done not knowing. Come on. Or stay out here and pray to the snow."

The wolf growled. Low, continuous, a sound like a millstone turning.

Brannic glanced back at it, and for just a moment the grin slipped, and something older and hungrier looked out of his face. "Keep your beast leashed," he said. "We're almost home."

* * *

The passage descended in spirals, and it was warm.

That was the detail Wendell's mind kept snagging on as they went down—warmth, in a mountain, at depth, where there should be ice and silence. The walls sweated. The prayer-iron chains that studded the corridor like ribs were black with age but free of rust, and when Wendell's fingers brushed one in passing, the metal *flinched.* Not metaphorically. It contracted away from their skin like a living thing touched by fire, and somewhere far below, the great pulse stuttered.

"They're wards," Wendell murmured. "Not chains. Chains hold things down. These hold it *in*—they're made of answered prayers, hammered flat. Every miracle anyone ever begged from Orsen, folded into bars."

"Then the church should love them," Brannic said. His voice echoed strangely; the corridor ate the edges of sound. "Prayer-iron. Half the liturgy of the new gods is built on stolen old-god scrap."

Ahead, the corridor opened.

Wendell stepped into the vault and forgot how to breathe.

It was a cavern the size of a cathedral's nave, domed in that same fused black stone, and every surface was covered in prayer-iron—chains by the thousand, thick as tree trunks, running from wall to ceiling to floor, all of them converging on the center of the chamber like the roots of some inverted tree. And at the center, suspended in the webbing at the height of a man's heart—

It was the size of a fist. That was the obscene thing. Thirteen years of dread, a war waiting to be born, a god unmade and scattered, and the cause of it all was a shape no bigger than a clenched hand, beating in its cradle of chains.

It glowed. Not steadily—it *breathed*, swelling gold-red with each pulse, dimming between, and the light moved across the iron roots like firelight across water. The warmth of the chamber came from it. The faint sweet smell, like blood and honey, came from it. Wendell understood, standing on the threshold, that the dreams had been kind. Memory could not carry what this actually *was*. This was divinity with nowhere to go, pressing outward against thirteen years of prayer, patient as pressure, and the whole mountain rang with it like a struck bell that would not finish sounding.

The wolf would not enter the chamber. Wendell looked back and saw it crouched at the corridor mouth, ears flat, a tremor running through its whole body—and its eyes fixed on the heart with an expression Wendell had no name for. Grief, maybe. Homesickness. The look of a dog outside a door it has scratched at for years.

"We take it," Brannic whispered. He had gone pale, and sweat stood on his forehead despite the warmth, and still he moved forward, step by step, like a man walking into surf. "Careful. Careful. The chains first—there'll be a lock, a word, something—"

"Brannic." Wendell's voice came out thin. "Listen to me. What did you promise Vael? What did you tell her to get her off our trail at Hollowford? Because she let us go. She *let us go*, and Sera Vael doesn't let anything go."

For a moment Brannic kept walking. Then he stopped, halfway across the chamber floor, directly beneath the hanging heart, and his shoulders began to shake.

She wasn't laughing. Wendell realized with a slow drop of the stomach. He was crying—or something near it—and when he turned, his face was wet and blazing and beautiful with certainty.

"She offered me a pardon," he said. "And a chapel. A *chapel*, Wendell. My own congregation, my own tithe, my name spoken in the liturgy. All I had to do was bring her to the heart." He spread his arms, and the red light washed over him, and his oath-marks seemed to move beneath the fresh ink. "But do you want to know what I told her? I told her I'd deliver the location and nothing more. Because I'm not giving *this*"—his voice cracked upward—"*this*—to the church. To the new gods. To men who'd nail it to an altar and charge admission to look at it. This belongs to us. To the sworn. We bled for Orsen. We *starved* for Orsen when everyone else knelt to the new altars and called it wisdom. And if the god won't come back for it—" He drew the long knife from his belt, the one Wendell had seen him sharpen every night for five days. "Then the power goes to the last ones who kept faith. That's not greed. That's *justice*. That's the oldest law there is."

"The last ones who kept faith," Wendell repeated slowly, "are standing behind me and won't cross the threshold. Look at yourself, Brannic. You're shaking."

"With awe!"

"You're shaking because it's already in you." Wendell took one careful step forward, hands open and empty, the way you approached a wounded animal or a lit fuse. "It's whispering to you right now. Telling you everything you've ever wanted to hear. It told the other gods the same things, before they buried it here. That's *why* they buried it here, Brannic. Not because it was weak. Because it was honest. It offers exactly what it is, and what it is will eat you hollow and wear your skin to the war."

"You don't understand," Brannic said, and his voice dropped into something gentle and terrible, a man explaining to a child. "None of you ever understood. You hid. You smuggled salt and kept your head down and called it loyalty. I *waited*. Thirteen years, I waited, and now I'm standing under the heart of a god, and you want me to walk away?" He laughed—a real laugh, bright and broken. "I can feel it, Wendell. Right now. It knows my name."

The heart pulsed.

The light flared, and Brannic gasped, arching, and the chains around him sang—a high silver note that Wendell felt in their fillings, in their bones, in the scar-tissue of their oath-marks. And in that moment of flare, Wendell saw what the light showed: the veins standing black in Brannic's neck, branching, spreading, prayer-iron shadows crawling up under his skin from the marks on his arms. The heart was reaching for him. Or he was reaching for it. From this distance, Wendell could no longer see the difference.

"Put down the knife," Wendell said. "Walk out. We seal the passage, we go to the oracle, we find another way—"

"Another way." Brannic turned fully now, and the knife caught the red light, and his eyes had gone the color of the heart itself, gold bleeding into gold. "There is no other way. There never was. There's only who holds it when the moon turns." He raised the blade—not at Wendell, Wendell understood with horror, but at the nearest chain-root, thick as a man's waist, humming with thirteen years of strain. "One cut. One prayer answered, and everything changes. No more hiding. No more silence. No more *salt smugglers*—"

"Wendell," said a voice from the corridor mouth.

Wendell's blood went to ice, because the wolf did not speak, the wolf had never spoken, and yet the voice was deep and tired and achingly familiar, and it came from the wolf's direction, and it said:

"*Do not let him touch the iron.*"

Brannic heard it too. His head snapped toward the corridor, and for one instant the greed fell away from his face and left only naked, human terror—the face of a man realizing, thirteen years too late, what he had followed down into the dark.

Then he swung the knife at the chain.

* * *

Everything happened at once.

Wendell threw themselves forward. The wolf came out of the corridor like a grey landslide. The knife met the prayer-iron with a sound like the sky cracking, and the chain did not break—it *answered*, flaring white-hot along its entire length, and every other chain in the vault answered with it, and the great pulse below the mountain stumbled, doubled, raced.

Brannic screamed. The blade had fused to his hand, or his hand to the blade, and the black veins blazed up his throat and across his face, and he turned to Wendell with the heart's own light pouring out of his eye sockets, and he was still smiling, still saying something—*faithful, faithful, faithful*—as the wolf hit him like a falling tower.

They went down together in a tangle of fur and burning flesh. The heart above them surged against its chains, and the whole vault groaned, dust raining, and somewhere impossibly far above, Wendell heard—felt—the mountain begin to shift its weight.

The wolf stood over Brannic's body, sides heaving, muzzle dark. Brannick's chest still moved. Barely. The light in his eyes guttered, and in the last of it, Wendell saw the man again underneath—the mercenary who'd shared fires with them for eleven winters, who'd taught them dice, who'd once carried them three miles on his back with an arrow in his thigh.

"Wasn't greed," Brannic whispered. Blood frothed at his lips. "Swear it. Wasn't... it just finally listened to me. Like nobody else ever..."

His eyes fixed on Wendell. Pleading. Asking for something Wendell couldn't give.

Above them, the heart pulsed faster, straining, and the first chain-link split with a sound like a bell dying, and down the corridor behind them, faint and far, came a sound Wendell knew from a lifetime of border wars and had prayed never to hear again in these mountains.

Boots. Many. Descending.

Torches bloomed in the passage mouth, and a woman's voice carried down the warm dark, level and cold and utterly unsurprised:

"Inquisitor Vael. Nobody moves. Nobody touches anything."

The second link cracked.
