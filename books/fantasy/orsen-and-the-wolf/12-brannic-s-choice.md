## 12. Brannic's Choice

The heart was warm.

That was the first thing Brannic noticed, and the last ordinary thought he would ever have. He had expected it to be cold—prayer-iron and grave-dust, something dead that had been buried a long time. Instead it lay in his cupped hands like a sleeping bird, heavy as an anvil and warm as a living throat, and through the black lattice of chains that had held it for thirteen years he could see light moving. Not shining. *Moving*, the way blood moves under skin.

Behind him, Wendell was shouting something. The vault shook with it. Brannic did not turn around.

He had imagined this moment for twenty years, back when he still bothered to imagine anything at all. In those imaginings he always felt triumphant. Vindicated. The oath-marks on his forearms—the same faded wolf-paw sigils Wendell carried, the same brand every sworn sword of Orsen's company had taken in the days when the mountain god still answered—would blaze with purpose, and he would stand in the ruined dark holding a god's heart and know that all his years of bitterness had been a kind of faith after all.

The reality was quieter than that. And hungrier.

*Take me,* said the heart.

It did not speak in words. It spoke the way a fire speaks to cold hands, the way a cliff edge speaks to a tired man standing too close. It spoke in the language of what he lacked, and Brannic had lacked so much for so long that he understood it instantly, fluently, the way a starving man understands the smell of bread.

*You knelt for a god who never came. You bled for a mountain that went silent. You grew old in the service of nothing.*

"That's not true," Brannic said aloud, and heard how thin his voice sounded in the vault's vast dark. "The silence wasn't his fault. We know that now. He was stolen from—"

*And who left you? Who let you be stolen from? Who watched you bury eleven of your sworn brothers in the passes and never once sent you so much as a dream?*

Wendell's footsteps were coming fast across the vault floor, boots ringing on prayer-iron flagstones. Brannic turned, and saw them stop dead when they saw his face.

"Wendell," he said, and was surprised by how reasonable he sounded. How calm. "It's all right. I'm not going to hurt it."

"You're holding it." Wendell's voice cracked down the middle. They looked terrible—three days of hard running written across their face, their oath-cloak scorched at the hem where the freed divinity had licked out at them, one eye swollen half-shut from the rockfall at the stair. But their hand was steady on their blade, and their eyes were fixed on the heart in Brannic's hands with an expression Brannic recognized because he'd seen it in mirrors: fear of a thing they loved. "Brannic. Put it down."

"It's warm," Brannic said. "Did you know that? Everyone talks about it like it's some cursed relic, some weapon. Nobody says it's *warm*."

"Brannic—"

"Twenty years I kept the oaths." His voice was rising now and he couldn't stop it, the words coming up out of him like water out of a cracked dam. "Twenty years I told the young ones the mountain god was only resting. Twenty years I lit the little shrines and paid the toll-keepers and never took a contract that broke the old laws, even when breaking them would have fed us. And where was he? Where was Orsen while we starved faithful?"

"He was chained under a mountain," Wendell said. "Same as his heart. It wasn't a choice, Brannic. None of it was a choice."

"Everything's a choice." The heart pulsed against his palms, and warmth ran up his arms like wine. "You taught me that, Wendell. Back when you were worth teaching."

*They abandoned you too,* the heart murmured, gentle as a mother. *They knew where I was and they came alone. They never meant to share me. Look at them. Look how they watch you. That is not the look of a friend. That is the look of someone deciding whether they can take it from you.*

Brannic looked at Wendell. He tried to see his oldest comrade, the scout who'd pulled him out of the Whitefen with two arrows in his leg, the one who'd stood vigil over Dara's body for a full night because no one else would. But the heart was in his hands, and everything it touched it colored, and what he saw instead was a rival. A keeper of secrets. Someone small and frightened hoarding the one treasure in the world that could make his life mean something.

"I could heal the company," he heard himself say. "The ones who renounced. The ones who forgot. I could bring them back. All of them. Even the dead ones, maybe—I don't know what the limits are. Do you understand what I'm saying, Wendell? I could unmake thirteen years."

"You'd be remaking them into something else." Wendell took a step forward, slow, hands open. "You've felt it already, haven't you? What it wants. It doesn't want to heal anyone. It wants to be *used*. That hunger isn't yours, Brannic. It's wearing your voice."

"The hunger is mine." And God help him, it was. It had been his for years before he ever touched the thing—hunger for meaning, for restitution, for the lost weight of a life that had once mattered. The heart hadn't put it there. It had only found it, the way water finds a crack. "It's been mine since the day the mountain went quiet and everyone pretended it didn't matter. You pretended, Wendell. You went off and played smuggler and told yourself staying invisible was loyalty. At least I stayed angry. At least anger is honest."

Something moved behind Wendell's eyes—not hurt, exactly. Recognition. That was worse.

"Vael is coming," Wendell said quietly. "Her whole company. We have minutes, maybe less. Give me the heart and we run, together, like we planned. Like you swore."

"I swore a lot of things." Brannic looked down at the heart. Through the broken chain-links, the light inside had begun to move faster, brighter, turning like a wheel. "I swore to Orsen. I swore to the company. I swore to Dara on her deathbed that her sacrifice would mean something, and then I spent thirteen years watching it mean nothing." He raised his eyes. "This is the first promise I've ever held in my hands that could actually be kept."

*Yes,* breathed the heart. *Yes. Lift the last chain. Break it open. You will never kneel again. You will never want again. Every prayer in Thornfell will pass through you first, and you will decide which ones are answered, and the new gods will break their teeth on you, and the old gods will remember your name—*

His thumb found the final chain-link. Prayer-iron, forged from ten thousand answered prayers, and it parted under his touch like wet bread. The heart sighed open.

What came out was not light.

It was *want*. Pure, undiluted, ancient want, the distilled appetite of a god that had been starved for thirteen years, and it poured into Brannic through his palms and his oath-marks and the soft wet doors of his eyes, and for one impossible moment he was everywhere. He felt the snowpack on Mount Ceren groaning under its own weight. He felt every shrine in Thornfell, the tended ones and the moss-choked ones, like teeth in his own mouth. He felt the church riders on the south road, four hundred hearts beating, and he could have stopped every one of them with a thought. He felt Wendell, close, terrified, *loyal*, and beneath the loyalty—buried deep, deep, where people keep the things they're ashamed of—a small cold wish that Brannic had died at Kestrel Notch, because a dead friend is easier than a rival.

He could burn that wish out of them. He could burn out everything ugly in everyone. He could remake Thornfell clean.

All he had to do was hold on.

"Hold on," he whispered, not knowing if he was commanding himself or begging.

The heart poured deeper. And underneath the glory, Brannic began—as Dara must have begun, as every soul the old war had ever chewed up must have begun—to feel the edges of himself going soft. Not pain. Dissolution. The heart did not want a servant; servants end. It wanted a *vessel*, worn through, and it was drinking him the way he had meant to drink it, and somewhere very far away he heard Wendell screaming his name and understood, distantly, with the last part of him that was still Brannic Okonkwo-of-the-Ninth-Company, that he had been wrong about everything. The gods hadn't gone to war over this thing because it was precious.

They'd gone to war because it was *hungry*, and hunger this size makes everything look like food.

*I'm sorry,* he tried to say. To Wendell. To Dara. To the boy he'd been, kneeling at the moss-choked altar, certain that devotion was a ladder.

The bolt took him through the chest.

* * *

There was no sound of the crossbow. There would be later—in the ringing aftermath, Wendell would swear they'd heard the string, heard the bolt's flat hum across the vault—but for Brannic there was only the impact, a fist of iron punching through him from behind, and the sudden astonishing absence of the heart's voice.

Silence rushed in where the hunger had been, and in that silence, dying, Brannic discovered he could think again.

He was on his knees. He didn't remember falling. The heart lay on the flagstones a arm's length away, its light guttering, cheated, furious—and dimming, he realized. Dimming because its vessel was leaking out around a crossbow bolt, and divinity, whatever else it was, needed somewhere to live.

Footsteps. Measured, unhurried, the boots of someone who had done this many times and felt each time exactly as much as the job required. A woman's shape resolved at the edge of his blurring vision: grey cloak, steel gorget, a face like a winter river—cold, moving, carrying things beneath the surface no one was meant to see.

Inquisitor Sera Vael stopped beside him and looked down.

"Brannic of the Ninth Company," she said. She knew his name. Of course she did; she'd reconstructed his life from toll records and tavern debts and the testimony of a smuggler-handler who'd sold her oldest friend for the price of her own neck. She probably knew his mother's name. "You were sworn to Orsen."

"Was," Brannic managed. Blood filled his mouth when he spoke; he swallowed it. "Renounced. Eight years ago. Didn't take."

"No," Vael said. "It appears it didn't." She crouched, unhurried, and studied his face with an expression that was not cruelty. That was the horror of it, Brannic thought, as the dark came up his throat. It was *attention*. Real attention, the kind no one had paid him in twenty years, given freely at the exact moment he had nothing left to buy with it. "For what it's worth, I believe you meant to use it well. Most of them do. That's how it works. That's why my order exists—to kill the well-meaning before they become the catastrophe." She paused. "You became the catastrophe in about ninety seconds. Nearly a record."

Brannic laughed. It cost him a rib's worth of pain and produced more blood, but he laughed, because it was either laugh or weep and he had spent his whole ration of weeping years ago. "Ninety seconds," he rasped. "Tell the company. Tell them... tell them Brannic finally broke a record."

"Where is Wendell?" Vael asked.

He looked past her shoulder. Across the vault, in the shadow of the shattered chain-rack, Wendell stood frozen with their hand still half-raised toward him, toward the heart, toward the last twenty years—and beyond them, at the tunnel mouth, grey shapes were pouring through, Vael's riders, crossbows leveled, and the wolf was nowhere to be seen, and Wendell was looking at him the way you look at a door closing.

*Don't tell her,* Wendell's eyes said. *Don't you dare.*

Twenty years ago, Brannic would have obeyed that look without thinking. Ten years ago he'd have sold Wendell out of spite. Ninety seconds ago, full of godlight, he'd have burned them both for the small cold wish he'd seen in their heart.

Dying, he found a fourth option, the one he should have found at the start.

"Tunnel behind the third pillar," he said to Vael, loud enough, clear enough, and watched Wendell flinch as if struck. Then, softer, with the last air in his lungs, in the old company cant that Vael's scholars had never decoded and never would: *"Run low, run left, third stone sings. Go home, little scout."*

Vael rose, signaling her riders with two fingers, and three of them peeled toward the pillars. But Wendell was already moving—low, left, exactly as instructed—because some lessons outlive the man who teaches them, and the third stone sang under their boot as they vanished into the dark.

Brannic smiled. It hurt. Everything hurt, and beneath the hurt the darkness was rising fast and warm, almost like the heart's warmth but honest, empty, asking nothing.

Vael lingered a moment longer. "Why?" she asked. Genuine question, no trick in it. "You gave yourself up to save them. Why?"

Brannic looked at the heart, lying dim and silent on the stones between them, waiting patiently for its next pair of hands.

"Because somebody has to show you," he whispered, "what it does."

Then the dark took him, and the last thing Brannic of the Ninth Company felt was Vael's gloved fingers closing his eyes—gentle, after all, gentle at the very end—and the distant, fading thunder of the thirteenth moon beginning to set.

* * *

Sera Vael stood alone in the vault for the space of ten breaths, her riders fanning out through the tunnels, her crossbow still warm in its cradle.

The heart lay at her feet.

It did not speak. It did not need to. She could feel it the way you feel a stove across a small room—warmth on her face, her hands, the backs of her closed eyelids. And beneath the warmth, faint as a pulse under a bandage, something whispering in a voice she knew better than any living voice:

*Sera. Sister. I could give her back to you.*

Vael looked down at the heart of a stolen god, and her hand—steady through forty executions, steady through the burning of Greyhollow's shrines, steady through every compromise she had ever dressed as necessity—trembled.

She reached for it anyway.

From the dark of the tunnel, something enormous began to growl.
