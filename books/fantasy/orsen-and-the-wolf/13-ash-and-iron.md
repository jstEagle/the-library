## 13. Ash and Iron

Brannic fell with a crossbow bolt in his shoulder and the heart of a god cradled against his chest like a stolen infant.

Wendell saw it happen from across the vault floor—saw Brannic stagger back against the prayer-iron chains that had held Orsen's heart for thirteen years, saw him clutch the thing tighter instead of letting it go, and understood, in the cold way one understands a rockfall already falling, that nothing good would come of what happened next.

"Put it down," Wendell shouted over the ringing of the vault. The freed heart was screaming—not with sound but with *presence*, a pressure like standing beneath a waterfall of light. Every chain in the chamber hummed. The walls wept condensation that hissed where it touched the heart's glow. "Brannic, it's not yours to hold!"

"It's mine to *free*!" Brannic's voice came out wrong, layered, as if two throats spoke through one mouth. His eyes were too bright. Blood ran freely from his nose and he didn't seem to notice. "Thirteen years I kept the oath while you hid on your roads! Thirteen years of silence, of being laughed at for serving a dead god—and now it's *here*, Wendell, it's finally here, and you want me to put it *down*?"

The heart pulsed.

Even at thirty paces, behind the broken remains of an iron censer stand, Wendell felt it like a fist closing around their sternum. Warmth first—the warmth of every fire they'd ever huddled beside, every hand they'd ever held, distilled into a single unbearable invitation. *Come closer*, the warmth said. *You've been cold so long.*

They knew what it was doing. They'd felt its whisper in dreams since the drowned chapel, patient as erosion. It wasn't malevolent. That was the horror of it. It simply loved, the way a landslide loves everything it carries downhill.

Then hooves.

The sound came down the entry tunnel ahead of the riders themselves—dozens of shod hooves striking the old processional road, echoing up through the mountain's throat like a drumroll announcing an execution. Torchlight bloomed in the tunnel mouth.

Vael.

* * *

Inquisitor Sera Vael rode into the vault of Mount Ceren the way she did everything: precisely, without wasted motion, her grey cloak dusted with three days of hard riding and her face showing none of it. She reined up at the tunnel's edge and took in the scene with the flat assessment of a woman counting coins.

Her company fanned out behind her—two dozen riders, crossbows leveled, spreading along the vault's upper gallery where the pilgrims of the old days had once stood to sing. Their torches threw long shadows across the chains of prayer-iron, each link thick as Wendell's wrist, all of them snapped now, hanging from the ceiling like the ribs of some enormous slaughtered beast.

"Wendell Zhou." Vael's voice carried without effort. "Step away from the artifact."

"The artifact has a name," Wendell said. They were moving as they talked, low and sideways, toward the rubble slope below the gallery. "It's a heart. And the man holding it is dying of it."

Brannic laughed—a sound with no humor in it, only heat. He had risen to his feet somehow, though the bolt still stood in his shoulder, and the light pouring off the heart had sunk into his skin until he glowed at the seams, at his eyes, at the split in his lips. "Dying? I've never been more alive." He raised the heart above his head, and the whole vault flared white. "Look at me! I am the first priest of the returned age!"

Somewhere in the gallery, a young rider made a small involuntary sound—wonder or terror, impossible to tell.

Vael's crossbow didn't waver. "Zhou. Twenty seconds."

"You're going to shoot at a man holding divine fire," Wendell said. "In a sealed vault. Under a mountain. Think that through."

"I think about consequences for a living, smuggler." But something flickered across Vael's face—doubt, quick as a fish under ice. "Lower it," she called to Brannic. "Whatever you believe you've found, the church will—"

"*The church*," Brannic spat, and the word cracked like a whip. "Your new gods chained this one and buried it. Your new gods started this war before either of us was born. And now—" he turned, slowly, the heart blazing in his grip, sweeping its light across the riders like a lighthouse across drowning men "—now the old god offers itself to whoever is strong enough to carry it. Not you. Not your church. *Me.*"

The heart pulsed again, and this time Wendell heard it speak—or felt it, in the marrow rather than the ear, the way one feels thunder.

*I remember all of you,* it said. *I will give you all of yourselves.*

A rider in the gallery screamed. Another dropped his crossbow and pressed both hands over his ears. Vael's horse screamed too, rearing, and for one heartbeat the discipline of the church's finest company simply—broke.

Brannic smiled.

"No," Wendell breathed. "*Brannic, no—*"

He brought the heart down toward his own chest.

* * *

What happened next, Wendell would spend the rest of their life failing to describe properly. Words were built for weather, for wounds, for the price of salt. They were not built for a god's heart meeting human flesh with intent.

Light did not fill the vault; it *replaced* it. For an instant there was no vault, no mountain, only white, and inside the white every memory Wendell possessed played at once—the oath altar, moss-choked, thirteen years silent; Maren's face as she betrayed them; the wolf's amber eyes in the dark of the pass; the drowned chapel oracle with water streaming down her cheeks as she counted moons. All of it offered up, laid open, *read*—the heart leafing through them like a merchant checking coin.

Then the white tore, and the world came back wrong.

Half the gallery was gone. Not collapsed—*gone*, sheared away as if a knife had cut the mountain, and beyond the cut was sky and falling stone and evening light where solid rock should have been. Riders who had been standing there simply weren't. Those who remained were scrambling, screaming, some kneeling in prayer to gods who could not hear them over the roar of the thing they'd spent their lives opposing.

And in the center of the floor, Brannic stood transformed.

He was burning. Not consumed—*burning*, flame pouring off him in sheets without touching his clothes, without casting heat. The bolt wound in his shoulder had closed. The glow had spread through his whole body until he looked like a man-shaped window onto something vast and hungry on the other side. When he turned his head, his eyes were molten.

"Wendell." His voice filled the chamber from every direction at once. "Brother. Look what it gives."

Wendell's hands were shaking so badly they could barely keep grip on the rubble. The heart's attention swung toward them, warm and enormous, and they felt the pull like a hook behind the breastbone. *Come. You swore the oldest oath. Come and be remade.*

"That's not Brannic anymore," Wendell said aloud—to Vael, to the survivors, to anyone still capable of hearing. "That's what's left when the heart finishes eating someone. Do you understand me? It doesn't share power. It *wears people*."

Vael had regained her seat on her horse. Her face was ash-pale, blood trickling from one ear, but her voice came out level. "Company! Withdraw to the tunnel mouth! Archers, covering line!" Then, to Wendell, across fifty feet of ruined stone: "How do we kill it?"

"You don't." Wendell was already climbing, hauling themselves up the broken slope toward the gallery, because the oath-cloak was there—dropped in the fight, tangled in snapped chain, and it was the only thing in Thornfell ever woven specifically to hold what couldn't be held. "You can't kill a heart. You can only decide who owns it. And right now the answer can't be him."

Brannic—or the thing wearing Brannic—watched them climb with terrible patience. "You'd steal it to destroy it. I know. I've seen it in you since the Stair." He began to walk forward, and where his bare feet touched stone, the stone glazed to glass. "The god wants to live, Wendell. Even reduced. Even caged. Can you blame it? Can you look at that—" he gestured at the severed vault, the open sky, the wounded world "—and say the world wouldn't be better with its maker awake?"

"The last time your god was awake," Wendell snarled, hauling over the lip of the gallery, "there was a war. I read the casualty stones at Greyhollow. There were so many names they stopped carving surnames."

"The new gods' stones say the same."

"Yes," Wendell said. "They do."

They grabbed the oath-cloak. Old rite, oath-woven, dyed in blood and ash and the smoke of a vow spoken over a silent altar thirteen years ago. It had never been tested against anything like this. Nothing had.

Below, the thing that had been Brannic raised the heart again, and this time Wendell didn't wait to see what it would do with it.

* * *

The battle, if it could be called that, lasted less time than it took to tell.

The heart's bearer moved through the vault like a slow catastrophe. He didn't attack the riders; he simply walked toward them, and the divinity around him did the rest. Men who knelt were lifted by nothing at all and set down gently, weeping, changed—one of them began laughing and did not stop. Crossbow bolts fired at him turned to ash mid-flight. A section of ceiling came down and he passed through the fall untouched, stone splitting around him like water around a prow.

Vael fought the way she probably did everything—precisely, without wasted motion. Wendell watched her rally the shattered company from horseback, voice cracking over the chaos: *"Fall back by pairs! Torv, take the left gallery! Nobody engages the bearer—nobody, do you hear me?"* She was buying time, Wendell realized. Buying it with her men's terror and her own composure, pulling her company out of a killing ground piece by piece while the mountain groaned overhead.

She caught Wendell's eye across the ruin once—just once—and jerked her chin toward the tunnel. *Go. Get clear.* It wasn't mercy. It was arithmetic: whatever Wendell intended to do with that cloak was the only plan in the mountain that didn't end with everyone dead.

Wendell meant to take the offer.

They got halfway to the tunnel before the heart noticed what they carried.

The pressure came down on them like deep water. The pull behind the breastbone became a hook, then a hand, then a gravity with opinions. *Mine,* the heart said, in Orsen's voice, in the mountain's voice, in the voice of every silent prayer Wendell had ever swallowed rather than speak aloud. *You are sworn. You have always been mine. Bring it here.*

Their knees hit stone. The oath-cloak dragged behind them, suddenly weighing as much as an anvil—as much as a vow. Through streaming eyes Wendell saw Brannic turning toward them, molten-eyed, smiling, extending one burning hand in invitation.

"See?" the thing said gently. "It calls you home. We could be together, brother. All of us. Forever."

Wendell thought about the wolf. About amber eyes in the dark of Kestrel Notch, watching, waiting, patient through thirteen years of silence. About a servant so loyal it had become the very thing it served. About what loyalty meant when the thing you were loyal to asked you to burn down the world.

"No," Wendell said, through their teeth, against the weight of a god. "Loyalty isn't obedience. That's the lesson, Brannic. Wish you'd learned it before the fire did."

They wrapped the cloak around their arm, seized a length of fallen prayer-iron chain—it sang at their touch, resonating with the heart like a struck bell—and drove it into the vault floor as an anchor, and pulled.

The heart screamed.

Not Brannic—the *heart*, a soundless shriek that bent the air and burst every torch in the chamber and sent cracks spidering up the walls. Brannic's body locked, his burning hand outstretched toward what he could no longer feel. For one moment his face cleared—just the man underneath, terrified, the bolt wound reopened and black, mouthing a single word that might have been *run*—

Then the vault's remaining ceiling chose that moment to finish dying.

* * *

Wendell ran.

Stone fell around them in slabs the size of cottages. The floor tilted as the mountain shrugged off the violation done to it. Ahead, the tunnel mouth was a shrinking square of torchlight full of screaming horses and grey cloaks; behind, Brannic stood motionless in the collapsing light, arms spread, laughing or weeping, growing smaller and brighter and then simply lost.

The heart fought the whole way. It burned through the oath-cloak's folds like a coal through paper—slowly, grudgingly, but burning—and where its light leaked through the weave, Wendell's skin blistered and their oath-marks, the old faded brands on their forearms from the swearing day, lit up like coals in their own right. They ran anyway. They ran with their eyes streaming and their lungs full of stone dust and the heart howling promises against their chest—power, restoration, *I will bring them all back, everyone you've buried, just let me out*—and they ran because the alternative was to stop, and stopping meant listening, and listening meant losing.

The tunnel was half-blocked a hundred yards in. Wendell went through the gap sideways, tearing cloth and skin, the wrapped heart scraping stone and shrieking fury. Behind them the vault's last supports gave way with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and a wall of dust rolled up the passage, chasing them, swallowing the torchlight, swallowing the screams.

They broke out into night air on Ceren's lower shoulder and kept running, downhill, stumbling through scree and dwarf-pine, until the mountain's roaring faded behind them and their legs simply quit, dumping them into the shelter of a boulder field under a sky crowded with stars.

One star was missing. Where the thirteenth moon should have been rising, thin and silver over Ceren's peak, there was only a smudge of cloud—or maybe not cloud. Maybe the moon itself, dimming, guttering, its turn almost finished.

Wendell lay on their back in the dirt and laughed, once, a broken sound with no humor anywhere in it.

Against their chest, wrapped in an oath-cloak that smoked at every seam, the heart of Orsen beat patiently, warmly, endlessly. Waiting. It was very good at waiting. It had had thirteen years of practice.

*Give me back,* it whispered. *You know you will. Everyone does, eventually. Even the ones who love you.*

Wendell sat up, checked the cloak's burning edges, and looked down the dark slope toward the tree line—toward the passes, the roads, the long way to anywhere at all.

Two amber points of light opened in the darkness below.

Watching. Waiting.

Coming up the hill.
