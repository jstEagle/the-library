## 14. Vael's Mercy

The stockade at Greyhollow had been built to hold smugglers, and it held them the way a cupped hand holds water—loosely, briefly, with inevitable spillage. Sera Vael had chosen it anyway. Not for its walls, but for its distance from the vault. Every hour she spent away from Mount Ceren felt like an hour spent holding her breath.

She stood now in the cell Wendell Zhou had been given—the best one, if such a thing existed: dry straw, a shuttered window, a door that locked with iron instead of prayer-iron, because after what they'd pulled from that vault she trusted no metal that had ever been blessed. The prisoner sat on the cot with their wrists chained to the wall behind them, head tipped back against the stone, watching her through half-lidded eyes with the particular patience of someone who had been interrogated before and had learned exactly how little of it mattered.

"You're not going to torture me," Wendell said. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"Because you don't need to. You already have what you came for." They lifted their chin slightly toward the satchel on Vael's hip, where the heart lay wrapped in three layers of oath-cloth and one layer of lead. "And because you think I'll talk if you wait long enough."

"Because you'll talk when you have something to say," Vael said. "Not before. Torture only produces words. I want meaning."

Wendell smiled without warmth. "The church trained you well."

"The church trained me to burn things. I trained myself." She dragged the cell's single stool closer and sat, elbows on knees, close enough that the smell of the prisoner reached her—woodsmoke, sweat, mountain dust, and beneath it something else, faint but unmistakable, like ozone after lightning. The heart's scent. It clung to everything now. To her own hands. She'd stopped noticing it two days ago, which frightened her more than noticing ever had.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said. "You can listen or not. It changes nothing about your situation."

"Everyone loves a story," Wendell said. "Go on."

* * *

Her sister's name had been Ilse, and she had died of a fever in the spring of Vael's nineteenth year, in a village so small the new gods' church kept no permanent priest there, only a circuit rider who came four times a year and was, that spring, three months late.

Vael remembered the details the way she remembered all her failures: completely, and without mercy toward herself. Ilse's skin hot as a hearthstone. The well water gone bad from the thaw. Their mother praying at the roadside shrine until her knees bled through her skirts, and the shrine staying silent, because the new gods answered prayers the way merchants answered letters—eventually, selectively, and always with an eye toward profit. A healing could be granted. Healings were simply expensive. Devotion measured in decades. Tithes measured in generations. Their family had neither.

Ilse died on a Tuesday, at dawn, while Vael sat in the doorway counting the hours since the last prayer and hating herself for counting.

The circuit rider arrived the following week. He performed the funerary rites without charge, which was the church's way of apologizing without admitting fault, and he told Vael, gently, that grief was a door through which false gods crept. That her anger was natural. That the old gods of Thornfell had once promised healings freely, and looked what that promise had bought: war, worship of beasts, mountains full of hungry altars.

He'd meant it kindly. She understood that now. But she had carried the sentence out of that room like a coal in her bare hands, and everything she built afterward was built around it: *the gods who answer are the gods who own you.* If no god answered anyone—if divinity itself were struck from the world like a splinter—then no child would ever die waiting on a miracle that cost more than her family could pay. No mother would bleed at a silent shrine believing silence was her own failure.

She joined the church at twenty. She became an Inquisitor at thirty-one, the youngest in Thornfell's history, not because she believed harder than the other candidates but because she believed *differently*. The others hunted heresy to protect doctrine. She hunted it to end the argument entirely. Where they wanted the old gods suppressed, she wanted them finished—unmade, root and branch, every altar toppled, every oath severed, every whispering power in the deep places strangled in its sleep.

When the old gods began answering again thirteen years of silence later, when prayers started landing and shrines started bleeding light, most of the hierarchy panicked. Vael did not. She had waited her whole career for this. Because answers meant presence, and presence meant location, and location meant that for the first time in history, the church could kill a god rather than merely starve one.

That was why she'd ridden into the vault at Mount Ceren with forty soldiers and come out with thirty-one and a prize.

That was why Wendell Zhou was currently chained to her wall instead of dead in a ditch.

* * *

"So," Vael said, finishing the story in the present tense of interrogation, "you understand my position. I don't hate you. I don't even particularly blame you. You swore an oath to a dying god because someone had to, and loyalty is a virtue I respect more than faith. But you're carrying the last ember of a fire that burned this world once already, and I am going to put it out."

Wendell had listened without moving. Now they said, quietly: "Your sister."

"What about her?"

"If the heart works the way I think it works—" They stopped. Started again. "You've had it near you for two days. Has it spoken to you?"

The question landed like cold water. Vael kept her face still, but something behind her sternum shifted, the way ice shifts on a river before it breaks.

"It's an object," she said.

"It's a piece of a god. And pieces of gods don't stay quiet. What does it say to you, Inquisitor? When you're alone with it at night. When you take off the lead wrapping to check the seals. What does it offer you?"

"Nothing."

"It offered Brannic power. It offered the oracle visions. It's offering you something right now, and you know it, and you're sitting here telling me a story about your dead sister because the story is how it got its hook into you." Wendell leaned forward as far as the chains allowed, voice dropping. "What did it promise you, Vael?"

"*Nothing.*"

But her hands had closed into fists on her knees, and Wendell saw it, and they both knew.

* * *

It had begun the first night, in the tent outside the vault, while the wounded screamed themselves quiet and the survivors pretended to sleep.

She had unwrapped the heart to inspect the containment seals—a necessary procedure, logged, witnessed—and the moment the lead fell open, the tent had filled with warmth. Not heat. Warmth. Like standing in a doorway with sunlight on the other side. And a voice that was not a voice, that arrived fully formed inside her own thoughts wearing her own cadence, so that for three terrible seconds she believed she was thinking it herself:

*I remember her.*

She had snapped the lead shut and sat in the dark with her knife drawn, breathing hard, telling herself it was exhaustion, grief-residue, the mind's ordinary treachery.

The second night it said: *Ilse liked the river in autumn. She used to float leaves downstream and race them.*

A detail Vael had never told another living soul. A detail she had not consciously remembered in fifteen years, and here it was, delivered whole and warm as a hand on her shoulder.

The third night, last night, it had made its offer.

*I can give her back.*

Not resurrection—that word was too crude, too carnival. What the heart described was subtler and infinitely worse. Restoration. Continuity. Ilse, remade from the memory the heart had read in Vael's own mind, complete down to the leaf-races and the way she'd hummed when braiding hair, alive and warm and *real*, because what was realness, the heart asked reasonably, but continuity of pattern? The church's own miracles healed flesh; why should flesh be the limit? Why should death be the limit, when death was only a gap, and gaps could be bridged by anything strong enough?

All it asked in return was to be carried east. Away from the mountain. Toward the old gods gathering in the lowlands. Toward the war it had been stolen to start.

Vael had sat with the offer until dawn. That was the part that shamed her—not the hearing, but the *sitting*. A true believer would have burned the thing or fled from it. She had done neither. She had weighed it, the way she weighed everything, and found it heavy, and set it down only because setting it down was procedure.

Procedure. She had built her entire life into a fortress of procedure, and now she understood, with the clarity of a woman reading her own diagnosis, that the fortress had a door, and the heart had found it, and the door was Ilse.

* * *

"You know what it's doing," Wendell said. They had not moved, but their voice had changed—softer now, almost gentle, and that gentleness was somehow worse than accusation. "You're an intelligent woman. Smarter than your superiors. You've seen possession before, corruption, the whole curriculum. You know the signs. So tell me what you see when you look in the mirror."

"I see an Inquisitor."

"You see a woman who hasn't slept in three nights. Who keeps the heart within arm's reach even though protocol says thirty paces. Who interrogates her prisoner alone, without witnesses, in violation of her own written orders." Wendell tilted their head. "I know the signs too, Vael. I've watched a good man named Brannic walk this exact road over the past month. It starts with a story. It ends with you kneeling in front of that thing calling it *sister*."

"Brannic is dead."

"Brannic is what happens next."

Silence stretched between them, taut as wire. Outside, the stockade bell rang the change of watch, and somewhere a horse complained, and the ordinary sounds of the living world went on indifferently.

Vael stood. Walked to the window. Opened the shutter a hand's width and let the grey afternoon light fall across the floor between them.

"Here is what happens now," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, professional, and utterly unconvincing. "You'll be transferred to the cathedral at Solmere for formal judgment. There will be a trial. You'll likely be executed, though I'll argue for commutation—you're more useful talking than hanging. The heart goes into the deep reliquary under armed guard until the Convocation decides—"

"And the Convocation will decide to use it," Wendell said. "You know that too. You've met those men. The moment the church has a piece of a god in a box, they won't destroy it. They'll *study* it. Weaponize it. Pray to it, eventually, whatever they call their praying. Your whole life's work, Vael—ending the argument—ends with the church becoming the very thing it hunts."

"I said the Convocation decides—"

"You said the heart goes in a box. Boxes get opened." Wendell's eyes were steady. "There's a third option. You've already thought of it. You thought of it the second night, when it spoke to you, and it terrified you because it's the only path that actually leads where you've always claimed to want to go."

Vael said nothing.

"Destroy it," Wendell said. "Not contain it. Not study it. Unmake it. End every divine power in Thornfell—old gods, new gods, miracles, curses, all of it. No more gods who answer, which means no more gods who own. No more child dying while her mother bleeds at a shrine. Isn't that what you've wanted since you were nineteen years old? You just never let yourself imagine the price, because the price was your career, your church, your certainty. And now the price also includes the thing whispering in your ear, and you can't tell anymore whether wanting to destroy it is strategy or self-defense."

"That is treason against the church," Vael said. "Against everything—"

"Everything that failed your sister," Wendell said. "Yes."

The light through the shutter had gone the color of old pewter. Vael became aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking, and made them stop, and they started again, and she let them.

* * *

That night she did not sleep either. She sat in her quarters with the heart on the table before her, wrapped in its layers, and she performed the inspection ritual with hands that were perfectly steady, because she forced them to be, and when the lead came away the warmth rose up to meet her like a tide.

*You've decided,* the heart said, pleased. *I can feel it. You're going to open the door.*

"I haven't decided anything."

*You came to me alone. At night. Again.* A pause, gentle as snowfall. *Would you like to see her? Just for a moment. Just to be sure. You wouldn't have to choose anything. Only look.*

"Don't."

*She's nineteen forever, in my keeping. She's floating leaves on the river. She's asking for you.*

"I said *don't*—"

And there, at the edge of the warmth, at the rim of the light, something moved. Small. Familiar. A figure with Ilse's narrow shoulders and Ilse's habit of pushing hair behind her ear, standing in a place that was not a place, turning—

Vael slammed the lead wrap down and drove the sealing spikes home, all six of them, harder than the ritual required, and then she put her palms flat on the table and hung her head and breathed, and breathed, and breathed.

When she finally looked up, her reflection stared back at her from the darkened window: a hollow-eyed woman with a knife on her belt and a god's heart on her table and her sister's face printed on the inside of her eyelids.

Corruption didn't arrive like a storm, she thought. It arrived like this. Like a reasonable voice in a quiet room. Like a story you already knew, told back to you by something that wanted you to believe it was yours.

She thought of Wendell's voice, level and merciless: *it ends with you kneeling in front of that thing calling it sister.*

She thought of her mother, bleeding through her skirts at a silent shrine.

She snuffed the candle. And in the dark, she took out her map of Thornfell, and her pen, and after a long time she drew a single line—from Greyhollow, east, toward the summit shrine below the thirteenth moon's fading arc—and beside it, in her small precise hand, she wrote the word that would either save her soul or damn it beyond any theology's reach:

*Unmake.*

Then she blew out the lamp, and went to find her prisoner, and woke them with a hand on the shoulder and a key already turning in the chains.

"Get up," Vael said. "We're leaving. Both of us. And Wendell—you're going to tell me everything about how a heart gets destroyed, and you're going to start now, because we have four days, and the moon is dying faster than either of us planned."
