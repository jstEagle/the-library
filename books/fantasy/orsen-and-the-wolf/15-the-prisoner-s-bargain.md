## 15. The Prisoner's Bargain

They kept Wendell in the old granary at the edge of the stockade, because it was the only building with walls thick enough to muffle what the heart said.

Wendell learned this on the second day, from a guard who talked too much. The man was young, freckled, terrified in the way of soldiers who had been told too many stories about the thing locked in the strongbox two doors down. He brought bread and watered wine and stood as far from Wendell as the cell allowed, and he talked, because fear talks.

"They won't let anyone sleep near it," he said. "Brother Aldous tried. Said he'd keep vigil, prove it was just—just an object." The guard's throat worked. "He's in the infirmary now. Won't stop praying. Won't stop *answering himself*, if you follow me. Talks in a voice that isn't his."

"I follow you," Wendell said.

The guard set down the tray and left quickly, and Wendell sat on the straw pallet and listened to the silence where the heart's whisper should have been, and understood that the church had built its cage around the wrong prisoner. The oath-cloak they'd wrapped the heart in still carried Wendell's scent, Wendell's sweat, thirteen years of sworn service soaked into the wool—and so the heart called to them through stone and timber and forty feet of prayer-iron chain the church had hung around the granary like festival garlands, and Wendell heard it the way you hear your own name across a crowded market: faint, persistent, *known*.

*Come back*, it said, or seemed to say. *You know where I belong.*

"You don't belong anywhere," Wendell told the empty room. "That's rather the point."

The third day, Vael came.

She came alone, which was either arrogance or respect, and she dismissed the guards with a flick of two fingers before seating herself on the stool across the bars. She looked worse than she had at the vault—or better, depending on how you read exhaustion. There were new lines around her eyes, and her hands, folded in her lap, were very still in the way of hands that have been trained out of trembling.

"You've been asking for me," she said.

"I've been asking for paper," Wendell said. "And ink. And someone who can count."

"Count?"

"Wounds. Miracles. Days." Wendell rose from the pallet, joints aching from two nights on stone-cold straw, and came to the bars. Up close, Inquisitor Sera Vael smelled of horse and woodsmoke and something underneath both—incense, maybe, burned down to nothing. "Your Brother Aldous is hearing voices. Your guards are drawing lots to avoid the night watch. And every hour that heart sits in your strongbox, it gets louder. You can't count that high, Vael. Nobody can."

"The heart is secured."

"It's *feeding*." Wendell gripped the bars. The iron was cold and sang faintly against their palms, prayer-iron resonating with the marks burned into their skin—the oath-marks, Orsen's brand, still dark along their forearms like old lightning frozen in flesh. "Every prayer your priests whisper near it, it drinks. Every fear your guards carry, it tastes. You didn't capture a relic. You captured a mouth."

Vael regarded them for a long moment. She had the kind of face that gave away nothing by design, but Wendell had spent thirteen years reading smugglers and liars and men deciding whether to kill them, and beneath the discipline they saw what she'd come here carrying: not certainty. A question.

"You offered my sister back to me," Vael said quietly.

Ah. There it was.

"In the vault," Wendell said. "I remember."

"It spoke to me. Through the chains, through the chaos, while your friend bled out on the floor—it found the shape of her voice inside my head and it *used* it." Her hands unfolded, refolded. "Lira died eleven years ago. Fever. I held her hand while the healers of the Radiant Choir did everything their doctrine allowed and it wasn't enough, and I have spent eleven years telling myself the new gods' silence on that matter was a test of faith." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "And in one moment, a stolen piece of a dead god offered me exactly what my whole life of devotion never delivered. So tell me, Zhou. Tell me why I shouldn't open that strongbox."

"Because it wasn't Lira."

"No."

"Because it was hunger wearing your grief like a coat."

"Yes." Vael exhaled slowly. "I know. I knew even as I listened. That's the part that keeps me awake—that some part of me *knew*, and wanted it anyway." She stood, paced once, the way caged things pace, though the cage in question was invisible. "I've interrogated forty people touched by the old powers. Heretics, prophets, madmen. They all describe the same thing. The old gods answer prayers like a mother answers a child—immediately, indulgently, without judgment. And the new gods—" her jaw tightened, "—the new gods demand years. Decades. A lifetime of discipline for a single small miracle, and half the time it doesn't come. Do you know why the church fears Orsen waking, Zhou? Not because he's powerful. Because he's *generous*. Generosity is contagious."

Wendell leaned against the bars. "Then we agree on more than you think."

"We agree on nothing."

"We agree the heart is poison." Wendell held her gaze. "You want to destroy the old gods. I've spent three days in your cell working out how to give you exactly that. All of them. Permanently. No war, no waking, no thirteenth moon turning over into something none of us survive."

Something shifted behind Vael's eyes—the hunter's stillness breaking apart into calculation. "The seal breaks when the moon completes its turn. Fourteen days. The old gods rise, the war begins, and my church burns Thornfell to stop it. That is the future I'm preventing."

"That's the future you're *choosing*, because nobody offered you another one." Wendell pressed closer to the bars. "So here it is. Offered. The heart isn't just Orsen's power. It's the root all divine power in Thornfell grows from—old and new alike. Your Radiant Choir, your miracles, your answered prayers—they drink from the same well. The gods went silent thirteen years ago because the well was dammed. Break the dam wrong, everyone drinks. Destroy the well entirely—"

"—and there are no miracles at all," Vael finished. Her voice had gone flat. Careful.

"There are no gods at all. Old or new. The war dies because there's nothing left to fight with. No divinity in Thornfell ever again. Prayers go up and hit sky, same as they already do for most people most days." Wendell spread their hands. "You lose your church's power. I lose my patron—which, given that my patron is currently a wolf sleeping above the cloudline, I've mostly made peace with. Everyone loses. Equally. Forever."

The silence stretched long enough that Wendell could hear the wind moving through the prayer-chains outside, a low metallic moaning like distant bells rung by a hand that had forgotten why.

"And your proof?" Vael said at last. "For any of this?"

"An oracle in a drowned chapel. A god's own memories, dreamed in a cave shrine. And a wolf who used to be a mountain." Wendell smiled without humor. "I know how it sounds."

"It sounds like heresy with better production values."

"It sounds like the only exit from a burning building." Wendell lowered their voice. "Vael. In fourteen days, that moon turns, and you will be standing in a war between things that treat armies the way we treat weather. Your doctrine says the new gods will win. Your doctrine also said they'd protect Brother Aldous. Go look at him in your infirmary and then come back and tell me what your doctrine is worth."

Vael turned away. For a moment she only stood there, looking at the wall, at the prayer-iron chains visible through the high window, glinting in the grey light. When she spoke again, her back was still turned.

"If I believed you—if I *acted* on it—I wouldn't be an Inquisitor anymore. I'd be the worst heretic in the history of the Radiant Choir. The woman who murdered God." A short, bitter breath. "There are seven hundred souls in this stockade who pray to the Choir every morning. Mothers with sick children. Farmers praying for rain. You're asking me to take all of that away from them based on the word of a smuggler and a wolf."

"I'm asking you to take it away before the sick children's mothers learn what it costs to keep it."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then in fourteen days we'll both find out together, at the bottom of a war." Wendell let go of the bars. "But you already know I'm not wrong. That's why you're here alone instead of sending a scribe. That's why you haven't handed the heart to the Cathedral in Corvale. You could have. Three days ago. Reinforcements, ceremony, the whole choir singing it into the reliquary." They tilted their head. "Why haven't you?"

Vael was quiet for a long time.

"Because the escort I sent ahead to Corvale," she said finally, "reported yesterday. Eleven riders. Somewhere past the Salt Road, their prayers started being answered. All of them. Instantly. Whatever they asked for." She turned back, and her composure had cracked clean through; what showed underneath was fear, honest and human and enormous. "They asked for water in a dry stretch and springs opened. One of them prayed his bad knee whole. And by the second day they had stopped speaking to each other. Stopped eating unless the heart permitted it. My sergeant described it as—" she faltered, steadied, "—as being loved by something enormous. They had to tie two of them down to get the strongbox back. It's in the stockade chapel now, under triple seal, and my own chaplain has requested transfer."

"There's your proof," Wendell said softly. "Not my word. Your riders'. Your chaplain's. Your own sleepless nights. The well is poisoned, Vael, and every day it sits in your chapel it poisons deeper, and when the moon turns, it opens whether you like it or not."

Vael crossed to the bars. Close now, close enough that Wendell could see the fine tremor she'd trained out of her hands had taken refuge in her jaw.

"If we do this," she said, "if I do this—we destroy it before the turn. Which means leaving this stockade with the most guarded object in Thornfell, past my own garrison, many of whom would kneel to that thing if it asked, and across country crawling with every faithful soul between here and wherever you intend to do the deed."

"Mount Ceren's summit shrine. Where the seal is thinnest. Where the heart was made." Wendell held out their hand through the bars—an absurd gesture, a prisoner offering truce to an inquisitor, and knowing it, doing it anyway. "I know the smugglers' roads. Every goat track, every blind valley, every route the church has never mapped because the church doesn't believe the mountains belong to anyone but God. You know your garrison, your seals, your people. Neither of us makes it alone."

"And the wolf?"

"Will find us. It always does." Wendell shrugged. "Consider it an omen. Or a threat. I've stopped trying to decide."

Vael looked at the offered hand. Wendell watched her weigh it—not the hand itself, but everything behind it: eleven years of a dead sister, seven hundred praying souls, a sergeant describing what it felt like to be loved by something enormous, a chaplain requesting transfer, a moon climbing toward its final turn.

Faith against fear. Wendell had watched that scale tip a hundred ways in a hundred hearts. Mostly it tipped toward fear, because fear was heavier and always had been. But every so often—

"You understand," Vael said, taking off her glove, "that if this is a trap, I will kill you somewhere on the Salt Road and tell the Choir you died resisting."

"Understood. If it's not a trap, I'll consider forgiving you for Maren."

"Maren sold you. I bought. We are not the same crime."

"No," Wendell agreed. "Yours is bigger."

And Sera Vael—inquisitor, hunter, the woman who had put a crossbow bolt through Brannic's chest and hesitated only once in her career—took Wendell's hand through the bars and shook it, once, hard, like a blade being sheathed.

"I'll need your smugglers' roads by dawn," she said. "And Zhou—if we reach that summit and destroying the heart means destroying *you* along with it, I want you to know I've made my peace with that arithmetic."

"Funny," Wendell said, letting go. "So have I."

Outside the granary, the wind moved through the prayer-chains, and far to the north, beyond the stockade walls, beyond the grey line of the hills, the thirteenth moon rose early over Mount Ceren—swollen, silver, and one turn short of full.

In the chapel two doors down, under triple seal, something that had been loved by something enormous began, very softly, to sing.

* * *

They left before dawn, four days later, in a cart of salted fish.

It was Vael's idea, which Wendell found privately hilarious—the chief inquisitor of the Radiant Choir smuggling herself out of her own fortress like common contraband, wrapped in oilcloth between barrels, breathing fish stink and trying not to. Wendell drove the cart, having convinced the gate guards that the prisoner was being transferred to Corvale under a decoy detail, per sealed orders from the Inquisitor herself. The orders were genuine. Vael had signed them with a steady hand and then gone to sit among her fish.

Only two rode with them: Sergeant Okonwe, the one soldier in the garrison whose prayers the heart had never managed to touch—he claimed he simply didn't pray, a confession that had nearly gotten him transferred until Vael recognized usefulness—and a lean, silent tracker named Pell who had stopped speaking altogether after one night's watch near the chapel and communicated exclusively in gestures now, all of them urgent.

The gates opened at the fourth bell. Grey light, cold wind, the smell of the Salt Road. Wendell flicked the reins and the cart rolled out under the eyes of guards who saluted the fish.

They made six miles before the mountain noticed.

It began as a hum in Wendell's oath-marks—a low, rising note, like a struck bell that refused to fade. Then the horses shied. Then Pell grabbed Vael's sleeve through the oilcloth and pointed north, and Wendell turned on the bench and saw, on the ridgeline behind them, the stockade chapel's windows bleeding light into the pre-dawn dark. Not lamplight. Not any light the church had a name for. It poured upward like smoke from a fire that burned nothing, and across it, faint and vast, moved a shadow with wings that no bird in Thornfell had ever owned.

The horses screamed. The cart lurched. And from the chapel, thin and clear across six miles of morning air, came a sound that froze the blood of everyone on the Salt Road, traveler and soldier and smuggler alike:

Singing. Wordless. Joyful. Getting louder.

Wendell whipped the horses forward. Behind them, the stockade's bells began to ring—all of them, unscheduled, unbidden—and above the ridgeline the thirteenth moon, impossibly, visibly, began to swell.

"Fourteen days," Vael said from inside the cart, her voice muffled by oilcloth and barrel staves and the certain knowledge that everything had just changed. "Zhou. Tell me we have fourteen days."

Wendell looked at the bleeding windows, the swelling moon, the road unwinding north toward Ceren.

"We have whatever it gives us," they said, and drove the fish cart toward the end of the world as fast as the horses could pull.
