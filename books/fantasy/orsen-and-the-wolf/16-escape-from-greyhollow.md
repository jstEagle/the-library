## 16. Escape from Greyhollow

The thirteenth moon was dying by inches.

Wendell felt it through the stone floor of their cell in the church stockade—a tremor at first, so faint they mistook it for a cart passing on the road outside. Then another. Then a long, grinding shudder that set the iron lamp swinging on its chain and sent a fine rain of dust sifting down from the ceiling beams like flour through a torn sack.

They had been counting the days since the vault. Six of them. Long enough for the burns on their palms—where the heart's light had scorched through the oath-cloak—to scab over into ridges that ached when the weather turned. Long enough for Vael to visit three times, each visit shorter than the last, her questions thinning out until the final one had been no question at all: just her standing at the bars, watching Wendell with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to.

Now the whole building groaned around them like a ship in heavy weather.

Wendell got up off the cot. The cell was small—one window, barred, high on the wall, looking out over the stockade yard toward the grey bulk of the temple district. Beyond it, Greyhollow's rooftops climbed the hillside in crooked tiers, and beyond those, the mountains. Thornfell. Somewhere up there, under Mount Ceren, a seal older than any kingdom was coming apart stitch by stitch, and every mile between here and that summit felt like a debt coming due.

The moonlight through the window was wrong. They'd noticed it two nights ago and told themselves they were imagining it. But there was no imagining it now: the thirteenth moon hung low and swollen over the peaks, and its light did not fall white. It fell *thin*—stretched, watery, as though the moon were a coin worn smooth by too many hands, its face rubbed nearly blank.

Thirteen turns. The oracle in the drowned chapel had said it plainly enough: when the last turn completed, the seal would break whether or not anyone found the heart. Wendell had the heart wrapped in their oath-cloak, hidden somewhere even Vael hadn't found—they'd checked; she'd searched them thoroughly enough—and the seal was breaking anyway, and the world was starting to notice.

Another tremor rolled through. Outside, a dog began to howl, then another, then a dozen, a ragged chorus rising from every yard and alley in the town. Not frightened howling. Wendell knew frightened animals. This was something else—the sound dogs made when they smelled lightning before it struck.

Then the screaming started.

It came from the direction of the small shrine on the corner opposite the stockade, the one dedicated to some minor saint of thresholds whose name Wendell had never bothered learning. Through the barred window they could see it: the little alcove with its candle-lit niche, and the light inside it—not candlelight anymore, but something brighter, pulsing, spilling out onto the cobbles like blood from a wound. A woman knelt before it, arms raised, and even from here Wendell could hear what she was saying. *Please. Please, he's feverish, please, I'll give anything—*

And the shrine answered.

That was the horror of it. The new gods' shrines weren't supposed to answer. That was the entire architecture of the church—prayers went up, miracles came down on the church's schedule, dispensed through proper channels by properly ordained hands. But the seal was failing, and the old machinery of divinity was leaking through every crack, and the shrine on the corner was pouring light over the kneeling woman like water over a drowning person, and her voice changed mid-plea—from begging to something higher, wilder, a laugh or a sob or both—

And then the light went red.

Wendell turned away. They didn't need to see what came next. In the vault beneath Mount Ceren they had stood close enough to the heart to feel it thinking, and they knew now what raw divinity did when it touched an unprepared mind. It didn't comfort. It *filled*. And most vessels broke.

The cell door rattled.

Wendell spun, hand going instinctively to their belt where a knife should have been and wasn't. The lock clicked—the mechanism, not a key—and the door swung open on a figure holding a lantern low, its light climbing a familiar grey coat, a tired face, close-cropped hair gone half-white at the temples since the vault.

Inquisitor Sera Vael stood in the doorway of Wendell's cell, holding it open.

"Up," she said. "We have perhaps forty minutes before the garrison stops being distracted by the end of the world. Less if the north wall goes."

Wendell stared at her. "You're letting me out."

"I'm escaping with you," Vael corrected. "There's a difference, and you'll understand it before we're over the wall. Move."

* * *

Vael had done this before, Wendell realized, twenty minutes later, as she led them down a service stair that smelled of tallow and rat droppings—done it in other towns, in other years, extracting people who needed extracting, back before the church had given her a title and made extraction a sin. She moved through the stockade's bones with the certainty of someone who had helped build them. Twice she stopped at junctions and listened. Once she pressed Wendell flat against a wall while four acolytes clattered past overhead, shouting about the shrine on Tanner's Row that had started weeping actual blood.

"The whole city's lit up," Wendell whispered when the footsteps faded. "Every shrine?"

"Every shrine in the province, if the reports are true." Vael's voice was flat, controlled, but Wendell had spent thirteen years reading people who were trying very hard to seem calm, and they could see the seams in her. "Greyhollow, Kestrel Ford, the river towns. Prayers are being answered without ordination. Some of them well." Her jaw tightened. "Most of them not. There's a family in the Low Quarter where a boy prayed for his father's recovery and got it—the father woke from a six-month sleep an hour ago, healthy as a horse, and hasn't stopped screaming since. He says he can hear everything. Every prayer in the city, all at once, forever."

"That's what the heart does," Wendell said quietly. "When it touches someone. It doesn't ask what you can carry."

"I know." Vael glanced back at them, and in the dark her eyes held something Wendell hadn't seen in them before—not doubt exactly, but the bruise left behind where doubt had been. "I've spent my life believing the church's miracles were the safe ones. Rationed. Contained. I thought ending the old gods would end the danger." She shook her head once, sharply, like a woman clearing water from her ears. "But your heart is loose in the world, Zhou, and the seal is failing, and everything divine is leaking out of every container ever built for it. The church's included."

They descended another flight. Below them, through a grate, Wendell could see the stockade yard: torches, running soldiers, a wagon being loaded with reliquaries as the church evacuated its holiest objects ahead of whatever was coming. The thirteenth moon hung over all of it, thin as a fingernail paring.

"The north wall," Wendell said. "You said less time if it goes."

As if the mountain had been listening, the ground bucked.

Not a tremor this time—a lurch, violent, sideways, throwing them both against the stair rail. Somewhere above, masonry cracked like a snapped bone. Dust avalanched down the stairwell. From the yard below rose a sound Wendell had only heard once before, at Kestrel Notch, the day the wolf first saved them: the particular scream of many people realizing at once that the solid world was not solid.

"The aqueduct," Vael breathed. "It runs along the north wall—it's been shaking loose for days—" She was already moving, taking the stairs two at a time, dragging Wendell by the sleeve. "Change of plan. We go now, through the yard, while everyone's looking at the wall."

"You're insane."

"Almost certainly. Keep up."

* * *

The yard was chaos in the specific flavor the church produced best: order dissolving into ritual. Soldiers formed lines because forming lines was what soldiers did, even as the lines' purpose evaporated. Priests chanted wardings that flickered and guttered like lamps in wind. And above it all the north wall leaned—visibly, horribly *leaned*—a crack jagging up its face where the ancient aqueduct had torn free of its moorings and hung by a single arch, sixty feet of stone channel swaying like a pendulum.

Vael walked into it wearing her full regalia—grey coat, silver chain of office, the cold authority of rank—and the crowd parted the way water parts around a keel. Wendell walked behind her with their head down and their wrists still manacled, playing the prisoner being transferred, which required no acting whatsoever.

They were ten feet from the postern gate when the aqueduct let go.

The sound was enormous—not a crash but a *tearing*, stone unmaking itself along seams laid by dead engineers, and then sixty feet of channel came down across the north wall and the world turned white with dust. The shockwave knocked Wendell to their knees. When the dust thinned, the postern gate was simply gone, buried under rubble, and the wall had a breach in it wide enough to drive a cart through, and nobody—*nobody*—was watching the prisoners anymore.

Vael hauled Wendell up. For a moment they stood in the settling dust, and through it Wendell saw the mountains beyond the breach, lit by the failing moon, and saw—though they would spend years deciding whether they'd truly seen it—a line of pale light moving along the high ridge of Mount Ceren, like a seam of fire stitching itself open.

"It's waking up," Wendell said. The words came out of them without permission. "Whatever's under Ceren. It's not just the seal weakening. Something's pushing."

"Then we run faster." Vael pulled a key ring from her coat and unlocked the manacles with hands that almost didn't shake. "There are horses in the temple stable, saddled—I arranged it before I understood quite how much I was arranging. The east road is watched. The smugglers' paths aren't, because the church doesn't believe they exist."

"They exist," Wendell said. "I've been paying for their existence my whole adult life." They hesitated. "Vael. Why? You had me in a cell. You had the heart's location—or you thought you did. All you had to do was wait for the seal to break and take it from the wreckage. Why throw away your career, your freedom—"

"Because I interrogated Maren for six hours," Vael said, and her voice cracked, just once, like ice underfoot. "And at the end of it she asked me if the church would save her soul, and I opened my mouth to say yes, the way I've said yes my whole life, and nothing came out. Because I don't know anymore, Zhou. Because the shrines are bleeding and the prayers are eating people and my own ordination sigil burned my hand yesterday when I tried to pray with it." She looked at her palm—at a red mark there, fresh, blistered, shaped like nothing holy. "I don't know what I believe. But I know what I *fear*, and right now I fear the thing under that mountain more than I fear the church's tribunals. So we're going to go and destroy your heart, and we're going to do it before the thirteenth moon finishes setting, and after that you can turn me in to whoever's left to be turned in to."

She stepped through the breach in the wall, into the night, into the broken country beyond.

Wendell followed.

* * *

The horses were where she'd promised them, in a stable whose groom lay snoring under circumstances Vael declined to explain. They rode east through streets that grew stranger with every block. Shrines blazed on corners like signal fires, some gold, some red, one shade of green that made Wendell's teeth ache to look at. People knelt in the open streets, praying in whispers, in screams, in languages Wendell was fairly sure the speakers didn't know. A procession of penitents crossed their path bearing candles, marching toward the burning shrine of Saint Aldric, singing a hymn that kept sliding out of tune into something older underneath it—an old-gods cadence, a harvest rhythm, rising through the hymn like a drowned body surfacing.

By the time they cleared the eastern gate, the thirteenth moon had touched the peaks.

It was setting. It was actually setting, and with every finger's width it sank, the tremors came faster, and the air itself developed a texture Wendell had no name for—a pressure, a *listening*, as though the whole sky had become an ear pressed against a door.

They rode hard through the foothills, off the road within the hour, following trails only Wendell knew: a smuggler's path behind the slate quarry, a ford through the Greywater that the toll-keepers never watched, a switchback climb through pine forest so steep the horses had to be led. Vael rode like a soldier—competently, joylessly—and said little, conserving herself, and Wendell found, to their surprise, that they trusted her more for the silence. Zealots talked. Believers explained. Vael just rode, and watched the mountain, and tightened her jaw a little more each hour.

It was near dawn, grey light seeping through the trees, when the wolf appeared.

Wendell saw it first—a shape at the edge of vision, upslope, motionless among the pines. Huge. Grey as weathered slate. Watching them with those pale eyes that had followed Wendell across half of Thornfell, through ambush and avalanche and the burning of the vault, that had bled for them at the Weeping Stair, that had spoken to them once—in Orsen's voice, in a dream, or maybe not a dream—before falling silent again.

"There," Wendell said, reining in. "Vael. Look upslope. Don't reach for anything."

Vael's hand had already gone to her belt knife. She froze, followed Wendell's gaze, and went very still. "That animal is the size of a pony."

"It's not an animal." Wendell swung down from the horse. Their legs ached; their burned palms throbbed; none of it mattered. "It's been leading me toward Ceren since Kestrel Notch. Every time I've gotten lost, it's been there. Every time I've nearly died, it's been there. And I bound myself to it at the Stair, and when I did—" They flexed their hands, remembering the flood of pain and memory, the god's grief pouring through the bond like winter water. "I think it knows a way up the mountain that doesn't exist on any map. I think it's been waiting for us."

"For *us*?" Vael's voice was dry. "Zhou, I put a crossbow bolt through its shoulder at the vault."

"And it forgave you, apparently, which is more than I can say for most of my acquaintances."

The wolf descended.

It came down through the trees without hurry, without sound, the way snow comes down, and stopped ten feet away, and looked at Wendell for a long moment with an attention that had weight, that had *temperature*. Then it turned its head and looked at Vael—looked at her the way a judge looks at a petitioner—and something passed across its pale eyes that might, in a face built for such things, have been called wry.

Then it turned and trotted east, toward the mountain, and paused, and looked back.

"It wants us to follow," Wendell said.

"Of course it does." Vael dismounted slowly, never taking her eyes off it. "Everything in Thornfell wants something this week. The question is whether what it wants is the same thing we want."

"It wants the heart destroyed," Wendell said quietly. "I'm sure of it. I've been sure since the Stair." They met Vael's eyes. "It's the only creature in this whole war that's never wanted anything *else*."

Above them, through a rift in the clouds, the thirteenth moon touched the highest ridge of Mount Ceren—and the mountain answered. A sound rolled down out of the heights, deep and vast and unmistakably not geological: a long, resonant tone, like a bell the size of a valley being struck once, and every shrine in the country behind them flared bright enough to cast shadows at dawn, and the wolf threw back its head and howled at the peak in a voice that was not entirely a wolf's.

The seal was breaking. Hours, maybe less.

The wolf looked back at them again, impatient now, and started up the slope.

Wendell took the lead rope of Vael's horse and followed.
