## 17. Where the Wolf Sleeps

The wolf did not stop climbing when the trees gave out.

Wendell had followed it for two days now—up the spine of Mount Ceren's western shoulder, past the last stunted pines bent sideways by wind like old men leaning into a gale they'd been arguing with for centuries—past the snowline, past the line where even the smugglers' maps went blank and honest about their ignorance. The air grew thin enough to taste of iron. Each breath was a small negotiation.

"You could have told me," Wendell said, for perhaps the hundredth time, "that we were going *up*."

The wolf glanced back once, amber eyes catching the low sun, and said nothing. It had said nothing for thirteen years, in fact, and though something had changed since the vault—since the heart had burned through its chains of prayer-iron and into Wendell's arms, since Vael's crossbowmen had died screaming at the edges of its light—the silence between them remained the same silence it had always been. Familiar. Almost comfortable. Like a coat worn so long you'd forgotten you were wearing it.

But not quite the same anymore. Not since the Weeping Stair. Not since the blood rite.

The bond sat under Wendell's skin like a second pulse. When the wolf paused at a ledge, Wendell felt the pause before it happened—a pressure of intent, a thought shaped like a place rather than a word. And when Wendell stumbled on the scree, knees buckling under a pack that held a god's beating heart wrapped in an oath-cloak, the wolf was simply there, shoulder wedged beneath their arm, bearing weight no animal should have understood how to bear.

"Almost there," Wendell muttered, and didn't know why they'd said it, because the words hadn't come from them.

*Almost there,* came the answering pressure, warm as a hand on the back of the neck.

They climbed through the cloud itself. For an hour the world was white and wet and soundless, the kind of blindness that made a person doubt the existence of anything beyond arm's reach. The heart pulsed against Wendell's chest inside the cloak—slow, slow, slower than it had beaten in the vault, as if even divinity grew drowsy this high above the world—and each pulse sent a faint ache through the oath-marks on Wendell's forearms, the old brands that had burned cold for thirteen years and now would not stop whispering heat.

Then the clouds thinned, tore, and fell away below them like a curtain drawn back by a careful hand.

Above the cloudline, the sky was a blue so deep it looked deliberate. The sun hung huge and unhurried. And ahead, set into the bare granite shoulder of the mountain like a socket set into a skull, was a cave mouth ringed with stones so ancient their carvings had weathered to suggestions: spirals, antlers, a figure with too many arms holding up a roof of stars.

Wendell knew those stones. Had known them before knowing anything else, it seemed, because the dream-memories rose unbidden—the theft, the betrayal, the burial of a heart in prayer-iron while three gods stood watching with faces like closed doors.

"This is where you hid it," Wendell said. "Before. This is where you hid *yourself*."

The wolf walked into the dark of the cave without answering, and after a moment, Wendell followed.

* * *

Inside, the den opened wider than the entrance promised—a hollow chamber floored with soft grey sand, lit by a seam in the rock overhead that let down a column of gold. Along one wall ran a shelf of stone, and on the shelf lay things that made Wendell's throat tighten: a bronze bell green with age, its clapper removed. A clay cup, chipped, painted with mountains. A child's wooden wolf, worn smooth by years of small hands. Offerings. Offerings from the time before the silence—or from someone who had never stopped believing the silence would end.

And in the deepest corner, half-buried in sand, a shape that stopped Wendell's breath.

A skeleton. Human-sized. Seated upright against the wall with the patient dignity of the deliberately arranged, its bones fused to the stone around it as if the mountain had grown through it while it waited. Around its neck, on a rotted cord, hung a medallion Wendell recognized the way you recognize your own face in a mirror gone dark.

An oath-mark. The same spiral-and-antler sigil branded into Wendell's own forearms.

"No," Wendell said quietly.

The wolf sat down in the center of the chamber, wrapped its tail over its paws, and looked at the skeleton with an expression Wendell had spent thirteen years refusing to name, because naming it would have meant admitting what it was. Grief. Long, patient, domesticated grief, the kind that learns to live in a house with you.

"That's mine," Wendell said. "That's my oath. That's—" They stopped. Started again. "Who was he?"

The wolf's mouth opened.

What came out was not a growl. It was a voice—low, layered, like wind moving through a canyon, like many voices speaking in unison and agreeing perfectly—and it filled the chamber the way water fills a cup.

*"His name was Aldric. He swore his oath to me four hundred years ago, on the altar at Thornfell, and he kept it until his body failed. He asked to be buried here, where I slept, so that he could keep watch even dead."*

Wendell did not move. Did not breathe. The heart against their chest pulsed once, hard, as if it too had heard.

"You," Wendell said. The word came out broken into pieces. "You're—"

*"I am what is left."* The wolf rose, and as it moved toward the shaft of light, its outline seemed to waver—not the wavering of heat-haze but of something larger trying to fit inside something smaller, a sea poured into a cup. *"I am Orsen. What remains of him. What fled into flesh when they took my heart."*

The chamber tilted. Or Wendell tilted. They put a hand against the wall and found the stone warm, impossibly warm, warm as a living thing.

"Thirteen years," Wendell managed. "Thirteen years I prayed at your altar. Thirteen years I carried your oath through every pass and border town in Thornfell, and you were—" A laugh escaped them, ugly and disbelieving. "You were *walking beside me*. You were eating from my hand at the Kestrel fire. You were sleeping against my back in the cave shrine while I dreamed your memories."

*"Yes."*

"Why?" The question tore loose with more force than Wendell intended, echoing off the stone. "Why hide? Why let me think you'd abandoned us—all of us, every sworn blade who starved and bled for a god who'd gone *quiet*? Do you know what that silence did? Brannic renounced you. Half the company is dead or worse. I built my whole life around staying invisible because I thought the god I served had *died*, and I couldn't afford to be seen mourning him!"

*"I know."* The voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. *"I heard every prayer. Every curse. Every renunciation. Silence was not my choice, Wendell—it was my sentence. When they stole my heart, they left me only enough divine spark to remain aware. Aware, and bodiless, and unable to speak across any distance greater than a whisper. I could not answer prayers. I could not touch the world. All I could do was endure, and wait, and hope that someday, somewhere, one of my sworn would find what I could not reach."*

"So you sent the dreams."

*"I spent everything I had left on those dreams. It nearly unmade me. But yes—I pushed the memory of the theft into the mind of anyone still bound to me, hoping one of you would follow it. Most never received it. Those who did thought them nightmares."* The wolf's eyes held Wendell's, and in them was an exhaustion older than kingdoms. *"You are the only one who listened."*

Wendell slid down the wall until they were sitting in the sand, the pack cradled in their lap, the heart pulsing its slow drowsy pulse. Above the cloudline the light through the seam had begun to go gold-to-copper, evening coming on.

"The heart," Wendell said. "You sent it away. Before they took you."

*"It was the only way. A god's power lives in the heart; the body is only a house. If they had seized both, I would have ended—truly ended, unmade past all return. So I tore my heart free and hid it in prayer-iron beneath Mount Ceren, and I fled into the smallest vessel I could find. Flesh. Mortal flesh. A shape so humble that hunting me would have seemed beneath them."* A pause, and then, quieter: *"They hunted anyway. For thirteen years they have hunted a grey wolf through these mountains. I killed some of them. I outran most. And I watched my sworn servants die believing themselves forsaken, and I could do nothing but watch, because a god in the body of a beast has no voice that men will heed."*

"I heeded," Wendell said. "Eventually."

*"Eventually,"* Orsen agreed, and there was something in the layered voice that might, in a man, have been a smile.

Wendell looked at the skeleton in the corner—Aldric, four hundred years faithful—and then at the wolf, at the impossible, weary, magnificent thing wearing the shape of a beast, and felt the full weight of the last weeks settle onto them at last. The answered prayer at the ruined altar. Maren's betrayal. Vael's crossbowmen. Brannic's face in the vault-light, hunger dressed as faith, and the bolt that had ended him. The heart's heat soaking through the oath-cloak into Wendell's ribs even now, whispering things they refused to hear.

"You should have told me," Wendell said again, but softly this time, without anger. "On the pass. At the Stair. Any of it. You let me bind myself to you by blood rite not knowing—"

*"Would you have done it, if you had known you were binding yourself to a god?"*

Wendell opened their mouth. Closed it.

*"No,"* Orsen said. *"You would have knelt. You would have prayed. And I did not want your worship, Wendell. I have had four hundred years of worship, and it tastes of fear. On the Weeping Stair, when you cut your palm and named me companion rather than beast—that was the first act of loyalty I had been given in thirteen years that asked nothing back. I could not bear to spoil it with the truth."*

The bond pulsed between them, and through it Wendell felt what the god felt: the ache of the missing heart, vast and hollow, like a wound the size of the sky. And underneath the ache, something else. Something that made Wendell's eyes sting.

Loneliness. Thirteen years of it, pressed flat into a wolf's body, walking beside humans who could not hear it, sleeping alone above the clouds with only the bones of the faithful for company.

"Well," Wendell said roughly, scrubbing a hand across their face. "You're stuck with me now. Blood rite. No take-backs. That's the law, even among gods. Especially among gods."

This time the smile was unmistakable—a huff of breath, a tilt of the great head, warmth flooding the bond like sunlight through the seam in the rock.

Outside, the copper light deepened toward red. Somewhere far below, muffled by miles of cloud and stone, the mountain shuddered—a long rolling groan, the third such tremor in two days. The thirteenth moon was waning. The seal was weakening. Wendell had felt it growing stronger with every league they climbed: the heart beating faster, the oath-marks burning hotter, the dreams pressing closer to the surface of sleep.

Orsen went still, head lifting toward the tremor, and when the voice came again it had lost its gentleness.

*"There is something you must understand, Wendell, now that you know what I am. Now that you carry what I lost."* The wolf crossed the chamber and stood over them, close enough that Wendell could see the individual hairs of its coat stirring in the draft from the seam, could see that the amber eyes were not entirely wolf-shaped after all. *"The moon turns in six days. When it completes its turn, the seal breaks whether the heart is found or not—and every old god still imprisoned will wake hungry, and they will come for my heart first, because with it they can end me forever and begin the war they have dreamed of through all their long captivity."*

"I know. The oracle told me. That's why we're here, isn't it? To decide." Wendell's hand rested on the pack. On the heart. "Return it to you, and you fight them. Destroy it, and everything ends—them, the new gods, the church's miracles, your survival. Everything."

*"Everything,"* Orsen agreed.

The chamber was very quiet. In the corner, Aldric's bones kept their four-century vigil. The copper light crawled slowly up the wall.

"And you'll ask me for it," Wendell said. "When the time comes. You'll ask for your heart back."

The wolf was silent a moment too long.

*"I will ask,"* Orsen said at last, and the layered voice was careful, and the care in it frightened Wendell more than any threat the church had ever posed. *"And Wendell—when I ask, you must remember this room. Remember that I chose a wolf's body over a war. Remember that I watched my servants die rather than feed them lies. Remember who I was before the hunger began."*

"Before?"

The wolf turned away, toward the seam of dying light, and its shadow stretched long and strange across the sand.

*"The heart has been whispering to you,"* it said. *"I can feel it through the bond. It whispers to me too, even from inside that cloak. And I am not certain, anymore, which of its promises I would refuse."*
