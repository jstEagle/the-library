## 18. Loyalty vs. Truth

The den was a hollow in the world, a cave behind the fall-line of a frozen waterfall, and it smelled of stone and old snow and something underneath both—something like the air after lightning, like the inside of a temple no one had swept in thirteen years.

Wendell stood just inside the entrance, the oath-cloak bundled against their chest, and watched the wolf lie down on the cold floor as though it were a throne.

"You," Wendell said. Their voice came out cracked from the climb. "Say it again."

The wolf's eyes caught the thin grey light that leaked through the ice. They were not wolf's eyes. They had never been wolf's eyes, not since Kestrel Notch, not since the first time Wendell had looked too long and felt *seen*. Now they held a depth no animal should carry, a patience worn smooth by centuries, and when the mouth opened, the voice that came out was the mountain speaking through a throat of fur and blood.

*You have carried my heart well, oath-sworn.*

Thirteen years ago, kneeling on a moss-choked altar with a knife laid flat across both palms, Wendell had heard that voice for the first time. It had said: *I accept.* Then it had said nothing more, ever again, and Wendell had spent a decade telling themselves they had dreamed it, that gods did not speak to smugglers, that the silence was simply what silence was.

Now it filled the den like water fills a cup.

"Orsen." Saying the name aloud felt like setting down a weight so old Wendell had forgotten they carried it. The god's name sat strange in the small room, too large for the walls. "It was you. All this time. The wolf on the pass. You've been—" Wendell stopped. Started again. "You sent your heart away. That's why you went quiet. That's why they couldn't find you. You made yourself *small*."

*Small enough to survive,* Orsen said. *A god who cannot be found cannot be unmade. I scattered myself into flesh and hid the burning part of me where only an oath could follow it. And one came. One always comes.* The great head lowered, chin resting on forepaws, and the eyes closed briefly—an old creature's weariness, or a performance of it. *You have walked farther for me than any servant in three ages of the world. Bring it here, Wendell Zhou. Let me be whole.*

The bundle in Wendell's arms pulsed once against their ribs, warm as a sleeping bird.

Wendell did not move toward it. Instead they slid down the wall until they were sitting, knees up, because the last two days were finally arriving all at once—the vault, the prayer-iron chains snapping like harp strings, Brannic's face as the bolt took him, Vael's crossbow lowering by inches, the long run upward through snow that came past the knee. Sitting felt honest. Standing felt like a lie about how much strength remained.

"Before I do anything," Wendell said, "tell me what happened in there. In the vault. When I cut the chains."

*I woke further,* Orsen said simply.

"That's not an answer."

*It is the whole answer.* The eyes opened again. *Every year of my imprisonment, I grew smaller. Quieter. I learned to want little, because wanting much was agony. But near the heart—in its presence, touching its power—I remember what I was. I remember the size of my wanting.* A pause, and then, softer, almost gentle: *I remember what I promised you, long ago. Shelter on the roads. My name over your name. No harm done under my mountains while you drew breath. Have I not kept it? Did I not come for you at the Notch? On the Stair? Every mile, oath-sworn. Every mile.*

"Yes," Wendell said quietly. "You did."

The bandits at Kestrel Notch, scattered like leaves. The ambush at the Weeping Stair, ended by a grey shape out of the dark moving faster than anything alive should move. The wolf had bled for them twice. It had nearly died once, and Wendell had sat up all night with their hands buried in its fur waiting for breathing that kept almost stopping.

That was the trap of it. That was the thing that made Wendell's chest ache even now. Everything the god said was true.

"Then hear me," Orsen said, and the den itself seemed to lean in. "When I am whole, the war that your church fears will not begin—it will *end*, in a day. The new gods are thieves and children playing at divinity. Their miracles are borrowed light. I was old when their kind first learned to whisper. Give me back what is mine and I will sweep them from Thornfell before the next moon turns, and there will be peace such as these valleys have never known. Real peace. Peace with teeth in it."

"And the people who pray to them?" Wendell asked. "Greyhollow's full of them. Half the border towns. Kids lighting candles to the Lantern God because their mothers did."

*They will grieve,* Orsen said, *as all faithful grieve. And then they will forget, as all faithful forget. I will be gentle with them, Wendell. I swear it by the oath between us. And you—you will want for nothing again. No more salt runs in winter. No more hiding in tunnels like something hunted. I will raise you higher than any priest of theirs ever climbed. Name it. A house. Land. A life where no one can touch you or yours. You have earned rewards beyond measure, and I am a god who pays his debts.*

There it was. The offer, spread out gleaming on the cold stone floor like contraband on a merchant's table.

And beneath it, faint but unmistakable now that Wendell knew to listen, the other sound: the heart, pulsing against their chest through layers of wool and oath, whispering in a language without words. *Take. Take. Take.* It whispered the same thing to everything near it. It had whispered it to Brannic until Brannic turned on the only friend he had left. It had whispered it to Vael until she'd stood in the snow with her sister's name in her mouth and her crossbow shaking in her hands.

It was whispering it now through Orsen's own voice. *Reward beyond measure. Peace with teeth. Sweep them away.*

Wendell looked at the wolf—at the god—and let themselves see it clearly for the first time since the den began.

The fur along the shoulders had gone coarse and dark where it had been silver. One forepaw tapped the stone, restless, arrhythmic, though the voice stayed calm. And the eyes—when the light shifted, the eyes were not the patient grey of the pass anymore. There was amber in them, deepening, and in the amber something hungry moved the way fire moves under ice.

"You're changing," Wendell said. "Being near it. Even like this—even mortal—you're already changing."

*I am becoming* myself*.*

"No." Wendell shook their head slowly. "You're becoming what the heart wants you to be. There's a difference, and I think you can't see it anymore, and that frightens me more than Vael does."

Silence. The waterfall ticked and groaned behind its shell of ice.

*Careful, oath-sworn,* Orsen said, and the gentleness had a new edge under it, thin as wire. *You speak to a god.*

"I speak to someone who bled for me," Wendell said. "Who slept across the cave mouth every night so the cold would hit its body instead of mine. Who carried me down the Weeping Stair with three arrows in its flank and never once complained. That's not a god. Gods don't bleed. That's my *friend*, and the friend is still in there somewhere, and I'm talking to him, and he'd better listen."

The tapping paw stopped.

For a long moment the wolf only breathed, and Wendell watched the amber flicker, gutter, flare—watched something vast wrestle something vaster inside a body the size of a pony—and then the eyes dimmed toward grey, not all the way, not cleanly, the way muddy water almost clears.

*You would refuse me.* Not angry. Wondering. As if refusal were a phenomenon it had read about in old texts.

"I would."

*I saved your life four times.*

"And you know I'd give it back five. That's not what this is about and you know that too." Wendell pulled the bundle tighter against their chest—not clutching it away from the god, exactly, but the instinct was there and they didn't bother hiding it. "Brannic held this thing for less than a minute. Less than a minute, Orsen. He swore the same oath I did. He walked the same miles. And the heart got into him so fast he was ready to kill me before I finished standing up. It doesn't matter how good you are. Nothing is good enough for this. Not a god. Especially not a god."

*I am stronger than your Brannic.*

"You were stronger than Brannic *before*. Listen to yourself. Listen to the promises you're making. 'Sweep them away.' 'Peace with teeth.' Thirteen years ago you told me to keep my head down and stay invisible, that pride was how servants of dead gods got killed. Now you want a war won in a day and a servant raised on a hill. That's not your voice. That's its voice wearing yours."

*And if you are wrong?* The growl lived low in the words now, barely leashed. *If the corruption you fear is only my true self returning, and you stand between a god and its own heart—what then, Wendell? What will you do?*

Wendell had been asking themselves the same question since the tree line, and the answer hadn't changed, and the answer hurt worse than any wound taken on this road.

"I'll say no," Wendell said. "Again. As many times as it takes."

*Why?* And suddenly it wasn't a challenge. It was almost a plea, raw under the divinity, the way a child asks *why* about a punishment it half understands. *I have been alone in the dark for thirteen years. Chained in my own prison with nothing but my memory, and half that memory is the theft, the betrayal, my own kin standing around my broken body deciding I was worth less than my parts. Do you know what that does to a mind, oath-sworn? I am owed. I am owed everything. And you hold the only thing in the world that can make the ledger balance.*

"I know." Wendell's throat was tight. "I know what they did to you. I dreamed it, remember? I carry it now too. The theft. Your brothers and sisters standing in a ring with your heart in a box between them, weighing whether to destroy it or use it, and using it winning. I know why you hate them. I'd hate them too."

*Then—*

"But here's the thing I learned from watching Brannic die, and from watching Vael almost die, and from watching you change one shade darker every hour we spend together." Wendell leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough to see the individual hairs stirring in the cold air off the ice. "Everyone who touches this thing starts keeping a ledger. Everyone. It whispers *owed* into whoever holds it, and the arithmetic always ends the same way—with everyone else owing them, and a war to collect. The old gods stole it because they thought the future owed them. The new gods hunt it because the present owes them. You want it back because the past owes you. It's the same debt, Orsen. Same numbers, different dates. And if I give it to you, in a hundred years some other smuggler is going to be sitting in some other cave listening to some other god explain why *this* time the war will end things forever."

The den was very quiet.

*You speak of wars,* Orsen said at last, *and I ask you to consider the alternative. When the thirteenth moon completes its turn—two nights from now, Wendell, perhaps less—the seal breaks regardless. The old gods wake whether I lead them or not. Unleashed, leaderless, each certain of what it is owed. That is not peace. That is a storm with no eye. At least with me whole, the storm has a shape. At least with me whole, someone strong stands at the front of it.*

"So don't stand at the front of it," Wendell said. "Stand *against* it."

The amber flared. *What did you say?*

"There's another option and you know it, because you helped me see it in the vault." Wendell's hands were steady. That surprised them. Thirteen years of staying invisible, of never choosing anything bigger than a route or a buyer, and now their hands were steady. "The heart isn't just power. It's the *reason*. Every divine thing in Thornfell draws from it—the old gods chained below, the new gods in their shining churches, every miracle and answered prayer and oath-mark, including mine. Destroy it, and it all goes out. Not defeated. Just... out. Like a lamp at dawn. No war because there's nothing to fight with and nothing worth fighting over. The gods go quiet—not imprisoned this time, just *mortal-shaped*, living whatever lives they can live. The way you're living now."

*You are asking a god to consent to its own diminishment.*

"I'm asking my friend to survive."

*Survival as what?* The wolf rose, at last, to its feet, and even diminished it filled the den, shadow climbing the ice walls. *As this? Fur and hunger and a lifespan of ten winters? As a beast that remembers being a mountain? You would leave me a ghost in a skin.*

"I'd leave you *alive*," Wendell said, "and free, and clean. No hunger in you that isn't yours. No ledger. No whispers." They swallowed. "I've thought about what I'd want, if someone gave me the choice between getting everything I'm owed and staying the person I actually am. And I know what I'd pick. Because I've met what I'd become otherwise. I've seen it in Brannic's eyes and Vael's hands and yours."

*And if I refuse?* The voice was very soft now. Very dangerous. *If I tell you that I will have my heart, that you will place it between my teeth yourself or I will take it from your cooling hands—what is your oath worth then, little smuggler? What will you do?*

Wendell stood up. Legs aching, back screaming, hands steady.

"Then I'll fight you," they said. "Not to win. I can't win. But I'll make you kill me for it, and I think—I *hope*—that somewhere under all that amber there's still enough of you left that you can't."

The wolf stared.

And Wendell watched the war happen. Watched it in real time, the way they'd watched weather come down a valley—first the far-off darkness, then the wind, then the moment of terrible stillness before the break. The amber surged. The grey fought back. The heart pulsed against Wendell's chest like a second heartbeat trying to sync with theirs, whispering *take, take, take* even now, offering them everything, offering them Orsen's gratitude, offering them a god's eternal favor if they would only walk those three steps and put the bundle down—

Three steps. It would take three steps.

Wendell did not take them.

And slowly—like ice going out of a river in spring, grudgingly, in groaning pieces—the amber drained. The great head lowered. The growl unwound from the air. What remained was tired, and grey-eyed, and mortal-sized grief in a too-large body.

*You would truly destroy it,* Orsen said. *Even knowing what it costs you. Your marks. Your strength on the road. Whatever small favors the old bond still grants you.*

"They're already burning away," Wendell said, and showed their forearm, where the oath-marks that had ridden their skin since the altar stood out pale and fading, like scars remembering they used to be wounds. "Been fading since the vault. I figure I've got maybe a day of whatever's left in them. So let's spend it well."

*On what?*

"On the summit." Wendell nodded upward, toward the peak lost in cloud above the den, where the old altar stones waited under snow—the high shrine, the oldest one, the one every dream had pointed to. "The thirteenth moon rises tomorrow night. The seal breaks. Every god in Thornfell will feel it and start moving, and most of them will be moving toward that summit, because that's where the door is. If we're going to end this—if we're going to end it *all*—it has to happen there, at the exact moment the seal opens, before anything arrives that can stop us. Which means we climb tonight."

*And Vael?* Something that might have been wry humor touched the great voice, faint as sun through smoke. *Your inquisitor. The one who aimed her bolt at your heart and then could not loose it. Will she climb with us?*

"She broke me out of her own stockade," Wendell said. "She gave me back my blade. She's either the bravest person I've ever met or she's setting up the longest con in church history, and honestly, either way, I want her at my back tomorrow." Wendell shouldered the pack, wincing. "Besides. Someone has to keep me honest. Apparently I can't even say no to a god without an audience."

The wolf huffed—a real sound, an animal sound, and for one heartbeat it was just a wolf again, tired and huge and glad in some simple way. Then it turned toward the cave mouth, toward the white and endless climb, and the last of the light caught its eyes, and Wendell saw that the amber had not entirely gone. A thread of it remained, deep at the center, patient as a coal under ash.

Waiting.

*Climb, then,* Orsen said, and stepped out into the snow. *But know this, oath-sworn, and weigh it on the road: tomorrow, when the seal breaks and the sky opens, you will not be saying no to a promise. You will be saying no to everything I am. And I do not yet know—which of us is stronger. The love. Or the wanting.*

Wendell followed it out into the falling dusk, the heart beating against their chest like a countdown, and did not answer.

There wasn't an answer. There was only the climb, and the moon coming, and one night left to decide what loyalty was allowed to cost.

* * *

They made the high col by midnight, snow to the waist in the drifts, the wind screaming across the saddle like something with a grievance. Below and behind them, far down the southern slopes, fires had begun to appear—small at first, then dozens, then a hundred points of orange strung across the black mountainside like a constellation fallen to earth.

Vael, somewhere ahead with her company, or the old gods rising, or the new gods' pilgrims marching on the summit. Wendell couldn't tell. Maybe no one could, anymore. Maybe the distinction had already stopped mattering.

The wolf paused at the top of the col, breath steaming, and looked north.

North, where the sky above Mount Ceren's peak was beginning—very faintly, very wrongly—to glow.

"It's early," Wendell said. "The moon isn't even up yet. The seal shouldn't be weakening for another day."

The wolf's ears flattened. And from the summit, rolling down through the wind and the snow and thirteen years of silence, came a sound Wendell felt in their teeth before they heard it with their ears:

Stone, somewhere far above, beginning to open.
