## 19. The Thirteenth Turn

The summit shrine of Mount Ceren had not been climbed in thirteen years, and it showed. Snow filled the bowl of the altar like milk in a cup. The prayer-iron ring that had once held a hundred votive ribbons stood bare, its chains rusted to the color of dried blood. Above it all hung the sky—vast, black, and crowded with stars that seemed, tonight, to be leaning closer.

Wendell climbed the last stretch of switchbacks with the heart wrapped against their chest, inside the oath-cloak, and still they could feel it. Not warmth exactly. *Attention.* The way a sleeping animal feels when you carry it—weight that knows it is being carried.

The wolf walked ahead, breaking trail through knee-deep drifts without effort, as though the snow remembered being afraid of him. Behind, Vael came up the mountain at a soldier's pace, steady and uncomplaining, her crossbow slung across her back and her church-given miracles conspicuously absent. She had not prayed since the stockade. Wendell had noticed. He suspected she had noticed him noticing.

"Tell me again," Vael said, breath steaming, "why we're delivering the thing instead of burying it in a river."

"Because burying it doesn't work." Wendell's voice was hoarse from three days of climbing. "The old gods sealed it once, in chains of prayer-iron under a mountain, with a god standing guard over it, and it still got out enough to poison everything. You don't bury a fire. You starve it or you feed it. Tonight we choose which."

"And your choice is?"

Wendell didn't answer. The truth was he hadn't made it—not fully, not in the way Vael deserved. He had made half a choice on the Weeping Stair, when he'd cut his palm and bound himself to the wolf; another half in the vault beneath Ceren, when he'd wrapped the screaming heart in cloth that still carried his oath. The rest of the choice waited at the top of this mountain, and it had teeth.

They crested the final ridge as the sun went down behind the western peaks, and the shrine opened before them: a circle of standing stones older than any church, older than any kingdom, each one carved with a god's name in a script no living scholar could read. In the center, the altar. On the altar, a shallow depression shaped like nothing so much as an open hand.

The wolf stopped at the edge of the circle and would go no further.

"It can't cross," Wendell said quietly. "Old ground. Consecrated before it fell silent. It's not allowed."

"Not allowed," Vael repeated. "By whom?"

"By whatever's left of the law that put the heart in the ground in the first place." Wendell stepped past the wolf, and felt the animal's eyes on his back like twin coals. "Which means whatever happens here tonight, I do it alone."

He knelt at the altar and began unwrapping the cloak.

* * *

Sera Vael had killed men on worse ground than this, but she had never stood on ground that *hated* her quite so personally.

She felt it the moment she crossed into the stone circle—a pressure against her skin, against something deeper than skin, like wading upstream. The old gods' country. She was a servant of the new gods, and this mountaintop remembered what her faith had done, even if her faith insisted it had never happened.

She kept her hand near her crossbow anyway. Habit. Doctrine. Both.

Below them, Thornfell spread out in the blue dusk—valleys going dark one by one, the smoke of evening fires rising straight up in the windless cold. Somewhere down there were her riders, forty of the church's best, whom she had ordered to hold position at the tree line with a directness that would cost her her rank if she survived the night. Somewhere down there too, she knew, were things that were not riders. Things the earthquakes had shaken loose. Things the answered prayers had fed.

For three days the world had been fraying, and Vael had watched it happen from the saddle beside the smuggler she should have executed. Shrines bleeding light. Wells running bitter. A chapel in Lowmark where every candle lit itself and burned green until the priest tore the building down with his own hands. The seal was failing. Whatever cage the old gods had built around their stolen heart—and around their own silence—it was coming apart, and everything divine in Thornfell was pressing against the seams.

Her doctrine said this was good. Her doctrine said the old powers dying meant the war dying unborn, meant peace, meant her sister's death finally meaning something other than collateral damage in a conflict between things that wore mountains like coats.

Her doctrine had been very quiet lately.

"Wendell." She watched the smuggler peel back the last fold of the oath-cloak, and had to look away from what lay beneath. "When you make your choice—whatever it is—make it fast. They're already moving."

"I know." Wendell's hands hovered over the heart. "I can feel them coming. Like weather."

"What does it feel like? Exactly."

Wendell was quiet a moment. "Like the moment before an ambush," he said. "When you know the road is wrong but you can't say why yet."

Vael turned and looked south, toward the pass, and saw it: light where there should have been no light. Not torchlight. Torchlight flickered. This rose off the peaks in columns, pale gold and patient, like the mountains themselves had opened their eyes.

"They're converging," she said. "All of them."

* * *

The heart lay bare on the altar, and Wendell understood for the first time why the old gods had gone to war over it.

It was beautiful the way a forest fire is beautiful from a ridge—the way it makes you forget, for one treacherous heartbeat, that beauty is not the same as mercy. It did not look like a heart. It looked like a piece of the first morning of the world, folded small: light that moved like water, that breathed, that *wanted*. And underneath the wanting, faint as a pulse through a wall, Wendell could feel the hunger Brannic must have felt in the vault, the whisper Vael had heard in the stockade. *Take me in. Be more. Be more. Be more.*

His oath-marks ached down both arms—thirteen years of faded scars suddenly hot, suddenly awake, as though the ink remembered what it had sworn to.

"You're early," Wendell told it. His voice cracked on the second word. "You weren't supposed to wake until the moon turned."

The heart pulsed. Somewhere far below, deep in the roots of Mount Ceren, something vast shifted in its sleep, and the mountain rang like a struck bell—a sound felt in the teeth rather than heard. Snow slid from the standing stones. One of the ancient carvings, a name worn smooth by ten thousand winters, began to glow.

Then the second one. Then the third.

"The names are waking," Wendell called back to Vael. "Every god who ever had a shrine on this mountain—they're waking up, and they're all looking at us."

"How long?"

Wendell looked east. Over the shoulder of the world, low and swollen and the color of old iron, the thirteenth moon was rising.

It had taken thirteen years to complete its turn through the hidden houses of the sky—the drowned oracle had explained it in her chapel full of black water, counting months on webbed fingers while Wendell's blood ran cold. Thirteen years of silence. Thirteen years of imprisonment. And now the last turn, the last hours, the last few miles of sky between the moon and the place where its cycle ended and the seal ended with it.

"We have until it clears the ridge," Wendell said. "Maybe less."

* * *

From the tree line, four miles below, Sera Vael's second-in-command watched the lights come down out of the high country and sent no report, because there was no one left to report to who would believe it.

There were seven of the lights. No—nine. They moved the way avalanches move, inevitable and unhurried, each one trailing a wake of disturbed air. Where the first passed over a frozen lake, the ice sang. Where the second crossed a shepherd's hut, every sheep within a mile went mad and broke through their pen as one body, fleeing downhill in perfect silence.

The old gods were coming to reclaim their heart. Or to destroy it. Or simply to arrive, after thirteen years of silence, and see with their own ancient eyes what had become of the world while they slept.

And above them, higher than any of it, the thirteenth moon climbed.

* * *

Wendell stood at the altar with his hand hovering an inch above the heart, and could not close the distance.

This was the moment he had climbed for. This was the choice the whole rotten year had funneled down to: drive the oath-blade through it and end every god in Thornfell forever—or give it back, and let Orsen rise whole and terrible and victorious, and let the war come, and let the world burn clean.

Except it wasn't two choices anymore. That was the trap he'd only just seen, standing here with the light of the thing washing over his hands. The seal was breaking regardless. When the thirteenth moon completed its turn, the prison opened whether the heart sat on this altar or rotted at the bottom of the sea. Which meant destroying the heart wasn't ending the war anymore.

It was ending the *combatants.*

Every old god stirring toward this mountain right now was drawing power from the same wellspring the heart had been cut from. Destroy the heart, and they didn't march home defeated. They unraveled mid-stride, all of them, everywhere, along with every miracle, every blessing, every answered prayer—including the new gods', including the church's, including—

Including Orsen's.

Wendell looked back at the edge of the circle, where the wolf sat in the snow, motionless, watching him with eyes that had spoken with a god's voice in a den above the clouds. *Give me my heart,* Orsen had said there, in that voice like rockfall, like hearthfire, like every winter night of Wendell's childhood. *Give it back and I will win the war before it starts. I will reward you beyond measure. You have been faithful, little shadow. Faithfulness deserves its wage.*

Beyond measure. Beyond measure was a word for people who hadn't watched Brannic die with the heart's light pouring out of his mouth.

"Wendell." Vael's voice, tight. "The moon."

He looked. The thirteenth moon had cleared the eastern ridge, huge and rust-colored, and was climbing with a speed no moon should have—as though the sky itself were hurrying now, eager to be done with thirteen years of waiting.

The glowing names on the standing stones brightened. The pressure against Vael's skin became a wind that blew inward, toward the altar, toward the heart, drawn like breath to a flame. Far below, the nine lights quickened. The mountain rang again, louder, and this time cracks spidered up through the snowpack around the shrine, weeping threads of golden light from deep in the rock.

The seal wasn't breaking. It was *shattering.*

"Wendell!" Vael drew her crossbow—not aiming at him, aiming past him, at the tree line, at the lights, at nothing, because there was nothing in the world worth aiming at anymore except the act of defiance itself. "Whatever you're going to do—"

"I know." Wendell pulled the oath-blade from his belt.

Thirteen years ago he had sworn it to a silent god on a moss-choked altar: *my blade, my road, my life, until you speak or until I die.* The god had never spoken. The god had been a wolf in the snow all along, wearing its own silence like a wound. And now the blade in Wendell's hand hummed against its sheath-leather, awake, alive, remembering the oath it had been forged to keep—and Wendell understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the oath had never been to a god at all.

It had been to the thing that stayed. The thing that walked beside him through the passes. The thing that bled for him at Kestrel Notch and spoke to him in the den and asked, with a mortal throat, for its heart back.

Loyalty to a person. Truth to a god. Thirteen years thinking they were the same thing.

The moon touched the crown of the sky. The standing stones blazed. The nine lights reached the snowline and began, impossibly, to climb. And at the edge of the circle, the wolf rose to his feet and took one step forward onto consecrated ground he should not have been able to touch—and the ground let him, because the law was breaking too, everything was breaking, the whole architecture of the old world coming down at once—

"Wendell," the wolf said, in Orsen's voice, and it was not a command this time. It was almost gentle. "It's time."

Wendell raised the oath-blade over the heart.

The heart looked up at him—all of him, every version of him, the smuggler and the mercenary and the child who had prayed at a ruined altar because silence was safer than hope—and offered him everything, one last time, in a voice like his own mother's, like Maren's, like forgiveness.

And Wendell brought the blade down.

* * *

Sera Vael would remember the sound for the rest of her life, however long that proved to be.

Not a scream. Not a shatter. A *sigh*—enormous beyond comprehension, the sigh of something that had been holding its breath for thirteen years and finally, gratefully, let go. The light of the heart went out like a lamp pinched between fingers. The golden threads in the mountain's cracks guttered and died. The nine lights climbing through the snow faltered, flickered, and came apart into ordinary darkness, and across all of Thornfell, in a hundred shrines and a thousand dreams, the old gods stopped mid-stride and were gone—not dead, perhaps, but *unmade*, unwritten, returned to the quiet they had come from.

The new gods went with them. Vael felt it leave her like a tooth pulled—the small warm presence at the back of her skull that had answered her prayers since childhood, simply absent, the way a room is absent of a person who has died. She sank to her knees in the snow. She did not pray. There was nothing left to pray to.

Above the shrine, the thirteenth moon finished its turn, hung for one long breath at the crown of the sky—

—and set, in the east, against every law the heavens had ever obeyed, sinking down behind the ridge it had risen from, taking the age of gods down with it into the dark.

Silence. Real silence, the kind the world had not known in thirteen years. Just wind, and snow, and the sound of a man breathing hard over an empty altar, an oath-blade buried to the hilt in ash where a heart had been.

Wendell pulled the blade free. The oath-marks on his arms were burning—fading—flaking away like old paint, and he watched thirteen years of servitude dissolve into bare skin and felt, unexpectedly, like crying.

At the edge of the circle, the wolf swayed. Sat down hard in the snow. The glow went out of his eyes, the vastness out of his presence, and what remained was smaller—still huge for a wolf, still grey as stormcloud, but *mortal* now, breathing in white plumes, blinking at the world with ordinary animal wonder.

"Orsen?" Wendell crossed the circle in six strides and dropped to his knees in the snow beside him.

The wolf looked at him for a long time. When he spoke, the voice was still Orsen's voice—but thinner now. Smaller. A hearthfire banked to embers.

"It's quiet," the god said, and there was wonder in it, and grief, and something that might have been relief. "Thirteen years I dreamed of silence. I never thought it would sound like this."

Behind them, Sera Vael got slowly to her feet, crossbow hanging forgotten at her side, and looked out over a Thornfell with no gods in it at all.

"Wendell," she said quietly. "What have we done?"

And down in the valleys below, all across the darkening land, the first screams began.
