## 20. Unmaking

The summit shrine of Mount Ceren had not been built by human hands.

Wendell had known that the moment they crested the last ridge and saw it: a ring of standing stones taller than any man, each one veined with silver that pulsed like slow heartbeats, arranged around an altar of black glass that had been poured, not carved. The old gods had raised this place when Thornfell was young and wild, back when worship was not a transaction but a conversation. Now frost sheathed the stones. Now the silver veins flickered erratically, like a lamp running out of oil.

And now the sky was full of gods.

They came as light first. That was how Wendell had always imagined divinity would arrive—not as thunder or glory, but as a wrongness in the air, a pressure behind the eyes. To the north, over the Greyhollow road, a column of white radiance stood up from the horizon like a pillar holding up heaven. To the south, above the drowned chapel's valley, something green and many-limbed unfolded against the stars, vast as weather. East and west, more lights, more shapes, some beautiful and some so alien that Wendell's mind slid off them like rain off oiled leather.

The old gods, waking. Converging. Coming to claim what was theirs—or to destroy it before anyone else could hold it.

"They can see us," Vael said beside them. Her voice was steady, but her knuckles were white on her crossbow. "The whole mountain is lit up like a signal fire."

"It *is* a signal fire," Wendell said. "It has been for thirteen years. We're just the ones who finally lit it."

Between them, at the altar's edge, the wolf stood with its head lowered and its ears flat. It had not spoken since the den above the cloudline. It did not need to. Wendell could feel Orsen through the blood-bond now—a constant low ache, like a tooth gone bad, like grief with a pulse. The god's attention was fixed on the bundle in Wendell's arms: their oath-cloak, wrapped around something the size of a newborn child, something that beat.

*Give it to me,* the ache said, without words. *I will end this. I will end all of it.*

"You said that already," Wendell murmured aloud, and Vael glanced at them, hearing only half the conversation. "You said it sweeter the first time."

The moon was rising.

Not one moon. Wendell had spent their whole life under Thornfell's sky and had never questioned why the almanacs spoke of thirteen moons when only twelve ever showed. The thirteenth rose now for the second time in living memory—thin, bruise-colored, hanging low over the eastern peaks like a wound in the world. As it climbed, the seal beneath the shrine groaned. Wendell felt it in their boots, in their teeth, in the oath-marks that coiled from wrist to shoulder: thirteen spirals of old-rite ink and scar tissue, burning hotter with every heartbeat.

Below them, the mountain shook. Somewhere far down, in the vault they'd fled three days ago, prayer-iron chains were snapping one by one.

"The seal breaks fully when the moon clears the ridge," Vael said. She'd done the arithmetic herself; she was good at arithmetic, at toll records and supply lines and the cold logistics of war. "When it does, every god in that sky arrives at once. And whatever happens then—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I've seen what happens when two of them touch. In the visions. There won't be a Thornfell after."

"There might not be a world after," Wendell said. "Brannic saw it too, at the end. That's why he reached for the heart. Not greed. Fear." They looked down at the wrapped bundle. Through the cloak's weave, a red glow seeped, pulsing in time with the silver veins in the stones. "He understood faster than I did. This thing doesn't want to be returned. It wants to be *used*. Returning it just picks which hand holds the whip."

"And destroying it?" Vael asked. But she knew. They had struck the bargain in the stockade, and she had not reneged, even when her own church's miracles began to gutter, even when her superiors sent riders to drag her back in chains and she'd left them bleeding in the snow. She had chosen. She was only asking to hear it said once more, the way soldiers ask a plan be repeated before the horns sound.

"Destroying it ends everything," Wendell said. "Every miracle. Every answered prayer. Every oath-magic, every blessing, every curse. The new gods' voices go silent in their cathedrals. The old gods collapse into stone and story." Their throat tightened. "And my patron goes out like a candle."

The wolf's head turned. Amber eyes caught the thirteenth moon's light.

*I know,* the ache said. For the first time since the den, there was no hunger in it. Only a terrible, patient sadness. *I have known since you drew the blade at the Weeping Stair. A god knows its own death, little smuggler. It is the one prayer we are never allowed to answer.*

"I'm sorry," Wendell said.

*Don't be.* The wolf stepped closer, pressing its great grey flank against their leg, the way it had on the long cold nights in the high passes when warmth was survival and pride was a luxury. *Thirteen years I lay in the dark of my own flesh, listening to the world forget me. Do you know what I learned? That silence is not the worst thing. The worst thing is becoming the kind of thing that would burn the world to keep from being silent.* A breath moved through the bond, warm and ragged. *You are giving me back to myself. Whatever is left when the fire goes out—that will be enough. You taught me that. You, who survived thirteen years by being small.*

Wendell knelt. Set the bundle on the black-glass altar. Unwrapped the cloak.

Orsen's heart lay bare under the bruised moon.

It was smaller than they remembered from the vault—divinity condensed, perhaps, by everything it had endured—but no less terrible. It was not flesh. It was a knot of light and iron, veins of silver branching through a core of molten gold, turning slowly, endlessly, like a wheel that had been rolling since before the mountains were named. Where its glow touched the frost, the frost did not melt; it *sang*, thin crystalline notes rising off the stones. Where it touched Wendell's shadow, the shadow writhed.

And it whispered.

It had whispered to Brannic until he wept and reached for it with both hands. It had whispered to Vael until she'd pressed her crossbow to her own temple to make it stop. It whispered to Wendell now—not in Orsen's voice, but in a chorus, hundreds of voices braided together, offering everything: *your mother's face, your brother's name, the road home, the war unwaged, the oath unbroken, the years returned—*

"Shut up," Wendell said, and their voice cracked across the summit like a whip. "You're not him. You're what was cut out of him when he got too hungry. I've carried stolen goods my whole life, and I know contraband when I feel it."

The chorus laughed. It was the worst sound Wendell had ever heard.

Above the eastern ridge, the thirteenth moon touched stone.

The world broke open.

Every silver vein in every standing stone flared white. The seal beneath the shrine—the thing the old gods had built to imprison their traitor brother, the thing that had held Orsen's power apart from his heart for thirteen years—shattered with a sound like ten thousand bells dying at once. The shockwave knocked Vael to her knees. It lifted the wolf off its feet and slammed it down again, and Wendell heard, through the bond, a howl of agony that was also a word: *NOW.*

Because the gods were coming. Wendell felt them arrive at the edge of the summit like a tide reaching a seawall—the northern pillar of light suddenly *close*, close enough that the snow on the northern stones began to smoke; the green many-limbed shape unfolding over the southern ridge, its attention landing on the altar like a physical weight. More behind them. More behind those. The old gods, freed of the seal that had caged them alongside their prisoner, pouring toward the heart with thirteen years of starvation in every line of them.

And among the lights, Wendell felt something else: the new gods, far away but leaning in, their church-candles guttering as their power strained toward the same prize. Two pantheons converging on one point. One heartbeat of time before contact.

One chance.

Wendell drew the oath-blade.

It was not a fine weapon. Maren had given it to them eleven years ago, hilt-worn and slightly crooked, saying *a smuggler's blade should look worthless.* They had sworn their oath on it at the ruined altar at nineteen years old, kneeling in moss and rain, and the oath-marks had bloomed up their arm like frost flowers. Since then it had opened crates and cut ropes and, exactly once, defended their life. It had never been meant for this.

But it was soaked in oath. Thirteen years of loyalty lived in the steel. And the heart—Wendell understood this now, with the clarity that comes only at the very end of things—could only be unmade by something that had never wanted power. Not a god's hand. Not a hero's ambition. Just a blade that had been used, honestly, for small things, by someone who had stayed small on purpose.

"Wendell." Vael's voice, raw. She had risen. Her crossbow was leveled—not at Wendell, at the northern light, absurdly, uselessly, because she was Sera Vael and she would die facing the enemy even if the enemy was a god. "Whatever happens after—I want you to know the church was wrong about you. About all of this. I was wrong."

"You were right about the war," Wendell said. "That counts."

The wolf limped to the altar's edge and stood beside them, shoulder to shoulder, facing the converging lights. *With you,* the bond said. *To the last breath and past it.*

The first god crossed the ridge.

It did not walk. It *arrived*, unfolding into the shrine's ring like night falling upward, a shape of white silence and enormous patience, and its attention fell upon the heart and Wendell saw, in the space of a single heartbeat, what thirteen years of imprisonment had made of the old gods: not wise, not ancient and serene, but *starving*. The light reached for the altar. Behind it, the green shape crested the southern stones. Behind that, more. The sky itself seemed to lean.

Wendell raised the blade.

The heart screamed. All its hundred voices at once, offering, begging, threatening—*I can give you your patron back whole, I can give you the roads forever safe, I can make them remember your name, I can make you a god, I can make you anything, anything, ANYTHING—*

"No," Wendell said. "You can't. That's the whole point of you."

And they drove the oath-blade through the heart of Orsen.

There was no resistance. That was the strange part. Thirteen years of chains and seals and wars unborn, and the heart parted around honest steel like water around a stone, and for one impossible instant Wendell stood with their arm buried to the wrist in molten gold and felt everything—

Every prayer ever answered in Thornfell. Every miracle, every curse, every bargain struck at a thousand altars. The new gods' cathedrals, candlelit and singing. The old gods' shrines, moss-choked and waiting. The web of power that had lain over these mountains since before the first human footprint, vast beyond comprehension, ancient beyond memory—and threaded through all of it, small and bright and stubborn, one thread that was theirs: thirteen spirals of oath, wrist to shoulder, burning.

Then the blade's edge touched the heart's center, and the thread was cut.

The silence was total.

Not quiet—*silence*, the absence of sound the way a dead star is the absence of light. The heart's scream ended mid-note. The whispering ended. The singing frost ended. And across all of Thornfell, in the same instant, every divine voice in the world stopped speaking.

Wendell felt them go.

The northern god collapsed mid-stride—not falling, *unfalling*, its light folding inward like a banner taken down, its attention withdrawing from the world the way a tide withdraws, leaving nothing behind but wind and snow and a strange pale afterimage burned into the air. The green shape came apart into drifting motes that winked out one by one, like lanterns carried away down a long road. The others followed, east and west and south, the whole converging host of the old gods dissolving into stillness before they ever touched the summit, before the first blow of the war that would have ended everything.

The war died unborn. Wendell felt it go too—all that momentum, all that hunger, all that terrible destiny simply *unhappening*, futures closing like doors down a corridor until only one remained: a morning, ordinary and gray, in a world with no gods in it.

On the altar, the heart went dark.

The gold cooled to dull iron. The silver veins stilled. What remained was small and heavy and utterly, perfectly dead—a thing that had been eternal and now was only an object, a relic, a stone. Cracks spread across its surface with a sound like ice settling on a winter lake, and then it crumbled, all at once, into ash that the wind took south toward the drowned chapel, toward Greyhollow, toward every altar where someone had ever knelt and waited for an answer that would never come again.

The oath-blade snapped at the hilt. Wendell caught the falling piece without thinking—an old smuggler's reflex—and stared at it. Ordinary steel. Worthless. Maren would have approved.

Then the fire reached them.

It started at the wrist. The oath-marks—the thirteen spirals that had been part of them longer than any scar, that had warmed them in the high passes and steadied their hands in bad bargains and told them, always, that they belonged to something larger—ignited. Not pain, exactly. Something worse: *departure*. Wendell felt thirteen years of connection unraveling up their arm, thread by thread, each spiral flaring white-hot and then fading to pale skin, smooth and unmarked, as if the oath had never been sworn at all.

"No," they heard themselves say. "Wait—"

But there was nothing to wait for. The bond stretched thinner, thinner, a rope fraying strand by strand, and through the last of it Wendell felt Orsen—felt the god's presence contracting, dimming, the vast slow intelligence that had spoken through the wolf narrowing down and down toward something small and mortal and terrified—

*Thank you,* the bond said, from very far away, in a voice that was almost human. *For staying small. For staying mine.*

And the last mark burned off Wendell's shoulder, and the bond was gone, and they were alone in their own skull for the first time in thirteen years.

It was the loneliest feeling in the world.

Vael caught them as their knees gave. Together they went down onto the frozen glass of the altar, and Wendell knelt there shaking, staring at their bare forearm—at skin that had carried a god's signature and now carried nothing—and felt the silence of Thornfell settle over the mountains like fresh snow.

No voice in the cathedrals. No answer at the altars. No power anywhere, for anyone, ever again. They had promised Vael exactly this, and Vael sat down heavily beside them and began, quietly, to weep—not grief, Wendell thought, or not only grief. Relief. The relief of a soldier setting down a pack after a march that had lasted her whole life.

The wind moved over the summit. The thirteenth moon, its purpose fulfilled, began to set—sinking behind the western peaks, growing dimmer with every moment, until it was a bruise-colored smudge, then a memory, then nothing. Twelve moons remained in the sky of Thornfell, and they were only moons.

The shrine's silver veins went dark one final time.

And in the ringing silence, Wendell became aware of a sound that did not belong to the dead and godless world: breathing. Ragged, wet, effortful breathing, somewhere near the altar's edge.

They turned.

The wolf had fallen onto its side. Its flanks heaved. Its amber eyes—eyes that had held a god's patience and a god's sorrow and a god's terrible age—were half-closed, and as Wendell crawled to it through the ash, they watched the amber fade. Watched it drain out of the iris like color out of a sunset, leaving behind something browner, softer, younger. The great grey body shuddered once, twice. The breathing slowed. Steadied.

And then the wolf opened its eyes—ordinary eyes now, a mortal animal's eyes, confused and blinking in the wind—and looked up at Wendell without recognition, without divinity, without anything except the dazed trust of a creature that has woken in pain and found a familiar face above it.

Its mouth moved. No voice came out. It tried again, and what emerged was not Orsen's thunder, not the bond's wordless ache, but a rough, rusty, entirely mortal sound—a whine, a huff, the ghost of a bark—as if a creature were trying to remember how speech worked and finding, to its surprise, that it didn't need to.

Wendell's hand hovered over its fur, afraid to land.

"Well," they said, and their voice broke on the word. "Hello, stranger."

* * *

They stayed on the summit until dawn, because neither of them could stand.

Vael dozed against a standing stone, her crossbow across her knees out of habit rather than need—there was nothing left in the world for it to bless, and precious little for it to fear. Wendell sat with the wolf's head in their lap, feeling its breath move, counting it the way they'd once counted heartbeats through a bond that no longer existed. The animal slept in fits, twitching, making small sounds. Once it woke fully and stared at its own paws for a long time, flexing them, as though checking whether they were real. Once it woke and pressed its nose hard into Wendell's palm and held it there, trembling, and Wendell understood that some part of it remembered—that whatever Orsen had become now, the shape of those thirteen years was still inside it, the way a scar outlives the wound.

Toward morning, the clouds below the summit turned pink, and Wendell looked out over a Thornfell that had never existed before: a Thornfell with no gods watching it. The valleys lay gray and quiet. Far to the south, where Greyhollow's cathedral spire caught the early light, Wendell imagined the priests waking to find their miracles gone, their voices unanswered, their whole architecture of certainty collapsed overnight into ordinary stone. Some of them would rage. Some would kneel anyway, praying to nothing, unable to stop. Most, Wendell suspected, would simply get up and start solving their problems the way people always had before the gods learned to answer—with their hands, and each other, and a great deal of complaining.

The wolf stirred. Lifted its head. Looked east at the rising sun with an expression Wendell had never seen on any animal: wonder. Pure, unguarded wonder, as if it had seen ten thousand dawns and never once, until now, been small enough to be surprised by one.

Then it looked at Wendell, and its tail moved—once, uncertainly, against the frozen glass.

Wendell laughed. It came out wet and cracked, halfway to sobbing, and they didn't care. They scratched the rough fur behind ordinary ears, and the wolf leaned into their hand with its whole weight, trusting, heavy, alive.

"Come on, then," Wendell said, when the sun had cleared the ridge and the cold began to bite in earnest. "We should get off this mountain before the church decides to blame us for the silence too." They paused. Looked down the long switchback trail, toward the passes, toward the roads, toward a life of smuggling that was over and a life of something else that hadn't started yet. "Unless you'd rather stay. I hear the view's supposed to be good."

The wolf stood on unsteady legs. Shook itself, ash flying. And then it walked to the head of the trail and sat down, looking back over its shoulder at Wendell with unmistakable expectation—the oldest gesture in the world, older than any god: *are you coming or not?*

Wendell got up. Their oath-arm was bare and light and strange, and the silence of the world sat on their shoulders like snow, and none of it, none of it, felt like loss.

They went down the mountain together, one step at a time, into a country with no gods in it—and no answers either, which meant, for the first time in thirteen years, that everything that happened next would be up to them.
