## 21. A God Made Mortal

The fire had burned down to a red heart of its own by the time Wendell finished burying the oath-blade's scabbard.

They did it without ceremony, in the soft earth just outside the den, where the mountain grass grew pale and stubborn above the cloudline. There was nothing left inside the scabbard to bury — the blade itself was gone, spent, unmade along with everything else it had touched — but the leather still carried the shape of thirteen years of carrying it. It seemed right that the ground should have that too.

Wendell patted the earth flat, sat back on their heels, and realized their hands were shaking.

Not from cold. Not from fear. The battle at the summit shrine was two days behind them now, and the gods' war had died unborn, and the silence that followed it was so complete it felt like a held breath the whole world was slowly letting out. Their hands were shaking because there was nothing left to hold them steady. No oath-marks burning along their forearms. No divine weight pressing at the back of their skull. For the first time since they were nineteen years old and kneeling before a moss-choked altar, swearing themselves to a god who never answered, Wendell Zhou was entirely, ordinary, mortal.

It felt like being naked in winter. It also felt, they admitted, like breathing.

"You're staring again," said Orsen.

The wolf lay just inside the den's mouth, chin on forepaws, grey fur silvered by the last light of the setting sun. Its voice — when it chose to use one, which was not often — came from somewhere deeper than its throat, a voice like stone settling in an old wall. But smaller now. Quieter. Every day since the unmaking it had grown a little more like an ordinary animal's growl shaped into words, and a little less like the thing that had once made mountains flinch.

"Am I?" Wendell said.

"You look at me the way mourners look at an open grave. Waiting to see if I'll sit up."

"I'm waiting to see if *you* know what you are yet."

The wolf's ear twitched. That was all. But Wendell had spent three weeks bound to this creature by blood and older things than blood, and they had learned to read whole conversations in the angle of those ears.

What they were, of course, was a question with no clean answer. The heart was destroyed. The divinity that had poured out of it — through it, around it, into every prayer-iron chain and every answering voice in Thornfell — was gone, collapsed into silence mid-stride as the old gods converged on the summit. Vael had seen it happen from twenty feet away and described it afterward, haltingly, like a woman trying to describe a color that no longer existed: *they were coming, all of them, and then they were shapes, and then they were wind.* The thirteenth moon had set on a world where prayers went up like smoke and stopped at the ceiling of the sky.

But the wolf remained. Whatever Orsen had been before — a god who sent its own heart away to survive, who wore the body of its last servant for thirteen years, who walked beside a smuggler through snow and ambush and betrayal — the flesh of it had endured the fire. And the memory. All of it. Mountains rising. The first theft. Four thousand years of watching small creatures live and die in the valleys below.

None of the hunger. Wendell checked, more than once. They had seen what divine hunger did — Brannic, hollowed out and grinning in the vault-light; Vael, crossbow trembling as the heart whispered her sister's name. They knew the signs. The wolf showed none of them. When it looked at Wendell, there was want in it, but only the ordinary kind: warmth, company, the next meal. A dog's wants. A friend's.

"Come inside," the wolf said. "You're doing your brooding in the wind again. You'll cough all night."

"I don't brood."

"You have brooded at that grave-shaped patch of dirt for two days." A pause. "I can smell the leather from here. It smells like nineteen years old and terrified. Burying it will not bury that."

Wendell snorted despite themself and crawled into the den.

* * *

The den was larger than it looked from outside — a throat of grey stone opening into a chamber floored with pine boughs and old, flattened grass, generations deep. Wolf-generations. Wendell had asked once, carefully, how long the wolf had lived here, between its duties of haunting passes and saving smugglers from bandits, and the answer had been: *since I understood that a god cannot sleep, and I was very tired.*

Now it slept. That was the strangest miracle left in Thornfell, the only one. Orsen slept curled in pine boughs like any animal, and dreamed — real dreams, small ones, Wendell could see the paws twitch — instead of the vast grinding vigilance that a god calls rest.

Tonight the wolf watched Wendell bank the fire with an expression Wendell had learned to distrust.

"You're deciding something," Wendell said.

"I am considering whether to say something."

"That's the same thing, for you."

The wolf huffed — almost a laugh, these days. Then it said, "The roads are open. The snows will come late this year; I can smell it. Greyhollow is half a week's walk down. Maren will be there, or what's left of Greyhollow's forgiveness for her. Vael sends word through the toll-keepers now instead of crossbow bolts. There is work for a guide who knows these passes better than anyone living." It paused. "There is a life down there. You keep not going back to it."

Wendell fed the fire another branch. Watched the sparks climb toward the crack of sky above the den mouth, where the first stars were coming out — fewer stars than there used to be, some swore, now that the gods were silent. Others said there were exactly as many as ever, and people were only finally looking at them properly.

"Maren sold me to the church," Wendell said. "To cover debts she owed to men worse than the church."

"Yes."

"She wept when I saw her. She meant it. She'd do it again tomorrow."

"Yes."

"And Brannic—" Wendell's jaw tightened. "Brannic I loved. Twenty years in the company. He'd have worn my face if it got him the heart. So no. There isn't a life down there waiting for me. There's a market down there, and I've seen what I fetch."

The wolf was quiet a while. Firelight moved in its eyes, amber over amber.

"When I was a god," it said at last, "people said things like that to me constantly. *There is nothing left worth loving among mortals.* They said it as worship. They thought it honored me." Its tail shifted against the pine boughs. "I never believed them. But I never had proof otherwise either. Divinity keeps you at a distance from everything you might have loved. It turns every affection into a transaction — they love what you can give them. You begin to suspect that if the giving stopped, so would the loving."

"And now?"

"Now the giving has stopped." The wolf lifted its head. In the firelight, for just a moment, Wendell saw past the grey fur to something older and vaster looking out — not hunger, never hunger anymore, but the sheer accumulated weight of four millennia of watching. "And you are here anyway. Cooking terrible food in my den. Burying leather. Talking to a beast because there is no one else who remembers what I remember." The head lowered again. "I find I wanted proof. I did not know that until I had it."

Wendell stared into the fire until their eyes stung.

"You're saying thank you," they said finally. "Four thousand years old and you can't just say thank you."

"I am a wolf," said Orsen with dignity. "We express gratitude through proximity. I have been proximate at you for three days."

That broke something loose in Wendell's chest, and they laughed — really laughed, the first time since the summit, maybe the first time in years, the kind that bent them double and wouldn't stop for a full minute. The wolf watched them laugh with an expression of patient, ancient satisfaction, and when Wendell finally wiped their eyes, the creature had scooted closer across the boughs, a deliberate six inches, proximate as promised.

* * *

Three days later, Wendell went down to the treeline and stood on the road.

They told themself it was only to look. The pass road wound away below them, brown and ordinary, heading east toward Greyhollow and the low country beyond. Smoke rose somewhere far off — hearth-smoke, honest smoke. Somewhere down there a carter would need guiding through Kestrel Notch before the first snow, and would pay in coin or bread, and would never know that the person walking him safely through had unmade every god his grandmother prayed to.

Wendell stood there for a long time, arms crossed against the wind, feeling the pull of the road the way other people felt the pull of home.

Behind them, deliberately loud on purpose — a wolf learning to be mortal was still learning that it did not need to announce itself — came the crunch of paws on frost.

"You could go down," Orsen said, stopping at Wendell's shoulder. "I would manage. I have managed alone for a very long time. I am, in fact, extremely good at it. It is one of my defining characteristics."

"I know."

"The roads were yours before they were mine. I only haunted them."

"I know that too."

"So go."

Wendell didn't move. Below, the road glittered with frost, running east, running anywhere. Thirteen years it had been their whole world — the smuggling runs, the invisible years, the safety of silence. Before that, the company, and before that, a village that no longer existed except in Orsen's memory too, because the god remembered everything and Wendell had let the god carry it.

"No," Wendell said.

The wolf said nothing. Waited. It had gotten very good at waiting, these past days — mortal patience, or perhaps it had always been patient and simply never had anything worth waiting for.

"There's nothing down there I want," Wendell said. "And there's everything up here I—" They stopped. Started again, plainer. "I spent thirteen years serving a god who couldn't answer me. Then three weeks learning that the god had been walking beside me the entire time, wanting to." They turned to look at the wolf directly, and the wind pulled at their hair, and they found the words came easier than any prayer ever had. "I'm not going back to the version where we're apart and I talk at you. If you're mortal now, then be mortal with me. That's the whole offer. Bad cooking. Terrible conversation. You'll have to teach me how to hunt, because I refuse to starve."

Something moved through the wolf's whole body then — a shiver starting at the ears and ending at the tail tip, the way a dog shakes off rain, the way something much larger once shook off the weight of heaven.

"You understand," Orsen said, and its voice cracked in the middle of the word, actually cracked, a mortal flaw in an immortal instrument, "that I would have given you kingdoms. I offered. At the end, I offered, and you refused me, and it saved me, and I have had days now to consider what it means that the finest act anyone ever performed on my behalf was telling me no."

"I remember."

"Kingdoms, Wendell. War won. Eternity rewarded. And instead you gave me—" the wolf gestured with its nose at the den, the buried scabbard, the frost, the thin bright morning, "—this. Breakfast you will burn. A mountain with no miracles in it. Your company, which is worth less on any market in Thornfell than it is right here."

"It's the only thing I had left to give."

"It was the only thing I ever wanted." The wolf sat down, heavily, the way old animals sit. "Very well. I accept. Be warned: I remember four thousand years of human cooking, and yours is genuinely remarkable in its badness. I will say so often."

"Noted."

"And I snore."

"You're a wolf."

"I snore *magnificently*. Ask the dreams I've been having. They complain."

Wendell laughed again and turned their back on the road.

They climbed back up together, slow through the frost, the wolf's shoulder bumping Wendell's knee at every third step — proximate, always proximate — and by the time they reached the den the sun had cleared the eastern ridge and laid a bar of gold across the chamber floor, across the pine boughs, across the shallow depression where a scabbard lay buried under new-turned earth already whitening with rime.

Wendell paused at the entrance, looking at that small pale mound.

Thirteen years ago, they had knelt at an altar and sworn themselves to a power that never answered, because the safety of the world depended on its silence, and they had built a whole life inside that silence — invisible, careful, alone. Now the silence was total, permanent, the inheritance of everyone in Thornfell, and Wendell discovered that they did not mind it at all. Prayers went unanswered everywhere now. People would have to answer each other. It would be slower. It would be harder. Looking at the wolf shaking frost from its coat by the dead fire, Wendell suspected it would also, in the end, be enough.

"What are you thinking?" Orsen asked, following their gaze to the grave of the scabbard.

"That we should plant something there in spring," Wendell said. "Something that blooms. Something loud and unnecessary."

The wolf considered this with the gravity it brought to all things.

"Poppies," it decided. "I remember poppies. They grew on the slopes of Ceren before the first temples. Reckless flowers. Bloomed like they had nothing to lose."

"Poppies, then."

"Poppies." The wolf settled onto the boughs, circled twice — a mortal ritual, learned in days, performed with ancient satisfaction — and looked up at Wendell with firelight warming its eyes. "Well? Sit down, smuggler. Tell me about the roads. I have four thousand years of memories and not one single story about you that doesn't end in snow. Start with something warm. We have time now. We have nothing but time."

Outside, far below, a carter's bell rang faintly on the east road, and faded, and no one answered it, and no one needed to.

Wendell sat down, and began.
