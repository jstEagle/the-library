## 22. The Road Without Gods

The first snow of the year came down the night before the pilgrims did, and by morning it had already begun to fail, melting in grey slush along the lower switchbacks where the sun touched. Wendell stood at the mouth of the Kestrel Notch trail with a walking staff they no longer needed and watched six travelers pick their way up from the valley—merchants, mostly, and two young sisters carrying bundles of dyed wool, all of them dressed for weather that had already changed its mind.

A year ago, Wendell would have let them pass unseen. A year ago, invisibility had been a craft, honed like any other: the right cloak, the right gait, the right way of standing at the edge of a fire's light so that eyes slid off you like rain off slate. Now they wore a guide's badge of braided red cord on their left shoulder—the color of the mountain passes, the color Orsen had once worn in the old carvings—and stood in plain sight waiting to be hired.

The wolf sat beside them, enormous and grey, watching the travelers with an expression Wendell had learned to read as patience.

"You could still do it," Wendell said quietly. "Vanish. I taught you how."

The wolf's ear flicked. It made no answer, because it never did—not in words, not anymore. Whatever voice had spoken to them above the cloudline, in the den where a god had slept in mortal flesh, had gone quiet after the unmaking, the way all voices had gone quiet. What remained was a creature with amber eyes and Orsen's memory behind them, and memory, Wendell had learned, was not the same as speech. The wolf remembered what it was to be a god the way a man remembers being young: vividly, painfully, and without the power to return.

The travelers reached the notch. The elder of the merchants, a broad woman with frost in her beard and a ledger strapped across her back, looked at the badge, then at the wolf, and did not flinch. That was new too. A year ago, a wolf that size would have cleared the road.

"Zhou?" she said. "They said you'd take people over Ceren's shoulder before the deep snow."

"They said true." Wendell shouldered their pack. "Six of you? The pass is open three more days, maybe four. After that we go the long way round through Greyhollow, and Greyhollow charges."

"Greyhollow always charges." She counted coins into Wendell's palm without haggling, which told Wendell everything about how the year had gone. Guides who took travelers over the mountain were not common. Guides who came back alive were rarer. "The wolf comes too?"

"The wolf goes where I go."

She studied the animal a moment longer. "My grandmother used to say there was a god in these mountains who kept wolves the way farmers keep dogs. She left him bread at midwinter. Just bread, on a stone."

"What happened to the stone?"

"Gone now. Everything's gone." She shrugged, unhurt by it, or pretending to be. "Bread's cheap, though. I'll leave some anyway. Habit."

Wendell said nothing. Behind them, the wolf's tail moved once, slow, like a door swinging shut.

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and Thornfell unfolded around them the way it always had—brutal, beautiful, indifferent—but it was an indifference Wendell had come to find honest. There was no god in the wind anymore. No presence pressing at the edges of things, no half-heard murmur under the sound of water. When Wendell prayed—and they still did sometimes, out of habit, out of love, out of the simple stubborn refusal to stop talking to someone just because they couldn't answer—the silence that came back was clean. Empty the way a room is empty, not the way a grave is empty.

The difference mattered. Wendell had spent thirteen years learning the difference.

At the high shoulder below Mount Ceren, they stopped to let the sisters catch up, and one of them—younger, wind-burned, maybe seventeen—looked out over the drop to the valley and asked the question Wendell got asked on every crossing now.

"Is it true there was a war here? Last winter? Between the gods?"

"There was going to be," Wendell said. "It died before it started."

"But the shrines went dark. All of them, all at once. My aunt swears she saw the drowned chapel at Lowmere bleeding light the night it happened. And the earthquakes—" She hugged her bundle of wool tighter. "People say the old gods are dead. People say the new gods abandoned us. People say—"

"People say a lot of things." Wendell crouched, scooped a handful of snow, pressed it into a rough ball, and held it out so she could feel how fast it melted against the sun-warmed air. "Here's what I know. The gods stopped answering. Whatever they were to each other—whatever they wanted from each other—it ended. And now when someone falls in a river, it's another person who pulls them out. When someone starves, it's another person who feeds them. Nobody's listening from above anymore, so we've started listening to each other instead." They stood, brushed the meltwater off their hand. "Some days that seems like a loss to me. Most days it doesn't."

"And you?" The girl's eyes were sharp, too sharp for her age. "You were here for it. Everyone says the guide with the wolf was here for it."

"I was here for it," Wendell agreed. "And what I remember most isn't the earthquakes or the burning shrines. It's a friend who chose wrong for the right reasons, and an enemy who chose right for the wrong ones, and a wolf who kept faith when faith didn't deserve keeping." They turned up the trail. "Keep walking. We lose the light early past the shoulder."

* * *

They made the far side of Ceren's shoulder by late afternoon and camped in the lee of an old waystone—one of the few that hadn't been torn down. Someone had left bread on it recently; the crumbs were fresh, and a jay was picking at them with proprietary confidence. The wolf lay down facing the valley with its chin on its paws, and Wendell built the fire small and sat close enough that the animal's warmth reached them through the cold coming off the snowfields.

This was the part of every day Wendell lived for now, though they'd have cut out their own tongue before admitting it. The fire. The quiet. The weight of a body beside theirs that had once been the weight of a mountain.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" Wendell said, low, for no one. "Maren."

The wolf's ear turned toward them. Listening. It always listened.

"A year, and I still don't know if I forgive her. She sold me to save herself, and then she cried while she did it, and somehow that made it worse, not better. If she'd been cold about it, I could hate her clean." Wendell fed a stick to the flames. "But here's the thing I figured out on the road this spring. She wasn't loyal to me. She was loyal to surviving. And I spent thirteen years doing exactly the same thing—staying invisible, staying silent, letting the whole world burn around my little corner of it as long as my corner stayed warm. I just called it prudence and called her a traitor." A dry laugh. "The gods' war didn't teach me that. You did. Watching you keep faith with a god who'd sent his own heart away—that taught me the difference between loyalty and survival. One of them costs something."

The wolf lifted its head and pressed its muzzle briefly against Wendell's shoulder—a gesture it had begun making sometime in the spring, unprompted, and which Wendell had privately decided was worth more than every answered prayer in the history of the world.

Then it went still, ears rising, looking east down the long fall of the valley.

Wendell heard it a moment later: hooves. Two riders, moving at a traveler's pace, not a chase. They came up the valley road as the light failed—a heavyset courier in church grey, and beside him a rider in a plain travel cloak whose posture Wendell would have known anywhere, even after a year, even blind. Sera Vael rode like a woman who had once commanded a hundred lances and had learned, since, to ride like nobody.

The travelers stirred nervously, but Vael dismounted at the edge of the firelight and held up empty hands, palms out—an old soldier's courtesy.

"Wendell Zhou." Her voice was the same as Wendell remembered: level, precise, stripped of anything she didn't intend to say. "I was told I'd find you on the Ceren crossing. The innkeeper at Greyhollow sells your route for two coppers. You should talk to him about that."

"He'd charge me five." Wendell stayed seated. "Inquisitor."

"Not anymore. The conclave dissolved the office in the spring. Turns out when the miracles stopped, most of the church's authority stopped with them. We're 'stewards of charitable works' now." Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and was gone. "I distribute grain. It's less glamorous than hunting heretics. The pay is worse."

"And yet you're sitting on a frozen mountain talking to a smuggler."

"To a guide." She glanced at the wolf, and Wendell watched her hold the animal's gaze without fear—which very few people managed, and which Wendell had never decided whether to admire or distrust. "I brought letters. And news, if you want it. And a request, which you're free to refuse, because I'm not an inquisitor anymore and you're not a fugitive, and I find I miss being able to ask things plainly."

"Ask."

Vael sat down across the fire uninvited, accepted a cup of tea from the wide-eyed merchant woman, and drank it like a woman remembering how.

"The peace holds," she said. "Fragile, but it holds. The old cults are rebuilding—not as powers, just as... customs. Bread on stones. Names spoken at graves. The new church keeps its hospitals and its schools and has stopped claiming its priests can hear anything but their own consciences. There are fights, of course. There will always be fights. But it's men fighting men over men-things now, land and grain and grievances. Nobody's fighting heaven's corner." She set down the cup. "There's a treaty being drafted in Thornvale. A charter for the mountain territories—shared roads, shared granaries, arbitration instead of feuds. They want signatures from people both sides trust. Old-guard names and new-church names together."

Wendell felt it coming before she said it, the way you feel weather change.

"They want yours," Vael said. "And mine. Side by side, on the same page. The smuggler who broke into the vault and the inquisitor who hunted them, ending the war neither of us fought." She paused. "I told them you'd say no. I told them you'd earned the right to say no. But I rode up here anyway, because asking cost me nothing and not asking might have cost me sleep."

Wendell looked into the fire for a long time.

A year ago, their name on a church document would have been a death sentence written in their own hand. A year ago, Sera Vael had put a crossbow bolt through Brannic's chest ten feet from where Wendell stood, and had captured them, and had almost—almost—taken the heart's offer for herself. Wendell had seen the wanting in her face in the stockade, the shape of a dead sister rising behind her eyes like a second moon. And Wendell had seen her put it down. Had watched her choose the third path with her hands shaking and her faith in ruins around her, and had known in that moment that they would trust this woman with anything, including the end of everything.

"I'll sign it," Wendell said. "On one condition."

Vael waited.

"When it's done, you come to Greyhollow and drink with me. Publicly. Where everyone can see the inquisitor and the smuggler sharing a table." Wendell smiled, thin and real. "Half the peace is paper. The other half is people seeing that it's possible."

"That's a terrible condition," Vael said. "I accept it."

* * *

She stayed the night at the edge of the camp, refusing a place closer to the fire with the same courtesy she refused everything, and left at dawn with her courier. Before she mounted, she walked to the waystone, took a loaf from her saddlebag, and set it on the stone without ceremony or explanation.

The wolf watched her do it. Vael met its eyes one last time.

"I'm sorry," she said—to the wolf, or to whatever it had been, or perhaps to the mountain itself. "For the vault. For what I was willing to become there." She inclined her head, precise as a signature, and rode away down into the valley, and did not look back.

Wendell stood beside the wolf and watched her go until the road took her.

"She never asks what you are," Wendell said. "Did you notice? Everyone else asks. She just decides it doesn't matter."

The wolf leaned its weight against their leg, nearly knocking them over, which was its way of laughing.

* * *

They took the pilgrims down into the southern valleys over the next two days, through country waking into winter-brown quiet, and delivered them safe to the shrine-town of Bellharbor, where the ferryman charged honest rates and the tavern keeper had named his house The Silent Altar, which Wendell found either very funny or very sad depending on the hour.

On the third evening, walking back alone—or alone as they ever were—Wendell took the high road instead of the low, the smugglers' road, the one that threaded the ridgelines above Greyhollow and knew every hidden door in the mountain's skin. Their feet found it without thinking. Thirteen years of secrets, worn into the stone like a path.

But they weren't secrets anymore. That was the strange grace of it. Wendell walked the hidden ways openly now, in daylight, with a red badge on their shoulder, and the hiding places had become shortcuts, and the shortcuts had become simply *roads*. Somewhere along the way, without noticing the exact moment, they had stopped being a ghost and become a landmark.

Near the top of the ridge, where the pines opened onto a shelf of granite overlooking the whole spine of Thornfell—Ceren in the north wearing its glacier like a crown, the passes stitching east to west, the smoke of a hundred small hearths rising straight in the still air—the wolf stopped, and Wendell stopped with it, because the wolf only stopped like that when it wanted them to see something.

They stood together in the last light. Below, tiny with distance, a caravan crawled the valley road, lanterns lit. Above, the first stars were coming out over the summit where everything had ended—the thirteenth moon long set, the seal long broken, the gods long gone into whatever silence waits behind silence. Somewhere down in those valleys, people were leaving bread on stones and pulling strangers out of rivers and arguing about grain boundaries and signing charters, answering each other because no one else would answer them.

"Loyalty outlived the truth it served," Wendell said aloud, testing the shape of it. "That's what I'd tell the girl on the pass, if she asked again. The truth died. The loyalty didn't. It's still walking these roads every day, wearing a red badge, guiding merchants through the snow." They scratched the wolf behind its ears, in the spot that made its hind leg twitch. "Turns out that was the whole point all along. Not the heart. Not the war. This."

The wolf made a sound then—low, rough, almost a word, almost the beginning of one—and pressed its great head under Wendell's hand and held it there.

And far to the north, at the crown of Mount Ceren, where the glacier caught the last red light of the dying year, something glinted. Brief. Bright. Gone.

Wendell stared at it until the stars swallowed the mountainside, and told themselves it was ice catching the sun, the way ice does, the way it always has.

The wolf was staring too.

It did not move for a long time.
