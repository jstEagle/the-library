## 1. The Silent Tithe

The snow came down in the flat, patient way that meant it intended to stay, and Ada Eastwick stood at the head of eleven mules and four men who trusted her more than they trusted their own mothers, and thought about her grandmother's hands.

She thought about them because the shrine was close now—another half mile, around the shoulder of the mountain where the wind funneled through a notch in the rock like breath through clenched teeth—and because every year on this day she paid her tithe, and every year on this day her grandmother's hands were what rose up to meet her. Broad hands, cracked with lye and wool-work. Hands that had taught her to fold the offering cloth into thirds, never quarters, because quarters were for the dead.

"Keep them moving," she said to Halloran, the nearest of the four men, without turning her head. "Slow is fine. Stopped is dead."

Halloran grunted and passed it down the line. He was a good man in the way smugglers were good men: reliably, within limits, and only so long as the limits held. His brother had frozen to death on the Wethermark pass three winters back because he'd stopped to tighten a girth strap and sat down "just for a moment." Ada had found him in spring, sitting upright against a boulder with his eyes open, wearing an expression of mild surprise, as if death had been a stranger asking directions and he'd only just realized he'd pointed the wrong way.

That was Thornfell. The mountains did not hate you. They simply did not know you were there.

She climbed. The mules climbed. Behind her the caravan strung itself out along the switchback like beads on a greased cord, and the snow filled in their tracks behind them as fast as they made them, which was the entire point of traveling in snow, though none of these men would ever say so aloud. You didn't thank the weather for your safety. That was how you got its attention.

The shrine sat in the notch where the wind came through—a low shelf of granite overhung by a slab of darker stone, sheltered enough that the old offerings hadn't all blown away. There had once been a carved lintel above it. Ada knew this because her grandmother had told her, and her grandmother had known because her own mother had told her, and somewhere back along that chain of women the carving had been chiseled off by men with government hammers, leaving only a scar in the rock shaped like nothing at all. Like everything. Depending on the light.

Ada halted the caravan below the shelf.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "Water the beasts. Nobody comes up here."

Nobody asked why. That was rule two, and even the new man—the one from Coldharbour with the soft hands and the nervous eyes—had learned it inside a week: *no passengers, no questions, no names.* Rule one kept the cargo honest. Rule two kept the men alive. Rule three—

Rule three was hers alone, and she'd broken it exactly once, and she did not think about that.

She climbed the last ten feet to the shrine alone.

* * *

It looked worse than last year. Everything did; that was the nature of things under the Usurper's peace. The offering stone had split along an old fault line, and someone—some pilgrim braver or more foolish than Ada—had left a twist of dried heather in the crack, gone grey and brittle with frost. A candle stub lay melted into the granite beside it, burned down to nothing long ago. Someone else had come here. Someone else still remembered.

Ada knelt in the snow. She didn't feel the cold anymore, not really; twenty years of guiding men through these passes had negotiated a treaty between her body and the mountain, and both sides honored it. She unwrapped the bundle she'd carried from the station: a cloth folded in thirds, and inside it, a measure of barley, a copper cut with the old sunburst—illegal currency now, worth its weight in trouble—and a lock of hair from her own head, grey coming in early at the temples, snipped that morning before dawn.

The tithe. Barley for the body, coin for the road, hair for the memory. Her grandmother's voice recited it in her skull the way it always did, in the old tongue first and then in Common, the way you'd teach a child:

*Barley for the body, that it may be fed.*
*Coin for the road, that it may be walked.*
*Hair for the memory, that I may be remembered.*

And then the answer. There was always supposed to be an answer. Not words—the god of oaths did not speak in words—but a feeling, a settling, like a hand laid briefly on the back of your neck. *Heard. Kept. Witnessed.* Every oath sworn in the old days had ended with that settling, and whole kingdoms had been built on it, because a promise witnessed by the god could no more be broken than a river could flow uphill.

Her grandmother had felt it for the last time on a Tuesday. That was the detail Ada always snagged on, the absurd domestic horror of it. Not at the trials, not at the burning. On a Tuesday, hanging washing, mid-prayer, and then nothing. Silence. The god of oaths, the Witness, the Voice of Ten Thousand Mouths, had simply—stopped. All across Thornfell, all across the world, at the same hour, if the stories were true. And six months later the Usurper's armies had come over the passes with their hammers and their lists, and the Choir—the god's singers, his mouths, his ten thousand voices—had been exterminated to the last throat, and the official history had settled over the mountains like the snow: *the Choir was a heresy, the Choir is extinct, the silence proves the Usurper's god is the only god.*

Ada laid the barley on the stone. Laid the copper on the barley. Laid the hair on top, and weighted it all with a pebble against the wind.

Then she waited, out of habit, out of grief, out of some stubborn animal loyalty she'd have denied under torture, for the settling. For the hand on the back of her neck.

The wind came through the notch. It moved nothing. It said nothing.

"Twenty years," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange and small in the shrine, flattened by the snow. "You could have left me something. A word. A sign. Anything but this."

The stone said nothing. The stone had said nothing for twenty years, and she had come anyway, every year, on the same day, because that was what faith was when you stripped away the miracles: showing up. Showing up with your barley and your illegal coin and your hair, kneeling in the snow for a god who would not look at you, and getting up afterward and doing your work.

She pressed her bare hand flat against the offering stone, just for a moment. It was cold. Of course it was cold. It was rock, in winter, in Thornfell.

But she stayed until her fingers ached, and then she rose, brushed the snow from her knees, and went back down to the living.

* * *

The new man was waiting too close to the shrine path, which was how she knew he'd be a problem.

"You prayed," he said. Not accusing. Worse—curious.

"I checked the route."

"That was a prayer. My mother prayed like that. Kneeling, and then angry after."

Ada looked at him. He flinched, which was correct. "What's your name?"

He hesitated. "Tomas."

"Tomas isn't a name, it's a sound. Rule three: no names. You're Coldharbour until you're gone off my mountain, and then you're nothing at all." She pulled her mittens back on. "And rule two. You broke it just now."

"I didn't ask anything."

"You answered a question nobody asked you. Same thing, up here." She shouldered past him toward the mules. "The inquisition pays silver for informers and iron for heretics, and they don't much care which one you are. So we don't talk about gods, we don't talk about routes, and we don't talk about each other. We walk, we camp where I say, we cross where I say, and in nine days you're in the free cities telling yourself whatever story lets you sleep. Understood?"

"Yes," he said, and then, because he was young and soft and doomed to be a problem: "But you *did* pray."

Ada took a breath of air so cold it hurt her teeth. Somewhere behind them, further up the trail, a bird called out—one sharp note, high and clear, hanging in the stillness longer than any birdcall had a right to.

She turned her head. The notch was empty. Snow, stone, wind.

"One note," Halloran muttered behind her, uneasy. "Wrong time of year for anything to be singing up here."

"It's a bird," Ada said.

It was a bird. It was certainly a bird.

They made camp that night in a hollow below the treeline, and Ada set the watches herself, and slept the way she always slept: lightly, with a knife in reach and one ear open. In the morning they crossed the high col in whiteout, and she navigated by count and compass and the feel of the slope under her boots, and brought all eleven mules and four men over alive, which was the whole of her job and the whole of her pride.

On the ninth day she handed them off to Bren's people at the timberline relay, collected her fee in unmarked silver, watched Coldharbour and the others descend toward the valley and the free cities and their stories, and turned back up the mountain alone.

* * *

The warden station sat in a fold of the mountain two days' climb above the relay—a squat stone house with a turf roof, built generations ago for the wardens who used to patrol these passes in the god's name, keeping the roads safe and the shrines tended. Now it was just hers. The Usurper's surveyors had marked it on their maps as *uninhabited ruin,* which suited Ada fine. Ruins paid no taxes. Ruins attracted no garrisons. Ruins were left alone.

She stocked it, she repaired it, she lived in it eight months of the year, and in the other four she went down to the valley towns and sold her services to caravans and drank very little and spoke to almost no one, and if anyone ever asked why a woman of her skill lived alone in an uninhabited ruin in the highest passes in the world, well—no one asked. Rule two applied to her too.

The station was cold when she let herself in. She lit the stove, hung her coat, and went through the ritual of return: water from the covered barrel, lamp trimmed, the shutters checked, the oath-stones by the hearth dusted with the goose wing.

The oath-stones. Three of them, palm-sized, fitted into the wall beside the fireplace where the old wardens had sworn their watch-oaths every dusk. *I keep the road. I keep the sleepers. I keep the fire.* Every warden for four hundred years had spoken it, and the stones had glowed faintly in answer—witnessed, kept—and travelers in the old days would detour for miles just to sleep a night under stones that glowed, because a night under witnessed oath was a night without fear.

Ada hadn't spoken the watch-oath in twenty years. She dusted the stones anyway. They were dull grey, ordinary as river pebbles, and touching them was like touching a grave marker: you did it for the dead, not because the dead would notice.

She was heating broth over the stove when she heard the knock.

Three knocks. Faint. Then two more, weaker, spaced wrong—the knock of someone using the last of what they had.

Ada went still, spoon in hand. No one climbed to the station. No one *knew* the station was here; the trail that led to it was a warden's path worn into the rock two hundred years ago and invisible unless you already knew it. Caravans passed a mile east. Hunters kept to the valleys. And no traveler lost in Thornfell in deep winter knocked—lost people didn't climb *up.*

Two more knocks. Weaker.

She took the knife from the block, doused the lamp, and opened the door onto the dark and the falling snow.

A boy stood on her step.

He was small—nine years old, maybe ten, though it was hard to tell with starvation, which erases age the way water erases letters. He was dressed in summer clothes gone to rags, crusted with ice, and he had walked—he had *climbed*—through Thornfell in winter, in the dark, to a door that did not exist on any map. His lips were blue. His eyes were open and fixed on her with a terrible, deliberate focus, the focus of someone who has decided to stay upright through sheer refusal, and when he saw her he tried to speak.

What came out of him was not Common. It was not any language of the free cities, nor the Usurper's imperial speech, nor the trader-mix of the caravan roads. It was a stream of syllables, high and strange and rhythmic, rising and falling like something meant to be sung rather than said—and Ada's whole body went cold in a way the mountain had never managed, because she knew that language.

She had heard it twice in her life. Both times through a courthouse wall, at her grandmother's heresy trial, muffled, forbidden, translated in whispers afterward by a woman who was executed for knowing it.

The tongue of the Choir. The dead language of the dead voices of the dead god. Spoken, fluently, desperately, by a starving child on her doorstep, in the middle of the night, in the middle of nowhere.

"No passengers," Ada said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "No names. No questions. Turn around and go back down."

The boy swayed. He said something else in that impossible language—pleading, it sounded like, pleading with a rhythm like a hymn—and then his legs gave out, and Ada Eastwick, who had survived twenty years on three rules and a silence, caught him before he hit the snow.

Behind her, inside the station, in the dark, the three oath-stones by the hearth gave off a light so faint she would spend the rest of her life wondering whether she had truly seen it—or whether, for one moment, the god of oaths had opened one eye.
