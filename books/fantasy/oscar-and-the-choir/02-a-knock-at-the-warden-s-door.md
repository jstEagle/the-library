## 2. A Knock at the Warden's Door

The storm came down off the high passes an hour after dark, the way storms did in Thornfell—without warning, without mercy, and without any interest in what a body might be doing when it arrived. Ada Eastwick heard it coming before she saw it, a low grinding roar up the valley like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep, and she did what she always did: she banked the fire, shuttered the lantern, and went out into the yard to make certain nothing of hers would be scattered to the four winds by morning.

The warden station had been built by better hands than hers, hands that had believed the mountains needed warding. It was a squat stone structure crouched against the cliffside, half-swallowed by lichen and two hundred years of weather, with a watchtower that leaned like a drunkard and a shrine attached to its eastern wall where the oath-stones sat in their niches, cold and gray and silent as they had been silent for twenty years. She kept the place because it was hers, because no one else wanted it, and because from its door she could see every approach through the Narrows—the smugglers' road she guided people along for coin, the one trade she'd found that asked nothing of her but competence.

Her rules were simple. No passengers. No questions. No names. She led caravans through the passes and she took her pay and she walked away, and if the men she guided whispered about the silent god whose shrines dotted the mountain like cairns, she let them whisper. Grief was a private country. She had stopped letting visitors across its border years ago.

She was checking the tie-downs on the woodpile when the wind shifted and brought her something that didn't belong: a smell of blood, faint under the snow-smell, and beneath that—fainter still, so faint she might have imagined it—a sound. Not a cry. Cries carried in the mountains; she knew all their varieties, wolf and wind and man. This was smaller. A knocking, almost. Rhythmic. Like a fist on wood, if the wood were flesh and the fist had very little strength left in it.

Ada stood in the falling snow and told herself she hadn't heard anything.

The storm redoubled. The sound came again.

*No passengers,* she thought. *No questions. No names.* The rules had kept her alive when faith hadn't, when the usurper's purges swept the lowlands and even the mountains learned to be quiet. Rules were load-bearing walls. You pulled one out and you never knew which part of the house came down on your head.

She went inside anyway. She told herself it was to bar the door against whatever was out there, and she supposed that was even true, in the way most lies are true.

* * *

She found him on her doorstep twenty minutes later, or rather she found what the storm had left of him.

He couldn't have been more than eight or nine years old, small for it, starved for certain—all elbows and knuckles under a coat that had been made for someone twice his size, someone dead, judging by the way it had been cut down and re-stitched with rough twine. His lips were the blue-gray of old pewter. Ice had built itself into his hair and his lashes, and his bare feet—bare feet, in Thornfell, in the month of Deepfrost—were blackened past the ankle. He had crawled, she realized. There were drag-marks in the snow behind him, ten yards of them, leading back toward the tree line, and the snow had already begun erasing them, filling them in, doing what the mountains always did with evidence.

He wasn't knocking anymore. He was past knocking. He lay with one hand pressed flat against the bottom panel of her door, fingers curled like a dead bird's feet, and his chest moved in shallow, stuttering pulls that she could count from three feet away.

Ada stood over him with her hand on her knife and did the arithmetic of survival. One mouth to feed in a winter she'd provisioned for one. A child who could not walk could not be turned out to die quickly; he would die slowly instead, loudly, over days, and she would hear it through her own door. And if anyone found him here—if the boy belonged to anyone, if someone was following him—then the invisible life she had built, stone by careful stone, would come apart around her ears.

Turn him out. That was the answer. Every instinct she trusted said turn him out, roll him off the step, let the mountain take back what it had caught. The mountain was honest. The mountain didn't ask you to explain yourself afterward.

His hand slid down the door and left a smear. Not blood. Meltwater, and under the meltwater, where his palm had been, a mark on the wood itself—a small pressed impression, like a seal, like something had been *stamped* through his skin into her door. She stared at it. She had seen brands before, on cattle and on criminals, but this was neither. It was older-looking, a spiral wound tight around a vertical line, and her stomach dropped through the floorboards of her certainty, because she knew that mark. Everyone over forty in the lowlands knew that mark. They'd burned it into doorposts during the trials, and then they'd burned the doors, and then the houses, and then the people.

The mark of the Choir.

"No," Ada said aloud, to the boy, to the storm, to whoever or whatever had sent this thing crawling up her mountain. "No. Absolutely not."

The boy's eyes opened. They were brown, ordinary, fever-bright—and fixed on her face with a focus that had nothing childish left in it, as though something behind them were looking out and taking her measure. His cracked lips moved. What came out was not a word in any tongue she knew.

It was a sentence. Low, cracked, half-frozen—but structured, deliberate, with the cadence of speech and not of delirium. Three syllables rising, two falling, a stress laid on the last note like a hand placed gently on a table. And Ada's whole body went cold in a way the storm hadn't managed, because she *knew* that cadence. She had heard it exactly once, thirty years ago, standing between her grandmother's knees in a crowded square while a woman in gray robes read accusations from a scroll, and her grandmother had answered the accusations in that same rising-falling rhythm, calm as water, right up until they put the hood over her head.

The tongue of the Choir. The dead language. The language of the god's many mouths, the ones the usurper's armies had exterminated root and branch a generation ago, the ones that weren't supposed to exist anymore except in archives and ash.

Children didn't speak dead tongues. Starving, freezing children didn't speak them on her doorstep in the middle of a storm that had killed grown guides that season.

"Where did you get that?" she demanded, and hated how her voice shook. "Who taught you? Who sent you?"

The boy blinked, slow, and the thing behind his eyes receded, and suddenly he was only a child again—terrified, exhausted, nine years old at most. He tried to speak again and got out something thinner, broken, and this time there was no mistaking the shape of it even if she couldn't parse the words: *please.*

Ada looked at the mark on her door. She looked at the drag-marks in the snow, already vanishing. She looked at the blackened feet, and thought about what it cost a person to crawl ten yards through Deepfrost on them, and what kind of desperation waited at the end of a crawl like that, and what would have happened to him if her station had stood another mile up the valley.

Her rules rose up in her like a wall. No passengers. No questions. No names. She had buried her mercy thirty years ago in a crowded square along with her grandmother, and she had built her life on top of the grave precisely so that nothing like this—nothing small and freezing and marked—could ever dig it up again.

But her grandmother had also taught her the other thing, the thing before the trials, the thing she'd spent three decades pretending she'd forgotten: that the god of oaths kept no rules except one, and it was this—that when someone knocks, you either open the door or you become the door that stayed shut, forever, and both of those things are oaths whether you say them aloud or not.

"Damn you," Ada said—to the boy, to her grandmother, to the silent god equally—and bent, and hauled him up out of the snow. He weighed less than her pack. That was the worst part, how little he weighed. "This doesn't mean anything," she told him, grunting with the effort, dragging him over the threshold into the light. "You hear me? One night. You're warm until the storm breaks, and then you're gone, and I never saw you, and you never saw me."

The boy's head lolled against her shoulder. His lips moved against her collarbone, and though she couldn't have translated a word of it, she felt the words more than heard them—vibrations, hummed more than spoken, a single sustained tone that passed through her ribs like a bell struck somewhere deep in the house of her body.

Behind her, unlit, unattended, in its niche against the eastern wall, one of the old oath-stones gave the faintest possible shiver.

Ada didn't see it. She was carrying a dying child toward her fire, and telling herself—with the desperate fluency of long practice—that the prickle along her spine was only the cold leaving her bones.

* * *

She worked on him for two hours and did not once think about the stone.

That was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and she worked anyway. She cut away the ruined coat—stopping when she found the inner lining stitched through with dozens of tiny sewn pockets, all empty now save one, which held a smooth gray pebble no bigger than a thumbnail. An oath-stone. A traveler's token, the kind pilgrims had once carried to shrines to be blessed, worthless for twenty years, worth less than nothing now, the kind of thing that got a family reported in the wrong village. She should throw it in the fire. She put it in her pocket instead, and hated herself for that too.

She thawed his feet in snowmelt, working the blood back into tissue that had started to go waxy, and the boy screamed himself hoarse and then fainted, which was a mercy, and she bound his feet in goose grease and linen and propped them higher than his heart. She forced broth between his cracked lips spoonful by spoonful, waiting each time for his throat to remember how to swallow. She checked him for fever, for frostbite's deeper brother, for the wounds she'd smelled under the snow—and found them: a long gash along his ribs, scabbed badly, infected at the edges, and older bruises in patterns she recognized. Patterns made by hands, many hands, gripping and dragging. Someone had held this child. More than one someone. Recently.

Every professional instinct she owned told her to search his belongings properly, learn everything, plan accordingly. Instead she found herself being gentle, and gentleness frightened her more than the mark on her door did.

When the work was done she sat back on her heels beside the pallet she'd made him by the fire, and she watched him breathe, and she let herself feel the full weight of what she'd done. She had broken her first rule in eleven years. Whatever followed him—soldiers, pursuers, debts, disease—now had a trail leading directly to her door, and she had lit a lamp in the window besides, because he'd been afraid of the dark, mumbling it even unconscious, and she was apparently the sort of fool who lit lamps for strange children now.

"You should have crawled past," she told him quietly. "Another mile. Any other door."

The boy stirred. His eyes opened halfway, unfocused, finding the rafters. And then he began to talk in his sleep—or near-sleep, that gray country between—and it was worse than the doorstep, because on the doorstep it had been one sentence, an accident, a parrot-trick maybe, something a child could have picked up anywhere, from anyone, and she could have told herself stories about it. This was fluent. This was a running commentary, low and rhythmic, phrases flowing one into another with the ease of a mother tongue, rising and falling in that cadence she'd heard exactly once before, at the end of her grandmother's life.

And the fire behaved strangely while he spoke. Not dangerously—it didn't leap or gutter—but it *leaned*, subtly, flames bending toward the pallet as though listening, holding themselves quieter than fire holds itself. The kettle on its hook began, very softly, to sing. Not whistle. Sing—one clear, thin harmonic, steady as a struck glass, holding a note that matched, exactly, the pitch his voice kept returning to.

Ada sat very still on the floor with her back against the table leg and her knife loose in its sheath, and she listened to a starving child speak the language of a murdered priesthood, and she felt the old grief come up in her like floodwater—not the grief she'd gotten good at carrying, the manageable daily weight of it, but the other kind, the young kind, the kind with teeth. Because if the tongue lived, then something had taught it. And if something had taught it, then the extermination—the clean, total, official extermination the usurper's criers proclaimed every festival day—had not been clean, and had not been total, and everything she had made peace with for twenty years was built on a lie she'd swallowed whole.

"That's not possible," she said to the fire. Her voice sounded very small in the stone room. "They're dead. All of them. I watched the last of the trials. Everyone watched."

The boy's voice climbed. Found a note. Held it.

And in the eastern wall, behind stone and plaster, in the niche where they had sat untouched and silent through twenty winters, the old oath-stones of the warden station began—very faintly, very softly, like a sleeper turning over—to hum.

Ada's head turned toward the sound slowly, the way heads turn in nightmares. She knew those stones. She had dusted those stones every week for eleven years and they had never done anything but sit there, gray and dead, monuments to a silence she'd mourned and then stopped mourning and then simply inhabited, the way you inhabit a missing limb. Pilgrims had once traveled months to touch them. Supplicants had sworn oaths on them that were said to bind beyond death. And now, in a firelit room with a fevered child murmuring in a dead tongue, they were humming—three notes, low, wavering, answering the boy's voice the way a shore answers a wave.

She crossed the room on legs that didn't feel entirely like hers. She put her palm flat against the largest stone.

It was warm. Warm as a living thing. Warm as skin.

Every rule she had, every wall she'd built, every careful invisible year—she felt them all standing there in the firelight, intact, load-bearing, and underneath them she felt something else moving, vast and patient and awake, something that had gone quiet for twenty years not because it died but because it was *hiding*, the way she hid, the way everyone in the mountains had learned to hide—

The boy whimpered in his sleep. The stones fell silent instantly, guiltily, like conspirators interrupted.

Ada snatched her hand back. She stood in the middle of her own floor, breathing hard, one fist closed around the small gray pebble from his coat, and she understood with terrible clarity that she was standing at a crossroads that had been waiting for her her whole life, dressed up as a doorstep.

One night. She had promised him one night. Storm's end and then gone, and never seen, and no names.

Outside, the storm howled and showed no sign of breaking. Inside, the boy slept, and the fire leaned toward him, and the oath-stones in the eastern wall waited in their niche, warm and humming faintly to themselves, like something that had just remembered it had a voice.

Ada looked at the lamp burning in her window, and at the boy, and at the stones, and she said, to all of them, with the weariness of a woman who already knew she'd lost:

"One night. Just the one."
