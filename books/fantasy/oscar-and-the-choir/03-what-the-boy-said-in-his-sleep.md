## 3. What the Boy Said in His Sleep

The fever came at moonrise.

Ada had known it would. The boy had come through the pass half-dead, and the mountain had a way of finishing what the cold started. She'd built the fire high, boiled snow with juniper needles, stripped his soaked coat off him, and set him on her own cot wrapped in every blanket she owned. Now she sat across from him on her one stool, mending a strap that didn't need mending, and watched the firelight move over a face too young to have walked out of Thornfell alone.

The station was small—one room of stone below, a loft above for grain and gear—and it had never held another living soul in the six years she'd kept it. She was aware of that fact the way she was aware of the wind: constantly, and against her will. The boy's breathing filled the room. His boots, cracked through, stood by the hearth drying into shapes that would never hold together again. Someone had sewn those boots twice, badly, with love or necessity; Ada couldn't decide which was sadder.

She should have turned him out. She'd told herself so three times since dark, and three times she'd heard her grandmother's voice answer—*Ada Eastwick, you were born under an oath-stone, don't pretend you can't feel them*—and three times she'd told her grandmother to stay dead where they'd put her.

The boy stirred. His hands worked at the blanket, plucking at nothing.

"No passengers," she said quietly, to him or to herself. "No questions. No names." Her rules. Twenty years old now, older than the silence, and they had kept her alive when faith hadn't. "I broke two already letting you past my door."

His lips moved.

She thought at first he was asking for water, and she got up to fetch the cup from the warming stones—but what came out of him wasn't a request. It wasn't anything she knew. The sounds were round and layered, vowels stacked on vowels, words that seemed to lean against each other like singers finding their pitch. Not babbling. Fever-babble has no architecture. This had architecture. It rose and fell, phrase answering phrase, the way—

The way the hymns used to go. Before. In her grandmother's stories, in the burned books, in the fragments old women whispered when the inquisitors' wagons had rolled out of sight. The tongue of the Choir. A language the usurper's scholars swore had died with its last speakers, twenty years ago this winter, in the purges Ada's mother's generation still called *the Silencing* when they dared speak of it at all.

She stood very still with the cup in her hand until the boy subsided into ordinary sleep. Then she drank the water herself, though she wasn't thirsty, because her hands wanted something to do and she didn't trust them near a blade just then.

"Coincidence," she said aloud. The station walls took the word and gave back nothing. "Some village nurse taught you scraps. Some smuggler hummed where you could hear. Words are just words."

But her grandmother's voice, dead these fifteen years, said in the tone she'd saved for heresy trials: *Words aren't just words if the stones know them.*

The oath-stones were set into the eastern wall, four of them, hand-sized and grey, mortared there by whoever had raised this station before the wars—wardens of the old order, keepers of the passes who'd sworn their oaths aloud each dawn with palms flat against stone. When the Choir sang, the story went, the stones answered. When the god spoke, the stones carried it down the line of shrines like runners passing a torch, from Thornfell to the coast, so that no warden ever prayed alone.

They had been silent for twenty years. Ada had pressed her palm to them every morning of her six years here anyway—the tithe, the ritual, the habit that outlived its object—and every morning they had been exactly what they appeared to be: rock. Cold rock. Rock that had let her pray into a silence so complete it had stopped being grief and become geography.

She looked at them now across the sleeping boy, and made herself not look at them, and looked at them anyway.

*It's the fever,* she told herself. *Fever makes voices strange.*

The boy began to speak again near midnight, and this time she did not pretend it was anything but what it was.

* * *

He talked in his sleep the way other children cried—not continuously, but in waves, coming up from some deep place and going under again. And the language did not degrade as the night went on. That was the thing Ada kept circling and refusing to land on: fever should have shredded it, scrambled it, sunk it into nonsense. Instead it grew more precise. By the second hour he was pronouncing sounds she couldn't make, consonants that seemed to arrive from behind the teeth and leave by way of the throat, and the phrases had begun to rhyme—or not rhyme, exactly, but *correspond*, the way lines of a litany correspond, each one built on the bones of the last.

She caught herself understanding a word.

Not translating—understanding, the way you understand your own name. *Vessia.* It passed through his mouth in a run of syllables and she felt it land somewhere beneath her ribs, warm as a coal: *vessia*, and she knew without knowing how that it meant *remain*, or *stay*, or possibly *beloved-thing-that-stays*. One word. Out of a dead tongue. Out of a boy she'd known for half a day.

Her grandmother had been executed for less than one word. Well—imprisoned first, tried publicly, made to kneel in the market square at Highmoor while a man in grey read the charges, and then executed. Ada had been nine. She remembered the snow, and the grey man's patient voice, and her grandmother's face turned up toward the sky as if listening for something that wasn't coming. *They silenced the Choir,* her mother had said after, once, drunk, once only, *and then they silenced everyone who remembered the Choir, and now they'll silence anyone who asks why.* Her mother had never said it again, and had gone into the mines the following spring and never come out, and Ada had learned her arithmetic early: memory was a debt collected with interest, paid in full and in public.

So she did what her whole life had trained her to do. She banked the fire low. She barred the door. She sat down between the boy and the oath-stones, facing the wall, and waited for morning with her knife loose in its sheath and her mind working the problem like a route through a whiteout: find the fixed points, count the risks, choose the path that keeps you breathing.

Fixed point one: the boy was a child, starving, frostbitten, alone. Whatever he carried, he hadn't chosen it. Fixed point two: the inquisitors climbed these passes twice a year on circuit, and their circuit maps marked every station, shrine, and sympathetic household in Thornfell. Fixed point three: she had taken him in. There was no version of the ledger where that entry erased itself.

Somewhere past the deepest hour of night, the humming started.

At first she thought it was the wind in the flue—a low, resonant note, felt in the floorboards before it reached the ear. But the flue-note shivered when the gusts came, and this did not. It held steady, one sustained tone, and it was coming from the wall. From the stones. Four grey rocks that had been silent since before this child was born were *humming*, softly, in tune with the sleeping boy's litany, rising when his voice rose, resting when he rested, like dogs lying at the feet of someone singing.

Ada did not scream. Screaming was for people whose plans included being rescued. She sat very still and felt the hair rise along her arms and the old, buried, unspeakable part of her—the nine-year-old in the market square snow—lift its head like an animal scenting home.

The boy murmured *vessia* again, and the stones swelled around the word, and Ada put her face in her hands.

"Dawn," she said into her palms. "We test it at dawn. If I'm mad, I'm mad at dawn."

* * *

Dawn came thin and blue through the arrow-slit window, and the boy slept on, sweat-soaked but breathing easier, the fever breaking the way fevers do—as if bored of its own drama. Ada had not slept. She had spent the dark hours doing what she did instead of praying, which was planning, and by grey light she had a plan, and the plan was simple, and the plan was terrible, and she hated it.

She was going to wake him. Or rather, she was going to try to wake him, gently, and if he surfaced enough to be coherent, she was going to ask him to say the words again. Deliberately. With witnesses. Because if the stones answered a fever-dream, it might be fever all the way through—his heat, her exhaustion, the wind, suggestion, madness, all the soft explanations stacked like sandbags against the hard one. But if the stones answered when he was awake and lucid and *choosing* the words—

Then it wasn't weather. It was a door, and someone was knocking from the other side.

She fed the fire, melted snow, woke him with watered broth and a hand on his shoulder that she kept deliberately light, deliberately indifferent, the touch of a woman checking cargo, not a heart.

"Can you hear me?" she said.

His eyes opened. They were brown, unremarkable, red-rimmed with fever—and behind them, for one unguarded moment before consciousness fully arrived, something looked out at her that made her think absurdly of the inside of the shrine at Highmoor: vast, dim, and full of things waiting to be sung. Then it was just a boy, blinking, confused, trying to remember where walls came from.

"You're at my station," she said. "Warden's station, north ridge. You came out of the pass yesterday. Don't get up yet."

"I'm sorry," he said, in the common tongue, voice cracked and small. "I didn't mean—I was looking for—" He stopped, swallowed. "Thank you for the fire."

"What's your name?"

He hesitated. Not the hesitation of a liar composing; the hesitation of someone who has been told, recently and painfully, that names cost. "Oscar," he said finally.

"Oscar." She set the bowl down. Kept her voice level, warden-flat, the voice she used for smugglers counting out payment. "You talked in your sleep last night. Do you know that?"

Fear moved through his face like weather. "Was it—the old words? Did I say the old words?"

There it was, then. He knew. Whatever else he knew or didn't, he knew what lived in his mouth.

"The old words," she agreed. "And I'm going to ask you something strange, Oscar, and I need you to believe I'm not going to hurt you either way. I need you to say them again. Awake. Just a few. Can you do that?"

He pulled the blanket to his chin. For a long moment she thought he'd refuse, and part of her—a part she refused to name—hoped he would, hoped the whole thing would collapse back into fever-nonsense and cold mornings and her ordinary invisible life, intact.

"They told me never," he whispered. "The people who hid me. They said if I ever spoke them awake, outside, where anyone—" His eyes went to the walls, the window slit, the door. "Are there soldiers here?"

"No soldiers. It's just us and the mountain." *And whatever is in that wall.* "That's why here. That's why now. Nobody will ever know what happens in this room unless I say it happened. Understand? My silence is the one thing in this world that's never failed me."

Something in him steadied at that—the way lost travelers steadied when she told them she knew the route. He nodded, once, small.

He pushed the blanket down. Sat up slowly, swaying, and she let him, watching his color. Then he looked at the eastern wall—at the four grey stones, plain as river pebbles, mortared in their row—and she saw him see them. Really see them. His breath caught.

"There are stones here," he said wonderingly. "Warm stones. Why are they warm?"

"They're not warm." Her voice came out strange. "They've been cold for twenty years."

"But they're singing," Oscar said simply, as if describing bread or birds. "They've been singing since I came in. Quietly. Like they're shy."

Ada crossed the room in four steps and put her palm flat against the nearest stone.

Cold. Stone-cold, silent, exactly as it had been every morning of her tenure, exactly as it had been the night before while the boy's fever-litany filled the room and the humming had—

The humming had been real. She'd heard it with her own ears. But under her hand the stone gave nothing, told nothing, and the discrepancy opened under her like a crevasse. Unless the stones weren't mute. Unless they were *discreet*—answering only to the voice they were built to answer, keeping quiet under a stranger's palm the way a smuggler keeps quiet under a checkpoint lantern.

"Say them," she said. Her mouth had gone dry. "Just a few. Any few."

Oscar looked at her a moment longer—checking, she realized, whether she was afraid. She made her face into the thing she'd spent twenty years building: nothing to read. Apparently satisfied, he closed his eyes.

What came out of him was not the fever-version. Fever had frayed it; this was seamless. Three lines of the old tongue, unhurried, pitched low, a child's voice carrying something with the weight of a grown century—and the stones woke.

Not the humming of the night, tentative as a sleeper stirring. This was full-throated. All four stones sounded at once, a chord with no instrument behind it, rising out of the mortar itself, and light came with it—not flame-colored, not reflected from the hearth, but a pale gold rising *from within* the grey rock, the way dawn comes from within cloud. The warmth hit Ada's palm like a hand clasping hers. Twenty years of cold ended in one heartbeat, and the stone under her hand was *alive*, pulsing faintly, keeping time with the boy's voice like a pulse keeping time with a heart.

The third line he sang, she understood.

Not translated—understood. It poured through her the way her own name would: *the vow is not broken, only sleeping; the vow is not broken, only sleeping; the vow is not broken, only sleeping,* three times, a litany, and with each repetition the stones brightened, and Ada Eastrick—who had tithed to silence for six years, whose grandmother had died staring at an empty sky, who had built an entire life on the load-bearing certainty that her god was dead and the world was simpler and crueler and safer for it—felt that certainty crack down the middle like river ice in spring.

The song ended. The light sank. The stones cooled—but not all the way. Not to dead. To *waiting*.

In the ringing quiet, the boy opened his eyes and found her staring at her own hand, still flat against the stone, unwilling to lift it, because lifting it would end the only conversation her god had initiated in twenty years.

"He says you're angry," Oscar said softly.

Ada's head came up. "What?"

"When you touched the stone. He says—" The boy tilted his head, listening to something on the far side of the air. "He says you're angry, and he says he's sorry, and he says—" Oscar's brow furrowed, a child struggling with a difficult passage. "He says he was quiet so that *you* wouldn't be found. All of you. Everyone who remembered." His gaze settled on her, guileless and unbearable. "Were you hiding too?"

The question went through her like a spear through wet canvas. She thought of her grandmother's face in the snow. Of her mother's one drunken sentence. Of six years of palm-on-cold-stone every dawn, speaking into a silence she'd told herself was a corpse.

"Yes," she said, before she could stop it. "Yes, we were hiding."

Oscar nodded gravely, as if this confirmed something. Then his eyes drifted shut, the fever's last wave pulling him under, and he slid back down onto the cot, asleep almost instantly, breath evening out, one hand curling loosely in the blanket.

Ada stood in the middle of her station with her palm still burning and her whole invisible life in ruins around her, and understood, with the clarity of the newly doomed, that the silence had never been death. It had been a hiding place. And she had just found what it was hiding.

Outside, far down the southern pass, faint as a thought, she heard—she would swear afterward she heard—the bells of a circuit wagon. Inquisitors' bells, coming up the mountain road, weeks ahead of any schedule she knew.

She looked at the sleeping boy. At the glowing-cool stones. At the door.

"No passengers," she said hoarsely, to no one who would honor it.
