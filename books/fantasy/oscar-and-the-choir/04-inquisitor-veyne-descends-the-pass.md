## 4. Inquisitor Veyne Descends the Pass

The snow began an hour before dawn, and Inquisitor Halvane Veyne took it as a courtesy. Weather like this kept the roads empty, emptied the villages of their courage before he ever arrived, and made honest men grateful for any roof that would take them in—including the roof of the King's justice. He rode at the head of twelve men, and the thirteen horses behind him sounded, on the frozen road, like a slow drum.

He had been three weeks in the Thornfell passes now, and the mountain had given up nothing but silence, which was itself a kind of testimony. The Echo—*the last echo*, the writs said, though Veyne disliked the phrase; an echo implied an original still standing somewhere—had been heard, or claimed to be heard, in seven villages along the southern face of the range. Seven claims. Six had collapsed under questioning into spite, superstition, or the simple human appetite for attention that flourished in poor places. The seventh had not yet been asked.

That was Greymil. A day's ride ahead, if the weather held its courtesy.

Veyne was fifty-one years old, and he had spent thirty of those years in the service of the Office of Sacred Inquiry, and in all that time he had learned one doctrine more surely than any other: that truth was not a thing to be seized. It was a thing to be *waited for*. Liars exhausted themselves. The guilty improvised, and improvisation frayed. But truth sat still, like a stone under snow, and waited to be uncovered by anyone patient enough to keep brushing. He had watched younger inquisitors burn their way through villages in a fortnight and produce nothing but ash and false confessions, and he had watched those confessions rot from underneath within a season, and he had understood then that cruelty was not merely wicked—it was inefficient. Fear made men say anything. He did not want men to say anything. He wanted them to say the one thing that was so, and for that, gentleness was often the sharper instrument.

So when Greymil's headman came out to meet them at the village edge—knee-deep in snow, hat in his hands, breath steaming around a face already gray with dread—Veyne dismounted first, stamped the ice from his boots, and smiled.

"Brother Aldous," he said, reading the man's collar-pin as easily as a page. "You've a cold parish. I'm sorry to bring you more of it."

"Your Grace honors us," Aldous managed. His eyes went past Veyne's shoulder to the twelve riders, counting, the way all of them counted. "We—we had word you were coming through the low valleys. We didn't think—"

"No one ever thinks," Veyne said kindly. "It's why the Office exists." He clapped a mittened hand on the headman's shoulder, felt the flinch beneath the wool, and pretended not to. "Now. First things first. My men have been in the saddle since moonrise, and they'll want hot water and bread, and they'll pay for both. Where do we stable the horses?"

It was, he knew, the correct opening move. Not the chapel, not the questions, not the writ unrolled with its wax seal the size of a fist. Horses and bread. Let the village see that the wolf could be reasonable. Let them begin, even now, in the marrow of themselves, to hope—and hope, once planted, was the most harvestable crop in the world. Men who hoped would tell him everything eventually, because they believed there was a version of events that would let them keep what they loved.

There almost never was. But they needn't know that yet.

* * *

Greymil was forty households strung along a frozen creek, a sawmill, a shrine of the Silent God with its tithe-bowl crusted over in ice, and a chapel of the Ascendant King built twenty years ago atop the bones of something older. Veyne noted the chapel's foundations as they walked past—the dressed stones below were not dressed stones at all but oath-stones, their carvings chiseled flat, and the mortar between them was newer than the stones by centuries. The Ascendant masons had been efficient everywhere in the mountains, but never quite thorough. The old god's architecture kept leaking through, the way roots lift a pavement.

He said nothing about it. Not yet. Everything in its season.

They fed him in the headman's house, at the long table, and the headman's wife set out dark bread and salt fish and a pot of barley soup, and her hands shook as she ladled it, and Veyne ate two full bowls and complimented her seasoning and asked after her children. Two sons. One dead in the King's last levy against the eastern clans; one here, chopping wood, who came to the door twice to look at the inquisitors and twice left quickly. Veyne memorized his face without appearing to look at it. Later, perhaps. Most likely never. But it cost nothing to remember.

Only after the meal, only when the fire had burned down to a comfortable roar and the family had been sent to bed and the headman alone remained across the table from him with a cup of spirits neither of them drank—only then did Veyne fold his hands and open the matter.

"Aldous. Tell me about singing."

The headman's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "Singing?"

"The Office keeps records, as you know. Tithes, censuses, baptisms, heresy trials. Your parish has a clean record for twenty years. Immaculate, really. Almost suspiciously so." Veyne smiled again, to show it was a joke. It was not a joke. "And yet a tinker who passed through here in autumn reported that on the eve of First Frost, he heard music coming from one of your houses. Old music. Music in the old tongue."

"We don't—I wouldn't know anything about—"

"Of course you wouldn't." Veyne let the words sit. Then, gently: "Aldous. Look at me. I am not a stupid man, and you are not a stupid man, and we are sitting in your warm house while your wife sleeps upstairs, and I am asking you to help me understand a small thing. A hymn. That is all this may be. A grandmother's lullaby, half-remembered. Words whose meaning has worn away, like the carvings under your chapel floor. The Office does not execute women for humming." He paused. "It executes women for lying about humming. There is a difference, and the difference is entirely in your hands."

The headman looked at his cup for a long time. Outside, the wind moved down the creek valley with a sound like a great slow breath drawn through stone.

"Marta Ellowen," he said at last. "Widow. Lives at the far end, by the mill. She... she sings to herself. She always has. Her husband died in the Purification, and people said grief made her strange, and we let it be, because she's old, Your Grace, she's older than the King's reign, she minds no one, she—"

"Does she sing in the old tongue?"

"I don't know what the old tongue sounds like. None of us do. That's—" He stopped himself, wisely.

"That's precisely the point," Veyne finished for him, softly. "No one knows anymore. And so no one can say what they heard. Isn't that convenient? For everyone?" He rose, unhurried, and buttoned his coat. "Show me where the widow lives."

* * *

Marta Ellowen answered her door before they knocked. Perhaps she had seen them coming up the lane; perhaps she had been waiting twenty years for exactly this knock, and had simply gotten tired of waiting. She was small and bent and wrapped in shawls, and her eyes—milk-pale, half-blind—went straight past the six soldiers in her yard and found Veyne's face as if she'd known it all her life.

"You'll come in out of the cold," she said. Not a question. "There's tea. There isn't much else, but there's tea."

She poured it herself, steady-handed, into clay cups too fine for a widow's shelf—glazed with the old mountain patterns, the interlaced knots that the Ascendant catechism called *pagan vanity* and every grandmother in the range called *pretty*. Veyne turned his cup slowly in his hands and studied her while she settled into her chair across the hearth, arranging her shawls with the fussiness of a bird.

"Do you know why I've come, Marta Ellowen?"

"I expect it's the same reason the last ones came." She sipped her tea. "My husband's. Before him, my mother's. You people come up the pass every ten years or so, like a tax. Somebody sang something somebody shouldn't have, and you come and burn whoever's nearest to hand, and then you go away again, and we bury what's left, and life goes on until you're back." She looked at him with those clouded eyes. "I'm eighty-one, Your Grace. I've buried four rounds of you. I'd rather you just get on with it."

Something in Veyne's chest tightened—not pity, exactly. Recognition. This was the other kind of patience, the mountain's own kind, and it was formidable. He set down his cup.

"Tell me what you sing."

"Hymns. Lullabies. Whatever my mother taught me before your people cut her tongue out for teaching it." No bitterness in the voice. Only fact, laid out like linen. "I don't know what the words mean anymore. I know how they go. When you're my age, Your Grace, the songs are the last part of a person that stays whole. My knees are gone. My eyes are nearly gone. But the songs—" she touched her breastbone briefly— "in here, they're perfect. Every note. Better than the day I learned them."

"And who taught your mother?"

Her mouth curved. "You know the answer to that."

"Say it anyway."

"The Choir." The word came out of her like a bell struck once, softly, in an empty church. The soldiers in the corner shifted; one of them crossed himself with the Ascendant sign, quick and involuntary. Veyne did not move. "The Choir taught her mother, and her mother's mother, back to the beginning. We were the god's singers, all of us, every village in these mountains, and when the Choir fell, the songs didn't fall with it. Songs are like seeds, Your Grace. They wait. They're patient. They only need one voice, one time, in the right season, and—"

"Enough." He said it quietly, and she stopped, not because she feared him but because she had said what she came to say. Twenty years of silence, and she had spent it all in one evening, and she looked, Veyne thought, lighter for it. Unburdened. Ready.

"Who else knows the songs?" he asked. "In this village. In these mountains. Anyone. A child you hummed to. A neighbor who listened at your window."

"No one listens to old women sing." She smiled. "That was always our protection. And our tragedy."

"There are reports, Marta Ellowen. Reports of a voice. A child's voice, singing in the old tongue, in these passes. Reports that when it sang, bells rang that no hand touched. That oath-stones glowed. That the sick felt well." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost intimate, almost gentle. "If such a child existed—if the Choir's last voice survived the Purification, hidden, growing, waiting—what would that mean to you?"

For the first time, the old woman's composure cracked. Not with fear. With something so naked and fierce that the soldiers looked away from it, embarrassed, as if they'd caught sight of a body.

"It would mean," she whispered, "that everything we buried wasn't dead. That the god kept a mouth. That we weren't abandoned after all, we were just—" her breath caught— "*hidden*. Oh, Your Grace. If you have found that child, then you should know what you're carrying in your own two hands. You should kneel. We should all kneel."

"He is not a child of God," Veyne said. "He is an abomination wearing a child, as a thief wears a coat. The Choir was cut out of the world because the world was dying of it—a hundred mouths all claiming to speak for heaven, a hundred voices, a hundred contradictions, and every tyrant and fool in the mountains claiming divine sanction for his own greed. The King ended that. One throne, one word, one law. What you call abandonment was mercy. What you call hiding was surgery."

"Surgery," Marta repeated. She laughed—a dry, rusted sound, with no humor anywhere in it. "Is that what you call it. I watched them do the surgery on my mother. In the square at Harrow Ford. She sang while they did it, Your Grace. Through the iron. She sang until she couldn't, and the notes went up into the air, and every person in that square heard them and no one has ever been able to forget them, and that is why your King had to kill every singer in the mountains. Because the songs don't die. Because they *wait*."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a horse shifted in the cold.

Veyne stood. He found that his hands were perfectly steady, which was good, because inside he felt something he had not felt in many years—not doubt, nothing so dramatic as doubt, but a faint pressure, like a finger pressed against the far side of a wall. He put it away. He was very practiced at putting things away.

"Marta Ellowen," he said formally, "you stand confessed of the crime of the Forbidden Song, under the statutes of the Ascendant Concord, year nine. Do you recant?"

"No," she said pleasantly, as if declining a second helping.

"Do you repent?"

"I repent that I lived to see the god's own name become a crime. Nothing else."

He nodded, once, and gave the order, and the soldiers moved her gently—Veyne insisted on gentle; it was policy and also principle—and led her out into the snow to the mill-yard, where a stake had already been raised, because his sergeant was efficient and Veyne had stopped pretending to himself that he did not always know, walking into a village, how it would end. He always knew. Patience was not uncertainty. Patience was certainty, stretched thin enough to look like mercy from a distance.

They bound her at the stake. The village gathered, because they always gathered, drawn by the same gravity that drew mourners to graves. Headman Aldous stood at the front with his hat off and his face the color of tallow. Veyne read the charge aloud, the statute, the sentence. The old woman listened with her eyes shut and her face tipped up toward the falling snow, and when he finished, she opened her mouth—

And she sang.

Not the hymn. Something smaller, older—a single rising phrase, five notes, the kind of thing a mother hums over a cradle, and the sound of it in the frozen air was so clear and so strange that the entire yard went silent in a way yards do not go silent. Even the wind seemed to hold. The notes hung there, five bright points in the dark, and Veyne felt them land in his chest like five small nails, and around him he saw hardened soldiers sway on their feet, and saw Aldous press a fist against his mouth, and saw, at the edge of the crowd, a young woman drop to her knees in the snow without knowing she had done it.

Then the torch went in, and the song became smoke, and the smoke became night.

* * *

Afterward, alone in the headman's house with the fire banked and the household asleep, Veyne knelt for his evening offices as he did every night of his adult life, on the worn boards, facing east toward the capital and the Iron Throne and the Ascendant spire. He prayed the prescribed words. He prayed them fluently. He prayed them the way a man signs his name.

And afterward he stayed kneeling a while longer, in the dark, because of the five notes.

Because here was the thing he would write in no report, confess to no superior, examine in no confession of his own: the old woman had sung five notes in a language officially extinct for twenty years, and Veyne—who had helped extinguish it, who had read the archived transcriptions, who knew the phonetics better than any living man outside the Office—had understood her. Not recognized. *Understood.* The five notes meant what the archive said they meant. *Sleep, little one; you are carried; you are not alone.*

Which meant the knowledge was not extinct. Which meant it lived somewhere—in archives, in old women, in the deep folds of memory where the King's fire had never reached. Which meant the Purification had failed in the one way that mattered, because you cannot burn a song out of the species that sings.

Or—which meant nothing at all. A coincidence of melody. A trick of the wind. An old woman's dying conjurer's trick played upon tired nerves.

He rose. He blew out the candle. And in the darkness he admitted, with the honesty that was the last virtue he permitted himself, that the pressure against the wall inside him had grown stronger tonight—that somewhere behind it, faint and patient and unmistakably alive, something was humming.

At dawn, a courier came up from the low country through the snow: a dispatch from the Office's southern bureau, marked with the seal reserved for matters touching the Purification. Veyne broke it standing in the yard, his breath smoking.

Three lines. A warden's station high on the western face, abandoned and reoccupied. Oath-stones, silent for twenty years, glowing in the night. And a boy.

Veyne read it twice. Then he folded it into his coat, over his heart, and turned to his sergeant, and his voice, when he spoke, was calm and clear and carried all the way down the frozen street of Greymil.

"Saddle the horses. We ride west."
