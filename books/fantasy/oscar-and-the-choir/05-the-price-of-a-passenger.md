## 5. The Price of a Passenger

The boy had been at the station for nine days, and Ada had already broken three of her rules.

No passengers. No questions. No names.

She'd broken the first by letting him past the threshold at all. She'd broken the second on the fourth night, when she caught herself asking—quietly, into the dark above his fevered head—*who taught you that tongue?* She had broken the third this morning, without meaning to, when she said *Oscar* aloud and watched him flinch like a struck animal before deciding, slowly, that he liked hearing it.

Three rules in nine days. In eleven years of warden work, she had broken none.

She told herself it was temporary. The snow would clear from the high passes within the week; the thaw-melt would open the smuggler roads; and then she would hand the boy to someone else's care and go back to being a woman who moved cargo and answered no prayers. That was the plan. It was a good plan. It was also a lie, and she knew it was a lie every time she looked at the oath-stones in the cellar and saw the faint, patient glow still living in them like embers under ash.

He was sitting by the hearth now, mending a tear in his coat with clumsy, careful stitches—the same coat whose lining she had cut open twice since he arrived, checking and rechecking the oath-stone sewn there against his shoulder blade. He hummed while he worked. Not a tune she recognized. Not a tune anyone alive should have recognized. It was one of the Choir's cadences, the ones her grandmother had been dragged out of her own kitchen for humming, sixty years ago, and Ada stood in the doorway with an armful of split firewood and let him do it because there was no one left to drag either of them anywhere.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up. His voice had lost some of its rawness. Fed and warm, he might have been ten or twelve; it was hard to say. Starvation had sanded the years off him.

"I'm thinking."

"That's worse." He bit the thread. "When you think, you get the face you're making now."

"And what face is that?"

"The one people make when they're going to give you away."

Ada set the firewood down harder than she needed to. "Nobody's giving you away."

"You thought about it." He looked up then, and his eyes were too old for his face, the way eyes got when they'd seen soldiers burn things. "First night. You stood over me with your knife out and you thought about carrying me up to the pass and leaving me for the crows. I don't blame you. It's what I'd have done."

"I don't leave children for crows."

"No," he agreed mildly, and went back to his stitching. "You leave them at shrines."

That landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there. He knew about the shrine. Of course he knew about the shrine—he'd heard her tithe ritual through the floorboards, the old words poured out over stone that never answered, night after night, a woman watering a dead garden. She hadn't known he was listening until the third morning, when he'd asked her why she kept talking to something that wasn't there.

*It isn't dead,* she'd snapped. *It's quiet.*

And the stones in the cellar had hummed.

She went down to check them again after breakfast, because checking them had become a compulsion she couldn't shake. The cellar was cold and smelled of old tallow and older stone. Seven oath-stones sat in their iron cradles along the wall, each one carved with a name that had once belonged to a warden of Thornfell, each one silent for twenty years—until the boy's fevered sleep had set them murmuring like a congregation remembering its lines.

They were glowing now. Faintly. Steady as banked coals.

"Stop that," Ada said.

The stones did not stop. They never stopped anymore. They simply glowed, and waited, and made her feel like a woman standing in a house where all the furniture had shifted two inches to the left overnight.

She climbed back up and found Oscar watching her from the hearth with that terrible, gentle patience children sometimes had, as if they understood everything and forgave most of it.

"We need papers," she said.

His needle paused. "Papers?"

"Safe-passage writs. Sealed ones, free-cities issue, the kind that get a body through a checkpoint without being opened, weighed, or questioned." She was pacing now, which she hated herself for. Wardens didn't pace. Wardens stood still and let trouble come to them. "You can't stay here. Veyne's people are in the low valleys already—I've had smoke signals off the southern beacons for two days, and beacon-smoke doesn't lie. Sooner or later they'll work the passes, and sooner or later they'll find this station, and I am not going to be here when they do."

"Where will we be?"

"*We*—" She stopped. Put her palm flat against the mantel, the way she did when she needed the wood's honest grain to steady her. "I haven't decided what we are yet."

"You have, though." Oscar set down the coat. His hands were small and scarred and very steady. "You've been deciding since the night you didn't turn me out. You just keep hoping the decision will change its mind."

Eleven years old, maybe twelve, and he talked like a man who'd buried a family. Which, she reminded herself, he had.

"Papers cost," she said. "Papers cost more than gold. Gold I have. What they cost is favors, and favors are debts, and debts are how invisible women stop being invisible." She rubbed the bridge of her nose. "I'm going to have to spend people, Oscar. People who trust me. And when it's done, I can't ever use those roads again, and neither can forty other men and women who never did anything wrong except know my name."

"Then don't," he said quietly. "Turn me out. You almost did once. The snow's softer now."

Ada looked at him for a long moment—at the mended coat, the half-eaten bowl of barley porridge going cold beside him, the small stubborn shape of him sitting in her chair as though he'd always been there—and felt the last of her invisibility tear loose inside her like a stitch pulled from old leather.

"Get your coat," she said. "We're going to see a man about a lie."

* * *

Bren Halloran kept a counting-house in the river town of Marrowgate, three days' travel down from the high passes, and he had known Ada Eastwick since before either of them had anything worth counting.

They had come up together out of the border wars—she a scout, he a quartermaster's clerk with a genius for paperwork and a conscience he'd learned to fold small enough to fit in a ledger. When the wars ended and the usurper's peace settled over the mountains like a lid on a pot, Bren had gone legitimate with the smoothness of a man stepping out of wet clothes, and Ada had gone underground, and between them they had built the only thing either of them trusted: a system. Her routes, his seals. Her silence, his signatures. For eleven years notarized paper had flowed between them like water through a millrace, and in all that time Bren had never once asked what moved along her roads.

That was the arrangement. That was the friendship. Cargo stayed cargo, names stayed unspoken, and both of them slept soundly in the knowledge that ignorance, properly maintained, was the strongest lock ever forged.

Which was why Ada spent the whole ride down to Marrowgate composing the sentence she would have to say, and why, standing in his counting-house amid the smell of ink and lamp oil, she found that every version of it died in her throat.

Bren looked older than the last time she'd seen him. Greyer at the temples, thicker through the middle, with the permanent squint of a man who read fine print by bad light. But his eyes were the same—quick, kind, and appallingly hard to fool.

"Eastwick." He came around the desk and clasped her forearm, brief and firm. "You look like a woman who's been sleeping in a barn."

"I have been sleeping in a barn. It's called a warden station."

"It's called a ruin with a roof." He waved her toward a chair, and that was when he saw the boy hovering in the doorway behind her, hood up, face shadowed. Bren's gaze went from the child to Ada and back again, and Ada watched eleven years of carefully maintained ignorance begin, visibly, to die.

"Sit down, Bren," she said.

"I'm standing fine."

"Bren. Sit down."

He sat. Slowly, the way men sit when their legs have guessed something ahead of them.

Ada laid it out flat, because there was no other way to lay it: the boy, the pass, the inquisitors in the low valleys, the need for sealed passage-writs through the Thornhollow checkpoint and the river tolls beyond. She gave him the shape of the problem without the heart of it. A child, orphaned, marked by circumstance, needing to move west to the free cities. Nothing more. She watched him listen with his whole body, the way he'd listened to supply reports across a burning camp twenty years ago, and she watched him arrive—inevitably, the way weather arrives—at the question underneath the request.

"Those writs aren't cheap," he said at last. "Sealed free-cities passage, checkpoint-proof? I'd have to call in marks from three different magistrates. Magistrates ask questions, Ada. Magistrates want to know what they're sealing." He leaned forward, elbows on the desk, and his voice dropped into the register he'd used across campfires, the one that meant *don't lie to me, I'm too tired for it.* "So before I spend thirty years of goodwill on one piece of paper, you're going to tell me what I'm carrying."

"You're not carrying anything. You're signing parchment."

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip, and then he mastered himself, and his voice came back down soft and hurt. "Don't do that. Don't give me the road-voice. I've known you since you were seventeen and starving and lied about your age to carry a spear. I carried you off the field at Red Ford with half your blood outside your body. I have signed four hundred documents for you and never asked a single question, and you know why? Because you told me once—drunk, the only time I've ever seen you drunk—that whatever moved on your roads, it was never people. Never flesh. You swore it on your god's name, back when you still had one." His hands were flat on the desk now, trembling slightly. "And now you walk in here with a child wearing a hood in springtime, and inquisitor beacons burning in the south, and you ask me for the kind of paper that gets used exactly one way."

The silence stretched. Somewhere upstairs, a clerk's footsteps crossed the floor. Outside, Marrowgate went about its commerce, barges knocking gently against the quay, the ordinary world grinding on, oblivious.

Oscar shifted in the doorway. Ada put out one hand—not looking, just knowing where he was—and touched his sleeve. *Stay.* He stayed.

"Bren," she said. "If I tell you, you're in it. All the way in. There's no ledger entry for getting back out."

"I know."

"If they catch you holding those seals, they won't hang you as a smuggler. Smugglers get fines. This gets fire."

"I know that too." He swallowed. "What I don't know is whether I can live with myself if I sign blind. Because here's my secret, Ada, the one nobody pays for: I'm not brave. I never was. I was good at paperwork in a war because paperwork keeps you three miles behind the line. Every favor I've ever done you, I did in the dark, where I couldn't see it. And now you're asking me to do the biggest one of all, and you want me to do it—" his voice caught, steadied— "*sighted.*"

Ada looked at her oldest friend, and understood, finally, the true price of the passenger. It wasn't the gold. It wasn't the routes she'd burn or the contacts she'd spend. It was this: that the arrangement which had protected them both for eleven years—the beautiful, cowardly architecture of not-asking—was about to collapse, and what it collapsed into was trust, which was heavier than ignorance and far more fragile.

She could lie to him. He'd believe her; he wanted to believe her; and the writs would flow, and the arrangement would hold, and someday the inquisition would peel it open anyway and Bren would learn that his oldest friend had fed him one more comfortable darkness right up until it killed him.

Or she could tell him.

She glanced back at Oscar. The boy had pushed his hood back without being asked. He stood in the lamplight of the counting-house, small and plain and watchful, and around his shoulders the mended coat hung with its hidden stone, and Ada thought: *he deserves better than to be cargo. Even for one hour. Even here.*

"Bren," she said. "Do you remember my grandmother?"

He blinked. "The heretic? The one they—yes. Everyone on the border remembers."

"She used to sing to me. Songs in a language the usurper says died twenty years ago, sung by people the usurper says he killed to the last soul." Ada's voice was level and quiet and cost her more than any speech she'd ever given. "This boy speaks it in his sleep. And the oath-stones in my cellar—stones that haven't answered a prayer since the Choir fell—glow when he breathes near them. I don't know what he is, Bren. I know what the inquisitors think he is, and I know what happens to him if they find him, and I know that I have broken every rule I ever built to keep him breathing. That's the cargo. That's what you're sealing. A dead language in a living throat, and the last prayer my god has answered in twenty years."

Bren Halloran sat very still for a long time.

Then he laughed—one short, broken, disbelieving sound, the laugh of a man whose ledger has just balanced against all odds—and pressed both palms over his face.

"A god," he said through his fingers. "Twenty years of nothing, and now a god shows up in a child, on my desk, wanting three magistrate seals." He dropped his hands and looked at Oscar—really looked, the way a quartermaster looks at a thing he must now count among his stores. "Boy. Do you understand what she's asking me to risk?"

"No," Oscar said honestly. "But I understand what she's risking. She told me on the ride down. All of it. She said you were the best man she knew and the worst liar, and that if you said no, she wouldn't blame you, and she'd find another way even if it killed her." He shrugged, small and grave. "I mostly hope you say yes. But I wanted you to know she already paid. Before you decide."

Bren stared at the boy. Then at Ada. Then he did something she had never seen him do in twenty-five years of friendship: he opened the bottom drawer of his desk, took out a bottle of plum brandy older than the war, and poured three cups.

"To sighted work," he said grimly, and raised his cup. "God help us all. Apparently he's short-staffed."

They drank. And while Bren sharpened his pen and pulled out the magistrate letters, Ada stood at the rain-streaked window of the counting-house and watched the southern horizon, where—far off, faint as a scratch on glass—a beacon smoldered that hadn't burned yesterday.

Two beacons now. Moving north.

Veyne was climbing.
