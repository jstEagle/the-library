## 6. Hymns in the Dark

Oscar counted the voices the way other children counted sheep.

There was the deep one, low as a well, that hummed when he was frightened and made his ribs feel like a warm hearth. There were two that sang together always, a man's voice and a woman's voice braided so tightly he could not tell where one ended and the other began; they sang about bread, mostly, and rain on rooftops, and once—only once—a lullaby in words he did not know but understood anyway, the way you understand warmth without being able to explain it. There was a young one, barely older than him, who liked to name things: *that is a hawk, that is snow coming, that is a lie.* And there was the last one, the quietest, who never sang at all but only listened, and whose listening felt like being held.

Seven voices. No—six, and the listening one. He had counted wrong for a long time, before he learned to be careful. The seventh had gone away the night of the fire, and when it went, it took its face with it, so that now Oscar could not remember whether it had been tall or short, old or young, kind or otherwise. Only that the space where it had been ached, like a tooth pulled too soon.

He lay in the dark of the warden station's back room, under a blanket that smelled of cedar smoke and something older, something like stone left out in the sun, and he counted them again to make sure they were all there.

*Deep one. The two-who-sing. The namer. The listener.*

Four, plus himself. Five.

He frowned into the darkness. Last night there had been five besides himself. The night before, six. They came and went like birds at a window, and he could not tell if they were leaving him or arriving, and this frightened him more than the soldiers had, though he would never have said so aloud. You did not say such things aloud. Saying things aloud was how the soldiers found people.

*You are safe,* said the deep one, and the humming moved through his chest like water through a reed. *The door is shut. The woman is awake. She walks the floor below us, counting her steps.*

Oscar knew that already. Ada—the woman with the grey eyes and the hands that never stopped moving—walked her floors at night the way a dog circles before lying down. He had heard her through the boards for three nights now. Twelve steps, pause. Twelve steps, pause. She did not sleep much. He wondered if she was afraid of him. Grown-ups were usually afraid of him, though they tried to hide it behind kindnesses, and Ada did not even try very hard to hide it, which Oscar privately appreciated. It was restful, being around someone honest about their fear.

*She is not afraid of you,* said the two-who-sing, gently, in their braid of voices. *She is afraid for you. It is not the same thing.*

*It feels the same,* Oscar said—not aloud, never aloud, but in the place inside where the talking happened, the place that had no mouth.

*It is the same shape,* the namer agreed, *but it is not the same weight. Fear-of weighs nothing. Fear-for weighs everything. That is why she carries it in her shoulders.*

Oscar considered this. The voices were always saying things like that, small true things arranged in strange orders, and he had learned that if he held still and let them settle, they made a kind of sense, the way snow makes sense after it stops falling.

He turned onto his side. Through the gap beneath the door he could see lamplight moving in the main room, and hear the scratch of Ada's pen. She wrote a great deal. Lists, he thought. His mother—he stopped. He reached for his mother the way you reach for a stair that isn't there in the dark, and the two-who-sing caught him before he fell, the way they always did, wrapping the memory in song until it was only an ache and not a wound.

His mother had sung. That was the whole of what remained: not her face, not her hands, but the fact of singing, the fact of her voice rising over the crackle of the burning thatch while the soldiers stood in the square and watched, and the strange, terrible beauty of it, how she had sung louder as the house grew hotter, how the song had not been a scream, how it had gone up and up like a bird released from a cage that was also a pyre—

*Hush,* said the deep one. *Not tonight. Tonight we will give you the river instead.*

And they gave him the river: a memory that was not his, of green water sliding over brown stones, of willows trailing their fingers, of a summer so long ago it belonged to no one alive. Oscar breathed with it. In through the nose, out through the mouth, the way the listener had taught him without ever speaking. The ache receded. The stairless dark became only dark.

This was what the voices did for him. This was what no one else could know.

* * *

In the morning, Ada was frying barley in a black pan, and the smell made his stomach announce itself with embarrassing loudness. She glanced at him from under her cropped hair and said nothing, but she scraped half the pan onto his tin plate, which was more than half, and he understood that this was how she talked.

"You slept," she said. "Better than yesterday."

"The fever broke," Oscar said. "I told you."

"I know what you told me." Her eyes narrowed, just slightly. "You talk in your sleep."

Oscar's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. "Do I?"

"In a language I haven't heard since I was younger than you." She turned back to the pan, but her shoulders had gone stiff, twelve-steps-pause stiff. "Where did you learn it?"

"Nowhere." The word came out too fast. He made himself slow down. "It's not—I don't learn it. It's just there."

"Things are not *just there*, boy."

"My mother sang it," he said, and then, because the namer was nudging him, because honesty seemed suddenly important in a way he couldn't explain: "But she didn't teach me. I knew it before she sang it. When she sang, it was like hearing my own voice come back to me from far away."

Ada set down the pan harder than she needed to. The clang rang off the stone walls and one of the voices—the deep one—rumbled a warning, soft as distant thunder, and Oscar pressed his hand flat against his own sternum as if he could hold it in.

"What was that?" Ada asked sharply. She had turned around. She was looking at his hand on his chest.

"Hungry," Oscar said. "Stomach growling."

They looked at each other across the small room, the liar and the worse liar, and Oscar felt a hot shame climb his throat, because he had never lied well, and because he could see that she knew, and because knowing made her fear-for heavier, and all of it was his fault, somehow, the way everything was his fault, the way the fire had been his fault—

*No,* said the two-who-sing, fierce now, both voices at once. *No, little one. You were four years old. You were asleep in a cellar. Whatever came to you that night came looking for a hiding place, and you were kind enough to open the door. Kindness is not guilt.*

"But it lives in me now," Oscar said, and realized he had spoken aloud, and clapped his hand over his mouth.

Ada had gone very pale. "What lives in you?"

And here was the fork in the road, the one he had been walking toward for three days. He could lie again. He was getting better at it, or worse—he couldn't tell which. Or he could tell her the truth, which was that he did not know the truth, which was that he had carried a secret so large for so long that he no longer knew its edges, and that every person he had ever told—even telling was too strong a word, even hinting—was dead.

"The singing," he said finally. "It doesn't stop. Even when I'm awake. Even when I want it to."

Something crossed Ada's face that was not fear. It was older than fear, and sadder. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone hearing a hymn from a church that burned down before she was born.

"Sing it now," she said quietly. "Just a little."

"No." He gripped the edge of the table. "Please. Not inside. Bad things happen when I sing inside."

*Bad things happen when anyone sings inside,* murmured the deep one, with a bitterness that startled him. The deep one almost never spoke of itself. *That is rather the point of us.*

"Outside, then," Ada said. She pulled open the heavy door, and the mountain air poured in, cold and clean and smelling of pine and snowmelt. "Come on. Nothing out here to break but the wind."

* * *

The station sat in a fold of the Thornfell passes like a coin dropped in a pocket, ringed by scree slopes and black pines, and above it—Oscar saw them properly for the first time that morning—stood a circle of standing stones, frost-rimed, taller than a man, each carved over its whole surface with writing in the language he dreamed in.

He stopped walking. The voices went silent. All of them, even the listener, even the deep one, and the silence was so total and so sudden that Oscar swayed, feeling suddenly alone in his own body for the first time since the fire.

"They're reading," he whispered.

"What?" Ada had stopped a few paces ahead. "Reading what?"

"The stones." He pointed. His finger shook. "They're—they're reading themselves. Out loud. All the time. Can't you hear it?"

Ada stared at the stones for a long moment. Her jaw worked. "Those stones have been silent for twenty years," she said. "My grandmother died for less than what you're describing. Everyone said the stones went dark when the Choir fell. Everyone said—" She stopped herself, the way people do when they are stepping back from a cliff edge. "Say something to them."

"I don't know how."

"You sang in your sleep. Sing now."

"I told you—"

"*Boy.*" Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "I have spent twenty years building a life on the certainty that all of this is dead. Dead gods, dead songs, dead stones. If you are going to take that from me, you will do it to my face, in daylight, where I can watch it happen. Sing."

The cruelty of it was also a kindness, and Oscar knew that, and hated that he knew it. He closed his eyes. He reached inward, past the silence, past the empty places where voices should have been, and found the smallest thread of the two-who-sing's melody still caught in him like a burr in wool, and he pulled it gently, and opened his mouth.

What came out was not loud. It was hardly more than breath shaped into sound, four notes rising, the way his mother had risen, the way the river rises in spring. But the mountain took it. The mountain took it and gave it back multiplied, and the standing stones began to glow.

Not all at once. One first, the tallest, a faint gold waking along its carvings like embers breathed upon, and then its neighbor, and then the next, light running from stone to stone around the circle as if the circle were a sentence being read aloud at last after twenty years of holding its breath. The sound was underneath the hearing, more felt than heard, a hum in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the wet dark of the ears—and inside Oscar, the voices came flooding back all together, all seven spaces filled at once, the deep one and the two-who-sing and the namer and the listener and the lost seventh, the one whose face was gone, singing, singing, *he is here, he is here, we thought we had lost him too—*

Oscar fell to his knees in the frost.

He was crying and did not know when he had started. The light washed over him, warm as the hearth-hum, and the voices were so glad, so unbearably glad, and their gladness hurt worse than grief, because gladness meant hope, and hope meant that all of this was real, that it had always been real, that the soldiers had burned his village and murdered everyone who loved him over something that could not die.

"Stop," he gasped. "Make it stop, I don't know how to make it stop—"

Hands on his shoulders. Ada, kneeling beside him in the frost, her grey eyes enormous, reflecting the golden light of the stones.

"Breathe," she said. "Look at me. Breathe with me. In through the nose."

"In through the nose," he sobbed, "out through the mouth—"

"That's it. Again."

Slowly, note by note, the light dimmed. The hum sank back beneath the world. The voices settled, one by one, into their usual quiet, and the last to go was the seventh, the returned one, who touched some part of Oscar that had no name and left there, unmistakably, a single word:

*Soon.*

Then it was morning again, ordinary and cold, and Oscar knelt in the frost with a grown woman's hands gripping his shoulders, and the stones stood dark and dumb as any rocks.

Ada did not let go of him for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough, as if she had been the one singing.

"When the soldiers come asking," she said, "and they will come, boy, sooner than either of us wants—you tell them nothing. You sing nothing. You are a deaf-mute orphan from the lowlands if you have to be. Do you understand me?"

"Why?" Oscar whispered. It was the question underneath all his questions, the one the voices would not answer, the one he was almost afraid to ask. "Why does it matter? Why do *they* care? It's only singing."

Ada looked at the dark stones, and then at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her decide something; he could see the decision land in her like a stone dropped into a well.

"It's never been only singing," she said. "Twenty years ago, men marched into these mountains and killed every soul who could sing those songs, and they called it holy work, and they built a crown on top of the graves. If even one voice is left—" She stopped. Shook her head. "If even one voice is left, boy, then everything that crown is built on is a lie. And lies that big don't die quietly. They send inquisitors."

Oscar didn't know the word. But the deep one did, and its rumble rolled through his chest like a rockslide starting somewhere far above, and the listener, the quiet one, the one who only listened, said, in a voice like a closing door:

*They are already climbing.*
