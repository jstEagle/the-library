## 7. The Toll of Thornhollow Bridge

The bridge announced itself an hour before they reached it.

Ada felt it first in the wheels—a change in the road's song, the ruts smoothing as the old pilgrim way was repaired for the first time in a generation. Then Bren's outrider came loping back down the line with his hood thrown back and his face gone the color of tallow, and she knew before he spoke.

"Checkpoint," he said. "Thornhollow Bridge. Inquisition banners on both towers."

Ada did not curse. Cursing was a luxury, and luxuries were for people whose cargo was wool and salted fish. She rode forward along the caravan instead, past the wagons with their false bottoms full of untaxed brandy, past the muleteers who would not meet her eye because everyone knew what a warden's face looked like when the day went wrong, until she stood at the head of the line where the road bent around a shoulder of granite and she could see the bridge itself.

Thornhollow had been built by believers, back when belief was safe to build with. Two stone towers flanked the span, their faces carved with saints whose features some pious vandal or pragmatic mason had chiseled smooth a generation ago—faceless now, anonymous as any god who'd stopped answering. The usurper's black-and-silver hung from both towers, and beneath it, arranged across the near end of the bridge with the unhurried precision of men who had done this many times, stood perhaps thirty soldiers and four figures in grey.

Inquisitors' agents. Not the grey-robed priests themselves—those came only for heresy trials and burned villages—but the lay arm, the clerks and questioners and quiet-eyed men with ledgers. They were checking papers. They were pulling travelers aside. And at the far end of the span, half-hidden behind a supply wagon, stood something that made Ada's stomach drop through the mountain beneath her: a cage on wheels, iron-barred, empty, waiting.

She turned her horse and rode back down the line, and this time she let herself curse, quietly, in the smugglers' cant that no agent of any inquisition had ever bothered to learn.

* * *

They gathered in the lee of the third wagon, the six people who needed to know: Bren, his two outriders, Marta the cook who doubled as their eyes at checkpoints, and Ada. Oscar stayed in the grain wagon under the false floor, where Ada had put him an hour ago on nothing more than instinct—the same instinct that had kept her alive through twenty years of mountains, and which she was beginning to understand had been screaming at her since the boy first knocked on her door.

"Routine toll," Marta said. "Except it isn't. They're not weighing cargo. They're looking at *faces*. There's a clerk with one of those memory-books—you know, the ones with the charcoal sketches—and every man, woman, and child over the age of five is getting turned toward the light."

"A description," Bren said. "They're hunting someone specific." He glanced at Ada, and there it was again, the question he'd been carrying since the night she'd traded half her contacts for those forged passage papers: *what are you carrying?* He didn't ask it. He'd learned not to. But it sat between them like a stone neither would pick up.

"They're hunting a child," Ada said. "A boy, ten or eleven years old, traveling alone or with a woman not his mother. That's what the village criers were shouting two valleys back, if you'd been listening instead of drinking."

"I was listening," Bren said quietly. "I also noticed you bought a boy new boots in Harrowgate and told me he was your sister's son, and your sister died childless, Ada, because I carried her coffin."

The silence stretched. Marta looked at the ground. The outriders found urgent business with their horses.

"Bren," Ada said, and made her voice flat and cold and professional, the voice of the warden of Thornfell, the woman with rules. "You can turn me over to them right now if you want. Walk me up to that bridge and collect whatever bounty they've posted. I won't fight you. But if you do it, know that whatever they're paying, it won't cover what happens after. Because the men running that checkpoint don't leave witnesses to bargains. You've moved enough contraband past enough magistrates to know how the honest ones behave when they think God is watching."

"That's not fair."

"No," Ada agreed. "None of this is fair. Fairness died with my grandmother on a pyre. What's left is arithmetic." She held his eyes. "Can we cross?"

Bren exhaled through his nose, long and slow, the breath of a man counting costs he couldn't name. "We can cross. But not as us. If they see the whole caravan, they'll want to open everything, and we've got forty casks of brandy in false bottoms that'll hang all of us regardless of your boy." He rubbed his jaw. "Split it. The front half crosses clean—Marta, the food wagons, the mules, papers in order, nothing worth finding. The back half turns off at the shepherd's track above the gorge and comes over the ford at Lowstone, three days' detour but no bridge, no clerks."

"And the middle?" Marta asked.

"The middle is the corpse-wagon," Bren said, and didn't look at Ada when he said it.

* * *

The corpse-wagon had come up out of the lowlands two days ago, hired at obscene expense from an undertaker in a river town who asked no questions because undertakers never do—it's the living who make trouble. It was a plain black box on wheels, draped in mourning cloth gone grey with road dust, and it smelled of lime and old flowers and the particular sweet-rot sweetness that no amount of lime fully kills. Inside, packed in straw, lay three bodies: a drover dead of a fever, a miller's wife dead of childbirth, and a child of four dead of the coughing sickness, all bound for family plots in the hill villages, all legitimate, all documented, all genuinely, verifiably dead.

It was the one cargo on the entire caravan that no inquisitor's agent wanted to inspect. Grief was tiresome. Death was paperwork without profit. Every soldier on that bridge had opened a smuggler's false bottom at least once; none of them had ever willingly put their hands into a box of plague-fear and lime.

Ada stood at the back of the wagon and looked at the small shape huddled against the wheel well, wrapped in a dead woman's shawl, and hated herself with a thoroughness that surprised her.

"You'll be fine," she said. "You'll be still, and you'll be quiet, and in an hour it's over."

Oscar looked up at her. His fever had broken two days ago, but it had left him thin and strange-eyed, and he kept doing a thing she'd noticed now several times—tilting his head slightly, as if listening to something just behind her shoulder. She refused to wonder what. Wondering was how people died on this mountain.

"There are bodies in here," he said. Not accusing. Just stating it, the way children state terrible things, because they haven't yet learned that terrible things require performance.

"Yes."

"The little one doesn't mind." He said it simply, and Ada felt the hair rise on her arms. "He says the straw is warm. He says—" Oscar paused, tilted his head again, and his brow furrowed with the effort of translation. "He says his mother is at the end of the road and she has bread."

"Don't," Ada said. Her voice came out harder than she intended. "Don't say things like that. Not here. Not ever, where anyone can hear."

Oscar flinched, and pulled the shawl tighter, and became very interested in the floorboards, and Ada stood there in the lime-smelling dark feeling like the worst kind of monster—the kind that knows exactly what it's doing.

"Here." She knelt, took his coat from the straw, and turned it inside out. Along the inner seam of the left side, she'd sewn a small leather pocket days ago, and into the pocket she now slipped a disc of grey stone the size of a coin, worn smooth, its face marked with a spiral worn nearly to nothing. An oath-stone. Her grandmother's. One of the few things Ada had taken from the shrine before the world decided shrines were evidence.

"This stays against your skin," she said, threading the coat back onto him. "If anyone asks, it's a saint's medal. Old ones look like that—smooth, no face. Nobody questions a mourning child wearing his mother's medal."

"What does it do?"

"It's a rock, Oscar. It does what rocks do."

He looked at her with those too-old eyes, and she had the sudden vertiginous certainty that he knew she was lying, and worse, that he understood *why* she was lying, and forgave her for it, which was unbearable.

"Will you be close?" he asked.

"Close enough."

* * *

The split went smoothly, which should have been the first warning. Nothing in Ada's life went smoothly; smoothness was the mountain setting its trap with extra care.

The front half of the caravan rolled up to the bridge in the grey light of late morning, and Ada watched from the treeline above the road as Marta presented papers, as soldiers waved torches under wagon beds and found nothing, as the clerk with the memory-book turned each face toward the light and found it forgettable. Twenty minutes. Thirty. Then the front half was across, dwindling down the far road, and Ada brought the corpse-wagon and its single escort—herself driving, Bren riding flank—out of the trees and onto the bridge approach.

"Halt. Wagon."

The soldier was young, with the raw knuckles and chapped lips of a garrison man, and behind him stood one of the grey agents—a narrow, neat man with ink-stained fingers and the patient expression of someone reading a document he already knew by heart. His eyes went to the mourning cloth, and Ada watched the calculation move through him like water through a channel: death, distaste, the desire to be elsewhere.

"Papers," the agent said.

Ada handed them over. Three death certificates, properly sealed, properly witnessed, the undertaker's manifest, her own passage papers listing her as contracted transport for the bereaved families. Forged, all of it, but forged by the best forger money could buy, and money was the one thing Ada had spent freely these past weeks.

The agent read. The soldier shifted. Somewhere ahead, on the bridge, another traveler was being questioned, and a woman was crying—not loudly, just steadily, the way people cry when they've learned there's no point in volume.

"These families," the agent said. "Hill folk?"

"Valley and hill both," Ada said. "The drover's people are at Colder's Mill. The woman's husband holds a croft above Lowstone. The child—" she let the smallest hitch into her voice, measured to the grain, "—the child goes to his grandmother at Thornhollow village. She couldn't travel to fetch him. He was four."

Something moved behind the agent's eyes—not suspicion, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who had processed a thousand griefs and filed them all under the same heading. He stamped the manifest.

"Open the—"

"My lord." Ada's voice, soft, apologetic, immovable as bedrock. "I'd ask you not to. Fever touched the drover at the end, and the little one—" she shook her head. "The undertaker dosed them heavy with lime, but the roads have been warm. If your superiors ask why an inquisition agent handled plague-suspect remains, I'll tell them truthfully that you were thorough. But I'd hate for you to carry that story home."

The pause lasted three seconds. Ada counted them the way she counted everything—heartbeats, switchbacks, the distance between lightning and thunder. On the third second, the agent's distaste won, as she'd known it would, because distaste always wins with men who work death as a clerical matter. He stepped back and waved the wagon through with two fingers, like flicking away a fly.

The wheels rolled onto the bridge. The river ran brown and loud beneath the boards. Ada kept her eyes forward, her hands loose on the reins, her breathing even, and allowed herself the first breath of relief—

And the oath-stone sang.

One note. Clear as a struck bell, pure as ice water, rising out of the black box of the corpse-wagon and hanging in the air over the bridge like a thread of silver. It was not loud. It did not need to be. It was the kind of sound that went through cloth and wood and bone and rang in the teeth, and Ada knew—instantly, totally, with the cold clarity of a woman falling—that of the thirty soldiers and four agents on that bridge, every single one of them had heard it, and every single one of them had spent their childhood in villages where the old women still whispered about the Choir, and every single one of them knew, in the marrow-deep way that people know church bells and wolf howls, that this was neither.

The soldier by the tower turned his head. The crying woman stopped crying. Down the whole length of the span, faces lifted.

The narrow agent with the ink-stained fingers stepped back into the road.

"The wagon," he said. "Stop the wagon."

Ada stopped it. There was nothing else to do; flight meant arrows, and arrows meant a dead boy in a box that would then be opened anyway. She set the brake with steady hands while her mind moved at the speed it moved in avalanches and ambushes, sorting options, discarding most.

The agent walked the length of the wagon slowly. He put his palm flat against the mourning cloth. "What is in this wagon?"

"Grief," Ada said. "Three bodies and grief. I told your colleague—"

"You told my colleague a story about lime." He wasn't angry. That was the worst part. He sounded almost gentle, almost sorry, a man regretting a necessary chore. "That sound came from inside. I've read the field manuals, mistress. Resonant phenomena, unexplained acoustic events, the *echo-signs*. The manuals say the last echo announces itself with sound that has no source." His eyes came up to hers, and they were pale and patient and absolutely certain. "So I'm going to open this wagon now. And you're going to stand very still, because if there's a child in there, you're going to want to be very cooperative about what happens next."

Ada looked at him. Behind him, soldiers were spreading along the bridge, cutting off the far end. Bren had frozen fifty yards back, hand nowhere near his sword, doing the arithmetic of thirty against two and arriving at surrender. Ahead, the far tower, the road beyond, the hills where Marta and the front half were already miles away and unreachable and safe.

And inside the wagon, behind her, in the straw with the dead, a four-year-old's voice said clearly, in a language twenty years extinct, words that Ada's grandmother had died rather than translate:

*Be not afraid.*

The agent heard it. Of course he heard it. Everyone heard it. His certainty flickered—for just a moment, one heartbeat, the first crack in the porcelain—and in that heartbeat Ada Eastwick, warden of Thornfell, breaker of her own rules, did the only thing left in her ledger.

She laughed.

Loud, rough, contemptuous, the laugh of a hard woman at a soft man, and she threw back the mourning cloth herself before he could touch it, hauling it aside to reveal the straw and the shrouded bodies and the stench rolling out like a weapon. "There's your echo, my lord!" she barked, gesturing at the corpses with a corpse-handler's coarse familiarity. "Dead men's guts singing! You want to search a plague-box for a miracle child, climb in. Climb in and put your clever hands on him. But do it quick, because I'm not standing downwind of your courage."

The agent stared into the wagon. At the drover's shroud, sunken where a face should be. At the small bundle that was, thank every silent god, actually a dead child, actually four years old, actually beyond anyone's help. The stench hit him in waves. His certainty warred with his stomach, and his stomach had better arguments.

"Search it," he said hoarsely, to the young soldier. "Not me."

The soldier searched. Hands in the straw, under the bodies, along the wheel wells, gagging twice. He found the brandy flask Ada had planted under the drover's shroud—untaxed, illegal, a real crime, a findable crime—and the agent seized on it with the relief of a drowning man seizing driftwood.

"Smuggling," he said. "Confiscate the wagon's contents. Fine the transporter. Detain her at the tower for processing." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and would not look at the straw again. "The echo-sign report will note... ambient resonance. River wind in the boards. Nothing further."

"Nothing further," Ada repeated, and her voice was flat and obedient and, underneath, cold as the river below.

They marched her off the bridge toward the tower, and behind her the corpse-wagon rolled on across the span with its escort, its legitimate dead, and its living passenger curled motionless in the straw with a warm stone against his chest—one clear note richer, one secret deeper, and a grandmother at Thornhollow village who was about to receive, by prearranged signal, a coffin she would bury with astonishing speed and no questions at all.

Ada walked between the soldiers with her wrists already out for binding, and thought, with something that was not quite hope and not quite prayer: *Twenty years, you were silent. Now you sing at checkpoints. Whatever you're playing at, old man, you had better be worth what this is going to cost me.*

Behind her, faint under the noise of the river, she could have sworn the stones of the bridge hummed once more—softly, briefly, almost like laughter.
