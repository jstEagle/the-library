## 8. Bren's Betrayal

The debt notice came on the first cold morning of autumn, nailed to the door of Bren's counting-house with a knife.

Not a proper knife — one of the cheap single-edged things rivermen carried, the kind that cost two coppers in any market from here to the coast. But it went through the oak door and through the paper beneath it and stood there quivering when Bren pulled back the bolt, and the message was plain enough without anyone having to say it aloud: *we know where you sleep.*

He read the paper twice. The figures hadn't changed since the last notice, but they'd been rewritten in red ink, which was its own kind of sentence. Four hundred crowns to the Harrow Guild, plus interest compounded at a rate that had stopped being arithmetic and become theology some months ago. Below the figures, in smaller script, the usual courtesy: *Payment due by first frost. Alternative arrangements may be discussed at the Guild's discretion.*

Bren knew what alternative arrangements meant. He'd seen a man named Col Vask come out of the Harrow offices in the spring with his fingers wrapped in boiled wool, all ten of them, and a smile on his face like a man who'd been told he could keep his tongue if he kept smiling. Col had smiled for three weeks. Then they'd found him in the millrace with his pockets full of stones and his ledger burned, and everyone in Saltmarsh had understood that the Guild did not forgive; it only deferred.

Bren folded the paper carefully, worked the knife out of the door, and went inside to count money that wasn't there.

* * *

His counting-house was a good room. That was the cruelty of it. High windows, a solid desk his father had bought secondhand from a dissolving monastery, shelves of ledgers going back thirty years in his own careful hand. Everything orderly. Everything accounted for. And underneath the orderliness, like rot under sound plaster, the simple fact that Bren Oskarsson owed more than everything he owned to men who expressed their patience in knives.

It hadn't started with greed. He needed that said, even if only to himself, in his own head, where no one could contradict him. It had started with a bad winter and a ship called the *Merrow's Daughter* that went down off the Teeth with his cargo and someone else's investment both aboard. It had continued with a loan he'd taken to cover the loss, because Bren Oskarsson paid his debts, always had, and then another loan to service the first, and then — well. There was a point on every road where a man looked back and couldn't see the beginning anymore. Bren had passed that point sometime around midsummer and had decided, as a matter of policy, not to look back again.

The trouble with Ada Eastwick was that she was the one honest thing left in his life, and honesty, these days, was the most expensive commodity on the books.

She'd been coming to him for eleven years. Not often — four, five times a year, when her caravans came down out of Thornfell with goods that had never seen a customs seal. She paid fair. She paid quiet. She never asked him to move anything through the harbor, never asked him to fix a tally or lose a name in the rolls, and in exchange he never asked her what she carried or how she got it over passes that killed imperial couriers twice a season. That was the arrangement. That was the friendship, if you wanted to call it that, and Bren did call it that, privately, in the part of himself that still kept honest books.

And she'd come to him three weeks ago with a boy.

He turned it over now, sitting in his empty counting-house with the debt notice flattened on the desk before him like a dead thing. Ada, who took no passengers. Ada, whose rules were older than their acquaintance — *no passengers, no questions, no names*, carved into her manner so deep you could read them in the dark. She'd sat across from him in this very room, in that chair by the window, and asked him for safe-passage papers out of the free cities, papers with seals he'd have to buy from men he didn't trust, and when he'd demanded to know what she was carrying she'd looked at him with those grey eyes gone flat as slate and said, *You don't want to know, Bren. I mean that as a kindness.*

A kindness. He'd laughed at that, later, alone. There was no kindness left in the world that weighed against four hundred crowns.

Except — and here was the thing he kept circling, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth — except that he didn't actually know what he'd be selling. That was the comfort he built for himself, brick by brick, night after night. He wasn't selling the boy. He didn't know about any boy, not really; he'd seen a shape under an oilcloth in the back of Ada's wagon, heard nothing, been told nothing. What he'd be selling was a route. Lines on a map. Which passes were watched, which shrines had warden-stations still manned, where the old smuggler roads crossed the high country and came down to the lowlands east of the tolls. Information. Cartography. And cartography wasn't betrayal — it was trade. Men sold maps all the time. Half the Guild's income was maps, in one form or another.

The boy, if there was a boy, would be long gone by the time anyone used the map. Ada moved fast. Ada survived things that should have killed her; the mountains themselves seemed to look out for her, or she for herself, which amounted to the same thing. Whatever she was carrying, she'd carry it clear, and the inquisition would get a handful of cold trails and empty stations, and Bren would get four hundred crowns and his fingers and his life, and nobody — nobody — would ever need to know.

That was the story. He told it to himself the way other men prayed: nightly, kneeling, and without much faith.

* * *

The man from the Harrow Guild came to him on a Thursday, which Bren would remember afterward, because Thursday was the day he settled accounts with the fishmongers and the coincidence felt like an omen with a sense of humor.

He didn't look like a knife-man. That was the new style, Bren supposed. He was young, soft-spoken, dressed in good grey wool with a lawyer's ink stains on his right cuff, and he ordered tea in Bren's own kitchen as though he'd been invited. His name was Master Ellery, and he had a clerk's face and a moneylender's eyes, and he set a leather folio on the table between them and did not open it.

"Master Oskarsson. Thank you for seeing me."

"You nailed a knife to my door."

"A regrettable flourish by one of our field agents." Ellery said it the way a priest regrets a bell rung out of turn. "The Guild prefers subtlety. As, I understand, do you."

Bren poured the tea himself because his housekeeper had the afternoon off and he wanted something to do with his hands. "What do you want?"

"Information." Ellery accepted the cup, blew on it, drank. "Nothing more. The Guild has recently entered into a contract with certain parties from the capital. Parties with an interest in the Thornfell passes. They require a guide's knowledge of those passes, and they are prepared to pay extremely well for it."

"I'm a harbor factor. I don't know the passes."

"No," Ellery agreed pleasantly. "You know a woman who does."

The tea went bitter in Bren's mouth. He set the cup down with exaggerated care, because his hands wanted to shake and he would not permit it. "I don't sell my friends."

"Of course not." Ellery opened the folio at last and turned it around. Inside was a single sheet, and on the sheet, in red ink, the figure was not four hundred crowns. It was not even close to four hundred crowns. "This is what the parties from the capital are offering. The Guild's fee comes out of it, naturally, and your debt retires entirely, and there is enough remaining that a man could leave Saltmarsh altogether. Start clean somewhere the weather is kinder." He slid the sheet across the table. "We're not asking you to betray her, Master Oskarsson. We're asking you to remember things you already know. There's a difference. Most of the law agrees there's a difference."

"And if I refuse?"

Ellery's expression didn't change at all, which was itself an answer, and a worse one than any threat. "Then we'll continue our conversation in the spring," he said, "with whoever inherits your ledgers."

* * *

For six days Bren did nothing, and the doing-nothing was the worst work he'd ever done.

He went to the harbor. He checked his tallies. He ate dinner with his sister's family across town and let his nephew climb on his knee and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling, *this is what I'm buying.* Not the money. The small warm weight of a child who trusted him, the ordinary evenings, the whole unremarkable architecture of a life that debt was about to demolish. Every man who ever sold something he shouldn't have had a room like his sister's kitchen in his mind, Bren suspected. A room he told himself he was protecting.

He walked up past the salt-works to the headland where you could see the mountains on a clear day, and Thornfell stood against the sky like the jawbone of the world, blue-grey and indifferent, and he tried to imagine Ada up there somewhere, moving along roads only she remembered, and he tried to feel what she'd feel when the soldiers came. He couldn't do it. That was the terrible discovery of the headland: he couldn't make himself feel it. He could picture the soldiers easily enough — grey cloaks, the inquisition's white hand sewn at the shoulder — but Ada in front of them stayed a painting, flat, unreal, because eleven years of friendship had never once required him to imagine her afraid. She was the person fear happened *around*. She was the fixed point. And a man can sell almost anything if the person he's selling stays a painting.

On the sixth night he dreamed of his father. In the dream his father sat at the big desk in the counting-house, doing sums, and did not look up, and Bren stood in the doorway trying to explain about the debt and the knife and the red ink, and his father just said, in the voice he'd used for arithmetic lessons, *show your work, boy,* and woke him sweating at the hour when the gulls start.

Show your work. Fine. Here was the work: a route drawn from memory onto good paper, the high passes marked, the warden-stations marked, the crossings and the water-sources and the places where the old road dipped below the tree line and a mounted patrol could move fast. Eleven years of Ada's confidences, distilled into ink. He did it in one sitting, after midnight, and it took less time than a letter to a supplier, because the truth was he had always known it, all of it, held it in his head the way he held shipping lanes and interest tables, and the knowing had cost him nothing until now.

He told himself, sealing it into the folio, that the boy — if there was a boy — would be gone. Long gone. Over the border, into the free cities' western reaches, beyond anyone's reach. Ada was the best guide alive on either side of the mountains. She'd be gone too. All the inquisition would find was stone and silence, and maybe, if they were fools, they'd waste a season learning it, and what harm in that? What harm to anyone?

The lie fit comfortably. That was how he knew it was a lie. Truth never fit that well.

* * *

Master Ellery collected the folio on a morning of thin rain, counted out the payment in Guild scrip convertible at three banks, and shook Bren's hand with a clerk's dry warmth.

"The parties from the capital thank you," he said. "They asked me to convey that your discretion will be remembered."

"Tell them—" Bren stopped. His mouth had shaped words he hadn't authorized. *Tell them she's carrying a passenger, tell them about the boy, tell them—* No. He hadn't said it. He wouldn't say it. Selling the route was trade; selling the boy would be something else, something with a name he refused to learn. "Tell them nothing," he finished. "We're done."

"We are indeed." Ellery paused at the door, rain silvering behind him, and glanced back with something that was almost sympathy. "For what it's worth, Master Oskarsson, the parties from the capital are not interested in your friend. They're interested in what she's carrying. And between us — they believe she's carrying a person. A child." He let that sit in the room like a draft. "So you may wish to consider whether the map was the whole price. Or merely the deposit."

Then he was gone, and Bren stood in his counting-house among his beautiful orderly ledgers, and the silence was the loudest thing he'd ever heard.

He told himself it was a bluff. Clerks bluffed; it was half their trade. He told himself Ada was already west, already clear, already laughing at the empire from some high cold pass where knives and debts and inquisitors couldn't follow. He told himself the story one more time, kneeling at it, faithful as ever.

But that evening, walking home through the rain with his collar up, he passed the shrine at the corner of Tanner's Row — one of the little wayside ones, a squat stone with the worn face of the oath-god, silent these twenty years like all the rest — and he saw fresh flowers in the offering-bowl. White ones, mountain flowers, the kind that didn't grow anywhere near Saltmarsh. Someone had carried them a long way.

And Bren Oskarsson, who had spent his whole life reading the fine print, felt the fine print of his soul rearrange itself, and understood with perfect clerical clarity that the account was not yet closed — that somewhere east, in the high cold country he had just sold, a debt of a different kind had come due, and the collection was already riding down out of the passes toward a ruined shrine where a woman he'd betrayed might be praying.

He stood in the rain a long time. Then he went home, and took out his pen, and began — God help him, far too late — to draw a different map.
