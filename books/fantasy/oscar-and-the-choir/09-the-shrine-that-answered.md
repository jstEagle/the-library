## 9. The Shrine That Answered

The shrine found them before they found it.

Ada had known it was there the way she knew every fold of Thornfell—by the way the mountain's shape changed around it, by the absence of trees where a road had once been cut and then abandoned, by the particular quality of silence that gathered in places where people had prayed for centuries and then stopped all at once. The old pilgrim road to Vael Serran ran along this ridge, or had run, before the usurper closed the passes and let the snow take back what the faithful had built.

She had not meant to stop here. Stopping was against her rules, too—another entry on the long list she kept behind her teeth. *No passengers. No questions. No names. No fires after dusk. No shrines.* But Oscar's fever had come back with the altitude, climbing with them step by step until the boy could barely put one foot in front of the other, and Bren's maps showed no shelter for six more miles, and the light was going the color of an old bruise.

So: the shrine.

It crouched in the elbow of the mountain like something that had crawled there to die. Once it must have been beautiful—the way these places always were, built by masons who believed their work was itself a kind of prayer. A low dome of grey stone, its roof fallen in on the eastern side so that sky poured through the wound. Snow had drifted through the gap and lay across the floor in pale sheets. The walls were blackened in places. Someone had burned things here—not just wood. Ada had seen enough burned things in her life to know the difference between fuel and testimony.

The statue at the center had been toppled and broken, but not destroyed. That was typical of the usurper's men, in those first years. They wanted the god humiliated, not erased. Erasure would have been mercy. They left the faceless torso lying on its side in the rubble, hands still raised in blessing toward a ceiling that no longer existed, and they let the faithful walk past it every day and remember what had been done.

Ada stepped over the threshold and felt the familiar nothing.

Twenty years ago, walking into a shrine had been like walking into warm water. You felt the Oathkeeper the way you felt weather—a pressure, a presence, something vast moving just beyond the skin of the world. Her grandmother had said you could taste it, faintly, like iron at the back of the tongue. Then the Choir died, and the taste went out of the world, and every shrine from Thornfell to the sea became what this one was: a roofless room full of cold stone and other people's grief.

"Sit," she told Oscar, and he sat, folding down against the wall beneath the one intact arch, small and grey-faced and shivering in a way that had stopped being about the cold. She got the firewood out of her pack because rules about fires after dusk were rules for smugglers on open ground, and this was neither, and the boy needed warmth more than she needed discipline. She built the fire small and shielded, the way her grandmother had taught her to build fires in places where smoke could betray you, and she set water on to heat, and she did all of it with the brisk economy of a woman who refused to notice where she was standing.

The oath-stones watched her do it.

There were nine of them, set into the wall above the ruined altar, each the size of a fist, each carved with a word in the old tongue. They had been white once. Now they were the color of old teeth. Every shrine had them; every warden's station had them; every doorpost in every village that had ever sworn to the Oathkeeper bore at least one, worn smooth by the touch of hands making promises. They were supposed to hold the record. They were supposed to *listen*. When a vow was spoken truly, they warmed. When an oath was broken, they went cold as river ice, and everyone who touched them afterward knew.

Ada's own station had three of them, bolted above her door by some pious warden dead before she was born. She used them to hang her cloaks.

She did not look at these ones. She looked at the fire instead, and at the boy, whose eyes had closed, whose breathing had gone shallow and fast, and she told herself the tightness in her chest was only the altitude.

* * *

The fever broke near midnight, and that was when the talking started.

She had heard it before—at the station, in the nights after she'd pulled him half-frozen off her doorstep, when his small body burned and his mouth moved and sounds came out of it that were not any language spoken west of the mountains. She had told herself then that it was delirium, that fevered children babbled, that the fact that the babbling made the hair on her arms stand up meant nothing except that she was an old woman with old wounds.

But here, in the shrine, with the firelight crawling up the broken walls, she could not pretend the sound was nothing. It had structure. It had rhythm. It rose and fell the way speech rises and falls, and underneath the rhythm there was something else—something layered, as if the boy's single throat held more than one voice and they were speaking *together*, in unison, the way a congregation speaks a liturgy.

Her grandmother's voice came back to her across twenty years and a shallow grave: *That is the Choir-tongue, girl. The god's own speech. Learn it and they will burn you beside me.*

Ada had been seven years old. She had learned exactly two words before the soldiers came, and she had spent the rest of her life pretending she'd forgotten both.

Now she heard one of them in the boy's sleep-talk, clear as a bell struck in an empty room. *Vessia.* It meant, if her grandmother's lessons could be trusted, *we hear.*

The fire popped. Oscar turned his head against the wall, and his eyes opened—not all the way, not awake, but open, fixed on the oath-stones above the altar with the blank certainty of the dreaming.

And the stones began to hum.

It was very soft. It might have been wind in the broken roof, except there was no wind, and it might have been the fire settling, except the sound did not move when the fire moved. It was a single sustained tone, low, patient, coming from nine dead stones that had been silent since before Ada could walk. As she watched, the nearest of them changed color—not glowing, not yet, but warming, the grey leaching out of it the way frost leaves a window when a hand is pressed to the other side.

Oscar smiled in his sleep. He said, in the flat accent of a child who does not know the words are holy: *"Vessia. Keth mara."*

*We hear. Do not be afraid.*

The stones glowed. All nine of them, softly, steadily, gold-white like coals covered in ash, and the light lay across the boy's sleeping face, and Ada Eastwick—who had not knelt in twenty years, who had sworn on her grandmother's grave never to kneel again—found herself on her feet with her knife drawn, pointing a blade at a wall.

* * *

She did not sleep again. She sat with her back against the far wall, opposite the boy, the knife across her knees, and she watched the stones fade slowly back to grey as the night deepened, and she listened to Oscar breathe, and she conducted an argument with a god who was not present and had not been present for two decades and had apparently just decided to change that.

Because that was the thing she could not get past. Not the miracle of it—she was a mountain woman; she'd seen avalanches turn and rivers run backward in flood; the world was full of things that should not happen. What she could not get past was the *timing*.

Where had the god been?

She asked it plainly, in the privacy of her skull, the way she had asked it every night for twenty years, kneeling nowhere, addressing no one: Where were you when they burned the Choir? Where were you when they came for my grandmother, when they made the village watch, when she sang while the rope went tight because singing was the only prayer she had left? Where were you when I paid tithe at your shrines year after year, coin and blood and breath, into a silence so complete I finally understood it was an answer? Where were you when I buried my faith in the same hole where I buried her, and built a life on top of it, and called the life good?

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. That was the whole architecture of her survival: a god who did not answer was a god she could stop waiting for.

Except now the stones had glowed. Except now the god had a child's mouth and was speaking in its sleep, and the message—the entire content of the first divine communication in twenty years—was *do not be afraid*, addressed, as far as Ada could tell, to the boy himself. Comfort. For the vessel. While the woman who had kept the faith alive alone in the high passes, who had paid her tithe into emptiness, who had carried the old tongue in her mouth like a swallowed coal for two decades without ever once letting it out—

While she got nothing.

Dawn came up grey through the broken roof. Oscar woke slowly, blinking, the fever gone out of him, looking around at the shrine with the puzzled calm of a child waking somewhere new.

"You talked in your sleep," Ada said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"I know." He said it simply, without embarrassment. "They talk through me sometimes. When I'm asleep they don't have to ask."

"Who asks?"

"The voices." He looked at her, and there was nothing eerie in his face at all—just a thin, tired boy with chapped lips and his mother's coat buttoned wrong. "You heard them. Didn't you? The stones woke up. They always wake up when I'm close. I thought you knew that's why I came here."

Ada's mouth was dry. "Why you came here."

"To the mountains. To the wardens." He frowned a little, working at the memory. "My mother said, go east, go high, find the ones who still keep the oaths. She said the stones would know me even if the people forgot how to listen." He looked past her, at the nine grey circles in the wall, and his voice dropped. "Did I do something bad? They're grey again."

"No," Ada said. "No, they're fine. Eat your bread."

He ate. She watched him eat, and the argument in her skull went quiet, and in the quiet she admitted the thing she had been refusing to admit since the stones first hummed:

She was not angry that the god had come back.

She was angry that the god had come back *like this*—silent through the slaughter, silent through the burning years, silent through every tithe and vigil and whispered heresy, and now suddenly present, suddenly urgent, wrapped around a child like a second skin. Gods, in her experience, were like the rich: they returned only when they wanted something, and they always sent someone else to pay for it.

* * *

They stayed through the day. There was no choice in it; the pass above the shrine was drifted shoulder-deep, and the storm that had chased them up the valley arrived in the afternoon and sealed them in with a curtain of white. Ada told herself the delay annoyed her. She caught herself, twice, standing at the broken altar with her hand hovering near the stones, and twice she took her hand away.

Near dusk, with the storm roaring over the fallen dome and the fire fed high, Oscar sat cross-legged by the flames and said, without preamble: "They want to tell you something."

Ada went still. "The voices."

"They've been asking all day. Since we came in." He turned to look at her, and in the firelight his eyes had that layered quality again, depth behind depth, a boy's eyes with something older looking out through them—not possessing him, nothing so crude, just *present*, the way a singer is present in a song. "I told them you'd be angry. They said that was all right. They said—" he paused, listening to something she could not hear, his head tilting slightly, "—they said anger is also a kind of listening."

"Tell them," Ada said, and her voice was iron, "that I have been listening for twenty years, and the only thing I ever heard back was my own voice."

Oscar flinched. Then he didn't. Something steadied in him, and when he spoke again the words came slower, spaced apart, as though he were carrying them one at a time across a long bridge—and though the voice was still a child's voice, it was no longer only a child's voice. It had harmonics in it. It had *congregation*.

*"You think we were silent,"* the boy said, and the firelight bent, and every hair on Ada's arms stood upright, because the oath-stones in the wall had begun, very softly, to glow. *"We were hiding. There is a difference, warden, and you of all people know it—you have hidden too. We hid the last of ourselves inside a child and we went dark, all of us, everywhere, so that the hunters would believe the hunt was finished. Twenty years we held our breath. You called our holding of breath an abandonment."*

The stones brightened. The storm outside seemed to lean closer.

*"We are sorry for the cost of our silence. We will not insult you by calling it small. But hear us now, daughter of the keeping: the hunters have learned the breath was not held forever. They are climbing. And we do not ask you for comfort, or forgiveness, or worship. We ask you for the only thing you have ever been willing to give."*

Ada stood in the glow of nine stones that had waited twenty years for her, and she heard her own heartbeat, and she said, in the old tongue, the two words her grandmother had taught her before the soldiers came—the first time she had spoken them aloud since she was seven years old:

"*Kessa vael.*" Speak, then.

The stones flared white. Oscar's eyes were fully open now and fully his own, frightened and resolute, and the voices spoke through him one final time, gently, terribly, like a hand laid on her shoulder:

*"Carry him east. Carry him to the capital. Carry him before the throne of the man who unmade us—and when the moment comes, do not protect him from what he is. Let him sing."*

The light went out. The storm filled the silence. Somewhere below them, far down the valley, faint on the edge of hearing, a hunting horn sounded twice—the signal of the usurper's road-wardens, the signal that meant a search was moving through the high passes.

Oscar looked at her with his own young eyes and whispered: "Was that... was that them? Did I say that?"

Ada picked up her pack. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she would have expected them to shake.

"Yes," she said. "And now we go east."
