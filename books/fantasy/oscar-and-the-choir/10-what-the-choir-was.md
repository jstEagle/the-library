## 10. What the Choir Was

The voices came to Oscar most often in the hour before dawn, when the shrine was coldest and the dark outside the broken windows had that particular stillness, like a held breath. He had learned not to fight them. Fighting made the singing sharp, and sharp singing woke Ada, and Ada needed her sleep—she had been walking for days, scouting the passes, muttering about roads and soldiers and the price of flour in towns they would never reach.

So he lay on his bedroll beneath the leaning altar and let them come.

They always began the same way: a warmth behind his breastbone, like swallowing a coal wrapped in wool. Then the hum. Not a sound his ears could catch—it lived somewhere deeper, in the hollows of him—and it spread outward until his ribs felt like the strings of one of Bren's lutes, trembling at a pitch just below hearing. And then, gently, the way a hand settles on a shoulder, the visions began.

He saw a garden first. He always saw the garden.

It was not a garden of flowers. It was a garden of stone—a high courtyard ringed by white walls, with a single tree at its center whose leaves chimed when the wind moved through them. People stood among the branches. That was the part that never stopped astonishing him: they *stood* in the tree as if it were a hillside, barefoot on the boughs, robes the color of unbleached wool, their faces turned upward toward a sky that seemed very slightly brighter than any sky Oscar had ever seen under. And they were singing.

Not with their mouths only. With everything. Their throats and their hands and the soles of their feet against the bark, and the tree itself sang back through its roots into the mountain below, and the mountain carried it down into the bones of the world, and the world answered.

*This is what we were,* said the voices, in the tone they used for true things—the tone that felt less like words than like warm water poured over a cold head. *We were the many mouths.*

*I know,* Oscar thought back at them, because he did know this part now; they had shown him before. But they showed him anyway. They always showed him everything again from the beginning, patient as rain, because—this was another thing he understood—they were afraid he would forget. Or perhaps they were afraid *they* would forget, if they ever stopped telling it.

*Before there were kings who claimed the god's favor,* the voices said, *there was no need for claim. The god spoke, and the god's speaking had a thousand throats. A weaver in her shop might open her mouth and speak comfort in the god's voice to a grieving customer. A shepherd boy on a hillside might suddenly pronounce judgment on a thieving lord, and the lord would kneel in the mud and weep, because there was no mistaking it—not for anyone. The word belonged to everyone. It fell like weather.*

In the vision, the courtyard filled and emptied, filled and emptied. Years moved across it like shadows of clouds. Pilgrims came up the mountain road, thousands of them, and knelt among the roots of the chime-tree, and whatever they carried—grief, guilt, sickness, doubt—they left some portion of it there, and the singers in the branches took it up and wove it into the song until even sorrow sounded like something you could live inside.

Oscar watched a woman give birth in the courtyard's corner while the whole tree sang the child into the world. He watched an old man climb the road on his knees and die smiling at the top, and the song bent around his dying the way a river bends around a stone, without stopping, without being stopped.

*Then came the man who wanted the word for himself alone.*

The sky over the courtyard went the color of iron filings. The vision shifted, and Oscar's stomach clenched, because he knew what came next, and knowing had never once made it easier.

He saw armies. He had seen armies before—from Ada's stories, from the burned village that lived at the bottom of his memory like a stone in a well—but these were different. These marched under a banner he had never seen in waking life: a black field bearing a single silver circle, closed upon itself, a ring with nothing inside it. An empty circle. He hadn't understood it the first time. Now, watching it flap above ten thousand spears, he understood it perfectly. A circle with nothing in the middle. A mouth with no voice. A crown.

*He was not born wicked,* the voices said, and this was new—this they had never told him before, and it frightened him more than all the fire, because it was harder. *He was born small, in a country where the god's word passed him by. Every time a singer opened a mouth near him, the voice spoke to someone else—to his brother, to his neighbor, to the beggar he stepped over in the street. Never to him. And a man who has never been spoken to will come to believe there is no word at all, or worse: that the word is a thing that can be owned, hoarded, kept. He decided the god should have one mouth only, and that the mouth should be his throne, and that every other throat in the world should be cut so his silence would be called holiness.*

The armies reached the mountain.

Oscar tried to look away. There was nowhere to look away *to*—the vision was inside him, behind his eyes, in the coal-warmth of his chest—but he squeezed shut whatever it was that saw, the way he squeezed his eyes shut during Ada's stories when she described the executions at the heresy trials, and it helped almost not at all.

He heard the tree stop singing.

That was the worst of it. Not the soldiers climbing the walls, not the fire crawling up the white courtyards, not the singers falling from the branches like ripe fruit shaken loose. It was the moment the song itself faltered—one voice gone, then five, then fifty, then the terrible arithmetic of it, the song thinning note by note like a rope unraveling strand by strand, until what remained was not a chorus but a handful of people screaming in harmony, and then fewer, and then—

*One.*

The vision narrowed to a single figure running down the mountain's far side in the dark, away from the burning, with a bundle pressed to her chest. The bundle was wrapped in unbleached wool. The bundle was crying—not the angry cry of a hungry infant but a low, rising, impossible sound, a sound with too many voices folded inside it, a newborn's throat carrying harmonies no newborn should know.

*You,* said the voices, very quietly. *You were the bundle.*

Oscar lay still beneath the altar. His own breath sounded enormous in the ruined shrine. Somewhere above, wind worried the broken window frames, and he thought: *that is me. That little noise in the wool. I was there. I was the last note of the song, and someone ran down a mountain with me in her arms while everything else was put out like lamps.*

*Her name was Sera,* the voices said. *She held you for eleven days. She walked at night and hid by day. She gave you to a farmwife whose own child had died of fever, and she told the woman never to let you sing aloud, never to let you hum, never to let you learn that the sounds inside you were anything but dreams. Then she went back toward the mountain, because the soldiers were following the last echo of the song, and she meant to lead them away from you.*

*Did they find her?* Oscar asked. He didn't want to ask. He asked anyway. This was another thing he had learned about true things: you had to look at all of them, or they festered.

The voices were silent for a long moment. In that silence he felt them gather around the answer the way people gather around a grave.

*Yes.*

He didn't see it. They spared him that, or couldn't show it, or chose mercy for once—he didn't know which, and didn't ask. He only knew that the woman who had run down the mountain with him in her arms had died so that the soldiers would chase a fading song instead of a living child, and that the farmwife who raised him had died in the same fire, and that the reason—the whole reason, the entire architecture of every death that had ever touched him—was a man who had never once heard the god say his name.

Oscar began to cry. He did it silently, the way Ada had taught him without ever teaching him—breath shallow, face still, tears running sideways into his hair—because crying loudly was a thing children did in houses with doors that locked, and he had not lived in such a house since the fire.

*Why do you stay in me?* he asked them, when he could. *Why didn't you go into someone strong? Someone who could fight?*

*Because we are not fighters,* the voices said. *We are mouths. We were never anything but mouths. And a mouth does not choose where it is fed.* A pause, and then, gentler: *And because you were the last place the song was still alive. When Sera ran, the song ran with her. When she gave you away, the song slept in you. We did not choose you, little one. We survived into you. There is a difference, and we are sorry for it every day.*

*Sorry for what?*

*For living in a child. For making your dreams heavy. For the fact that every person who has ever loved you has burned for it.* The warmth behind his breastbone flickered, and for one naked moment Oscar felt what the voices actually were underneath all their calm—not a chorus but a crowd of terrified survivors huddled in the last lit room of a burning house, holding the door shut with their bodies. *We are sorry most of all that we cannot promise you it ends differently.*

Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges. Dawn soon. Ada would wake soon, and check the pass-road, and count their food, and do all the quiet competent things she did instead of saying kind things, which was how she said them.

But the visions weren't finished. He felt it—the way you feel the last pages of a story still ahead of you. The voices drew him back once more, and this time they showed him not the past but a shape of the future, vague as a figure seen through fogged glass: a great city, a square paved in pale stone, ranks of soldiers in black and silver, and himself—older? taller? it hardly looked like him at all—standing small and alone in the middle of all that steel, opening his mouth.

*There is a door ahead of you,* the voices said. *It is a terrible door. On the other side of it, either the song dies forever, or the man with the empty circle loses the only thing that holds his armies together—which is their belief that the god speaks through him. We cannot make you walk through it. No mouth can be forced to sing. That is the whole of what we are, Oscar. We are the opposite of forcing.*

*What happens if I refuse?* he whispered inside himself.

*Then we sleep in you until you die of old age in some quiet valley, and the world goes on believing the lie, and the lie grows children of its own.* Another pause. *It would not be a wicked choice. It might even be a wise one. We want you to know that, before anyone else tells you otherwise. Before she tells you otherwise.*

*She?*

The warmth receded, drawing back like a tide, leaving him alone on the cold sand of his own skull. But at the very edge of it, as it went, he caught one last thread of the chorus, aimed not at him but past him—at the grey light in the doorway, where a shadow had appeared, boots crunching softly on fallen plaster.

Ada stood there with her pack half-shouldered and her hand already resting on the knife at her hip, watching him with an expression he had never seen on her before. Not suspicion. Something older and wearier than suspicion. She had heard him, he realized. Not the humming—she'd put a stop to the humming weeks ago—but the *other* thing, the thing he hadn't known he was doing: lying in the dark before dawn, breathing in perfect unison with a hundred invisible throats, his chest keeping time to a song that had been dead for twenty years.

"Get your coat," Ada said quietly. "We're leaving today."

"Where?" Oscar asked, though his heart had already begun to sink, because he knew—had known since the visions showed him the pale square and the wall of steel—that there was only one direction left, and it was east, toward the city with the empty circle flying over it.

Ada looked out the broken window at the mountains, at the passes, at the whole western world where a boy might theoretically grow up hidden and safe, and then she looked back at him, and her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was about to break one of her own rules.

"The capital," she said.
