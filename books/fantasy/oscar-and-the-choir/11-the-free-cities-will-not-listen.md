## 11. The Free Cities Will Not Listen

The free cities announced themselves to travelers the way a rich man announces himself at a funeral: loudly, and with the expectation of gratitude. Ada had seen the approach from three miles out—white walls rising from the river plain like teeth, banners snapping in the flatland wind, the great toll road striped with fresh gravel. Thornfell's passes ended here. Everything she knew how to survive ended here.

Oscar walked beside her with his hood up and his hands buried in his sleeves, the way she'd taught him. Nine days out of the mountains, and he had learned the shape of her rules without being told twice: walk small, speak nothing, be furniture. He was good at it. That was the part that broke something quiet in her every time she noticed—a child so practiced at being unseen that invisibility sat on him like a coat cut to his shoulders.

*You were made for hiding,* she thought, and hated the thought, because it wasn't a metaphor. Someone had sewn him into secrecy before he could walk. The Choir's last mouth, wrapped in a smuggler's hood, walking a toll road built on the usurper's coin.

"Ada." His voice came muffled through the wool. "There are so many people."

"There are always people where there's money." She adjusted the strap of her pack, checking by feel that the oath-stone rode against her ribs where it belonged. It had been warm all morning. It was always warm now, since the shrine, since the god had put its task into her hands like a debt collector presenting terms. "Keep your eyes down. We're nobody. We're two nobodies going to waste the time of important men."

"Will they listen?"

She looked at him then—at the chapped lips, the frost-scars on his knuckles, the eyes that held more patience than any nine-year-old should have been forced to grow. *Will they listen.* As if listening were a thing councils did.

"They'll hear us," she said. "That's different."

* * *

Corvale was the oldest of the free cities, and it wore its age the way Veyne wore his vestments: as armor, as argument, as proof of rightness. Ada moved them through its streets along routes she'd memorized years ago running contraband—back lanes behind the tanneries, the covered stair that bypassed the customs gate, the alley where the watch never bothered because the watch was paid not to. Old habits. Her whole life was old habits, and every one of them was burning behind her in the mountains, ash on the wind, Bren's maps folded into her pack like a dead man's letters.

The council hall squatted at the city's heart behind a colonnade of petitioners' statues, each one a merchant or magistrate with a nameplate greened by weather. Ada read none of them. She climbed the steps with Oscar's hand locked in hers and presented herself at the warden's desk with the only currency that opened this door: a problem too large to ignore and too expensive to verify.

"Name," said the clerk, without looking up.

"Eastwick. Ada Eastwick. Mountain guide, licensed under the Corvale charter, seal number—" she recited it, the number she'd bought fifteen years ago and kept clean ever since, no smuggling charges that stuck, no names given, no questions answered. A spotless record built entirely on being unremarkable.

The clerk looked up then, and Ada watched him do the arithmetic. A woman in road-worn leathers with a mountain accent and dust in the seams of her. A boy whose hood she did not remove. Nothing about them matched the marble of this hall.

"Purpose of petition?"

"I need to address the council. Today."

A dry sound, almost a laugh. "The council hears petitions on Thirdday. Appointments are made through—"

"The usurper's army crossed the Serevan ford eleven days ago with thirty thousand men and siege trains. They're moving west along the pilgrim road. By month's end they'll be at your eastern trade gates, and everything you sell them will stop, and everything they take will be taken." She let that sit. Let the clerk's quill go still. "I'm the person who knows that, and I'm standing here instead of sending a letter, because a letter can be ignored. I can't be."

It was a gamble dressed as confidence. Half of what she'd said was inference stitched from rumor and burned villages; the other half was true, and the truth was the part that would damn her if anyone checked. But fear travels faster than verification, and the clerk's face had gone carefully blank in the way of a man deciding whether to pass a problem upward or become responsible for it.

"Wait there," he said, and disappeared through the door marked with the council's scales-and-tower sigil.

Oscar's fingers tightened around hers. "They believed you?"

"No." Ada looked down at him. "But they believe their masters will want to know I exist. That's how institutions work. Nobody believes anything; everybody forwards it upstairs."

* * *

They waited four hours on a bench beneath a window that framed the colonnade like a painting of justice—distant, symmetrical, unreachable. Ada used the time the way she used all waiting: cataloguing exits, counting guards, noting which clerks left for supper and which doors stopped being watched. Oscar slept against her shoulder for an hour, and when he woke he didn't ask where they were or what would happen. He just pressed closer and watched the light crawl across the floor.

At dusk a herald in council colors fetched them—not to the full chamber, but to a side room where three magistrates sat behind a table long enough to require its own geography. An older man with a magistrate's chain and a merchant's eyes. A woman in scholar's grey with ink stains to the elbow. And a young one, soft-handed, who kept glancing at a ledger as though the ledger might object to something.

"Madam Eastwick." The chained one, Master Halvern by the clerk's introduction. "Your reputation precedes you. The mountain guide. Reliable, discreet, and notably—unaffiliated."

"Discreet is why I'm useful," Ada said. "And why I'm here. What I carry is worth discretion."

"And what do you carry?" This from the scholar, leaning forward. There was hunger in her face—the genuine kind, curiosity that hadn't learned manners yet. "Your message mentioned armies. But the warden's note says you requested audience regarding a *religious matter*. Which is it? Logistics or theology?"

"It's both." Ada stepped aside, and her heart did something complicated, because the next part was breaking her own rule one last time—the rule she'd built her life on, the rule that had kept her alive through twenty years of silence. "Show them," she said quietly, to Oscar.

He pushed back his hood.

To his credit—and Ada felt the strange parental pride of it even now—he didn't flinch under three pairs of eyes. He stood straight and let them look. The scholar drew breath. The young magistrate frowned. Master Halvern's expression didn't change at all, which told Ada he'd already decided, and the rest was theater.

"You see a boy," Ada said. "I'll tell you what else he is. When the usurper burned the Choir out of existence twenty years ago, they missed one. This boy carries the Choir's voice. The old tongue lives in him. The oath-stones answer him. At Thornhollow Bridge, in front of the usurper's own inspectors, a stone sewn into his coat sang loud enough to be heard across a courtyard. In the low town of this very city, three nights ago, every bell in the district rang unstruck, and half the street fell to their knees. Your own watch has reports. Check them."

"We have heard rumors of bell-ringing," the scholar said slowly. "Crowds. Weeping. The temple authorities attributed it to—"

"To nothing. To mass hysteria, because the alternative is admitting the history everyone is comfortable with is a lie." Ada took a step forward, and let twenty years of mountain cold into her voice. "Here is what I know. The usurper did not destroy the Choir because they were heretics. He destroyed them because they were the god's voice, plural, distributed, impossible to control—and he wanted a monopoly on divine word. He's spent twenty years selling his sanction as singular and official. And now his armies are coming west, and the one thing that can break that sanction isn't soldiers. It's a nine-year-old singing the truth about every oath the man swore and broke."

Silence. The good kind, she thought, for exactly three seconds.

Then Master Halvern folded his hands. "Madam Eastwick. Do you know what we are?"

"A council."

"We are merchants," he said, "who discovered that cooperation is cheaper than war. That is the entire miracle of the free cities. No gods in it. No destiny. Tariffs, treaties, and the mutual understanding that everyone prospers more by trading than by burning. You are asking us to stake that—thirty years of it—on a child's voice and a mercenary's testimony."

"I'm asking you to look at what's already marching toward you."

"The Serevan crossing is unconfirmed. Our agents report troop movements consistent with border patrol rotation. Even granting your account—even granting the bells—" he raised a hand against her interruption, "—what precisely do you propose? That we parade a boy through the streets declaring himself the voice of a god our charters deliberately omit? Do you understand what that does to our treaty with the temple states? To our grain contracts? You are proposing, madam, that we choose a side in a war of heaven, and the free cities exist specifically because we declined to make that choice."

"He's not a weapon," Ada said. "He's a child."

"Then all the more reason not to use him as one." For a moment—just a moment—the merchant's polish cracked and something tired looked out of Halvern's face. "I have read the histories, madam. The Choir wars killed more people than any plague in living memory. Whole valleys emptied over which hymn was correct. We built peace on the grave of that argument, and you are asking me to dig it up and hand it a sword."

"He doesn't want a war. He wants—"

"What does *he* want?" The scholar again, sharp, sudden. She had risen halfway from her chair, staring past Ada at the boy. "Child. Is it true? Do you carry it? What does it feel like?"

Oscar looked at Ada first—asked permission with his eyes, still, after everything, unwilling to speak without it—and something in Ada's chest turned over and went quiet.

"I don't carry it like a thing," Oscar said. "It's more like... voices. They sing when they're happy. They got scared when the soldiers came to my village, and after that they don't sing much except at night." He paused, choosing words with a child's terrible precision. "They remember oaths. All the oaths anybody ever said out loud near them. That's mostly what they are. Remembering."

The scholar sat down slowly. The young magistrate had gone pale. And Ada thought, *there, now you've seen it, now the room has changed*—

"Remarkable," Halvern said, unmoved, and Ada understood with a lurch that to him it genuinely was merely remarkable. A curiosity. An item for a committee. "And verifiable by whom? Temple scholars would call it possession. Our own physicians would call it performance. Madam Eastwick, I believe you believe all this. I believe you've been through something terrible in those mountains—I've read the reports on the Thornfell fires; the inquisition has been active there for months. But belief is not evidence, and evidence is not policy."

"So that's it." Ada heard her own voice go flat, the way it went flat before violence, the way it hadn't gone in years. "Thirty thousand men on your road, and you'll wait for confirmation."

"We will send envoys to the Serevan. We will convene a proper inquiry into the bell incidents. If the boy consents, our scholars may study—" 

"He's not a specimen."

"—and in the meantime," Halvern continued, with the gentle inexorability of a closing ledger, "you will understand that we cannot act on the word of a woman with no fixed address and no allegiance, carrying a child of unknown origin, claiming miracles. The law requires more. The law *is* more, madam. It is slow and blind and it does not leap, and that slowness and blindness is the only reason any of us sleep at night."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. Then at the scholar, who wouldn't meet her eyes. Then at Oscar, who had pulled his hood back up himself, unprompted, folding himself back into invisibility with that awful practiced ease.

"The law," Ada said, "is going to arrive late. It always arrives late. It'll get here after the fires, and it'll write excellent reports about them, and it will conclude that everyone involved behaved reasonably right up until the moment they didn't." She took the council's token from the desk—copper, stamped with the scales—because refusing it would make things harder later, and Ada wasted nothing, not even gestures. "Thank you for your time, masters."

"Madam Eastwick." Halvern's voice stopped her at the door. "Where will you go?"

She almost laughed. Almost told him the truth—that there was nowhere, that every route she'd ever carved was smoke, that the only door left open was the one pointing east toward the thing she'd been running from. Instead she said: "Somewhere the law won't find me. You should try it sometime."

* * *

Outside, night had come down over Corvale, and the city was lighting itself lamp by lamp, beautiful and oblivious, a body that hadn't noticed the knife. Ada walked with Oscar through streets that smelled of bread and river water, past taverns spilling fiddle-music, past a temple of the sanctioned faith where a priest was locking up for the night and gave them the bored glance one gives beggars.

Oscar's hand found hers. Small, cold, certain.

"They didn't say no," he said finally. "They said *later*. Is later worse than no?"

"Later is what no says when it wants to avoid an argument." Ada guided him down a side street, toward the cheap lodging house above the chandler's, where they'd spend one more night in a city that had decided not to see them. "Councils are machines for turning urgency into paperwork. I've smuggled past checkpoints less blind than that room."

"Then who listens?" Oscar asked. "If the cities won't, and the temples hate us, and the inquisitors want to cut it out of me—who's left to listen?"

Ada stopped walking.

Because there was an answer, and she had been circling it for nine days like a bird over a fire, refusing to land. The free cities would not listen. The law would arrive late. Every institution between here and the coast was either bought, blind, or afraid. Which left the audience that needed no persuading, that was already assembled, that had been gathered by the usurper himself in his capital for the winter mustering—thirty thousand soldiers, camp followers, priests, and citizens, all of them waiting for a coronation-season spectacle, all of them raised on songs about the Choir's wickedness.

All of them listening.

*East,* said the thought, in a voice that sounded unpleasantly like the god's. *The way you never go.*

Oscar was watching her face. He was learning to read her, God help them both. "What?" he said. "What did you think of?"

Ada looked east, down the long avenue where the lamplight ran out and the dark began, the direction of the capital, of Veyne, of the iron throne and the armies kneeling before it.

"Nothing," she lied, and tightened her grip on his hand. "Come on. We need to be gone before the watch changes."

Behind them, in the district of Saint Aldhelm's, a church bell trembled in its yoke—just once, softly, unstruck—like something clearing its throat.
