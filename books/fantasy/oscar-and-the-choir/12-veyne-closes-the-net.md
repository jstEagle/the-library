## 12. Veyne Closes the Net

The lower city smelled of fish brine and tallow, and Inquisitor Veyne found the stink of it a kind of penance. He had been raised in the high quarters of Vhalcrest, where the air came down off the mountains scoured clean, where the cobbles were pale granite and swept by servants whose names he had never needed to learn. Here in Marrowgate—the free cities' oldest port, all leaning timber and salt-rotted pilings—everything clung. The damp got into his coat, into his ledger, into the spine of him. He did not mind. Dirt was the honest condition of the world, and his work was to find what gleamed beneath it.

He had been in Marrowgate nine days. Nine days of listening to informants who lied with the fluency of men reciting catechisms. Nine days of watching a city that called itself free practice its small freedoms: smuggling, usury, heresy sold openly in the night markets alongside charms against toothache and love potions cut with river water. The Council of Marrowgate had granted him three interviews and no cooperation, which was itself an answer. The free cities would not hand over a child they did not believe existed, hunted for a crime they did not believe could be committed.

Veyne believed. Belief was the one thing the usurper's church had never managed to take from him, though it had tried to reshape it often enough.

He sat now in the back room of a chandlery off Rope Walk, a room that smelled of rendered fat and hemp, with a table between himself and a woman named Sefa who brokered information the way other brokers dealt in grain futures. She was a compact woman with ink-stained fingers and the unbothered eyes of someone who had sold secrets to both sides of three wars.

"The warden," she said again, "is not for sale."

"Everyone is for sale," Veyne said mildly. "Most people simply haven't been shown their correct price."

"This one has. I've offered."

"And what did she say?"

Sefa smiled without warmth. "She said nothing. That's the trouble with her. Ada Eastwick doesn't talk, doesn't drink, doesn't gamble, doesn't keep a mistress or a boy or debts. Twenty years running the Thornfell passes and there's nothing to squeeze. Men like that don't exist. Either she's a ghost or she's already dead inside, and I can't price either."

Veyne turned his cup slowly on the table. It was cheap clay, chipped at the lip. He drank the bitter grain spirit because refusing hospitality closed doors, and doors were what he collected.

"She has one weakness," he said.

"You'll forgive me, Inquisitor, but I've spent four months mapping this woman's life. She has none."

"She has a boy."

Something flickered behind Sefa's eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. Veyne saw it. He always saw it. It was why the church paid him in trust rather than gold.

"You know something," he said.

"I know rumors."

"I am not interested in rumors. I am interested in you continuing to be alive and prosperous, and both of those outcomes improve dramatically if the next words out of your mouth are true ones."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. He let her look. He had learned early in his career that silence was a tool most men couldn't bear to hold; they filled it the way children fill quiet with humming. Sefa lasted longer than most. But she blinked first.

"There's a caravan master," she said. "Bren Hallor. Old friend of the warden's—he's the one who's kept her routes supplied these twenty years. Three months ago he started borrowing against future hauls. Bad rates. Desperate rates." She tapped the table once. "Two nights ago he asked me what a person might get for selling a route. Not cargo. A route. And a name."

"A name," Veyne repeated.

"He didn't say whose. He didn't have to. There's only one name attached to every road out of Thornfell." Sefa leaned back. "I told him I'd think about it. I'm telling you instead. Consider it my tithe."

Veyne allowed himself the smallest smile. "Your piety does you credit."

"My piety keeps me breathing." She stood, smoothing her apron. "One more thing, Inquisitor, gratis. Bren Hallor is a decent man. Decent men sell you things and then hate themselves for it, and hating themselves makes them careless, and carelessness gets them killed. If you want your warden, move before his guilt does something stupid."

* * *

Veyne walked the long way back to his lodgings, as he did each evening, past the shrine-row where the free cities permitted their hundred small gods to squat in niches like beggars. He paused at the largest of them—an alcove where a merchant guild maintained a statue of some river deity with a broken nose—and he did not pray to it. He never prayed to it. But he stopped, because stopping was part of his discipline, the nightly acknowledgment that false gods were still gods-shaped, and that hunger for them proved the existence of food.

His god had no statue. His god had never been permitted one, not since the Concord, not since the church declared that the divine word flowed through the Throne alone, that the Choir had been a corruption, a many-mouthed abomination through which demons had spoken in heaven's accent. Veyne had been six years old when his mother taught him otherwise, whispering the old hymns into his ear in the dark of their kitchen, her hands rough with lye, her voice so soft the walls couldn't carry it. She had sung the descent hymn—the one that welcomed the god's attention into a room—and he had felt it even then, even at six, a pressure behind his breastbone like a hand laid flat.

She had died on a pyre when he was nineteen. He had lit it himself. That was how the church tested its converts in those years: you burned what you loved, or you burned with it.

He had believed, lighting it, that he was saving her soul from the thing that had colonized her voice. He was fifty-one years old now, and some mornings, washing his face, he still believed it, and those were the good mornings. The other mornings he remembered the sound she made—not screaming, singing, singing to the last breath—and he understood that he had spent thirty years building a church over the grave of the only holy thing he had ever touched.

He did not permit himself these thoughts after dark. After dark was for work.

* * *

The work came to him at moonrise, in the form of a boy.

Not Oscar. One of his own—a novice named Pell, sixteen, eager, cruel in the thoughtless way of boys who had never been hungry. Pell came up the lodging-house stairs two at a time and knocked with the rapidity of bad news.

"She's moving tonight," Pell said. "Hallor sent word to the harbormaster's clerk—bribe of ours, third tier. A wagon train going south along the cliff road at first light. Eastwick signed the manifest herself."

"Passengers?"

"None listed. Cargo's salt fish and dye."

Veyne dressed slowly, by candlelight, folding each item as he removed it. He had found that ritual steadied the hand better than prayer did, these days. Prayer had become complicated. Ritual remained simple: fold, buckle, check the edge of the knife he was not permitted to use but carried anyway, out of respect for older traditions than the church's.

"Hallor," he said.

"Yes, Inquisitor?"

"Has anyone spoken to Hallor directly? Face to face?"

"Not yet. We've been letting him stew."

"Then no one approaches him. No one follows the wagon. No one so much as looks at the cliff road until I say." He pulled on his coat. "If Eastwick smells the net before she's in it, she'll cut loose everything—cargo, contacts, the boy—and vanish into the mountains where we will spend ten years finding nothing but her cold fires."

Pell's face fell slightly. Boys wanted spectacle. Veyne remembered wanting spectacle. He also remembered his instructor at the seminary striking his knuckles with a rod and saying: *God is patient. Be worthy of Him.*

They went out into the wet dark together.

* * *

He caught her at the tide-stairs below the customs house, at the hour when the lamps guttered low and the watch changed and the harbor lay black and heaving under a sky the color of wet slate. She was alone, checking knots on a moored skiff, and she heard him on the stones before he reached her—he knew she did, because her hands never stopped moving and yet somehow she was already turned toward him when he stepped into the lamplight, balanced on the balls of her feet like a woman deciding which direction to spend her violence.

"Inquisitor," Ada Eastwick said.

Her voice was exactly as the reports described: flat, unhurried, stripped of anything a listener could hold onto. Her face was weathered to leather, her gray eyes set deep, and she looked at him the way mountain folk looked at rockfall country—with assessment, not fear.

"Warden Eastwick." He inclined his head. "You know me."

"I know your coat. Church gray travels badly. Everyone in the lower city knows the coat."

"I'd hoped to do this differently. Over wine. With courtesy." He gestured to the steps beside the skiff. "But you are a difficult woman to invite anywhere, and time is a currency I've stopped wasting."

"Then say what you came to say."

"May I?" He indicated the step. She didn't answer, which he took for permission, and sat, arranging his coat with care. He left space between them. Space put people at ease; closeness put them on guard, and he needed her ease the way a surgeon needs a steady field.

"You guided caravans through Thornfell for twenty years," he began. "Before that, you served in the border levies. Before that—" he glanced at her, gently, "—you were a choir-child. Shrine-raised. Your grandmother sang the old rites, and was tried for it in the purges, and your family name appears in the trial rolls of Harrowfen. You were eleven years old."

For the first time, something moved in her face. Small. Controlled. But real.

"You read files," she said.

"I read everything. It's my devotion, Warden. Other men fast or flagellate; I read." He folded his hands. "I tell you this not to threaten you. Your history is known to the church and has been for twenty years, and no one has ever come for you, because you did nothing with it except bury it. You buried it beautifully. I want you to understand that I respect that. Grief deserves privacy."

The tide sucked at the pilings below. Somewhere across the water a ship's bell counted two bells into the dark, and Veyne watched her flinch—so slight that anyone else would have missed it—at the sound.

"But now," he continued softly, "you are carrying something. And grief has stopped being private. It's walking around in a child's body and humming in dead languages, and it rang every bell in the Lantern District three nights ago, and half this city knelt in the streets weeping at a miracle that should not exist."

"You have proof of that?"

"I have forty sworn statements and a bell-founder who swears his bells hung untouched. Yes, Warden. I have proof." He let the silence sit. "And so the question is no longer whether your grandmother's faith survived the purges. It's whether you're going to drown trying to smuggle it."

She said nothing. He hadn't expected her to. Silence was her native tongue too; it was, he suspected, the only thing they truly shared.

So he gave her the offer.

"The church will issue full pardon," he said. "Not amnesty—pardon. Erasure. Your grandmother's trial record, sealed. Your name, struck from the rolls. The twenty years of smuggling, the tariffs evaded, the warrants outstanding in three provinces—all of it, dissolved. You walk away clean. More than clean." He drew a folded paper from his coat and laid it on the step between them, weighting it with a stone so the harbor wind wouldn't take it. "A commission. Warden of the Eastern Passes, crown-appointed, salaried, garrisoned. Legitimacy instead of invisibility. A house with a roof that doesn't leak. A name that means something again."

She looked at the paper without touching it.

"And the boy?"

"Goes to the seminary at Vhalcrest. He'll be examined—gently, I swear it on the Throne—and whatever is speaking through him will be removed, and then he'll be fed, clothed, educated, and given to a farm family in the riverlands who will never know what he was. He lives, Warden. I give you my word on that, and I want you to understand that my word is the only currency I have never debased."

"You'll cut it out of him," she said. Flat. Not a question.

"We will remove the contamination," Veyne said. "Yes."

"And if it kills him?"

"Then he dies clean, in a warm bed, with priests praying over him, instead of dying dirty in a ditch when the army comes for him—which it will, Warden, with or without me. I am not the danger to that child. I am the gentlest thing hunting him." He leaned forward, and here he let his voice drop into the register he used perhaps twice a year, the register he saved for truths. "Listen to me. I loved my god. Do you understand? Not the Throne's version—the real one. My mother sang to Him in a kitchen while the world called it witchcraft, and I have spent my whole life serving a church that murdered her, because I believed—I *believe*—that the thing in that boy is not our god. It wears His voice. It wears His hymns. The Choir were mouths, and mouths can be possessed, and what speaks through your passenger is the reason my mother burned. Deliver it to me, and you are avenging her. Deliver it to me, and every soul the Choir ever corrupted finally rests."

The lie—or the truth; even now, on some mornings, he did not know which it was—came out of him smooth and warm, and he watched it land. Watched her jaw tighten. Watched her eyes go, for one unguarded moment, somewhere far away—to a shrine, maybe, or a kitchen, or a pyre.

That was the cruelty of it, he reflected. He wasn't lying about the love. The love was real. It was only aimed wrong.

"No," Ada Eastwick said.

Just that. No heat, no speech, no sermon. She picked up the paper, weighed it once in her palm like a coin of doubtful metal, and held it back to him.

"Keep your pardon," she said. "I've seen what the church does with its promises. So has everyone on this coast. You want the boy because he's a threat to a throne built on a dead god's name, and you dress it up as mercy because mercy is cheaper than soldiers." She rose, unhurried, and looked down at him with twenty years of mountains in her face. "Come get him yourself, Inquisitor. Bring your coat. Let's see how it travels in Thornfell."

She stepped past him and went up the tide-stairs into the dark, and she did not hurry, and that frightened him more than any blade could have. People who hurried were running. People who walked were choosing.

Veyne sat alone on the step for a long time, the rejected pardon drying in the sea wind, watching the black water work beneath the pilings.

Pell appeared at the top of the stairs, waiting for orders. Veyne gave them quietly: watchers on the harbor gates, the cliff road, the southern causeway. Seal the city's exits by dawn under pretext of a quarantine inspection. And send word to the army column camped at the border marches.

All of it, in its proper order. God was patient.

But as he rose and climbed the stairs himself, one thought followed him up out of the dark like a second set of footsteps: the boy had rung the bells of a whole district without touching them, and Veyne's mother had sung to a god who answered in kitchens, and the church had told him for thirty years that these were two different things.

He was beginning, very quietly, in the place where he kept the parts of himself the seminary had never fully burned away, to suspect that they were not.
