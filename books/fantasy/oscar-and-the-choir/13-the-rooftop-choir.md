## 13. The Rooftop Choir

The voices had been quiet for three days, and Oscar had almost learned to hate the silence.

He crouched in the dark beneath a fishmonger's awning, his knees drawn up against his chest, watching the lamplighters work their way down Copperlane with long brass poles. The lamps bloomed one by one, little orange suns in the gathering dusk. Somewhere behind him, Ada was speaking to a woman with a scarred face and a boat, arranging passage across the harbor. Somewhere ahead of him, always ahead of him now, were the men in grey.

*Stay here,* Ada had said. *Don't move. Don't talk to anyone.* She had said it the way she said everything—flat and quick, like she was paying out rope and didn't want to look at how much was left.

So he stayed. He had gotten good at staying. He could make himself small the way Ada did, fold himself into shadows and doorways until the city forgot he existed. For three days the voices inside him had been still as a held breath, and he had told himself that was good, that quiet meant safe, that the thing in his chest that made bells ring and old women weep was sleeping.

But tonight the quiet felt wrong. Tonight it felt like the hush before the moment when you realize you've stepped off a stair that isn't there.

He heard them before he saw them—the boots. Not soldiers' boots; these were softer, deliberate, the boots of men who had learned to walk without being heard. Three of them came down Copperlane from the north, moving apart from each other the way fishermen move along a bank, casual and spread out, and every one of them wore a grey scarf knotted at the throat.

Oscar's whole body went cold.

They were checking doorways. The first one paused at the mouth of an alley, lifted a lantern, and swept its light along the walls. The second stopped to speak to a chestnut-seller, showing her something—a paper, a face drawn in ink. Oscar knew whose face it was. He had seen it on notices nailed to the harbor gates that morning: a boy, dark-haired, thin, *last seen in the company of the fugitive Ada Eastwick*, and beneath it a reward written out in numbers so large they looked like a mistake.

The third man was coming toward the awning.

Oscar pressed himself back into the shadow between a stack of empty crates and the fishmonger's shuttered stall. His heart was slamming so hard he could feel it in his teeth. He thought about running—but which way? The street north was closed by two of them, the alley behind him was a dead end he'd already checked, and Ada was somewhere at the docks where he couldn't reach her without crossing open lamplight.

*I should have stayed on the mountain,* he thought, and then, absurdly, *I want Ada.*

The lantern light slid along the cobblestones toward him. Ten feet. Eight. He could hear the man breathing through his nose, slow and even, a man doing routine work.

And the voices woke up.

It wasn't like the other times. It wasn't a whisper or a hum or the soft pressure of words he half understood. It was a roar—not sound, not exactly, but something underneath sound, a great deep chord swelling up from the floor of his chest as if a hundred throats had opened inside him all at once. *Run*, the voices said, or maybe they said *hide*, or maybe they said nothing at all and only screamed, because Oscar suddenly understood that the voices were afraid too, that whatever lived inside him had been holding its breath for three days for the same reason he had.

"No," he whispered. "No, please, not now, not *here*—"

The voices did not listen. They never listened when it mattered most.

The chord rose through him like water rising in a locked room. He felt it fill his ribs, his throat, behind his eyes. He clamped both hands over his mouth but it didn't come out of his mouth. It came out of everything.

The first bell rang from the chapel of Saint Vessa, three streets away, one clear bronze note with no ringer in the tower.

Then the next bell. Then the next.

Oscar heard it happen the way you hear rain approaching across a rooftops—a ripple of sound moving outward from wherever he stood, bell after bell catching the note and passing it on, the whole district's bells waking in a wave. Saint Vessa. The salt-merchants' chapel. The little cracked bell over the tannery gate that hadn't rung in years. The great dumb bell of the Harbor Watch tower, which needed four men and a windlass, swinging on its beam and tolling like a heartbeat.

And under the bells, the chord itself—the voices, pouring out of him into the evening air, and this time everyone could hear them.

* * *

Later, people would describe it differently. A woman selling chestnuts swore she'd heard a choir of children singing somewhere above the roofs, though no choir had stood on any roof in Copperlane. A lamplighter said it sounded like his mother's voice, and his mother had been dead eleven years. A dockhand who had sworn off the old faith since he was a boy dropped his load of rope and fell to his knees in the middle of the street, weeping, because—and this is what he told anyone who would listen for the rest of his life—he had recognized the song. Not the words. He didn't know the words. But some part of him, some part older than his name, knew the tune, the way a body knows the rhythm of its own blood.

That was the thing about the Choir's voice. It didn't tell you anything new. It reminded you of what you had always known and been taught to forget.

Oscar didn't see any of this. He was curled in the shadow of the crates with his hands over his ears, shaking, while the city came apart around him in wonder.

Doors opened. Windows filled with faces. People poured out of the tenements and the taverns and the boarding houses, looking up, turning in circles, trying to find the source of a song that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere—from the sky, from the stones, from inside their own chests. An old woman in a widow's shawl walked to the center of Copperlane, lifted her arms, and began to sing along, in a language she could not possibly have known, her voice cracking and true. Others joined her. They didn't decide to. Their mouths simply opened and the old tongue came out, syllables surfacing from somewhere deep, the way a drowned thing surfaces.

The man with the lantern stood frozen ten feet from the awning, and Oscar watched through his fingers as the lantern sank slowly down to the man's side. The grey scarf at his throat looked suddenly absurd, a child's costume. His lips were moving. Whether he was praying or counting or cursing, Oscar couldn't tell. Then the man turned and ran—not toward the boy in the shadows, but away, up the street, stumbling, one hand pressed flat against his ear as if the song had gotten inside it and wouldn't stop.

*They're leaving,* Oscar thought wildly. *They're afraid of me. They're—*

And then, cutting through the miracle like a knife through bread: a whistle. Sharp, short, answered by another. The spies' signal, passed from rooftop to rooftop. Somewhere in the city, men were not kneeling. Men were writing things down.

The bells kept ringing. Oscar couldn't stop them. He tried the trick Ada had taught him, the breathing, the counting, gripping the cold iron of a crate-edge until his fingers ached, but the chord had him now, was using his throat and his ribs and his terror as its instrument, and the more frightened he became the louder it sang, which meant the more frightened he became the more people came, and they were coming now, hundreds of them, filling the streets, streaming toward the sound the way water finds a low place.

"Where is it?" someone shouted. "Where's it coming from?"

"The boy—it's the boy from the notices—"

"He's here. He has to be here."

Hands. Dozens of hands, gentle and terrible, pulling the crates aside, lifting the awning's edge, and then torchlight found him and a hundred faces looked down at the small shaking boy curled on the ground, and instead of grabbing him, instead of shouting for the watch, the whole street went silent except for the bells, and then, one by one, they knelt.

Not all of them. Oscar would remember that. Some stood at the edges with their arms crossed, afraid or unconvinced or simply unwilling. But most of them knelt, right there in the fish-gut filth of Copperlane, merchants and beggars and watchmen off duty, and an old woman reached out and touched his hair the way you'd touch something you couldn't believe you were allowed to touch.

"Blessed," she whispered. "Oh, blessed, blessed child."

"I'm not," Oscar said, and his voice came out small and cracked and ordinary. "Please, I'm not. I don't know what I am. I just want—" His throat closed. "I want Ada."

The name moved through the crowd like a spark through dry grass. Someone had seen her. Someone knew the smuggler woman, the warden from Thornfell, the one they said had gone east with a bounty on her head. And Oscar understood, with a sick lurch, that he had done the one thing Ada had begged him never to do. He had made himself visible. He had drawn a map to himself in fire and sound, and every spy in the free cities was copying it down.

The bells were slowing now, one by one, winding down like hearts calming after a run. In the strange quiet that followed, Oscar heard the whistles again—closer, coordinated, converging. And he heard boots on the chapel roof above him, several pairs, and a voice, low and official, calling down:

"In the name of the Council of the Free Cities, secure the child. No one is to touch him. No one is to speak to him." A pause, and then, quieter, in a tone that made the old woman flinch: "And find out who else heard. Make a list."

The crowd stirred, angry, confused. The wonder was curdling even as Oscar watched, becoming something else—becoming custody, jurisdiction, argument. Men were shouting at the council agents on the roof. Women were forming a chain around the boy. And through it all Oscar sat in the trampled mud of Copperlane with his hands pressed flat against the ground, feeling the last of the chord drain out of him, feeling emptier than he had ever felt, hollowed like a bell with the clapper torn out.

That was when a hand closed around his ankle in the dark under the awning, and pulled.

He yelped, kicked—and then the hand let go and gripped his wrist instead, and he knew the grip. Rough, dry, strong. He was hauled backward through a gap between the fishmonger's stall and the wall, into a passage he had never noticed, smelling of brine and old wood, and then Ada Eastwick was crouched in front of him in the dark, and her face—

Her face was the worst thing he had seen all night. Not angry. Not even afraid. Just stripped bare, the way a house looks with its roof burned off, everything exposed that was meant to be hidden.

"You sang," she said.

"They were going to find me. I didn't mean—I couldn't stop it, Ada, I *tried*—"

"I know." She closed her eyes for one breath, two. When she opened them, the shutters were back up, the warden's face rebuilt, but Oscar had seen what was underneath and he would never unsee it. "The boat's gone. Every route I had is burned as of tonight—you understand that? Every friend, every door, every name I trusted. There are lists being written right now with my name at the top of two of them." She took his chin in her hand, firm, not cruel. "Look at me. You didn't do anything wrong. Do you hear me? Whatever happens next—that was not wrong. What you did, those people felt something true for the first time in twenty years. But true things are expensive, Oscar. And we just paid."

Above them, beyond the mouth of the passage, the crowd was still arguing, still kneeling, still weeping, and the council agents were shouting orders, and somewhere a whistle sounded again, closer than before.

Ada pulled him deeper into the dark.

"We move now," she said. "And this time, boy, we don't hide you. We can't anymore. Now we decide where they see you—and we make certain it's exactly where we choose."

Oscar didn't understand what she meant. But her hand on his wrist was steady, and her voice had gone cold and clear as mountain water, and he had heard that voice once before, on the night she had decided not to turn him out into the snow.

He got up, and followed her into the dark.
