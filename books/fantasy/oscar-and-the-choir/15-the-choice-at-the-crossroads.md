## 15. The Choice at the Crossroads

The crossroads had no name. That was why Ada chose it.

It sat in a fold of hills two days east of the free cities' council hall and three days west of the pilgrim road that climbed toward the capital—a bare place where the old smuggler track split like a river around a stone, one branch running west into the lowlands and the sea roads beyond the usurper's reach, the other climbing east into the passes where Veyne's banners flew from every watchtower. There was nothing there but a leaning waymarker, its inscription worn to a smooth blankness by centuries of weather, and a stunted rowan tree that had given up trying to grow anything but sideways. No names. No history anyone would trouble to dig up. Just dirt, wind, and the choice.

Ada stood at the fork with her back to the rowan and watched Oscar crouch at the edge of the westward track, turning over a flat stone in his small hands as if it might tell him something the adults wouldn't.

Two days since the council hall. Two days since she had stood before men in good wool coats and asked them to look at a miracle, and they had looked instead for paperwork. Two days since a clerk with ink-stained fingers had said, *the girl claims a child is a weapon of foreign divinity; we recommend detention pending review,* and Ada had gotten them both out through the coal cellars before the guards finished tying their boots. The free cities would not listen. She had known it in her bones before she ever reached their gates, and knowing it hadn't made the door slamming shut hurt any less.

Now the road was all that remained, and the road went two ways.

West: the coast, the sea lanes, the free ports where coin bought silence more reliably than any god granted grace. She could have them on a ship within the week if she spent the last of Bren's maps and the last of her reputation calling in debts she'd sworn never to call in. Beyond the usurper's reach. Beyond Veyne. A boy growing up in some salt-stained harbor town with a new name and a false history, learning to mend nets or keep ledgers, never singing above a whisper, never letting the voices rise past a hum. Alive. Safe. Small.

And east of them—east, the war would come anyway. It would come down out of the passes the way winter came, patient and total, and the free cities would burn one by one because they had looked at a miracle and seen contraband. She could run west and live, and spend the rest of her life listening for bells in harbors that were not hers, knowing what she'd left behind her to burn.

East: the capital. The iron throne. Ten thousand soldiers drawn up in ranks, and a boy standing in front of them with a dead language in his throat.

She had run the arithmetic a hundred times since the rooftop, since the bells rang unstruck and half a district fell to its knees weeping. Every sum came out the same. West was survival. East was a gamble with worse odds than any pass she'd ever walked—and the only stake was everything.

"You're doing the face," Oscar said.

"What face."

"The one where you've already decided and you're waiting for me to agree." He set the stone down carefully, the way he did everything, and looked up at her. Nine years old, maybe ten—he didn't know himself, and neither did she—but his eyes were older than that, the way wells are deeper than they are wide. "Bren used to do it too. Before."

"Bren is dead," Ada said, "because I decided things for people."

The words came out flatter than she meant them. Oscar flinched anyway. The wind moved through the rowan's crooked branches, and somewhere far off, too far to be real, a bell tone hung in the air and faded—the Choir stirring inside him, restless, the way they always stirred when he was frightened. Ada had learned to hear it now. That was the trouble with miracles. Once you'd heard one note, you heard them everywhere.

* * *

They had sheltered the night before in a shepherd's bothy with a leaking roof, and Ada had lain awake doing what she did best: planning routes.

West first. Down through the Kettle Valley, avoiding the toll road, picking up the coastal path at Grey Harrow where the smugglers' stairs still ran down the cliff face if you knew which gorse bush hid the top of them. Then a boat. Old Sefa at Saltmere owed her—not money, something heavier than money, a life weighed against a life—and Sefa ran cargo to the free ports without asking what slept under the tarps. Three weeks, maybe four, and Oscar would be on open water with a new name stitched into his collar. *Osk.* Or *Tam.* Something plain. Something that belonged to no choir, no prophecy, no war.

She had planned it down to the hour. She had planned it the way she planned everything, because planning was the only prayer she'd trusted since the god went silent, and plans had kept her alive when faith hadn't.

But plans were made by people who assumed they knew what mattered. And lying in the dark listening to the boy breathe, hearing him murmur fragments of the old tongue in his sleep—*hallowed be the many mouths, hallowed the hands that carry*—she had understood, with a cold settling in her chest like snowmelt, that she was doing it again. Deciding. Choosing for him the shape of his whole life because she couldn't bear the shape of losing him.

Veyne wanted to cut the Choir out of him. The council wanted to lock him in a room and study him until he stopped being a person. And Ada—Ada who loved him, and that was the truth now, however much she'd fought it—Ada wanted to fold him up small and hide him forever, and call it safety.

All three of them wanted the same thing, when she was honest about it. To make him into what they needed. A silence. A specimen. A secret.

Only the difference between her and the others, she told herself, was that she wanted him to live. But wanting someone to live wasn't the same as asking how they wanted to live. And he had heard the bells ring. He had felt hundreds of strangers kneel in the street, weeping, reaching for him. He knew now what was inside him, and what it did, and what it might mean—and she had watched him carry that knowledge for two days like a pack too heavy for his shoulders, saying nothing, because she was afraid of what he'd say if she asked.

Protection, she thought, staring at the bothy roof. Protection was just ownership wearing a kinder coat.

* * *

"Sit down," she said now, at the crossroads. "We should talk before we walk."

Oscar came and sat on the flat rock beside the waymarker, knees drawn up, coat—her coat, cut down for him, the oath-stone still sewn into the lining—bunched around his ankles. Above them the sky was the color of a blade held to the light, high thin cloud moving fast from the east. Weather coming off the capital. Weather carrying news.

"I'm going to lay out both roads," Ada said. "Everything I know. All of it, no holding back. And then—" Her jaw worked. This was the hardest sentence she had ever tried to say, harder than the testimony she'd begun to speak in the council hall, harder than any oath she'd ever sworn or broken. "And then you choose. Not me. You."

Oscar blinked. "You always choose."

"I know." She crouched so she was level with him, forearms on her knees, close enough to see the chapped skin at his knuckles and the soot that never quite washed out of the creases of his ears. "That's the problem. I've been choosing since the night you knocked on my door. Where to walk, when to hide, who to trust, whether you rode in a corpse-wagon smelling of dead men while agents searched the wheels six feet away. I chose all of it because you were a child and I was the one with the maps. That part was right. That part was my job." She took a breath. "But this isn't a route anymore. This is your life. And I don't get to own it just because I love you."

The word landed between them like a dropped coin. Neither of them moved to pick it up.

"The west road," she said, and made herself speak plainly, like reading out a contract. "Down through the Kettle Valley to Grey Harrow, then the smugglers' stairs to the coast. Sefa takes us to the free ports. New name, new country, the usurper's writ doesn't run past the straits. You learn a trade. You grow up. You never sing above a whisper again, and the voices go quiet, or you learn to live around them, or—" she paused— "or they fade. Maybe vessels wear out. Maybe that's mercy. I don't know. What I know is: you live. Probably. If the passes hold and Veyne doesn't find another Bren."

"And the war comes here anyway," Oscar said quietly.

"And the war comes here anyway. The free cities, the valley towns, everyone who turned us away. They burn, and we hear about it in letters that take months to arrive, and we can't do anything, because we'll have spent our one miracle buying a fishing boat." She let that sit. "I won't pretend otherwise. Running isn't clean. It's just survivable. Mostly. For us. For nobody else."

Oscar looked down the westward track, where the land fell away green and soft toward the distant smudge of the lowlands. Somewhere down there were ships, and salt air, and a life with no bells in it.

"And east?"

"East is the pilgrim road to the capital." Her mouth tasted like ash saying it. "Ten days' walk if the weather holds and the checkpoints don't. We go in under penitent's hoods—we'd be two among thousands; the roads are full of refugees and pilgrims and the usurper likes the spectacle of penitents, it makes his rule look merciful. And then we put you in front of the army."

"In front of the whole army."

"In front of the whole army. On the steps, in the square, wherever the usurper shows his face. You sing what the voices showed you. Every oath he swore and broke. The true history of the Choir. What was done to your village, and to every village after it." She watched his face while she said it, and hated herself for watching, and watched anyway. "If it works—if the soldiers kneel the way the city knelt on the rooftop—his divine sanction dies in front of ten thousand witnesses, and armies don't fight well for gods that die. Maybe the war ends before it starts. Maybe the free cities get a year to arm. Maybe the whole rotten thing comes down."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then Veyne has you," Ada said, "and I will be executed beside you, and the Choir dies with us, and the only thing we'll have bought is that we tried." She held his gaze. "Those are the odds. I won't dress them up. West, you probably live and lots of people die. East, maybe everybody lives and probably you die. Those are the choices. Nobody else gets to make them for you—not me, not the voices, not the god. You."

For a long moment Oscar said nothing. The wind came down off the eastern hills and pushed at them, cold, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and iron—the smell of the capital, though he couldn't have known that. Ada waited. Waiting had never come naturally to her; she was a woman of routes and decisions, of forward motion. But she made herself wait, hands loose on her knees, and discovered that waiting, done honestly, was its own kind of courage.

"The voices want to go east," Oscar said finally.

"I know."

"They've been pulling at me since the rooftop. Like birds in a cage when the window opens." He pressed his palm flat against his own chest, over the sternum, where Ada knew the song lived. "Sometimes it's so loud I can't hear anything else. Sometimes I think—I think they're not asking me. I think they're driving me. And that scares me worse than Veyne does." His voice cracked on the last word and he steadied it, jaw tight, an old man's discipline in a child's face. "Because if I go east because they push me, then I'm just their mouth again, and the usurper was right about what we are. Weapons. But if I stay west because *you're* scared—"

He stopped. Looked at her.

"—then I'm just yours," he finished softly. "And you'd hate that. Even if I never knew. You'd hate it."

Ada's throat closed. Twenty years she had survived by keeping herself closed, sealed, silent—a warden station with no fire showing, invisible on the mountainside. And this child, this impossible vessel of a murdered chorus, had just read her more truly in nine years of living than anyone had managed in forty-three.

"Yes," she said. "I would hate it."

"So what do I do?" And there it was—the question underneath all of it, the one she'd been dreading, the one no route-map answered. His eyes were wet and furious and terribly awake. "Everyone wants something from me, Ada. The god wants a mouth. Veyne wants a corpse. The council wants a specimen. You want a son you can hide. The voices want—I don't even know what they want, they're so old and so sad and they miss being *many*. How do I pick? How does anybody pick?"

Ada reached out and took his hands. They were small and cold and chapped, and they shook, and she held them the way you hold something you have no right to but are going to protect anyway.

"You shake," she said, "and you're terrified, and you pick anyway. That's all courage is. I told you once that the mountain doesn't care if you're brave, only if you're careful. I lied. Or I was wrong. The mountain cares whether you keep walking. That's all. You pick the road you can live inside—not the safe one, not the loud one, the one where, whichever way it ends, you're still *you* at the end of it." She squeezed his hands. "And whatever you pick, I walk it with you. Not ahead of you. With you. That's the promise. That's the only promise I've got left."

Oscar looked west. Then east. The wind gusted, hard, from the direction of the capital, and in the rowan behind them a branch creaked—and beneath the creak, faint as breath, a single clear note sounded, unstruck, unmistakable. The oath-stone in his coat lining, singing to the wind off the throne of lies, like a compass needle finding north.

Oscar laughed—a wet, startled, nine-year-old laugh—and wiped his nose on his sleeve.

"They're not pushing," he said wonderingly. "They're *asking*. They've been asking this whole time, and I couldn't hear it over being scared." He stood, still holding her hands, pulling her up with him, and faced the eastward track where the hills climbed blue and cold toward the passes, toward the pilgrim road, toward the iron throne and the ten thousand spears. "Ada. I want to go east."

His voice didn't shake when he said it. Or rather—it shook exactly as much as it should have, and he spoke through the shaking, and that was the bravest thing Ada Crane had ever witnessed, braver than any pass she'd crossed, braver than anything she'd ever done in her long careful invisible life.

"Not because the voices want it. Not because you'd be proud. Because—" he swallowed— "because everyone who ever hid me died for it. My mother. The ferryman. Bren. Everyone who carried me got burned. And if I run west, I just make the next person who loves me into the next person who burns." He looked up at her, chin trembling, eyes steady. "So I'd rather stand in front of the army myself and give them something worth burning for. That's my choice. That's mine."

Ada nodded. She found she couldn't speak. She pulled the hood of her cloak up against the wind, shouldered her pack, and checked the straps twice because her hands needed something to do that wasn't trembling.

"We walk tonight," she managed. "Cold camps, no fires, off the pilgrim road where it bends at Saint Alder's Ford. Ten days if the weather holds." She looked east, at the long climb into the darkening hills, at the road that ended at a throne built on a lie. "And Oscar—if we get there, if you stand in that square—you sing like the world is ending. Because either it is, or it's being reborn, and nobody in that square will know which until you open your mouth."

Oscar shouldered his own small pack. At the fork in the road he paused, looking back once at the westward track, soft and green and safe, already fading into the dusk.

Then he turned east, and started walking, and Ada Crane—who had spent twenty years surviving by never following anyone anywhere—fell into step behind him.

Behind them, at the nameless crossroads, the rowan shivered without wind, and every leaf of it rang, very softly, like a thousand tiny bells being told: *come home.*
