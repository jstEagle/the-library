## 16. Marching on the Capital

The army moved east like a wound opening across the land.

Inquisitor Veyne rode at the head of the third column, his grey cloak buttoned to the throat against the autumn wind, and watched the road unspool beneath ten thousand boots. The dust of the march hung in the air like a curtain that never quite fell, and through it he could see the banners—white and iron-grey, the sunburst of the Ascendant Throne stitched in thread that caught the light and threw it back as if the cloth itself were proud.

He had made this march before. Seven years ago, when the border lords of the eastern marches had grown too bold, he had ridden with a smaller column and a lighter heart, and he had spent the nights in his tent transcribing confessions by candlelight, each page a small act of devotion, each signature a nail driven into the roof of the true church. He had been certain then. Certainty had been the warmest thing he owned, warmer than his cloak, warmer than the braziers, warmer than the blessing the Archprelate had pressed into his palm before the campaign began—a sliver of consecrated iron, worn smooth by a thousand thumbs, said to have been touched to the tongue of the Prophet-King himself.

He still carried it. He found, these days, that he carried it the way one carries an old debt: not out of love, but because putting it down would mean admitting what it had cost.

* * *

They passed through Harrowmere on the fourth day, or what was left of it.

Veyne had ordered no burning there. That mattered to him, though he could not have said why, and he noted the fact in the ledger he kept of his own conduct—the private ledger, the one no superior would ever read, in which he recorded his deviations with the same scrupulous honesty he demanded of heretics. The village had burned anyway. Soldiers were soldiers; fear moved through a column like fire through dry grass, and somewhere behind him a granary had caught and taken six houses with it. By the time Veyne rode in, the villagers stood in the road watching their homes smoke, and the soldiers watched them back, and nobody spoke.

A child was drawing in the ash at the edge of the square. Veyne saw her from horseback—small, maybe five years old, dragging a charred stick through the grey powder in long deliberate strokes. He reined in without deciding to. It was the kind of thing he did now, these small unscheduled stops, and he resented each one even as he made it.

She was drawing bells.

Not well. A child's bells, lopsided loops with clappers like dropped tears, a whole row of them across the ruined ground where the shrine of the old god had stood before anyone alive could remember. Veyne sat his horse and looked at them for a long moment while his escort waited in embarrassed silence.

"Who taught you to draw bells?" he asked.

The girl looked up. Her face was smudged; her eyes were the flat, unafraid eyes of a child who has already seen the worst thing she will see for some years. "Nobody," she said. "I heard them."

"The bells here were melted down before your mother was born."

"I heard them anyway." She said it the way children say things that are simply true—the sky is up, the fire is hot, I heard the bells. She went back to her drawing. "They rang when the man went past. The boy in the wagon. Everybody cried and didn't know why."

Veyne's escort shifted. One of the troopers, a veteran named Callow with a face like a closed door, muttered something about moving on. Veyne raised a hand. The column waited. Somewhere behind them, ten thousand men waited too, and the whole machinery of the advance paused for the space of a conversation with a five-year-old in ash.

"When did this happen?" Veyne asked.

"Before you came. Days and days." She drew another bell. "Mama says don't talk about it. But Mama isn't here."

No, Veyne thought. Mama was very likely in one of the houses, or in the pit behind the shrine where the village dead had been laid in haste. He looked down at the row of drawn bells, and the consecrated iron in his pocket seemed suddenly heavy, a stone sewn into his coat rather than a relic pressed into his hand.

"You heard them ring," he said carefully, "unstruck."

"They rang *sad*," the girl said, as if this were the important part, as if the mechanics of the miracle were beside the point entirely. "Like at a funeral. Like they were sorry."

* * *

That night, in the command tent that smelled of tallow and wet canvas and the particular sourness of men who have marched too far, Veyne took out his report kit and began to write the dispatch the Archprelate expected.

*The column advances on schedule. Morale is sound. Provisioning adequate. No sign of the heretic woman Eastwick or the vessel known as the echo. Local populations subdued.*

His pen stopped above the word *subdued*. It was not false. It was also not the whole truth, and Veyne had built his entire life on the principle that the whole truth was the only offering worth making—that a half-truth given to God was a counterfeit coin, an insult dressed as devotion. He had extracted confessions from dying men with the promise that only full confession could save them, and he had meant it every time, and he had never once lied in an official report in twenty-two years of service.

He wrote instead: *Local populations disturbed by reports of unstruck bell-ringing in the district of Harrowmere, following passage of an unidentified caravan. Investigation ongoing.*

It was less than the whole truth—he had not mentioned the child—but it was more than nothing, and he told himself the difference was prudence rather than cowardice. Prudence was a virtue. He had a list of virtues, written in his own hand at nineteen, folded into his breviary, and he consulted it the way other men consulted maps.

Callow ducked into the tent with the evening's dispatches, and Veyne set the report aside unfinished.

"There's a priest asking for you," Callow said. His tone was carefully neutral, which from Callow meant he disapproved. "Brother Anselm. Says he knew you at the seminary."

"Anselm." The name arrived with its whole history attached: the thin, quick boy who had shared Veyne's cell in the novitiate years, who had argued doctrine until the lamps guttered, who had washed out of the inquisitorial track and into the archive service because he wept too easily during interrogations. "Where is he?"

"Being fed in the quartermaster's tent. He says he has papers for you. He wouldn't give them to anyone else." Callow hesitated, which he almost never did. "He looks bad, sir. Like a man walking in his sleep."

* * *

Anselm had gone grey at thirty-eight. He sat hunched over the quartermaster's rough table with a bowl of barley soup untouched before him, and when Veyne entered he rose too fast, knocking the bench, and gripped Veyne's forearm with both hands as if the tent were pitching in a storm.

"Corvus," he said—the seminary name, the name Veyne had not heard spoken aloud in fifteen years. "Thank the god. Thank the god you're on this road."

"You look terrible."

"I've been riding for nine days." Anselm laughed, a cracked sound. "I stole a horse. Do you understand? I, Anselm of the Archive Service, committed theft, forgery of travel writs, and abandonment of post, all in one afternoon, because—" He stopped. Looked around the tent. The two orderlies pretending not to listen. "Because I need to show you something. Alone."

Veyne dismissed the orderlies and drew the tent flap closed. Outside, the camp murmured—fires, horses, the endless low tide-sound of an army at rest—and inside, Anselm opened the satchel he had guarded across nine days of stolen roads.

"Papers," he said. "From the deep vault under the Archprelate's palace. The sealed collection. You know what the seal means."

"No one opens the sealed collection. Not without the Archprelate's own key."

"No one is supposed to." Anselm's hands were shaking as he unwrapped the oilcloth. "There was a fire in the west annex last month. Small. Contained. But it breached the wall of the vault, and I was assigned to assess water damage to the adjacent holdings, and Corvus—I was *there*, alone, for three hours, and the seal on the Choir archive had cracked, and I—" He exhaled. "I looked. God forgive me, I looked, and now I can't stop looking, and I need someone to tell me I'm mad, because everyone else I could ask would burn me for it."

He spread the papers on the table.

Veyne recognized the format immediately: official transcripts, inquisition-grade, the careful clerical hand of the Reconciliation—the great purging, twenty years ago, when the Choir had been exterminated root and branch and the usurper's claim of sole divine sanction had been written in blood and then in ink. Every inquisitor learned the history the way they learned their prayers. The Choir had been corrupt. The Choir had hoarded the divine word, sold absolution, whispered lies into the ears of kings. The usurper—no, the *Ascendant*, history insisted on the title—had acted not from ambition but from necessity, cleansing the church so that the god's voice might speak through one pure mouth instead of a hundred venal ones. Veyne had memorized the catechism of it at twelve. He had taught it to novices at thirty.

The documents on the table did not match the catechism.

"This is a census," Veyne said slowly. "Of the Choir."

"A pre-war census. Commissioned by the Ascendant's own chancery, three years before the purging." Anselm tapped the columns. "Look at the holdings. Look at what they actually *owned*. Corvus, the Choir held nothing. No treasuries. No lands beyond their cloisters. Their total recorded wealth—" he turned a page with a finger that trembled, "—was four hundred crowns and a library. Four hundred crowns. The parish churches of a single diocese hold more. Where is the corruption? Where are the bribed kings? There's *nothing here*."

"Records can be falsified."

"These are the Ascendant's own records. Commissioned by his own chancery. Why would he falsify them against himself?" Anselm pulled a second document from the stack, older, its edges soft with handling. "And this. This is the order itself. The extermination decree. Read the justification clause. Read it *carefully*, the way we'd read a suspect's confession."

Veyne read it. He had read it before, a hundred times, in the standard histories. *The Choir, having proven themselves monopolists of the divine word and corrupters of its meaning, are to be extirpated utterly, that the voice of god may be restored to purity.*

But this was not the standard history. This was the original, and the original contained a line that had been cut from every copy issued since—a line struck through in the drafting hand and initialed, and the initials belonged to the Ascendant himself.

*Provided,* the excised line read, *that no mouth remains through which contrary word may be spoken.*

"It changes nothing," Veyne said. His voice came out level, and he was distantly proud of that. "The Choir may have been poor. Poverty is not virtue. The theological question stands independent of their accounts."

"Yes," Anselm agreed quickly, eagerly, the way a drowning man agrees with the riverbank. "Yes, exactly, that's what I keep telling myself. The theology stands. Except—" He drew out the last document, a single sheet, and laid it down as if it might bite. "Except this is a transcript. An interrogation transcript, from the first months of the purging. A Choir cantor, a woman named Sera Lune, captured at the western cloister. And Corvus—she was asked the question. The central question. Whether the god spoke truly through the Choir, and she answered under examination, under the rite of full truth, and her answer was *witnessed and sealed* by three inquisitors whose names are in the canon histories as heroes of the Reconciliation."

Veyne looked at the transcript. He did not pick it up.

"What did she say?"

"She said the god had never spoken *through* the Choir at all." Anselm's whisper barely reached the canvas walls. "She said the Choir didn't speak for the god. They spoke *with* him. Many mouths, one breath. And she said—" his voice failed and he steadied it, "—she said that when the last mouth was silenced, the god would not die. He would go quiet. He would hide whatever remained of his voice in flesh, in a humble place, among the poor, and wait. Those are nearly her words. *He will go quiet, and he will hide his voice where your swords will not think to look, and he will wait.*"

The tent seemed smaller. The lamp flame leaned in a draft that Veyne could not feel.

"That is a prophecy," he said. "Delivered under rite of full truth, witnessed and sealed, twenty years ago. And it was buried."

"It was buried *by the men who heard it*." Anselm pressed his palms flat against the table as if holding it down in wind. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? They heard her say it. They certified her answer as truthful—it's in the seal formula, Corvus, *answer given under compulsion of the rite, assessed true*—and then they hid it, because the truth of it was worse than any lie. Because if she was right, then the extermination didn't destroy the god's voice. It scattered it. It sent it into hiding. And everything since—every doctrine, every execution, every hymn rewritten—has been built on top of a sealed testimony that says the whole edifice stands on a lie."

Veyne stood very still. Twenty-two years of discipline held his face in place, held his hands at his sides, held his breathing slow and even. Inside, something was happening that he had no training for, no catechism for, no line in his list of virtues. It felt less like doubt than like vertigo—the sudden understanding that the floor one has stood upon all one's life is not floor at all but a painted ceiling, and beneath it, depth.

"Bells rang in Harrowmere," he heard himself say. "Unstruck. When a boy passed through in a wagon. A child drew them in the ash. She said they rang sad. Like they were sorry."

Anselm stared at him. "You've seen it too."

"I've seen a report of it. Secondhand." Which was technically true, and technically a lie, and Veyne noted the distinction in his private ledger with a sensation like swallowing a stone. "Anselm. These documents cannot leave this tent. If the Archprelate learns—"

"He'll burn me. I know. I'm not asking for protection." Anselm began wrapping the papers again, movements jerky, exhausted. "I'm asking for judgment. Yours. You're the finest examiner the service has ever produced—you taught us that truth is a load-bearing wall, that you can remove anything except that. So examine me. Examine these. Tell me I'm mad. Tell me the seals are forged. Tell me *something*, Corvus, because I have been carrying this for a month and I am so tired, and tomorrow the army reaches the crossroads at Marrow Ford, and after that the capital, and if the boy is real—if the echo is real—then we aren't marching to suppress a heresy. We're marching to finish what was started twenty years ago. We're marching to cut out the last of the god's voice. And I don't think I can watch that happen knowing what I know."

Outside, a trumpet sounded the changing of the watch, thin and clear over the sea of fires. Ten thousand men settled toward sleep, dreaming whatever soldiers dream—home, plunder, glory, nothing. Somewhere ahead of them, days away, a boy who might be a mouth of god was running through the free cities with a disgraced mercenary, and the whole weight of the Ascendant's armies was rolling east to meet him.

Veyne picked up the transcript at last. He read the cantor's words once, twice, three times, the way he read all testimony—hunting the flaw, the evasion, the seam where the lie showed through. He found none. The document was authentic; he would stake his career on the authentication marks, and he realized, with a strange calm, that he already had.

"It changes nothing," he said again, but the words came out differently this time, hollowed, and Anselm heard it.

"Doesn't it?" Anselm asked softly.

Veyne folded the transcript along its original creases and placed it inside his breviary, between the psalms and the list of virtues he had written at nineteen. The iron relic pressed against his chest, smooth and cold, touched—so the story went—to the tongue of the Prophet-King.

Or touched, perhaps, to something else entirely. Or perhaps never touched to anything at all.

"At dawn," Veyne said, "you'll ride back the way you came, and you'll forget my face, and I will forget yours. If anyone asks, you brought me nothing but greetings from the archive." He met the grey eyes of the boy he had once argued doctrine with until the lamps guttered. "And Anselm. If we take a prisoner on this road—an old woman, a singer, anyone who speaks the old tongue—I want to conduct the examination personally. Under the rite of full truth."

"You think there are more echoes."

"I think," Veyne said, "that I have spent my life listening for lies, and I have just discovered I was never taught what the truth sounds like. I intend to hear it. Whatever it costs."

He slept that night in his armor, with the breviary under his hand like a soldier sleeps beside a weapon, and dreamed of bells ringing sad in a burned village, sorry, sorry, sorry—and woke before the trumpets, certain of exactly one thing less than he had been the day before, and marching anyway.
