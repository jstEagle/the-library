## 17. The Long Walk to the Gates

The hood smelled of ash and old sweat, and through the weave of it the world came to Oscar in slivers: grey road, grey sky, the hem of Ada's coat swinging ahead of him like a metronome.

*Left foot. Right foot. Breathe.*

He had learned to count his breathing in the corpse-wagon at Thornhollow Bridge, when the agent's boots had stopped a hand's width from the boards beneath which he lay curled among the dead. Counting was what the voices did when they were frightened — they counted, all of them together, a thousand hearts keeping time inside his chest until he could not tell which beat was his own.

*Breathe in for four. Hold for four. Out for four.*

He was shaking anyway.

They had been walking since before dawn, when the free city's eastern gate was still a smudge of torchlight behind them, and now the pilgrim road climbed away from the river lowlands in long switchbacks crowded with everyone else who was fleeing the war. Carts stacked with chickens and cradles. Old men leading goats. A woman carrying a loom on her back because she could not carry her house. Nobody spoke much. The road itself seemed to have taken a vow of silence, and every footfall on the frozen mud sounded like a confession.

Oscar walked with his head bowed under the penitent's hood, hands folded as if in prayer, exactly as Ada had taught him. Penitents were invisible. That was the whole trick of it, she'd said, tying the cord at his throat with fingers that did not tremble even though his did. *Nobody looks twice at someone who is already sorry.* The hood was dyed the grey-brown of unwashed wool, patched at the shoulders, and it stank convincingly of a life spent kneeling on cold stone. Underneath it, sewn into the lining where no inspector would think to search, lay the oath-stone that had sung one clear note at Thornhollow Bridge and nearly killed them both.

Ada had wanted to leave it behind. In the end she hadn't been able to, and neither had he. It hummed against his collarbone now, faint and constant, like a cat purring in its sleep.

*It knows where we're going,* said one of the voices, the old woman one, the one who smelled of bread in his mind even though minds could not smell. *It is glad.*

*Be quiet,* Oscar thought at them, not unkindly. *Please. Not today.*

The voices went quiet, mostly. They had gotten better at that, in the weeks since the rooftop, since the bells rang unstruck over the lower city and three hundred strangers fell to their knees weeping in the street. Since then there had been no more hiding in cellars. There had only been running, and then Bren's maps spread on a floor by candlelight, and then Ada sitting very still for a long time before saying, in a voice like a door closing: *East, then.*

And Oscar had said, *I'll go,* before she could decide for him.

He held onto that now, walking up the pilgrim road. It was the one thing he owned that nobody could take from him, not the usurper, not the inquisitors, not even Ada. He had chosen this. He was choosing it still, one footstep at a time, and if his knees knocked together like a frightened foal's, well — courage, Ada had told him once, wasn't the absence of shaking. Courage was the decision you made while you shook.

He just wished the decision didn't have to be made so many times. Every hundred paces or so, the fear caught up with him again, and he had to choose all over again: keep walking.

So he kept walking.

* * *

Near midmorning they came around the shoulder of a hill and saw the gallows.

There were three of them, strung across the road like a grim gateway, their beams black with tar and older rope. Two hung empty, creaking. The third did not. Beneath it, a squad of soldiers in the usurper's iron-grey livery checked papers and prodded cart-loads with spear-butts, and a clerk at a folding table called out names from a ledger in a voice gone hoarse with repetition.

Ada didn't slow down. That was the first thing Oscar noticed — her stride never changed, never shortened, never lengthened. She walked the way water flows downhill, inevitable and unremarkable, and he understood suddenly that this too was a kind of courage, the kind that looked like nothing at all. He copied her. He put his fear into his feet and let them do the work.

They joined the queue of penitents. There were perhaps a dozen others — gaunt men and women in matching hoods, walking east toward the capital not to flee the war but to surrender to it, seeking absolution or bread or simply the strange comfort of being near power even when power might kill you. One of them, an ancient man whose hood had slipped, glanced sideways at Oscar with milky eyes and whispered something in a language Oscar knew without knowing how he knew it.

*Little mouth,* the old man murmured. *Little mouth, walk soft.*

Oscar's heart slammed. But the voices stirred gently, and translated: *He means no harm. He is one of the ones who remembers. There are more of them than anyone thinks — people who kept a word here, a phrase there, hidden in lullabies for twenty years.*

The line moved forward. The clerk took Ada's papers — safe passage, bought at ruinous cost from a harbor official with soft eyes and softer principles — and squinted at them, and squinted at her, and asked the questions they had rehearsed.

"Purpose of travel?"

"Pilgrimage." Ada's voice was flat as slate. "My nephew has taken a vow of silence. Don't expect conversation."

"And him?" The clerk's quill hovered. "Name?"

"Brother Ash," Ada said. "He doesn't speak. He atones."

Something in the clerk's face flickered — boredom, mostly, but underneath it the particular exhaustion of a man who processed a hundred sorrows a day and had stopped seeing them. He waved them through with a grunt.

They passed under the occupied gallows. Oscar kept his eyes on the mud, on Ada's heels, on the rhythm of his own counting. But the voices would not stay silent this close to death; they never could. They rose in him like water finding a crack, and though he kept his lips sealed, he felt them press outward, felt the air around the hanged man grow strangely warm, and heard — only with the ears inside him, the ears the voices used — a single note of mourning pass through the square like wind through wheat.

The soldiers shifted. One frowned and touched his sword hilt, looking around for the source of a sound he couldn't name.

Ada's hand found Oscar's shoulder through the crowd. Steady. Heavy. *Not here. Not yet.*

He swallowed the note back down. It hurt, going down, like swallowing a coal.

* * *

Beyond the gallows the road emptied into a valley of refugees, and here the hood became almost a mercy, because Oscar did not have to meet anyone's eyes.

He heard them instead. That was worse, some ways. A mother telling her children a story about a city where the walls were made of cake, her voice bright as glass over something bottomless. An old soldier explaining to a boy of maybe ten how to hold a spear, patient and terrible. Somewhere a baby crying with the flat, exhausted persistence of hunger, and the mother's milk long dried, and the whole column of the displaced flowing around that small sound like a river around a stone.

*We remember this,* the voices said softly. *We sang for columns like this once. Before.*

*Before what?*

A pause. Then, carefully, the way you'd hand a child a knife handle-first: *Before they taught the world that singing was a crime.*

Oscar walked. The oath-stone pulsed against his chest. And somewhere in the middle of the afternoon, on a stretch of road where the refugees thinned and the mountains showed their teeth ahead, he finally asked the question he had been carrying since the crossroads, since Ada had laid out the two roads and refused, for the first time in all the weeks he'd known her, to choose for him.

*Why me?* he asked the voices. *Out of everyone. Why did they hide the last mouth in me?*

The voices were quiet for so long that he thought they wouldn't answer. Then the old woman voice came again, and behind it others, layering, harmonizing, until it was less like one speaker and more like a congregation deciding what truth it could bear to tell.

*Because your mother sang,* they said. *When the soldiers came to the birthing house, she held you and she sang the old tongue, and the Choir — what was left of us, the last few mouths dying in the snow — we heard her. We heard a voice that did not break. And we were so tired, little one. We had been hunted for nineteen years. We were a handful of whispers looking for a room big enough to live in. So when her heart gave out, we did not let you follow her. We moved in. We are sorry. We have been sorry every day since. We know what we cost you.*

Oscar stopped walking.

Ahead, Ada took three more strides before she noticed, and then she turned, and even through the hood he could feel her looking at him — reading his shoulders, his breath, the set of his fists, the way she read everything, weather and men and lies.

"You need to piss?" she asked, loud enough for any watcher to hear, in the bored tone of an uncle inconvenienced by a child. "Behind the wall. Quickly."

There was no wall. There was a shrine — a tiny roadside thing, a niche in the rock with a rain-faded carving of a dove, one of hundreds that dotted the pilgrim road, most of them defaced. This one had been spared, or missed. Ada stood between him and the road, arms crossed, facing outward, and while she pretended to scan the horizon for bandits, Oscar stood in the shallow shelter of the stone and let himself cry.

Not loudly. He had gotten good at crying quietly; it was one of the skills the last year had taught him, along with counting breaths and walking past gallows. Tears ran down inside the hood and he wiped them on the coarse sleeve and the voices wrapped around him, humming, wordless, the way they had hummed in the burned village when he was small enough to be carried.

*I didn't ask for you,* he told them.

*No.*

*I'm scared all the time.*

*Yes.*

*And I'm still going to do it.* He pressed his palm flat against the stone, against the faded dove. *You should know that. Whatever happens at the gates. I'm still going.*

The humming changed. He couldn't have described how — it was like hearing a smile. And then, faintly, from deep in the stone under his hand, from the mountain itself, from the ground of the world: a single answering pulse, warm as a hearthstone, brief as a heartbeat.

Ada's head turned sharply. She'd felt it too. Twenty years of silence, and the god had just cleared its throat under her boots, and her face — the part he could see below her hood — went through grief and fury and something that was almost hope, all in the space of a breath, before she locked it down into mercenary blankness.

"Done?" she said. "We lose the light if we dawdle."

"Yes," Oscar said. His voice cracked on it. He didn't care. "I'm done."

* * *

They walked the rest of the day, and the capital grew out of the haze ahead of them like something rising from the sea.

First it was a smudge. Then it was walls. Then it was walls and towers and the glint of a thousand spears along the ramparts, and above it all, on the highest hill, the Iron Throne's palace with its banners hanging limp in the windless cold — the usurper's crest, the crown-and-chain, stitched in thread that was supposed to look like gold and only looked like brass.

The pilgrim road fed into the great eastern highway, and the highway fed into a river of people, and the river funneled toward gates tall enough to swallow a cathedral. Refugees were being sorted at the approaches: those with trade skills to one side, those with money to another, those with neither into long pens fenced with sharpened stakes, where they would wait days for permission to enter the city that needed bodies for its walls and hands for its forges.

And everywhere, everywhere, the iron-grey soldiers. And everywhere, nailed to posts and gateposts and trees, the proclamations, printed in bold black type:

*By order of the Throne: any person harboring the heretic echo known as the Last Voice of the Forbidden Choir shall be put to death. Any person bearing information concerning the same shall report to the Office of Inquiry. Reward: land, title, and the forgiveness of the Throne.*

Oscar read it upside down as they passed, because the voices made sure he could read anything, in any language, living or dead. *Forgiveness,* they repeated, and there was something in their tone that was not fear. It was colder than fear. It was almost amusement. *He offers forgiveness. He who burned our mouths and salted our shrines. Little one, when you stand before him — and you will stand before him — remember that he is more afraid than you are. Fear is why he built all this.*

The queue crawled. Dusk came down blue and bitter. Somewhere behind them a child began to wail, and was hushed, and began again. Ahead, torches were being lit along the gate towers, one by one, until the whole eastern face of the capital blazed like a candelabrum, beautiful and horrible, the doorway to everything they had walked a hundred leagues to find.

At the gate itself, inquisitors in white masks stood beside the soldiers, and each traveler passing through was made to sing.

That was the new thing. Ada had heard about it from a grain merchant two days back, and Oscar had watched her go very still when she heard it, and now he understood why. A line of peasants stumbled through the archway one at a time, and a masked figure held up a hand, and each one was ordered: *Sing.* Anything. A harvest song, a hymn to the Throne, a nursery rhyme. Most of them sang badly, tunelessly, terrified. The masked figures listened with heads cocked like birds, hunting for harmonics that shouldn't exist, for the resonance of the old tongue hidden in a common melody. One old woman was pulled from the line for singing a lullaby slightly wrong. They took her aside gently, almost kindly. She did not come back.

Ada stopped at the edge of the torchlight, in the shadow of a wayshrine, and turned to him.

"This is the door," she said quietly. "Past it, I can't unsay what we've done. Past it, the plan stops being mine and starts being yours." Her eyes searched his face under the hood. "Last chance, Oscar. There's a smuggler's track west of here. Three days hard walking and we're in the passes, and after that the world gets big, and no one ever finds us. I promised myself I'd offer you that. Once. Honestly."

The voices rose in him, not pushing, just present, the way they always were — a thousand souls folded into one small boy, waiting to see what he would do.

Oscar looked at the gate. At the white masks. At the proclamation flapping on its nail. At the pen where the skill-less poor waited in the freezing dark for a city to decide if they were worth feeding. He was terrified. His hands would not stop shaking, and his stomach was a knot of ice, and some traitor part of him was already composing the words of retreat.

But underneath the fear, quieter than the fear, steadier than the fear, was the memory of the old man on the road whispering *little mouth, walk soft*, and the mother with the dried milk, and the note he had swallowed at the gallows like a coal, still burning in his chest, asking — demanding — to be let out.

"I'm willing," he said. His voice came out small and cracked and completely certain, all three at once. "For real this time. Not because you chose it. Because I do."

Ada closed her eyes for one second. When she opened them, something in them had changed — settled, the way a load settles onto a back that has decided to carry it.

"All right," she said. "Then listen. When they order you to sing, you sing *badly*. You sing like a boy who hates singing. Off-key, off-rhythm, miserable. Can you do that?"

"It's the hardest thing you've asked me yet."

"I know." And — he was almost sure — she smiled. "Do it anyway."

They stepped out of the shadow of the wayshrine and into the torchlight, and the river of people carried them toward the gate, toward the masks, toward the throne on its hill, and Oscar counted his breathing — in for four, hold for four, out for four — and made the choice again, and again, one footstep at a time, all the way to the archway where a white mask turned toward them and a gloved hand rose and a voice like dry paper commanded:

"You. Boy. Sing."
