## 18. Before the Iron Throne of Lies

They took her hood off in the antechamber, and the light was so sudden and so gold that Ada thought for one blind moment she had died and found the afterlife disappointingly well-lit.

Then her eyes adjusted and she saw it was only wealth — candle-wealth, gilding-wealth, the kind of light that could only be bought by a man who taxed a kingdom's breath. The antechamber of the Hall of Audiences ran long as a church nave, hung with banners of black and iron-grey, and every banner bore the sunburst-and-scepter of Aurelian Vane, the man who called himself king by right of heaven.

Her wrists were chained in front of her. They had not beaten her on the road — Veyne's orders, she supposed; a prisoner delivered intact testified better than a broken one — but four days in a cage-wagon had done their own quiet work. Her shoulders ached from sleeping sitting up. Her mouth tasted of iron and dust. Somewhere behind her, muffled through stone, a crowd was gathering: thousands of feet, a low animal murmur, the sound a city makes when it has been told to come and watch.

*Good*, she thought, and was surprised at herself. *Let them all come.*

A clerk in grey robes approached with a ledger under his arm and the expression of a man who had catalogued ten thousand deaths without once looking up from the page. "Name," he said.

Ada looked at him.

"Your name," he repeated, pen poised. "For the record."

She let the silence stretch until it became its own answer. The clerk's pen hovered, wavered, and wrote something — *the mercenary woman, no name given* — and moved on to questions of provenance: where taken, when taken, by whose authority. Inquisitor Veyne's name went into the record three times, each time like a nail driven into wood.

Veyne himself stood at the far end of the antechamber, speaking quietly with two officers of the palace guard. He had not looked at her since the gates. That, too, was deliberate. She knew the technique from the other side of it — she had used patience as a weapon her whole life, letting silence do what threats could not. It unsettled her, faintly, to see her own craft worn so comfortably by a man who believed he was God's honest servant.

The doors at the end of the nave were iron-bound oak, twenty feet tall, carved with the triumph of some battle that had happened before anyone in this room was born. Beyond them, the crowd murmured like the sea.

"Mercenary." One of the officers had come over, a captain with a waxed mustache and the bored cruelty of a man promoted past his imagination. He circled her once. "Veyne says you walked the boy into the city yourself. Up the pilgrim road. Under a penitent's hood." He smiled. "That's either treason or madness. Which would you say?"

"I'd say it was Tuesday," Ada said.

The captain blinked. Behind him, one of the guards made a sound that might have been a swallowed laugh and might have been a cough.

"You understand what happens today," the captain said, leaning close enough that she could smell the wine on him. "The boy will be examined before the throne. If he is what they say — an echo, a vessel, whatever the priests call it — he'll be silenced. Quietly, if the crowd behaves. Loudly, if it doesn't. And you'll watch. That's the mercy being offered you. Most traitors don't get to watch."

Ada held his gaze. Four days in a cage had stripped her down to something lean and simple, and what remained was strangely calm. All her life she had been invisible — the woman who paid tithes at dead shrines, who led smugglers through passes no map admitted existed, who kept no name, asked no questions, carried no passengers. Invisibility had been her armor, her trade, her religion's last poor substitute. And now here she stood in the brightest room in the world, about to spend every coin of it in a single purchase.

*You don't hoard a thing forever*, her grandmother used to say, back before the trials, back when the old tongue was still warm in the family's mouth. *A coin buried is a coin wasted. Spend it where it matters or die rich in nothing.*

"Hold still," the captain said, and reached up to unlock her chains.

* * *

The Hall of Audiences held perhaps three thousand people, and it felt like more, because every one of them had been arranged.

Ada saw it immediately, with a smuggler's eye for terrain and a mercenary's eye for staging. The galleries were packed — guildsmen in their colors, foreign envoys in silks, common folk crowded up into the stone tiers where the seats were cheapest and the view was worst. But the floor of the hall had been left open in a wide avenue from the great doors to the dais, and along that avenue stood ranks of soldiers in parade black, six hundred at least, motionless as fence posts. The crowd filled the walls. The army filled the middle. Nothing in that arrangement was accidental. Anyone who stepped onto that floor would step into the jaws of the state itself.

And at the far end, on a dais of black marble beneath a canopy of cloth-of-gold, sat the throne.

It was called the Iron Throne of Lies now — not officially, never officially, but the name had crossed half the continent in whispers, and seeing it, Ada understood why. It was built, so the stories said, from the melted regalia of the old dynasty: crowns hammered flat, scepters snapped and welded, the consecrated sword of the last true king folded into the armrests like a broken wing. It glittered. It was ugly. It sat under a window of colored glass through which the morning sun fell in a column of jeweled light directly onto the seat, so that whoever occupied it appeared to burn with holy fire.

Aurelian Vane understood theater. That had always been the terrible thing about him. He had not merely seized a kingdom; he had staged it, lit it, scored it with hymns rewritten to name himself. For twenty years the god had been silent, and Vane had filled the silence with his own voice, and a kingdom that once heard divinity everywhere had learned to hear it nowhere except from the throne.

He was smaller than his statues. That was Ada's first thought, absurdly, as the guards marched her down the avenue between the ranks of soldiers. Grey at the temples, neat, a narrow face built for portraits rather than crowds. He wore white and gold, and he watched her approach with the mild interest of a man watching weather.

They put her standing in the open space before the dais, alone, chains hanging loose at her wrists. A herald read the charges — heresy, seduction of a sacred vessel, conspiracy against the crown's divine mandate — in a voice trained to make even nonsense sound procedural. The crowd rustled. Somewhere in the tiers, a child cried and was hushed.

"The woman will have the opportunity," the herald concluded, "as the law provides, to renounce her crimes before the throne and receive the crown's mercy."

There it was. The same bargain, third time offered. Veyne in the lower city: deliver the boy, be pardoned, restored, remembered. The captain in the antechamber: renounce, and watch instead of burn. Now the throne itself, before three thousand witnesses, holding out the same hand.

Ada lifted her head and looked at Aurelian Vane, and Vane looked back, and for a moment neither of them performed anything at all.

"Speak," said the herald.

She thought of the shrine in Thornfell, the little stone hollow where she had paid her tithe for twenty years into a silence that never answered. She thought of the oath-stones humming in the dark of her station, waking like old men hearing a grandchild's voice. She thought of Oscar asleep by her fire, fevered, whispering in the tongue her grandmother had died for, and the way the flames had leaned toward him as if listening.

She thought of Bren, who had sold her route for gold and then died carrying her maps out of the fire, and whose last look through the smoke had been an apology she had accepted too late to say so.

Invisibility had kept her alive for forty years. It had cost her everything else.

"Before I speak," Ada said, and her voice carried further than she expected, out over the silent ranks of soldiers, up into the tiers, "I want everyone in this hall to know what I am. I am nobody. I have no title, no lands, no name worth writing in your ledger. For twenty years I guided smugglers through the Thornfell passes, and I survived by keeping my mouth shut and my face forgotten. I am the least important person in this room."

A ripple of confusion moved through the crowd. This was not how confessions went.

"And yet," she said, "in all those years, walking the high places where the old shrines still stand, I never once heard the god answer me. Not once. Twenty years of tithes. Twenty years of prayers said correctly, in the old forms, at the old stones. Silence." She turned slowly, letting her gaze travel the galleries. "How many of you can say the same? How many of you have knelt at altars built by your great-grandparents and heard nothing? How many of you have been told the silence is *holiness*? That the god withdrew from the world because we were unworthy? That the Choir had to be burned so that the faith could be *purified*?"

"Enough." The herald's voice cracked like a whip.

"They told you the Choir were heretics," Ada said, louder, riding over him. "They told you the god spoke only through the throne now. And you believed it, because the alternative was to believe that the men who burned your grandmothers were liars, and that is a hard thing for any people to believe about its own army. I believed it too. I buried it. I built a whole life on top of the grave of it." Her voice caught — not performed, not entirely — on Bren's name unspoken. "But I am standing here today to tell you that the grave was empty."

"Seize her—"

"Because the god never died!" The words came out of her in the old tongue first — *Elu veth karai, elu veth soranne* — the language of the Choir, the language of her grandmother's whispered prayers, rising out of her throat after twenty years of disuse like water finding an old channel. The syllables rang off the vaulted stone with a strange doubled quality, as if the hall itself were repeating them a half-beat behind her. Three thousand people went still. Half of them did not know the words. All of them felt them, somewhere below thought, the way you feel a change in the weather.

"—the god went *quiet*," she finished, in the common speech again, "to hide something precious from the man on that throne. And he has hidden it so well that Aurelian Vane had to drag it into his own capital in chains to find out whether it was real."

The silence that followed had weight. Ada stood in the center of it, breathing hard, her chains clinking softly with each breath, and understood that she had just spent herself completely — every year of careful anonymity, every unwritten name, every closed door. There was no route back to the mountains after this. No smuggler caravan would carry her, no warden station would shelter her, no false name would hold. She had bought exactly one thing with the whole of her invisibility, and it was this: three thousand pairs of ears, opened, waiting.

Somewhere above, in the tiers, a woman began to weep — not loudly, just steadily, the way people weep at funerals — and the sound spread like a spark in dry grass.

On the dais, Aurelian Vane rose from his burning throne.

* * *

"Bring the boy."

The command was quiet, and the hall bent toward it.

Doors opened at the side of the dais, and the guards brought Oscar in.

They had dressed him in white — of course they had, the theater demanded it — a plain penitent's smock, his hair combed, his face scrubbed of the road. He looked small. He looked terrified. His hands were shaking, and he clasped them together in front of him the way children do when they are pretending very hard to be brave, and Ada's heart broke along a seam she hadn't known was there.

But he did not look at the throne. He did not look at the soldiers, or the herald, or the weeping galleries.

He looked at her.

And Ada, who had spent four days in a cage composing speeches and stratagems, who had rehearsed a hundred ways this moment might go, found that everything she had prepared evaporated, and what she did instead was the simplest thing in the world: she nodded. Once. Slowly. The way you nod to a traveler at a fork in the road when you cannot walk beside him anymore.

*Your choice*, the nod said. *Not mine. Not theirs. Yours.*

Oscar's chin came up. His hands stopped shaking — not entirely, but enough. He turned to face the hall.

Vane descended one step from his throne, arms spread in benediction, sunlight pooling around his white boots. "Child," he said, and his voice was honey poured over iron, pitched perfectly to reach the last row of the galleries. "You have been deceived. Used. Filled with echoes and lies by people who mean this kingdom harm. Whatever voices you have heard, they are not divine — there is one voice of heaven in this world, and it speaks from where I sit. Renounce them, and you will be fed, and schooled, and loved. You are a child. No harm need come to you. Only say the words."

The hall waited. Six hundred soldiers waited. The weeping woman in the tiers waited.

Oscar opened his mouth —

— and every bell in the capital began to ring.

Not struck. Not rung by any hand. Ada felt it before she heard it, a pressure in her chest like the moment before lightning, and then the sound came rolling in through the high windows from every tower in the city at once — deep, harmonious, impossible — dozens of bells, hundreds, ringing in a single chord that no bell-founder had ever cast and no composer had ever written, a chord that seemed to contain all the other chords the way a river contains its tributaries.

The colored glass in the great window shivered. Dust sifted from the vaulting. In the galleries, people were on their knees — not forced, not commanded, simply folded downward, the way grass bends under wind — and the soldiers in their perfect ranks stood rigid with their spears trembling in their grips, and the herald had both hands pressed over his ears though it did nothing, because the sound was not coming through the ears at all.

Ada alone, standing in the open floor with her chains swinging, did not kneel. She could not have said why. Perhaps because she had spent twenty years kneeling at a silent shrine, and some stubborn ledger in her soul refused to mark this moment as owed.

Above the throne, in the shivering colored glass, the light changed.

And Oscar, in his white smock, in the ringing heart of his enemy's capital, drew a breath that three thousand people somehow heard —

— and began to sing.
