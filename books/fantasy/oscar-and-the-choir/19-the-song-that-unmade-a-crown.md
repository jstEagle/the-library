## 19. The Song That Unmade a Crown

They had dressed him in white.

Oscar stood in the center of the great square of Vhal Corren, and the stone under his bare feet was cold as river ice, and the robes they had put on him were the color of nothing—of snow before it is trodden, of paper before it is written on. A penitent's color. They had meant it as mockery, he thought. They had meant to lead him out in the clothes of a sinner about to be cleansed.

But Ada had told him once, in a mountain pass with her breath steaming and her hand steady on his shoulder: *White is what you wear when you have nothing left to hide behind.*

The square was full.

He had never imagined so many people could exist in one place. The maps in his head—the ones the voices had painted for him over years of fevered sleep—showed Vhal Corren as a shape, a sprawl of streets around the Iron Throne's citadel. The maps had not shown him this: ten thousand soldiers ranked in their companies along three sides of the square, pikes upright like a forest that had been planted and then killed. The other two sides were citizens, packed behind barricades, kept there by chains and by curiosity in equal measure. Above them all, on a balcony of black iron, stood the usurper.

King Aldric Vane. The man who had sworn nine oaths and broken eight.

Oscar knew them all by heart now. He had not learned them from books; there had been no books. He had learned them the way he learned everything—the voices had carried them up out of whatever deep place they lived in and laid them before him like offerings. The oath at his coronation, sworn on the Choir's own altar with the last of the true singers forced to witness. The oath to the eastern lords, promising their borders would stand while he lived. The oath to the granary cities when the famine came. The oath to his brother, made in a garden, in the dark, the night before the garden burned.

Nine oaths. Eight broken. And the ninth—

*The ninth one,* said the voices, soft as ash settling, *he has not broken yet. He has been saving it.*

Oscar's hands were shaking. He looked down at them and watched them shake and did not try to stop them, because Ada had taught him that too—that courage was not the absence of shaking but the decision made inside it. *You'll be afraid,* she'd said, the night before they walked through the gates. *Be afraid with your spine straight.* His spine was straight. His hands shook. Both things were true, and he was learning to hold two truths at once; the voices had been teaching him that his whole life.

Chains bound his wrists—not tight, not cruelly, because Aldric Vane wanted no marks on him before the crowd. The usurper wanted the crowd to see a boy, small and mortal and breakable, and then to see the boy silenced, and then to go home believing that the gods belonged to kings and only to kings.

That was the whole architecture of the empire, Oscar understood suddenly, standing in the cold with his breath making little ghosts in front of his face. Not cruelty for its own sake. Architecture. You built a throne out of other people's certainty, and you maintained it by making sure no one ever heard anything true.

A herald's voice rolled across the square, amplified by trumpets, announcing crimes Oscar had not committed. Heresy. Sedition. The corruption of the faithful. Somewhere in the ranks of soldiers, a man coughed, and the sound was enormous.

Then the trumpets stopped, and the square went quiet in the way only very large groups of frightened people can be quiet, and King Aldric Vane stepped forward onto his balcony and looked down at Oscar with an expression that was almost gentle.

"Child," the king said, and his voice was warm, trained, beautiful—a voice built for balconies. "I am told you believe you carry something holy. I am told you believe the old stories. That the Choir still lives." He spread his hands, and the crowd murmured. "I want you to know that I bear you no hatred. Whatever lies were fed to you, whoever used you—you are a victim here, not a villain. Renounce it. Say aloud, before these good people, that the Choir is dead, that the god's voice ended twenty years ago, and that the only true word is the word spoken through your king. Do this, and you will leave this square alive. You will be fed. You will be forgiven."

*He means it,* whispered the voices, and there was something almost like pity in them. *He always offers first. It makes the second part easier to watch.*

Oscar lifted his head.

He found Ada without meaning to. She was being held at the edge of the square by two soldiers, and she looked exactly as she had the day she'd opened her door to a freezing boy and should have turned him away—tired, hard-eyed, her jaw set like something carved. But she nodded. Once. Small. The smallest nod in the world, and the largest thing in the square.

He thought about the warden station. The oath-stones humming in the dark. The shrine that answered. Bren's face the last time Oscar saw him, gray with guilt and blood. The corpse-wagon, and the note the stone had sung, one clear thread of sound in a place full of men listening for it. The bells, unstruck, ringing over the free cities while hundreds fell to their knees. Every person who had died so that he could stand here, in white, on cold stone, with his heart going like a bird trapped in a room.

He opened his mouth.

And the Choir came up through him like dawn coming up through a valley.

It did not feel like singing. That was the first thing he would remember afterward, when he tried to describe it and found that language kept failing him. It felt like being *opened*. Like every door in his body swinging wide at once, and behind the doors not rooms but weather—not sound but season. The voices that had lived in him since the soldiers burned his village rose together, and they were not many anymore; they were one, the way a thousand streams are one river, and they poured out of his small human throat with a force that should have torn it apart and instead felt like coming home.

The first note went out over the square the way light goes out over water.

Every person in Vhal Corren heard it differently. The soldiers nearest him heard it as a voice speaking directly into their ears, low and close, a mother's voice or a dead friend's. The citizens at the back heard it as a bell rung inside their own chests. The king on his balcony heard it as the sound of his coronation, twenty years ago, the choir loft full and the incense rising—and he went white, because he had spent two decades paying men to make sure no one could ever make that sound again, and here it was, unkillable, coming out of a child in penitent's white.

And the words—when the words came—they were not Oscar's words. He was only the door. What came through was memory.

*You swore,* sang the Choir, in the tongue that had no living speakers left except one boy and one woman who had learned it from her grandmother's heresy trials, *on the altar of the Ninefold Voice, with our singers standing witness, that you would keep the law of mercy. Show us the law of mercy.*

And the square saw it. Not imagined—*saw*, the way you see a memory that belongs to someone else. The granary cities in the famine year, the queues stretching past the walls, and the king's seal going onto the order that moved the grain east to pay his armies. Children with the particular stillness of the starving. A mother folding her child's hands over its chest because there was no wood for a coffin and no strength left to dig.

In the ranks of the Third Company, a soldier named Harl Voss—who had marched on the granary cities and stood guard while the grain ships loaded—made a sound like a man being unmade, and dropped his pike, and knelt.

*You swore to the eastern lords that their borders would stand while you lived. Show us where they stand.*

The passes at Thornfell, burning. Villages emptied. An old woman dragged from her doorway in a mountain settlement, executed in the snow for a hymn her grandmother had taught her—an old woman whose name the Choir knew, whose name they had carried all this way to give back to the world: Maren Ostwick, who sang *The Lantern and the Tide* badly and beautifully on the night she died.

Somewhere in the citizen crowds, people began calling that name aloud, tasting it, passing it hand to hand like bread.

*You swore to your brother, in the garden, in the dark.*

And this the Choir gave gently, almost tenderly: two young men among winter roses, one laughing, one already lying, and the smell of smoke three days later, and a grave dug in secret, and a crown taken from a dead man's table.

The kneeling spread. It went through the army the way fire goes through dry grass—not uniformly, not everywhere, but in patches, in companies, in clusters of men who had each carried some private knowledge of a broken promise and had each believed themselves alone in carrying it. Pikes clattered against stone. Officers shouted. One officer drew his sword and then stood holding it, trembling, unable to remember what it was for.

On the balcony, King Aldric Vane gripped the iron rail, and his face had gone from white to gray, and he was shouting orders no one could hear over the singing.

Oscar stood in the middle of it all and wept.

Not because it hurt—though it hurt, oh, it hurt, the way childbirth must hurt, the way any true thing costs—but because the memories were so sad. That was the secret no one had told him, the thing the voices had saved until now: the Choir was not a weapon. It had never been a weapon. It was a *witness*. Its whole purpose, its entire nature, was to remember what people swore and hold it up to the light, and what it remembered, always, everywhere, was how badly people kept their promises—and how much they wept when they finally saw themselves clearly.

He sang the ninth oath last.

*You have one oath unbroken,* the Choir said, and the square leaned in, ten thousand people breathing as one. *You have saved it, hoarded it, hidden it beneath your throne. Shall we show them what it is?*

And the king—for the first time in twenty years, in front of his army, in front of his city—looked afraid.

"No," Aldric Vane said, and his beautiful trained voice cracked in the middle like cheap pottery. "No. Guards. Take the boy's throat. Take his—"

But the Choir was already singing it, because the Choir did not take orders, and the ninth oath bloomed over the square like a terrible flower:

An oath sworn in private, years ago, to no audience at all—to a mirror, in fact, in a locked room, which is where men say the truest things they will ever say. *Let my line end rather than my power. Let my city burn rather than my name. If it comes to choosing, let everything I have built perish with me, for I did not build it to outlive me—I built it to BE me.*

The selfish oath. The real one. The one underneath all the others, the foundation stone, and now it stood revealed in the open air of the square, and everyone heard it, and everyone understood at once what kind of man they had been kneeling to.

The silence that followed the song was worse than the song.

It lasted perhaps four heartbeats. In those four heartbeats, Oscar later understood, a kingdom ended. Divine sanction is a strange thing—it cannot be measured or minted, only believed, and belief is a single thread running through ten thousand minds, and the song had cut it cleanly through. When the silence broke, it broke into sound on every side at once: soldiers removing their helmets, citizens surging against the barricades not in rage but in something closer to grief, an old sergeant of the Second Company walking slowly toward the balcony with his sword reversed, offering it hilt-first to no one, to everyone, to the air where a legitimate king ought to have been standing.

King Aldric Vane backed away from the rail. Backed away from his own city. And the crowd watched him do it, and no one moved to stop him, and somehow that was more damning than any blade would have been.

Oscar's knees buckled.

The song had gone out of him all at once, like water through a burst dam, and he came back into his own body the way a diver comes up from deep water—too fast, gasping, the world tilting. The stones were cold. Someone was screaming, far away. His wrists ached in the chains. He was just a boy again, eleven years old, barefoot on frozen rock in a square full of ten thousand strangers, and he had done the thing, the impossible thing, the thing everyone had died for—

—and terror flooded in behind the triumph like the sea behind a breached wall, because it was done now, it could never be undone, and the whole world had heard him, and the whole world would come looking.

Hands caught him before he hit the ground. Soldier's hands, gauntleted, careful. A face swam above his—a young officer, tear-streaked, uncomprehending, saying something over and over: *"What are you? What are you?"*

"He's a child," said another voice, Ada's voice, cutting through like a knife through canvas, and then she was there—she shouldn't have been there, the soldiers should never have let her through, but the square had stopped obeying anyone, and she dropped to her knees in the frozen filth and pulled him against her shoulder, and he smelled woodsmoke and leather and home. "He's a child, and he's freezing, and if any of you still have one honest bone, you'll get him a blanket."

For a moment, the young officer just stared at her. Then he shrugged off his own cloak and wrapped it around them both.

Above them, on the black iron balcony, the doors slammed. The king had gone inside. But Oscar, pressed against Ada's shoulder, half-conscious, saw what the crowd did not: figures moving in the shadows behind the balcony's pillars. Inquisitors in their gray. And at their head, tall and still, a man whose face he knew from nightmares and from the bridge at Thornhollow—from the moment a single sung note had nearly ended everything.

Veyne. Chief Inquisitor of the realm. And he was not looking at the king's retreating back, or at the kneeling army, or at the weeping city.

He was looking at a scroll case under his arm. A suppressed archive. And his lips were moving—silently, privately, in the middle of the collapse of everything he had served—as though rehearsing something he intended, very soon, to read aloud.

Ada's arm tightened around Oscar.

"Don't look," she murmured into his hair, in the old tongue, the dead tongue, the tongue that had just unmade a crown. "Whatever happens next—don't look away either. We're not finished."
