## 22. The Warden's Empty Station

The road up Thornfell had not changed, though everything else had.

Oscar climbed it on foot, as he always did now, leaving the pilgrim carts at the last switchback where the mountain grew too proud to be ridden. The path was wider than he remembered—widened by twenty years of feet, by the thousands who came each season to touch the stones and leave their small offerings of ribbon and bread and whispered names—but under the widening it was still the same track Ada had walked with her smuggler caravans, still the same scree and pine and thin cold air that tasted of iron and snowmelt. He knew every bend of it in his body before his eyes confirmed them. Some things a person carried in the marrow.

He was thirty-one years old, and his hair had gone grey at the temples early, the way it did for those who had stood too close to something vast. The free cities called him *the Last Voice*, which was a lie he had spent two decades gently correcting: he was not the last voice anymore. He was barely a voice at all. He was a man who taught children letters and kept bees and flinched, sometimes, when bells rang unstruck, because for one terrible heartbeat he would think they were calling him back.

They were not calling him back. They never called him anymore. That was the whole of his grief, worn smooth now like river stone, and he had come up the mountain to set it down for a while, the way you set down any burden you intended to pick up again tomorrow.

The station appeared around the ninth bend, exactly where it had always been, and exactly where it could never be again.

It had been rebuilt—or rather, the free cities had built a shrine *around* what remained of it, the way an oyster builds pearl around a wound. The warden station's low stone walls still stood, blackened at the corners where Veyne's men had burned it in the year after the capital fell; Oscar remembered the smoke from a distance, remembered Ada's face when she heard, remembered understanding even then that she had already stopped living there. But the walls remained, and someone had roofed the central room in cedar shingles and opened the south wall entirely to the sky, so that the old hearth and the old table and the old oath-stones sat open under the weather like relics in a glass case made of air. Pilgrims' ribbons hung from the rafters in their hundreds, red and white and blue, stirring in the wind with a sound like slow applause.

And everywhere, everywhere, the stones.

Oscar stood at the threshold a long time before entering. It was a habit he had learned from her—the pausing, the waiting, the refusal to take a threshold for granted—and he practiced it now with his heart going hard against his ribs like a fist on a door. Because the stones were singing.

Not loudly. Not the way they had sung that first fevered night, when a starving boy babbled a dead language in his sleep and the oath-stones of a silent god hummed awake for the first time in twenty years. This was quieter than that, older, more patient: a vibration below hearing, felt in the soles of the boots and the roots of the teeth, the way you feel a bell after its sound has finished. The whole shrine hummed with it. The mountain hummed with it. He suspected, though no scholar had ever proven it, that the whole range hummed with it, all the way down into the roots of the world.

She was in there. She was always in there.

*Ada,* he thought—not prayed, because he had never been able to make himself pray, prayer was for people who didn't know the address—and the humming shifted, almost imperceptibly, the way a sleeping person shifts toward warmth.

He stepped inside.

* * *

The interior smelled of cedar and cold stone and, faintly, impossibly, of woodsmoke—as if a fire had recently burned in a hearth that no one tended. Pilgrims reported this. Scholars had theories. Oscar knew the truth, or believed he did: some part of her still kept the old habits, still banked a fire that wasn't there, still swept a floor that needed no sweeping. Twenty years of devotion had not made her a spirit. It had made her a woman with nowhere else to be.

He crossed the room to the hearth and knelt, the way he had knelt here once as a boy of eleven, shaking with fever, while a mercenary with a smuggler's cold eyes held his hands and told him, in a voice that pretended to be annoyed, that she supposed she'd have to keep him alive since turning him out would be more trouble than it was worth. He had known even then that she was lying. He had loved her for the lie.

"Hello, Ada," he said aloud.

The humming did not change. It never did, at first. That was part of the ritual, though no priest had designed it: you spoke, and the silence answered, and the silence was her answer, and you had to be patient enough to hear the shape inside it. Most pilgrims left offerings and asked favors and went away disappointed, telling each other the shrine was only stone. They were not wrong. It was only stone. But stone, Oscar had learned, is just another kind of mouth, and mouths remember what they are for.

"I brought honey," he said, setting the clay jar on the old table, next to a drift of other jars, other breads, other small domestic offerings left by people who had somehow understood, without being told, that the presence in these stones had once been a person who liked practical gifts. "From my own hives. The ones behind the schoolhouse. You'd laugh at me—you always said I'd get stung stupid."

The wind moved the ribbons above the open wall. Somewhere down the valley a hawk cried once and gave up.

"I teach now," he went on, settling onto the floor with his back against the hearthstone, the way she used to sit when she was mending gear and pretending not to listen to him talk. "Letters and numbers and a little history, though I'm a poor historian. I tell them about the Choir, and I leave out the parts that would frighten them, and then I feel guilty, and then I tell them one frightening part, and then I feel guilty about that instead." He smiled at nothing. "You'd say guilt is a tax on having a conscience, and better paid than avoided. You said that once. I've checked the memory a hundred times. You said it."

Stillness. Stillness. And then—

The humming changed.

It was never words. That was the thing the scholars never understood, the thing he could never make the archivists of the free cities accept no matter how many hours he spent in their cool marble rooms explaining: what lived in the stones did not speak. Speaking was for throats and tongues. What lived in the stones *answered*, the way the Choir had answered, the way a chord answers a single note—not with meaning laid out in a line, but with meaning arriving all at once, whole, undeniable, like sunrise. You did not translate it. You simply found, afterward, that you knew things you had not known before, the way you know your own name.

What arrived, as the sun moved past noon and the light through the open wall crawled across the old floorboards, was this:

*You came.*

Not surprise. Never surprise. She had known he was climbing since the ninth bend, probably since the last switchback, possibly since he'd left his house in the river country three weeks ago—the stones carried vibration, and vibration was language, and language was hers now, all of it, every footfall in the range and every word spoken over any oath sworn anywhere near a shrine-stone. But knowing he was coming and acknowledging it were different things, and acknowledgment was a courtesy, and courtesies were how you knew she was still in there. Still Ada. Still the woman who had broken every rule she ever made for herself, one rule per chapter, until the rules were gone and only the woman remained.

"I came," Oscar said. His voice cracked on it, thirty-one years old and grey-templed and it cracked anyway, the way it cracked every year. "I come every year, Ada. You know that."

*I know,* the stones answered, gentle as snowfall. *I count.*

"You count." He laughed, wet-eyed. "Of course you count. You counted tithes and cargo and days between caravans. Why wouldn't you count the years?"

Silence, but a warm silence, an amused silence, and he sat in it and let it hold him, and outside the ribbons applauded softly, and the mountain breathed.

* * *

In the afternoon he did what he always did: he sang.

Not much. A few bars. The opening of the Lament for the Nine Mouths, the first hymn the voices had ever shown him, back when they lived inside his chest and he thought everyone carried voices and simply didn't mention it. He sang it quietly, half-voice, an old man's caution in a youngish throat, and as he sang the shrine woke around him the way a house wakes when someone comes home.

The oath-stones glowed. All of them—the great standing ring the free cities had raised in the yard, and the small pocket-stones sewn into pilgrim cloaks left hanging on pegs, and the fragments embedded in the old walls, and the one smooth grey pebble in the hearth that had once been sewn into a boy's coat and had sung one clear note in a corpse-wagon at a checkpoint while a mercenary stared down an inquisitor's agent with murder in her level voice. That pebble glowed brightest. It always did. It had been closest to her longest.

And beneath the glow, beneath the hum, the answering rose.

This was the part no one else ever heard, because no one else sang to her in the old tongue. The pilgrims prayed in Common, in trade-tongues, in the new liturgy the free cities had drafted and redrafted and argued over for two decades. Their prayers went out and mostly came back as silence, and they called the silence holy, and perhaps it was. But when Oscar sang the dead language—the language that was not dead, only quieted, only gathered in—the stones answered in kind, and the answering had texture, had timbre, had *her*.

It sounded like a contralto voice heard through a foot of granite. It sounded like wind through a specific stand of pines above a specific warden station. It sounded, when he let himself believe it, which he only allowed himself once a year, on this day, kneeling on this floor—it sounded like Ada Eastwick saying *I'm still here, boy, stop crying and sing the rest.*

So he sang the rest.

He sang the Lament, and then the Oath of the Nine Mouths, and then—he saved this for last, always, the way she'd saved the sweet tea for last—a song with no name in any archive, a melody the voices had given him in a dream the winter after her surrender, a melody he had come to understand was hers before any of it: a walking song, a smuggler's counting song, the kind of tune a woman hums on a cold pass to keep her blood moving and her thoughts to herself. Fourteen verses. He only knew six. He suspected the other eight were in the stones, and that she withheld them deliberately, the way she had once withheld her real name from clients, because some things you kept back so that you stayed a person and did not become a function.

When he finished, the shrine was silent in the way a room is silent after laughter—not empty, but full of something that has chosen to rest.

*Thank you,* the stones said.

"You're welcome," Oscar said. Then, because it was tradition now, because it was the wound he opened and cleaned once a year so it would heal crooked but clean: "Was it worth it? Tell me the truth. You owe me that much. You owed me it twenty years ago and you paid it in a cellar in the lower city with Veyne's men four streets away, and I've never decided whether I believe you."

The humming deepened. Took its time. She had all the time there was, and she had learned, in her long stone patience, to spend it the way she had once spent coin: deliberately, and never without purpose.

What came back was not one answer but a cascade, the Choir's way, all of it at once:

*A child alive. A crown unmade. A war ended before it began. Twenty years of oaths kept because the keeping of them could finally be heard. Yes.*

And beneath it, quieter, in the register that was purely hers and not the Choir's at all:

*And you. Which was the worth of it. The rest was accounting.*

Oscar put his forehead against the hearthstone and wept, briefly, efficiently, the way she had taught him without ever teaching him—that grief was work like any other, best done with good posture and a clear end.

* * *

He stayed until the light went long and gold, and then he did the last thing, the hardest thing, the thing that had taken him ten years to manage without breaking.

He said goodbye.

"It's late," he said, standing, brushing off his knees. "I have to go down before dark. The innkeeper at Thornhollow saves me the corner room; she thinks I don't know she does it." He paused at the threshold, hand on the old doorframe, the wood worn glossy by twenty years of pilgrim palms. "Same time next year. Bring honey. Complain about my singing. Count."

*I will,* the stones said. *I always do.*

And then, as he stepped through—for the first time in twenty visits, in twenty years, the first time ever—the answering did something new.

It rose. Not the low granite hum, not the Choir's chord, but a single thread of it, thin and clear and unmistakably shaped like a voice, shaped like *her* voice, and it said—in the old tongue, in the language of the Choir, in the language she had spoken aloud exactly once, in a throne room, to buy a boy a crowd's attention—one word:

*"Oscar."*

Just his name. Nothing more. Just the name, spoken the way she had never spoken it in life—she had called him *boy*, *cargo*, *the passenger*, *that damned kid*, anything but his name, because names were a rule and rules were how she survived—and now, with survival long since traded away, she spent it freely, at last, on him.

He stood in the doorway with the evening wind moving through the ribbons, and he did not turn around, because turning around would mean looking at stones, and he wanted, for one more moment, to live in the belief that if he turned, she would be there—grey-eyed, arms crossed, leaning against the hearth with that expression that meant affection disguised as exasperation.

"Next year," he said to the empty station, to the humming stones, to the woman inside them, "teach me the other eight verses."

Behind him, soft as snowfall, amused as ever, patient as the mountain itself, the shrine laughed.

He smiled all the way down the mountain, and the stones hummed behind him in the dark, counting.
