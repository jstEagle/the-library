## 1. The Taster's Spoon

The spoon was silver, and it had never once been poisoned.

Mara Kavanagh knew this the way she knew the weight of her own hands—not as a fact learned but as a fact lived, worn smooth by repetition until it felt less like knowledge and more like bone. Still, she tasted everything. That was the whole of her office, her title, her reason for rising before dawn in a palace that served no kingdom: she put the spoon in her mouth first, and if she did not die, the king ate.

Eleven years she had held the post, and in eleven years not one dish had killed her, sickened her, or so much as disagreed with her digestion. This was, depending on who you asked, either the most boring job in the Valley of the Unnamed or the most important. Mara had made her peace with it being both.

She stood now in the king's breakfast pantry—a room with copper pots hung like sleeping bats and a window that looked out on fog, because every window here looked out on fog—waiting for Cook to finish the porridge.

"You're hovering," said Cook. Her name was Bramble Hodge, she had arms like a blacksmith and a heart like a wet nurse, and she had been saying *you're hovering* to Mara every morning for eleven years.

"I'm waiting."

"Hovering with extra steps." Bramble stirred the pot with a wooden spoon the length of a canoe oar. "Honey's going in last. King likes the honey last."

"The king," said Mara, "likes nothing. The king hasn't had an opinion since the Frost Year, and even then his opinion was mostly about hats."

Bramble snorted, which from her was practically a sonnet of agreement. "He asked for honey last Tuesday."

"He mumbled at a wall. I was there. It was a load-bearing wall."

"Mumble counts. In this house, mumble counts." She ladled porridge into the royal bowl—the blue one, chipped at the rim, because there were exactly four bowls in the royal service and three of them were for show—and drizzled honey over it in a slow spiral, the way she did when she wanted the morning to feel like an occasion.

Mara took up the taster's spoon, dipped it, and ate.

Barley. Cream. Honey. A whisper of salt. Warmth spreading down through her chest like a small, well-behaved sun. No bitter edge at the back of the throat, no numbing of the tongue, no metallic ghost of arsenic or the almond lie of cyanide. Just breakfast. The safest food in the Valley of the Unnamed, and possibly the only food anywhere that had been personally guaranteed against death by a nineteen-year-old girl standing in slippers.

"Not dead," she announced.

"Praise be," said Bramble, without looking up. "Toast next."

* * *

The palace of the Unnamed Kings stood at the center of the valley like a wedding cake left out in weather too damp for weddings. It had once, according to the tapestries—which showed kings on horseback, kings at hunt, kings receiving ambassadors from countries with names—been white. Now it was the color of old teeth, softened at every edge, its banners faded to suggestions of color, its throne room drafty in a way that felt less like architecture and more like personality.

It served no kingdom. There was no kingdom to serve. The Valley of the Unnamed did not appear on maps; indeed, Mara was fairly sure maps went out of their way to avoid it, the way crowds part around someone talking to themselves. Outside the border stones lay the world—countries with names, rivers with names, wars with names, all of it loud and mapped and real. Inside the border stones lay fog, barley fields, eleven villages, one dying king, and a great deal of patience.

The valley had been sealed for longer than anyone could count, which was easy to say because counting required numbers, and numbers about the valley's age always came out slightly wrong, like a sum done twice that refuses to agree with itself. Pell the archivist had tried. Pell had spent forty years trying. Pell had eventually written in the margin of his own ledger, in ink that trembled: *the arithmetic disagrees with me, and I suspect it is right.*

Mara carried the breakfast tray down the long gallery, past tapestries of kings whose faces had been woven deliberately vague, as though the weavers had started each face and then thought better of it. Past the portrait hall, where the frames held landscapes. Not portraits of people—landscapes. Hills, rivers, one memorable painting of a turnip. Somewhere back along the centuries, someone had decided the walls should hold nothing with a name, and the painters had obeyed.

She liked the gallery best at this hour, when the fog pressed gently at the windows and the light came through gray-gold, like tea with too much milk in it. She liked the squeak of her shoes. She liked that nobody else was awake yet except the kitchen, and the kitchen loved her in the way kitchens love anyone who arrives hungry and leaves appreciative.

Here is what Mara Kavanagh was proud of, though pride was a word she used privately and rarely: nobody knew her.

Oh, they knew *of* her. The taster. The king's spoon. A figure at the edge of state occasions, thin and watchful, eating first from every dish while courtiers pretended not to watch her chew. But nobody knew her *name*. Names were expensive here. Names had been spent, long ago, like coin in a war chest, and what remained of the family that had paid most dearly was a girl with a quick tongue and a cast-iron stomach, hired to eat.

Invisibility, properly maintained, was a craft. Mara practiced it the way other women practiced embroidery. She walked at the edge of corridors. She spoke only when spoken to, and then briefly. She dressed in colors that fog approved of—grays, browns, the green of things growing quietly. At feasts she sat where the candlelight didn't reach. When courtiers needed someone blamed for bland soup, they reached past her for the scullery boy. When the archivist needed someone to carry boxes, he handed them to her and forgot she'd taken them within the minute.

And she was *good* at it. Eleven years, and Lady Vess Corvane—who noticed everything, who collected secrets the way other ladies collected fans—could not have told you Mara Kavanagh's face with any confidence. Eleven years, and the king himself, in his lucid hours, looked at her the way one looks at furniture that has always been in the room.

There were worse fates than being furniture. Furniture was safe. Furniture got to listen.

Mara pushed open the door to the king's morning chamber with her hip, balancing the tray.

* * *

King Aldous the Last—they called him that privately, though never where Pell could hear, because Pell insisted he was technically King Aldous the Only, there having never been another—sat by the window in a nightcap shaped like a deflated balloon. He was seventy-something, or sixty-something, or possibly ninety; the valley's arithmetic again. His eyes had gone milky at the edges, and his mind went in and out like a badly hung door, swinging open onto clarity and shutting again without warning.

This morning, the door was shut. He was watching the fog with the expression of a man waiting for a ship.

"Your Majesty," Mara said. "Breakfast."

He didn't turn. "They've stopped writing," he said.

"Who has, sire?"

"The countries. The ones with names." He tapped the windowsill, where frost would sit in winter and condensation sat now. "Used to be letters came. Ambassadors. Petitions. Now the fog just—" he made a gesture, fingers fluttering, "—just sits there. Being fog. You'd think after two hundred years something would knock."

Two hundred years. Pell said three hundred. The cook's grandmother had said four hundred, but the cook's grandmother had also claimed to have danced with a river spirit, so her testimony carried a discount.

Mara set the tray on the table by his elbow, moved the honey pot into the reach of his good hand, and stepped back into her corner—the corner by the tapestry of a hillside, where the light was poor and the shadows were friendly. Tasters waited in corners. That was half the job. The chewing was the other half.

The king looked at the porridge. Looked at her. Looked at the porridge.

"You ate it," he said.

"Yes, sire."

"And you're not dead."

"No, sire."

"Hm." He picked up his spoon with the concentration of a man defusing something. "You know, when I was young, the taster was a fat fellow named Odo. Ate like a threshing machine. Died at fifty-eight of a bad heart, which everyone agreed was ironic." He took a mouthful, chewed, swallowed, and seemed faintly surprised to still exist. "You're not fat."

"No, sire."

"Odo was happier, though. Or louder, anyway. Loudness reads as happiness at a distance." He ate another spoonful, staring out at the fog. "What's your name?"

Mara's spine went very still, the way a rabbit's does.

"I'm your taster, sire."

"I know you're my taster. I've watched you eat my breakfast for—I want to say nine years. Could be nineteen. The numbers wobble." He turned his head, and his eyes, milky-rimmed but still sharp at the center, found her in her friendly shadows. "I asked your *name*."

"It's—" And here Mara performed the maneuver she had perfected over eleven years, the small verbal sidestep, the polite shrug of a person whose name isn't important enough to bother with. "Kavanagh, sire. Kavanagh will do."

"That's a family. I asked for a you." But the door of his mind was already swinging shut; she could see it happening, the attention draining out of him like water from a cracked bowl. He turned back to the fog. He ate his porridge. After a while he said, mostly to the window, "Odo never would have told me either. Tasters are all the same. You're all somebody's secret wearing an apron."

Mara stood in her corner and let the silence close over her head, warm and familiar, like bathwater.

Being somebody's secret was fine. Being somebody's secret was, in fact, the entire arrangement, and the arrangement had kept the valley fed and the king alive and Mara herself breathing for eleven unpoisoned years. She was proud of it. Quietly, privately, the way you're proud of a scar that never reopened: this is mine, this worked, nobody saw.

She gathered the tray when he was done, curtsied to a man who was no longer entirely present, and let herself out.

* * *

The rest of the morning proceeded in its usual order, which was to say it did not proceed at all but simply accumulated, the way dust accumulates, the way fog accumulates, the way days in the valley piled up softly on top of one another without ever quite becoming history.

Mara tasted the midday broth (leek, potato, no death). She tasted the afternoon bread (rye, dense enough to stop a door, no death). She tasted a suspicious elderflower cordial presented by the under-steward with too much ceremony and determined it contained no poison but an unforgivable quantity of sugar, and sent it back with a note reading *the king's teeth are the crown's business.* The under-steward sulked for an hour. Mara counted it a triumph.

Between tastings, she did what she always did between tastings: she listened.

A taster who only tastes is half a taster. Food is how you kill a king, yes—but talk is how you kill a kingdom, and the valley had a king but no kingdom, so the talk drifted toward her ears like ash after a fire, settling on everything. She stood in the pantry crushing garlic for Bramble—tasters helped in kitchens; it was expected; idle spoons invited suspicion—and listened to the scullery boys argue about whether the fog was thicker this year.

"Fog's the same as ever," said one.

"Fog's thicker," insisted the other. "My dad says the border stones used to hum. Now they just sit. Something's gone out of 'em."

"Your dad says the moon follows him home."

"It *does* follow him home. Ask anyone."

Mara smiled into the garlic. This was the valley entire: a place where nothing changed and therefore everything was worth remarking upon. If the fog sat differently on a Tuesday, it was an event. If the king woke lucid, it was talked about for a season. If a goat gave birth to twins instead of the usual single kid, the mother goat received a name, which was extravagant, and the name was Goat, which rather undercut the extravagance.

Pell came through the pantry at noon, as he did every day, collecting the king's meal records—a ledger Mara kept faithfully in a hand so neat it looked printed. Pell was a long man folded into a short life, all elbows and ink stains, with spectacles he polished when nervous, which was always.

"Morning, Kavanagh," he said, taking the ledger without opening it, because he wouldn't read it until evening, alone, with a lamp and a cup of something medicinal.

"Archivist."

"Any incidents?"

"Elderflower cordial. Sugar-based attack."

Pell permitted himself the ghost of a smile, the smallest expression he owned. "The crown endures."

"The crown endures," Mara agreed.

He paused at the door. He did this some days—paused with his hand on the frame as though the doorway had asked him a question. Today he looked back at her, and the look lasted a half-second too long, and behind the spectacle-glass his eyes had the particular trouble of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Has the kitchen calendar been mended lately?" he asked. Casual. Too casual. Pell's casual was a costume that never quite fit.

"Bramble keeps it. Why?"

"No reason. Old habit. Calendars drift, in old buildings." He polished his spectacles, put them back on, and left.

Mara looked across the pantry to the calendar nailed by the flour bin—a board of little painted moons, one carved off with a knife at each month's turning. She'd glanced at it ten thousand times. She glanced at it now out of ordinary curiosity, the kind you aim at a thing someone else has just looked at strangely.

Twelve moons to a year. Everyone knew twelve. Even here, even in a country erased from every map and most memories, the year had twelve moons, because the year belonged to the sky, and the sky was not the valley's to edit.

The board had thirteen marks.

Mara frowned at it. Counted. Twelve, thirteen—no. She counted again, touching each painted circle with a floury finger, lips moving. Thirteen moons, evenly spaced, painted in the same faded pigment, worn by the same years of knife-marks at their edges. As though it had always been so. As though the thirteenth moon had been hanging in the valley's year forever, quiet as a guest who arrived early and was never questioned.

"Kavanagh," said Bramble, without turning from her dough. "You're hovering."

"It's nothing," said Mara, and wiped the flour from her finger, and did not hover, and did not leave, and stood there a moment longer counting a number that refused to come out right—twelve, thirteen, twelve, *thirteen*—while somewhere above the fog, unseen, patient, a moon she had never once thought to count began, imperceptibly, to wax.
