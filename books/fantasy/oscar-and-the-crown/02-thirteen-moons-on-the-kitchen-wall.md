## 2. Thirteen Moons on the Kitchen Wall

The calendar on the kitchen wall had been there longer than Mara had been alive, which in the Valley of the Unnamed was the closest thing to eternity anyone could vouch for.

It was not, properly speaking, a calendar. It was a plank of grey oak, older than the palace around it, older probably than the king himself, studded with little wooden moons on iron pegs — twelve of them, or so Mara had believed her entire life, one for each turn of the year's wheel. Every moonrise, Cook — a broad, flour-dusted woman named Hetty who had held her post through three reigns and one very confusing regency nobody liked to discuss — would move the smallest peg along its row with a thumb worn smooth as a river stone.

"New moon," Hetty would announce to no one in particular, and the kitchen would grunt its acknowledgment, and that was that.

Mara was scrubbing tallow off the tasting spoons when she noticed it.

She'd like to say she noticed it because of some taster's instinct — the same trained vigilance that let her detect henbane in honeyed wine at forty paces, or arsenic under a crust of almond paste. She'd like to claim it as professional pride. But the truth was far less dignified: she was bored, and boredom made her count things. She counted stairs (forty-one), she counted the dents in the copper pots (too many to bother with), and on slow mornings she counted the moons on the wall.

There were thirteen.

She stood with a wet spoon dripping down her wrist and counted again, slowly, touching each one with her eyes the way she'd been taught to touch each dish before it went to the royal table. One, two, three — the fat moons at the top, carved by someone with more enthusiasm than skill. Four through nine, smaller, neater, replacements from some later century. Ten, eleven, twelve — old, dark with grease and age.

Thirteen sat at the end of the row, low and slightly apart, as though it had been trying not to be noticed.

And here was the thing that made the back of Mara's neck prickle: she could not remember when it had arrived.

* * *

"Hetty," she said, "when did we get the thirteenth moon?"

Hetty didn't look up from her dough. "Don't be clever, girl."

"I'm not being clever. I'm being observant. It's my job." Mara pointed with the spoon. "There are thirteen moons on that wall. There should be twelve."

"There have always been thirteen." Hetty said it the way she said *the ovens run hot on the left*, or *never trust a fishmonger with good teeth* — flat, settled, immovable as the plank itself.

"That can't be right. A year has twelve moons. Everyone knows that."

"A year has whatever moons this wall says it has," said Hetty, punching the dough with sudden violence, "and this wall has always had thirteen, and if you've got time to stand about counting moons then you've got time to fetch me the second sieve."

But Hetty's hands, Mara noticed — and noticing things was, in fairness, actually her job — had gone still over the dough. Just for a breath. Just long enough.

Mara fetched the sieve. But she kept looking at the wall while she worked, and the more she looked, the stranger it became. The thirteenth moon wasn't just extra. It was *wrong*. The other twelve were arranged in a neat double row, six above, six below, an order so old it had worn itself into habit. The thirteenth hung below them all, on its own nail, at the height of a child's eye. And its wood was pale. Not new-pale — nothing in that kitchen was truly new — but pale the way something is pale after a long illness, bleached rather than aged, as if it had spent years in a dark place and only recently come out into the light.

She asked Tam, the scullery lad, if he remembered hanging it. Tam was fourteen and remembered everything, mostly other people's embarrassments. He looked at the wall for a long moment, frowning, and then his face did something she'd never seen it do before: it slid sideways. Smoothed over. He smiled and said, "Always been there, miss," in a voice that was half a tone too pleasant, and went back to scraping pots, humming a tune she didn't recognize and couldn't quite hold on to.

That was the second prickling.

* * *

By afternoon she had done what any sensible person does with an unanswerable question in a sealed country: she stopped asking people and started asking paper.

The palace archive was Pell's kingdom, and Pell — thin as a bookmark, dusty as a psalm — ruled it with the gentle tyranny of a man who has read everything twice. He was the only soul in the valley who seemed to enjoy questions more than answers, which is precisely why Mara liked him. Also he kept biscuits, which mattered.

"Twelve moons," she said, perched on a stool among the ledgers. "A year is twelve moons. That's not opinion, that's arithmetic. So why does our kitchen wall have thirteen?"

Pell's eyebrows climbed with what could only be described as delight. "Has it always?"

"That's what everyone says."

"And has anyone ever *counted*?" He was already up, hauling a ledger down with the tenderness of a man handling a sleeping cat. "You'd be astonished how few people count, Mara. Counting is how errors get caught. Most folk prefer their errors comfortable."

He found what he was looking for — a household inventory from the reign of the king's grandfather's grandfather, back when the palace had still pretended to serve a kingdom. His finger traveled down columns of candlesticks and cheese-weights and then stopped.

"*One moon-plank, thirteen marks,*" he read aloud. His voice had changed. It had gone quiet in a way that made the archive feel suddenly very large. "*Kept in the kitchens, where the counting is done daily.*"

"Thirteen," Mara said. "Two hundred years ago."

"So the wall is faithful. It's the year that's lying." Pell took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve without looking at them, put them back on, and took them off again. This was, Mara knew, his version of pacing. "Every calendar in this valley agrees on twelve. Every almanac, every farmer's rhyme, every child's lesson. Twelve moons, twelve months, tidy as a dozen eggs. Except one plank in one kitchen that has always known better."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," said Pell slowly, "that either some long-dead cook made a carving error that everyone since has politely preserved out of respect—" he paused— "or the wall remembers something the rest of us were made to forget."

"*Made* to forget?"

"Mm." He was looking at the inventory page with an expression she'd never seen on him before. Pell's face usually ran the full weather range between mild curiosity and intense curiosity. This was neither. This was a man reading a letter from an enemy. "The founding treaties are in the restricted case. I don't suppose you'd want—"

Someone knocked. A chamberlain's assistant, breathless, with a message: the king's supper required tasting an hour early, His Majesty was restless, and would the taster please attend. Mara rose, and Pell closed the ledger with a soft, deliberate thump, like a door being shut on a room you're not sure you locked.

"We'll continue this," he said. "Count your moons, Mara. All of them. Daily. If the number changes—"

"If the number changes?"

"Tell me immediately." He almost smiled. "I've waited my whole career for the number to change."

* * *

She tasted the king's supper — venison, root mash, a honey cake — and found nothing but dinner, and the king ate it staring at the fog beyond his window with an expression she couldn't read, and the evening came on the way evenings always did in the valley: gray deepening to gray, lamps lit against nothing in particular, the fog pressing gently at every window like a polite guest who refuses to leave and refuses to explain why they've come.

Mara walked back through the kitchens long past midnight, because she couldn't sleep, and because there was a question sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

The kitchen was empty. Embers ticking in the great hearth. The moon-plank on the wall, catching the last light.

She counted.

One through twelve, fat and familiar, worn smooth by two hundred years of a woman's thumb.

And then the thirteenth — pale, patient, hanging low where a child might see it.

Except.

She stepped closer. Her heart was doing something foolish and quick. Because the moon was no longer pale. It had darkened overnight to match its siblings — the grease and the years had somehow caught up with it, as though it had decided, belatedly, to look like it had always been there. As though it were settling in.

And beneath it, where yesterday there had been bare wood, someone had scratched a small mark into the grain. Thin. Fresh. Deliberate.

Not a word. Not a symbol she recognized.

It looked, she thought with a coldness that started in her stomach and spread outward, like the beginning of a tally.

Behind her, in the darkness of the scullery passage, a floorboard creaked — the particular creak of a board taking weight, unhurried, close. Mara turned, spoon raised in a grip that would have embarrassed her swordmaster, had she ever had one.

Oscar stood there in his nightshirt, the kitchen boy, eleven years old and all elbows, holding a stub of candle. His face was pale and his eyes were enormous.

"Mara," he whispered. "The goat's dying. I prayed over him and — I need you to see what happened to the candle."
