## 3. A Prayer Overheard

The goat's name was Buttercup, which was a lie, because goats do not have names in the Valley of the Unnamed, and also because Buttercup was a billy with the temperament of a tax collector.

Oscar had given him the name anyway, whispering it into the coarse fur between his horns on the first day they'd met, three winters ago, when Buttercup had been a kid small enough to fit in a bread basket and stupid enough to eat half of one. Naming things was against the rules. Oscar knew this the way he knew most rules: vaguely, resentfully, and only after breaking them. But nobody had ever explained *why* it was against the rules, and a boy who spends his days hauling water and scrubbing pots needs something to talk to, and the pots were terrible conversationalists.

"Come on," Oscar murmured now, kneeling in the straw of the far pen where the palace kept its dwindling menagerie of animals that existed mostly to be eaten. "Come on, you great grumpy lump. You've eaten worse than this and complained less."

Buttercup lay on his side in the dirty straw, legs stiff, breath coming in shallow, rattling pulls. His amber eye — the good one; the other had gone cloudy two days ago — rolled toward Oscar with an expression of profound grievance, as if the dying itself were Oscar's personal failing.

"It's not my fault," Oscar said. "I didn't feed you anything. I checked. I *always* check."

This was true. Working in the kitchens of a king who ruled nothing but fog meant working one door down from Mara Kavanagh, the royal taster, and Mara had taught him things without ever meaning to. Check everything twice. Smell before you taste. If it smells like almonds and nobody put almonds in it, run. Oscar had absorbed her habits the way straw absorbs spilled milk — thoroughly, and without anyone noticing until it mattered.

Whatever was killing Buttercup, it wasn't in the food. It had started with a shiver, then a stumble, then this: the goat folded up like a dropped puppet, burning hot one hour and cold as cellar stone the next. The cook, Brann, had looked at him once and said, "Nothing to be done," in the flat voice he used for spoiled cream and dead soldiers' pensions, and had gone back to his dough.

*Nothing to be done.* In the Valley of the Unnamed, that wasn't fatalism. It was arithmetic. There was no medicine worth the name, no herb-wife with second sight, no priest at any shrine worth lighting. Magic didn't work here. That was the whole point of the valley, though nobody under thirty could have told you why, and nobody over sixty would tell you anything at all except that some doors are better left shut.

So there was nothing to be done, and Oscar knelt in the straw anyway, because there is a kind of stubbornness that has no interest in arithmetic.

"You're not allowed to die," he told the goat. "You still owe me for the bread. A whole loaf. Half a loaf. Fine, three crusts, but I'm counting them."

Buttercup made a sound like a hinge that had given up on its door.

Oscar looked around. The stable loft was empty. Evening service was hours off, and Brann was elbow-deep in tomorrow's pastry, and the other kitchen boys were down by the fog-line gathering what passed for firewood. Nobody came out here. Nobody ever came out here. That was rather the point of Oscar's entire existence: he was the boy nobody looked at, the one whose name — the fake one, the whispered one, the one that lived only between him and a goat — was safe precisely because no one cared enough to ask.

He cupped his hands around Buttercup's muzzle, feeling the fever-heat of the little breaths.

"I know how this works," he said quietly. "Or how it doesn't. I know prayers don't get answered here. I know that's sort of the whole... arrangement." He glanced at the ceiling beams, at the fog-grey light leaking through the slats. "But I also know nobody's listening. So it can't hurt, right? It's like shouting into a well. Nothing comes back up. Everyone knows that."

Everyone knows that. It is remarkable, the things everyone knows, and how rarely anyone checks.

Oscar bowed his head. He'd never been taught a prayer — there were no priests to teach them — but he'd heard the old ones mumble fragments over graves, half-words, shapes of words, and he stitched something together from those scraps and from the plain desperate grammar of his own heart.

"Please." His voice cracked on the word, embarrassingly, and he pushed through it. "Please. He's just a goat. He's mean and he ate my bread and he bit Cook's apron and once he head-butted Lady Corvane's palfrey and honestly it was the best thing I've ever seen, please don't take him, he's mine, I named him, and I know I'm not supposed to have done that, I know names aren't ours here, but he's *mine*, and I'm asking. Whoever keeps the lost-and-found. Whoever's on duty. Please."

Silence.

Straw settling. Fog pressing at the walls the way fog always did, patient as a debt collector. Buttercup's breath rasping slower.

Oscar let out a long breath of his own and felt foolish in the specific, familiar way he felt foolish most days. Of course nothing happened. This was the valley. Things did not happen here. Things had not happened here for so long that "nothing happening" had been promoted, somewhere back in history, to a virtue.

He reached out to stroke Buttercup's ear one more time and say whatever it is you say to finish a failure —

— and the candle went *sideways.*

There was no candle in the stable loft. That was the first thing Oscar noticed, in the strange slowed-down way you notice things when the world stops behaving. There was no candle, and yet there was light, a thumb-sized flame standing in the air above Buttercup's forehead like a small golden thought, and it was bending. Not flickering — candles flicker, that's their whole personality — but *bending*, leaning toward Oscar's mouth as though his voice were a draft, as though his words were wind, as though something on the other side of the air had turned its face toward him and was listening so hard that even fire wanted to see.

Oscar stopped breathing.

The flame leaned further. The light it cast bent too, shadows swinging around the loft like weathervanes in a gale, all of them pointing at him, all of them pointing *in*, converging on the boy in the straw with his hands still cupped around a dying goat's nose.

"Oh," said Oscar, very softly. "Oh, hello."

The flame brightened. Warmth spread across his palms, across Buttercup's muzzle, and beneath Oscar's fingers he felt the goat's breathing change — deepen, steady, the rattle smoothing out like a wrinkled cloth drawn flat. The cloudy eye cleared, amber flooding back in, and Buttercup blinked up at him with an expression of restored grievance and, unmistakably, appetite.

Then the flame went out, politely, the way a guest leaves when the conversation ends, and the shadows swung back to their honest places, and the loft was just a loft again, smelling of straw and animal and evening.

Buttercup stood up.

He stood up the way you or I might stand up from a nap — a stretch, a shake, a brief offended glance at the straw stuck to his flank — and then he shoved his head into Oscar's chest hard enough to knock him backward, and began eating the straw with the focused enthusiasm of a creature making up for lost time.

"You're fine," Oscar whispered. He was aware, distantly, that his hands were shaking. "You're completely fine. You were nearly dead. You're eating bedding. That's — that's normal, that's what goats do, that's fine."

It was not fine. Nothing about it was fine. Oscar sat in the straw while a miracle chewed beside him and tried to make the pieces fit together, and they wouldn't, because the pieces included a sentence he had said himself, minutes ago, in a voice barely above a whisper:

*Nobody's listening.*

Something had been listening.

* * *

He told no one. Obviously. In the valley you learned early that the safest place for a strange thing is inside your own head, padded with silence, sat on firmly. He fed Buttercup double portions and watched the goat eat with the wariness of a man who has seen a drowned man walk out of a river, and Buttercup watched him back with an expression that suggested the goat remembered everything and considered it, on balance, Oscar's problem now.

For two days, nothing else happened, and Oscar almost managed to convince himself he'd dreamed it — except that dreams don't cure goats, and besides, he still felt it sometimes: a faint warmth behind his breastbone, like a coal banked under ash, that glowed a little brighter whenever he spoke kindly to something. To the cat in the scullery. To the sourdough starter, which Brann treated with more tenderness than he'd ever shown a person. Once, absurdly, to a stuck drawer, which opened.

On the third evening, he couldn't stand it anymore.

The kitchen was emptying. Brann had gone to argue with the cellarer, the other boys had been dismissed into the fog, and Mara Kavanagh was at her table by the window, cleaning her tasting spoons with the methodical care she gave everything, each spoon wiped and wrapped and laid in its row like sleeping soldiers. She was the closest thing the palace had to an authority on impossible things, being herself slightly impossible — the girl who could drink poison like tea — and she was also the only person in the building who had ever spoken to Oscar as though he were a person rather than a pair of hands.

"Mara," he said.

She looked up. She had grey eyes that took inventory of people the way her spoons took inventory of soup. "You've got a look," she said. "The look means either you broke something expensive or you found something cheap that isn't yours. Which?"

"Neither." He hesitated. "Do you ever think about why it's quiet here?"

"Quiet how?"

"Just — quiet. Inside. Like the valley's holding its breath. Like when you're carrying a full pot across a room and everyone goes silent so you won't spill."

Mara set down her spoon. She considered him for a moment, and Oscar had the odd sensation of being tasted — not unkindly, but thoroughly, like a broth tested for something invisible.

"Why?" she asked. "What did you hear?"

"Nothing," Oscar said quickly. Too quickly. "Nothing. Forget it. Just a thought."

Her eyes stayed on him a moment longer, and he had the distinct impression she was filing him away somewhere, in one of those neat rows, next to the spoons. "If you find something that isn't yours," she said quietly, "you bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. Understood? Before *anyone*. Not Vess Corvane, not the archivist, not the king. Me."

"That's a strange rule."

"It's the only rule I have." She returned to her spoons. "Go home, Oscar. It's getting dark, and the dark's been getting wrong lately. Haven't you noticed?"

He hadn't, quite — but walking back through the fog that night, along the lane where the lamps burned low and blue, he noticed it now. The fog was thicker than it should be, and it moved wrong, drifting against the wind, curling toward him gently, attentively, like something turning over in its sleep to better hear a sound it had waited a very long time to hear again.

At his lodgings above the cooper's shop, Oscar lit his one candle and sat on his cot, and the warmth behind his breastbone glowed, and the flame leaned — just slightly, just a degree, the way a flower leans toward a window — toward his mouth.

"Who are you?" he whispered.

The flame leaned closer. And somewhere far away — beyond the border stones, beyond the fog, in the dark country where the maps stop — something vast and patient and terribly glad drew a breath, and answered, in a voice like antlers growing:

"*Little borrower. At last.*"

The candle went out. In the darkness, Oscar sat very still, holding his own mouth with both hands, and understood with perfect clarity that he had shouted into a well, and the well had been occupied all along, and it had been waiting — for years, for decades, for however long wells wait — for someone, anyone, to call down and ask if anyone was there.

And now it knew his voice.
