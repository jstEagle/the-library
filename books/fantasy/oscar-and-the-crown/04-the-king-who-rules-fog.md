## 4. The King Who Rules Fog

The summons came at half past porridge, which was itself an omen, because nothing in the Palace of the Unnamed had happened at a specific time in living memory.

"His Majesty requests the taster," said the page, a boy of perhaps ten who had the haunted look of someone who had been sent to deliver this message to four other people first and been refused by all of them.

Mara set down her spoon. The porridge in the royal bowl sat cooling beside her, already tasted, already pronounced safe — safe being a relative word in a valley where the most dangerous thing on any given morning was usually the weather's mood.

"Requests," she repeated.

"That's what I said." The boy shifted from foot to foot. "Cook said to tell you it's probably nothing. Cook said His Majesty hasn't left his rooms since the frost fair, and even then he had to be carried, and mostly he just says things about geese."

"And now?"

"Now he's sitting up." The boy lowered his voice as though confessing to a crime. "Sitting *up*, miss. And he asked for breakfast he didn't eat, and then he asked for *you*. By your function, mind. He said 'the taster,' not 'that Kavanagh girl.' So that's something."

It was indeed something. In the Valley of the Unnamed, people were called by their functions when their names were too heavy for casual use — or, in Mara's case, because she didn't strictly have one. She was the taster. Before that she had been the girl-who-eats-first, and before that, nothing anyone could put a finger on. Her mother had named her once, privately, in a whisper against her hair, and then spent the rest of her life making sure no one else ever heard it.

"Tell His Majesty I'm coming," Mara said, and immediately regretted the briskness of it, because the page flinched as if briskness were contagious.

* * *

The throne room of the Palace of the Unnamed was the largest room in the valley and the least used, which meant it had developed the particular loneliness of a church on a Tuesday. Dust hung in the shafts of grey light like an audience that had arrived early and lost its nerve. The banners on the walls showed no crest, no colors, no sigil — they were simply grey cloth, dyed greyer with age, because a country erased from every map cannot very well advertise itself.

The king sat on the throne.

This alone would have been remarkable enough to make Mara stop in the doorway. King Aldous the Third — though "Third" was generous, since no one could confirm there had ever been a First or Second, and Pell the archivist had once suggested, delicately, that the numbering might be aspirational — had not occupied his own throne in eleven years. He had instead taken up residence in a chair by the window of his private apartments, wrapped in three blankets, conducting long negotiations with geese that only he could see.

Now he sat upright beneath the empty canopy, hands folded on the armrests, and he looked — there was no gentler way to think it — *awake*. His eyes, usually the color of dishwater left out in rain, had a sharpness to them. He was thin as a winter branch, but he sat like a man who remembered what thrones were for.

"Your Majesty," Mara said, and bowed the precise amount owed: deep enough for a king, shallow enough to remind everyone involved that this particular king ruled fog, and fog had never once obeyed him.

"Mara Kavanagh." He said her family name slowly, tasting it, and something in the way he did it made the back of her neck prickle. "Or rather — the daughter of Kavanagh. Isn't that how it goes? You're all daughters and sons of somebody here. Nobody is simply themselves."

"The valley has its customs, Majesty."

"It has its *sentences*," the king said, with sudden bitterness, "and we are all serving them." He gestured with one knuckled hand. "Come closer. No — closer than that. I'm old, not deaf, but the acoustics in this room were designed by an optimist."

She came closer. Up near the dais she could smell him: camphor, cold tea, and underneath both something faintly strange, like the air after lightning, or before it.

"Do you know why I've summoned you?"

"No, Majesty."

"Liar," he said, almost fondly. "You're a taster. You know exactly why I've summoned you. You've known since the moon miscounted itself on your kitchen wall, and you've been waiting three days for someone official to say it aloud so you'd stop feeling mad." He smiled, and it transformed his face into something that might once, generations ago, have been handsome in a lean and difficult way. "The whole court is pretending nothing is wrong. That's the one thing we've always been excellent at. But you don't pretend, do you? You taste. It's the most honest profession in the palace."

"Someone has to check the soup," Mara said.

"*Someone has to check the soup.*" He laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a gate opening after years shut. Then the laugh stopped abruptly, and he leaned forward, and the strangeness in the air around him thickened until Mara could almost feel it pressing on her skin like static before a storm.

"I am lucid," the king said quietly. "Do you understand what that means? For eleven years my head has been full of fog — real fog, girl, not the metaphorical kind, actual grey mist with things moving in it — and now, suddenly, it has cleared. Do you know why?"

"No, Majesty."

"Because the fog is leaving me. It's going back to where it came from." His eyes fixed on hers, and they were not kind eyes anymore; they were frightened ones wearing a king's face. "The seal is cracking. You know about the seal. Everyone knows about the seal the way everyone knows about gravity — without thinking about it, without wanting to look down."

"I know my family keeps the lock," Mara said carefully. "That's what my mother told me. That's what her mother told her. We keep the lock, and the lock is made of—"

"Of absence." The king's voice dropped. "Of everything your ancestors gave up so that magic couldn't get a foothold here. Their names, first. A name is a door, Mara. Did your mother ever tell you that? A name is a door, and magic walks through doors. So your people closed theirs. Gave them up, buried them, forgot them on purpose. And the land agreed to hold them safe, and the crown—" he touched the armrest, not the circlet itself, which sat elsewhere, gathering dust in a locked drawer, "—the crown holds the agreement shut. Jailers, my family. That's all we've ever been. Kings of nothing, wardens of a door."

He was quiet a moment. Somewhere high above, in the unseen reaches of the ceiling, a pigeon rearranged itself with the sound of a small argument.

"Why are you telling me this?" Mara asked. "Why now? Why me?"

"Because I'm dying." He said it the way other men announced dinner. "Not today, not tomorrow — the fog took its time leaving and it left damage behind — but soon. Weeks. Perhaps less, if the moon keeps misbehaving. And when I die, certain things become... available. Certain questions get asked. There are people in this court, Mara, who have spent their whole lives smelling the inheritance under the floorboards, and when the warden dies, they will come digging."

"Lady Corvane," Mara said, before she could stop herself.

The king's eyebrows rose. "So the kitchen hears more than the council chamber. Good. Yes. Vess Corvane and others like her — polished, patient people who believe the valley's isolation is a prison rather than a mercy. They want the Door Out. They want what's behind the seal. They want, gods help us, to be *remembered*." He shook his head slowly. "And the worst of it is, I understand them. I ruled fog for forty years. I have wanted to be remembered since I was a boy. Wanting it isn't the sin. The sin is what you'll trade to get it."

Outside the tall windows, the valley's famous fog pressed against the glass like a cat asking to be let in. Mara noticed, with a small cold turn of her stomach, that it seemed thicker than yesterday. Closer.

"There's something else," the king said. "And this is the part I need you to hear properly, so stop planning your escape routes and listen."

Mara, who had absolutely been planning her escape routes, went still.

"When the seal cracks, the first thing that comes through is hunger. Old hunger. The thing your ancestors bargained with — whatever it was, god or land or something older than either — it has been owed for centuries. And what it wants back is simple." He drew a breath. "It wants a name. Not just any name. It wants the name that locks the door, and the name that locks the door is—"

"Don't," Mara said.

The word came out of her sharper than she intended, and the king stopped, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other across the dusty light like duelists.

"You were going to say it's mine," she said, more quietly. "You were going to say my family's name. My mother's name. The name she whispered to me once and never again."

"I was going to ask you for it," the king said. "That's different. Saying it and asking for it are different things entirely, in this valley especially." He spread his thin hands. "I need to know if you still have it. If it can still be spoken. Because if it can, then you are the most dangerous person in this country, and there are people who will figure that out around the same time I die, and I would like to protect you before then, which is the only kingly thing I have ever been positioned to do."

The throne room felt suddenly much colder. Mara thought of her mother — of flour-dusted hands and the recipe book full of antidotes, of all those years of small poisons administered with love, building an immunity, building armor, building a daughter who could swallow anything except, apparently, this conversation.

"My mother didn't give me a name," Mara said. "Not a public one. Not a real one. Whatever she kept, she kept it the way she kept everything — hidden, and bitter, and for my own good."

"And do you remember it?"

Did she?

She reached for it the way you reach for a dream upon waking — knowing it had been there, knowing its shape, feeling it dissolve. There had been a night, she was seven years old, feverish, and her mother had bent over her and whispered something into her hair, over and over, like a charm, like a rope thrown into dark water. And she had gripped it. She had gripped it all night. And in the morning—

In the morning her mother had looked at her with such naked relief, and had said, "Good girl. Let it go. Names are hooks, love. Everything in this world that wants you needs a handle to pull you by."

"I remember that she made me forget," Mara said at last. "Deliberately. On purpose. She fed me forgetting the way she fed me hemlock and foxglove — a little at a time, so I'd survive it." Her voice did something unsteady on the last words, and she hated it. "So no, Majesty. I don't have it. I'm sorry to disappoint you. I'm nobody's key. I'm just the woman who checks the soup."

The king studied her for a long moment. Outside, the fog had climbed halfway up the window now, blotting out the courtyard, the hills, the world. It moved, Mara thought, with terrible patience. Like something that had waited a very long time and saw no reason to hurry now that the waiting was nearly over.

"You believe that," the king said finally. "That's the tragedy of your whole line, you know. Every Kavanagh believes it. *I'm just the woman who checks the soup. I'm just the man who feeds the geese.*" He smiled again, tiredly. "But here is what eleven years of fog taught me, Mara, and I give it to you freely, as my last act of governance: the things we forget don't leave us. They go down into the cellar of a person and sit in the dark, growing. And when the door above finally cracks — and yours is cracking, girl, I can smell it on you same as I can smell it on the weather — everything in the cellar comes up the stairs at once."

He settled back against the throne, and Mara watched, with a grief she hadn't expected, as the sharpness began to drain out of his eyes already, the lucidity ebbing like a tide that knew better than to stay.

"Go home," he murmured. His gaze drifted toward the windows, toward the fog. "Check the soup. Watch the moon. And when the antlered thing comes asking — and it will come asking, it always asks politely first — remember that you may not have your name, but you are the only person in this valley who was ever *taught to refuse a gift*."

"Your Majesty—"

"Geese," the king said serenely, gazing through her at something far away. "There's a matter with the geese. Someone really ought to speak to them about the winter arrangements."

* * *

Mara walked back through the palace corridors with her heart hammering a rhythm she didn't recognize. Servants nodded to her and looked away, the usual choreography of her invisibility, and for the first time in her life the invisibility felt less like a cloak than like a wound.

In the kitchens, the thirteenth moon-mark glared up from the wall calendar where it had no business being. Cook was arguing with the scullery boy about whether turnips could sulk. Oscar was scrubbing a pot with the ferocious concentration of a boy avoiding thought.

Everything was normal. Everything was exactly, precisely, deliberately normal.

"Mara," Cook said, spotting her. "Was it bad? What did he want?"

"He wanted to know my name," Mara said.

The kitchen went quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Well," said Cook at last, wiping her hands on her apron with great deliberation. "That's not ominous at all."

And outside the window, low over the valley, the fog curled once against the glass — softly, patiently, the way a hand tests a door it has every intention, sooner or later, of opening.
