## 5. What the Seal Is Made Of

Pell had spent forty-one years in the archives beneath the palace, and in all that time he had developed exactly three rules for a long life among old paper: never eat soup over a folio, never trust a lock that opens too easily, and never, under any circumstances, let the archivists of legend — the ones who came before him, whose marginal notes still haunted the shelves like polite ghosts — catch you skipping to the end of a document.

He broke the third rule the morning after the king asked Mara Kavanagh her name.

He had not meant to. He had gone down before dawn with his tea and his lamp, intending only to check on the Treaty Codex, which had been behaving strangely since the moon miscounted itself. The Codex sat in its case of smoked glass, chained shut with three locks, and when Pell lifted the lamp to it he saw that the pages inside had turned themselves to the founding accord — the one page no one was permitted to read whole, because reading it whole was said to be how you *agreed* to it.

Pell set down his tea. He wiped his hands on his cardigan, which was the color of weak onion soup and nearly as old. And then, because he was an honest man who knew himself well enough to know he would do it anyway, he unlocked the case.

The page did not resist him. That was the first dreadful thing. Old paper always resisted; it curled and sulked and shed its skin onto your sleeves. This page lay flat as a pond at dusk, and it was warm under his fingers, warm the way a sleeping animal is warm.

"Well," Pell said to the empty archive, because talking to oneself was the fourth unwritten rule and the only one he kept faithfully. "Well, well."

* * *

Mara arrived at midmorning, summoned by a note he had sent up through the kitchen, written in his smallest hand so that whoever carried it would have to squint and therefore think about what they were carrying. She came down the archive stairs the way she did everything — quietly, without announcing herself, so that he only noticed her when she was already standing beside the catalog drawers, looking around at the shelves with the expression of someone visiting a foreign country where she suspected everyone spoke about her behind her back.

Which, Pell reflected guiltily, they rather did. The archives spoke about her constantly. Half the documents in this room mentioned the Kavanagh line, if you knew where to look, and he had known where to look since yesterday afternoon, and he had not slept much since.

"You got my note," he said.

"I got half of it." She held it up. "The other half is smudged. It says something about a codex and something about my family, and I've been imagining six different disasters since breakfast, all of them worse than the last."

"That's the correct way to read anything I write," Pell said. "Come and sit down. Would you like tea? I have tea. It's terrible. The king's cook sends me the leaves he's decided are past honoring, which means I drink the ghosts of tea, but it's hot and it's mine."

She sat. She took the cup he offered without comment, tasted it out of pure habit — he saw her do it, the little pause, the tongue against the roof of the mouth, the professional assessment — and then made a face of such delicate diplomacy that he laughed for the first time in two days.

"It's awful," she admitted.

"Isn't it? I'm very proud of it. Consistency is the soul of scholarship." He drew his chair around beside hers, and the humor went out of him the way heat goes out of a stone when the sun moves. "Now. Before I show you anything, I want you to understand that I have read this document four times since dawn, and each time I have hoped to find that I was wrong, and each time the document has declined to cooperate. Scholars hate being wrong, Miss Kavanagh, but we hate being right about terrible things even more. It ruins the whole profession."

"Just show me," she said.

So he did.

The Treaty Codex opened to the founding accord, and the founding accord was not, as the palace rumors had always claimed, a grand declaration in a dead language with wax seals the size of dinner plates. It was a list. A long, plain, heartbreaking list, written in a careful clerk's hand, of names — or rather, of the spaces where names had been. Each entry read the same way:

*Surrendered, this turn of the moon, by the hand of [space], the name of [space], freely given, that the door may be barred.*

Hundreds of entries. Hundreds of blank spaces where names should have been, and beside each space, in the margin, the clerk had recorded what remained — a trade, a trade-mark, a thing left behind so that descendants could know their own people. *Left: a crooked second finger. Left: a laugh like a gate hinge. Left: red hair, uncommon in the valley. Left: the making of bread that rises twice.*

And there, near the bottom of the second column, in the same clerk's hand, though pressed harder into the paper as though the writer had been crying or angry or both:

*Surrendered by the hand of Mave Kavanagh, the name of the Kavanagh line entire, freely given — and let it be written that it was not given lightly, and that she wept, and that she gave it anyway.*

Below it, the left-behind things: *a talent for tasting true. A stubbornness noted by all who met her. A daughter, unnamed, who will carry the lock in her blood.*

Pell watched Mara read it. He watched her finger come up and rest, very lightly, on the words *the Kavanagh line entire*, and he watched her not breathe for a count of five, which for a food-taster, he thought, must be a kind of discipline.

"My mother used to say we were keepers," Mara said at last. Her voice was steady. Her finger was not quite. "Keepers of recipes. Keepers of the king's plate. I thought it was a joke about our boring lives."

"The joke," Pell said gently, "is that nothing here is boring. It only looks boring. That is rather the entire design of the valley."

* * *

He told her the rest over the terrible tea, because some stories go down easier with something bitter in your hands.

"Two hundred and sixty years ago," he began, "this valley was not sealed. It was famous. There are records — outside records, smuggled in, hidden in false book spines — describing the Valley of the Unnamed as it was before it had that name. Pilgrims came here. Kings came here. Because magic was *thick* here, Miss Kavanagh. Thicker than anywhere else in the world. The land itself was alive with it — every stone a shrine, every river a small god, every hedge a door. Wonderful, if you were welcome. Catastrophic, if you were not. And the gods of that age were not the gentle sort. They answered prayers, yes. But they answered them the way a fire answers cold. Completely. Without regard for what else was standing nearby."

Mara nodded slowly. "My grandmother's grandmother had a saying. *Ask carefully.*"

"*Ask carefully,*" Pell repeated, delighted despite himself. "Yes! That's a survival phrase. That's a phrase that gets handed down when the asking has killed people. You see? Your family has been keeping the memory alive without knowing why. That's how a seal works, incidentally — not with walls, but with habits. With sayings. With a calendar that has always had thirteen moons and a cook who insists it has always been so."

Her head came up sharply. "You know about the calendar?"

"I know everything that happens in this palace within a day, mostly through the kitchen, because kitchens are the true government of any building. But that is beside the point. The point is the bargain." He turned the page of the Codex, and there it was: the terms, laid out in that same clerk's careful hand, in language so plain it could only have been negotiated by exhausted people at the end of a long war.

"When your ancestors realized the gods could not be fought — and they tried, there are casualty lists, Miss Kavanagh, whole villages, gone in a night because someone wished for rain and the rain *came* — they did the unthinkable. They went to the oldest god of all, the god of the land itself, the one whose body is this valley, and they made a trade. Magic stays out. All of it. Every prayer unanswered, every wonder barred at the border stones. In exchange—"

"In exchange, the land's own name," Mara said quietly. "That's what the seal is made of."

Pell stared at her. "How did you—"

"The king asked me my real name yesterday. And he looked at me like I was a keyhole." She wrapped both hands around the terrible teacup. "I've been putting it together since. If names have weight here — and everyone knows they do, we just don't ask why — then a seal built of missing names would need... a lot of missing names."

"Hundreds," Pell said. "Every family in the valley surrendered at least one. Most surrendered all. The Kavanaghs—" he tapped the page, gently, the way he touched anything he respected, "—surrendered theirs as the final payment. The closing payment. Their name was the last thing to go into the lock, which makes their bloodline the last thread holding it. The lock in your blood, Miss Kavanagh. Literally. The treaty specifies it. Listen." He read aloud, and his voice, which he prided on being dry as good parchment, wobbled once in the middle:

*"Let the line of Mave Kavanagh be tasters and keepers, unnoticed and unnoticing, until the debt comes due. Let them live small and safe behind the seal their name has become. And when the thirteenth moon rises full above a valley that has forgotten itself, let the last Kavanagh choose: restore the name, and unbar the door — or carry the silence forward, alone, forever."*

Silence filled the archive, the good kind and the bad kind at once.

"Choose," Mara said finally. "It says *choose*. Like it's a menu."

"Treaties always pretend the future will be reasonable," Pell said. "It's how treaties sleep at night."

"And the thirteenth moon — the one on the kitchen wall that the cook swears has always been there—"

"—has been there for two hundred and sixty years, waiting. It isn't a mistake, Miss Kavanagh. It's a countdown that only starts counting when the seal begins to crack. And the seal began to crack the moment prayers started getting answered again." He closed the Codex, and the pages settled with a sigh, and he noticed — and pretended not to notice, because she had enough weight to carry — that the page had turned itself back to the Kavanagh entry before the glass case shut over it.

Mara sat very still for a long moment. Then she said, in the voice of a woman reaching for the last solid thing in a tilting room: "The goat. Oscar's goat. Something answered him."

"Yes."

"And the king fell ill after eating food I cleared myself."

"...I had wondered about that," Pell admitted. "I'd hoped I was wrong about that too. I've been wrong about a great deal lately. It's very humbling. I recommend it to no one."

"If sickness can arrive through answered prayer, then my immunity is—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at it, watched the professional calm hold and then not hold. "Useless. I've spent my whole life being the one person who couldn't be poisoned. And now the poison comes through the *air*, through wishes, through a kitchen boy kneeling next to a dying goat, and I can't taste-test a prayer."

"No," Pell said softly. "But you can read one. Come here. There's something else I haven't shown you."

He led her deeper into the stacks, past the census rolls and the weather diaries and the forty-year argument he was having with himself about proper shelving, to a narrow drawer marked, in his predecessor's handwriting, *DO NOT FILE ANYTHING HERE, PELL, I MEAN IT.* (He had filed many things there. It was the best drawer.)

From it he took a single loose leaf, thin as onion skin, covered in a cramped hand he recognized as Mave Kavanagh's own — the founder, the weeper, the woman who had given away her family's name and then apparently stayed up nights writing warnings to whoever came after.

Mara read it aloud, slowly, because the handwriting fought back:

*"To the last of us: the god does not want worship. It wants its name. It has been patient for longer than patience has a word for. When it comes to you — and it will come — remember that it is not the thief. We are. Remember that it loved this valley before we ever named ourselves upon it. And remember, above all: whatever it offers you, ask it what it is offering to take. Nothing in this valley is free. Not anymore. Not since us."*

Pell had read it perhaps two hundred times over the years, always believing it was allegory, always hoping it was allegory, the way scholars hope everything frightening is allegory right up until it knocks on the archive door.

Mara finished reading and stood there with the leaf trembling very slightly in her hands.

"Pell," she said. "Yesterday, when the king asked my name — after he asked, after I ran — I heard something. On the stairs. Like breathing, but bigger. And it said—" she swallowed, "—it said, *at last, the lock walks.*"

The lamp flame leaned, very slightly, toward her voice.

Neither of them moved. Outside, far above, through however many feet of palace and fog and careful forgetting, the kitchen bell rang for the noon meal, ordinary as bread, and the sound came down the stairs like a rope thrown to a drowning scholar.

"Right," said Pell, reaching for his tea with a hand that was almost steady. "Well. First: more tea. Second: we tell no one. Third—" he paused, because the third thing was the thing he had been dreading since dawn, the thing the Codex had shown him on his fifth illegal reading, the thing that had made him send for her in the first place.

"There's a third?" Mara asked.

"There is always a third. Scholars number things to feel brave." He took a breath. "Miss Kavanagh — Mara, if I may, since we appear to be conspiring now — the treaty lists the terms of the choice. Restore the name, or carry the silence. But there is a clause at the very bottom, added later, in different ink, unsigned. Someone amended the treaty. Someone wanted a third option written down, even if no one dared sign it."

"What does it say?"

He turned the Codex back open, to the last line, and read it, and watched her face as she heard it:

*"There is a third path. It is written in the mother's recipe book. Ask the taster's daughter to look in the back."*

The lamp flame leaned again — toward the Codex this time, eager as a dog at a door.

And somewhere above them, faint through the floors, a bell that was not the kitchen bell rang once, from a direction in which the palace had no bell at all.
