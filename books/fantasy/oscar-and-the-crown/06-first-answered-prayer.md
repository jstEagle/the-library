## 6. First Answered Prayer

Tomas Hale had farmed the same six acres at the valley's western edge for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned exactly one lesson worth passing on: the blight always came back.

It came with the autumn fogs, a gray rot that crept up the stalks of his barley and turned the grain to soft black mush. His father had fought it. His grandfather had fought it. There were remedies — lime spread at the field's edge, prayers muttered to shrines that never answered, a charm involving a dead crow and considerable dignity lost — and none of them worked for longer than a season. The blight was simply part of the valley, like fog and taxes and the king nobody saw.

So when Tomas walked out on the morning after the first hard frost, pulling his coat tight and rehearsing his annual sigh of resignation, he stopped mid-step in the frozen mud and forgot how to breathe.

The barley stood golden.

Not sickly gold, not the jaundiced yellow of dying stalks, but the deep, proud, heavy-headed gold of a harvest that had decided, against all precedent, to happen. Every stalk upright. Every head full. Where the blight had been gnawing at the eastern rows for three weeks — he'd shown his neighbor Aldous just yesterday, made him put his nose right down in it, *smell* it, Aldous, that's not right — there was nothing. No rot. No gray. The stems were clean as if they'd been scoured by hand.

Tomas walked the rows slowly, touching things, because a man who has been disappointed by his own fields for three decades does not trust them at first sight. He rubbed a head of barley between his palms and blew away the chaff, and the grains fell into his cupped hand plump and hard and smelling of bread that hadn't been baked yet.

"Well," he said, to no one, because there was no one else in the field. "Well."

He looked east, toward the border stones, where the fog sat thicker than usual, piled up against the hills like something leaning its weight against a door. The fog looked, this morning, almost attentive.

"Thank you," Tomas said experimentally, feeling foolish, and then, because foolishness had already been achieved and might as well be furnished: "Whoever you are."

The fog did not answer. But somewhere behind it, distantly, something that was not quite wind moved through the barley without bending a single stalk.

* * *

Three miles south, in the village of Lowmarsh, Wren Ashby woke before dawn out of habit she could not break, reached for her crutch the way she had every morning for eleven years, and found her leg did not need it.

She lay very still under the blanket, staring at the ceiling beams, and conducted an inventory. Toes: present, wiggling, five of them. Ankle: turning when asked. Knee: bending, smoothly, without the grinding complaint it had made since the fever took her at age six. She flexed the whole leg beneath the covers like a secret, then again, faster, then kicked the blanket off entirely and sat up and looked at it in the gray light — two legs, matching, both of them hers, both of them *working*.

Wren had spent eleven years being the girl who limped. It was not the worst thing to be. People gave you seats. People forgave your temper. Old Marta at the bakery saved you the heel of the loaf because, as Marta said, a girl who walks everywhere deserves the best part. Wren had built a whole self around the limp the way a river builds around a stone, and now the stone was gone and the river didn't know which way to run.

She stood up. She stood up without holding anything, which she had not done since she was small enough that falling was still funny. She took a step. Then another. Then, because caution had never been her strongest virtue, she ran the length of the cottage, out the door, through the frost-silvered yard, and back again, barefoot, laughing so hard her neighbor Bram came stumbling out in his nightshirt holding a fire poker as though the house were being robbed.

"Wren? What in the — are you *dancing*?"

"I'm *walking*," Wren said, and demonstrated, a full circuit of the yard, arms out, heels striking the frozen ground like drumbeats. "Bram. Bram, look. Look at me go."

Bram looked. Bram, who had carried her piggyback to the shrine of the Nameless Lady every festival day for eleven years while she apologized the entire way, looked at her legs and then at the sky and then at the old stone shrine at the crossroads, where — he noticed now for the first time, though it must have started in the night — a faint light was glowing between the cracks of the stones. Not firelight. Not moonlight. Something warmer, and patient, like embers that had remembered how to be a hearth.

"Your mother's going to want to know how," Bram said.

"My mother's going to want to know *who*," said Wren, still circling. "That's the part I can't stop thinking about. Somebody fixed me, Bram. In my sleep. Without asking." She stopped, and for the first time since waking, the joy on her face developed a hairline crack. "Who does that?"

Neither of them had an answer. Behind them, the shrine glowed on, gently, like a lamp left burning for someone expected home.

* * *

In the palace kitchens, the morning began with eggs, as mornings must, and then immediately departed from all tradition.

Cook — a broad, flour-dusted fortress of a woman named Hesta, who had held her post for forty years and held opinions for longer — cracked the first egg into the bowl and froze. "Mara," she said. "Mara. Come look at this."

Mara Kavanagh set down the tasting spoons she'd been laying out in their careful row and came over. The egg in the bowl was perfect. That was not the remarkable part; Cook's eggs were always perfect, selected with a tyrant's eye. The remarkable part was the yolk, which was twice the size of any yolk Mara had ever seen, a great orange sun sitting in the white, so rich it seemed lit from within.

"Where did these come from?" Mara asked.

"Same cart. Same farmer. Same hens, I'd wager, though I'll ask." Cook cracked another. Another enormous yolk. A third. She stepped back from the bowl as if it might be armed. "Something's got into the chickens."

"Something's got into everything," said Pip, the scullery boy, arriving with the milk churn and setting it down with a thump. "You should see the cream. It's gone thick as butter. And the baker says his starter doubled overnight and tried to climb out of the crock. He had to talk to it."

"Talk to it," Cook repeated flatly.

"He talked to it," Pip confirmed. "Said it settled right down. Mind you, he'd had a couple of ciders."

Mara touched the milk in the churn — cool, sweet-smelling, richer than it had any business being. All across the valley, apparently, the food had decided to excel. She thought of the king's breakfast tray upstairs, of the porridge she would taste in a moment, and felt the small professional satisfaction of her trade curdle into something more uneasy. Good food was easy to taste. Good food was safe food. But plenty had never been the danger in this kitchen; the danger, whatever it was, had come in over the walls without passing a single spoon.

"The calendar," Cook said suddenly, and went still in a way that made everyone else go still too.

"What about it?" Mara asked.

Cook didn't answer. She was looking at the kitchen wall, at the great painted wheel where the year was marked — twelve moons, one for each turn, painted by some cook dead two hundred years and repainted by every cook since. Except that this morning, in the space where the empty thirteenth section had always been — the blank wedge at the top of the wheel that cooks pointed at when they meant *never*, as in *I'll give you extra honey when the thirteenth moon fills* — there was a faint mark. A crescent. Thin as a fingernail paring, pale as new cheese.

"That wasn't there yesterday," Pip said.

"It's never been there," said Cook. "It's the *empty* moon. It's the moon that isn't." She wiped her hands on her apron, once, twice, a fourth time, though they were dry. "Somebody get Pell. Get the archivist. Don't run — we're not animals — but walk *quickly*."

Mara looked at the little crescent and felt the same cold thread slide down her spine that had been visiting her lately, uninvited, whenever the world behaved strangely. Time was miscounting. Food was showing off. Shrines were glowing. Somewhere out in the fog, something had begun answering, and it had not thought to consult anyone first.

Upstairs, the king's porridge waited to be tasted. For the first time in her career, Mara wished it were poisoned. Poison she understood.

* * *

Old Dell the shepherd had not prayed in fifty years, and he wanted that understood.

He made a point of mentioning it to anyone who would listen, which that morning was half the village of Stony Ford, gathered at the crossroads shrine in their coats and shawls, staring at the light seeping from the ancient stones. Dell stood apart with his crook planted like a flagpole, radiating skepticism.

"Fifty years," he told Mrs. Tallow, who was holding her hat on against a wind that wasn't blowing anywhere else. "Not one word upward. My wife prayed every day of her life and it got her a bad chest and an early grave. I keep my accounts with the sky settled at zero."

"Mm," said Mrs. Tallow.

"And now look at this. Light in the stones. Crops coming in gold. The Cooper's boy's fever broke in an hour last night — an hour, after three days of cooking him. Everyone wants to fall on their knees and start thanking." He snorted. "Thank *whom*? That's what I want to know. You don't thank a knock at the door till you've seen who's knocking."

"Mm," said Mrs. Tallow again, with the patience of a woman who had buried a husband exactly like him.

Dell glared at the shrine. The shrine glowed back, softly, unbothered, the way it had glowed — or rather hadn't — for as long as anyone could remember, until two nights ago. The stones were older than the village. Older than the king's line, the peddlers claimed, though peddlers claimed everything. They were smooth and nameless, worn blank, like every shrine in the valley: no faces carved, no names inscribed, just shapes suggesting that somebody had once been meant to stand there.

"You're not the Lady," Dell told the stones. "We'd know. She's in the songs."

The glow warmed slightly, or he imagined it did.

"And you're not the Horned One, neither, because he's a story for frightening children off the border hills, and stories don't fix barley." He shifted his grip on the crook. "So what are you?"

For a long moment, nothing. The villagers murmured among themselves. Somewhere a rooster crowed with unusual confidence.

Then, faintly — so faintly that later Dell would spend considerable effort convincing himself he'd imagined it — the air near the stones pressed inward, the way it does before thunder, and a sensation passed through the gathering like a shiver through a flock. Not words. Nothing so generous as words. Just an impression, brief and vast, of something on the other side of a wall becoming aware that the wall had grown thin. Of attention, ancient and patient and *hungry in a polite way*, turning toward the valley like a head turning toward a sound.

Mrs. Tallow gripped Dell's arm. Several people knelt. One woman began, tentatively, to pray aloud, and the glow brightened around her voice like a plant leaning toward sun.

Dell did not kneel. But he did, quietly, take one step backward, and plant his crook a little deeper, and decide that perhaps his accounts with the sky were not quite as settled as he'd believed.

"That's what I thought," he muttered. "Nobody knocks like that unless they've been waiting a long time outside."

* * *

By evening, the whole valley knew, because the whole valley had eyes, and because Greta the peddler had walked end to end of it collecting news the way other people collected coins.

Greta held court in the inn at Lowmarsh that night, a mug of the suspiciously excellent ale in front of her, and laid it all out for the room: the barley at Hale's farm, healed overnight. The lambs born at Middle Reach, every single one alive, which had never happened, not once in living memory. The well at Coldbrook running sweet again after years of tasting of iron. The lame Ashby girl walking circles around her yard like a windmill. Fevered children cooling. Aching joints easing. And every shrine from the western farms to the border stones glowing like coals under ash.

"So either the valley's finally having a good year," Greta concluded, "or something's arrived and it's in a giving mood."

"A good mood," corrected the innkeeper, wiping mugs. "A giving mood implies it might stop."

"Everything stops," said Greta, who had peddled long enough to distrust abundance on principle. "Question is what it charges when it does."

The room argued happily for an hour — hope, in the valley, was a rare vintage and people meant to drink it slow — but underneath the argument, in every corner of it, ran the same question, asked in different words by different mouths. The miller asked it as *where's it from*. The innkeeper's daughter asked it as *why now, after all these years*. Old Dell, who had walked over from Stony Ford specifically to be contrary in a larger audience, asked it loudest of all:

"*Who is answering?*"

And no one could say. That was the strange heart of the whole shining day: gifts had arrived, addressed to everyone, signed by no one. The valley had lived so long inside its seal — no letters out, no travelers in, no gods answering, no magic at all, just fog and fields and the slow faithful round of years — that its people had forgotten what it felt like to be heard. Now they were heard. Every prayer, even the careless ones, even the ones muttered into ale. Everything spoken to the dark came back changed.

Which was wonderful.

And also, if a body thought about it too long — which most bodies were carefully not doing — was the sort of thing that happened to people right before the bill arrived.

* * *

Oscar heard about all of it secondhand, in fragments, while he sat up past midnight in the stable loft with a candle stub and a goat.

The goat was named Buttercup, and she was dying, and had been dying for a week in the slow, shrinking way goats have, and Oscar had carried her up to the loft himself because the ground was cold and he couldn't bear it. He'd fed her warm mash from his hand. He'd talked to her. And tonight, hearing the others below carry on about miracles — the barley, the girl, the glowing stones, the whole valley drunk on answered wishes — he had done something he wasn't sure he was allowed to do.

He had prayed. Properly. On his knees, candle lit, hands folded the way the old women did at the blank shrines, whispering into the dark above the sleeping goat:

*Please. Please, whoever's listening now — everyone says somebody's listening now — please let her live. She's mine. I'll owe. I'll pay anything. Please.*

The candle flame bent toward his mouth.

Not flickered — bent, deliberately, the way a flower turns, leaning in toward his whisper as though his voice were warmth and the flame were cold and starving. Oscar stopped speaking. The flame stayed leaned, quivering, attentive, aimed at his lips like an ear.

"Buttercup's breathing," he said aloud, very quietly, testing. "Did you hear me? Is that — are you still there?"

The flame straightened slowly, innocently, a candle again. Below in the stable, Buttercup shifted in her straw, and her breathing — Oscar climbed down and pressed his ear to her ribs with his heart hammering — her breathing had changed. Deeper. Steadier. Not well, not yet, but *turned*, the way a fever turns at the hour when fevers decide things.

Oscar sat back on his heels in the straw, looking up through the loft boards at nothing, feeling the largest feeling of his life arrive and refuse to introduce itself.

Everyone in the valley had gotten something today. Golden fields, strong legs, sweet wells, whole children. And Oscar had gotten — what? A leaning flame. A listening dark. A goat with a chance.

An answer.

He blew out the candle, and in the dark, without meaning to, without deciding to, he whispered: "*Thank you.*"

Somewhere far to the north, past the farms and the shrine-stones and the sleeping villages, against the border hills where the fog lay deepest, something that had waited a very long time heard him — and smiled, in whatever way such things smile, and began, patiently, to count.
