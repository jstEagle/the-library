## 7. The God at the Border Stone

Oscar had never been good at keeping still, and the kitchen had never been good at letting him forget it.

"You'll scrub that pot until it shines," Cook had said that morning, jabbing a spoon at him like a general dispatching troops. "Shines like the moon, do you hear me? And then you'll peel the winter squash, and then—" she'd narrowed her eyes, "—you'll stop looking out that window every time a bird goes by."

"I wasn't looking out the window," Oscar said.

"You were born looking out the window. I've seen your birth-face in every face you've made since." She turned back to her dough with the finality of a mountain settling. "The valley doesn't need gawking at, boy. It needs feeding."

That was the trouble with the Valley of the Unnamed, as far as Oscar could tell: nobody in it ever looked *out*. They looked at their bread, their boots, their ledgers, their neighbors' fences. They looked at the fog that rolled down off the hills each evening like a housecat claiming a chair. But out? Out was a direction people's eyes slid off of, the way a hand slides off wet soap. There was even a word for it—or there wasn't a word for it, which amounted to the same thing. When Oscar asked Pell the archivist what lay beyond the border stones, Pell had looked at him over his spectacles and said, "Beyond?" as though Oscar had asked what came after the end of soup.

But something had changed. Oscar felt it in his teeth the way you feel a storm coming, or a tune you can't name. The candle flame had bent toward his voice three nights ago when he'd prayed over poor Bramble the goat, and the goat had lived, and Cook had called it a miracle of good nursing, and Oscar had let her believe it because the alternative—that he had *done* something, that something had heard him—was too large to hold in a kitchen.

He'd tried not thinking about it. He'd lasted four days, which for Oscar was a personal record in the discipline of not-thinking.

So on the fifth day, when Cook sent him to the far pasture with a wheel of cheese for the shepherd's family—a legitimate errand, an errand with witnesses, an errand he could point to later if anyone asked—he went. And then he kept going.

* * *

The border stones were nothing like he'd imagined, which was fitting, since he'd never been able to imagine them at all. He'd pictured walls, maybe, or gates, or at minimum a fence with an attitude. Instead there were just seven gray stones standing in a loose ring on a bald hilltop, each about as tall as a man, worn smooth as river pebbles. Sheep-cropped grass ran right up to them and stopped, as if the grass itself had agreed to a treaty. Beyond the ring, the land went on—hills, a thread of stream, more hills—but it went on *wrong*, somehow. Flatter. Quieter. Like a painting of land rather than land itself.

Oscar stood at the edge of the ring and listened.

Wind. A lark somewhere, doing its show-off spiraling song. His own heart, which had gotten loud and rude about the whole enterprise.

"Hello?" he said, and immediately felt foolish, because who was he greeting? Stones?

The wind stopped.

Not died down. *Stopped*, all at once, the way a conversation stops when someone important walks into a room. The lark quit mid-spiral. Even the stream seemed to lower its voice. And in the new silence, Oscar became aware that he was being watched—not from any one direction, but from all of them at once, the way you feel watched in a dream.

"Hello," said a voice, and it came from everywhere and nowhere and, when he finally turned toward it, from a figure standing just inside the stone ring that had absolutely not been standing there a moment before.

Oscar did not scream, which he would later consider one of the great achievements of his life. What he did instead was drop the cheese.

The figure caught it.

It moved like water deciding to be a person: one moment empty air, the next a tall shape wrapped in a cloak the color of late autumn, and where a head should have been there rose a crown of antlers—vast, branching things, wider than the figure's shoulders, hung with pale lichen and a few stubborn leaves that should have fallen weeks ago. Its face, beneath the antlers, was long and gray-brown and calm, with dark liquid eyes set deep, and its expression was the most unsettling thing about it, because the expression was *patience*. Not menace. Not hunger. Patience, the way a stone is patient, the way a season is patient.

"Little borrower," the god said—it was unmistakably a god; Oscar's spine knew before his mind did—and it held out the cheese with two long fingers, delicate as a heron's.

"I'm not—I didn't borrow anything," Oscar managed.

"You breathe borrowed air. You eat bread grown from borrowed soil. You live in a country built on a debt so old the creditors forgot the ledger." The god's voice was low and dry and warm, like wind through wheat, like a story told by a fire. "Everyone here is a borrower, little one. Most have simply forgotten whom they borrowed from." It tilted its antlered head. "You haven't forgotten. That is why you walked here. That is why the flame bent toward your voice."

Oscar's mouth went dry. "You heard that."

"I hear everything said in hope." The god settled onto the ground across from him—not sat exactly, but *arranged* itself downward, folding like a heron, until its strange calm face was nearly level with his. Up close, Oscar could smell it: rain on stone, crushed mint, something older underneath, like the smell of a forest floor after ten thousand autumns. "A prayer for a goat. Small. Humble. Sincere." The dark eyes glinted. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone in this valley prayed sincerely?"

"How long?"

"Longer than your kitchen has had a wall. Longer than your king has had a crown. Longer than the fog has had its job." Something moved through the god then—a ripple under the skin of its stillness, grief or anger or both wearing the same coat. "They took my voice away, little borrower. They walled me out of my own body and called the wall peace."

Oscar glanced at the border stones. "Those are... yours? I mean—are you the valley?"

The god was quiet for a moment. When it spoke again, its voice had gone softer, careful, the way people get careful around old wounds. "I am what the valley was, before it was a valley. Before it was anything with edges. And I am patient." It smiled, and the smile was gentle and terrible at once, like spring thaw. "I have learned to be patient. But patience is not the same as forgiveness, and my name is still owed to me."

"My name?" Oscar blinked. "You want my name?"

"No, no." The god made a sound that might have been laughter, dry leaves rustling. "Your name is your own, such as it is. Though—" the dark eyes considered him, unhurried, thorough, the way Cook considered a pot that needed scrubbing, "—such as it is, is the trouble, isn't it? Oscar. Kitchen boy. Orphan of no particular family. The cook calls you 'boy.' The taster calls you 'the boy.' Even the goats know you only by your hands." It leaned closer, and the antlers shifted against the sky like branches in slow wind. "You were given a name the way you were given secondhand boots. Someone else wore out the fit first."

Oscar's throat tightened. It was absurd—he'd never told anyone how much that chafed, how some nights he lay in the loft above the kitchen turning his own name over like a coin that wouldn't spend—but the god's voice found the sore place in him the way water finds a crack in stone.

"What would you give me instead?" he asked, and hated how eager he sounded, and kept talking anyway. "What kind of name does a—a god give?"

"A true one." The god spread its long fingers, and between them the air gathered, faintly luminous, like breath on a cold morning. "Names are not decorations, little borrower. They are hinges. A true name opens the thing it names. Your mother's mother knew this; your valley's founders knew it; that is precisely why they stole mine and locked it behind their blood." The light between its fingers pulsed, soft as a heartbeat. "I cannot give you what is owed to me. But I can give you a small piece of myself. A splinter of true naming. Whispered so only you will hear it. It will not make you a king or a sorcerer. It will make you *lucky*. Luck is only the world remembering your name when you call on it."

"And the cost?" Oscar asked, because Cook had raised him to always ask about the cost, usually while pointing at the thing he'd broken.

The god regarded him with something that looked almost like approval. "Ah. You ask about the cost. Good. The ones who don't ask are the ones who pay double." It folded its fingers closed around the little light. "Everything borrowed comes due. Luck spent must be replenished—from you, from your years, from whatever you have that I lack. I will not lie to you: magic is a loan, and I am a lender, and lenders keep books." A pause, and then, gentler: "But I will tell you also that I am not hungry the way the stories say gods are hungry. I have been locked out of my own house for longer than your people have had names to lose. I do not want worship. I want *acknowledgment*. I want to be remembered by the country that forgot it was standing on me." The antlers dipped, almost a bow. "And I confess a fondness for the small ones. The kitchen boys. The goat-prayers. The borrowers who ask about the cost."

The light between its fingers waited.

Oscar looked at it for a long time. Behind him, the flat wrong hills went on being flat and wrong. Ahead of him, somewhere back down the track, the kitchen waited with its pots and its steam and Cook's spoon and Mara saving him extra bread when she thought he wasn't looking. Everything safe. Everything small. Everything secondhand.

*Someone else wore out the fit first.*

"If I take it," he said slowly, "do I owe you right away? Or—"

"When you spend it. Only when you spend it. And only what the spending costs—no more, no less. I keep honest books, little borrower. It is the one virtue patience teaches." The god extended its closed hand, and the light leaked warm between the gray fingers. "Take it or leave it. I have waited centuries. I can wait while a boy thinks."

That was the moment Oscar understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he was going to take it. He'd known since the flame bent. Maybe since birth. Some part of him had been walking toward this hilltop his whole life, the way rivers walk toward the sea without ever once consulting a map.

He reached out.

The god opened its fingers, and instead of placing the light in his palm, it leaned close—antlers blotting out the sky, rain-and-mint smell flooding his nose—and whispered against his ear a sound that was not quite a word and not quite a note, something that hooked into a place behind his sternum and settled there, warm, like a coal wrapped in wool. He couldn't repeat it. He couldn't write it down. He knew, with absolute certainty, that he would never forget it.

"There," the god murmured, drawing back. Its smile was softer now, and sadder, and Oscar would spend many nights afterward wondering which of those was real. "Now the world knows you, Oscar of the Kitchen. Try not to break anything important."

"That's exactly what everyone's always afraid I'll do," Oscar said.

"Yes," said the god, rising, unfolding upward until the antlers brushed the clouds that hadn't been there a minute ago. "That is generally how the important things get broken. And mended." It began to thin at the edges, becoming hill-colored, becoming suggestion. "Go home, little borrower. Mind the fog. And when the moon looks wrong to you—when it counts past twelve—remember that you knew me first, and kindly."

"Kindly what?"

But the hilltop was empty except for seven gray stones, a dropped wheel of cheese, and a lark resuming its spiral as if nothing in the world had ever paused.

* * *

The walk back took half the time it should have. Oscar noticed this around the second mile, when he cleared a stile he usually had to wrestle, and his boot found a firm stone in the middle of a mud patch he'd sunk in a hundred times, and a blackberry bramble that normally ambushed travelers offered him its fruit at exactly mouth height, ripe and sweet.

Luck. Warm in his chest, quiet as a banked fire.

By the time the palace kitchens rose out of the evening fog, he'd decided on a policy of telling no one anything, which lasted approximately eleven seconds.

"You're late," said Mara, catching him at the door with flour on her arms and suspicion in her eyes. "Cook's been asking. And you've got—" she frowned, leaning closer, "—leaves in your hair. And you smell like rain. It hasn't rained."

"It's going to," Oscar said, which was true, and also felt suddenly, enormously insufficient, because how could he explain any of it—the antlers, the patience, the whisper behind his sternum—without sounding like a boy who'd eaten the wrong mushroom?

Mara studied him the way she studied the king's food: thoroughly, professionally, hunting for poison. Whatever she saw made her frown deepen rather than clear.

"Oscar," she said quietly, "what did you do?"

And that was when the strangest thing happened. The kitchen cat, a fat orange tyrant named Duchess who had ignored Oscar for six years, rose from her basket, crossed the room, and wound herself around his ankles, purring like a small thunderstorm.

Mara stared. Oscar stared.

"Lucky," he said weakly. "I'm just having a lucky day."

Above them, through the high kitchen window, the moon was rising over the fog—and Oscar counted, without meaning to, the way the god had told him to count—and got thirteen.

He didn't tell Mara that either. But he thought, all through supper service, all through the scrubbing and the stacking, about a voice like wind through wheat saying *my name is still owed to me*, and about the thirteenth moon climbing fat and patient over the hills, and about the fact that luck, however small, is a loan—

—and somewhere out past the border stones, in the dark between the hills, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, very gently, to count along with the moon.
