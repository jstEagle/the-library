## 8. Poison, or Prayer

The king's breakfast arrived at half past seven, as it always did, carried up the back stairs by a boy whose name I had never learned because he never stayed long enough to be worth learning. The palace went through kitchen boys the way other households went through candles. Oscar was different — Oscar had lasted four months now, which in palace time made him practically furniture — but this morning he was not the one carrying the tray. This morning the tray came with Cook herself, which was the first wrong thing.

Cook did not carry trays. Cook had not carried a tray since the coronation of nobody, which was to say since before I was born. She stood in my doorway holding the silver dome with both hands, as if it might fly away from her, and her face was the color of old porridge.

"Mara," she said. "Taste it twice."

I looked at her. "I always taste it twice."

"Taste it *twice*," she said again, with the particular emphasis of a woman who has decided that repetition is a form of prayer, and set the tray down on my little table and left without waiting for me to lift the dome.

The breakfast was the same as every morning: porridge with honey, soft-boiled eggs, brown bread, tea brewed weak because strong tea made His Majesty philosophical and philosophy made him weep. I tasted everything twice. The porridge was porridge. The honey was honey, faintly of clover, faintly of the hives on the south wall where the bees had been dying for thirty years and refusing to entirely stop. The eggs were eggs. The bread was bread. The tea was tea.

Nothing was poisoned. I would have known. I have eaten enough poison in my life to recognize its handwriting — the bitter signature of monkshood, the sweet lie of oleander, the numbing courtesy of hemlock water-dropwort, which apologizes to your tongue even as it kills you. My mother fed me all of them, in doses small enough to survive and large enough to learn, until my body became a library of poisons and every new one was simply a book I'd already read.

So when nothing on the tray so much as whispered of harm, I should have felt the ordinary satisfaction of a job well done, the quiet pride I took in being the most invisible and most necessary person in a palace full of people who were neither.

Instead I stood over the empty plates with my tongue still cataloguing and felt the second wrong thing settle into my stomach like a swallowed stone.

Because Cook had been right to be afraid. I just didn't know yet how she knew.

* * *

The king's name was Aldous, though almost no one called him anything at all. In the Valley of the Unnamed you learned early not to say names aloud unless they had been given to you deliberately, wrapped in cloth, so to speak, by their owner. The king had given his to no one in forty years, not since the fog thickened and his mind went soft as winter pears. He ruled nothing but fog, as everyone said, and everyone said it fondly, the way you speak of an old dog that barks at chairs.

He received me in the morning room because the throne room was being swept, which it always was, perpetually, by servants dusting a throne that had never once been sat upon in majesty. He was at the window with his hands behind his back, looking out at the garden, and when I came in he turned and smiled at me, and the smile was wrong.

It was too clear. For years his eyes had been the eyes of a man looking through frosted glass at a world he'd misplaced. Now they focused. They focused on me with an attention that made my skin prickle, the way a room prickles before lightning.

"You're the taster," he said. "You're the girl who eats my death for me."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Come here." He sat down heavily in his chair and gestured at the breakfast things, untouched. "You ate all of it?"

"Every bite, sire. Twice."

"And?"

"And nothing," I said. "It was clean. It was good. The honey's particularly fine this year."

Something moved across his face — not a cloud exactly, more like the memory of a cloud, a shadow remembering how shadows used to work. He put one hand flat on the table, and I saw that it trembled, and the trembling was not age. Age trembles slowly, like a branch in wind. This trembled fast, like something trapped.

"It started an hour after breakfast," he said. "A coldness. Here." He touched his breastbone. "Like swallowing winter. And then the dreams began."

"What dreams, sire?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and I understood suddenly why kings keep tasters, and it is not only for poison. It is because a king cannot afford to be seen afraid, and a taster is already paid to know what fear looks like from the inside.

"I dreamed of my grandmother," he said. "She has been dead sixty years. She stood at the foot of my bed and she said, *Aldous, we are counting.* And I said, counting what? And she said—" 

His voice failed. Not from weakness. From the specific failure of a man trying to repeat words that his mouth knows are dangerous.

"She said, *counting the moons, dear. We miscounted. There were always thirteen.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere below us a pot lid clattered, ordinary sound, blessedly ordinary.

"And then," the king said, "the coldness got into the dream, and my grandmother reached out her hand, and she had no name written anywhere on her, do you understand, no name anywhere, and she said, *you should have kept better books,* and I woke up freezing in July."

"Sire," I said carefully, "has anyone else—"

"No one else dreams it. No one else is cold. Pell thinks I'm mad, and Vess thinks I'm lying, and both of them think the other is worse." He laughed, a dry papery sound. "But you. You eat poison every day of your life and it slides off you like rain off a slate roof. You're the only person in this palace who knows what it feels like when something gets into the body that shouldn't be there."

I did know. That was the whole of my life's education: knowing.

"And?" I asked, though I dreaded the answer.

"And it doesn't feel like poison," he said. "Poison is rude. Poison shoves. Whatever this is—" he pressed his hand again to his chest, over the heart, over the place where a man keeps his prayers whether or not he believes in them — "whatever this is, it was *invited*."

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what I do best, which is being unnoticed in useful places. I was in the kitchens when the physician came down to argue with Cook about the king's diet, and I was behind the flour barrels when the argument turned, as arguments in that palace always eventually turned, to blame.

"He ate nothing," the physician said. "His food was tasted. Tasted twice, I'm told, by the best taster the valley's ever produced. So either your kitchen has finally managed to poison someone with clean food, or—"

"Or what?" Cook demanded.

"Or the sickness didn't come through the food at all."

There was a silence in which I could hear the flour settling.

"That's superstition," Cook said at last, but her voice wobbled on the word, the way voices do when they're holding up something heavy and pretending it's light.

"Everything in this valley is superstition," the physician said wearily. "That's rather the point of the valley. But I'll tell you what isn't superstition: his pulse. I've doctored this man for thirty years. Poisons have signatures. This has none. Whatever is killing him, it isn't in any of my books." A pause. "Though I'll tell you something strange, and you can call it superstition if it makes you feel better. When I listened to his heart this morning, I could have sworn — only sworn, mind — that there were two rhythms. His own, and something underneath it. Slower. Patient. Like a second heartbeat that had been there a long while and only recently decided to make itself heard."

Behind the flour barrels, my blood went cold in a way no poison had ever taught it to go cold.

Two heartbeats. An illness with no signature. A coldness that arrived an hour after a clean meal. A dead grandmother saying *we miscounted.*

And underneath all of it, arriving quietly, the way dread arrives — not all at once but floor by floor, like water rising in a house —

Oscar. The kitchen boy who prayed over a dying goat. The boy who told me afterward, laughing, embarrassed, that the candle flame had bent toward his voice like a listener leaning in. I had laughed with him. I had told him he had a good imagination.

Something had answered him. Something had begun answering, all over the valley, and the seal my ancestors had bought with their names — with *my* name — was cracking, and the first crack had opened directly under the roof of the only person I was paid to protect.

* * *

I found him at dusk in the goat pen, which was really a corner of the yard with a fence around it, mucking straw. He worked with his sleeves rolled up, and I noticed — the way you notice a thing your body understands before your mind consents to — that his hands were steady. Too steady. Kitchen boys' hands shake; they're young and they're tired and they hold too many hot things. Oscar's hands moved like a surgeon's, like a clock's, like something that had been wound by a careful finger.

"The king is ill," I said.

He looked up. In the last light his face was open and guileless and fourteen years old, and I hated how much I wanted it to stay that way.

"I heard. Is it bad?"

"He ate nothing harmful. I tasted everything twice."

"Well." He shrugged, cheerful. "Then maybe he's just old. Old happens. Not everything's poison, Mara."

"Not everything's poison," I agreed. "Some things are prayer."

The shovel stopped moving.

The silence between us stretched long enough for the first star to come out over the wall — one star, then, absurdly, a second beside it, close together, like two eyes opening. I had lived my whole life under a sky with a fixed number of stars. Everyone here had. We counted them as children the way children elsewhere count sheep. There were three hundred and twelve. There had always been three hundred and twelve.

There were now three hundred and fourteen.

"Mara," Oscar said, very quietly, "why are you looking at the sky like that?"

"Count later," I said, and hated myself for the gentleness I was about to ruin. "Oscar. The night the goat died. What exactly did you ask for?"

His face did something complicated — the look of a boy deciding between a lie and a truth, weighing them in those too-steady hands. "I asked for a miracle," he said finally. "I didn't mean it. You don't mean prayers, everyone knows you don't mean them, that's what makes them safe."

"And something answered."

"A candle bent," he said. "That's all. Candle flames bend. Drafts exist." But he wasn't looking at me when he said it. He was looking at his own hands, turning them over, examining the palms as though checking them for stains.

"How is the goat?" I asked.

He blinked. "Dead. Dead a week."

"Is it," I said. "Then who's been feeding the goat in the far pen? Because I walked past it this morning, and something in that pen was eating, Oscar, and this valley hasn't held a living goat with those markings since before I was born."

I watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out. Behind him, in the far pen, in the gathering dark, something that had been dead for a week lifted its head, and chewed, and looked at us with the patient, untroubled eyes of an animal that has been brought back by something that does not fully understand goats, or death, or perhaps mercy.

"Oh," Oscar said. Small. Very small. "Oh, no."

"Yes," I said. "And the king is dying of something that was invited in, and I can taste every poison ever brewed and it is worth *nothing*, do you understand? Nothing. My whole life, my mother's whole terrible life of feeding me horrors so that I'd be safe — and the thing killing the king doesn't come on a spoon. It comes on a prayer. And I can't taste prayers."

He was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice that tried for steadiness and mostly found it:

"Then we'd better find someone who can read them."

Which was when we both thought, at the same moment, of the same person — the archivist with the ink-stained fingers and the locked drawers and the guilt he wore like a coat he kept hoping no one would notice —

and behind us, softly, in the dark, the dead goat lay back down to eat,

and above the wall, unremarked by either of us, the moon rose one night fuller than any calendar in the kitchen said it had any right to be.
