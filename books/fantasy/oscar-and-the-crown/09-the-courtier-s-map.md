## 9. The Courtier's Map

Vess Corvane had three rules for acquiring things that did not belong to her, and she had never once broken them.

The first rule was: never let them see you want it. Wanting showed in the hands, in the throat, in the little involuntary lean of the body toward the desired object like a plant toward a window. She had trained herself out of it decades ago, the way one trains out of a regional accent—patiently, and with a certain private satisfaction at each success.

The second rule was: never be the first to say it aloud. There was always someone else willing to name a forbidden thing; let them. Let them take the risk and absorb the scandal, and then you could stand beside them looking reasonable, even reluctant, while the idea took root as though it had been everyone's all along.

The third rule—the one she loved best, the way some people love a difficult child—was: always have a rescue ready. People forgave ambition dressed as salvation. They forgave nearly anything dressed as salvation. A woman who carried you out of a burning house was not asked how she happened to be standing there with a ladder.

Vess was thinking about ladders on the morning she unrolled the map.

She did this alone, in her rooms in the east wing, which were the coldest rooms in the palace because she had asked for them specifically. Cold kept the mind sharp. Cold also discouraged visitors, and Vess Corvane had reached the age—at thirty-one, an age the valley's courtiers spoke of in the hushed tones usually reserved for plague years—where visitors arrived with only two purposes: to pity her, or to propose marriage as a form of pity. She had declined eleven proposals with such exquisite courtesy that three of the men wept, and she counted those tears among her finer achievements.

The map came out of a false bottom in her writing desk, beneath a layer of correspondence she genuinely did not care about. It was made of something that was not quite leather and not quite bark, and it was old in the way that made her fingers feel briefly dishonest just touching it—as though age itself were a lock and she were picking it.

She weighted the corners with her inkwell, a candlestick, a paperweight shaped like a fog-wisp (the palace gift shop sold them; the palace gift shop was, in Vess's opinion, the single most honest institution in the valley, since it sold souvenirs of nothing), and a small silver spoon she had taken from the kitchens purely because no one would ever miss it.

And there it was. Again. She had looked at it forty times now, and it still did the thing to her chest—that tight, bright ache, like the first note of a song remembered from childhood.

The valley, drawn whole. Not the fog-bound smear that appeared on the king's official charts, where the cartographer had clearly given up halfway and written *here be administrative difficulties* across the interior. This was the valley as it must have been before the sealing: rivers named, roads named, towns named, every hill and hollow labeled in a hand so fine it looked embroidered. And along the eastern edge, where the cliffs rose above the border stones, a mark she had come to think of as hers.

A door. Drawn open. With words beneath it in the old script, which Pell would translate for her eventually whether he meant to or not, but which she already understood well enough: *The Way That Was Left.*

A door out of a country that no longer existed. Out of the fog, out of the stasis, out of the gentle suffocating forever of a place where nothing aged properly and nothing changed and the same six conversations were held at every dinner party until the end of time.

Vess wanted out the way other people wanted air.

There was more than escape in it, of course. She was careful with herself, careful to keep the noble portion of the motive topped up like a household accounts ledger. The valley deserved rescuing. Its people deserved to be remembered by the world. Somewhere beyond the borders, history was happening—she was certain of it, the way you are certain of weather behind a wall—and the valley's people were missing all of it, generation after generation, tending shrines to gods who never answered and eating meals that tasted exactly the same as the meals their grandparents had complained about.

Someone had to lead them out. And if, in the leading, Lady Vess Corvane happened to become the woman who had done the impossible thing—the woman whose name went down in whatever histories existed outside—if her name was the one carved over the door, well.

Rescue was not incompatible with legacy. Rescue was *made* of legacy.

* * *

She chose her first recruit with the care of a surgeon choosing a knife.

Master Halloway, the keeper of the granaries, was a widower with soft hands and a hard ledger, and he had one quality Vess prized above all others: he was frightened. Not of her—of the thirteenth moon. He had stood in the counting-house three weeks ago and said, too loudly, "There are thirteen marks on the kitchen calendar and I have checked the arithmetic four times," and the room had gone quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone has noticed the wrong thing and agreed silently never to speak of it again.

Frightened men wanted exits. Exits required patrons. It was nearly mathematics.

She invited him to take wine with her on a grey afternoon, and she did not mention the map at all for the first hour. Instead they discussed grain yields, which in the Valley of the Unnamed meant discussing the same yields as last year, and the year before, and the year before that, back through living memory and beyond it into what Vess privately called the Long Tuesday.

"You seem troubled, Master Halloway," she said at last, pouring his second cup herself—an honor she dispensed like coin.

"Troubled." He laughed, a dry little sound. "I am a man who counts things, my lady. And lately the things refuse to be counted. Thirteen moons. Thirteen. I have begun dreaming in thirteens. My late wife—" he stopped, and his face did something complicated. "My late wife has been dead nine years, and last week I could not recall her voice. Not faded. *Gone.* As if something had reached in and taken it."

"Names fade," Vess said gently. "It's the valley's oldest affliction."

"It wasn't her name I lost." He looked at his wine. "It was worse than a name. A name you can write down somewhere. You can't write down a voice."

Vess felt the familiar cold bright click inside her, the sound of a lock accepting a key. Grief. Grief was the finest lever ever forged. Ambition could be argued with; fear could be soothed; but grief simply *ached*, and aching people would follow anyone who promised the ache meant something.

"Master Halloway," she said, "may I show you something?"

She unrolled the map between the candlesticks, and watched his face do what every face did: first confusion, then recognition, then hunger—the quick animal hunger of a man seeing a picture of food while starving. His hand hovered over the eastern cliffs without touching, the way one hovers over a relic.

"What is this?" he whispered.

"A way out."

"There is no way out. There has never been a way out. The founding treaty—"

"The founding treaty," Vess said, "was written by people who wanted something kept from us. I have spent four years and a great deal of money learning what they kept." She let him look a moment longer, then began rolling the map away, slowly, watching his eyes follow it like a child's follow a departing sweet-cart. "But of course, a thing like this requires discretion. And allies who understand that what I intend is not theft. It is *rescue*."

"Rescue," Halloway repeated. His voice had changed. It was the voice of a man being offered a rope in deep water, and Vess noted, with professional satisfaction, that he had not once asked how she came to have the map, or what she wanted in return. Frightened men rarely did. That was the tragedy of frightened men, and the opportunity.

"I'll need the granary ledgers," she said. "Movement of stores, quietly, over months. If we go, we go fed."

He nodded before she finished speaking.

* * *

The second recruit was harder, because Sergeant Brann Odo was not frightened. He was bored, which was nearly as useful and considerably more dangerous.

Odo commanded the palace guard, insofar as a palace guarding a king who ruled fog required commanding. Twelve men, ceremonial halberds, and a duty roster that had not varied since Odo was a young man. Vess found him on the wall walk at dusk, watching the fog do its slow eternal performance, and stood beside him in companionable silence for a full ten minutes before saying anything. With soldiers, silence was a currency. Spend it first and they trusted your later words at a better exchange rate.

"Do you know what's beyond the fog, Sergeant?" she finally asked.

"No, my lady."

"Neither do I. Isn't that remarkable? Neither does anyone. We are the only country in creation that doesn't know its own neighbors. We don't know if there's war out there, or famine, or wonders. We don't know if our trade goods would make us rich or laughable. We don't know anything except this." She gestured at the fog with a hand that conveyed, elegantly, *all of this, forever.* "You've stood on this wall for twenty-two years, Sergeant. Has the view improved?"

Odo's jaw worked. "There was a night last month," he said, grudgingly, "when the fog thinned near the east stones. Just for a minute. I saw stars I didn't recognize. More of them. A whole sky's worth extra." He turned to look at her, and his eyes were the flat bright eyes of a man who has been hungry without knowing the word. "What do you want, Lady Corvane? Nobody stands on a cold wall to chat."

Straight to it, then. Good. She respected that in a man, and told him so, and then she told him about the door—not showing him the map, not yet; soldiers valued things they hadn't fully seen—and watched boredom curdle into purpose across his broad face like milk left out.

"When?" was all he asked.

"Soon. Before the thirteenth moon fills. Can you have twelve men ready to move on an hour's notice, with reasons to be elsewhere that hold up under questioning?"

"My lady," Odo said, and there was almost a smile in it, the first she'd ever coaxed from him, "my men have been ready to move somewhere for twenty-two years. They'll march on rumor and a strong feeling."

* * *

The third recruit was not recruited so much as collected, the way one collects a stray that keeps appearing at one's door.

Young Tam Wicker, under-clerk to the chancery, had been leaving anonymous notes in Vess's letterbox for a month. The notes were terrible—overwrought, misspelled, sealed with wax he'd clearly colored himself—but their content was invaluable. Tam had access to the chancery's restricted shelves, and Tam had read things, and Tam wanted desperately to matter to someone.

She summoned him to her cold rooms on the pretext of a dictation error, let him stand sweating by the fireless hearth for a suitable interval, and then set the map on the desk between them.

Tam made a sound like a kettle beginning to boil.

"You've seen references to this," Vess said. It was not a question. "In the restricted folios. Don't insult us both by denying it."

"The Door Out of—" he pressed both hands flat on the desk. "Lady Corvane, they burned most of the copies. Pell has fragments. Everyone says Pell has fragments, but nobody can prove—"

"Pell," Vess said, tasting the name, "is an old man who loves his archives more than his country. Old men who love archives can be managed." She watched Tam's face carefully here, because this was the true test, the one that mattered: would the boy flinch at the casual dismissal of the archivist everyone adored?

He didn't flinch. He leaned in.

Vess gave him the smile she kept for moments of confirmed allegiance—warm, brief, devastating—and assigned him his task: copy everything, trust no one, and above all find out what Pell knew about the founding treaty's original terms. Specifically, she wrote on a slip of paper and slid across the desk, *what was given, and by whom, and whether it can be given back.*

Tam tucked the slip into his coat like a love letter and left walking too fast, colliding with a serving girl in the corridor, apologizing to the wall instead of the girl.

Alone again, Vess stood over the map a long while, candlelight moving on the ancient ink.

Three recruits. Grain, steel, and secrets. It was a respectable beginning. But she was honest enough with herself—alone, in the cold, honesty being safe when no one could see—to admit the shape of the hole in her plan.

The map was real. The door was real. But doors locked, and this one, according to every fragment Tam had smuggled her and every inference she'd wrung from Pell's guarded ramblings over four years of strategically shared sherry, was locked with something the valley had very little of.

A name. A true one. Royal blood, the fragments whispered, in the oblique maddening way of documents written by people who assumed their readers would still exist to decode them.

And there was precisely one person in the palace whose blood ran anywhere near the old royal line, whose position gave her access to every room and every ear, whom nobody watched because everybody had learned not to see her—

Vess caught herself leaning toward the thought, toward the name, and straightened, annoyed. Rule one. Never let them see you want it—even when *they* was only yourself.

Still. The taster. The Kavanagh girl. Invisible as smoke and twice as quiet, and carrying, if the genealogies in the chancery basement were half right, the last drop of the founders' blood in the valley.

A key that walked around unnoticed, tasting soup.

Vess rolled up the map and returned it to its false bottom, and sat down to compose an invitation—one of her famous dinners, the kind the court pretended not to covet. She addressed it to the royal kitchens, marked *for the attention of Her Majesty's taster*, and smiled at the small delicious absurdity of the phrase.

If the girl would come willingly, so much the neater.

And if not—well. Rule three. Always have a rescue ready. Vess Corvane had been saving the valley in her imagination for four years, and she had grown very good at it, and she was not a woman who confused the saving with the saved.

Outside her cold windows, the fog performed its evening curtain-call, and above it—thin, early, wrong—the thirteenth moon was already climbing.
