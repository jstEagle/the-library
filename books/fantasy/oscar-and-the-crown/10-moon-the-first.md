## 10. Moon the First

Pell had spent forty-one years in the palace archives, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things about the building he worked in.

First: the archives were older than the palace. This was obvious to anyone who looked at them—the archive wing leaned like a tired grandfather against the palace's more fashionable shoulders, and its floors creaked in a dialect of complaint no younger structure could have mastered.

Second: the king never came down here. Not once. In four decades. Pell had long ago stopped taking it personally and had instead come to regard it as a professional courtesy. Kings and archivists were natural enemies, in a way—both custodians of the past, but one wanted it polished and the other wanted it *accurate*, and accuracy is very rarely flattering.

Third—and this was the one that mattered tonight—the thirteenth moon on the kitchen wall was not a mistake.

Pell knew this because he had just spent six hours proving it, and proving it had made his hands shake so badly that he'd had to set down his magnifying lens twice and breathe at the ceiling like a man talking himself down from a ledge.

He sat now at his worktable with the evidence spread before him in what his late wife would have called "your archaeology of papers," and outside the narrow window the first moon of the count hung over the fog like a coin someone had dropped into milk.

It had started, as most catastrophes in the Valley of the Unnamed did, with a small domestic detail.

* * *

Three days ago, Mara Kavanagh had appeared in his doorway holding a candle stub and wearing the particular expression of a person who has noticed something wrong and is trying to decide whether announcing it will make her ridiculous.

"The kitchen calendar has thirteen moons," she said.

"Thirteen marks," Pell corrected gently, because he was an archivist and correction was his love language. "The moon itself remains regrettably singular."

"You know what I mean."

"I do." He had set down his pen. "And you want me to tell you it's a counting error made by some cook two hundred years ago who couldn't do arithmetic."

"Yes."

"Ah." He had removed his spectacles, which was, those who knew him understood, the equivalent of another man drawing a sword. "Then I'm afraid I'll disappoint you. Come and sit down, Miss Kavanagh. This may take a while."

It had taken three days.

Because here was the thing about the valley's records: they were meticulous. Obsessively, pathologically meticulous. A country erased from every map, sealed against magic, forgotten by the world outside—that kind of existence required bookkeeping. Every birth recorded without a name attached, only a bloodline sigil. Every treaty clause copied in triplicate. Every calendar, every almanac, every farmer's almanac marginalia and monastery fragment and tax ledger, all of it cross-referenced by Pell himself across forty-one years until he could find any document in the dark, which was fortunate, because the lamps in the lower stacks had been temperamental since the reign of the current king's grandfather.

And every single lunar calendar in the collection—every one, going back to the Founding—had thirteen moons.

Not twelve plus an error. Not twelve with a scribe's slip corrected later. Thirteen. Deliberate. Consistent. The thirteenth moon always drawn slightly apart from the others, in a different ink, sometimes in red, sometimes with a small symbol beside it that Pell had catalogued decades ago under the heading *decorative motif, unknown significance* and had felt fine about ever since.

He did not feel fine about it now.

Because three days ago, Mara had also told him—hesitantly, apologetically, the way she told people anything, as though her own observations were an imposition—that the thirteenth mark on the kitchen wall was filling in. That when she'd first noticed it, weeks ago, it had been a thin crescent. And that each night since, it had grown.

Calendars did not do that.

Ink did not do that.

Unless, said a voice in Pell's head that sounded distressingly like his old tutor, the calendar is not recording the moon. Unless the calendar *is* the moon. Or rather—a countdown wearing a calendar's clothes.

* * *

So tonight Pell sat alone in the archives with every founding-era document he possessed arranged around him like a defensive formation, and he read the Treaty of Surrender for perhaps the nine-hundredth time, and for the first time he read it *properly*.

The relevant clause was Clause Nine. It was short. Founding clauses usually were; the founders had understood that precision invites loopholes, and loopholes are how gods get in.

*Let the thirteenth moon stand apart from the twelve,* the clause read, in handwriting so old the letters seemed embarrassed to exist, *and let it fill only when the Lock is broken or the Bargain renounced. While the valley keeps its silence, the thirteenth moon shall keep its darkness.*

Pell read it four times.

Then he took off his spectacles, folded them with great care, placed them on the table, and put his face in his hands.

"While the valley keeps its silence," he said aloud, to nobody, in the tone of a man addressing a room full of dead lawyers. "The thirteenth moon shall keep its darkness."

And the thirteenth moon was not dark anymore. The thirteenth moon was a waxing crescent drawn on a kitchen wall in ink that had been dry for two centuries, and it was growing, and downstairs a kitchen boy had prayed over a dying goat and something had *answered*, and crops across the valley had healed overnight, and shrines that hadn't glowed since before Pell's grandfather's grandfather were glowing now like festival lanterns.

The seal was cracking. Everyone with eyes half-open knew it. What nobody knew—not the courtiers whispering in corridors, not Lady Corvane with her hungry smile, not even the king in his rare lucid hours—was that the crack had a schedule.

When the thirteenth moon filled, the seal would fail. Entirely. Not leak. Not fray. *Fail.* Clause Nine didn't specify what happened after, because the founders had assumed—Pell could see it between the lines, could feel their confidence radiating off the vellum like warmth off a hearth—they had assumed the thirteenth moon would never fill. They had built a clock and hidden the hands and trusted their descendants never to look up.

Their descendants had looked up. Their descendants had a goat-healing kitchen boy and a god at the border stone offering names like a peddler offering ribbons, and a moon on a kitchen wall quietly counting down to the end of two hundred years of safe, gray, fog-wrapped anonymity.

Pell put his spectacles back on. His hands were steadier now, in the way of a man who has finished being frightened and moved on to being busy. Fear was a luxury for people without work to do.

"All right," he said to the empty archive. "All right. Let's find out what happens when it fills."

* * *

The problem, he discovered over the next several hours, was that the founders had been maddeningly vague on consequences. Clause Nine ended at the failure. Everything after was blank space, and then Clause Ten, which concerned fishing rights along the western fog-line and was, Pell felt, a catastrophic editorial decision.

But there were other documents. There were always other documents; that was the archivist's creed and his consolation. Marginalia. Letters. The private papers of the first Warden of the Seal—a woman named, ironically, nothing at all, whose nameless state Pell had always found professionally inconvenient and personally moving.

Her journal survived in fragments. And in one fragment, water-stained and brittle, wedged inside a ledger of grain tariffs where some terrified descendant had clearly hidden it, Pell found this:

*My granddaughter asks what we are waiting for. I tell her: nothing. We are not waiting. We are holding. But she asks what happens if we stop holding, and I cannot lie to her, so I tell her the truth my mother told me: the valley is a held breath. If we let it out, the world breathes us back. Everything we gave up comes home to be counted. Every name returns to its owner. And the owner of ours—*

The page tore there. Pell turned it over. The reverse was blank except for one line, pressed hard enough to emboss:

*—the owner of ours has been patient for a very long time, and patience is not the same as forgiveness.*

Pell sat back.

"Everything we gave up comes home to be counted," he repeated softly. "Every name returns to its owner."

He thought of the plague that wasn't happening yet but would—he was suddenly, horribly certain of it—of villagers waking unable to remember their own names, of the god at the border collecting early, gathering what it was owed before the door of the cage swung wide. He thought of Mara Kavanagh, born nameless, her family listed in the founding rolls among the surrendered, her bloodline the lock itself. If every name returned to its owner—

Whose name was the land's? Who had the founders stolen it from?

He had a suspicion. It had been forming for three days, cold and slow, like frost on a window. He wrote it down anyway, because writing a thing down was how Pell distinguished between a fear and a fact, and he filed the note in a drawer marked PERSONAL, UNSORTED, DO NOT SHOW ANYONE, which was the fullest drawer in the archive.

* * *

Near dawn, footsteps on the archive stairs. Light ones, careful of the creaking boards—she'd learned the boards' language months ago. Pell allowed himself a small smile before arranging his face into appropriate gravity.

Mara came in carrying a covered dish. Of course she did. She was the royal food-taster; she expressed concern through comestibles.

"You haven't eaten," she said. "Cook sent up stew. I tasted it. You're welcome."

"You taste everything?"

"Everything. Occupational habit." She set it down, glanced at the documents covering his table, and her eyes snagged on the torn journal page. "You've been up all night."

"I've been up all *several* nights." He pulled out the chair opposite. "Sit, Miss Kavanagh. I have news, and you're not going to like it, and I'd rather you were sitting down and armed with stew."

She sat. She waited. She was very good at waiting; he'd noticed that about her. The whole valley was good at waiting—it was practically the national industry—but Mara waited like someone who had practiced on purpose.

"The thirteenth moon," Pell began, "is real."

"I know. I told you—"

"No. Listen." He leaned forward. "I don't mean the mark on your wall. I mean it is a *real* moon, Miss Kavanagh. A thirteenth moon written into the founding treaty itself, standing apart from the twelve, dark for two hundred years by design. It is not decoration. It is not a cook's error. It is a countdown, and it has begun, and according to Clause Nine of the Treaty of Surrender—" he tapped the vellum—"when it fills, the seal fails. Completely. Irreversibly. The valley opens whether we wish it or not."

Mara had gone very still. Tasters, Pell reflected, probably developed stillness the same way soldiers developed reflexes.

"How long?" she asked.

He had done the arithmetic four times, hoping for a different answer each time, and gotten the same answer each time, because arithmetic was loyal that way.

"Twelve turns of the moon," he said. "A year. Perhaps a few weeks less—the mark on your wall is already ahead of the sky-moon, which suggests the countdown isn't waiting politely for astronomical agreement. Call it eleven months and some change." He heard his own voice go gentle. "Miss Kavanagh, I need you to understand what I'm saying. For two hundred years this valley has been a held breath. In eleven months, we exhale. Everything we gave up comes home to be counted. Every name returns to its owner."

"And our names?" Her hand had drifted, unconsciously, to her own throat. "We don't have any. We traded them. What happens to people who owe a debt they already paid?"

"That," Pell said, "is the question I've been sitting with all night, and I confess I don't have an answer. Only a suspicion." He hesitated. He was not, by nature, a man who shared suspicions before breakfast; suspicions unverified were gossip with a diploma. But the look on her face—the taster's look, the woman-who-swallows-poison-for-a-living look, calm on top and braced underneath—made him decide she could carry it.

"My suspicion," he said, "is that the bargain was never quite what the treaty says it was. The treaty calls it a trade. Names given, magic barred, fair exchange. But I think—" he touched the torn journal page—"I think our ancestors didn't buy safety. I think they stole it. I think the names weren't payment. I think they were *lock components*. And I think there is an owner somewhere beyond the border stones who has been waiting two hundred years for the lock to open, and who is not currently in a forgiving mood."

Outside the window, the first moon of the count hung pale and patient over the fog.

Mara looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked at the stew, pushed the bowl toward him, and said, in a voice that had decided something:

"Eat your breakfast, Master Pell. And then you're going to show me every document in this archive that mentions my family."

Pell blinked. "May I ask why?"

"Because if there's an owner coming to collect," Mara Kavanagh said, rising, pulling her shawl tight, "then somebody in this valley had better figure out what exactly we're holding in pawn."

She was gone down the stairs before he could answer, light on the creaking boards, and Pell sat alone amid his papers with the stew steaming and the dawn coming up gray, and thought: *eleven months.*

Then he thought: *she asked about her family.*

Then he thought, with the specific dread of a man who has read too many founding documents: *oh no. She doesn't know yet what her family actually did.*

He picked up his spoon. It was excellent stew. Cook always excelled under pressure, and apparently the whole valley could smell the pressure coming, the way animals smell weather.

Above the fog, the first moon climbed a degree higher, unhurried, keeping perfect count.
