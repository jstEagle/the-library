## 11. Immunity

The first thing Mara ever remembered tasting was hemlock.

She was four years old, sitting on a stool too tall for her in the kitchen of a house that leaned against the palace wall like an old friend leaning on a younger one, and her mother set down a cup of goat's milk with something dark and syrupy stirred into it, and said, "Drink that, love, and then we'll have honey bread."

Mara drank it. The world went strange at the edges — the firelight went soft and swimmy, her fingers felt very far away, and her mother's voice came to her as if through a wall of wool: *Stay awake, Mara. Stay with me. Count the spoons on the wall.*

She counted. There were forty-one spoons hanging from their hooks above the hearth, and she counted them eleven times before the strangeness let go of her, and by the time she finished, there was honey bread waiting, thick with butter, and her mother's hand heavy and warm on her head.

"Good girl," said Sera Kavanagh, who had never once in her life said *poor girl*, not even when Mara wept into the honey bread, which she did, that first time, for nearly an hour.

"Why did it hurt?" Mara asked.

"It didn't hurt," her mother said. "It lied to you. There's a difference. Now eat your bread."

* * *

By six, Mara could tell you what had been put in her porridge by smell alone. By seven, she could name the liar and describe exactly how it would lie.

"Bittercress root," she'd announce, pushing the bowl away. "Makes your tongue feel like it's wearing mittens."

"Foxglove," she'd say, wrinkling her nose at the tea. "Sweet at first, then it starts knocking on your heart like a rude visitor."

"Wolfsbane," she'd whisper, awed, because wolfsbane was expensive and her mother only bought it twice a year, "makes everything go cold, starting with your feet."

Her mother kept a ledger — a fat, grease-spotted book with a cracked spine — and after every dose she made Mara write down what she'd been given, how much, and what it did to her, in her own wobbly hand. *Bittercress, one spoonful, tongue mittens, gone by supper.* The ledger grew with her. It was, though neither of them said so out loud, a diary written in poison instead of ink.

"You're building a wall," her mother told her once, when Mara was eight and furious about the arsenic in the apple slices. "Brick by brick. Every poison is a brick. One day nobody will be able to touch you."

"That's not fair," Mara said, because eight-year-olds are philosophers of fairness. "Nobody else has to eat arsenic apples."

"Nobody else," her mother said, slicing another apple with terrible calm, "is going to matter as much as you do."

Mara didn't understand that then. She understood it later, much later, standing over a king's breakfast tray with a silver spoon, but by then it was too late to be angry about it properly.

* * *

There were good days too. That's the part people never believe, when she tells them — and she tells almost no one. But there were whole weeks when nothing dangerous crossed the threshold, and her mother taught her other things: how to read a face while pretending to look at the cheese; how to stand so that no one remembered she was in the room; how to walk through a market and leave behind her, in the words of her mother, "less impression than a cat."

"A cat leaves more," Sera said. "A cat wants to be fed. You want nothing. Wanting is how they find you."

"What if I want honey bread?"

"Then want it quietly."

So Mara learned to want things quietly, which is a skill that sounds small until you try to live without it. She learned to hold her wanting behind her teeth like a coin held under the tongue, warming it, never spending it. And because her mother praised her — sparingly, but truly, and always with food — Mara came to think of endurance as a kind of belonging. The poisons were terrible guests, but they came every day, regular as family, and after a while she stopped being afraid of them and started, in a way she never admitted to anyone, being fond of them. Foxglove's rude knock. Wolfsbane's cold feet. Hemlock's woolly dark. They knew her. They visited her. In a valley where names were traded away before birth and everyone drifted through life half-erased, Mara Kavanagh had at least this: even the deadliest things in creation recognized her on sight and, eventually, gave up on her entirely.

"You're the only person in the Valley of the Unnamed who is completely herself," her mother said once, brushing Mara's hair by the fire. "Do you know why? Because everything that could change you has already tried, and lost."

"I'm not sure that counts as being myself," Mara said, ten years old and pedantic. "Maybe it just means I'm stubborn."

Her mother laughed — a rare sound, low and surprised, as if it had escaped rather than been released. "Stubbornness is the oldest recipe there is, love. Everything else is seasoning."

* * *

At twelve, the doses got serious.

Up until then it had been kitchen-garden stuff, the sort of harm a clever woman could buy or grow without questions. But the winter Mara turned twelve, a man came to the house — gray-cloaked, soft-voiced, with the careful hands of someone who handled valuable things — and he brought a lacquered case, and inside the case were vials, and inside the vials were things with names Mara had only read about in her mother's ledger's back pages, the pages headed *Someday.*

"Someday is now," her mother said.

"Who is he?" Mara asked.

"He's the reason we have a roof," her mother said, which was true and also not an answer, and Mara had learned by then not to spend her questions where answers wouldn't grow.

That winter she learned what heartstop felt like (her mother's fist on her sternum, pounding, pounding, shouting *come back, come back, I didn't mean tonight*) and what lungrot felt like (three days of drowning on dry land, her mother sitting up with her the whole time, reading aloud from the ledger so Mara would have something to count) and what the gray vial felt like, the one with no name in the ledger, only a smudge where the label had been scraped off.

The gray vial took two days of her life she never got back. When she surfaced, her mother was asleep sitting up, still holding her hand, and there was honey bread going stale on the table, and Mara understood two things at once, the way children understand big things all at once and then spend decades unpacking them:

One: her mother would kill her slowly, on purpose, out of love.

Two: her mother would die of grief the moment it stopped working.

Neither of those facts made the arsenic apples taste better. But together they made a kind of sense, the way a knot makes sense — ugly, tight, holding.

* * *

At fifteen, the summons came.

Not for her mother — for her. A palace page, all elbows and borrowed importance, standing in the doorway: *The Steward requires the taster's daughter. The old taster's hands shake now.*

"The old taster?" Mara said. "I thought Mother was—"

"Your mother recommended you," the page said, and fled, the way sensible people flee from kitchens.

Mara turned to her mother, who was stirring a pot with the particular serenity she wore whenever she had engineered something.

"You signed me up."

"I mentioned your existence to a woman who mentioned it to a steward. Signing up implies paperwork." Her mother tapped the spoon on the pot rim. "It's the safest post in the palace. Everyone thinks it's the most dangerous, which is why it pays well and why nobody schemes for it. And you'll be invisible, Mara. Perfectly invisible. A girl with a spoon. Nobody looks at a girl with a spoon."

"But you'll be here alone."

"I've been alone my whole life. It's a skill, like yours." Her mother looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment the serenity slipped and something raw showed underneath — pride, terror, love, all fighting for the same window. "You'll do the work better than anyone they've ever had. Not because you're brave. Because you're built. I built you. Every meal was a stone in the wall. Do you hate me for it?"

Mara considered the question honestly, because her mother hated dishonesty worse than insolence.

"Not today," she said. "Ask me again after the first week."

Her mother smiled. "Fair terms. Shake on it."

They shook on it, over the pot, and that was the closest thing to a blessing the Kavanagh women ever exchanged.

* * *

The first week in the palace, Mara tasted for a king who ruled fog, and asked her body afterward: *Well? Anything?*

Nothing. Belladonna in the wine — she caught it by the faintest sweetness underneath, like a lie told beautifully. Ground pearl in the soup, which was less poison than insult. A slow-burning compound in the honey cake that would have taken the king a year to die of and her, she estimated, a bad afternoon.

She wrote it all in her own ledger now, a new book, cleaner than her mother's, and at the end of the week she went home and reported, and her mother listened with her eyes closed like a woman tasting a memory.

"And?" her mother said.

"No bricks needed yet. The wall's holding."

"Good." Her mother opened her eyes. "Now tell me about the cooks. What does the head cook do when he's nervous? Where does the steward keep his keys? Which courtier watches the trays?"

"Mama. I'm a taster, not a spy."

"You're a Kavanagh," her mother said. "Same thing, different hat."

* * *

For nine years, the wall held.

Nine years of trays and spoons and quiet wanting. Nine years of learning the palace the way her mother had taught her to learn markets — by leaving less impression than a cat. Nine years of Sunday visits home, honey bread, ledger comparisons, her mother growing grayer and smaller and no less serene, until the last visit, when her mother handed her the old grease-spotted book itself.

"Keep it," Sera said. "Everything I know is in there. Everything I survived is in there. And—" she hesitated, which was so unlike her that Mara actually looked up, "—there are some recipes in the back I never made. Never will. But they're correct. I checked them three times. If the day comes when you need them, you'll know. If the day doesn't come, burn the book and consider yourself lucky."

"What kind of recipes?"

"The kind a mother hopes her daughter never reads." Her mother closed Mara's fingers around the book. "You were born in a locked country, love, and I spent your whole childhood locking you tighter, because I thought the lock was safety. Lately I've been wondering whether locks keep things out or keep them in. Eat your bread."

That was the last thing her mother ever said to her that made complete sense. Three months later Sera Kavanagh died in her sleep, quietly, wanting nothing, having wanted everything, and Mara found the honey bread recipe memorized and the ledger annotated in margins she is still, years later, discovering — little notes in her mother's cramped hand, *antidote follows, double the milk, watch the color of the lips* — a whole second book hidden inside the first, a mother's voice continuing past death, correcting, protecting, insisting.

* * *

Which brings us, more or less, to now.

To this morning, in fact. To Mara standing over the king's breakfast tray with her silver spoon, in a palace that serves no kingdom, in a valley sealed against magic, on a morning when the kitchen calendar shows thirteen moons where twelve should be and the cook swears it has always been so.

She tastes the porridge. Clean. The tea: clean. The honey cake — and here her hand pauses, because the honey cake smells faintly wrong, not poisoned-wrong, not any of the forty-one liars she knows by heart, but wrong the way the calendar is wrong, the way time itself seems lately to be miscounting.

She tastes it anyway. Built for this. Brick by brick.

And for the first time in her life, Mara Kavanagh tastes something her immunity cannot name — something that slides past the wall her mother built as if the wall isn't there, as if the poison isn't traveling through her body at all but around it, through the room, through the prayer someone whispered somewhere in the dark—

—and far below, in the sickroom, the king begins to cough.
