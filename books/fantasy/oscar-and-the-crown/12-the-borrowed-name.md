## 12. The Borrowed Name

Oscar had not slept properly in nine days.

He knew the number because he counted it against the kitchen calendar on the wall—the one with thirteen moon-marks that Cook insisted had always been there—and because every night since the border stone, sleep arrived like a guest who knocked and then stood on the step, patient, waiting for him to answer. He never answered. He lay in his cot above the stables and listened to the fog press against the shutters, and somewhere out in that fog, he was almost certain, something with antlers and a great deal of patience was also lying awake, thinking about him.

Not hunting him. That was the strange part. He'd tried to make himself afraid of it—properly afraid, the way you're afraid of a bull or a debt collector—but what he felt instead was closer to the feeling of being *remembered*. Like someone had finally said his name correctly after years of everyone else getting it slightly wrong.

Except nobody said his name at all. That was rather the point.

"Oscar! The butter isn't going to churn itself!" Cook's voice came up through the floorboards with the force of a minor avalanche. "And if you've been talking to that goat again I'll have your ears!"

"I'm coming!" he called back, and then, to the goat—who did, in fact, live in his room now, because the palace had more rooms than purposes and no one checked—"She doesn't mean it," he told her. "Mostly."

The goat chewed thoughtfully. She had survived whatever had nearly killed her the night he prayed over her, and she had grown fat and glossy and faintly smug about it, which Oscar chose to interpret as gratitude.

* * *

The morning went the way mornings went in the Valley of the Unnamed: porridge tasted by Mara, bread proved by Cook, floors scrubbed by Oscar, all of it performed for a king who ruled fog and a court that served no one. But today the routine snagged on things.

The first snag: he reached into his pocket for a bit of twine to tie the flour sack and found instead a perfect brass button, polished bright, exactly the size of the loose button hanging off Cook's coat. He tied the sack with the twine from the other pocket and gave Cook the button before she noticed hers was gone, and she squinted at him like he'd grown a second head.

"Where'd you get that?"

"Had it."

"You didn't have it. I've seen your pockets. Your pockets contain string, crumbs, and despair."

"Lucky find," Oscar said, and the word *lucky* sat strangely in his mouth, like a coin under the tongue.

The second snag: the well rope, frayed for weeks, held. Not just held—when he hauled the bucket up, the rope ran smooth through his palms as if newly braided, and when he looked closely the frayed fibers seemed to have *woven themselves back together*, neat as stitching. He looked around. Nobody else had seen. The bucket came up full and cold and he drank from his cupped hands and thought: *this is what it feels like when the world is paying attention to you.*

It was a dangerous thought. He knew that even as he enjoyed it.

The third snag came at midday, and it came wearing Lady Vess Corvane's perfume.

She swept into the kitchen unannounced, which no one did, because Cook guarded that doorway like a dragon guards a hoard of extremely mediocre spoons. But the dragon was at market, and Lady Vess glided between the tables trailing rose-and-iron scent, and stopped in front of Oscar with a smile that had too many teeth in it to be entirely friendly.

"You're the boy who tends the goats."

"And the floors," Oscar said. "And the fires. And occasionally the king's mood, if the king is having one."

"Mm." Her eyes were pale gray and moved over him like a merchant appraising a horse. "You've been lucky lately. I hear things. A button. A rope. Old Marta down the lane says her cat came home after three years, and she lives next door to *you*."

"Cats come home," Oscar said. "It's a whole thing cats do."

"Not here they don't. Nothing leaves this valley and nothing returns to it. Except, apparently, that cat." She leaned in. The rose-and-iron smell got stronger, and underneath it was another smell, older, like a cellar that had been closed a very long time. "Whatever you've found, boy—whatever whispered to you at the stones—I'd think carefully about who else knows. Luck draws attention. And attention, in this valley, is a kind of wound."

She left. Oscar stood holding a wooden spoon he did not remember picking up, and the spoon was warm, and outside the window the fog rolled past the glass like something breathing.

* * *

He went to the border that night. He hadn't decided to; his feet decided while the rest of him was still arguing. The moon was the fourth of thirteen—he'd started counting them on the kitchen wall, marking them secretly beside Cook's marks, because if time was miscounting then somebody ought to keep their own books—and the fog was thin tonight, torn into rags by a wind that smelled of green things, growing things, things that shouldn't be growing in the cold.

The border stone stood where it always stood, a gray lump in a gray field, carved with letters so old they'd worn down to suggestions. Oscar stopped ten feet away.

"You can come out," he said. "I know you're there. You're always there. It's honestly a bit much."

The fog gathered. It took its time about it, the way it took its time about everything, folding itself upward and inward until it had the shape of a figure made of antlers and patience—which is to say, a shape that was mostly suggestion, mostly shadow, with a crown of branches where a head should be, and eyes like two coins at the bottom of a deep well.

"Little borrower," it said. Its voice was the sound wind makes moving through a forest, if the forest were very old and had opinions.

"That's not my name," Oscar said. "Nobody has a name here. That's the whole arrangement. My mother named me a placeholder word and the record-keepers wrote a dash in the ledger and that was that. So you can't call me that."

"No," the god agreed, and there was something in its voice that might have been amusement, or might have been hunger wearing amusement's coat. "But I could give you one."

The field went very quiet. Even the wind stopped to listen.

"A real one," the god said. "Small. True. Whispered so only you can hear it—not written, not spoken aloud, never taken from you because no one else will ever know it exists. A name that fits the shape of you the way water fits a cup. Would you like to hear it, little borrower?"

And here is the thing about Oscar Kell—no, not Kell, the dash, the placeholder, the nothing—that is the thing about Oscar-the-dash: he was not a fool. He had heard the stories Pell told in the archive dust, about bargains and prices, about how the ancestors had traded away names to keep magic out and how everything traded must someday be traded back. He knew gifts from gods were hooks dressed as bread.

But he was also seventeen, and nameless, and tired of being a dash in a ledger, and the god's voice wrapped around the offer like smoke around a candle flame, and the candle was him.

"What does it cost?" he asked. His voice came out smaller than he wanted.

"Ah." The antlered head tilted. Somewhere in the dark, a deer stepped carefully through wet grass. "A good question. The right question. Most borrowers don't ask it until the bill arrives." The god drifted closer, and the air grew warm and green-smelling, like the first day of a spring that hadn't been scheduled. "This costs nothing tonight. It costs what luck always costs: it spends. Every fortunate thing bends the world a little, and the world keeps accounts. Use it gently and the account stays small. Use it greedily—" the coin-eyes glittered "—and you will learn what I learned about debts. They are patient. I am patient. We are old friends, patience and I."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be. Reassurance is for people selling things worth less than they claim." The god extended something—not a hand, but a darkness shaped like the memory of a hand, and resting in it, a single drop of light, small as a seed. "I will tell you the truth, little borrower, because truth is rarer than luck and I have little enough of either to spare: I am owed a great deal, and I am collecting slowly, and one day soon the account comes due for everyone in this valley. When that day comes, you will want to know whose side you stood on. This gift tells you mine. What it asks of yours, we shall discover together."

"That's *definitely* not reassuring."

The god laughed. It sounded like leaves.

"The name, little borrower. Yes or no. The moon is counting, and so am I."

Oscar looked at the drop of light. He thought about the goat dying in his arms and the prayer that had bent the candle. He thought about the dash in the ledger. He thought about Mara, who moved through the palace like a ghost with excellent posture, and how even ghosts deserved better than dashes.

"Yes," he said.

The god leaned down—it had to lean a long way, further than its height suggested, further than geometry strictly allowed—and it put its mouth beside Oscar's ear, and it whispered.

And Oscar understood, in that instant, why the ancestors had given their names away rather than lose them to something like this. Because the name was *true*. It fit him the way water fits a cup. It was three syllables long and shaped like a boy who fed goats and scrubbed floors and kept his own books when the world miscounted, and hearing it was like hearing a door open in a wall he'd lived inside all his life without knowing the wall was there.

Then it was gone—not spoken again, not repeatable, folded away into the place behind his heartbeat where only he could reach it—and the drop of light sank into his palm like a pebble into snow, and the god straightened.

"There," it said, satisfied. "Now you are a *who*, and not a *what*. Spend it wisely."

"Wait," Oscar said. "That's it? No contract? No signature in blood? Don't get me wrong, I'm relieved, but—"

"Borrowers," the god said, already thinning back into the fog, "always want paperwork. Goodnight, little borrower. Mind the thirteenth moon. And mind the lady who smells of iron under her roses—she has been asking questions at the stones, and the stones are starting to answer her."

And then it was fog again, and the field was just a field, and Oscar walked home with a name burning quietly behind his ribs like a coal wrapped in wool.

* * *

The luck got worse. Or better. Or both.

Over the following days, small impossible things accumulated around Oscar like lint around a pocket. He caught a falling jar of preserves one-handed without looking, from across the room. The king's fire, which Oscar laid each evening, burned brighter than any fire in the palace, and the king—mad, fog-ridden, half-gone King Aldous—woke three mornings running lucid and asked, politely, for honey in his tea, which he hadn't wanted in six years. Cook won a bet with the fishmonger on the exact weight of a carp, to the ounce. The stable cat, who hated everyone, slept on Oscar's chest.

Word spread the way word does in a palace with nothing else to talk about. The servants began touching his sleeve as he passed, quick and furtive, the way you touch wood. Someone left a heel of good bread on his cot. Someone else left a sprig of rowan, which was either a blessing or a warning, depending on which grandmother you believed.

Mara watched all of it. She watched the way she watched everything—sideways, silently, with those taster's eyes that missed nothing—and it made Oscar feel like a dish she was deciding whether to swallow.

"You're different," she said one evening, cornering him by the scullery door. She had a spoon in her hand, tasting-spoon, worn smooth, and she gestured with it like a tiny scepter. "You walk differently. You used to apologize to the furniture. Now the furniture apologizes to you."

"It's nothing."

"Nothing doesn't leave footprints." She lowered the spoon. Her voice dropped to the register she probably thought of as gentle and which was actually terrifying. "Oscar. Something answered your prayer over that goat. Something is answering things all over the valley now—Wren's leg, the blight, the shrine lights. And now it's answering *you*, specifically, personally, like you're its favorite. In a sealed country where nothing answers anything, you are suddenly the most interesting person in the building. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Lucky?"

"It makes you a *door*," Mara said. "And doors get used, Oscar. From both sides."

She left him standing there with the scullery steam rising around him, and he pressed his hand flat against his chest, against the place where the name lived, and felt it pulse once, warm, like something glad to be mentioned.

*I'm fine,* he told it. *We're fine. It's a small gift. Small gifts are safe.*

The name pulsed again, and this time he couldn't quite tell whether it was agreeing with him or patting him on the head.

* * *

On the ninth night, the bill sent its first messenger.

It was nothing dramatic. That was the trouble. It was a letter—an invitation, really, heavy cream paper, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, addressed in a hand so elegant it looked embarrassed to be legible:

*Lady Vess Corvane requests the pleasure of the company of Mr. Oscar ——— at dinner, Thursday next, along with the royal Taster and the Archivist Pell. Dress: whatever you own that is cleanest.*

Oscar read it three times. A dinner party. Lady Vess, who smelled of iron under roses, who had cornered him in the kitchen and warned him about attention while clearly being the most attentive person in the valley, wanted him at her table, seated next to Mara and Pell, the archivist who had spent thirty years guarding the treaty that kept gods out.

Luck, he thought, looking at the invitation. This was luck. An ordinary kitchen boy did not get invited to dine with Lady Corvane. An ordinary kitchen boy got invited to scrub up after the dining was done.

So why did it feel like a hook going in?

Behind his ribs, the borrowed name turned over in its sleep, and for half a heartbeat Oscar felt it clearly—not as warmth but as *weight*, a small smooth stone that he had swallowed and would carry now forever, and the stone had a grain to it, a direction, a slight lean toward the border, toward the fog, toward the antlered patience waiting out there in the dark with its ledger open and its pen dipped.

Every fortunate thing bends the world a little, the god had said. And the world keeps accounts.

Oscar set the invitation down on the kitchen table, next to the salt, and stared at it for a long time. Then he picked it up again and went to find Mara, because if he was a door, he figured, he should at least stand next to the person who knew the most about poison.

He found her in the corridor outside the throne room, frozen mid-step, white as the palace linens, and when he asked what was wrong she said, in a voice he'd never heard her use—a voice with cracks in it—

"The king just asked me my real name."
