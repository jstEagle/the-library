## 13. Vess's Dinner Party

The invitation arrived on a silver tray carried by a footman who had clearly been instructed to look bored about it.

*Mara Kavanagh is requested to attend dinner at Corvane House, the seventh evening of the moon. Formal dress. Her services will be required.*

"Her *services*," Oscar read over her shoulder in the kitchen, where the invitation had been delivered instead of to her rooms, because everyone in the palace knew exactly where Mara lived: three doors from the larders, close enough to be summoned at a cough. "That's not an invitation, that's a work order."

"It's both," said Cook, not looking up from her dough. "Lady Vess doesn't do anything by halves. If she wanted you dead she'd have sent you a pie."

"That's very reassuring," said Oscar.

"She'd send you a *good* pie," Cook amended. "She wants something."

Mara turned the card over in her fingers. The paper was heavy and cream-colored, imported — or at least pretending to be; nothing was truly imported in the Valley of the Unnamed, everything simply *was*, the way it always had been, like the fog and the king and the thirteen moons on the kitchen wall. The ink was black as beetle shells. And beneath the printed words, in a smaller hand, sharp and slanted:

*Wear the grey dress. You'll want pockets.*

Mara stared at it for a long moment.

"Well?" said Oscar. "What does it say?"

"Nothing." She tucked the card into her apron. "It says I'm having dinner with Lady Vess Corvane, and if I survive it, I'm to tell no one what we ate."

"That last part sounds like a poison joke," Oscar said.

"It is a poison joke," said Mara. "It's how you know she's serious."

* * *

The grey dress had been hanging in her wardrobe for two years, unworn. It had appeared there one morning without explanation, which in the Valley of the Unnamed was more or less how all clothing arrived — the seamstress left things, the laundress took things, and somewhere in between garments migrated like patient ghosts. But this one had pockets. Real ones. Deep enough for a spoon, a vial, or — as Mara discovered the night of the dinner, when she finally checked — a folded square of paper she had never put there.

She unfolded it at her dressing table with the caution of a woman defusing something.

It was a list. Ten items, written in the same sharp slanted hand as the invitation:

1. Almond soup (check for bitter)
2. Quail in vine leaves
3. The fish course (Vess will watch your face, not the fish)
4. Spiced lamb
5. Do not taste the wine until the third pour
6. Sorbet — safe, but enjoy it, they'll expect you to suffer
7. Stuffed mushrooms (the mushrooms are fine, the stuffing is not)
8. Honey cakes
9. When the port comes out, listen.
10. You are the most dangerous person at this table. Act like it.

Mara read it twice. Then a third time, because her heart was doing something complicated and she needed a moment.

Her mother had been dead four years. Her mother had also, apparently, known this dinner was coming — or known Vess Corvane well enough to predict her, which amounted to the same thing. The handwriting was unmistakable: the cramped, impatient letters of a woman who thought faster than she wrote and resented every second spent holding a pen.

"You absolute menace," Mara said softly, to a woman who could no longer hear her. "You've been planning my social calendar from beyond the grave."

She folded the list into her pocket, next to the tasting spoon she never went anywhere without, and went down to meet her doom.

* * *

Corvane House was the grandest residence in the palace quarter, which was a low bar to clear in a country whose palace served a king who ruled fog, but Vess Corvane had cleared it anyway, and then kept clearing it, the way she cleared everything. Candles everywhere — real beeswax, not tallow — and flowers out of season, and a table set for eight with more silver than the royal treasury had probably seen in a decade.

Mara was announced by name, which made her skin prickle. Names had weight here. Most people called her *the taster*, or *girl*, or nothing at all, and she had spent thirty years cultivating that invisibility the way other people cultivated gardens. Hearing "Mara Kavanagh" ring off the marble felt like being splashed with cold water.

"Mara." Vess Corvane descended on her like weather. She was tall, silver-streaked, handsome in the way a hawk is handsome, and wearing green so dark it was nearly black. She took both of Mara's hands and squeezed them as though they were old friends, which they were not, and might never be. "I'm so glad you came. I know how you hate leaving the kitchens."

"I don't hate leaving the kitchens," said Mara. "I just see no reason to."

"There it is. That honesty. I've heard so much about it." Vess drew her toward the fire, where six guests were arranged in a half-circle of armchairs, all of them watching Mara with the frank curiosity of people who had never actually looked at their food-taster before. "Everyone, you know why she's here. Master Aldous, our archivist's colleague — Pell sends his apologies, he claims his eyes are failing him, though I suspect he simply didn't want to come. Magistrate Hurn. Captain Oskell of the border watch. Doctor Imre. And the Reverend Sallow, who has promised not to pray before the fish."

"A difficult promise," said the Reverend Sallow, who had the soft, damp look of a man built mostly out of apology. "In these times."

"In these times," agreed Magistrate Hurn, heavily. He was a big man with small eyes, and he did not smile at Mara, which she noted. Nobody else smiled either, but Hurn *didn't* with intent.

"Shall we?" said Vess.

They shall, apparently.

* * *

The almond soup arrived first, pale and gleaming in its porcelain bowls.

Mara tasted it standing, as tradition demanded — the taster eats first, visibly, while the host watches. She let the spoon rest on her tongue. Sweetness, cream, salt. And underneath, faint as a whisper in another room: bitterness. Not poison-bitter. *Almond*-bitter, the natural ghost of the nut itself, except that good cooks strained it out, and whoever had cooked this soup had either forgotten or been told not to bother.

She swallowed. Waited. Counted to ten in her head, the way her mother had taught her — *one for the tongue, two for the throat, three through ten for whatever's cleverer than both*.

Nothing happened.

"Bitter almonds," she said aloud, to the table at large. "Barely. Someone used the flour unblanched. It's not poisoned, but it's lazy."

There was a silence of the particular quality that follows a guest insulting the food.

"The cook is new," said Vess, smoothly, and there was something in her voice — satisfaction? Amusement? — that made Mara's neck prickle. "Thank you, Mara. You see, everyone? This is why we keep her. Sit, please. Eat."

Mara sat. The quail came, wrapped in vine leaves, and was clean. The fish course came — river trout, silver-skinned — and she tasted it while feeling, distinctly, Vess's gaze resting on her face rather than her plate, exactly as the note had promised. She kept her expression bland as porridge. Whatever Vess hoped to read there, she didn't get it.

It was during the spiced lamb that the conversation, which had been circling all evening like a dog looking for a place to lie down, finally settled.

"The crops," said Captain Oskell. He was a lean woman with a border-watch sunburn and no patience for subtlety. "Three farms east of the stones woke to barley standing straight where frost had flattened it. Overnight. My men swear the shrines were glowing. *Glowing.*"

"Old shrines," said Doctor Imre. "Abandoned ones. The ones nobody's swept since our grandmothers' grandmothers."

"And the Hale girl walked," said the Reverend Sallow quietly. "Little Wren Hale. Born lame, and on Tuesday she ran the length of the village green twice, laughing like — like something out of a story."

"Prayers are being answered," said Magistrate Hurn. His small eyes moved around the table. "That is either the best news this valley has had in five hundred years, or the worst. I'd like us all to agree on which."

"And which do you think it is?" asked Vess, delicately lifting a quail bone from her lips.

Hurn's gaze slid — briefly, involuntarily — to Mara.

She felt it land. She kept chewing. *Do not react*, her mother's voice said in her memory, from a hundred childhood lessons. *A taster's face is a wall. Build it high.*

"I think," Hurn said slowly, "that when a locked room starts answering knocks, the question isn't whether the visitor is friendly. The question is whether anyone remembers where we put the key."

"The key," repeated Mara, before she could stop herself.

Every eye at the table turned to her.

Oops, said a small voice in her head that sounded suspiciously like Oscar.

"Ah," said Vess, and her smile widened by a single degree, the way ice widens across a pond. "Yes. The key. You've heard the stories too, then. Everyone has. The Door Out of the valley. The way back into the world." She spread her hands, rings catching candlelight. "My grandmother swore her grandmother had seen a map of it. A real map, Mara. Marked. Measured. With a lock described in the founding treaty itself — a lock that turns on *royal blood*."

"Superstition," said Doctor Imre comfortably.

"Is it?" said Captain Oskell. "Because the king woke up lucid last month for the first time in years, and the first thing he did was ask a kitchen servant her *name*. And now prayers are getting answered through a seal that hasn't cracked since the founding. Something is happening. I'd rather it happened to people who've thought about it than to a mob."

*You are the most dangerous person at this table,* said the note in her pocket. *Act like it.*

So Mara set down her fork, dabbed her mouth with the napkin, and said, in the flat, unimpressed tone she used for reporting arsenic: "If any of you are planning to go looking for this door, I should tell you what happens to people who eat unknown plants off cliffs. Because that's essentially the plan. You're going to eat something wild and hope your stomach agrees with it."

"We'd bring you," said Vess, "to check."

The table laughed. Mara did not.

"The sorbet," she said instead, "is going to melt."

* * *

The sorbet was, as promised, safe. It was also excellent — sharp plum, cold enough to hurt — and Mara enjoyed it with visible, deliberate pleasure, partly because the note had told her to and partly out of spite. Let them watch her enjoy something. Let them wonder what else she knew how to do.

The stuffed mushrooms nearly killed her.

Not literally — the note had been precise: *the mushrooms are fine, the stuffing is not*. What was in the stuffing she couldn't immediately say. Something herbal, something bitter, something that made her tongue go numb at the edges and her heartbeat pick up a strange syncopated rhythm, like a drummer losing count. She stood, spat neatly into the provided basin — every formal dinner kept one for her; it was the single courtesy her profession had ever won her — and rinsed her mouth with watered wine while the table watched in fascinated horror.

"Not the mushrooms," she announced, when she could speak properly. "The stuffing. There's fool's-parsley in it, or something wearing fool's-parsley's coat. Whoever catered this bought their herbs from someone who doesn't know hemlock from hedge-greens, and *that* is worth knowing, because it means your caterer is either incompetent or new to the valley." She looked directly at Vess. "New people are interesting, aren't they?"

Something flickered behind Vess's eyes. Gone in an instant, but Mara had spent her life reading faces over spoons, and she knew what she'd seen: not guilt. *Recalculation.*

"Remarkable," murmured Doctor Imre.

"Yes," said Vess. "She is. Honey cakes, anyone?"

* * *

The honey cakes were fine. The port, when it came, was poured once around, and Mara did not touch hers — *do not taste the wine until the third pour* — and when the servants withdrew and the third pour went round, the conversation dropped its company manners entirely.

"The map exists," Vess said, without preamble. "I have it. I've had it for eleven years. I've spent those eleven years learning what it says, and what it says is this: there is a cliff on the western reach where a door stands in no wall, and it opens to crowned blood, and beyond it is the world. The *real* world. Maps with our valley on them. Markets. Weather that arrives on schedule. Children who grow up with names anyone can pronounce."

"And the thirteenth moon?" asked the Reverend Sallow. His hands were folded, damp and white. "Pell's arithmetic suggests—"

"Pell's arithmetic is why he wasn't invited," said Vess crisply. "When the moon fills, the seal fails. All of it. Every ward, every warding bloodline, everything our ancestors paid for — gone, all at once, whether we're ready or not. Magic pours in. *Things* pour in. We have perhaps—" she glanced up, as if she could see the moon through the ceiling, "—six moons left. Maybe seven."

"So we open the door ourselves," said Captain Oskell. "On our terms. Before the terms choose us."

"On *whose* terms, though," said Magistrate Hurn, and here it was, the thing the whole evening had been coiling toward. "Because a door opened by crowned blood needs crowned blood present. And the crown sits on the head of a dying man with no heir."

Silence.

Mara lifted her untouched port and held it to the light, as if inspecting it, which gave her an excuse to keep her face angled away. Her pulse was loud in her ears. *Crowned blood.* The king, lucid, asking her name. The weight of names in a valley that had traded them all away.

"Blood can be found," Vess said softly. "Royal blood runs thinner and wider than people think. There are families in this valley — old families — who kept registers even when registers were forbidden. Mine is one." She smiled around the table, generous as a landlord. "But that's a conversation for another evening, when we're fewer and the port is better. Tonight I only wanted honest people in a room together, agreeing that the valley's future shouldn't be left to a mob and a full moon."

Nods all around. Even Hurn nodded, though he looked like a man agreeing to a price he hadn't yet seen itemized.

And then, as the party began to break apart in a rustle of chairs and insincere warmth, Vess crossed the room to Mara, took her hands again, and said, low enough that only Mara could hear:

"You knew about the stuffing before you tasted it. Don't argue — I watched you. You looked at that plate the way a woman looks at an old enemy." Her grip tightened, pleasant and pitiless. "Someone taught you tonight's menu before you ever walked through my door, Mara Kavanagh. I would very much like to know who. Because whoever it was knows my household, my plans, and my table better than I do — and in the months ahead, I intend to collect every advantage in this valley. Including that one."

She released Mara's hands, stepped back, and raised her voice to bright, carrying cheerfulness: "Thank you all for coming! Captain, walk the doctor to her chair, it's foggy out. Mara — the carriage will take you home. Do give my regards to the kitchens."

* * *

The carriage was absurd — padded, curtained, drawn by two horses that cost more per month than the entire kitchen staff earned — and Mara rode home through the fog with the note open on her knee, reading item nine again.

*When the port comes out, listen.*

She had listened. She wished she hadn't.

*Crowned blood runs thinner and wider than people think.* Vess had said it like a woman discussing horse lineages. And she had a register. Eleven years with a map, and a register of old families, and a caterer who was either incompetent or *new to the valley* — and Mara thought suddenly of the footman who had brought the invitation, bored-looking, foreign-seeming, forgettable in exactly the practiced way that Mara herself had spent a lifetime perfecting.

Her mother had known this dinner was coming. Had planned against it, dish by dish, from beyond the grave. Which meant her mother had known Vess Corvane's mind well enough to simulate it — which meant her mother and Vess had *history* — which meant—

Which meant the grey dress with the pockets had not appeared in her wardrobe by accident, two years ago. Two years ago. Before the king grew lucid. Before the moons miscounted. Before anything.

The carriage rolled on through the fog, and Mara pressed the folded paper flat against her knee, and understood, with the particular chill of a woman who has just realized the recipe she's been following was written by someone who knew how the meal would end:

Her mother hadn't been protecting her from this dinner.

She'd been *sending* her to it.

Ahead, through the carriage window, the palace rose out of the fog — and above it, impossibly early, already climbing the sky like a debt coming due, hung the thirteenth moon, one night fatter than it had any right to be.
