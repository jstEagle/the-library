## 14. Shrine Fires

Wren had learned to count her blessings the way other people counted coins: carefully, and always with one eye on who might be watching.

She had a good leg now. That was the first blessing, and it was enormous, and it was also the problem.

Before the answered prayers, before the shrines began to glow like hearths in winter, Wren's left leg had been a crooked thing, turned inward at the knee since birth, so that she walked with a rolling gait the village children called *the crab-walk* until they were old enough to be ashamed of it and she was old enough to stop caring. She had spent eleven years measuring the world in distances — how far to the well, how far to the shrine of the Small Mothers, how many steps to the top of Bramble Hill if she gave herself the whole afternoon. She knew every handhold on every gate in Hollowmere. She could tell you which stones in the mill stream were honest and which would roll under your foot like liars.

Then, on the night after Moon the Third filled, she had limped out to the old waystone behind her grandmother's house — because everyone was doing it, because the whole village had gone prayer-mad, and because even skeptics will whisper a wish into a glowing stone when no one respectable is looking — and she had said, not really believing it: *Let me walk like other girls.*

And the stone had grown warm as bread fresh from an oven, and something had poured up through the soles of her feet like spring water, and by morning her leg was straight.

Straight. Beautifully, boringly straight. She had cried for an hour, then run to the top of Bramble Hill in forty minutes flat, then cried again, sitting at the top with her two identical feet dangling over the valley fog.

Her grandmother had taken one look at her striding into the kitchen and set down her ladle very slowly, the way you set down a knife when a stranger walks into the room.

"Well," said Gran. "That's that, then."

"That's *that?*" Wren had said. "Gran, I ran up the hill. I *ran.*"

"I can see that." Gran had looked at her leg for a long moment, and then at the window, where the waystone's glow still faintly lit the frost. "And what did it cost, do you think? Things that glow don't give change, girl. My mother told me that, and hers told her, back when our family still remembered why we don't say certain words."

Wren had laughed. She was still laughing about it when the first shrine burned.

* * *

The shrine of the Small Mothers stood at the crossroads below Hollowmere, a low stone cairn older than anyone's grandmother, crowned with offerings: ribbons, buttons, a child's shoe worn smooth, little clay figures with no faces. It had never glowed before the Third Moon. Now it hummed softly all night, a sound like a hive thinking, and people came from three villages over to press their palms against it and ask for things.

Old Tam Hobb asked for his lungs, which the damp had been eating for thirty years, and woke the next morning breathing like a bellows.

The Fenner twins asked for their father's boat to stop leaking, and the boat sealed itself overnight with a seam of wood no carpenter could explain, smooth as if the tree had grown around the hole and decided against it.

And Widow Marsh, who was ninety-one and sharp as a thorn, asked for nothing at all, and stood before the cairn every evening shaking her stick at it.

"You're a wicked thing," she told it. "You're a sweet-talking wicked thing, and I've buried two husbands and I know how sweet-talk works."

Nobody listened to Widow Marsh. Nobody ever had, which was perhaps why she'd lived so long.

It was Tam Hobb's daughter Bess who found the cairn burning.

Not burned — *burning*, is what Wren should say, because that was the horror of it. The fire didn't consume. The ribbons blackened and curled but did not turn to ash; the clay mothers split and smoked but held their shape; the whole cairn sat wrapped in flame like a hand closed around a bird, and the fire made no heat that Wren could feel from ten steps away, only a cold that reached into the teeth.

Bess came screaming down the lane at dawn, and half of Hollowmere came running with buckets, and it was Wren — quick now, quick as anyone — who got there first.

She stood before the burning shrine with a full bucket in both hands and did not throw it.

Because the fire was beautiful. That was the terrible thing. It burned in colors she had no names for, deep red shading into a green like moss on the north side of stones, and inside the flames the little clay mothers seemed almost to be moving, turning their faceless heads as if searching the crowd for someone.

"Don't," said Widow Marsh, arriving last and winded, leaning on her stick. "Don't you put water on it, girl."

"It's the shrine," Wren said. "We have to—"

"We have to stand here," said Widow Marsh, "and watch, and remember which of our neighbors are standing beside us, and which ones are standing over there looking pleased." And indeed, across the lane, a knot of villagers — the Fenners among them, and Tam Hobb upright and pink-cheeked and breathing gloriously — watched the fire with expressions that were not grief. They were something more complicated than grief. They were the expressions of people watching a debt being collected.

"Who would burn a shrine?" Bess kept saying, over and over, wringing her apron. "Who would *do* that?"

"Someone who thinks the fire is a message," said Widow Marsh.

"And who's it for?" Wren asked.

Widow Marsh looked at her — at her two straight legs, planted firm on the frozen mud — and said nothing at all, which was its own answer, and it went into Wren like a splinter and stayed there for weeks.

* * *

By the time the fourth shrine burned — the waystone behind Wren's own grandmother's house, still faintly warm from her own wish, wrapped in that same cold colored flame — the valley had split clean down the middle, the way ice splits, along a line nobody had drawn and everybody could see.

They called themselves nothing, at first. Names came later, as names do, dragged in reluctantly like cats. But everyone knew. In Hollowmere there were those who touched the shrines and those who wouldn't walk past them, and the two kinds of people had stopped sharing bread.

"They're calling us holdouts," Gran reported at supper, with relish, as though she'd been given a title. "Down at the market. Holdouts. Old Marta Kettleby said it right to my face. Said we were holding out against blessing, like blessing was the tax man and we were hiding silver in the mattress."

"What did you say?" Wren asked.

"Said my silver's my own business." Gran stabbed at her potatoes. "Pass the salt, miracle legs."

"I wish you'd stop calling me that."

"I wish you'd stop being able to outrun me. We don't all get our wishes." But there was no real bite in it, and Wren knew — had always known — that underneath Gran's flint was a fear with a very simple shape: *the gods took her leg away and gave it back, and next time they might take something else, and next time they might not ask.*

The believers, meanwhile, had begun meeting openly at the crossroads shrine — or where it had stood; the cold fire had gone out after three days, leaving the cairn blackened but whole, like a loaf forgotten in the oven. They met on rest-days and sang songs none of them quite knew the words to, humming along in the gaps, and the humming carried strangely on the wind, reaching houses it had no business reaching.

Tam Hobb preached, in his new loud bellows-lungs voice, that the gifts were freely given and the fires were the work of frightened men.

Widow Marsh preached back, from her doorstep, that freely given was exactly what worried her.

And in between stood everyone else — the Fenners, the Kettlebys, the miller's family, half the village — holding their healed children and their sealed boats and their mended lungs, saying, in voices that got quieter every week: *But it worked. Whatever it is, whatever it wants — it worked. My daughter sleeps through the night now. Are we supposed to spit on that?*

Wren understood them. That was the worst part. She lay awake at night cataloguing everything the gift had given her: the hill, the well-path, the mill-stream stones, the ability to dance at the harvest-coming without a chair to lean on. And against that she had only a splinter feeling, and Widow Marsh's silence, and the memory of the clay mothers turning their faceless heads.

*What did it cost,* Gran had asked. *Things that glow don't give change.*

And lately, when Wren pressed her palm flat against her own perfect knee in the dark, she thought she could feel something in there that hadn't used to be. Not pain. A kind of attention. As if the leg were listening.

As if the leg were *hers to take back.*

* * *

It came to violence on a grey morning in the week of Moon the Fourth's first sliver, and it started, as these things do, over something small.

The Kettlebys' boy, Piet — eight years old, born with a stammer that the crossroads shrine had smoothed away as easily as a thumb smearing wet ink — had been sent by his mother to leave an offering at the shrine of the Small Mothers. A ribbon. A blue ribbon, his mother's best, because Piet had come home from the shrine speaking clear as a bell and his mother would have given the ribbon off her own hair, and nearly did.

Widow Marsh was on her doorstep. She always was. She caught the boy by the sleeve as he passed.

"You give that back to your mother," she said, "and you tell her the Small Mothers never asked for ribbons in two hundred years, and they managed."

Piet's face crumpled. He was eight, and he loved his mother, and he loved his own unbroken voice more than he could articulate, and he did the thing that eight-year-olds do when the world splits into sides and they're standing on the crack: he burst into tears and shouted.

"You're just jealous! You're jealous because you're going to die soon and nobody's shrine can fix *that!*"

The lane went silent the way a forest goes silent.

Widow Marsh stood very still. And Wren, who had been carrying water past the Kettleby gate, saw the old woman's knuckles whiten on her stick, and saw something worse behind her eyes — not anger but a terrible, tired recognition, the look of someone who has heard the future early.

"There it is," Widow Marsh said quietly, to the whole lane, to the boy, to the gods themselves. "There's the price. First they fix your tongue, and then your tongue belongs to them. Ask him in a year what he'd shout at me if they hadn't touched his mouth. Ask him if he'd know."

"He'd know!" screamed Piet's mother from the gate, white-faced. "He's my son!"

"Is he?" said Widow Marsh. "Or is he partly theirs now? Look at the boy's shadow, Marta. Go on. Look properly."

And the lane looked — because when a woman like Widow Marsh tells a whole village to look at something, the whole village looks — and Wren looked too, and Piet's shadow lay thin and blue on the frosty ground, the way shadows do on frost, and it was only a shadow, it was only frost-light, it meant nothing.

Except that Wren's own shadow, she noticed, lying long and thin beside her, had its head turned slightly toward the crossroads. Toward the shrine. Herself, facing the well.

She dropped the water bucket.

Later — much later, years later, when she understood what she had seen — she would learn that shadows on frost lie, that low light lies, that a frightened mind will draw monsters out of anything. But that morning, in that lane, forty people saw a child's shadow lean toward a shrine like a plant toward sun, and whether it happened or not stopped mattering, because afterward it was *known*, and known things build walls faster than any mason.

By noon, someone had nailed a horseshoe over the Kettleby door. By evening, the Kettlebys had nailed one over Widow Marsh's, upside down, which was either an insult or a protection depending on which side of the split you stood, and both families treated it as both.

And two nights later, the shrine of the Small Mothers burned for the second time — and this time the fire was ordinary fire, orange and hot and smelling of pitch, and this time the clay mothers did not survive it, and this time there were boot prints in the frost leading away toward the mill, and everyone knew whose boots wore that pattern, and nobody said so out loud, because the valley had passed beyond saying things out loud.

Wren stood in the crowd around the smoking cairn and felt the split run through her own chest like a seam.

On one side: her leg. Her hill. Her forty minutes to the top of Bramble Hill, her dancing, her whole self remade. On the other side: an old woman on a doorstep, a boy's shadow, a blue ribbon, and the growing certainty that somewhere beneath the fog, something vast and patient was keeping accounts, and the ledger had her name in it — except that she'd never given it a name, had she, only a wish, and wishes, her grandmother said, were how they got their hooks in.

"Wren." Gran's hand on her shoulder, at the edge of the crowd. "Come home."

"It was us," Wren said. "Wasn't it. The holdouts. We burned it."

"Some of us did." Gran's voice was flat. "Fools on both sides now. That's how it goes. That's how it always goes, once the splitting starts — each side burns something to prove it isn't afraid, and proves the opposite." She tugged Wren's sleeve. "Home, girl."

But Wren didn't move. Because across the smoke, at the crowd's far edge, she had seen Tam Hobb — healed, hale, upright Tam Hobb — kneeling in the frost beside the ruined cairn, gathering the broken pieces of the clay mothers into his cap with enormous gentleness, as though they were bones, as though they were the dead.

And as he lifted the largest fragment — a little faceless figure, cracked through the middle — Wren saw the shard in his hands give one soft pulse of light, green as moss, steady as a heartbeat.

Tam Hobb froze. Then he looked up, slowly, across the whole crowd, past every neighbor he'd ever shared a fence with, until his eyes found Wren's.

He didn't smile. He didn't speak. He simply held up the glowing shard a little higher, the way a man holds up a lantern on a dark road — showing it to her, or showing her to *it* — and Wren felt the attention in her own knee turn toward the light like a flower toward morning.

"Come home *now*," said Gran.

Wren went. But all the way up the lane, in the dark behind her, she could feel that small green light burning at her back like an eye opening — and she could feel, with every straight and borrowed step, that it had seen her, and that it was not finished asking.
