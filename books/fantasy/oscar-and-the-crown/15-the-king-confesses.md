## 15. The King Confesses

The king's illness had a smell to it. Not the sour reek of ordinary sickness—Mara knew that one well enough from years of palace kitchens—but something sweeter, like honey left too long in a warm room, or flowers pressed between the pages of a book until they gave up being flowers and became only a memory of them.

She noticed it the moment she entered his chambers on the morning after Pell's revelation, and her stomach clenched with a taster's old instinct: *poison*. Then she remembered there was nothing left for her to taste. The sickness had come in through prayers, not porridge, and no amount of careful spoon-work could have stopped it.

The king lay propped against a mountain of pillows, and he was watching the window.

Not the view—the window itself. The glass, the frame, the way the fog pressed against it from outside like a cat wanting in. He had been king of the Valley of the Unnamed for forty-one years, ruler of a country that appeared on no map, sovereign over nothing but mist and memory, and Mara had served him breakfast nearly every day of her adult life. In all that time she had never once seen him look at anything the way he was looking at that window.

"Your Majesty," she said softly. "You asked for me."

"I did." His voice was stronger than she expected. Frighteningly strong, actually, for a man who three days ago could not lift a spoon. "Come here where I can see you, girl. And bring the chair. You'll want to sit down for what I have to say."

Mara fetched the chair from beside the cold fireplace and set it by the bed. She sat. She did not want to sit. Every instinct she possessed—the ones her mother had beaten into her alongside the immunity to nightshade and arsenic—told her that kings who summoned food-tasters to their deathbeds wanted something, and that something was rarely pleasant.

"You look better," she offered, because silence felt dangerous.

"I look better because I'm dying properly now." The king smiled, and it was such an odd, crooked smile that Mara almost laughed. "There's a difference, you know, between fading and dying. I've been fading for twenty years. Fog in the head, fog in the blood. But this—" he pressed a hand to his chest "—this is honest work. This is dying with my eyes open. First time in decades I've felt *clear*."

"That doesn't sound like something to be glad about."

"Clarity is always expensive." He turned from the window at last and looked at her, and Mara had the uncomfortable sensation of being *seen*—truly seen—for perhaps the first time since she'd taken up the taster's spoon. Kings were supposed to be bad at noticing people. That was rather the point of people like her. "Pell has been talking to you. I can tell. You've got his look about you—that scholarly constipation of the face."

"He told me about the bargain," Mara said carefully. "About the names."

"Mm. And did he tell you whose idea it was? Whose *duty*?" The king's eyes glittered. "No. Of course not. Pell is a good man, but he has spent fifty years arranging facts into piles, and some facts refuse to be filed. So let me tell you the part he left out, and then you may decide whether to hate me."

* * *

Outside, the fog moved against the glass in slow, deliberate curls. Somewhere below, the kitchen was arguing about whether the thirteenth moon-mark on the wall meant the calendar was broken or the world was, and Cook was winning the argument by virtue of owning the largest ladle. It all sounded very far away, as if the chamber had drawn its own small seal around the bed.

"The royal line," the king began, "were never rulers. Do you understand? We were never kings in any sense your kitchen boys would recognize. No armies. No laws worth the name. No taxes beyond what the palace needed to eat. What were we, then?"

Mara hesitated. "Ceremonial?"

"We were *jailers*." He said it plainly, without drama, the way a man describes a family trade—coopering, say, or sheep-farming. "The bargain your ancestors struck—the nameless ones, the ones who traded their names to bar out magic—it required wardens. A lock does not guard itself, girl. Someone must hold the key, turn it each night, walk the walls, watch the prisoners. That was the crown's whole purpose. Not power. *Vigilance.*"

"The crown is a key," Mara said slowly. Pell had hinted. The king had said it himself, once, in one of his lucid hours years ago—and then forgotten, and everyone had politely agreed to forget with him.

"The crown is *the* key. There is exactly one, and it has been sitting on a velvet cushion in a locked room downstairs for forty-one years while I drooled into my soup." He laughed—a dry, rusted sound. "Forty-one years of dereliction. Can you imagine? The jailer asleep at his post for two generations, and the prison so patient, so terribly patient, waiting for him to wake up."

"The god," Mara whispered. "At the border stone. Oscar says—"

"Oscar." The king's gaze sharpened. "The boy who prays. Yes, I know about Oscar. Word travels even through fog, especially when the fog is *listening*." He shifted against the pillows, wincing. "Yes. The god. Or rather, the prisoner. Girl, when Pell showed you the treaty pages, what did he call the thing we sealed out?"

"A god. One of the old gods, who came to collect a debt."

"And what did it *say*, when it spoke to the boy?"

Mara thought of Oscar's account, halting and half-ashamed, delivered in the kitchen doorway with flour still on his hands: *It called me little borrower. It said the valley owed it. It said it only wanted what was taken.* "It said... it wanted its name back."

"Its name," the king repeated. "Or the valley's? Sit with that question. Chew it like gristle. Because here is the truth Pell's burned pages held, and here is why I am telling you and not him: the thing behind the border stones is not a god who was robbed. It is the *land itself*—the valley's own true self, its name and body and hunger, caged by our ancestors and called a monster ever after, because it is easier to jail a monster than to admit you've stolen your own house."

The room seemed to tilt. Mara gripped the arm of the chair.

"That can't—" She stopped. Started again. "The valley is *here*. Under us. The fields, the river, the—"

"The fields are its skin. The river is its blood. And every prayer the people whisper into the ground falls on the ears of a prisoner who has been listening for four hundred years." The king's voice dropped low. "And lately, girl, it has begun answering. Do you see? It isn't breaking in. It was never outside. The seal doesn't keep magic *out* of the valley. The seal keeps the valley's own soul *down*, buried beneath it, bound and nameless. Your ancestors didn't build a wall. They built a lid."

* * *

Mara discovered she had stood up without meaning to. Her legs had decided, entirely on their own, that they preferred verticality for receiving catastrophic news. She stood at the window with her back to the king and watched the fog press its slow face against the glass, and for the first time in her life the fog looked less like weather and more like breath.

"My mother," she said. "She fed me poison. All those years. Was that—was that part of it? Keeping the taster alive to—"

"To keep the key's bearer healthy? No." The king's voice gentled in a way that startled her more than any harshness could have. "Your mother fed you poison because she loved you in the only language she'd been taught, which is a hard language, and I'm sorry for it. But she also knew what you are. Royal blood, girl. Thin as watered wine by now, four hundred years of tasters and cooks and scullery maids diluting it—but royal. When I die without an heir declared, the crown will go to the nearest drop of that blood. And we both know whose that is."

"No." Mara turned around. "No, absolutely not. I taste food. I am *nobody*. That is the entire point of me. My whole life is built on being nobody."

"Yes," the king agreed pleasantly. "That's precisely why it must be you."

"That makes no sense!"

"It makes perfect sense; you simply haven't heard the rest." He beckoned, and despite herself, despite everything, Mara came and sat again, because a dying king's beckoning is a difficult thing to refuse. "Listen. The crown is a key. Keys do not care who carries them, but locks do. The lock beneath this valley—the binding that holds the land's name down—is keyed to royal blood. To *nameless* royal blood, specifically, because the bargainers were clever: they made sure their jailers could never be bribed with names, never seduced by the prisoner's promises. A jailer with a name might want it back. Might trade it. So they took theirs first, and their children's, and their children's children's, all the way down to a food-taster who takes pride in going unnoticed."

He smiled that crooked smile again.

"You are the most qualified person in this valley, Mara Kavanagh-of-no-particular-surname-because-there-isn't-one. You cannot be tempted, because you have nothing to tempt. You cannot be threatened with exposure, because you were built invisible. And you have spent your whole life swallowing terrible things so that someone else wouldn't have to." His eyes were wet now, though his voice stayed steady. "Tell me that isn't a king."

Mara found, to her horror, that her throat was tight. "I don't *want* it."

"Good," the king said. "Neither did I. Neither did my father, nor his mother before him, nor any of us back to the first jailer, who according to Pell's surviving pages wept for three days upon accepting the crown. Wanting it disqualifies you. Not wanting it is the whole qualification." He coughed then, deep and wet, and the sweetness in the air thickened, and Mara was on her feet with the water pitcher before she remembered there was nothing left for her to intercept.

* * *

When the coughing passed, he waved off her fussing with the imperiousness of a man who had been fussed over badly for forty years, and gestured her back to the chair.

"There's more," he said. "The part I've dreaded saying aloud, because saying it makes it real, and I have been a great coward about real things." He drew a breath. "The crown is a key, yes. But keys open in two directions, girl. It can bind deeper—or it can unbind. And there are people in this court—I will not name them, you likely already know their faces—who have figured that much out. They think opening the Door Out and walking away with the valley's inheritance is rescue. They think the crown is treasure. They will come for it when I die, and they will come for *you*, because you're the lock it fits."

"Lady Vess," Mara said quietly.

The king's eyebrows rose. "You continue to surprise me."

"She hosted a dinner party. I tasted the quail. I also listened." Mara folded her hands in her lap, taster-steady. "She has a map. Old one. Shows a door in a cliff. She wants to reach it before the moon turns."

"Of course she does." The king closed his eyes briefly. "And she'll fail, or worse than fail, because the Door doesn't open for ambition. It opens for the key, and the key opens for blood, and the blood is yours." He opened his eyes and fixed her with a look of terrible kindness. "So here is my confession, complete at last, and you may hate me freely: I could have named an heir years ago. Any distant cousin, any noble brat, anyone but you. I chose not to. I let the succession drift toward you like a boat toward a dock, because I watched you, girl. For fifteen years I watched you taste my food and carry my secrets and ask for nothing, and I thought—there. There is the last jailer. There is the one who will not abuse the key."

"You *chose* this for me." Mara's voice came out flat and strange. "Without asking. Without telling me. Fifteen years."

"I did." He did not flinch. "As my father chose for me, and his for him. It is the family disease, choosing for the next generation. I'd apologize, but I suspect you'll find, someday, that apology is the one dish you can't taste for poison." He reached beneath his pillow with trembling fingers and drew out a small object, pressing it into her hands before she could refuse.

It was a spoon. Silver, worn thin at the bowl, ancient. A taster's spoon.

"This was the first jailer's," he said. "Passed down hand to hand, crown to crown. We kept it as a reminder: the crown is only a bigger spoon. You taste for the kingdom now. Whatever comes—Vess, the god, the thirteenth moon—you taste it first, and you decide what the valley swallows." His grip tightened on hers, surprisingly strong. "I'm sorry. Truly. But I am so *glad* it's you."

* * *

Mara walked back down through the palace in a daze, the old spoon heavy in her pocket, heavier than silver had any right to be.

In the kitchen, the argument about the calendar had collapsed into uneasy truce. Cook was kneading dough with unnecessary violence. Oscar sat by the fire, mending a goat's halter with hands that—Mara looked twice—seemed somehow older than they'd been last week, the knuckles broader, the skin faintly lined. He glanced up as she entered and managed a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

"How is he?" Oscar asked.

"Clear," Mara said. "Terribly clear." She hung her cloak on its peg and stood a moment, feeling the shape of the spoon through the fabric of her pocket, feeling the shape of everything Pell had shown her and the king had confirmed, clicking together like tumblers in a lock.

Oscar frowned. "Mara? You've gone the color of the fog."

"I'm fine." She untied her apron with hands that were almost steady. Almost. "Oscar—when the god spoke to you. At the border stone. Did it say anything about a key? About a crown?"

The boy went very still. The fire popped. Outside the window, the fog curled close against the glass, patient as a listener, and far above—though the day was grey and sunless—Mara could have sworn she felt the thirteenth moon waxing anyway, filling like a spoon raised to unseen lips.

"No," Oscar said at last, too quickly. "But it asked about *you*."

Mara's blood went cold and clear as well-water.

"What did it say about me?"

Oscar set down the halter. When he answered, his voice was barely above the simmer of the soup pot, and it was not his own fear in it but something older, borrowed, patient:

"It said, 'Tell the little jailer: the key remembers her name—even if she doesn't.'"
