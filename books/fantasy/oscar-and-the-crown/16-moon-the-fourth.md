## 16. Moon the Fourth

Oscar woke on the first morning of the fourth moon to find frost on the *inside* of the window.

He lay there a while in his cot above the goat shed, watching it, because it was very pretty and because he was not yet awake enough to be alarmed. The frost had grown in patterns — not the usual ferns and feathers but something more deliberate, like handwriting seen through paper, like a letter written by someone who knew letters existed but had never actually read one. Curves that almost meant something. Loops that almost closed.

"Goats," he said to himself, which was what he said every morning before getting up, as a way of reminding his body why it needed to. "The goats need you."

His hands were still wrong. He'd stopped looking at them much — the knuckles too big now for a boy of fourteen, the skin gone papery at the edges, an old man's hands stapled onto a boy's arms by some careless tailor. The luck they'd bought him had been good luck, no question. When the well rope snapped two weeks ago, the bucket had come up anyway, floating, gently, like it was being handed up by someone polite. When Wren's cousin fell out of the hayloft, she'd landed in the one spot where the straw was deep. Small miracles, all of them wearing his name like a borrowed coat.

But the hands were the price, and prices were real, and Oscar's mother had raised him to believe three things: say your prayers, count your change, and never trust anything that gives you something without asking what you'll pay.

*It didn't ask,* he thought, flexing the old-man fingers under the blanket. *That's the part that keeps me up at night. It just took.*

Downstairs, the goats were standing in their shed in perfect silence, facing east, all six of them, arranged in a neat row like churchgoers waiting for the service to start.

"Hullo," said Oscar.

None of them turned around.

He counted to ten, which was how long it usually took a goat to decide whether you existed. On eleven, the nearest one — Buttercup, fat and opinionated — slowly rotated her head toward him with the unhurried dignity of a queen acknowledging a minor ambassador. Her eyes were fine. Her eyes were normal brown, wet, stupid, lovely goat eyes. But her breath came out silver in the cold shed air, and it hung there longer than breath should hang, curling into shapes, and if you squinted the shapes looked like the frost-writing on his window, and Oscar decided firmly not to squint.

"Right," he said. "Breakfast."

* * *

By midmorning the whole village of Lowmarsh had come out to look at the snow.

That was the thing everyone kept saying — *look at the snow* — as though it might do something if watched closely enough. It had fallen in the night, thick and dry and glittering, and it should have been nothing, because winter came to the valley every year same as taxes. But this was the fourth moon, which by anyone's count was late summer, and the barley stood half-harvested in the fields, gold stalks poking up through white like candles on a cake nobody had ordered.

"It's beautiful," said Mrs. Tam, who ran the bakery, and she meant it; her face was turned up and her breath was steaming and for a moment she looked twenty years younger, the way people do when snow catches them unprepared.

"It's wrong," said Mr. Hobbes, who mended fences and had never once in his life found anything beautiful that couldn't also be sat on.

Both of them were right, and that was the trouble. Oscar walked the village lane with his hands jammed in his pockets and tried to see it the way each of them saw it, and mostly he succeeded at both, which gave him a headache.

The snow was beautiful the way the god at the border stone had been beautiful. That was the connection he couldn't stop making, no matter how hard he chewed on it. Everything the god touched came out beautiful first and strange second, and the strangeness arrived late, like guests after dinner, when you'd already set a place for them.

Look: the snowflakes weren't random. He caught one on his sleeve and studied it and it was a seven-pointed star, and then he caught another and it was the *same* seven-pointed star, exactly, perfectly, and another, and another, all identical down to the last crystal spur, thousands upon thousands of flakes stamped from a single mold. Snow wasn't supposed to have a signature. Snow was supposed to be chaos doing its best work.

Look: where the snow melted against warm stones, it didn't melt evenly. It melted into words. Small ones. He crouched by the bakery step and watched the letters form and fade, form and fade, and he could almost read them, could almost feel the meaning sitting just behind his eyes like a word on the tip of his tongue, and then Mrs. Tam opened the door and the draft scattered it and he was honestly, genuinely relieved.

"Oscar! In, boy, I've got rolls coming out." She held the door and warmth billowed over him, cinnamon and woodsmoke and ordinary human baking, and he went inside gratefully and did not tell her about the melting words, because half the village still spoke to him and he wanted to keep it that way.

They'd started calling it *the god's weather*. Not out loud, not all together — it had simply drifted into use the way drifts of things do, until one day you realized everyone was saying it. *The god's weather came early. The god's weather killed my lettuces. Pretty, though, the god's weather.* Nobody said which god. Nobody needed to. There was only the one doing interviews these days.

And here was the thing Oscar understood and most of the village didn't, or wouldn't: the god's weather was *kind*. That was the horror of it. The frost hadn't hurt the barley — quite the opposite. Where snow had fallen on the half-cut fields, the remaining stalks had ripened overnight, plump and heavy, ready for the sickle weeks ahead of schedule. The orchards had bloomed again, absurdly, pink blossoms crusted with ice, bees staggering drunkenly between flowers and snowdrifts. Old Widow Marsh's arthritis had quieted. Children's chilblains healed. The cold itself never seemed to bite anyone; it lay on your skin like cool silk, like a hand checking your forehead.

It was generosity, everywhere, indiscriminate, patient.

Oscar had received a gift from this particular giver already. He knew what the wrapping paper cost.

* * *

In the afternoon, Wren found him at the border path.

She walked well now — that was the miracle, that was the whole argument in two legs — striding up the hill with her stick carried like a parade banner rather than leaned on, her bad leg healed straight and strong under her skirts. She'd been the first answered prayer in Lowmarsh, or among the first, and the village hadn't decided yet whether that made her a saint or a symptom. She'd chosen, publicly and loudly, to be neither, which took a kind of courage Oscar admired and could not currently afford.

"You've seen it," she said, dropping onto the rock beside him at the top of the rise. From here you could see the border stones, a ragged line of grey teeth marching off into fog, and beyond them the fog itself, which had always been there, forever, the valley's whole horizon.

"Yeah," Oscar said.

"Say it properly. What did you see?"

He'd been hoping she wouldn't ask. He told her anyway, because Wren had a face that made lying feel like throwing rocks at a bird. "The fog moved."

"How much?"

"An arm's length. Maybe two. Toward us." He held up his own arm — the boy's arm, thank goodness, the wrongness hadn't spread past the hands — and demonstrated. "Like a tide coming in. Slow. Like it's got all the time ever made." He paused. "Wren, the stones are still there. Nothing's crossed them. But the fog's leaning on them now. Like a dog leaning on a door."

Below them, the nearest border stone stood hip-high and ancient, furred with moss, carved all over with spirals worn nearly smooth. And around its base, unmistakably, snow had piled up in a ring — but the ring extended past the stone, out into the fog-side, a finger's width of white reaching into country that officially did not exist. The snow didn't seem to know about the border. Or it knew and didn't care. Or — and this was the thought that made Oscar's stomach fold over — the border was a rule, and rules only hold while everyone agrees on them, and something out there had recently remembered how to disagree.

"My leg doesn't ache in the cold anymore," Wren said quietly. "You know that? Every winter since I was four, the cold got into the bad knee first, like it knew the weak board in the fence. Now—" she knocked on her thigh, solid as a barrel, "—nothing. The god fixed me and the god's cold can't find anything wrong with me. Sometimes I think it left a mark. Like a mason's stamp on the inside."

"Do you regret it?" Oscar asked. "Being healed."

Wren was quiet long enough that a crow flew over and commented on the silence. "I regret that I can't answer that," she said finally. "My ma says gratitude's a debt like any other. My da says debts to gods compound fastest of all. And my knee says *thank you, thank you, thank you* every single morning when I get out of bed without falling over." She shrugged. "All three of them are right. That's the trap, isn't it? You can't hate a gift. That's how gifts work. They make hating impossible first, so the taking feels like your own idea."

Oscar looked at his hands.

"Aye," he said. "Something like that."

* * *

Evening came down early and lavender-colored, and the village gathered in the square the way villages do when the world gets strange — not to decide anything, just to be numerous together. Someone had lit the big bonfire pit, months out of season, and someone else had dragged out barrels, and Mrs. Tam had brought rolls, and for an hour or two it was almost a festival, except festivals have reasons and this one had only weather.

Oscar sat on a bench near the fire with his hands wrapped around a mug of hot cider and listened to the village argue in the low, circular way villages argue, everyone saying things everyone had already said, because the saying itself was the point. Mr. Hobbes held that the snow was a judgment. Mrs. Tam held that any judgment that ripened your barley was welcome to judge away. The miller wanted to know who'd pay for flour ground from grain harvested in summer. Widow Marsh, wrapped in three shawls, said mildly that in her grandmother's day the valley had prayed to somebody or other every autumn for exactly this kind of kindness, and gotten none of it, and maybe they should think about why the answering had stopped for so long, and the argument rolled on over her like water over a stone.

Oscar drank his cider and watched the fire and felt the borrowed luck humming faintly under his skin, the way a struck bell hums after the sound seems done.

Then the snow began to fall again — soft, dry, glittering, every flake the same seven-pointed star — and the arguing stopped, because it really was beautiful, there was no getting around it, it was beautiful the way a siren song is presumably beautiful, and the whole village stood there in the firelight letting it fall on them with their faces turned up.

A child laughed. Somebody started singing, one of the old harvest songs, and others joined, and for a few minutes Lowmarsh sounded like Lowmarsh at its best, warm and rough and slightly out of tune.

And then the singing changed key.

Nobody decided to. Nobody noticed at first — Oscar noticed because he'd spent a year listening for exactly this sort of slippage, back when a candle flame had bent toward his voice and taught him what voices could do. The song was the same song. But underneath it, in the spaces between the singers, another voice had joined in, one octave down, vast and slow and patient, harmonizing. Not singing *words*. Just holding the note. Holding it the way the fog held the horizon. Holding it the way a debt holds a debtor.

The villagers trailed off one by one, unsettled, laughing nervously, blaming the wind, the barrels, the cider. Only Oscar kept staring at the fire, because the fire had bent — every flame in every torch and lantern in the square leaning a half-inch east, toward the border, toward the fog, like grass bending under a wind that wasn't blowing.

Like candles bending toward a voice.

*I know you,* Oscar thought at it, furious and small and fourteen years old, with his dead grandfather's hands. *I know what you are. I heard you first.*

The flames straightened. The vast note faded. The snow went on falling, identical stars, gentle as forgiveness.

But as the crowd broke up and Oscar headed home through the blue dark, he passed the notice board outside the tavern, and stopped, because there was fresh paper on it, and fresh paper in Lowmarsh meant either a tax or a summons, and both were worth knowing about early.

It wasn't either. It was a single sheet in a careful clerk's hand:

**BY ORDER OF HER LADY VESS CORVANE — A GATHERING OF THE VALLEY'S LEARNED AT CORVANE HOUSE, ON THE MATTER OF THE THIRTEENTH MOON AND WHAT IT MEANS. ALL SCHOLARS, ARCHIVISTS, AND KEEPERS OF RECORDS ARE REQUESTED TO ATTEND. REFRESHMENTS PROVIDED.**

*Refreshments provided.* Oscar snorted. Lady Vess providing refreshments was like a spider providing web.

But one line snagged him, and he read it three times under the light of a torch whose flame kept trying, very subtly, to bend: *all scholars, archivists, and keepers of records.* Which meant Pell. Old Pell, the archivist, who lived alone with his papers and his cough and his locked cabinets, and who had gone pale as whey three days ago when Oscar mentioned — casually, innocently, just making conversation — that the kitchen calendar had thirteen moons marked on it.

Pell had shut his window after that. Hadn't opened it since.

Oscar stood in the falling god's-weather, snow gathering on his old man's shoulders, and made a decision that felt less like deciding than like arriving somewhere he'd been heading all along.

First thing tomorrow, he'd go see Pell. Because if anyone in the valley knew what the thirteenth moon was counting down *to*, it was the keeper of records — and because Oscar had a growing, cold-handed suspicion that whatever Pell knew, someone had burned part of it, and that the burning was the most important fact of all.
