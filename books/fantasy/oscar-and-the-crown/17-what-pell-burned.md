## 17. What Pell Burned

The archives of the Valley of the Unnamed occupied the lowest floor of the palace, which was fitting, because the archives were where the valley kept everything it preferred not to think about.

Pell had worked there for fifty-one years. He knew every shelf by its smell—the leather-bound histories on the north wall smelled of dust and dignity, the tax ledgers of the eastern shelves smelled of mildew and regret, and the restricted cabinet at the very back, the one with three locks, smelled faintly, persistently, of smoke.

That smell had never made sense to him before this winter. Now it made all the sense in the world, and he wished it didn't.

He was re-shelving a volume of rainfall records—fascinating reading, if you enjoyed watching the same numbers repeat themselves for two hundred years, which Pell did, because repetition was the whole architecture of the valley—when he heard footsteps on the archive stairs. Slow ones. Deliberate ones. He knew those footsteps too.

"Archivist," said Mara Kavanagh from the bottom step.

"The food-taster," Pell replied, without turning around. "Come to check the king's soup against my records? I'm afraid the soup is not catalogued."

"I came about the treaty."

Pell's hands went still on the rainfall ledger. Rain did not fall in the valley; it had not fallen in living memory; the moisture simply arrived each morning like a servant who had given up knocking. And yet the rainfall records continued, year after year, dutifully recording nothing, because that was what the valley did. It recorded nothing, faithfully, forever.

"You mean the founding treaty," he said.

"I mean whatever's missing from it." Mara came down the last steps and stood among the shelves, arms crossed, looking younger than she was and older than she should have been, which was true of nearly everyone born nameless. "I've read it now. Three times. It's short, Pell. A bargain that unmade every name in a country, and the treaty describing it is four pages long. There are clauses referenced that aren't there. Article Nine mentions 'the schedule of surrendered names'—and then there is no schedule. Article Eleven refers to 'the terms of return'—and there are no terms. Someone cut them out."

"Not cut out," Pell said quietly. "Burned."

* * *

They sat across from each other at his worktable, between the lamp and the locked cabinet, and Pell poured tea from the dented pot he kept for occasions requiring courage. He had bought the pot forty years ago specifically for such occasions and had used it perhaps six times. The tea inside was old enough to have opinions.

"You have to understand what the valley was like when I was young," he began. "You've grown up in the stasis, so you think it's natural. Fog that never lifts. Crops that grow exactly enough and no more. A king who sleeps through his own reign. But when I was a boy, there were still people alive who remembered the sealing itself. My grandmother was one. She remembered her mother weeping over a name she could no longer say—not forbidden, understand, *gone*. She'd open her mouth to say it and there would be nothing there, like reaching into a pocket and finding the pocket has been sewn shut from the inside."

Mara said nothing. She was good at saying nothing; it was practically her profession. But she watched him the way she must have watched the king's cup, waiting for the poison to show itself.

"My grandmother told me the bargain was fair," Pell went on. "That's what everyone told everyone, for generations. Fair and necessary. Magic had come into the world like a flood, and our ancestors built an ark and closed the door, and yes, they drowned their own names to do it, but everyone aboard lived. That was the story. That was the story I believed when I took this post at nineteen years old, with ink-stained cuffs and a head full of other people's footnotes."

He turned his teacup in a slow circle. Outside, somewhere above them, the palace creaked through its endless afternoon.

"And then?" Mara prompted.

"And then I found the schedule."

* * *

It had been thirty-seven years ago, in autumn—or in what passed for autumn here, the season when the fog grew a shade grayer and the kitchen started making soup with turnips in it. Pell had been thirty-one, newly promoted, and had been given the task no one else wanted: inventorying the restricted cabinet, item by item, so that the archivist before him could finally retire with a clear conscience.

Old Archivist Hobb had been archivist for sixty years. He was a small, dry man who smelled of pipe smoke and lamp oil, and who had taught Pell everything worth knowing about the collection—including, though Pell hadn't understood it at the time, exactly which questions not to ask.

"The third lock was rusted," Pell told Mara. "Hobb gave me the key anyway. He sat in that chair—" he pointed "—and he watched me the entire time I worked. He'd never done that before. I thought it was pride. Passing on the collection. It wasn't pride."

Inside the cabinet, behind the histories and the sealed correspondence, Pell had found a folio bound in gray leather: *The Schedule of Surrendered Names, Being a True and Complete Accounting.* Four hundred pages. Every family in the valley. Every name given up, written out in full, in the handwriting of the bargainers themselves—and beside each name, in a second hand, something that had made Pell's skin crawl even before he understood what he was reading.

"What was beside them?" Mara asked. Her voice was very level. Too level. The voice of someone tasting something and refusing to react.

"A price," Pell said. "Every surrendered name had a price entered next to it. Not a payment received—a payment *owed*. Do you see? The treaty reads as though the names were given freely, a sacrifice, clean and final. They weren't. They were *held*. The land held them. And the second column recorded..." He stopped, and drank the terrible tea, and made himself finish. "It recorded interest. Accruing. Generation after generation. The debt of every name, compounding, marked in the margin in dates. The last entry in the folio was dated the year of my birth."

Mara had gone very pale. "Owed to whom?"

"To the land itself," Pell said. "Or to what the land was before the seal. I didn't know then. I know better now, with your thirteenth moon climbing the kitchen wall and prayers getting answered at the border. Whatever our ancestors bartered with—it wasn't a flood they were keeping out, girl. It was a creditor."

* * *

The lamp flickered. Neither of them moved to trim the wick.

"You burned it," Mara said. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table between them, like a dish to be tasted.

"I burned it," Pell agreed. "And I have regretted it every day since, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I don't expect you to make sense of that, because I can't."

He told her the rest, because she had come all this way down his stairs and deserved the whole poison, not a sip of it.

The schedule had listed the Kavanagh line on page one. Of course it had—they were first among the bargainers, the royal blood, the lock itself. And beside the Kavanagh name, in that second, patient hand, the owed price was different from all the others. The other families had surrendered single names. The Kavanaghs had surrendered a *name of names*—the entry ran three pages, and the marginalia grew denser and more agitated as it went, the old handwriting pressing harder and harder into the paper, until the final lines were gouged deep enough to read by touch.

And at the very end of the Kavanagh entry, there was a note in a third hand entirely—younger writing, hurried, almost frightened:

*Bargain amended by consent of the crown. The name shall be returned when asked for properly. Until then, let no record of its shape survive.*

"Asked for properly," Mara repeated.

"That phrase has been sitting in my chest for thirty-seven years like a swallowed hook," Pell said. "What does it mean, *asked for properly*? Who asks? What makes an asking proper? I was twenty minutes into wondering when Hobb cleared his throat."

* * *

"He didn't shout," Pell remembered. "That was the worst part. I had the folio half off the shelf, and I looked up, and there was this small dry old man looking at me with an expression I'd never seen on him—grief, but worn smooth, the way a stair is worn smooth by feet. And he said, 'You found the second column.'"

Pell had said something clever and scholarly, he couldn't remember what. Probably something about preservation and completeness and the archivist's sacred duty to the historical record. Hobb had listened to all of it, nodding gently, like a man hearing a child explain why the moon should be eaten.

Then he had said: "My predecessor burned the last copy of the amendment. Her predecessor burned the original petition. And my predecessor's predecessor—" here Hobb had smiled, terribly "—burned a man. A royal clerk who tried to take the schedule to the border and give it back. So you'll forgive me, boy, if I'm not impressed by duty."

"Why?" Mara whispered. "Why hide it? Why burn the truth for generations?"

"Because of who the amendment protected," Pell said. "Read the note again in your mind. *By consent of the crown.* The crown amended the bargain. The crown—the Kavanagh kings, your ancestors' descendants—took a debt that belonged to the whole valley and made it theirs alone. They said: the name of names will come back through us or not at all. And then they spent two hundred years making sure no one ever asked for it properly. Because as long as the debt stayed hidden, the seal held, and the valley slept, and nobody had to find out what the land would do when it finally woke up and presented its bill."

"They were jailers," Mara said slowly. "Of their own people. Of themselves."

"They were frightened people holding a door shut with their bodies," Pell said. "It looks like tyranny from the safe side of the door. I imagine it feels like duty from the other side. Hobb believed that. I think he *had* to believe it, or the burning would have been unforgivable." He set down his cup. "And then one night, near the end, he told me why the burning had to continue, and it wasn't duty at all."

* * *

"There was a person," Pell said. "A specific person, alive at the time of the amendment, whom the note was written to protect. Hobb wouldn't tell me the name—of course he wouldn't, names being what they are in this country—but he told me this much: when the crown amended the bargain, one person in the valley still carried a piece of the stolen name. Not the whole of it. A fragment. Enough that if the creditor ever came collecting, that person would be the first door it knocked on. The amendment hid the fragment. Hid it so well that the person themselves never knew what they were carrying."

Mara's breath had gone shallow. "Who?"

"Hobb died before he told me. He was dying when he decided to tell me at all—that's why he let me into the cabinet, why he watched me. He wanted a successor who'd seen the truth and chosen, knowingly, to keep burning. He got his wish." Pell laughed, a small unhappy sound. "I've burned things since, you know. Little things. A letter here, a marginal note there, anything that looked like it might lead a curious reader toward the second column. Fifty years of tidy little arsons. I told myself it was stewardship. Then your cook counted thirteen moons on her wall, and a kitchen boy's candle bent toward his voice, and I understood that the bill has arrived regardless of what I burned. The fire was never going to hold. Fire doesn't hold doors shut. It only makes sure that when the door opens, we open it blind."

Silence filled the archive, the particular silence of a room full of things no one wants to say.

"So the god," Mara said at last, carefully, "has been owed a name for generations. Centuries. And someone in this valley—maybe descended from whoever Hobb protected—is still carrying a piece of it without knowing."

"Yes."

"And the king knows. That's why he woke up. That's why he asked me—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. Pell noticed, and filed it away, and had the grace not to ask.

"The king is the last of the line that wrote that note," he said instead. "Whatever he knows, he's known it his whole life, asleep. Which brings us to the unpleasant arithmetic, food-taster. If the debt comes due when the thirteenth moon fills—if the seal fails and the land presents its bill to a valley that doesn't even remember what it owes—then the only mercy left to us is knowledge. We need to know the terms of return. We need to know what *asked for properly* means. And every document that could tell us—" he gestured at the cabinet, at fifty years of his own careful ashes "—is smoke."

"Is there anything left?" Mara asked. "Anything at all?"

Pell rose, joints cracking like old binding, and went to the cabinet. Three locks. He opened the first two without thinking, and paused at the third, and looked back at her.

"When Hobb died, I inventoried his effects, as archivist. Among them was a letter addressed to me, sealed, with instructions to open it 'when the moons miscount.' I assumed it was an old man's poetry. I put it in the cabinet and forgot it for thirty years." His hand rested on the third lock. "Two weeks ago, a kitchen girl told me her calendar had grown a thirteenth mark. I came down here that same night."

"Moon the Fourth," Mara murmured. "Winter came early. Oscar says the fog at the border stones moves *wrong* now."

"Then we're past due." Pell turned the key.

Inside lay a single folded letter, yellowed, soft as cloth at the creases, addressed in Hobb's cramped hand: *For my successor, when the counting fails.*

Pell broke the seal with hands that were not quite steady, unfolded it, and read.

He read it twice. Then he sat down heavily, the way old men sit when the floor has become optional, and pushed the page across the table to Mara.

"Read the last line," he said. "Read it aloud. Some things need air."

Mara read. Her lips moved once, silently, checking. Then, quietly, in the voice she probably used for telling the king his breakfast was safe:

"*The Door Out is real. I saw it once, as a boy. West cliff, past the last shepherd's stone. And Pell—if you're reading this, the counting has failed, and there is no more time to be careful. Find the Kavanagh girl. She is the only one who can open it.*"

The lamp flame stood perfectly straight. Somewhere far above them, muffled by stone and fog and fifty years of burned paper, the palace bell rang the hour—and rang one stroke too many.
