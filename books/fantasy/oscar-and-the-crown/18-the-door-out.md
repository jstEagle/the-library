## 18. The Door Out

They left before dawn, which in the Valley of the Unnamed meant they left into a fog so thick it seemed less like weather than like a decision the valley had made about them.

Mara carried the map.

She had stolen it from Lady Vess Corvane's own writing desk three nights ago, with hands that had not trembled since she was nine years old and her mother first put hemlock on her tongue. Stealing had been easier than tasting, in the end. Vess's desk had smelled of ink and ambition, and the map had been folded beneath a letter to a mercenary captain whose name made Pell go quiet in the way he went quiet only around things that were genuinely dangerous.

Now the map was rolled inside her coat, against her heart, where stolen things belong until you can decide what to do with them.

"You're walking too fast," Wren said behind her. Not complaining—Wren did not complain; she narrated grievances the way other people recited poetry—but informing. "Some of us have legs that are miracles now but still miracles that ache."

"Your leg works," Oscar said.

"My leg *works*," Wren agreed. "It also has opinions."

Mara slowed anyway. She had spent her whole life being unnoticed, and there was a particular skill in it—a taster learns to read a room without entering it, to know by the set of a cook's shoulders whether the soup is safe—and she found herself reading her little company the same way now. Pell walked with his arms wrapped around a satchel of notes as though the papers might bolt. Oscar kept glancing at his hands, opening and closing them, checking. And Wren limped beautifully, triumphantly, each step a small argument against every god who had ever given her anything.

None of them should be here. That was the thought Mara kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. She had asked them—all of them—to come, and all of them had said yes, and she could not decide whether that made them brave or foolish or simply people who had run out of valley to hide in.

The fog thinned as they climbed. It always did, near the borders. The fog was the valley's breath, and it kept itself close to home.

* * *

Pell stopped at midmorning at a shrine no bigger than an oven, one of the old ones, its stone worn smooth by centuries of hands that had nothing left to offer but touch.

"We should rest," he said. "And I should say something, before we arrive, because after we arrive none of us will be listening."

He sat down on the shrine's low wall with the care of a man whose bones had opinions of their own, and he opened his satchel, and he took out the map—not Mara's stolen copy but his own memory of it, sketched from fragments he'd burned decades ago and never stopped regretting.

"I told you I destroyed pages of the founding treaty," he said. "I told you it was to protect someone. Both true. But I never told you what was on the pages I burned, because I was ashamed, and shame is a very good reason to keep one's mouth shut and a very bad reason to write history." He smoothed the paper on his knee. "There was a door. There has always been a door. The founders sealed the valley, but a seal that cannot be opened is not a seal—it's a tomb. So they left themselves one way out, and they locked it with the only thing they had left that magic couldn't take."

"Their names," Oscar said quietly.

"The absence of their names," Pell corrected gently. "A name locks it. A namelessness holds it shut. Which means—" and here the old archivist's voice did something Mara had never heard it do, something small and human and afraid, "—which means the door will know Mara on sight. And it will not be pleased to see her."

Wren looked at Mara. Oscar looked at Mara. Even the fog seemed to look at Mara.

"Wonderful," Wren said. "So we've walked two days to deliver our friend to a door that hates her."

"Doors don't hate," Pell said. "But they remember. And this one has been remembering for four hundred years."

* * *

They came to the cliff in the last hour of afternoon light.

Mara had expected—she didn't know what she had expected. A cave mouth. A crack in the rock. Something apologetic, something hidden, something that would require belief to see. The valley had taught her that important things kept themselves small: the king ruled fog from a throne room the size of a chapel; the most dangerous woman in the country kept her ambitions in a writing desk; the end of the world had announced itself as an extra mark on a kitchen calendar.

The door was none of these things.

It stood in the cliff face the way a sentence stands in silence—suddenly obvious, impossible to unsee, and utterly certain of itself. A door, tall and dark and bound in iron gone green with age, set into bare gray stone. No wall around it. No frame holding it. No path leading to it, just scree and lichen and four hundred years of nothing. The cliff rose above it for a hundred feet and fell away below it for fifty more, and the door simply stood there in the middle of the rock as if the rock had grown around it out of politeness.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Wren said, "That's the ugliest miracle I've ever seen," and sat down heavily on a boulder, because some wonders demand to be sat down in front of.

Oscar stepped closer. Of course he did. He was a kitchen boy who had once prayed over a dying goat and been answered, and doors that stood in no wall were exactly the sort of thing his life had become. He raised his hand toward the iron—

"Don't," Mara said.

Her voice came out strange. Flat. The voice she used when she put a spoon down.

Oscar stopped. His hand hung in the air a foot from the door, and Mara watched him watch it, and she saw the moment he felt it too—the air between his fingers and the iron was not empty. It was full. Pressurized. Like the held breath of something enormous.

"It's warm," he whispered. "Mara, the air's warm. Like standing outside an oven."

"That's because something is cooking," Pell said grimly. "Metaphorically. I'd keep my hands to myself, boy. Doors like this don't open from curiosity."

"How do they open?" Wren asked.

Pell looked at Mara.

Mara looked at the door.

And the door, slowly, with the terrible patience of stone and iron and four centuries of waiting, began to notice her.

She felt it the way she felt poison—not in her mind but in her body, a wrongness under the tongue, a taste with no flavor and all of them at once. The door had no eyes, but it turned its attention on her the way a courtroom turns on the accused. Her nameless blood, the lock of the valley, the traded inheritance running in her veins—the door read all of it in a single long moment, and then it did the last thing she expected.

It shuddered.

Not opened. Not swung wide with welcome or groaned shut in anger. It shuddered, the whole impossible slab of it, like a dog hearing a whistle only it can hear, and from somewhere deep inside the cliff came a sound that Mara felt in her teeth rather than heard with her ears—a low, vast note, like the land itself clearing its throat after four hundred years of silence.

"What is it doing?" Oscar breathed.

"Asking," said Pell, white-faced. "Dear gods of everywhere, it's asking."

"Asking what?"

Mara knew. She knew the way she knew hemlock, the way she knew nightshade, the way she knew every poison her mother had ever fed her—not by study but by the body's terrible fluency. The door was asking her a question, and the question was a name, and it wanted hers.

Except she didn't have one. That was the whole point of her. That was the bargain, the seal, the lock. Her ancestors had poured their names away like wine down a drain, and what remained—what the door was straining against, what made it tremble—was the shape of the absence. A keyhole shaped like a girl who had never been called anything.

"It wants me to open it," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "And I can't. Because opening it needs a name, and I'm the thing that keeps names out."

"No," Pell said softly. He had gone very still, the stillness of a man watching a theory come true. "No, child, worse than that. Look at it. It's not asking you to open it. It's asking you to *let it be opened*. There's a difference. The lock isn't on the door, Mara. The lock is you. You're not the person who turns the key. You're the reason the key doesn't turn."

The sound from inside the cliff faded. The door went still. The warmth drained out of the air, and the evening cold came rushing back into the space between Oscar's fingers and the iron, and he pulled his hand away as if the rock had bitten it.

For a while, nobody moved. The sun went down behind them and painted the fog-filled valley below in colors it had no business wearing—rose and copper and gold, beautiful, borrowed, ending.

"So we came all this way," Wren said at last, into the dusk, "to find the way out of prison. And the way out is locked. And the lock is our friend."

"Our friend is the warden," Pell said. "Or half of her is. The other half is the prisoner. I honestly don't know which half is which anymore, and neither, I suspect, does she."

Mara reached out—not to the door, but to the stone beside it, and laid her palm flat against the cliff. Cold rock. Ordinary rock. Except that under her hand, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow, something was waiting.

Four hundred years, her ancestors had kept this valley sealed and safe and small. Four hundred years, and the land itself had been the price, caged under its own name, dreaming at the bottom of the fog. And now the moon was waxing toward its thirteenth turn, and the door was trembling, and the god at the border stones wanted what it was owed, and Lady Vess Corvane had a map and mercenaries and a plan, and none of them—not the god, not Vess, not even Pell with all his burned pages—knew what Mara suddenly knew, with her hand on the stone and her mother's poisons in her blood:

A lock can be swallowed.

She took her hand away. The heartbeat under the rock did not stop.

"We should get off the cliff before full dark," she said, in her ordinary voice, her invisible voice, the voice of a woman who tasted danger for a living. "And we need to move fast. Because we weren't the only ones with a map."

Below them, far down in the gathering blue, a line of lanterns was winding up through the fog toward the cliff. Eight of them. Nine. Ten.

Vess had not needed to steal anything after all.

She had let herself be robbed.
