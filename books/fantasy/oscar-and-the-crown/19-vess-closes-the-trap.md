## 19. Vess Closes the Trap

Vess Corvane had planned this dinner for eleven years, and she was not going to let a little thing like treason ruin the soup.

She stood at the head of the long table in her townhouse on Lantern Row, adjusting a candelabra by a finger's width, then another finger's width back to where it had been. The candles were beeswax, imported—well, not imported; nothing in the valley was imported—but rendered from wax that had been hoarded since before anyone could remember, which amounted to the same thing. Luxury in the Valley of the Unnamed meant old things. Old wax. Old silver. Old lies.

The soup was chestnut and smoked pork, her cook's masterpiece, and it would be tasted by the royal taster herself, which Vess had insisted upon with the particular courtesy of a woman who wanted something very much.

"Is she here yet?" she asked her steward.

"The party's coming up the hill now, my lady. The archivist is complaining about the stairs."

"Of course he is." Pell complained about stairs the way other men complained about weather: constantly, ritually, and without any expectation of improvement. "And the boy?"

"Oscar. Yes. He tried to pocket one of the candlesticks."

"Did you let him?"

"He dropped it when I looked at him."

"Shame," said Vess. "It would have been good practice for him."

This was, she reflected, almost true. Everything about tonight was almost true, which was the only kind of truth that survived contact with the Corvane family.

* * *

They arrived as the light failed—the taster Mara Kavanagh in her plain grey servant's dress, looking like a sparrow that had wandered into an aviary of peacocks and decided not to apologize for it; Pell in his archive dust-cloak, already breathing heavily from the stairs; Oscar trailing behind them with his hands jammed in his pockets like a boy who knew exactly what temptation lived in rich houses; and Wren, the lame girl—or formerly lame girl, Vess corrected herself, because the leg worked now, whatever else the gods' gifts might cost—walking with the careful wonder of someone still not quite convinced the ground would hold.

"Lady Corvane," said Mara. Her voice was flat as a serving tray. "You honor us."

"I honor *you*, Mistress Kavanagh. The others are ballast." Vess smiled to show it was a joke. It landed somewhere near a joke, the way a stone lands near a pond. "Come in. The soup is getting cold, and cold chestnut soup is an insult to both chestnuts and soup."

Dinner proceeded. Vess had seated them carefully: Pell beside her, because scholars could be managed through flattery of their footnotes; Wren next to Oscar, because the two of them had the nervous energy of young people who had recently discovered the world was larger than advertised; and Mara across from her, where Vess could watch her face while pretending to watch the candles.

Mara tasted everything. Of course she did. That was the whole point of her, wasn't it? A woman built like a lockpick, thin and unremarkable and utterly essential, trained since childhood to swallow death and report its flavor. Vess watched her take the first spoonful of soup, hold it, wait the customary count, and swallow.

"Well?" Vess asked lightly. "Do I live?"

"You live," said Mara.

"For now," said Pell into his wine.

"Pell." Vess laughed. "You've been reading doomsday documents again."

"I've been reading *all* the documents again. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm right." He set down his glass with the care of a man handling something explosive. "The moon is past half, Lady Corvane. The border stones are cracking. People are forgetting their own names in the outer villages—there's a woman in Millbrook who answered to 'what-was-it' for three days before someone remembered for her. And you're serving chestnut soup."

"That's why I'm serving chestnut soup," said Vess. "The world may end, but it will end fed."

It got the laugh she wanted—even Mara's mouth twitched—and under cover of it, Vess let her eyes move once around the table. Four guests. Four pieces. She had spent eleven years assembling lesser pieces: the mercenary captain Brannoc currently drinking her cellar dry in the kitchen with six of his men; the archivist's junior clerk who copied maps for coin; two palace guards whose loyalty was to their pensions. But these four at her table were the mechanism itself. The map. The blood. The key. And the two children who had gotten closer to the Door than anyone in living memory.

She only needed them all in one room. Preferably a room with a locked door.

* * *

The trap, properly speaking, closed between the fish course and the sweet.

Vess had chosen the moment with a general's precision. Fish made people sleepy; she'd ordered the heavy white wine for exactly this reason. Pell was mid-anecdote about a mislabeled folio, Wren was laughing at Oscar's impression of the antlered god—"he doesn't say anything, that's the thing, he just *waits* at you"—and Mara had relaxed by perhaps four percent, which for Mara constituted abandon.

Then the doors opened, and Brannoc came in with six men, and the temperature of the room changed the way a room changes when a dog stops wagging its tail.

"Oh," said Pell, in the small voice of a man watching eleven years of scholarship walk out the door ahead of him. "Oh, you *foolish* woman."

"Lady Corvane," said Mara. She hadn't stood up. Vess noted that; it was the most frightening thing about her. "What is this?"

"This," said Vess, rising, spreading her hands, letting the smile come—the real one, the one she kept for moments of triumph, which were rare enough that it felt strange on her face, like wearing someone else's shoes—"is rescue."

Nobody moved. Somewhere below, a mercenary laughed at something, and the sound came up through the floorboards like a bad omen.

"Rescue," repeated Mara.

"From ourselves, if we must be dramatic about it." Vess walked the length of the table, unhurried, her heels loud on the boards. "Sit, everyone. Brannoc, don't loom; it's rude and you're bad at it. Sit. All of you. Dessert is plum tart and I'll be furious if it goes to waste."

They sat. Even Pell sat, though he arranged himself like a man sitting on a spike. Oscar's hand had crept toward the bread knife, and Brannoc, without appearing to look, moved so that his shadow fell across the boy's arm. Oscar withdrew the hand. Good lad. Sensible instincts.

"Now," said Vess, taking up her wine again, because a speech went better with something to gesture at. "Let me tell you what I know, and then let me tell you what I intend, and then—this is the important part—you can all stop looking at me like I've poisoned the tart. Which I haven't. Mara would know."

"She would," said Mara, and there was something almost like humor in it, grim as a gravestone.

"I know about the map," Vess said. "Your map. The one you took from my clerk, who took it from my archive, who took it from the founding vaults where it has sat for three hundred years waiting for someone with the spine to use it. I know you found the cliff. I know you found the door." She paused, savoring it. "Yes. I know about the door standing in no wall. I have known about it since I was nineteen years old and found my grandmother's letters, and I have spent every year since preparing for the night I'm standing in right now."

"You've been planning this since you were nineteen?" said Wren.

"Nineteen," confirmed Vess. "It was a very formative year. My mother died, my inheritance turned out to consist of debts and candlesticks, and I read a letter in which my grandmother explained that our family had been guarding a secret so large that the crown itself didn't know the half of it. Most people would have taken up drink. I took up cartography."

"And the mercenaries?" Pell asked. His voice had gone thin. "The armed men? Is that also cartography?"

"The mercenaries are *logistics*, Pell, honestly, keep up." Vess set down the glass. "Here is what none of you understand, because you've each been staring at your own piece of it. You"—she pointed at Pell—"see the treaty, the bargain, the names surrendered. You see paper. You"—at Mara—"see the crown, the bloodline, the key turning in your veins. You two"—at Oscar and Wren—"have seen the god itself, which is more than I have, and I confess I envy you and resent you in equal measure. But none of you has done the arithmetic."

"What arithmetic?" Mara asked quietly.

"The arithmetic of the thirteenth moon." Vess let the silence sit. "When it fills, the seal fails. Not cracks—fails. Every name comes due at once. Every prayer gets answered by whatever is hungry enough to answer. The fog rolls off the borders and the outside world sees us for the first time in three centuries, and do you know what they'll see? A country of the nearly-nameless, rich in old magic, defended by no army, ruled by a dead king and a kitchen girl. We will be *eaten*, Mistress Kavanagh. Not conquered. Eaten. There is a difference, and the difference is that conquerors leave institutions."

"So your plan is to run," said Mara. "Through the Door. Before the moon fills."

"My plan is to open the Door for *everyone*," said Vess, and here she leaned forward, and let them see—not the ambition, not the coldness, but the thing underneath, the thing that had kept her working by candlelight for eleven years while her peers married and gossiped and died small. "Every soul in this valley. That is the plan. Not escape for Lady Vess Corvane and six hired swords. An exodus. Organized. Provisioned. In order of vulnerability—children first, then the old, then the sick. I have lists. I have *routes*. I have grain stores and carts and a schedule keyed to the moon's phases, and I have been drilling volunteers in the eastern villages under the cover of a wool cooperative for two years. When the seal breaks, this valley will not scatter screaming into a world that forgot us. It will *walk out*, in column, with its records and its seed corn and its dead properly buried first, because we are not animals."

Silence. Wren's mouth had fallen slightly open. Even Brannoc, by the door, shifted his weight in a way that suggested he was hearing the full scope of his employer's madness for the first time and finding it, perhaps, grander than expected.

"But," said Pell slowly, "the Door is locked."

"The Door is locked with a name," Vess agreed. "A royal name. The founders were thorough that way—they bound the lock to the bloodline that bargained, so that only the bargain's heirs could ever undo it. Which brings me to the part of the evening I've been dreading, and I want you to know I've dreaded it, because I am not a monster, merely a realist."

She turned to Mara.

"Mistress Kavanagh. Taster. Cook's niece, orphan, ghost of the palace corridors. You are the last royal blood in the Valley of the Unnamed, and tonight you are going to help me turn the key."

* * *

The silence afterward had texture. Vess had time to catalog it: the particular stillness of Pell recalculating everything; the way Wren's hand had found Oscar's under the table; the way Mara—

Mara was smiling.

It was a small smile, tired, and it frightened Vess more than shouting would have.

"You knew," Vess said. "You already knew."

"I suspected after the king told me about the crown. Pell confirmed it with the treaty rolls. Your grandmother's letters would just have been corroboration." Mara folded her hands on the table, calm as a woman waiting for bread to prove. "So no, Lady Corvane, you haven't surprised me. What surprises me is that you think you need *mercenaries* for this."

"I need insurance," said Vess. "Because I know you, taster. I've watched you for years—don't look shocked, watching people is my hobby and the palace is a fishbowl. You are the least ambitious person I have ever met. You want nothing. Which means I cannot buy you, cannot flatter you, cannot threaten you with anything you actually value, because you've arranged your life to value nothing that can be taken. It's the most infuriating thing about you. So yes. Insurance." She gestured at Brannoc. "You will come to the cliff. You will put your royal blood against that door, on my schedule, with my organization, in service of saving forty thousand lives. Or you will sit in a comfortable room until the moon fills and then die nameless along with everyone else, and I will find some other way, because there is always another way, but it will be worse, and I will hate it."

"You'd really open it for everyone," said Oscar. He sounded young suddenly, and uncertain. "All of it. The whole valley."

"Everyone," said Vess. "I told you. I'm not building an escape hatch. I'm building a *door*."

"And after?" Pell's voice was dry as his archives. "After the valley walks out into a world that has forgotten it, into kingdoms that will notice a nation of refugees emerging from a cliff with three hundred years of hoarded magic—after that, Lady Corvane, what happens to the woman holding the lists and the routes and the schedules?"

Vess paused.

She had known this question was coming. She had rehearsed answers to it in the bath, on the stairs, in the dark before sleep. The honest answer was that she didn't know—that she had never once in eleven years thought past the walking-out, past the moment the last cart cleared the cliff, because thinking past it felt like a soldier counting the war's cost before winning it.

"After," she said, "the valley will need a regent. Someone organized. Someone who can negotiate with the outside on our behalf. Someone who has, frankly, been preparing for the job since she was nineteen." She lifted her chin. "Yes, Pell. Me. I am telling you to your faces that I intend to lead the exodus and then govern what remains of us, and I am telling you this *now*, before you're committed to anything, because unlike certain gods and courtiers I could mention, I prefer my ambitions stated plainly. Judge me for it. But judge me *while I save your lives*."

"You believe it," said Wren softly. "You actually believe you're the hero of this."

"I believe," said Vess, "that heroes are a luxury of countries that exist. We don't get one. We get me."

* * *

They were escorted—Brannoc preferred the word *escorted*; Vess allowed him the fiction—to the guest rooms, which were comfortable and had locks on the outside, because Vess's grandmother had entertained interesting guests and believed in preparation.

Vess stood alone in her study afterward, listening to the house settle, and permitted herself, briefly, the exhaustion she'd been carrying since the moon turned.

It had gone well. It had gone as well as such nights go. The taster hadn't fought her; the scholar hadn't lectured her into paralysis; nobody had bled. Tomorrow they'd ride for the cliff with the full company, and in nine days the moon would fill, and the Door would open, and—

The candle nearest her bent.

Not guttered. *Bent.* Leaned toward the window like a flower toward sun, flame stretching, and the shadows in the room swung wrong, and the air acquired the specific patience of a held breath.

Vess did not scream. She was a Corvane; they kept records of their composure. But her hand found the edge of the desk and gripped it.

Outside the window, down the long slope of her gardens, at the gate where no figure should have been standing, something made of antlers and darkness waited among her rosebushes. It did not move. It did not need to. It simply watched the house with the expression of a creditor reviewing accounts.

"You have my guests," it seemed to say, without words, without sound, the way winter says things. "How thoughtful. Gathered together. Saved me the walking."

The candle straightened. The night resumed.

Vess Corvane poured herself the last of the white wine with a steady hand, drank it in one motion, and began, immediately, to revise her schedule.
