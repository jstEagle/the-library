## 20. The God's Bargain

The door stood in no wall.

That was the first thing Mara had to keep reminding herself of, because her eyes kept trying to fix the problem. A cliff face of grey stone, weathered and ordinary, lichen creeping down it like slow green spillage—and set into it, at the height of a person, a door. Oak, iron-banded, with a ring handle gone smooth from centuries of hands that had never touched it. Behind it: solid rock. Around it: solid rock. And yet when the wind came off the valley floor and reached the door, it stopped, politely, as if arriving at a threshold rather than a cliff.

"It's beautiful," Wren said, in the hushed voice people used in chapels. She was leaning on her stick—her healed leg aching, she'd said, from the climb, though Mara suspected the ache had more to do with what had fixed the leg than with the climbing. "It looks like my grandmother's pantry door."

"Your grandmother had good taste," Pell murmured. He was crouched before the door with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, copying the faint carvings around the frame into his notebook with the feverish diligence of a man who expected to be interrupted. He usually was.

The interruption came, as interruptions do, from behind them.

"Mara Kavanagh," said Lady Vess Corvane, "you are surrounded."

Mara turned. Six mercenaries stood along the ridge above the cliff path—Vess's men, the ones who had been "hunting bandits" in the eastern woods all season, which everyone had politely pretended to believe. They were arranged with professional care: two blocking the path down, four spread wide with crossbows held low but not too low. Vess herself stood in front of them in riding leathers, her grey-streaked hair pinned up neatly, as though she'd come for a picnic and merely brought soldiers instead of sandwiches.

Oscar stepped in front of Mara. It would have been gallant if he weren't fourteen and holding a kitchen knife he'd clearly grabbed on instinct, the kind used for trimming artichokes.

"Oh, put that down, boy," Vess said. "You'll hurt yourself, and I've no use for a maimed ambassador." Her eyes went past him, found Mara, and softened into something almost kind, which was the most frightening thing about her. "You were never going to open it without me, you know. I don't blame you. But the crown is needed, and the crown is on your head, and here we all are."

"You could have just asked us to walk together," Wren said.

"I did ask. In the root cellars. You said no." Vess shrugged, an elegant little motion. "I find 'no' is a word that stops meaning much once the moon gets full enough. Bind them."

The mercenaries came forward. There was a brief, undignified scramble—Pell clutched his notebook to his chest like a shield and got his wrists bound anyway; Wren swung her stick and connected satisfyingly with someone's shin before being relieved of it; Oscar actually managed to cut one man's sleeve before three of them sat on him, gently, the way you'd restrain a large dog you were fond of. Mara didn't fight at all. She stood very still and watched Vess, because Vess was watching the door.

That was the mistake. That was the tell. Lady Vess Corvane, who had spent months assembling allies and maps and mercenaries, who wanted the Door Out so badly she could taste it—that woman was not looking at the door.

She was looking past it. At the treeline below the cliff, where the fog had begun to gather.

* * *

It came up through the trees the way evening comes: not arriving, simply being noticed. The fog thickened, gathered itself, and out of its middle walked a figure made of antlers and patience.

Mara had seen it once before, at a distance, at the border stone—the night Oscar came back smelling of pine sap and secrets, saying *it called me little borrower, it offered me a name*. She had thought then that the god was tall. Up close, height seemed beside the point. The antlers branched and rebranched until they were less horns than a winter forest seen in profile, and between them there was a darkness where a face should have been, and in the darkness two points of light like distant windows in an otherwise dark house.

Every mercenary took a step backward. One of them dropped his crossbow, which discharged into the dirt with a sound like a rude cough.

"Lady Corvane." The voice was the fog's voice, if fog could be tired. It came from everywhere and slightly underground. "You have brought me the key."

"I have brought you nothing," Vess said, and her voice only shook a little, which Mara had to admire. "I've brought myself a way out of this prison you've made. Stand aside."

"The key stands before you wearing a stolen crown." The window-lights turned to Mara. "And the thief stands beside it wearing borrowed antlers of her own ambition. Everyone in this valley is wearing something that belongs to another." A pause, long enough that the wind filled it. "Except one."

Mara's mouth was dry. "If this is about the goat," she said, "that was Oscar, and honestly the prayer was very sweet, you should have heard it—"

"Mara." Her name in that voice was strange—not heavy, the way names were supposed to be heavy in the valley, but *light*, the way a thing is light when it has been carried a long way and can finally be set down. "Little lock. Little last daughter of the nameless line. Do you know what I am?"

"A god," said Pell, from the ground, involuntarily, the way a scholar answers any question put to him. "Or—a piece of one. Or—" His eyes went wide behind his bound hands. "Oh. Oh, you're not the god at all, are you. You're the *land*."

The antlered figure inclined its head, slowly, branches whispering against each other like pages turning.

"The valley is my body," it said. "My name is my breath. Your ancestors took my breath and locked it in their bloodline, and sealed my body in fog, and called it peace. For nine hundred years I have been a country that cannot say itself. Every prayer that bounced off your border stones bounced off *me*. Every child born here was born inside a held breath." The lights dimmed, gentled. "I am not angry, little taster. Anger requires the luxury of time, and I have spent all mine learning patience. But the seal is failing whether you will it or no. When the thirteenth moon fills, everything I am comes back at once—and a breath returning after nine hundred years does not knock. It breaks the house."

"And the Door?" Vess demanded. "What's behind the Door?"

"Nothing. It is the hinge of the seal, nothing more. Open it wrongly and the valley dies with the seal. Open it rightly—" the lights turned back to Mara "—and the name returns gently, and the valley wakes instead of shattering."

"How?" Mara asked. Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"You are the lock. Your blood holds my name the way a bottle holds a letter. Give me back the crown-key, and I will give you back the only thing your family ever surrendered that they did not choose to surrender." The god knelt—it knelt, the whole winter-forest of it folding down until those far-off window-lights were level with her eyes. "Your name, Mara. The one taken at birth. The one your mother starved and poisoned you to make you strong enough to live without. I have kept it safe all these years, the way you keep a spoonful of poison safe in a locked cupboard. I will pour it back into you, and you will be *whole*. You will be somebody. You will walk out of this valley a person with a name, and every door in the world will open to a named woman."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Mara became aware, distantly, that she was crying. She wiped her face with her bound hands, awkwardly, like a child.

"That's not fair," she whispered.

"No," the god agreed. "Bargains rarely are. That is why they are bargains and not gifts."

* * *

She had never known what she was missing. That was the cruelty of it, and also the mercy, and she had built her whole life on top of that mercy like a house on a filled-in well.

A taster has no name worth having. That was the doctrine she'd grown up inside. Names had weight in the valley—her mother had told her that while feeding her thimblefuls of bitter things, stroking her hair, saying *this is love, little one, this is how I keep you untouchable*. The nameless couldn't be cursed, couldn't be summoned, couldn't be bound. The nameless slid through the world like water through fingers. Mara had taken pride in it. Chapter one of her life, the pride of being unnoticed: the perfect servant in a palace that served no kingdom, invisible and therefore safe, and therefore free.

But standing in front of a kneeling god who held her name in its keeping like a candle cupped against wind, she discovered the flaw in the doctrine. You cannot miss what you never had—but oh, you can want it, suddenly and completely, the moment it is offered. It was like tasting a dish she'd never eaten and recognizing, instantly, that she had been hungry for it her entire life.

*Mara.* Not the sound of it—she'd heard that all her life, from cooks and kings and one kitchen boy who said it like it was a good joke they shared. The *weight* of it. To be a specific person, pinned to the world like a butterfly to a board, findable, summonable, blessable, curseable. To be touchable. To belong somewhere so thoroughly that the belonging had a handle.

She thought of the king, lucid for one afternoon, asking her real name with tears in his eyes because he knew what it cost her not to have one. She thought of her mother's recipe book, the final page, the recipe for unbinding written in a hand gone shaky: *for my daughter, who must be whole to do what I could not.*

Whole. Her mother had wanted her whole. The god was offering wholeness like a merchant offering bread.

"What happens to them," Mara asked, "if I take the deal? If I hand over the crown and take my name and walk out?"

"The seal fails as it would have failed anyway. Quickly, violently. Some will live. Many will not." No apology in it. Gods, even patient ones, do not apologize for arithmetic.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we come to the harder bargain, and I would rather not. I have waited nine hundred years, little taster. I can wait three more moons. But I cannot wait *kindly*."

"Mara, don't." Oscar had wriggled half upright between two mercenaries, his face blotchy, his hands—his old man's hands, aged ten years by a debt she still hadn't let him explain—clawing at the grass. "Don't listen to it. It gave me a name and it *cost*, it always costs—"

"This is different, little borrower," the god said mildly. "This is a return, not a loan."

"There's no such thing!" Oscar shouted. "There's no such thing as getting something back free! I *know*!"

"He's right," Mara said quietly, and something in her chest had gone very calm, the way it went calm when she lifted the taster's spoon, when the poison was already in her mouth and all that remained was to find out what kind. "Everything costs. That's the first rule of the kitchen." She looked at the god. "So here's my counter-offer."

The window-lights brightened with something that might have been surprise.

"You want your name back. Fine. But I'm not giving you the crown-key to break the seal fast and loud and let the chips fall. My mother left me a recipe. Unbinding. It's slow, and it's careful, and it requires exactly what I am—a crowned, nameless taster—willing to swallow your name like poison and meter it out the way I've metered out every deadly thing I've ever tasted. One measured dose at a time. The valley wakes *gently*. Nobody's house breaks." She swallowed. "In exchange—you don't give me my name back now. You hold it. And when the unbinding is done, when the valley can say itself again—then you give it back. Freely. As a gift. Because I'll have earned it, and because you'll owe me, and gods should owe somebody for once."

"*Mara,*" Vess breathed, and for the first time since the ridge, she sounded genuinely alarmed. "You stupid girl, you're negotiating with *it*—"

"Somebody has to," Mara said. "You brought soldiers. I brought a spoon. We'll see whose tool holds up better."

The god was silent for a long moment. Wind moved through the antlers with a sound like a forest deciding something.

"You understand," it said at last, softly, "that what you propose will hurt you more than anything I could have done to you. Taking a name in sips, across moons, while crowned and nameless and carrying it for a whole country—that is not a bargain, little taster. That is a martyrdom with better manners."

"I'm a food taster," Mara said. "Hurt is the job description. I've been swallowing terrible things for other people's sake since I was six years old." She heard her own voice steady, and marveled at it. "At least this meal is finally for everybody."

Pell made a small strangled noise that was either scholarly horror or applause.

The god rose, unfolding, the winter forest of it climbing back into the fog. And Mara saw—or thought she saw, in the last instant before the window-lights withdrew into the dark—something that might have been a smile, if a land could smile.

"Then we have terms, taster. Crowned and nameless. Sip by sip. And your name stays in my keeping until the valley can speak." The fog began to close around it like a curtain drawn by a careful hand. "One warning, freely given, as a gift costs nothing: Lady Corvane's patron is not patient, and not wounded, and not me. When it learns what you intend, it will not negotiate."

The fog swallowed the antlers. The cliff was just a cliff again, with a door in no wall, and six mercenaries looking at their mistress, and Vess Corvane standing very straight with her jaw set like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to.

"Well," said Vess. "That complicates matters."

Above them, through a tear in the clouds, the moon showed her face—swollen, ahead of schedule, one night fuller than any almanac allowed.

Three moons, the god had said. But the moon, apparently, hadn't signed the bargain.

"Untie them," Vess said abruptly, and when her mercenaries hesitated: "*Untie them.* All of you, back down the path. Now." And as the men scrambled, she looked at Mara with an expression stripped of all its polish—hungry, frightened, and terribly awake. "You and I need to talk about your patron, girl. Before mine finds out what you've just promised."
