## 21. Escape Through Fog

The rope bit into Oscar's wrists, and he thought, with the strange clarity that had been following him around lately like a stray dog: *this is what it costs.*

Not the rope. Not Vess's mercenaries standing at the treeline with their crossbows and their bored, professional faces. Not even Mara, kneeling in the wet grass beside him with her chin up and her eyes doing arithmetic. The cost was smaller than all of that, and heavier. It was a name whispered into his ear on a border stone, a name no one else could hear, warm as a coal cupped in both hands.

He'd been spending it in little ways for weeks now. A coin found when he needed one. A door unlatched. A goat that lived. Small luck, small payments—aches he couldn't account for, a gray hair at sixteen, a memory of his mother's face gone slightly out of focus, like a painting left in the sun.

But this was going to be different. He could feel it in his teeth.

"Oscar." Mara's voice was low, meant only for him. She was tied beside him, Pell slumped against her shoulder with blood drying at his temple, Wren white-faced and furious between them. "Whatever you're thinking about doing—don't."

"I'm not thinking about anything," Oscar said. "Thinking is how you get caught."

"You *are* caught."

"See? Proof it doesn't work."

Mara made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer, which was, Oscar was learning, the sound most people in this valley made eventually.

The mercenaries were loading them onto a cart. Vess Corvane stood apart from her soldiers, wrapped in a traveling cloak the color of wet slate, looking not at her prisoners but at the cliff behind them—the cliff where a door stood in no wall, patient as a held breath. There was something almost tender in her expression. That was the worst part, Oscar thought. She wasn't cruel. She believed she was rescuing them. People who believed they were rescuing you never untied your ropes.

The fog came down off the hills in the way it always did at dusk, thick and slow, like the valley pulling its blanket up. Except lately the fog had been wrong. Lately it moved like it was listening.

Oscar closed his eyes.

*Little borrower,* said the voice he'd been trying not to hear since the border stone. It didn't come through his ears. It came up through the ground, through the soles of his boots, through the bones of his feet. *You've been so careful with my gift. So sparing. I've enjoyed watching you count.*

"I need out of these ropes," Oscar whispered. His lips barely moved. "All four of us. And I need those men to look somewhere else for as long as it takes us to be gone."

*A big withdrawal.* The voice sounded pleased, and the pleasure in it was worse than anger. *And what will you pay?*

"You always take something," Oscar said. "I know that now. So take it. Take whatever's fair."

There was a pause. Somewhere in it, Oscar felt the name turn over inside him, considering, the way a cat considers a bird through glass.

*Fair,* the god murmured. *How rare. A borrower who asks to be billed.*

Then the luck came.

It didn't feel like anything at first. That was the trick of it—the payment arrived before the goods. A coldness started in Oscar's fingertips and spread up through his knuckles, his wrists, deep into the joints, and it wasn't painful exactly. It was *old*. It felt like being forty. Then fifty. Then sixty, all at once, in ten fingers and two wrists, in every hand he would ever have for the rest of his life.

And then the ropes fell off.

Not just his. All of them, all four, sliding loose like they'd been tied by someone who'd changed their mind. At the same moment, one of the mercenaries shouted—"Fire! The cart's alight!"—and every crossbow in the treeline swung toward a wagon that was, quite genuinely, burning merrily from the axle up, though there was no fire anywhere near it and no reason on earth for it to be there.

"Go," Oscar croaked.

Mara hauled Pell upright. Wren grabbed Oscar's arm—and then stopped, staring down at his hands.

In the dying light, in the fog-light, his hands looked like his grandfather's. The skin had gone thin and papery, mapped with veins, spotted brown at the knuckles. His fingers wouldn't quite close. They ached in a way he understood, suddenly and completely, was permanent—not an injury but a *withdrawal*, debited against a future he'd been spending without reading the terms.

"Oscar," Wren whispered. "Your hands."

"They're fine," he said, and tried to flex them, and failed, and laughed instead, because if he didn't laugh he was going to sit down in the wet grass and cry in front of everyone. "They're fine. Come on. Run first. Grieve later. That's the family motto I just invented."

They ran.

The fog took them the way a mother takes a child out of a room where something has broken—gently, thoroughly, all at once. Behind them, shouting; ahead of them, nothing but gray and the slope of the hill and Pell wheezing prayers under his breath, which, given recent events, was a genuinely alarming habit.

"Don't pray!" Oscar gasped. "Pell, don't—you'll get an answer!"

"I am praying *against* answers," Pell panted. "It's called a counter-prayer. Very advanced theology. I invented it Tuesday."

They ran until the shouting faded, until the burning cart was a smudge of orange behind them, until the fog thinned into the blue dark of evening and the ground leveled out into the old shepherd paths above the palace town. Then Wren pulled them all behind a drystone wall, and they collapsed in a heap of lungs and terror, and nobody spoke for a long time.

It was Mara who finally sat up and took Oscar's hands in hers.

She turned them over carefully, the way she handled everything—like it might be poisoned, like it might poison her back. Her thumb traced the new lines, the deepened creases, the spots at his knuckles. Her face did something complicated.

"How much?" she asked quietly.

"Ten years," Oscar said. "Give or take. Just the hands, though. Apparently the rest of me is still a bargain." He grinned at her. It hurt his face less than crying would have. "Worth it, though. Did you see that cart go? I didn't even ask for the cart. That was a bonus. Free of charge."

"Nothing is free of charge," Pell said grimly, from where he lay with his eyes closed. "That is the entire content of my life's work, boy. Nothing. Is free. Of charge."

"Yes, thank you, Archivist, very uplifting."

"The god gave you luck," Pell went on, opening his eyes. They were red-rimmed—he'd taken a knock at the cliff, and he was older than any of them, and he'd watched his archives burn or bleed depending on which story you believed. "Luck is a loan. You've just seen the interest rate. And here's the question that should keep you awake tonight: what happens when the debt exceeds the collateral?"

Oscar looked down at his old man's hands.

"What happens?" he asked.

"Repossession," Pell said, and closed his eyes again.

* * *

They walked through the night by paths only Wren knew—shepherd tracks above the fog line, where the stars showed and the world briefly remembered it was ordinary. Wren limped less than she used to. That was the gods' gift too, the healed leg, and she carried it now like a question she hadn't finished asking herself.

Near midnight they stopped in the lee of a ruined byre, and Wren built a small fire, and Mara produced from her coat a twist of paper containing hard cheese, two apples, and a heel of bread, because she was Mara, and of course she'd been carrying provisions through a kidnapping.

"Eat," she said, pressing food into Oscar's hands.

He tried to hold the apple and couldn't grip it properly. It slipped. He caught it against his chest, awkward, like a toddler, and there was a silence while everyone pretended not to have seen.

"I'll manage," he said.

"I know you'll manage," Mara said. "That's not the same as eating alone."

She cut the apple with her own knife, into slices small enough for old fingers, and handed them to him one at a time, and didn't make a ceremony of it, and that—that more than anything—was what undid him. He ate the apple slices and stared at the fire and let the tears come quietly, the way fog comes, from everywhere at once, and neither Mara nor Wren nor Pell said a word about it, and that was its own kindness.

"It's not fair," Wren said finally, into the flames. "You spent it saving us."

"Luck isn't moral," Oscar said. "That's sort of its whole thing. It doesn't care what you spend it on. It's like a river. Or a tax collector."

"You could hate the god for it," Wren said. "I'd understand."

Oscar considered this. He thought about the antlered figure at the border stone, patient as winter, calling him *little borrower* in a voice like roots growing. He thought about what Mara had told them afterward, in whispers, about what she'd learned at the negotiation—the stolen name, the caged land, the valley as the body of a wounded prisoner. He thought about a god so starved of its own name that it lent pieces of itself to children just to feel used.

"No," he said slowly. "I don't think I hate it. I think—" He flexed his ruined fingers, watching them tremble in the firelight. "I think it's lonely. And loneliness makes you spend things you shouldn't. Ask anyone in this valley."

"That's dangerously forgiving," Pell muttered.

"I'm a kitchen boy. Forgiveness is the one luxury I've always been able to afford."

* * *

Dawn found them on the ridge above the palace, looking down at the roofs emerging from the fog like the backs of sleeping animals. Somewhere down there, Vess Corvane was consolidating power over a kingless court. Somewhere down there, the moon that shouldn't exist was waxing toward full. Somewhere down there, people were forgetting their names one by one, and Pell had a list, and the list got longer every day.

"We can't go back through the gates," Mara said. "Vess will have watchers."

"There are other ways in," Wren said. "Ways that don't have gates. Ways that don't officially exist." She glanced sideways at Oscar. "Cellars. Tunnels. My grandmother cleaned for the palace forty years. She knew every door the palace didn't know it had."

"Pell?" Mara asked.

Pell was staring at his own hands, folded in his lap, and when he looked up there was something raw in his face that hadn't been there before the cliff.

"When we're inside," he said, "there are carvings beneath the palace. In the oldest cellars. I burned pages of the founding treaty thirty years ago, and I have regretted it every day since, and I believe—I *believe*—the originals of what I destroyed may survive in stone. If they do, then everything we need to end this might be written on the walls of the place Vess least thinks to look." He paused. "Also, I would like very much to sit down."

"He's bleeding," Mara said.

"I'm *resting*," Pell corrected. "Bleeding is incidental."

Oscar got up to help him, and his hands shook doing it, and he noticed—because he noticed everything now, the way you notice weather after it's ruined a harvest—that Mara saw the shake and looked away quickly, and that her jaw was set in the particular way it set when she was adding something to a list of debts she intended to pay.

"Don't," he said quietly, so only she heard.

"Don't what?"

"Don't put it on the list. Whatever list. I know you keep one. Don't put my hands on it."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. Down in the valley, the fog shifted, and for one strange second the whole palace seemed to float, unmoored, a kingdom of cloud with no country under it.

"It's already on the list," she said.

"Mara—"

"And I'll tell you what else is on it," she went on, fierce and quiet. "A thirteenth moon. A stolen name. A king dead without justice. And a kitchen boy who paid ten years of his hands to save four lives, one of whom was mine." She stood, and brushed off her skirts, and became, abruptly, the person who tasted poison for a living—calm, precise, unreadable. "So yes, Oscar. It's on the list. And lists get settled."

Below them, the fog parted along the palace walls like a curtain being drawn by a careful hand—and in the gap, just for a heartbeat, Oscar saw lights moving in the deepest cellar windows. Lights where no lights should be. Lights that glowed the soft, patient gold of shrines.

Or of something older, waiting under the palace, keeping its own count.

"Come on," Wren said, shouldering her pack. "Grandmother's door is this way. And Oscar—" she paused, and her voice gentled, "—when we get inside, I'll carry things. Both hands. That's what friends are for."

Oscar looked at his hands one last time—his grandfather's hands, his mother's mother's hands, hands that would ache every cold morning for the rest of his life—and he closed them into fists as best he could, which was not very well, and he smiled.

"All right," he said. "Lead on. And somebody remind me to stop borrowing."

Nobody reminded him. Down in the fog, the lights under the palace pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat—or like a ledger being opened—and the four of them started down the hill toward whatever was waiting below the kingdom that didn't exist.
