## 22. Refuge in the Root Cellars

Wren had always assumed the palace sat on the earth like a cup on a table. She was wrong. The palace floated—on cellars.

That was her first thought as Pell lifted the lantern and the light fell away from them into a darkness that did not stop. Steps went down and down, cut into stone so old it had gone soft at the edges, worn into shallow bowls by feet that had climbed them for centuries before there was a valley to climb them in. The air that rose to meet them smelled of cold earth, old onions, and something else—something mineral and deep, like the inside of a bell.

"Mind the third step," said Pell. "It minds you back."

Wren put her weight on the third step. It tilted, gently, like a hand turning palm-up. She hopped off it and looked down at it with respect.

"I like this stair," she said. "It has opinions."

"It has many," Pell said. "The whole cellar does. That is rather the point of where we're going."

Behind them, Oscar came last, carrying Wren's crutch even though she no longer strictly needed it. He'd offered to carry her too, earlier, when they'd fled through the fog-choked kitchens with Vess's mercenaries shouting somewhere above; he'd made it three steps before his knees informed him of their new age, and Wren had laughed until Mara told them both to *hush*, which they had, mostly. Now he set the crutch against the wall out of habit, and Wren let it stay there. Her leg—the leg the god's gift had straightened and made new—ached in the cold, but it ached the way a well-used muscle aches. Honestly. Earnestly. Like it wanted to work.

She still didn't know if she trusted it. She trusted the ache, though. The ache was hers.

* * *

The root cellars began properly at the bottom of the stairs, and they were enormous—not the cramped burrow Wren had imagined but vaulted halls of rough stone stacked with barrels, bins, and hanging bundles of herbs gone gray with age. Their footsteps echoed strangely, doubled, as though someone else were walking one room over.

"How big *is* this place?" Oscar whispered.

"Bigger than the palace," said Pell, who had produced the lantern from somewhere in his coat along with three books and, inexplicably, a tin of sardines. "Possibly bigger than the town. Possibly—" he hesitated, and lowered his voice as if confessing to a crime, "—possibly older than the valley itself."

Wren stopped walking. "Older than the *valley*?"

"The valley is four hundred years old," Pell said. "By its own accounting. Which is, I have come to believe recently and reluctantly, an accounting done by people who very much wanted certain questions not asked." He raised the lantern higher. The light caught the far wall—and Wren saw what he'd brought them down here to see.

Carvings covered the stone. Not scratched on—cut deep, deliberate, the way you cut something when you want it to survive floods and fires and forgetful archivists. Rows and rows of figures, human-shaped, standing in lines. And above them, larger, winding around the vault of the ceiling like a river seen from underneath: a great antlered shape, coiled, patient, its body forming the very ribs of the room.

Wren's skin prickled. She knew those antlers. Everyone in the valley who'd prayed lately and been answered knew those antlers, or would soon.

"That's him," Oscar said quietly. "At the border stone. That's what he looks like."

"Mm." Pell moved the lantern along the wall. "And here is the interesting part. Look at the figures. Look closely."

Wren looked. The carved people stood in ranks, and each one held something up—hands open, faces tilted toward the antlered shape above. Not cowering. Not fleeing.

Offering.

"They look happy," she said slowly. "Or—no. Not happy. *Willing.*"

"Yes," Pell said softly. "Yes, exactly. Willing." He set the lantern on a barrel and rubbed his hands together, and for the first time since Wren had known him, the archivist looked less like a man reading a terrible book and more like a man finally allowed to read a good one. "I spent forty years believing we were prisoners here. That our ancestors were dragged behind this seal, nameless, trapped. It's what the treaty fragments say—or what the fragments I *had* say. But these carvings predate every fragment in my archive. And they tell a different story entirely."

"What story?" Oscar asked.

Pell turned to face them, lantern-light flickering up his lined face. "That nobody was locked in," he said. "Somebody was locked *out*. And our ancestors built the lock. Volunteered for it. Gave up their names gladly—look, see here—" he pointed to a row of small carved marks beside each figure's head, blank ovals where a name should be, each oval polished smooth as if generations of fingers had touched it, "—they gave their names *to* the land, not to a jailer. As a gift. As medicine." His voice wobbled. "As an apology."

Wren frowned. "An apology for what?"

Pell opened his mouth.

From the dark at the far end of the cellar hall, something answered—a sound like wind through bare branches, except there was no wind underground, and no branches either.

* * *

They stood very still. Even Pell, who Wren suspected could lecture through an earthquake, went quiet.

The sound came again, closer now, rustling and dry, and then around the corner of the far vault walked a goat.

Not just any goat. Wren recognized her instantly—the kitchen goat, the one Oscar had prayed over when she was dying, back when all of this began, back when prayers were things that went unanswered and candles burned the way candles were supposed to. The goat had been half-dead then. Now she trotted out of the darkness glossy and fat-eyed and utterly unbothered, chewed thoughtfully on a hanging bundle of century-old thyme, and regarded them with the flat golden stare of a creature who had never once doubted her place in the world.

"*Bramble?*" Oscar said. "How did you—you followed us *down here?*"

Bramble chewed.

"She's been eating the offerings," said a voice, and Wren jumped so hard her good leg nearly buckled. A figure unfolded itself from between two barrels—an old woman, small and round and dressed in about eleven shawls, holding a candle stub. "For years. Decades, probably. Somebody has to keep the old shelves tidy."

"You live down here?" Wren demanded.

"Off and on." The woman shrugged, shawls shifting. "I'm the cellar-keeper. Well. I was the cook's mother, and then the cook's mother retired into the cellars, which is a traditional career path in this palace. Nobody bothers you. The cheese is excellent if you know which barrels." She squinted at Pell. "You'll be the archivist. You burned pages of my grandmother's treaty, you know. She talked about it for thirty years."

Pell went the color of old candle wax. "I—that is—I can explain—"

"Later." The old woman waved this off with magnificent indifference and shuffled forward, candle raised, to look at the carvings. "You've found the Wall of Hands, then. About time somebody did. My grandmother used to bring me down here when I was smaller than you, girl." This to Wren. "Told me the whole story, start to finish, and I forgot most of it because I was eight and hungry. Ask me anything."

"The apology," Wren said. "What were they apologizing for?"

The old woman's face changed. The flippancy drained out of it, and beneath it was something older and sadder and much more careful.

"Ah," she said. "That question." She looked at the antlered shape coiled across the ceiling, and the antlered shape, in the flickering light, seemed almost to breathe. "Come sit. All of you. Bramble, move your great bottom, you're sitting on the history."

Bramble moved her great bottom.

* * *

They sat in a half circle on barrels and crates, and the old woman—who gave her name as Maud, "just Maud, the rest went into the ground with everyone else's"—held her candle stub near the lowest row of carvings.

"This land wasn't empty when your ancestors came," she said. "It was full. Full of everything—magic like weather, gods like seasons, names growing wild like weeds. Wonderful and unbearable. Most people couldn't live in it. They'd wake up with the trees whispering their secrets back at them, or find their shadows doing things they hadn't agreed to. Some thrived. Most went mad, or got eaten, or got *used*."

"And so they left?" Oscar asked.

"Some left. The ones who became us stayed—but only after they made a bargain." Maud touched one of the blank oval name-marks. "The land itself was the worst of it, you see. Not cruel—never cruel—but vast, and hungry for closeness, the way a drowning man is hungry for air. It loved the people who lived on it too much. Held too tight. Knew too much. So the founders did the kindest terrible thing anyone has ever done: they took the land's own true name and buried it, and sealed the borders with their given names, and made a country where nothing magical could get a grip. Quiet. Foggy. Safe." She smiled without much humor. "Dull as ditchwater. But safe."

"But the carvings show them offering," Wren said. "Gladly."

"Because it *was* glad, child. That's what nobody wants to understand." Maud's voice gentled. "They weren't victims. They were surgeons. You don't hate the knife when it cuts the rot out. They loved this land enough to hurt it, and they knew it was a hurt, and they did it anyway, and they wept while they carved these walls. Every one of these figures is crying. Look."

Wren looked. She hadn't seen it before—she'd read the faces as tilted, willing, serene. But in the candlelight, with Maud's finger tracing the channels under each carved eye, she saw the grooves. Deliberate grooves. Tears cut in stone by hands that must have shaken.

"They knew the land would grieve," Maud said. "A nameless thing can't remember why it hurts. So they wrote the reason down, everywhere, in every cellar, under every foundation—so that someday, when the seal cracked, whoever found these walls would know the truth before they did anything foolish." She looked at each of them in turn. "So. Have you done anything foolish yet?"

Oscar raised his hand slightly, the way he did in lessons. "I may have accepted a small gift from the god at the border."

Maud looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached over and patted his aged hands with surprising tenderness. "Then you already know the interest rates," she said. "Good. You'll do."

"And the king," Pell said quietly. He had been silent through all of this, and when he spoke, his voice was thin. "The crown. Mara wears it now. If the founders were surgeons, what is the crown?"

Maud's eyes flicked up to the ceiling, to the antlered shape whose ribs formed the roof of the world.

"The crown is the hand that holds the knife," she said. "Every bargain needs a keeper—someone bound to maintain it, generation after generation, whether they want to or not. Your kings weren't rulers, archivist. They were wardens. The fog, the stasis, the way nothing in this valley ever quite changes or finishes—it wasn't laziness. It was maintenance. And the crown..." She paused, and Wren saw her choose her words with the care of a woman laying down cards one at a time. "The crown doesn't unlock the seal. Anyone could break a lock. The crown decides *how* it breaks. Whether the name comes back as a wound or as a healing. Whether the land wakes up forgiving, or wakes up hungry."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere overhead, faint as a memory, thunder rolled—though Wren was almost sure there had been no storm clouds when they fled inside. Only moonlight, and the wrong bright stars, and the thirteenth moon swelling fat above the border stones.

"Mara has the crown," Oscar said. "And she's going to the Door Out."

"Mara has the crown," Maud agreed. "And the Door Out is exactly where a frightened person runs and exactly the wrong place to run to, because a door is just a hole, and holes don't care what comes through them or in what order." She leaned forward, candle guttering. "But that girl's mother used to come down here too, you know. Sat right where you're sitting, girl. Asked the same questions. Left something behind, once, when she thought I wasn't looking. I was looking. I'm always looking. It's down the far corridor, behind the winter squash, if any of you are the sort who reads recipe books."

Wren felt the words land in her chest like a dropped stone.

"Her mother's book," she breathed. "The one with the unbinding in it. It was *here?*"

"Where else would a clever woman hide a dangerous recipe?" Maud said. "In a library, where archivists burn things? In a kitchen, where cooks lose things?" She snorted. "In the cellar. With the truth. Where it's cold enough to keep."

Pell was already on his feet, lantern in hand, torn visibly between scholarly urgency and the shame of a man who had burned pages being handed the unburnable. "Which corridor? How far? Is it—"

"Sit *down*, archivist. It's kept four hundred years; it'll keep till morning." But Maud was smiling as she said it, and Bramble the goat heaved herself upright and started ambling toward the far corridor with the unhurried confidence of a creature who knew exactly where the winter squash was kept.

Wren didn't stand. She stayed on her barrel a moment longer, looking up at the ceiling of antlers, at the carved ranks of weeping volunteers, at the polished-blank ovals where names used to be.

Her whole life, the valley had taught her one lesson over and over: that she was small, that she was lame, that she lived in a forgotten pocket at the edge of nowhere, ruled by a king who slept through his own kingdom. She had believed the forgetting was something done *to* them.

But the tears in the stone told another story. The forgetting had been chosen. Paid for. Carved in grief by people who loved this land enough to make it sleep—and who had left instructions, everywhere, for the day it woke.

"We're not the prisoners," she said aloud, to no one in particular, and her voice sounded strange in the vaulted dark, doubled by the echo, as though the cellar itself were agreeing. "We never were. We're the *apology.*"

Above them, muffled through four hundred years of stone, the thirteenth moon cleared the mountains—and every candle stub in the cellar bent, all at once, toward the far corridor where Bramble had stopped and stood waiting, golden-eyed, beside a shelf of winter squash and a small oilcloth-wrapped bundle that none of them had noticed before.

Maud sighed, and pinched out her candle, and said, in the voice of a woman whose evening had just become very interesting:

"Well. Fetch the book, then."
