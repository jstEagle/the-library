## 23. Moon the Seventh

The king died on a Tuesday.

I know this because Tuesdays were his good days — the one day a week he'd been lucid enough to complain about the porridge — and it seemed fitting that he should go out on one of them, mid-complaint, spoon halfway to his mouth. He had tasted the porridge first. I had tasted it second. Neither of us found anything in it but oats and salt and the long gray flavor of a valley that had forgotten how to end.

He set the spoon down. He looked at me. And then, very quietly, like a man setting down something much heavier than a spoon, he said: "Ah. There you are."

Those were the last words of the King of the Valley of the Unnamed. *There you are.* Two words, if you count generously. Kings have died with worse epitaphs, though few so small. He slid sideways in his chair with the unhurried dignity of a man who had been waiting years for permission, and Pell, who had been hovering by the window pretending to read a ledger upside down, dropped the ledger entirely.

"Mara," Pell said. "Mara, he's—"

"I can see he's dead, Pell. I'm a taster. I know what finished looks like."

It was not, I want it noted, the kind of thing I said because I was unmoved. It was the kind of thing I said because my hands were shaking and if I stopped talking they would shake visibly, and I had spent twenty-six years perfecting the art of not being visible. The king's face, in death, had gone smooth and young. He looked like someone who had never ruled anything at all, which was, in fairness, accurate. He looked like a man who had been holding a door shut for forty years and had finally, gratefully, let go of the handle.

I did not yet know how right that image was.

* * *

You would think there would be procedure for this. Bells. A chamberlain. Some ancient official emerging from a closet with a velvet cushion and a speech about continuity. But the palace served no kingdom; it had no bells worth ringing, no chamberlain, and the only velvet cushions belonged to Vess Corvane, who kept them under lock. What we had instead was Cook.

Cook arrived within the hour, having heard through whatever mysterious channels cooks hear through (I have never discovered them; I suspect the pots whisper). She stood over the king's body, wiped her hands on her apron, and said, "Well. Someone ought to make him decent," and did, folding his arms and straightening his collar as though he were merely cold.

"Who tells the valley?" Oscar asked from the doorway. He had flour on his cheek and grief all over his face, unhidden, the way he carried everything. "Does anyone tell the valley?"

"The valley will know," Pell said. He had retrieved his ledger and was clutching it against his chest like a shield. "When the crown passes, everyone will feel it. That's rather the point of a crown here. It isn't jewelry. It's a — a mechanism."

"A mechanism," I repeated.

"An inheritance engine," Pell said, with the particular cheerlessness he reserved for facts he wished were metaphors. "The royal line were the jailers, Mara. We've discussed this. The crown doesn't sit on heads. It sits on *duties*. When a head is vacated, it moves. Automatically. Inevitably." He swallowed. "To the nearest royal blood."

And then, because scholars are cowards in their own special way, he stopped talking and looked at me.

There is a moment — every taster knows it — when you put something in your mouth and realize, a half-second too late, from the smell or the color or the way the steam curls wrong, that this one is bad. Not poisoned exactly. Wrong. Your whole life narrows to the inside of your own tongue while you wait to find out what you've swallowed.

That was the moment. The kitchen went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"No," I said.

"Mara—"

"No. Absolutely not. I taste things. That is my entire function. My entire *identity*, such as it is. I am the woman nobody remembers at parties. I am furniture that chews. You cannot promote me to — to *mechanism*."

"It isn't a promotion," Pell said gently. "It's an arrest."

* * *

They gave me until sundown, which was generous of them, since none of us knew who *they* was anymore. I spent it where I always spend crises: in the pantry, among the jars, doing inventory of things that could not hurt me.

Here is what nobody tells you about being nameless: it is the best protection in the world. A god cannot call you. A spell cannot find you. Even memory slides off you like rain off waxed cloth — people forget you're in the room, forget they sent for you, forget, sometimes, mid-sentence, why they were angry. I had built my whole self inside that slipperiness. Invisible. Untouchable. Safe.

And now some dead man's hat wanted to land on me.

Because that was the truth underneath everything, wasn't it. Royal blood. I had known since the night the king asked for my real name — the name I didn't have, the name traded away before I drew breath. His line had bargained with mine. Jailers and lock-makers, married into each other until the blood ran thin and tangled and pooled in unlikely places. Like the bottom of a kitchen hierarchy. Like me.

I heard footsteps and knew them by weight before I knew them by sound. Oscar sat down on the floor beside me, between the salt barrels, without asking. He had brought bread. Of course he had brought bread. Oscar's answer to every catastrophe since I'd known him was baked goods.

"They're saying it has to be you," he said.

"They're saying a lot of things. They're also saying Wren's leg healed because of a miracle and that the thirteenth moon is a blessing and that Lady Corvane's new friends are philanthropists. People say things."

"Mara." He turned the loaf over in his hands, not eating it, which for Oscar was a sign of genuine distress. "Pell says when it happens, you'll feel it. Is that true?"

"Pell says a lot of things too. Pell once told me the cellar ghost was 'probably condensation.'"

"What happens if you just don't? Take it?"

I had been asking myself that all afternoon. I picked up a jar of preserved plums and held it to the light, mostly to have something to look at that wasn't his face. "What happens if a door decides it wants to be a wall, do you think? Does it get a vote?"

"You're not a door."

"I'm beginning to suspect I might be several doors and possibly a window."

He laughed — a short, startled laugh, quickly strangled, because laughing felt disloyal today. Then he reached over and took the plum jar out of my hands and set it down, and looked at me with that terrible open kindness of his, the kindness that made him such easy prey for antlered gods and made everyone else love him hopelessly.

"When it comes," he said, "I'll be there. Whatever it feels like. You won't be alone with it."

I should have said thank you. Instead I said, "If I start glowing, throw water on me."

"Deal."

* * *

It came at moonrise, because of course it did. Nothing in this valley has ever had the decency to happen at noon.

I was in the kitchen. Pell was there, and Cook, and Oscar, and half the household staff who had found reasons to be present that involved mopping already-clean floors. Wren had come up from town, leaning on her healed leg and her cousin's arm, refusing to leave. Even the goat Oscar had once saved with a whispered prayer stood in the yard looking in the window, which I chose to interpret as sympathy rather than hunger.

The moon came up over the fog-line, and it was the seventh moon, and it was more than half full, and I felt it the way you feel a hand settle on the back of your neck.

Not pain. Worse than pain. *Recognition.*

Something vast and patient and old, older than the valley, older than the bargain, reached into the world and found the nearest royal blood — found *me*, the lock's last living tooth — and closed around me like a ring closing around a finger. I felt the crown arrive. There was no metal. No procession. Just a weight settling onto a place in me I hadn't known was empty, a weight shaped exactly like duty, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, something turning over. Waking. Noticing.

Every person in that kitchen turned to look at me at once.

That was the worst part. Not the weight. The *looking*. Twenty-six years of perfecting invisibility, and in one moonrise, every eye in the room found me and stayed found. Cook's mouth opened. Wren's hand went to her heart. Pell dropped his ledger again, which at that point I think was simply its natural state.

"Oh no," I said. "Oh, no, no — stop that. Stop *seeing* me."

"Mara," Pell whispered. "Your eyes—"

"What about my eyes?" I demanded, and caught my reflection in the copper pot above the hearth.

My face was still my face. But it was lit from somewhere behind itself, faintly, the way a lantern glows through oiled paper, and my eyes had gone the deep amber of resin and honey and old kings' rings. The reflection looked back at me with the expression of someone who has just been handed a bill she cannot read and cannot refuse.

"It knows you," Wren said softly. "The crown. It's been waiting for you."

"Well, it can keep waiting," I said, with more conviction than I felt. "I never applied for this position. There was no posting. There was no interview. This is the single worst hiring decision in the history of governance, and I include whoever appointed the last king, whose primary qualification was napping."

Nobody laughed except Oscar, bless him, a small helpless snort, and even that died fast. Because the weight was still settling, layer upon layer, and I was beginning to understand what Pell had meant by *mechanism*. I could feel the borders. All of them. Every border stone in the valley, suddenly present in my awareness like teeth in my own jaw — and I could feel, along each one, hairline cracks, spreading, and behind the cracks, pressing patiently outward, something enormous breathing.

Halfway. Moon the seventh. Halfway to the seal failing entirely, and now the seal had a face, and the face was mine, and it was standing in a kitchen holding a jar of plums.

Cook broke the silence the way Cook breaks everything: practically. She took the crownless, weightless, invisible crown of the Valley of the Unnamed as seriously as she took a risen loaf. She knelt. On her kitchen flags, in her flour, the old woman knelt, and the staff followed her down one by one like dominoes, and Wren last of all, watching me with wet fierce eyes.

"Your Majesty," Cook said.

"Don't," I said. "Don't you dare. Get up. Cook, I have known you since I was nine years old and you fed me gristle to toughen me, you are not kneeling to me, I forbid it as—" I stopped. Swallowed. The word came up my throat on its own, tasting of ash and inheritance. "As your queen."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the yard, the goat bleated, which I choose to believe was supportive.

"There," Pell said quietly, retrieving his ledger for the final time that day and opening it to a fresh page with the resignation of a man who has done this arithmetic before and hates the answer. "It's done. The succession is automatic and irreversible. You are the Crown of the Valley of the Unnamed, Mara Kavanagh, whether either of us likes it or not." He dipped his pen. "Which brings us to the urgent question."

"Please," I said, "let it be about porridge."

"Vess Corvane believes the crown is a key. The king told you so himself. She has mercenaries, a map, a god of her own, and three days' head start on ambition." He looked up at me, and for once the scholarly dread in his face had settled into something steadier. Something almost like loyalty. "She will come for you, Majesty. Possibly tonight. So the urgent question is — what are we going to do about it?"

Outside, the seventh moon climbed higher, fat and wrong, and along every border stone in the valley, I felt the cracks spread another inch.

I set down the plums. I picked up the biggest knife in the kitchen.

"First," I said, "we lock the pantry."
