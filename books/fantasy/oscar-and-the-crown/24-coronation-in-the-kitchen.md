## 24. Coronation in the Kitchen

The king had been dead for six days, and the palace still smelled of him.

Not of the man himself—of the rituals. Incense from the throne room, tallow candles burning in every window, and beneath it all the sour-sweet fog that crept up from the valley floor now that no one was left to pretend they were keeping it out. The kitchens, at least, still smelled like a kitchen: bread and bay leaves and the iron tang of the great stockpot, which had not been empty since before I was born.

I stood at my old station by the tasting table, because when a person does not know what to do with her hands, she should put them where they have always been useful. There was a bowl of barley soup in front of me. I tasted it. It needed salt. Some things remained true even when the world came apart.

"You're doing it again," said Cook.

"Doing what?"

"Tasting things that don't need tasting. Nobody's poisoning anybody today, Mara. Today we're the ones doing the poisoning." She said it cheerfully, the way she said everything, and handed me a cloth to polish spoons. "Might as well look busy while you wait."

"I'm not waiting for anything."

"Mm." Cook turned back to her dough. She had been kneading the same loaf for an hour, which meant she was thinking hard about something she didn't want to say out loud. "Pell's coming at moonrise," she added, to the dough. "He asked me to have the big table cleared. And he asked—" here she paused, and thumped the dough with unnecessary violence, "—he asked whether I'd be willing to kneel."

The spoon stopped mid-polish.

"He asked *what*?"

"You heard me."

"Cook. He can't possibly think—"

"He thinks exactly what everyone who's read the succession laws thinks, which is that the crown doesn't care whether anyone wants it. It goes to the nearest royal blood, and the nearest royal blood is standing in my kitchen eating soup that needs salt." She finally looked up at me, flour on her cheekbones, eyes steady as a hearthstone. "And before you argue—and you will argue—I want you to know that I've already decided to kneel. So has everyone else. So save your breath for Pell, because he'll need less of it than you think."

* * *

They came at moonrise, as promised, though the moon itself was late. That was the trouble with the thirteenth moon—it kept its own schedule, waxing fat and wrong in a corner of the sky where no honest moon had any business being. Through the kitchen's high window I could see it hanging there like a coin someone had nailed up and forgotten to take down.

Pell arrived first, of course. Pell always arrived first; it was the only way he could guarantee being the most prepared person in any room. He carried a bundle wrapped in archive linen, and behind him came Wren, walking without her stick these days—the gods' gift, the leg they'd mended, the miracle she'd chosen to defend against her own neighbors—and behind Wren came Oscar, who looked like a boy carrying a secret too heavy for his pockets, which was precisely what he was.

And behind Oscar came the cooks.

All of them. Every hand in the palace kitchens, plus half the scullery staff and, inexplicably, the woman who cleaned the chimneys. They filed in quietly and arranged themselves along the walls, and none of them would meet my eye, which in a kitchen is the loudest possible announcement that something is happening.

"No," I said.

"Mara." Pell set his bundle on the cleared table with the reverence of a man laying out a relic, which, I supposed, he was. "Please sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Then stand. But listen." He unwrapped the linen, and there it was: the crown of the Valley of the Unnamed. I had seen it perhaps four times in my life, always on a head, always at a distance, always through the haze of ceremony. Up close it was smaller than I expected—a thin circlet of dark metal, unadorned, almost plain, except that it seemed to drink the candlelight rather than reflect it. Fog-metal, Pell had once told me. Smelted from the valley's own breath. A key wearing the costume of jewelry.

"The king died without declaring an heir," Pell said. "You know this. What you may not know—because I only confirmed it yesterday—is that the succession law predates the seal itself. It isn't a human law, Mara. It's part of the bargain. The crown must pass to royal blood, and royal blood passes to whoever carries it, whether or not anyone announces it. Vess Corvane understands this. It's why she's been gathering soldiers. If we do nothing, the crown will simply... arrive on your head one morning, in front of her army, and she will decide what happens next."

"So we do it first," Wren said. "In front of witnesses who love you instead of witnesses who want to use you."

"That's not how crowns work," I said. "Crowns are supposed to be public. There are trumpets. There's an anointing. There's—"

"There's a kingdom of forty thousand people hiding under a fog bank, waiting to find out if they're going to be eaten by a god or sold to a courtier," Wren said, not unkindly. "Trumpets would be a bad idea."

"She's right," Oscar said, and then, because he was Oscar, he immediately ruined the gravity of the moment by adding, "Also I checked the chimney lady in. She brought her own bread. I figured if we're doing this, we should do it properly."

"*Properly?*"

"Well." He rubbed the back of his neck. His hands were still wrong—older than the rest of him, knuckles thickened, a faint silver scarring where the borrowed name had taken its payment. He caught me looking and tucked them behind his back, the way he always did now. "There's bread. There's witnesses. There's a crown. In the stories, that's most of a coronation. The rest is just weather."

I looked around the kitchen. At Cook, flour-dusted and resolute. At the chimney sweep holding a loaf like an offering. At Wren, standing straight on her impossible leg. At Pell, whose hands trembled slightly as he smoothed the archive linen flat—not from fear, I realized, but from the particular terror of a scholar who has spent his life studying ceremonies and is now about to perform one from memory, alone, in a room that smells of onions.

And at Oscar, who met my eyes and did not look away.

"Why me?" I asked him. Quietly. Just to him, though the whole room heard it. "Of all the people in this valley. Why does it have to be the girl whose whole life was built on being nobody?"

"Because," Oscar said, "you're the only person I've ever met who knows exactly what things cost. You've tasted poison since you were six. You know what it does, how it works, what it takes to survive it." He shrugged, awkward and certain at once. "That's what a ruler is, isn't it? Someone who tastes the bad thing first so everyone else doesn't have to."

The silence after that was long enough for me to hear the fog pressing against the windows.

* * *

Pell had reconstructed the rite from three sources: a fragmentary account of the first coronation, a marginal note in a liturgical manual, and—this he admitted with visible embarrassment—a children's rhyme still sung by goose-girls in the eastern villages. The original ceremony, he explained, had involved seven days of fasting, a procession through every street of the capital, and the sacrifice of a white bull. None of which could be managed in a kitchen between the supper service and the washing-up.

"So we keep the parts that matter," he said. "The naming of the burden. The acceptance. The crown."

"And the rhyme?" I asked.

"The rhyme is load-bearing," Pell said solemnly, and I genuinely could not tell if he was joking. With Pell one rarely could.

Cook brought the bread—Oscar's contribution, baked that afternoon, dense and dark and good—and broke it into pieces, one for each witness. This, apparently, stood in for the bull. Then Pell took up the crown in both hands, and the kitchen went quiet in a way kitchens never do, and I understood that whatever happened next, I would remember the smell of it forever: yeast and bay and candle smoke and the cold mineral breath of fog-metal.

"Mara Kavanagh," Pell said, and my own name sounded strange in the air, half-remembered, like a word in a language I used to dream in. "Daughter of Sera, daughter of the nameless line. The law gives you a crown. The law does not ask if you want it. But we are asking." His voice wavered, steadied. "Do you accept the burden of the valley? Its hunger, its fear, its debts—do you take them into yourself, knowing what they cost?"

Every instinct I had, every lesson my mother ever fed me, screamed the correct answer: *refuse*. Spit it out. A good taster knows that some dishes cannot be made safe, only survived.

But I thought of the lame girl who could walk, and the shrines burned by frightened neighbors, and the king lucid at the end and asking for a name he knew I couldn't give. I thought of a god made of antlers and patience, waiting at a border stone for something that was owed. I thought of my mother's recipe book, and the last page, and the words I hadn't yet shown anyone: *unbinding requires a crowned nameless taster.*

The crown wasn't a prize. It was an ingredient. And I was the only one who could swallow it.

"Yes," I said. "I accept."

Pell lowered the circlet toward my head.

It was heavier than it looked. Of course it was. Everything in this valley that pretended to be small was secretly enormous. As it settled against my hair I felt something click—not in my ears but somewhere deeper, a lock turning in a door I hadn't known I was standing in front of. The fog-metal warmed against my skin. And very faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, I heard the valley exhale.

Then the cooks knelt.

All of them, at once, the way they'd clearly rehearsed when they thought I wasn't listening—Cook last of all, lowering herself onto knees that had stood over ovens for thirty years, looking up at me with an expression I couldn't bear to examine too closely. The chimney sweep bowed her head. Wren knelt. Even Pell, who had performed the rite and therefore technically outranked the moment, bent his knee among the pots and pans.

Oscar did not kneel. He grinned at me instead, wide and crooked, and mouthed something that was either "*your majesty*" or "*you've got flour on your face*." Given Oscar, it was probably both.

"Get up," I said. My voice cracked on the second word. "All of you, get *up*, this is a kitchen, not a—"

"Kitchen's exactly where a queen belongs," Cook said from the floor, utterly immovable. "Kings rule from thrones and end up ruling fog. You'll rule from where the food is. Mark me, that's why the crown picked you."

"The crown didn't pick me. The law picked me. There's a difference."

"Not tonight there isn't." She rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and pressed a piece of bread into my hand. "Eat, your majesty. Coronations are hungry work. And then—" her cheerfulness flickered, just for a moment, into something older and warier, "—then you'd best tell us what you saw. When it clicked. Because every soul in this room felt the valley move, and I'd wager my oven you felt more than we did."

I looked down at the bread in my hand. Warm. Ordinary. Safe—I tasted it anyway, out of habit, out of thirty years of instinct, and found nothing in it but grain and salt and the ghost of my mother's kitchen.

But underneath the bread's small honesty, the crown hummed against my temples like a struck bell that wouldn't quite stop ringing. And through its hum, faint as a voice through a wall, I could feel something on the other side of the seal. Something vast and patient and *listening*, the way a prisoner listens at the door of a cell.

It knew the crown had changed heads. And it was pleased.

"Pell," I said quietly, while the cooks passed the bread and Oscar lit more candles and the kitchen slowly, stubbornly, went on being a kitchen. "How many days until the thirteenth moon fills?"

Pell's face did the thing it did when the answer was worse than the question. "Nine," he said. "Perhaps eight. The count keeps changing. The moon, I'm afraid, is cheating."

Outside the high window, the thirteenth moon slid free of a cloud, fat and silver and wrong, and far away—at the border stones, at the cliff where a door stood in no wall, in the palace above us where Lady Vess Corvane was, at that very hour, learning what her new god wanted in exchange for power—something began, very softly, to laugh.
