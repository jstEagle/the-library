## 25. Vess's New God

Vess Corvane had been raised on the principle that anger was a luxury for people without plans.

Her father had taught her that at the dinner table, in his way—sliding the salt cellar out of reach whenever she raised her voice until she learned to ask for what she wanted sweetly instead. *Sweetly*, he'd say, passing it back. *The world gives to the sweet.* He had been a fool about many things, her father, including which of his daughters would inherit his cunning rather than his fortune, but he had been right about that. Sweetness was a tool. Anger was a leak in the hull.

So when the news reached her—Mara Kavanagh crowned in a kitchen among copper pots and flour-dusted cooks, Pell's thin hands lowering the crown onto that taster's stubborn head—Vess did not throw anything. She did not scream. She sat very still at her writing desk, listened to her steward stammer through the report, thanked him graciously, and dismissed him with a smile that made him walk backward out of the room like a man leaving a bear's den.

Then she locked the door, drew the curtains, and allowed herself exactly one hundred heartbeats of fury.

She counted them. It helped. By the nineties she had stopped seeing red and started seeing the shape of the problem: a nameless kitchen girl wearing the key to everything, protected by an archivist who should have died years ago and a goat-boy whose hands had aged a decade overnight, if the whispers were true. Her mercenaries were scattered. Her map was stolen. Her dinner party's careful web of alliances had been cut thread by thread by a girl whose great talent, as far as Vess could tell, was not dying.

*The world gives to the sweet,* her father said in her memory.

*The world gave me nothing,* Vess answered him silently. *I have taken every crumb myself.*

She looked at her hands. Elegant, uncallused, forty-one years old and still steady. They had signed letters, poured wine, adjusted the angle of a fork so that a visiting magistrate would feel subtly flattered. They had never once done anything that mattered.

That was the wound under all the others. Not the map. Not the crown. The hands.

Vess Corvane had spent her whole life being nearly indispensable. Nearly brilliant. Nearly the one they turned to. And always there had been someone else—the king in his lucid decades, the archivist with his precious archives, and now, absurdly, a food-taster with royal blood she hadn't even known how to use.

Well.

There were other powers in the valley now. Powers that answered.

* * *

She went down to the shrine behind the granary at dusk, alone, wrapped in a plain gray cloak that no one would look twice at. That was the trick of the valley: everyone had learned not to look at anyone. A whole country of polite blindness. She had exploited it for years, moving through the fog like a rumor with legs.

The old shrine was a sad little thing—a stone bowl, a stub of candle, offerings of bread gone green. But the new shrines, the ones that glowed, had been springing up along the eastern lanes since the first prayers were answered. Wren's leg. The Kettlefield crops standing straight and gold in a season that should have rotted them. People whispered about miracles the way they'd once whispered about weather.

Vess knelt at the glowing shrine because kneeling cost nothing and information cost everything.

"Who are you?" she asked the light.

The light did not answer. Of course it didn't. She'd watched the believers pray; they babbled gratitude, requests, small hopes. Please heal my mother. Please let the rain be kind. Please let my son come home from the border stones. Small prayers. Prayers from people who thought small.

Vess had never prayed in her life. Prayer, as far as she could tell, was asking nicely. And asking nicely was for people who couldn't take.

But she was furious tonight, and fury is a kind of honesty, and honesty, she suspected, was the only currency gods accepted.

So she did not ask nicely. She leaned close to the shrine-light and told it the truth.

"I want power," she said. "Not comfort. Not healing. Power. I want to open the Door Out of this valley and hand my people back their names, and I want to be the one who did it. I want to be remembered. I have been useful my entire life and useful is another word for invisible, and I am done with invisible. If you can give me that, take whatever you like in return. I'm not afraid of the price. I've been paying it since I was six years old."

Silence. The candle flame stood perfectly straight, indifferent.

Vess exhaled, felt foolish, and began to rise—

—and the flame bent toward her.

Not flickered. *Bent*. Like a reed leaning into wind that wasn't there, curving toward her mouth as though her words were something it could drink.

"Oh," said Vess softly. "Hello."

The light in the shrine deepened. It had been pale before, milky, the color of dawn through fog—the gentle glow the whole eastern lane had cooed over. This was different. This was the color of a coal just before it catches, orange going red going a red so deep it was almost purple, and the warmth coming off it was wrong for autumn, too heavy, too interested.

Something was looking at her. She could feel it the way you feel a stare across a crowded room—not on your skin but behind it.

"You're not the antlered one," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd heard Oscar's account secondhand, thirdhand, embroidered by a dozen gossips: patience, antlers, a voice like slow water. Whatever this was, it moved fast. She could feel it moving fast, circling her words the way a pike circles a lure.

*I am hungry,* said a voice, and it did not come through her ears at all. It arrived fully formed inside her skull, the way you remember a tune. *You are not.*

"No," Vess agreed. Her heart was hammering and she noted it distantly, the way she noted everything, filed it, filed herself. "I haven't been fed in years. Does that matter?"

*Everything that wants is food,* said the voice, with something almost like approval. *Most things here want so little. They want rain. They want their mothers' knees to stop aching. You want to be remembered forever. Do you know how long forever takes to eat?*

Vess should have run. She knew that. Some cold, sensible clerk in the back office of her mind stamped the memo in red ink: *This is the part where clever women die.* Lady Vess Corvane, who had survived three purges, two famines, and one disastrous engagement to a customs official with a gambling habit, knew precisely what it looked like when a bargain was too good.

But oh, the voice knew her name. Not her given name—her *want*. It had unwrapped her want and held it up to the light and found it worthy, and no living soul had ever done that. Her father had called her ambition unladylike. The court had called it tiresome. The king, in his rare lucid hours, had called it dangerous and meant it as an insult.

This voice called it delicious.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

*A door,* said the god. *A crown. A girl who swallows names. In time. For now—* and the warmth slid up her arms like bathwater, like silk, like something with intent— *for now, I will lend you what you need, and you will owe me what I choose. Fair terms. Sweeter than any mortal has offered me in an age.*

"That's not fair terms," Vess said. "That's a blank contract."

*Yes,* said the god, and laughed, and its laughter tasted like copper coins and struck matches. *You understand contracts. That is why I chose you.*

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Vess would examine later, in the years she had left, turning it over like a stone looking for the flaw underneath: she wasn't tricked. She understood exactly what she was doing. She walked into it with her eyes open and her chin level, because the alternative was another forty years of adjusting forks and being *nearly*, and she would rather be devoured than nearly.

"Done," said Vess Corvane.

The shrine went dark. All of it—even the little stub of candle, even the gray twilight outside—and for one heartbeat the world was a held breath.

Then warmth poured into her like wine into a dry cup.

* * *

She walked home through the lanes and the lanes noticed her.

That was the first sign. In a valley where invisibility was the national costume, people *looked* at her. The miller's boy dropped his armful of kindling. Two women by the well stopped mid-gossip and stared, then blushed and stared harder. An old man pressed his hat to his chest as she passed, uncertain why, doing it anyway.

By the time she reached her own gate, she had counted eleven stares, two bows, and one marriage proposal shouted from a rooftop by a man she was fairly sure had never spoken to her. Word traveled ahead of her somehow—or perhaps it simply traveled *with* her, riding in the warm space around her shoulders.

Inside, her steward Bramwell took one look at her and fell to his knees on the imported tile.

"Lady Corvane," he breathed. "Forgive me. I don't know why I—I saw you come up the path and I thought, *there stands the woman who will save us all*, and my legs simply—" He looked baffled at his own knees.

"Get up, Bramwell," said Vess, and heard her own voice and barely recognized it. It had a resonance now. When she spoke, the word seemed to keep going after she finished saying it, humming in the corners of the room like a struck bell. "Where are the others? Ferro? The twins?"

"Gone, my lady. After the coronation—they scattered, they said it was hopeless, they said the taster-girl has the crown and the archivist and luck itself on her side—"

"They're not wrong," Vess said pleasantly. "They're merely early." She removed her cloak and hung it with care. Underneath, she was still wearing the same gray traveling dress she'd knelt in. Nothing about her clothes had changed. Everything else had. "Send word to everyone who ever owed me a favor. That will take some time; make a list. Tell them Lady Corvane is receiving guests tomorrow, and that she intends to take the palace."

Bramwell's eyes were shining. That was the unsettling part—he wasn't afraid of her. He was *devoted*, suddenly, completely, the way men got devoted to storms. "And if they refuse?"

"They won't." She tested it as she said it, flexing the new power like a new ring, feeling where it fit and where it pinched. When she spoke with intention, people believed. Not compelled, exactly—nothing so crude. More like... *convinced*. As though her certainty were contagious. As though wanting, in her mouth, became fact. "Bring me wine, Bramwell. The good bottle, the one we were saving for a victory. We'll drink it tonight. I find I'm thirsty."

He hurried off, walking lighter than she'd ever seen him, and Vess stood alone in her parlor and let herself feel it—all of it, the whole tide of it—rolling through her chest.

Power. Actual power. Not proximity to power, not influence, not the long game of favors and leverage she'd played for thirty years with the patience of a fisherman. This was the rod of lightning itself, and it fit her hand like it had been cast from a mold of her grip.

There was a price. There was always a price; she was not a fool. She could feel it already, faintly, at the edges of the warmth—a small cold spot behind her sternum, like a pebble in a shoe, like a debt entered in a ledger in handwriting she couldn't read yet. The god had said *you will owe me what I choose*, and she had said yes, and somewhere in the dark beneath the valley a quill had scratched her name into a column.

She examined the cold spot the way she examined everything: calmly, thoroughly, looking for the exit.

There wasn't one. She'd known there wouldn't be. She poured her own wine because Bramwell was slow, drank it standing at the window watching the fog roll off the palace roofs, and caught her reflection in the dark glass.

For a moment—just a moment—her reflection's eyes were the color of a coal before it catches.

She blinked. Brown again. Ordinary brown eyes in a handsome, hard face.

"Hungry," she murmured, testing the word. "It said it was hungry."

And the cold spot behind her sternum pulsed, gently, like something swallowing.

* * *

She slept better than she had in years and woke with plans unfolding in her mind like a fan.

That was the second gift, and in some ways the most seductive: clarity. All her life her schemes had been tangled things, threads within threads, contingency wrapped around contingency. Now she woke and saw the whole board at once. The palace guard's captain gambled on dice and feared his wife. The western villages were loyal to the holdouts, but the holdouts followed Wren, and Wren was seventeen and had a healed leg and a conscience, and consciences could be leveraged. The archivist Pell was injured—she'd confirm that, but the siege at the shrine had been loud enough to hear in the capital, and wounded scholars bled slowly. And Mara—

Mara Kavanagh wore a crown she didn't want, in a palace full of servants who loved her, guarded by nothing but soup.

"She tastes everything," Vess said aloud over breakfast, amused despite herself. Bramwell hovered; the twins had crept back overnight, drawn like moths, sitting at her table with the expressions of men who had seen the weather change. "Every spoonful, every cup. Twenty-five years of poisons in that girl. You can't poison her, you can't bribe her, you can't frighten her—her mother saw to all three. Do you know what it's like to plan against someone whose only weakness is that she loves people? It's like planning against a fortress made of grandmothers."

"Then how?" asked Ferro.

"We don't go through her," Vess said. "We go around. She's crowned, yes—but a crown needs a kingdom, and half the valley is praying to new gods and liking the answers. We offer the believers a regent who speaks for heaven. Let the taster keep her pots and her loyal cooks. Loyalty doesn't hold a wall."

She said it lightly, and the table nodded, convinced, the way they were always convinced now, and Vess smiled and lifted her cup and felt the cold spot pulse again, a little stronger, a little closer to her heart.

That night she dreamed of eating.

Not greedily—reverently. In the dream she sat at a table longer than the valley, and dishes came and came, and each dish was a name. She could taste them. *Aldous*, which tasted of river water and patience. *Merrow*, which tasted of salt and singing. Names she'd never heard, names that had never been spoken aloud in her lifetime, served one by one on plates of dark wood, and she ate each one politely, with the good manners her father had drilled into her, and somewhere below the table something vast and patient said *good girl* in a voice like struck matches, and she woke with tears on her face and no memory of crying and an appetite so enormous she ate half a loaf before her hands stopped shaking.

*Just dreams,* she told herself.

The cold spot behind her sternum had grown teeth.

* * *

On the fourth day, she took the palace.

It was almost embarrassingly easy. She walked up the front steps with twelve followers and a speech she'd written at dawn, and by the time she reached the doors the guards had set down their pikes to listen, and by the time she finished speaking four of them were crying and all of them were hers. The kitchen staff barricaded themselves in with their queen—which was what they called Mara now, *the Kitchen Queen*, the name spreading through the lanes like butter on warm bread—and Vess let them. Let the girl keep her kitchens. A queen of pots ruled pots.

Vess seated herself in the throne room, on the steps below the actual throne, because the throne was a key and keys were for later, and received the city's petitions for six hours straight. Every petitioner left certain that Lady Corvane would save them. Most of them were probably right. That was the strange, dizzy thing—she *meant* it. The god hadn't hollowed her out and puppeted her; it had simply given her want a voice that carried, and her want had always been, in its cold and patient heart, genuinely for these people. She wanted them named. She wanted them free. She wanted the Door open and the fog rolled back and history to write *Corvane* across the page in letters a mile high.

If the price was a pebble behind her sternum that grew teeth—well. Everyone paid. At least she'd chosen her bill.

It was on the sixth night, alone in the king's old study with her maps spread out, that the voice returned.

*You have done well,* it said, warm as banked coals.

"I've done what you lent me the strength to do," Vess said, not looking up from the map. Best, she'd found, not to grovel. It respected directness. "Now. The Door. The map shows a cliff above the Greywater falls, but the map is old and the girl has it anyway. I need—"

*In time.*

"It has been six days. The moon is waxing. Your—*colleague*," she said carefully, "the antlered one, counts moons. If the seal fails before I reach the Door, there will be nothing left to open and no one to thank us."

A pause. The fire in the study hearth bent toward her, all the flames in the room leaning like courtiers.

*You misunderstand our arrangement,* said the god, gently, the way one corrects a beloved child. *I did not lend you strength so that you could open a door. I lent you strength so that you would gather. Look at what you have collected in six days, little borrower. A palace. An army. A city's love. A crown's location. The names of every believer in the valley, written in lists, in ledgers, in your own tidy hand.*

Vess went very still. "Those are musters. Rosters. For the march to the Door."

*Mm,* said the god, and she felt it smile, and the smile had far too many edges. *Names are how you feed a hunger, Lady Corvane. You have built me a pantry. Keep gathering. And do stop worrying about the Door—it will open exactly when I am ready, and not one breath sooner.*

The hearthfire righted itself. The presence withdrew, unhurried, sated, leaving Vess alone in the lamplight with her beautiful maps and her army of the willing and the cold, toothed weight behind her breastbone, which had begun, very faintly, to hum.

She looked down at her roster of believers—three hundred names in her neat, slanted script—and noticed, for the first time, that the ink had faded on the last page. Faded unevenly, as though something had been drinking it.

At the bottom of the list, where her own signature should have been, the parchment was blank.

Vess Corvane sat in the king's study for a long time, listening to the fog press against the windows, trying to remember what she had signed.

She could not recall the shape of her own name.
