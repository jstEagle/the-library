## 26. The Plague of Names

Pell had kept a ledger of the valley's names for thirty-one years, and until this week it had been the most boring document in existence.

He was proud of that boredom. Boredom meant stability. Boredom meant that page four hundred and twelve — *Hobb Fenwick, miller; wife: Sela Fenwick (née Marsh); children: Tam, Brindle* — could be copied out in his small, patient hand and filed away, and nothing would ever happen to it. The archive smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular melancholy of paper that no one would ever need to read. He loved it the way other men loved their gardens.

Then, on the morning after Moon the Seventh waxed full, Hobb Fenwick came to the archive door, hat in hand, and asked Pell what his own name was.

"Yours or mine?" Pell said, because he had a scholar's instinct for clarifying a question before answering it.

The miller frowned. "I... had one," he said slowly, as though feeling along a shelf in the dark. "It's on the tip of my tongue. It's been on the tip of my tongue since Tuesday."

"Hobb," said Pell. "Hobb Fenwick. You're the miller at Coldbrook. Your wife is Sela. Your boys are Tam and Brindle, and Brindle put a frog in my inkwell in the spring of last year, which I have not forgotten even if you have."

"Fenwick." Hobb tried the word like a bite of unfamiliar bread. "That sounds right. That sounds — yes." His shoulders sagged with relief, and then, horribly, with something like gratitude. "Thank you. I'd have asked Sela but she's got it worse. She keeps calling herself *the woman*. Just 'the woman.' She set six places at supper last night for five people because she couldn't remember whether she counted."

Pell wrote it all down. His hand was steady. He noted to himself, as a point of professional pride, that his hand was steady.

*Case the first,* he wrote in the margin of a fresh folio. *Fenwick, H., miller. Loss partial. Retained under prompting. Wife worse.*

Underneath, after a moment, he added: *God is collecting early.*

---

By the end of the week there were forty-one cases. By the following week, one hundred and nine.

The pattern, when Pell finally teased it out of his ledgers, was elegant and appalling. The forgetting did not strike at random. It went after names in order of how little they were used. Old men who lived alone lost theirs first. Then the unmarried. Then people whose names were mostly worn smooth by habit — "Mum," "Sir," "that fellow at the forge" — so that the actual word beneath had gone soft as an old coin. Children were safe, so far. Children's names were called a hundred times a day, shouted across yards, dragged out of mischief. A name that was spoken daily seemed to hold its shape, like a path walked often enough to keep the grass down.

But a name merely *kept* — written down, stored away, assumed rather than used — that name grew thin. And when it grew thin enough, something reached through and took it.

"It's not theft exactly," Pell explained to Mara, who had come down to the archive with a lantern and an expression he recognized from her mother's face, twenty years gone. "Or rather, it is theft, but of the most legalistic kind. Think of it as a creditor calling in debts early. The treaty says the valley owes its names back when the seal fails. The seal is failing moon by moon. So the god has simply begun... invoicing."

"Invoicing," Mara repeated flatly.

"I find precision comforting," said Pell. "You may find it insufferable. Both responses are valid."

She sat down across from him, in the chair she'd sat in as a girl, when her mother brought her here to copy herb-lore into the margins of agricultural reports. "Can it be stopped?"

"Delayed, perhaps. A name that's spoken aloud every day seems to resist. I've instructed the worst-hit households to say their own names at dawn and dusk, out loud, like medicine." He rubbed his eyes. "Old Mrs. Tallow now introduces herself to her own chickens each morning. 'Good day, I am Mrs. Tallow.' The chickens are unimpressed but she retains her name, so I count it a success."

"And the ones who've already lost them?"

Pell looked at the ledger. One hundred and nine entries. Some of the people behind those entries still wandered the valley, cheerful and hollowed, answering to whatever their neighbors called them. Some had stopped coming to market at all, because a person with no name finds crowds unbearable — everyone else's names press against you like elbows.

"There's a woman up past the shrine," he said quietly, "who has taken to standing in her doorway at dusk saying 'I am, I am, I am,' over and over. She can't recall what she *is*, only that she is. And honestly, Mara—" his voice caught on the scholar's terrible honesty, the kind that costs something— "honestly, I think she may be closer to the truth than the rest of us."

---

The archive became, without anyone deciding it, a refuge.

They came in ones and twos, the frightened and the half-forgotten, and Pell — who had spent three decades wishing people would leave his archives alone — found himself keeping the lamps lit late and setting out chairs. He taught them the dawn-and-dusk practice. He interviewed them, gently, and recorded what remained. He discovered that memory of a name could sometimes be coaxed back by its associations: a man remembered himself when Pell read out his father's name, because he'd spent his boyhood being called "Aldous's boy" before anyone bothered with his own.

"That's the horror of it," Pell told Oscar one evening, when the kitchen boy turned up carrying a basket of rolls from the palace kitchens, ostensibly as charity and actually, Pell suspected, because the archive was warmer than anywhere else and Pell told better stories than anyone's grandmother. "We thought we owned our names. We don't. We *rent* them, from the people around us. Every time someone says 'good morning, Pell,' they pay a bit of my rent for me. Take away the neighbors and the name starves."

Oscar set down the basket. He had been quiet lately, Pell had noticed. Quiet in a way that had weight to it, like a man carrying coins he didn't want to jingle. His hands, when he passed the rolls, were careful hands. Older-seeming than they should be.

"What happens," Oscar said, "if someone borrows a name? Instead of owning one."

Pell looked at him over his spectacles for a long moment.

"Then I imagine," he said carefully, "that the debt structure becomes somewhat more complicated. Interest accrues. And the lender—" he tapped the ledger, where the god's early collections were logged in his neat hand— "the lender tends to be very interested in borrowers."

Oscar's face did something complicated and young. "And if you give it back? The borrowed thing?"

"Then the account closes." Pell took a roll, because refusing hospitality in a crisis was its own kind of rudeness. "Accounts always close, Oscar. The only question worth asking is what you bought with the loan, and whether it was worth the price." He bit into the roll. It was excellent; everything from the palace kitchens was excellent; this was, Pell reflected, the single useful thing about ruling fog. "Was it worth it? Whatever you bought?"

Oscar looked at his own hands, turned them over, looked at the palms as though reading something there.

"I'm not sure yet," he said.

"An honest answer," said Pell. "Those are rarer than names lately. Write yours down while you still remember it. Top drawer, third folio. Everyone should be in the ledger."

Oscar hesitated — and Pell watched, with the particular ache of a man who has spent his life among documents, as the boy decided *not* to write his name down. As though writing it somewhere might make it easier to take.

"Maybe later," Oscar said, and left, and the door closed softly behind him, and Pell sat with his roll going cold and understood that he had just witnessed a confession without any words in it.

---

Moon the Eighth rose swollen and wrong-colored, the yellow of old ivory, and the collections accelerated.

Pell documented. It was the only thing he knew how to do, and he had come to believe — in the small hours, alone with his lamps — that it might be the most important thing happening anywhere in the valley. Someone had to remember the forgetting. Someone had to keep the list, so that when — *if* — the names came home, there would be a record of where each one had lived, whose mouth had held it, what it had sounded like over supper and in quarrels and at gravesides.

*Case 214: Dree Mallow, shepherd, sixty-one years. Lost fully on the eighth. Stands at the edge of his flock at dawn. The sheep know him. He does not know himself, but he says the work remembers his hands, and he has decided this is enough. Notes: wept when I read his name back to him. Asked me to say it again. Said it again eleven times. He has asked me to attend his funeral someday and speak it over his grave, "so it goes in the ground attached to me."*

*Case 215: Ilse Mallow, his wife. Retains hers. Says it is because he says it for her every morning — "Ilse, Ilse, breakfast" — without knowing why. Love, apparently, is also a kind of ledger. I did not know this. I am recording it here so that I never forget it.*

His hand paused over that line. He read it twice. Then, because he was who he was, he went back and formalized it:

*Addendum, general application: a name spoken daily by a loving mouth appears immune to collection. Recommend immediate widespread adoption of the practice. This is the strangest military strategy I have ever devised and I am reasonably confident it will save lives.*

It did, a little. The valley, hearing the theory, embraced it with the desperate practicality of people who had survived centuries of fog by taking sensible precautions. Wives called husbands' names across breakfast tables like sentries calling the watch. Mothers marched their children through recitations at dawn. The whole valley rang, faintly, with people saying their own names and each other's, a low persistent hum of identity, a village-sized act of defiance conducted entirely in vocabulary.

It worked well enough that Pell allowed himself, for nearly two days, a fragile and scholarly hope.

Then Lady Vess Corvane's household announced that names spoken aloud were superstitious clutter, that the regent-to-be regarded the whole affair as peasant panic, and that her servants would conduct themselves with dignity. Within the week, four of Vess's own retainers had forgotten themselves entirely, and Vess — Pell heard the report secondhand, and did not enjoy the shiver it gave him — had begun having them addressed only by their duties. "Doorwarden." "Steward." "Cook." Functional titles, empty of person.

She was feeding them to it, Pell realized. Or she simply didn't believe the plague was real, which amounted to the same thing, and would amount to the same thing right up until the night she opened her mouth to give an order and found she had no name to sign beneath it.

He wrote that prediction down too, dated it, and sealed it in the drawer where he kept things he hoped to be wrong about. The drawer was getting full.

---

On the ninth night of Moon the Ninth, Pell woke in the archive chair with a start, certain someone had called him.

The lamps had burned low. The archive was empty. But the air had that quality it got when the god was near the border stones — a pressure, a listening — and Pell sat very still and felt, unmistakably, like a man being *read*. Like a ledger opened by another hand.

"You are early," he said aloud, to the dark, because he was a scholar and scholars address primary sources directly. "The treaty specifies the thirteenth turn. You are owed nothing yet. Whatever your accounting says, mine says you are in breach."

The silence considered this. Pell had the distinct impression — absurd, unfalsifiable, and utterly convincing — that somewhere beyond the fog, something vast and antlered and patient tilted its head.

Then, faintly, from the shelf behind him, a sound: paper whispering against paper, as if a breeze had gotten into the founding treaty itself. Pell turned. The treaty lay closed where it always lay. But the drawer where he kept the pages he had burned, decades ago, the pages whose absence he had confessed to no one living—

The drawer was open.

He had locked it. He always locked it. Inside, his own confession note — the one he'd written the night he told Mara, the one detailing exactly which clauses he had fed to the fire and why, and whose name he had been protecting — lay on top, squared neatly, facing outward.

Someone had read it. Something had read it. And beneath it, in a hand that was not his own — thin, dry, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — a single line had been added:

*You kept one name safe all these years, archivist. Bring it to the door, or I will stop being gentle with the rest.*

Pell sat down again, slowly, because his knees had opinions.

He looked at the note for a long time. Outside, Moon the Ninth poured its ivory light through the archive window and lay across the floor like a spilled inventory. Somewhere across the valley, hundreds of voices were saying their names into the dark, dawn and dusk, stubborn as heartbeat — and somewhere nearer, one name sat locked in an old man's guilt, the last clause of a burned treaty, the debt underneath all the other debts.

"Well," said Pell, to the empty room, in the tone he used for misfiled documents and other catastrophes. "That complicates the bibliography."

He blew out the lamps, took down his coat, and went to find Mara — because there was one name left in the valley that mattered more than all the rest, and he had protected it for forty years, and it appeared the patience had run out.
