## 27. Wren's Choice

Wren had spent eleven years of her life learning every way there was to get from one place to another without walking.

She knew the shortcuts of the seated: the drainage ditch behind the mill that let you drag yourself downhill without the indignity of being carried; the particular stones in the market square that were flat enough to scoot across on a board with a greased runner; which doorways in Lower Fenwick had steps and which had ramps her uncle had built in the years before he died, because Uncle Tam had been lame too, and had believed — loudly, at every festival, with ale in hand — that a valley ought to be built for all its legs, not just the good ones.

She knew the arithmetic of stairs. Twelve steps up to the shrine at Hollow Hill. Fourteen down to the well. She had counted them the way other children counted stars.

And then, on the night the first prayers were answered, she had stood up out of her chair, taken one step, two, three — and fallen over into the gooseberries, laughing so hard she couldn't breathe.

That was eight moons ago now. Her leg worked. That was the miracle of it, the plain and stubborn fact of it: it *worked*. It ached when rain came, and there was a scar like a lightning-branch running from knee to ankle where the light had gone through her, but it worked. She could run. She could dance, badly. She could climb the twelve steps to the shrine at Hollow Hill, which was exactly what she was doing this cold grey morning, and hating every single one of them, because today the shrine was full of people who wanted her to choose sides.

* * *

The trouble had started small, as troubles do.

The god at the border — the antlered one, the patient one, the one Oscar swore called him "little borrower" — had healed Wren's leg as a kind of advertisement. She understood that now. Her walking was a sermon preached in flesh. Every time she crossed the market square, people looked at her stride and thought: *the gods give gifts. What have they given you lately?*

What most of them didn't know was what had happened to the Aldery farm, or to old Mother Sennet's shrine by the crossroads, or to the Hollis family, who had declined the gift offered to their sick son and woken three nights later to find their barn burned and the word NO written in ash on the door. Not burned *down*. Burned *onto*. Precise. Neat. A signature.

So the valley had split, the way a log splits — along the grain, quietly at first, then all at once with a crack you could hear for miles.

At Hollow Hill shrine this morning, both halves had come to pray.

Wren reached the top step, breathing hard, and paused in the doorway to take stock. On the left side of the shrine, nearest the old altar stone: the holdouts. Mistress Iva with her arms folded. The Hollises, sitting close together, the boy Bram between them, pale but alive, because his parents had nursed him through fever the hard way, with willow bark and no sleep. And near them, looking deeply uncomfortable in a way that made Wren's chest ache, sat Oscar.

Oscar didn't belong to either side. Oscar belonged to a third thing entirely, something secret and antlered and complicated, and he wore the expression of a boy standing in a doorway during an argument about whose fault the broken pot was.

On the right side: the believers. Maybe forty of them, kneeling toward the altar, and standing at their head — because believers apparently needed someone to stand at their heads — a tall man in a grey coat that Wren did not like. His name was Ferrow. He had come from somewhere past the mill in the second moon, said all the right things about gratitude and grace, and organized the believers with the brisk efficiency of a man stacking firewood. He had a voice like warm bread and eyes like a closed ledger.

"Fellow keepers of the gift," Ferrow was saying as Wren limped in. "We gather again to give thanks—"

"You gather again to frighten folk," said Mistress Iva, not unfolding her arms.

"We gather to give thanks," Ferrow repeated, with the patience of a man explaining weather to a child, "and to mourn those who turn away from mercy. The fire at the Hollis barn was a tragedy."

"It was a threat," said Mrs. Hollis.

"It was a tragedy," Ferrow said again, warmer, sadder, "brought about by refusal. The gods ask so little. A prayer. A candle. An open heart." He turned then, and saw Wren in the doorway, and his whole face changed — brightened, opened, became the face of a man greeting a sunrise. "Ah. *There* she is. Brothers, sisters — look. Look what was given. Wren of Lower Fenwick, who could not walk, who walks."

Forty faces turned to her. Wren felt the familiar heat rise in her cheeks, the old shame of being looked at, except this was worse than the old shame, because the old shame had at least been honest.

"Morning, all," she said, and made her way to the bench beside Oscar, twelve steps having earned her a sit-down.

"Good morning," murmured Oscar, not looking at her. He had a candle in his hands and wasn't lighting it. "You're late."

"I'm punctual. The stairs are slow." She squinted at him. "You look terrible."

"Thank you. I've been practicing."

"What's wrong?"

He turned the unlit candle over in his fingers. Under his sleeve, Wren glimpsed his wrist — and frowned. The last time she'd seen his hands, back before the coronation-in-the-kitchen business that half the valley whispered about and nobody confirmed, they'd been a boy's hands. Now there was something older in them, some settled quality around the knuckles, like hands that had done decades of work in a handful of months.

"Later," Oscar said.

"There's always a later with you."

"There's always a later with everyone worth knowing." But he glanced at her sidelong, and added, quieter: "Careful today, Wren. Ferrow wants something from you. He's been building up to it for a week."

"And what would that be?"

But Ferrow was already turning toward her, arms spread, voice rising into the register he probably thought of as holy and Wren thought of as the sound a salesman makes when he spots a customer. "Wren. Wren, daughter of Fenwick. Will you stand? Will you stand and tell these good people what the gift has meant?"

"I'm comfortable," said Wren.

A ripple of laughter from the holdout side. Ferrow absorbed it gracefully. "Then tell us sitting. Tell us what it is to be healed. Tell us why anyone would refuse such a gift — when you, of all people, know its sweetness."

And there it was. The hook, baited with her own leg.

Wren looked down at the scar branching along her shin. Lightning-tree. She'd traced it a thousand nights, wondering. Wondering whether the god had asked permission. Wondering whether the god had ever asked anyone anything, or simply given, the way a lord gives — generously, and with the giving itself a chain.

She thought about the night it happened. She'd been asleep. She'd dreamed of running through the barley, and woken mid-dream to white light pouring through the window, and a presence at the foot of her bed like a stag made of winter branches, vast and still and *attentive*, and a voice that hadn't spoken so much as arrived inside her skull, gentle as snowfall:

*A gift, little lame one. For the valley to see.*

Not for her. For the valley to see. She had been a shop window.

"No," said Wren.

Ferrow blinked. "No?"

"No, I won't stand up and sell your miracle for you." She got to her feet anyway, because if she was going to say it, she was going to say it on her own feet, thank you very much, even if her own feet ached from the stairs. "You want me to tell them the gift is sweet. It is. I can run. I danced at the harvest fire and stepped on four toes and apologized to none of them, and it was the best night of my life. I'm not going to pretend otherwise, because lying is how you lot work."

"Wren—"

"But here's what else I know." Her voice was shaking, and she let it. "I know that gift came into my bedroom without knocking. I know it used me as an advertisement. And I know what happened to the Hollis barn, and to Mother Sennet's shrine, and to the Alderys' well, and I know that every one of those was written off as a tragedy brought about by *refusal*. You don't give gifts, Master Ferrow. You give invoices. The bill just comes later, and it's always more than you said."

Silence in the shrine. Forty believers staring. The holdouts very carefully not smiling.

"The gods are returning," Ferrow said softly, "whether we welcome them or not. The only question is whether we meet them as friends or fools."

"Then answer me one question, friend." Wren pointed at him. "The name-takers. The ones forgetting their own names, all over the valley — Pell's been counting, hundreds now. Your gods are doing that too, aren't they? Collecting early. So when you say *gift*, I want to know: paid for with whose name? Yours? Or mine?"

Ferrow's warm-bread face did something complicated. For just a moment, behind it, Wren saw the ledger open — saw him calculating whether she was worth the trouble of persuasion or the cost of something else.

Then the shrine doors banged open, and the question stopped mattering, because the day caught fire.

* * *

They came down from Hollow Hill at a run — the holdouts scattering home, the believers shouting after them, and above it all, from the direction of the crossroads, smoke.

Not hearth-smoke. Shrine-smoke. Wren knew the smell of it now, bitter and resinous, the smell of old carved wood burning, and her stomach dropped through the twelve steps she'd just climbed, all the way down to the fen.

"Mother Sennet's," gasped Bram Hollis, skidding up beside her. "It's Mother Sennet's again — they're burning it *again* —"

"They rebuilt it twice," Wren said stupidly. "Why would they—"

But she was already running, which was still, after eight moons, a miracle she couldn't stop using, down the hill and along the fen path with her bad-good leg eating up the ground, Oscar pounding behind her, and the smoke getting thicker, and voices ahead — angry voices, dozens —

The crossroads shrine was a small thing, honestly. A post-and-lintel of black alder, carved with the old nameless marks, the spirals and eyeless faces that predated every map. Mother Sennet had tended it for sixty years, sweeping the leaves, leaving barley cakes, telling anyone who'd listen that the shrine didn't need a god, it needed *company*. It had survived the first burnings. The village had rebuilt it twice, each time adding one new carving, defiant little additions — a goose, a spoon, Uncle Tam's boot.

Now it was burning a third time, and around it stood perhaps thirty figures with torches, and among them, at the front, calm as Sunday, stood Lady Vess Corvane.

Wren stopped dead.

Vess Corvane. Regent, now, since the king died — self-declared, half-swallowed by whatever hungry thing she prayed to, her left arm wrapped in silk that everyone pretended was fashion and nobody believed was fashion. She had taken the palace. She had promised to open the Door Out for everyone, at a price. And now she was standing at a crossroads in the fen at dawn, watching peasants burn a shrine, wearing an expression of profound, patient disappointment, like a schoolmistress observing a lesson she had assigned.

"You," Wren said, before her brain could stop her mouth. "You're the regent. This is *treason against yourself*."

Vess turned. Up close, the silk-wrapped arm moved on its own, slightly, the way a sleeping animal twitches. "This is *clarification*," Vess said. "The Door opens within two moons. When it does, every soul in this valley will need to have chosen. There is no room in what comes next for people who hedge." She gestured at the burning shrine with her good hand, almost tenderly. "This little pile of sticks has stood for six hundred years telling my ancestors' ghosts that nothing needs changing. It is time it stopped."

"It's telling them their *names*," Wren said. "That's what the carvings are. They're not prayers, they're — it's a *ledger*, you absolute — it's everybody. Grandmothers. Babies. The whole valley, written in wood because we couldn't write them anywhere else, because our names were *taken*—" Her voice cracked. She hated that it cracked.

Something flickered across Vess's face. For a heartbeat — one heartbeat — she looked less like a regent and more like a woman standing in front of a fire she had ordered and wishing she were anywhere else. Then the silk arm shifted, and her spine straightened, and the schoolmistress returned.

"Holdouts gather at shrines," Vess said to her torchbearers. "You know your work."

And Wren, who had spent eleven years learning every way to move through the world without walking, and eight months learning what her walking was *for*, did the only thing her body knew how to do with fury in it.

She threw herself between the torches and the shrine.

"Oi!" she shouted, arms out, scarred leg planted, flames on either side of her. "Through me, then! Come on! I'm the miracle girl — the gods fixed me special — you'd be burning their own handiwork!"

It was, objectively, a ridiculous thing to do. Thirty armed believers versus one lame-born girl with a loud mouth. But it stopped them. Torchlight wavered. Because that was the poison of the whole wretched schism, Wren realized, heart hammering: the believers *believed*, and you cannot easily set fire to a person who is living proof of your faith.

"Move, child," said one of the torchbearers, an old man, almost kindly.

"No," said Wren.

"She's right, though," came a new voice, and Oscar pushed through the crowd, hands raised, palms out — and Wren saw the torchbearers see his hands, the old-young hands, and murmur. "She's the gift. Touch her and you answer to the antlered one for it." Which was, as far as Wren knew, a complete fabrication, and Oscar delivered it with the serene confidence of a boy who had learned magic's first lesson: most of it is timing and tone.

And then the fog came.

It rolled up the fen road out of nowhere — out of everywhere — thick and sudden and smelling of green things and deep water, and through it came a shape. Antlers first, branch upon branch, hung with mist. Then the rest: the stag-that-was-not-a-stag, the patient one, the god of the border stone, walking unhurried through the burning shrine's smoke as though smoke were a rumor it had heard about but didn't credit.

The torches went out. All of them, at once, like snuffed candles.

The believers fell to their knees. Some of them wept. Ferrow, arriving breathless from the hill, took one look and prostrated himself in the mud with the rest.

The god ignored all of them. It walked to the burning shrine, lowered its great branch-crowned head, and breathed in — and the fire went backwards. Flames drew themselves up off the blackened alder, gathered, shrank, and slid into the god's nostrils like thread into a needle. Where the carving had charred, wood re-emerged, pale as new birch. The goose. The spoon. Uncle Tam's boot. Whole.

In the silence afterward, the god turned its head — no eyes that Wren could find, only shadow and patience — and looked directly at her.

*Little lame one,* said the voice inside her skull, gentle as snowfall. *You stood in the fire for the names. Why?*

And Wren, who had never in her life been able to leave a question unanswered, answered it.

"Because they're mine," she said. "You gave me a leg. They gave me a *self*. I know which one I'd rather lose."

The god was quiet for a long moment. Fog moved through its antlers.

*Interesting,* it said at last. *The crowned one said something similar, once.* And then, before Wren could ask what that meant — before she could grab Oscar, who had gone rigid as a fencepost beside her — the god spoke again, and this time it was not speaking to her alone. Its voice filled the crossroads, filled the fen, filled the morning:

*TWO MOONS. THE DOOR WILL OPEN OR THE SEAL WILL BREAK. CHOOSE YOUR SIDES IF YOU MUST. BUT KNOW THIS, SMALL NAMED ONES: WHEN THE NAME COMES HOME, IT WILL PASS THROUGH ONE THROAT ONLY. AND EVERY PRAYER IN THIS VALLEY WILL KNOW HER VOICE.*

It stepped backward into the fog and was gone, the way a lamp goes out — the world exactly the same and darker for it.

Wren stood in the wet grass, heart slamming, and found Oscar staring at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before: fear, plain and undiluted.

"'The crowned one,'" he whispered. "Wren. It means Mara. It's talking about *Mara* — and if every prayer in the valley can reach her—" He grabbed her sleeve. "We have to warn her. Tonight. Before Vess's people figure out what she's worth."

Behind them, unnoticed by everyone, one small detail remained: on the restored shrine, amid the ancient carvings, a new mark had appeared that no villager had cut. A spiral, and inside it, small and precise as a signature, the shape of a spoon.

* * *

They walked to the palace through the grey end of the afternoon, Wren limping on purpose because Oscar was carrying something heavy and she refused to make him carry it alone, whatever "it" was — grief, mostly, she suspected, and guilt, and a name that wasn't his.

"So," she said, to break the silence. "Your hands."

"My hands."

"I've got eyes, Osc. They aged. Ten years, if they aged a day. When?"

He was quiet long enough that they crossed the whole mill bridge before he answered. "Moon seven," he said. "Spending luck. Breaking us out of Vess's trap at the Door." He flexed his fingers, watching them like they belonged to his father. "The god gave the luck back last week. More than it took, it said. Said my hands would heal when the land woke up." A hollow laugh. "The land's not awake yet."

"And until then?"

"Until then I've got my father's hands and my mother's son to feed with them." He shrugged, trying to make it small, and failed. "It's fine. It's fine, Wren. Magic costs. Everyone learns it eventually. Better me than—"

"Don't," Wren said. "Don't do the noble arithmetic. I've heard Ferrow do it and I hated it there too."

That startled a real laugh out of him, brief and cracked, and then the palace rose out of the evening mist ahead of them — a grand building serving no kingdom, windows lit unevenly, guards at the gate wearing Vess's grey — and the laugh died in both their throats.

The gate guards let them in, which surprised Wren until she realized why: word traveled fast in a valley this size, and by now everyone knew the lame girl from Fenwick had stood in front of thirty torches and a god, and come out unburnt. One of the guards touched her shoulder as she passed, lightly, the way you touch a lucky charm, and said, "Bless you, miss," and Wren didn't correct her, because she was tired, and because some part of her — the part that had counted stairs for eleven years — was not entirely displeased to be blessed for once instead of pitied.

They found the kitchens by smell. Bread, onions, and underneath it something stranger — herbs Wren didn't recognize, sharp and green, the smell of somebody cooking against despair.

Mara Kavanagh stood at the great hearth with a wooden spoon in her hand and flour to her elbows, and she looked, Wren thought, like the least royal queen in the history of crowns, and also somehow the only legitimate one. Pell sat nearby in a wheeled chair, one arm bandaged from the siege of the shrines, dictating something to himself in a whisper. The cooks moved around Mara with the easy deference of people who had already decided, months ago, whom they served.

"Mara," Oscar started, "we have to tell you—"

"Sit," said Mara, without turning. "There's stew. Both of you look like the bottom of a well."

"*Mara*—"

"I heard." She turned then, and her face was pale under the flour, and Wren understood that the god's voice at the crossroads had not stayed at the crossroads. "Every prayer in the valley knows my voice. Yes. I've been hearing them since sundown. Do you know what people pray for?" She laughed, short and unsteady. "Rain. A safe birth. Their mother's hip. One boy in Eastmarch prays every night for his dog to stop eating socks. It's — it's like drowning in neighbors." She set down the spoon. "But that's not why you came."

"No," Wren said. "We came because of what the antlered one said. Two moons. The seal breaks or the door opens. And—" she hesitated, then decided the truth deserved saying plainly, "—and because Vess was there, Mara. At the burning. Directing it. Whatever she's planning for the Door, it isn't rescue anymore. I watched her face. There's something *wearing* her."

Mara was quiet. Then she wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, and said, "Then it's a good thing I found my mother's recipe book this morning." She nodded toward the kitchen table, where a battered book lay open, its pages dense with handwriting — recipes, antidotes, marginal notes in two different inks. "Because the last entry isn't a recipe for bread."

Pell looked up from his whispering. "What is it?"

Mara looked down at the book, and Wren saw her swallow, the way you swallow before tasting something you already know is poison.

"It's called 'unbinding,'" she said. "And it calls for a crowned nameless taster willing to swallow the land's name like poison." She looked up, and met each of their eyes in turn. "It calls for me."
