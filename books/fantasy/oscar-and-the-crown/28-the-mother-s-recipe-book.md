## 28. The Mother's Recipe Book

The kitchen had not been built for a queen.

Mara discovered this on her third morning of secret coronation, when she tried to sit in the head cook's chair—a broad oak thing worn smooth by forty years of Brannigan's considerable weight—and found that queens, apparently, did not fit in it either. She had given up, perched instead on the flour barrel, and was eating cold porridge at dawn like any other servant, which was to say: exactly as she always had.

"You could eat in the throne room," Pell offered from the doorway, where he stood holding a stack of papers he clearly hoped she would not ask him to read aloud. He looked better than he had a week ago—the bruise along his ribs had faded from purple to a sickly yellow—but he still moved like a man carrying a fragile package inside his own chest.

"The throne room smells of dust and dead decisions," Mara said. "The kitchen smells of bread. I know which one I trust."

Brannigan, stirring something enormous in the background, made a sound that might have been agreement or might have been indigestion. With Brannigan it was often both.

It was Oscar who found the book.

He came tumbling down the back stairs—his hands, still aged and knuckled from the god's price, wrapped around a bundle wrapped in oilcloth—and skidded to a stop in a spray of flour that Brannigan would be mourning for days.

"Mara." He was breathing hard. "The mason's men. They're clearing the collapsed wall in the east wing—you know, where the ceiling came down during—" he waved vaguely, a gesture encompassing Vess's coup, the siege preparations, and the general apocalypse, "—all of it. And there was a space behind the plaster. A little hidden shelf. And this was on it."

He set the bundle on the table.

Mara knew what it was before she touched it. She knew it the way you know your own name—which is to say, imperfectly, and with a strange ache, since her name had been taken at birth and this had not.

Her mother's recipe book.

It was a fat, square thing bound in leather gone soft as chamois, its corners rounded by decades of hands. Her mother had carried it everywhere. Mara had a hundred memories of it: propped open against the flour crock while her mother cooked, stuffed into a market basket, balanced on her mother's knee while she corrected some apprentice's technique with a wooden spoon and a raised eyebrow. When her mother died—seven years ago now, quietly, in the way that people in the Valley of the Unnamed did everything—it had simply vanished. Mara had assumed one of the palace cooks had inherited it. She had grieved the book almost as much as the woman, which at the time had felt like a betrayal, and later had felt like the truest measure of love she knew.

"She hid it," Mara said slowly. "Behind the wall."

"Looks like it." Oscar hovered, hands twitching with curiosity but not touching. His old-man hands were careful now, always careful. "There were other things too. A bundle of letters, all sealed. Some jars of dried things I didn't recognize. The masons wanted to throw it all out, but I—" he shrugged, "—I said I'd take it to the kitchens. Old habit. Everything interesting ends up in the kitchen eventually."

"That is true," said Brannigan solemnly, "of food, secrets, and bodies."

"Thank you, Brannigan."

"Any time, Your Majesty."

"I've told you not to call me that in here."

"And I've told you," Brannigan said, turning back to his pot with dignity, "that a man kneels once, he kneels forever. It's in the rules."

"There are no rules."

"I wrote some."

Mara gave up, as she usually did, and turned to the book.

* * *

Opening it was like opening a door into a warm room she had thought long cold.

The first pages were exactly what they should have been. *Bread, plain* — her mother's handwriting slanting rightward, quick and sure. *Let the dough tell you when it's done arguing.* Underneath, in smaller script: *If it won't argue, you've killed the yeast. Start over and apologize.*

Mara laughed out loud, sudden and wet, and had to press the heel of her hand against her eyes.

Oscar pretended very hard to organize the spice shelf. Brannigan stirred his pot with tremendous focus. The kitchen, full of people pretending not to watch her grieve, was somehow the kindest place in the valley.

She turned pages. *Stew, wedding-grade.* *Soup for the sick — ginger first, courage second.* *Honey cakes, for bribing children and archivists.* Beside that last one, in different ink, someone had written: *Confirmed effective. — P.*

"Pell," Oscar said, peering over. "That's Pell's handwriting. Did your mother bribe him with honey cakes?"

"My mother bribed everyone with honey cakes. It was how she ran the palace. Half the court doesn't know they were governed by a cook for twenty years."

"Half the court still doesn't," Brannigan muttered. "They think it was them."

But it was the margins where her mother had really lived.

Because interspersed among the breads and stews, in that same quick slanting hand, were the antidotes.

Mara had known, abstractly, that her mother had been the one to build her immunity. Had known it since chapter eleven of her own life, so to speak—since the memory of being six years old and swallowing something bitter while her mother held her hands and counted backward from a hundred, telling her stories of foxes and moons until the shaking stopped. But knowing a thing and seeing it written down, methodically, in a recipe book, between instructions for plum preserves and notes on salting pork—that was different.

*Lesson 14: hemlock, quarter-dose, in milk.* Below it: *She cried. Held her anyway. Never let them see you hold your breath.*

*Lesson 22: arsenic, trace, in honey.* Below: *She asked why. Told her the truth: because the world will hurt you worse, and I would rather be the one who teaches you to survive it. She forgave me by supper. She always forgives faster than I deserve.*

*Lesson 31:* and here the handwriting slowed, grew heavier. *Nightshade, half-dose. She didn't cry. She held MY hands and counted backward from a hundred. I don't know when my daughter became braver than me. I suspect I never did.*

Mara sat with that one for a long time.

The kitchen noises went on around her—Brannigan's pot, the thump of the churn, somewhere above the ordinary creak of a palace pretending it wasn't under threat of regent, god, and moon. She read lesson after lesson. Thirty-eight of them. Thirty-eight poisons, dosed and documented, each followed by her mother's marginal confessions, each one a small act of violence wrapped in an act of love, or possibly the reverse; her mother had never decided, and neither, truly, had Mara.

"She wanted me untouchable," Mara said at last. "I used to think—I don't know. That she was hard. That she loved the work more than me." She shook her head. "She was scared. Every single entry, she was terrified, and she did it anyway."

"That's just what love looks like when it's practical," said Brannigan unexpectedly. "My ma taught me to swim by throwing me off the pier. I resented it for thirty years. Then I fell off a ferry and swam home, and I went to her grave and apologized."

Oscar blinked at him. "Did you really?"

"No, but I meant to. That counts."

"It doesn't count, Brannigan."

"It counts in the kitchen."

* * *

Mara worked through the afternoon, and the light through the kitchen windows went gold and then grey, and nobody interrupted her except Wren, who came down once to deliver a report from the holdouts and stayed to peel apples in companionable silence, her healed leg stretched out straight, her knife moving in small efficient circles.

It was near dusk, on the second-to-last page, that Mara stopped breathing.

The page was headed, in her mother's hand, with no title at all—just a single word, boxed in ink as if to fence it in:

**UNBINDING**

Below it, a list of ingredients. Mara read it twice, three times, her lips moving.

*A crowned nameless taster.*
*The land's own name, spoken whole.*
*A spoon.*

And beneath that, in writing that had grown cramped and careful, as though her mother had been afraid even of her own pen:

*I found this in the archives, in the part of the treaty Pell thinks I never read. I am a cook, not a scholar, but I can read a recipe, and this is a recipe. It is the worst recipe I have ever seen, and I once made haggis for the embassy of the fog-folk, may they rest wherever fog-folk rest.*

*Here is what it says, plainly, because if you are reading this, my darling, then plainness is owed to you:*

*The valley has a name. Not our names—we traded ours away, and that was the bargain, and the bargain was fair. But the LAND has a name, its own, older than the bargain, and our ancestors did not only give up their names. They took the land's name too, and locked it in the crown, and sealed the door, and called it protection. You cannot lock a country's name away without locking up the country. We did not build a shelter. We built a cage, and we moved in, and we called the bars a blessing.*

*The seal is failing. It was always going to fail. Names want to go home.*

*There are two ways this ends. The god takes the name back by force, and the valley breaks open like an egg dropped on stone. Or someone gives it back gently. Willingly. On their own terms.*

*The recipe requires a taster. Of course it does. What is giving a name back but tasting it—taking a thing into your body that was never yours, letting it become part of you, surviving it? And it requires a crown, because the name sleeps in the crown-key. And it requires namelessness, because a named mouth cannot speak a stolen name; the words would catch on their own edges.*

*Mara. My Mara. If you are reading this, then you are crowned, and nameless, and a taster, and I am so sorry, and I am so proud, and I do not know which of those feelings is bigger. I suspect they are the same feeling.*

*You will survive this. I taught you to survive everything. That was the whole point of every bitter spoonful. I would rather break your heart posthumously than let the world break it for me.*

*Taste it slow. Count backward from a hundred. Think of foxes.*

*All my love, longer than the recipe,
Your mother*

At the very bottom, in fresh ink—Pell's ink, Mara realized, added recently, perhaps last night—was a single line:

*The moon turns thirteen in two nights. Whatever we decide, we decide fast. — P.*

* * *

She did not cry. She had been trained out of crying the way she'd been trained out of dying, and besides, Wren was watching her with those sharp grey eyes, and Oscar had come back from feeding the goats, and a queen did not weep into her mother's cookbook.

What she did instead was stand up, walk to the window, and look east, toward the border stones, toward the cliff where a door stood in no wall, toward the antlered shape that patience had made flesh.

Two nights.

"We could run," Oscar said quietly behind her. "Through the Door Out. Vess's map, Pell's scholarship, Wren's leg that can actually climb now. We could be out before the moon fills. Let the valley break open behind us. It wouldn't be our fault."

"Yes, it would," Mara said. "That's the horrible part. My family built the cage. The Kavanaghs were jailers, the king told me that himself. Every year we kept the name locked was a year we starved something alive." She turned around. "My mother knew. She knew for years, probably, and she couldn't fix it, so she fixed me instead. She spent my whole childhood making me into the only person in the valley who could end it."

Wren set down her apple and knife. "Then the question isn't whether you can do it," she said. "It's whether you'll do it on your terms or the god's."

"That," said Mara, "is exactly what my mother would say, and I resent it deeply."

"Good," said Wren. "Resentment keeps the spine stiff. I find it very useful."

Pell arrived as the lamps were lit, moving carefully, his papers clutched to his chest like a shield. He saw the open book on the table and stopped as if he'd walked into glass.

"You found it," he said. "I wondered. After the east wall came down, I wondered if—" He crossed the room and looked down at the boxed word, and his face did something complicated. "She showed me the honey cake page once. Never this. All those years, and she never once told me she knew."

"She was protecting you," Mara said. "Or testing you. With my mother it was usually both."

"Both," Pell agreed softly. "Always both." He laid his papers beside the book—the treaty fragments, the translations, the terrible arithmetic of the thirteenth moon. "Mara. There's something else. Something I should have said sooner, and I'm saying it now because if I wait for a better moment, the better moment will arrive after the world ends." He took a breath. "The pages I burned, decades ago. The ones I told you about. I burned them to protect someone. I need to tell you who."

Outside, far off toward the great shrine, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the urgent triple-stroke that meant fire, or worse, and running feet were already crossing the courtyard below.

Pell looked at the window, then back at her, jaw tight.

"After," he said. "When we know what's burning."

Mara closed her mother's book, tucked it under her arm like a shield of her own, and went to see.
