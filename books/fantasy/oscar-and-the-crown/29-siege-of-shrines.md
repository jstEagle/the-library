## 29. Siege of Shrines

Pell had always believed that the worst thing that could happen to a scholar was a fire in the library.

He had been wrong, of course. He had been wrong about a great many things lately—the treaty pages he'd burned, the name he'd kept secret for forty years, the assumption that his knees would outlast his curiosity—but this morning, watching the first torch crest the hill toward the great shrine of the Valley of the Unnamed, he thought: *no, I had it right the first time. Fire is the worst thing. I simply failed to imagine so much of it.*

The shrine sat on the high pasture above the village of Lowmark, a ring of standing stones older than the founding bargain itself, and around it—this was the part that made Pell's chest ache—someone had built shelves. Over the past three moons, as the valley split into believers and holdouts, Wren and her people had carried their books up the hill: parish records, herbals, genealogies, the birth-rolls where every child's given name had been entered for six hundred years by people who did not know they were writing on kindling. The believers wanted to burn them because the books held names, and names were what the god was collecting, and Wren's people had decided—if you are going to take our names, you will have to take them from us one at a time.

Pell had argued, gently, that this was not how archives worked, that a book was not a person, that grief makes poor librarians. Wren had looked at him with her healed leg planted firmly on the shrine's threshold and said, "Master Pell, you've spent your whole life keeping things safe by hiding them. We're trying something else."

He had no answer to that. He rarely had answers to Wren. So instead he had spent two days copying the most irreplaceable rolls onto oiled cloth in waterproof ink, packing duplicates into the root cellar beneath the palace, and telling himself that preparation was a form of courage, which it was, which it also was not.

Now the torches came up the hill road, forty or fifty of them, and at their head walked Lady Vess Corvane in a grey traveling coat, carrying nothing but a lantern, which was somehow worse than carrying a sword.

"They're early," said Tam Broadfield, the shrine's self-appointed warden, a stonemason with forearms like loaves of bread. "I told them noon."

"Vess has never once in her life arrived anywhere at an appointed time," Pell murmured, squinting down the slope. "She arrives when she calculates you'll be least ready. It's the only form of punctuality she respects." He adjusted his spectacles. His hands were shaking. He noted this, catalogued it, filed it under *symptoms of terror*, and found that the filing calmed him somewhat. A scholar's trick: if you can name a thing, you can shelve it, even if only temporarily, even if the shelf is on fire.

* * *

The confrontation began, as all the valley's disasters did, with somebody quoting scripture nobody had written down.

"You keep dead words on those shelves," Vess called up the hill, her voice carrying with the particular clarity of someone who had practiced projecting across dinner tables full of enemies. "Dead words hold dead names, and dead names feed the thief. Burn the rolls and the god goes hungry. Open the Door and none of us need fear hunger again."

"That's not what the god wants," Pell called back, before he could stop himself, before the sensible part of his brain could remind him that he was seventy-one years old and archivists do not lead negotiations. "It doesn't want *fewer* names, Corvane, it wants *its own*. You've read my translations. Burning the birth-rolls is like burning a hostage to spite a kidnapper."

A ripple went through the crowd below. Some of them hadn't known there were translations. Some of them hadn't known there was a *kidnapper* rather than a benefactor. Vess's eyes found Pell on the shrine steps and he saw, with the peculiar clarity that terror lends to old men, that she was sorry to see him there—not out of affection, though there had been some, once, decades ago, over shared candle stubs and stolen wine in the palace undercroft—but out of arithmetic. He was a variable she had hoped would stay home.

"Master Pell," she said, warmer now, which was worse. "Come down. Bring your copies. There will be a place for scholars in the new valley. There must be. What is a country without its memory?"

"A country without its memory," Pell answered, "is exactly what we have now, madam. I've seen the alternative. It's called everything else."

That got a laugh from Wren's side of the hill—a thin, frightened laugh, but a laugh—and Pell felt something in his old chest straighten. This was, he reflected, the strange gift of scholarship in dark hours: you spend your whole life learning that most arguments have already happened somewhere, sometime, to someone, and that the words for them are lying in wait on a shelf. He knew what came next. He had read accounts of sieges. The next thing after the opening speeches was always—

A stone arced out of the crowd below and struck the nearest shelf.

Not the stones. Not the people. The shelf. Someone down there had done the same reading Pell had, and drawn the same conclusion: the fastest way through a siege of ideas is to burn the library first.

* * *

What followed was not, strictly speaking, a battle. Battles have lines and commands. This had neither. It had Tam Broadfield roaring at the holdouts to lock shields, and half of them doing it; it had believers surging uphill shouting that they meant no harm to persons, only to paper, which was a distinction that lasted approximately ninety seconds once the first shelf caught and the first holdout swung a hay-hook at the man holding the torch.

Pell did not fight. Pell had never fought anything except a stubborn footnote, and he saw no reason to break precedent. What Pell did was what Pell had trained his whole life to do, the thing his body understood the way other bodies understood swordwork: he turned and ran *toward* the fire.

The western shelves were already smoking—the genealogy rolls, three hundred years of Kavanaghs and Corvanes and Broadfields, the very document that proved Mara's bloodline, proving it in ink that burned at roughly the temperature of a warm bath. Pell got there ahead of the flames, coughing, sleeves pinned back, and started pulling rolls free with the brisk economy of a man who had rehearsed evacuations in his head for forty years the way other men rehearsed insults.

"The east alcove!" he shouted to a boy—he couldn't remember the boy's name, which these days meant either plague-of-names or simple age, and he chose to believe simple age—"the east alcove, carry, don't throw, and *mind the water damage*!"

The boy stared at him. Everyone always stared at Pell in moments like this, with an expression he'd learned to translate as *you're worried about the wrong catastrophe, old man.* But the boy went, and Pell went back for more.

Smoke was a scholar's oldest colleague. Pell had worked in the undercroft archive through two chimney fires and one memorable flood, and he knew smoke the way farmers know weather: it tells you where the trouble is coming from before the trouble arrives. This smoke came from the south, low and greasy—lamp oil, then. They had brought lamp oil to a prayer dispute. He filed that away too, in the drawer marked *evidence for the tribunal I may not live to attend.*

Then a torch came through the smoke and a hand grabbed his collar, and Pell found himself face to face with a young believer whose name he did know—Joss Merrow, the ferryman's son, who had sung in the shrine choir as a child with a voice like a wet whistle, and who was now holding fire four inches from Pell's nose.

"The books die, old man, or you do. Lady's orders. Nobody has to get hurt."

"Son," said Pell, with the calm of a man whose heart was trying to exit through his ears, "I have been threatened by better-read people than you. Put the torch down and I will show you a page that will change your mind, and if it doesn't, you can still burn me afterward. I'll wait. Scholarship has taught me patience, and also that being right is cold comfort, but occasionally it is *useful*—"

Joss hesitated. That was the tragedy of Joss Merrow, Pell would think later: he hesitated. He was twenty years old and had been promised miracles and meaning, and when an old man offered him a *page*, some buried scrap of the boy who'd sung off-key still stirred. He lowered the torch maybe a finger's width.

Which was when the shelf behind them went up all at once, oil-fed, with a sound like a gasp, and the world became heat.

* * *

Afterward, reconstructing it—as he reconstructed everything, lying in the infirmary with bandages like a mummy who'd died mid-argument—Pell could only account for his own actions by observing that the body, too, keeps archives. Somewhere in him was stored every instinct of forty years' custodianship, and when the genealogy rolls began to catch, that archive spoke, and it said: *not these. Anything but these.*

He remembered shoving Joss Merrow bodily out of the way—Joss stumbling backward into the grass, alive, unburned, staring. He remembered tearing his coat off and beating at the flames along the bottom shelf, remembering absurdly that wool smothers fire better than linen, thanking his late mother silently for dressing him warmly. He remembered the roll of the Corvane line coming loose in his hand, and the Kavanagh roll beside it, and thinking with a clarity that felt like falling: *these two families, bound in one box for three hundred years, and it ends here, in fire, like everything else.*

He remembered arms around his shoulders, hauling him back—Tam Broadfield, soot-faced, bellowing something about mad old men—and he remembered twisting free, because the top shelf was still intact and the top shelf held the founding fragments, the pre-bargain scraps, the handful of pages in the land's own tongue that Pell had sworn on his burned conscience to protect, and he was reaching for them when the burning shelf above came down.

He did not remember the rest. The rest, he learned later, in pieces, from witnesses who each told it differently and all of whom agreed on the essential fact: Master Pell had come out of the collapsing shelf with both arms full of burning documents, thrown himself flat over them to smother the flames against his own chest and back, and had remained there, curled around the papers like a hen over eggs, while Tam dragged the fire off him and Wren beat out the embers in his hair.

The documents survived. Two of them, scorched at the corners, damp with the water someone finally had the sense to throw. The founding fragments. The land's own tongue, curling at the edges but legible, legible, *legible*.

Pell, when they turned him over, was less legible.

* * *

He woke—it might have been hours, it might have been a day—to the smell of comfrey and beeswax and the sound of Mara Kavanagh arguing quietly with somebody about honey versus lard for burns, in the tone of a woman who had been dosed with poisons since childhood and considered herself the valley's authority on what the body could survive. He decided not to open his eyes yet. Opening his eyes would make it official.

"He's awake," said Wren, who had apparently developed the sight, or more likely was watching his eyelids, which any fool could do, which was why Pell liked her. "His eyebrows are moving. That's how you can tell he's composing a lecture."

"I am not," said Pell, opening his eyes, "composing a lecture. I am composing several. One on fire safety in archive design, which I have been meaning to deliver for thirty years. One on the tactical stupidity of bringing oil to a hilltop in wind. And one—" here his voice betrayed him, cracked down the middle like old vellum, "—one on gratitude, to be delivered to whoever pulled me out, assuming I ever learn to say it without crying, which at present seems unlikely."

Mara sat beside him. She looked, he thought, exactly like a queen, which was a problem they were all politely ignoring. Her hands were stained with salve. She took his wrist with a taster's gentleness—reading his pulse the way she read a spoon, professionally, hopefully finding nothing.

"You saved the founding fragments," she said.

"I saved two of nine." He closed his eyes. Even that small movement hurt; his back was a country he did not wish to govern. "Seven are ash. And the birth-rolls—"

"Gone. Most of them. Wren's people got the eastern third out, and your oiled-cloth copies were already in the root cellar, thank the—" Mara stopped. Thank the what? Nobody in the valley thanked anything anymore without checking first who was listening. "Thank *you*, Master Pell. For making us copy everything twice. You fussy, impossible, magnificent old man."

"Fussiness," Pell said, "is love wearing a cardigan," and was privately pleased when both women laughed, briefly, wetly.

Wren leaned closer. Her crutch stood against the wall—she used it some days still, habit more than need, and Pell suspected she kept it partly to remind the valley what the gods' gifts cost and partly because it was a very good crutch. "The believers broke when you went down," she said quietly. "Did you know that? Joss Merrow threw down his torch and just... sat in the grass. Half of them stopped. They came to burn paper, Pell, and they watched a seventy-year-old man wrap himself around it like it was a child." She swallowed. "Vess called them off. Said the objective was achieved anyway. But her soldiers wouldn't look at her."

"And the shrine?" Pell asked, though he could smell the answer in his own bandages.

"The center stones stand. The shelves are cinders." Wren's jaw tightened. "We'll rebuild. Books grow back. People—" She didn't finish. Three holdouts had died on the hill. Two believers. The number sat in the room with them, unarguable.

* * *

Night came. Mara dozed in a chair, refusing to leave, and Wren went down to organize watches, and Pell lay alone with the ceiling and took inventory, as scholars do.

The body: damaged. The back especially. He had heard the infirmarian use the word *grievous* to Tam outside the door, in the careful voice medical people use when they think the patient is asleep. Pell was not afraid of dying, particularly; he had spent his life among dead people's papers and had come to regard death as the final and least organized archive. What frightened him was unfinished work. Seven founding fragments burned. The remaining two needed translation before the thirteenth moon filled—two moons, perhaps less—and his hands shook now even when he wasn't terrified, which was going to make penmanship interesting.

There was something else. Something he had seen in the last instant before the shelf fell, when he'd reached into the flame for the founding fragments: a mark on the corner of the topmost page, a seal he had never noticed in forty years of study, because it was written not in ink but pressed into the vellum itself, blind-stamped, visible only when firelight passed through from behind. A circle of thirteen marks, twelve filled, and at the center, a word in the land's own tongue that Pell had spent his whole life pretending not to be able to read.

He could read it. He had always been able to read it. That was, in fact, the secret he had burned the treaty pages to protect, forty years ago, for a person he still could not name aloud.

The word was a name. Not a human one. The land's. And beneath it, smaller, stamped like a signature on a confession, a second word:

*Kavanagh.*

Pell lay in the dark, listening to Mara breathe in the chair beside him, and understood suddenly, completely, with the terrible elegance of a proof arriving too late to save anyone, why her family's roll had been shelved directly beside the Corvanes', why the crown was a key, why the god had come to *her* border stone first.

And why Vess—who had studied those same fragments, who was cleverer than Pell by half—might already have seen the same stamp by now, in the light of her own lantern, on whatever page her spies had salvaged from the ashes.

He turned his head, carefully, toward the sleeping queen in the kitchen chair, and began, silently, to compose the most difficult lecture of his life: the one in which he told her everything, before the moon finished filling, before Vess arrived with her arithmetic and her lantern, before the last page of the valley's memory burned through.

Outside, the thirteenth moon rose, one night fuller than the night before.
