## 30. Moon the Tenth

Oscar had learned to read the fog the way other boys read weather. It had moods. On good days it lay over the valley like a sleeping dog, heavy and warm and utterly indifferent. On bad days it paced the borders, pressing against the standing stones as if testing a fence it suddenly remembered it could jump.

This was a bad day.

He stood at the edge of his mother's turnip field with a basket of winter feed under one arm, watching the fog do something he had never once seen it do in sixteen years of living in the valley.

It was leaving.

Not all at once—nothing in the valley ever happened all at once—but in slow gray ribbons, sliding through the cracks that had opened along the border stones, pouring out into the world beyond like water finding a hole in a bucket. And where the fog went, Oscar realized with a lurch, the world could see in.

"Oscar!" His mother's voice cracked across the field like a whip. "Get away from the stones!"

"I'm not touching them!"

"You're *looking* at them! You know what looking leads to!"

In the Kavanagh household—and every household for three hundred years—the chain of sin had always been: looking, wondering, praying, damnation. His mother had the first two steps down to a science. The third was Oscar's private failing, and the fourth, lately, seemed increasingly out of everyone's hands.

But he didn't move away from the stones. He set down the basket instead, because what he was seeing deserved a steady view.

The nearest border stone stood at the top of Barrow Hill, a gray fang of rock older than any building in the valley, carved with spirals so worn they were more suggestion than pattern. For as long as anyone could remember, the stone had hummed. Not loudly. Just enough that you felt it in your teeth when you leaned against it, which children were forbidden to do, which meant every child in the valley's history had done it.

The humming had stopped three days ago.

Now there was a crack down the stone's face, thin as a hair but deep as a well, and through that crack the fog was escaping in a thin silver thread, and through the gap the fog left behind—

Oscar squinted.

Beyond the hill, past the stone, there should have been nothing. That was the whole point of the valley. Every map ended at the borders; every road walked you in circles back home; the fog swallowed the horizon on all four sides. There was no *beyond*. There had never been a beyond. Asking about the beyond was like asking what was north of north.

Except now, through the widening crack, Oscar could see hills. Green ones. With sheep on them. And a road—a real road, rutted and muddy, running off toward a horizon that kept going, that did not politely curve back around to the valley, that simply *continued*, indifferent and enormous.

And on the road, small with distance but unmistakable, was a man. A shepherd, maybe, or a tinker. He had stopped walking. He was staring at the crack in the air the way a man stares at a door in a wall where he is quite certain there has never been a door.

"He can see us," Oscar said aloud, to nobody, to the turnips, to himself. "We can be *seen*."

His mother arrived at a dead run, skirts hitched, wooden spoon still in her hand from whatever she'd been stirring, which in the Kavanagh house was less a tool than a sidearm. She planted herself between Oscar and the hill, took one look at the crack, and made a sound he'd never heard her make before. Small. Almost a whistle.

"Mam?"

"Don't say anything," she whispered. "Don't say your name. Don't say mine. Don't—" She stopped. Her knuckles whitened around the spoon. "Oh, saints. Oh, saints preserve. The fog's going *out*."

"It's been going out for days," Oscar said. "You've seen it."

"I've seen weather. This isn't weather." She grabbed his collar and hauled him back toward the farmhouse, and Oscar went, because you went when your mother hauled, that was physics. But he looked back over his shoulder the whole way, and the last thing he saw before the hedge cut off the view was the distant figure on the road taking a step closer to the crack.

Then another.

Walking toward the valley.

* * *

By evening, the whole village knew, because the whole village always knew, usually before the thing itself had finished happening. Oscar sat on the low wall outside the tavern—he was too young to go in, which had never once stopped him from knowing everything said inside it—and listened to the valley come apart at the seams.

"The tinker Bram saw a whole *caravan*," said old Hobbes, who had seen a caravan exactly once, in a dream, forty years ago, and had been dining out on it since. "Wagons and horses and a woman selling ribbons. Right there past the stones. Ribbons, Oscar's ma, colored ones."

"There's nothing past the stones," said Weaver Dunn, with the flat certainty of a man whose entire livelihood depended on the world staying a known size. "There's never been anything past the stones."

"And yet," said Hobbes, gesturing grandly at the horizon, where the evening fog was visibly leaking away like soup from a punctured pot, "there's the evidence."

"That's not evidence, that's *fog behaving badly*."

"Fog doesn't behave badly, Dunn. Fog behaves like fog. This is something else."

Oscar hugged his knees and said nothing, which was hard, because saying nothing was hardest precisely when he knew something. He knew things now. He knew the weight of a name whispered by something antlered and patient; he knew how luck felt in the palm, like a coin that was always slightly warm; he knew what magic cost, down to the ache in his hands that no amount of rubbing warmed anymore. Ten years old, those hands, older than the rest of him. He caught himself hiding them in his sleeves sometimes, the way you hide a scar.

And he knew, better than anyone in this tavern arguing about fog, that the valley had never been hidden by accident. Someone had built the walls. Someone had paid for them. And whatever was cracking them open now was collecting on a debt three centuries overdue.

The tavern door banged open and Wren came out, leaning on her stick even though she barely needed it anymore—the gods' gift had healed her leg months ago, though she still carried the stick, Oscar suspected, mostly for hitting people with. She spotted him on the wall and dropped onto it beside him with the groan of someone twice her age.

"Well?" she said. "What are they saying in there?"

"That it's fog behaving badly."

Wren snorted. "Dunn?"

"Dunn."

"He'd call the Flood 'rain with ideas.'" She was quiet a moment, turning her stick end over end in the dirt. Then: "I saw them too, you know. Past the stones. Three days ago, up by the eastern march. A family. Real people, Oscar. A woman washing clothes in a stream, like it was just an ordinary Tuesday, like the whole time we've been in here erasing ourselves, she was out there hanging socks on a line." Her voice did something complicated on the word *erasing*. "Do you know what I thought? I thought: she doesn't know we exist. Nobody out there knows. We could vanish tomorrow—all of us, every soul in this valley—and the world wouldn't even lose a sock."

"That's cheerful."

"It's true, though, isn't it? That's what the namelessness bought us. Safety that looks exactly the same as never having mattered." She flicked a pebble into the dark. "My gran used to say the valley was God's pocket, where He kept the things He didn't want to lose. I used to think that was sweet. Now I think it's more like a drawer. Things in drawers don't get worn out or loved to pieces. They just... wait."

Oscar thought about the god at the border stone. Antlers and patience. *Little borrower.* He thought about names being collected, moon by moon, and the plague of forgetting that had started in the far villages—Pell had shown him the ledger, page after page of names going blank like ink fading in rain.

"Wren," he said carefully, "if the valley gets remembered—if we go back into the world—do you think people will want us to have been waiting?"

She looked at him sideways. "That's a strange question."

"I'm a strange person. I have a borrowed name and old hands."

She laughed despite herself, then sobered. "I don't know. Maybe. Maybe they'll want to know why we hid. Maybe there's no answer that sounds brave from the outside." She tapped his knee with the stick, gently. "Why do you ask?"

Because, Oscar wanted to say, I met the thing our ancestors stole from, and it wasn't a monster, it was a landlord, and we are tenants who trashed the place and changed the locks. Because Mara carries a crown she never asked for and a name she can't speak, and in three moons one of those has to break. Because I spent my luck already and I know now that everything given is everything owed.

Instead he said, "No reason," which was the traditional response of kitchen boys throughout history, and Wren accepted it with the traditional eye-roll.

* * *

Two nights later, the first outsider came through.

Oscar was in the palace kitchens—it was safer there, lately, near Mara, near Pell, near the few people who knew the shape of what was coming—when the shouting started in the courtyard. He shouldered through the scullery door in time to see a crowd gathered around a figure sitting on the wet cobbles, dripping fog-water, holding a donkey by its halter with the desperate grip of a man who suspects the donkey is the only real thing left in the universe.

He was a wool merchant, it turned out. Master Aldous Fenwick, of somewhere called Harrowdale, which was, he insisted, a perfectly ordinary market town that appeared on perfectly ordinary maps. He had been following the road over the downs when the fog ahead of him had parted—"parted, sirs, like a curtain, like it was *invited*"—and there had been a valley where no valley was marked, and a gate in the mist, and being a merchant, he had done the only sensible thing a merchant does when presented with an unmarked territory.

He'd come in to see what the prices were like.

"They'll hang me in Harrowdale if I come back empty-handed," he was explaining to the growing crowd, with the wounded dignity of a man whose audience keeps changing size. "Three hundred years my family's traded these roads. My grandfather mapped every mile between here and the coast, may he rest, and there was *nothing* here, I tell you, nothing but fog and a feeling like being watched. And now—" he gestured at the palace, at the towers, at the impossible fact of a country inside a country, "—now there's a *city*. A whole city. With a *palace*. Whose palace? Who's king here?"

Nobody answered. The king was dying, the succession was a secret sleeping in a kitchen, and the question of who ruled the Valley of the Unnamed had recently become both unanswerable and extremely dangerous.

"A king of fog," said Hobbes, who had apparently traveled all the way from his tavern to be present at history. "Fog and turnips."

"Turnips," said Fenwick, perking up despite himself. "How *are* your turnips?"

"*Don't*," said Wren, appearing at Oscar's elbow, "tell the merchant about the turnips. He'll want samples. Samples lead to contracts. Contracts lead to the world knowing we exist, and then—" she faltered, and Oscar understood why. Then what? That was the question the whole valley was circling, night after night, like moths around a lamp that might be a lamp or might be fire.

Pell pushed through the crowd, robes flying, spectacles fogged, looking like an owl that had lost an argument with a library. He took one look at Fenwick and his donkey and pressed both hands over his face.

"Oh no," he said, muffled. "Oh no, oh no. It's starting."

"What's starting?" Oscar asked.

Pell lowered his hands. Behind the spectacles his eyes were red-rimmed and terrified and, underneath the terror, doing something Oscar recognized from his own mirror: shining. "Contact," Pell said. "Documented, witnessed, bilateral contact with the external world after three centuries of sealed isolation. Do you know what this means? Do you know what this *means*?"

"Yes," said Wren. "Trouble."

"—primary sources! External corroboration! I must interview him at once, before the memory contaminates itself—Master Fenwick! Master Fenwick, what did the fog feel like when it parted? Was there a sound? Was there a *smell*?"

Fenwick blinked at the mad archivist, then at the crowd, then at the palace rising behind them all, and Oscar watched something happen in the man's face—the exact moment wonder curdled into calculation, the merchant's ancient arithmetic of *what can I get and what will it cost me*.

"There's a smell," Fenwick said slowly. "There's a smell of a market with no competition. Tell me about the turnips."

* * *

They put the merchant up in the gatehouse, partly out of hospitality and partly because nobody trusted him out of sight. By morning there were two more travelers at the crack in the eastern stones, and by noon a shepherd boy from the green hills who claimed he'd only followed a lost lamb and had absolutely not been dared by his friends, and by dusk the first official-looking person: a rider in a slate-gray coat bearing, sewn on the shoulder, a badge Oscar didn't recognize—a tower beneath a crescent moon.

He saw the rider from the kitchen window while scrubbing pots, which was where Oscar did his best thinking and all his worst worrying. The rider stood at the border stone for a long time, studying the crack, studying the valley, making notes in a little book. Then, deliberately, the rider took out a surveyor's stake and drove it into the ground beside the stone.

Marking it. Claiming the observation, if not yet the land.

Oscar dried his hands—his old, cold hands—and went to find Mara.

He found her in the pantry, because of course he did; she lived half her life among the sacks and jars now, crowned in secret, tasting in public, carrying a kingdom in a room the size of a chapel. She was reading her mother's recipe book by candlelight, and she didn't look up when he came in, but she said, "You've got your worried face on. I can hear it."

"People are coming in," Oscar said. "Through the cracks. A merchant, a shepherd, and now a man with a badge on his coat. A tower and a crescent moon. He staked the border, Mara. Like marking a deer trail."

Now she looked up. In the candlelight her face did the thing it always did when bad news landed—not fear exactly, but a kind of grim settling, a weight redistributing itself. "A badge," she repeated. "Whose badge?"

"I don't know. That's the problem, isn't it? For three hundred years nobody came because nobody could. Now anybody can. And the first ones through the door aren't pilgrims or lost lambs—they're merchants counting our turnips and soldiers measuring our stones." He wrapped his arms around himself. "We spent three centuries being invisible, and I used to think that was the worst thing. Being forgotten. Being a drawer nobody opens." His voice cracked, traitorously young. "But Mara, I watched that rider write us down in his book, and I think—I think being *seen* by people who don't love you might be worse."

Mara closed the recipe book. When she spoke, her voice was quiet and steady, the voice she used when the poison was on the spoon and there was no choice but to swallow. "The seal was never going to hold. Pell told us that at Moon the First. The thirteenth moon fills, the bargain comes due, and either we give the name back on our terms or it's taken on theirs." She touched the book's cracked leather cover. "My mother left me a way. One way. It's terrible, and it costs everything, and I haven't told you the worst part yet because I keep hoping I'll find a better line in the margins."

"The worst part being?"

She almost smiled. Almost. "That I might actually have to do it."

Outside the pantry window, the fog was streaming eastward in long silver banners, thinner than it had ever been, and through the thinning Oscar could see stars he had no names for—stars that belonged to the world's sky, not the valley's, hanging over hills where people hung socks on lines and drew maps and drove stakes into the ground beside old stones.

Somewhere out there, the rider with the tower-and-crescent badge was writing the valley down. Somewhere out there, maps were about to stop agreeing with themselves. And somewhere much closer, in the dark of the borderlands, something antlered and patient was watching the cracks widen with what Oscar could only describe as satisfaction.

Three moons left. The drawer was opening whether they climbed out or were spilled.

"Mara," Oscar said, "whatever the recipe says—I'm going to be there when you cook it."

She looked at him for a long moment, this kitchen boy with ten extra years in his hands, and whatever she saw made her nod once, slow, like a queen accepting a vow she'd have rather refused.

"Then wash your hands," she said. "We start tomorrow."
