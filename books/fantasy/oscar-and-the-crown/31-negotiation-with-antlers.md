## 31. Negotiation with Antlers

Mara had faced down arsenic in a honeyed custard, henbane steeped in wine, and one memorable attempt at water hemlock ground into bread flour so finely that only her tongue's long memory caught the sweetness beneath it. She had learned, over years of quiet poisonings survived, that the worst things rarely announced themselves. They arrived dressed as kindness.

So when Pell told her that the god had agreed to meet—had *asked*, politely, through a border stone that hummed like a struck bell—she did not feel triumphant or terrified so much as professionally suspicious.

"It wants to negotiate," Pell said. He was moving through his archive with the frantic energy of a man who had recently discovered he might not live to finish cataloguing it, pulling scrolls and setting them down again as if none were quite the right scroll. "It named terms. A parley. Neutral ground."

"Gods don't have neutral ground," Mara said. "They're gods. The ground is them."

"Then we shall be negotiating on its person," Pell said, "which I admit is not ideal." He paused, and something gentler moved under the dread in his face. "You are not obliged to go, you know. Whatever the crown has made you—and I am sorry about the crown, Mara, truly—you can still refuse."

She thought about refusing. She thought about it the way she thought about most things lately: briefly, and then with the knowledge that refusal was a luxury someone else could afford.

"No," she said. "I want to hear what it says. And I want to look it in the—" she gestured vaguely at her own forehead, where antlers would presumably begin, "—whatever it looks out of."

"The meeting is set for moonrise tomorrow, at the old shrine by the border stones. The one that burned."

"That seems pointed."

"Everything about this is pointed," Pell said. "That is rather the problem with gods."

* * *

Oscar insisted on coming, which surprised no one who knew him, least of all Oscar.

"I'm the one it talks to," he said, arms crossed, standing in the kitchen doorway while Mara packed a satchel with the grim practicality of a woman who had once prepared for every meal as though it might be her last and now prepared for everything that way. "It calls me 'little borrower.' It gave me—" he flexed his hands, still too-old hands, knuckles like river stones, "—well. It gave me things. I should be there."

"You should be anywhere else," Mara said.

"Yes," Oscar agreed cheerfully. "But I'm coming anyway. Wren's holding the holdouts together at the shrine, Pell's dictating his testament to anyone who'll stand still, and Vess has half the palace. That leaves me. I'm the entire remainder of your retinue."

"A retinue of one kitchen boy."

"One kitchen boy with impossible luck," he said, wiggling the aged fingers. "Watch." He reached up and plucked an apple from the high shelf without looking, catching it behind his back. It was, admittedly, impressive. It was also, Mara noticed, followed by a faint wince, as if the luck had cost him a coin from some internal purse.

"How much does that cost you?" she asked quietly.

Oscar's grin flickered. He was nineteen and had gotten very good at grins that covered things. "Depends on the trick," he said. "The apple was cheap. Don't worry about me."

"I'm a taster," Mara said. "Worrying is the whole job."

He laughed at that, and then didn't, and then they stood in the kitchen for a moment among the pots and pans where she'd been crowned, listening to the fog press against the windows like something patient waiting to be let in.

"Mara," he said. "If it offers you your name back—I saw what it did to Wren's leg. What it did to my hands. Gifts from that thing come with teeth."

"I know," she said. "I've spent my whole life learning that the sweetest-tasting things hide the sharpest bones." She slung the satchel over her shoulder. "But thank you. It's nice to be warned by someone who likes me, instead of someone who wants to use me. It makes a change."

* * *

The burned shrine sat in a fold of hillside near the western border stones, its timbers charred to black lace against the grey sky. Fog pooled around it thick as wool. Wren met them there with six of the holdouts, armed mostly with farm tools and fury, and looked Mara up and down with the frank appraisal of a girl whose leg had been healed by a miracle and who had chosen, deliberately, to limp on it ever since.

"You're really going to talk to it," Wren said.

"I'm going to listen to it. Talking implies I expect to get a word in."

Wren almost smiled. "My gran used to say that about my grandfather." Then the humor drained out of her face, and she nodded toward the shrine. "It's already here. Has been since sundown. The air's been... thick. Like before a storm, but inside-out."

Mara felt it too. Not magic exactly—the valley had no magic, that was the whole point of the valley—but the *absence* of absence, a pressure like the moment before a sneeze, held forever. She walked past the holdouts, past the burned threshold, into the shrine's gutted heart, and the fog came with her, closing like a curtain.

And there it stood.

She had expected the figure from Oscar's stories: antlers and patience, a shape made of branches and shadow. What she found was both more and less than that. The thing in the shrine was tall the way a cliff is tall, and it wore antlers the way a mountain wears snow—not decoration but consequence. Its body was dark wood and darker space, and where a face should have been there was a depth, a hollow, and within the hollow two points of pale light that regarded her with an attention so total it felt physical, like being weighed.

"Taster," it said. Its voice was wind through a forest, and underneath the wind, unmistakably, there was pain. Old pain. The kind of pain that stops being a sensation and becomes a landscape.

"Crown," it added, with something that might have been dry amusement. "Queen. Nameless one. You collect titles the way your king collected dust."

"I prefer 'Mara,'" she said. "Though technically I can't prove it. It's the only name I've ever owned, and I bought it myself, secondhand, off a cook who liked the sound of it."

The pale lights brightened slightly. If a creature made of antlers could blink in surprise, this one did.

"Humor," it said. "From the lock itself. Your ancestors were a grim lot. They bargained in terror and built their walls out of shame. None of them joked."

"My mother joked," Mara said. "Usually while feeding me poison. It's a family tradition—we do our best laughing in terrible situations." She folded her arms. "You asked for a parley. So let's skip the part where you try to frighten me and I pretend it worked. What do you want?"

The god was silent for a long moment. Fog moved through the ruined walls. Somewhere outside, Wren's people shifted, and the border stones hummed their low struck-bell note.

"What do you know of how this valley was made?" the god asked.

"Pell's version," Mara said. "My ancestors surrendered their names to bar out magic. The valley was erased from maps. Sealed. Safe."

"Safe," the god repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. That is the version the jailers wrote. Would you like mine?"

"I've come all this way."

The god raised one vast antlered head, and the pale lights turned upward, as if looking at something far above the roofless shrine, above the fog, above the sky.

"This land had a name," it said. "Not a name like yours—a little human sound, a tag, a label. A true name. The kind of name that *is* the thing. Say it, and the rivers remember their courses. Say it, and the soil grows fat. Say it, and every living thing in the borders of this valley knows exactly what it is and where it stands. My name. My body. The same thing, for such as me. The land and the name were never separate, taster. The name was the land, awake."

Mara's mouth had gone dry. "And my ancestors took it."

"They *caged* it," the god said, and now the pain in the voice came forward, no longer hidden under the wind, and it was so enormous and so weary that Mara's knees nearly buckled with the sympathy of it. "They could not take it—no one can take a true name from what it names. But they could bind it. They built a seal of surrendered names, thousands of them, a wall of unlabeled souls, and they wrapped it around me like a bandage around a mouth. They locked my name inside their bloodline—" the lights fixed on her, "—inside *you*, crown-and-lock, last daughter of the bargain—and they erased the valley from every map so that no prayer from outside could reach me, and no voice of mine could answer. Do you understand what they made? They did not build a country. They built a cage, and they called the cage a kingdom, and they put a king inside to hold the key, and they called him a ruler."

"The fog," Mara whispered. "The king rules fog because—"

"Because fog is what a country breathes when its throat is held," the god said. "Every year of stasis. Every harvest that neither failed nor flourished. Every child born and given no name until the age of naming, because names were currency and the seal consumed them. Every generation that lived and died unremembered by the world outside. That was not safety, taster. That was suffocation, administered slowly, to everyone, forever. Your people were not the beneficiaries of the bargain. They were the padding of the cage. The soft lining, so the prisoner would have nothing to rage against."

Mara thought of the thirteenth moon on the kitchen wall. Of the calendar that miscounted. Of crops that grew adequately and never wonderfully, of weather that never surprised, of a lifetime spent invisible in a palace that served no kingdom. Her whole life, she had worn her invisibility like a modest pride. *No one notices the taster.* She had thought it was a skill. It had been a symptom.

"Why now?" she asked, and her voice cracked on it. "Why is the seal failing after all these centuries?"

"Because seals of names erode," the god said. "Every generation, fewer names freely given, more inherited as duty. And because someone burned pages of the treaty." The lights flickered toward the darkness beyond the shrine, toward the archive, toward Pell's shame. "And because a boy prayed over a dying goat with his whole heart, and for the first time in three hundred years, a prayer reached me. Do you know what that was like? To hear a voice after three hundred years of silence? I answered. I would have answered anything. I would have crawled across broken glass to answer." The great head lowered. "I frightened him. I am sorry. I have forgotten how to be small."

Mara stood very still. She had come expecting a villain. She had rehearsed refusals, hardened herself against temptation, prepared to face the seducer of kitchen boys and the burner of shrines. Instead she found—she searched for the word and hated it and could find no other—

A prisoner.

A wounded animal in a cage built by her own blood, licking wounds that had festered for centuries, and lashing out at the bars, and the bars were people.

"The shrines that burned," she said carefully. "The believers and the holdouts. The plague of forgetting. People losing their names."

"I am dying," the god said simply. "Or whatever passes for dying, for such as me. When the thirteenth moon fills, the seal fails entirely, and my name returns to me whether your people survive the return or not. The burning, the taking—it is a drowning man pulling at whatever floats. I am not proud of it. But pride is a luxury of the comfortable, and I have not been comfortable since before your valley had a calendar." The lights dimmed. "I asked for this parley because I am tired, taster. Because some part of me remembers that the first ones who bound me wept while they did it, and I have wondered for three hundred years whether any of their descendants might weep too. Whether any of them might *listen*."

The fog pressed close. Outside, the border stones hummed. Mara became aware that her cheeks were wet, and wiped them with the brisk irritation of a woman who had trained herself never to show reaction to anything she ingested.

"So this is the negotiation," she said. "You want your name back. My blood holds it. When the moon fills, you take it, and the valley—" she swallowed. "What happens to the valley? To the people? When the land wakes up after three hundred years asleep?"

"I do not know," the god admitted, and the honesty of it was somehow worse than any threat. "A limb that has slept too long may wake singing or wake screaming. I would rather wake gently. I would rather be *given* than *seized*. That is the negotiation, crown-and-lock. Find a way to give me back my name—one that does not break the people who live on my body—and I will spend whatever remains of my patience helping you find it. Refuse, and I will take it at the full of the thirteenth moon, and we will all discover together what waking screaming means."

"And if I give it to you," Mara said slowly, "on your terms, gently—what do I get? Besides a valley full of remembered people and a land that isn't suffocating. What happens to the lock? To me?"

The god was quiet for a moment. When it spoke again, the wind in its voice had gone almost tender, which was somehow the most frightening thing yet.

"You are the last of the nameless," it said. "Your family poured themselves into the seal until nothing was left but you. If the seal dissolves and the name comes home—if the valley is named and seen and written onto maps again—then the debt of the nameless is paid, every ancestor redeemed. All except you. You were born outside the bargain, taster. Bought your name secondhand. There is nothing in me to redeem." The lights steadied on her. "Which means that when the valley becomes visible, you will become visible with it. Utterly. Permanently. Every eye will find you. Every prayer will reach you. The woman who spent her life unnoticed will spend eternity noticed, and there is no power in heaven or under the hills that can undo it. That is the price. That is always the price. Visibility cannot be taken halfway."

Mara laughed. It startled her, coming out of her, a real laugh, helpless and a little unhinged, echoing off the charred beams.

"Of course," she said. "Of course that's the price. I finally get a country, and the cost is that I can never again sneak into my own kitchen." She wiped her eyes. "My mother would say I got off lightly."

Outside the shrine, a horn sounded—two notes, sharp and wrong, the signal Wren's people used for riders. Mara turned, and through the fog she saw torchlight moving along the ridge above the border stones. Many torches. Moving in formation.

Vess's people drilled like that. Vess's people had stolen Pell's map. Vess's people believed the Door Out was salvation, and had never once wondered what was on the other side of any door, or who might be kept in by it.

"They're going for the Door," Mara breathed. "Tonight. While the moon's waxing. While everyone's watching me."

Behind her, the god rose to its full impossible height, antlers scraping the stars, and the pain in its voice curdled into something older and colder.

"Then we have a problem, taster," it said, "because the Door does not lead out of the valley. It leads into *me*. And if your Lady Corvane opens it with royal blood and a stolen map, she will not free anyone." The pale lights flared. "She will let the cage's hunger out—and the cage has been hungry for three hundred years."
