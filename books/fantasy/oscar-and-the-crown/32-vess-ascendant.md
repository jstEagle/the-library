## 32. Vess Ascendant

The god had a voice like honey poured over gravel, and it never stopped talking.

Vess Corvane had expected power to feel like silence—the clean hush of command, the world finally arranged to her liking. Instead it felt like company. The thing that had answered her prayer in the ruined shrine of Saint Aldery lived now behind her sternum, and it narrated. It commented on the weather. It critiqued her posture. It sang, sometimes, in a language made entirely of the sound rivers make under ice, and Vess had learned to hum along without meaning to.

*You walk too fast,* it observed as she crossed the palace courtyard at dawn, her boots striking frost off the flagstones. *Rulers stroll. Strolling says: I have all the time in the world, and also, all of you.*

"I'm not ruler yet," Vess murmured through her teeth. A scullery maid curtsied as she passed, and Vess nodded back with the precise warmth she'd practiced for thirty years at dinner tables full of people she intended to outlive.

*Semantics.* The god's amusement rippled through her ribs like swallowed wine. *You hold the palace guard's oath, the granary keys, and my favor. In this valley that is a coronation missing only the hat.*

"The crown," Vess said, "is on the head of a dead man's corpse, and everyone knows it."

That was the truth of the morning. King Aldous had been dead eleven days, and the palace—like everything in the Valley of the Unnamed—had simply declined to notice. His body lay in state in the Blue Room, propped and powdered, because no one could agree who had the authority to bury him and no one dared be the first to say aloud that authority had gone somewhere else. The cooks still sent up his meals. Mara Kavanagh, wherever she was hiding, was presumably still tasting them, which struck Vess as either the most loyal or the most absurd act in the history of service.

And the crown sat on the velvet cushion beside the dead king's folded hands, gold and patient, doing what crowns do best: waiting.

*It calls to me,* the god said, wistful. *Like a bone buried by someone else's dog.*

"You don't get the crown," Vess said. "We've discussed this."

*We have. I remain unpersuaded.*

"We've *discussed* this," Vess repeated, with the flat finality of a woman who had spent her girlhood being talked over by men and had since become very good at talking last.

The god subsided, sulking in a way that made her molars ache. That was the bargain's fine print, the part no shrine-fire warning had covered: a god inside you did not make you its instrument, exactly. It made you its *roommate*. It wanted things constantly, and it wanted them the way a child wants the moon—loudly, sincerely, and with total disregard for physics. Keeping it fed on small obediences while refusing it the large ones was a discipline Vess practiced hourly, the way other people practiced scales.

She had sold herself to it, yes. She would not pretend otherwise, even alone in her own skull. But she had sold herself *for something*, and the something was not the god's. It was the valley's. It was the Door.

---

By midmorning the throne room was full, because Vess had ordered it full, and because in the Valley of the Unnamed an order from Lady Vess Corvane had quietly become the only kind of order anyone still obeyed.

She stood on the dais—not on the throne itself; she was not a fool, and symbols mattered here more than anywhere—and looked out at the assembled remnant of a court. Captains with grey faces. Clerks clutching ledgers they no longer believed balanced. Two of Pell's junior archivists, pale as paper, who had fled the burning archive and now flinched at any raised voice. And near the back, arms crossed, old Cook Brenna from the kitchens, who had come uninvited and stood there radiating the specific disapproval of a woman who had fed three generations of this court and judged all of them wanting.

Good. Let the kitchens see. Let everyone see.

"People of the valley," Vess began, and then stopped, because the phrase was wrong and she knew it. She tried again. "Neighbors."

The word landed differently. She felt it land. Somewhere behind her breastbone, the god went quiet and attentive, the way it did when she surprised it.

"For two hundred years we have been a country that does not exist. Our names were taken before we were born. Our grandparents are unrecorded. Our dead are unmourned by anyone outside these hills, because outside these hills there is no one who has ever heard of us." She let that sit. She had rehearsed this speech eleven times, and the rehearsal had taught her that the pause was where the persuasion lived. "I grew up being told this was safety. I have read every surviving page of the founding treaty, and I am here to tell you it was not safety. It was a cage, and we were the birds who agreed to stop singing so the cat would go away."

A murmur. Old Captain Hesse shifted his weight. Someone near the windows whispered *blasphemy*, or possibly *finally*; the fog in the room made voices hard to place.

"The seal is failing. You have seen it. The thirteenth moon climbs whether we consent or not. The border stones crack a little more each week, and the fog leaks out, and soon—very soon—the world will look in at us whether we are ready or not. We cannot stop that. What we can decide is *how* it happens." She stepped forward, to the edge of the dais. "I have found the Door Out of this valley. I know where it stands. I know how it opens. And I am telling you, here, now: I will open it. For everyone. Not for the royal bloodline, not for whoever finds it first, not for the gods circling our borders like gulls after a fishing boat. For *everyone*."

Silence. Then Cook Brenna, from the back, in a voice like a dropped pot: "At what price?"

Vess had known the question would come, and had prepared three answers, ranked by honesty. She chose the third.

"A tithe," she said. "Those who pass through the Door will owe a portion of their luck to the powers that make passage possible. One year in ten. Your harvests will run thin those years. Your children will skin their knees. Nothing more than that." *Nothing more than that,* the god echoed inside her, sly, and she pressed down on it like a hand on a lid. "Compare that to the price already being paid. Ask the people whose names are going missing. Ask the shrines that burned. Ask Wren of Millbrook—" she saw the girl wasn't there, and adjusted smoothly, "—who learned what the new gods' gifts cost when they came collecting. I am offering the only exit that doesn't end with us eaten."

"Or," said Captain Hesse slowly, "you're offering us to be eaten *by you*."

The room held its breath. Vess let hers out gently.

"Captain," she said, "I have served this court for twenty-two years. I have poured wine for men who signed away our grandchildren's names over dessert. If I wanted to devour this valley, I promise you I would have started with the parts that talk back." A ripple of uneasy laughter—good, laughter was a door opening. "I am not your queen. I am not asking to be. I am asking to be your *regent* until the Door opens and the valley can choose its own government in a world that remembers it exists. That is all. That is everything."

Brenna spat—ceremonially, toward the floor, an old kitchen verdict—and then, to Vess's genuine astonishment, jerked her chin in the smallest possible nod. *Provisionally. Against my better judgment. Get on with it.*

The rest of the room followed the cook, because in palaces as in kitchens, everyone follows the person who feeds them.

By noon it was done. By evening the proclamation was nailed to every door in the palace and carried by riders to every village: *In the absence of royal succession, and until the Door is opened, the Valley of the Unnamed shall be governed by Vess Corvane, Regent.*

The god purred so loudly she could feel it in her teeth.

*Regent,* it said, tasting the word. *A queen who has promised not to be one. My favorite kind of lie—the kind the liar believes.*

"It isn't a lie," Vess said, alone in the regent's study—which had been the king's study, which had been, according to Pell's oldest records, a shrine once, back when the valley still had gods it liked.

*No?*

"When the Door is open and the valley is safe, I will step down. I swear it on my name." She paused. "What's left of it."

The god laughed, and the candles guttered, and somewhere far below them, in the root cellars where fugitives hid among carvings older than the treaty, Vess imagined she could feel Mara Kavanagh flinch.

---

Here was the thing Vess did not say in speeches, the thing she barely admitted in the privacy of her own increasingly crowded skull:

She had not always wanted to save the valley. There had been years—she could count them, seven, between her father's disgrace and her mother's death—when what she had wanted was simply to *matter*. To walk into a room and have the room change temperature. The Valley of the Unnamed was a machine for preventing exactly that; it took ambition and ground it into dust as reliably as the mill ground barley. Every clever child she had grown up with had either left in their hearts long before their bodies or shrunk themselves down to fit the space allotted, which was none.

Vess had refused both options, and the refusal had cost her everything soft. She knew that. Some nights, running her thumb along the scar on her palm where she'd cut herself at nine years old swearing an oath over her father's confiscated ledger, she could almost mourn the girl she'd been before—the one who'd cried at the death of a hedgehog, who'd thought her mother's laugh was the finest music in the world.

But mourning was a luxury of people with futures, and Vess had spent thirty years buying hers installment by installment.

Now the installments were coming due in stranger currency. Her reflection had begun to lag—a half-second behind her movements, sometimes a full second, watching her with eyes that were hers but *arranged* differently, the pupils vertical as a goat's when it thought she wasn't looking. Her shadow moved correctly but cast slightly the wrong direction, toward the light instead of away. And twice now, waking, she had found frost on the *inside* of her window, spelled out in no alphabet she knew, though she suspected—with the weary certainty of a woman reading her own handwriting—that if she ever bothered to learn the god's language, she would find her own signature in it.

*You are becoming magnificent,* the god told her that night, watching her dress for the second proclamation ceremony with proprietary admiration. *When you are finished, they will build shrines.*

"They'll build archives," Vess said, pinning her collar. "Shrines are for gods. Archives are for people who did things."

*And which will you be?*

"Neither, if I do this right. The whole point of a regent is to be forgotten on schedule." She met her own lagging eyes in the mirror. "Open the Door. Hand over the keys. Retire to a house with a good library and terrible gossip. That's the entire ambition."

*Liar,* the god said fondly.

"Strategist," Vess corrected. "There's a difference. A liar deceives others. A strategist occasionally has to deceive herself to keep functioning, and knows precisely when she's doing it."

The god considered this. *I am going to enjoy the moment you can't tell anymore.*

"So am I," Vess said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it, and pinned the last clasp with a steady hand.

---

The trouble arrived on the third day of her regency, in the form of a kitchen boy with old hands.

Oscar walked into the great hall past two guards who should have stopped him and didn't—Vess noted that, filed it, worried about it later—and set a loaf of bread on the long table in front of her with the gravity of an ambassador presenting credentials.

"Brenna says you haven't eaten since the coronation-that-wasn't," he said. "She says tyrants who skip meals die stupid, and she'd take it personally."

"I'm not a tyrant."

"That's exactly what a tyrant would say." He didn't smile. That was the wrongness, Vess realized. The boy had been all crooked grins and stolen apples when she'd watched him from a distance over the years; every servant's child in the palace had adored him. Now he stood there with hands that looked like they belonged to a fifty-year-old stonemason, and his eyes had the flat shine of someone who had recently paid a bill he hadn't understood.

"What happened to your hands?" she asked, because she was a strategist, and strategists collect information even when it hurts to hold.

Oscar flexed his fingers, wincing. "Toll," he said shortly. "Roads out of this valley charge strange fares lately." He turned to go, then stopped. "Mara wants you to know she heard the proclamation. All of it."

Vess kept her face pleasant and her pulse level. "And?"

"And she says you're half right." He looked at her then, really looked, and there was something in his expression she couldn't parse—pity, maybe, or its cousin. "The valley does need to be remembered. But you're opening the wrong door, Lady Corvane. Or rather—you're opening the right door for the wrong reason, and the thing on the other side of it isn't a road out. It's a throat."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a *warning*," Oscar said. "Threats are for people who matter to each other." And he walked out past the guards who should have stopped him, leaving the bread steaming gently on the table, smelling of rosemary and woodsmoke and home.

Vess sat for a long time without touching it.

*He's lying,* the god said, but it said it too quickly, the way it did when it was frightened, and Vess—who had spent thirty years learning to hear what powerful things said underneath what they said—heard it clearly.

"No," she said softly. "He's telling the truth. That's what frightens you."

She ate the bread anyway. It was excellent. Brenna had never made anything else.

And in the study afterward, alone with her lagging reflection and her vertical-eyed shadow, Vess Corvane drew the stolen map across her desk, weighted its corners with candlesticks, and began, with the patience of a woman assembling a trap she genuinely believed was a rescue, to plan the march to the cliff door.

Three moons left. One regent. One key she no longer controlled, walking around the valley in the body of a food-taster who had crowned herself in a kitchen.

*Find her,* the god whispered, hungry, hopeful, certain.

"I intend to," said Vess—and for the first time since the prayer, she could not quite tell which of them had spoken.
