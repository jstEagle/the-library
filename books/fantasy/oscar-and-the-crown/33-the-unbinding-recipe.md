## 33. The Unbinding Recipe

The recipe book smelled of thyme and smoke and thirty years of kitchen fires, and Mara had been avoiding it for two days.

She told herself it was because there were more urgent things to do. There were always more urgent things to do now. Pell's bandages needed changing twice a day, and he complained about the linen the way other men complained about the weather, which was how she knew he would live. Wren had taken over organizing the holdout villagers in the root cellars with the brisk efficiency of someone who had spent years being carried everywhere and intended never to be useless again. Oscar sat by the lamp in the evenings flexing his old-man hands and pretending not to be in pain, which fooled no one, least of all the cooks, who had begun slipping honey into his tea without being asked.

All of that was urgent. None of it required her mother's recipe book.

But on the third night, when the root cellars went quiet and the only sound was the distant, wrong-sweet hum of the cracked border stones, Mara climbed the narrow stair to the old taster's quarters, lit a candle, and opened the book.

It was exactly as she remembered it from childhood: fat, water-stained, its spine held together with string and stubbornness. Her mother's handwriting marched across the pages in tight, slanted rows — *hen stock, skim TWICE, do not let Bram near the salt* — annotated in three colors of ink, because her mother had believed that poison and antidote were simply two entries in the same ledger.

Mara turned pages slowly. Here was the antidote for bitterleaf, scrawled beside the recipe for bitterleaf stew itself, because her mother had never seen the point of separating a danger from its cure. Here was a remedy for adder venom that involved an alarming quantity of goat's milk and one specific prayer to no god in particular — just a muttered phrase, "for the milk to remember what health is," which had seemed like kitchen superstition when Mara was nine and looked very different now that prayers had started working again.

Her throat tightened. She kept turning pages.

*For the king's cough.* *For fevers of the sealed sort* — sealed sort, as if her mother had known even then that some sicknesses came through cracks in the world rather than cracks in the skin. *For grief, take honey, warm milk, and time; the first two are optional.*

Despite everything, Mara laughed. It came out wet, but it was a laugh.

Then, near the back, past the preserves and the poultices, past a page headed *things I hope you never need*, she found it.

The last entry. The ink was different — darker, steadier, written by a hand that had made up its mind. Her mother had titled it the way she titled every recipe, plainly, without flourish:

**Unbinding.**

---

*Serves: one.*

Mara stared at those two words for a long moment. Of course. Of course her mother had written it like a recipe. That was how she had armed her daughter against the whole world — not with warnings, but with instructions. Not *never do this*, but *here is how it is done, so that if you must, you will do it properly*.

The ingredients list was short, which somehow made it worse.

*One crown, rightly held.*
*One nameless mouth, royal by blood, trained by poison.*
*One name of the land, freely given by its keeper.*
*Courage. (Do not substitute.)*

Beneath it, the method:

*A name is not a word. A name is a weight. The valley's true name has been held outside it for four hundred years, and a weight held too long does not vanish — it looks for somewhere to rest. The seal is the resting place. Break the seal wrongly and the name falls on the valley like a stone falls on an egg.*

*Therefore it must be swallowed. As you swallow everything else. Let it pass through the one mouth in this country built to take poison and survive it. Speak it from inside yourself, where it can be tasted before it is trusted.*

*Terms, so there is no confusion later:*

*You will taste it. All of it. Every year of its imprisonment, every prayer it never answered, every name your ancestors traded away — you will know them the way you know arsenic on the tongue, instantly and completely, and you will not be able to spit it out.*

*You will become visible. Permanently. To everyone. To everything. Whatever bargain your bloodline struck with obscurity, it ends in your throat. Afterward, no door will fail to find you, no eye will slide off you, no prayer will miss you. You will spend the rest of your life being seen, and you will never again have a single moment that belongs only to you.*

*And you may die. I have watched you survive things that should have killed you since you were four years old, and I still cannot promise this. A taster's training is a wall against small poisons. This is not a small poison. It is the largest one we have.*

*If you read this, then it has come to the last course, and I am sorry, and I am proud, and both of those things are true at once, which is the whole trick of being a mother.*

*— Serve warm. Do not serve cold. Nothing important was ever done cold.*

Mara read it three times.

On the third reading, she noticed what she'd missed: faint pinpricks along the margin, where her mother had pricked the page with a tasting needle — the same needle Mara still carried on a cord around her neck. Prick by prick, a pattern. She held the page up to the candlelight and tilted it, and the pinholes caught the flame.

They spelled a single word, pressed into the paper the way her mother had pressed everything: quietly, permanently, whether or not anyone ever looked.

*MINE.*

Not *yours*. Not *ours*. *Mine* — meaning: this burden was mine, and I am handing it to you the way I handed you everything, deliberately, with the antidote written alongside.

Or possibly meaning something kinder. With her mother it had always been impossible to separate the armor from the affection. Mara had spent her entire childhood swallowing things that hurt her because her mother loved her enough to want her untouchable. Perhaps this was the same. Perhaps love, in the Kavanagh family, had only ever been expressed in doses.

She set the book down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

* * *

"You've been up here three hours." Oscar's voice floated up the stairwell, followed by Oscar himself, carrying a plate. "I brought you supper. Don't argue. Cook said if you skip another meal she'll come up herself, and she's angrier than the god."

"Nothing is angrier than the god."

"Cook comes close. She's been praying again, did you know? Little ones. *Let the bread rise, let the bread rise.* And it rises. First time in months the bread's been decent." He set the plate down — stew, good and plain — and peered at the open book with the frank curiosity of a boy who had never learned manners and had recently decided that was a feature. "What is it?"

"A recipe."

"For what?"

"The end of the world."

"Oh." He considered this. "Is it complicated?"

"Oscar."

"Well, Cook says any recipe worth doing is simple and any recipe that's complicated is showing off." He pulled up the second stool, uninvited, the way he always did. His hands, resting on his knees, were an old woman's hands on a boy's arms, spotted and stiff, and he caught her looking and tucked them under his arms with as much dignity as he could manage. "It doesn't hurt," he lied.

"I know it hurts."

"It hurts less when people don't make faces about it." He nodded at the book. "Read it to me? Not the scary parts. Just — the shape of it. Sometimes when Pell explains things they get smaller once they're said out loud. Except taxes. Taxes get bigger."

So Mara read him the ingredients list. Crown, mouth, name, courage. He listened with his head tipped, the way he listened to anything, as though the words might change their minds mid-sentence.

"That's you," he said. "Crown, mouth, courage. You're three-quarters of a recipe already. Very efficient."

"And the fourth part is a name I have to take from a god who wants me dead."

"Wants its *name* dead — no. Wants its name *back*. Those are different appetites." He frowned at his hidden hands. "I've met its appetite. Up close. At the border stone." He was quiet a moment. "It wasn't cruel, Mara. That's the worst part. It was patient. Like winter. Winter isn't cruel either, it just doesn't care whether you've got a coat."

"Your hands say otherwise."

"My hands say I bargained badly, which is a lesson about me, not about it." He looked up, and in the candlelight his face was suddenly older than his hands. "What are the terms? Really. Pell says magic always lists the price in small print. What's the small print?"

Mara hesitated. Then, because he had paid ten years of his hands for her already, she told him. The tasting. The visibility. The death clause.

He didn't flinch at the last one, which she'd expected. He flinched at the second.

"*Never* invisible again?" he said. "Not ever? Mara — you *built* yourself out of not being seen. I've watched you stand in a doorway for ten minutes waiting for the room to forget you were in it. That's not a price, that's — " he groped for the word — "that's asking a fish to pay in water."

"Thank you, Oscar. Very comforting."

"I'm not trying to comfort you. I'm trying to be accurate, which is better." He chewed on it. Then, carefully: "Would it help if I said I'd look at you anyway? After. When everyone's looking. I'd still be looking *first*."

Something in Mara's chest went sideways. "That is the strangest kindness anyone has ever offered me."

"I've been practicing strange. It's going well." He stood, took the empty plate, then paused at the top of the stairs. "Cook also said — and I quote — *tell her the last course is always the cook's decision, not the guest's.* I don't know what it means. But she said it while sharpening a knife, so I wrote it down verbatim."

* * *

Pell received the news of the recipe propped up on his cot among the archives, one arm splinted, the other holding a pen because the man appeared constitutionally incapable of holding nothing. He listened to the terms with his eyes closed, and when Mara finished, he was silent long enough that she thought he'd fallen asleep.

"No," he said finally.

"Pell—"

"No. Listen to me. I have spent my life reading bargains, and this one has a flaw, and the flaw is *you*." He opened his eyes, fever-bright and furious. "A crowned nameless taster. Fine. But crowned *how*? The king died without declaring an heir. The crown passed to you by blood and proximity, yes, but there was no coronation rite — we did it in a kitchen with a soup ladle for a scepter, God forgive us both. Vess will argue the crown was never rightly held. The *god* will argue it. Terms like these live and die on technicalities, Mara, and right now our technicality is a pot of soup."

"Then we make it right."

"With what rite? The coronation rites are in the palace chapel, which Vess holds, guarded by men who answer to a half-eaten regent." He struggled upright, wincing. "Unless — no. No, wait. Wait." His pen began moving before his mouth caught up. "The founding treaty. The original. The parts I didn't burn." He said it flatly, the shame worn smooth now, an old stone in him. "The bargain was sworn in the old tongue, in the presence of the land itself. If the crown is to be *rightly held*, the swearing must match the founding form. And the founding form doesn't require a chapel. It requires witnesses, the crown, and the ground."

"The ground," Mara repeated.

"The valley is the injured party. Four hundred years caged. Any oath that binds it must be sworn *on* it, *to* it, within earshot of its stones." He looked up, and beneath the scholar's dread there was something almost like awe. "Mara. The treaty names the place. The place where the ancestors knelt. It's in the burned section, but I burned it, which means I *read* it, which means — give me a moment, it's been fifty years — "

He closed his eyes. His lips moved. Mara waited, hardly breathing, while an old man excavated his own guilt for treasure.

"The Hollow Stone," he said. "At the cliff. Beneath the Door Out. They swore the bargain in the shadow of the very door they closed." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "Of course they did. Arrogant, frightened, magnificent people. They wanted the land to watch what they did to it."

"So I swear the crown at the cliff," Mara said slowly, "and then I swallow the name at the door beside it. One trip. One night."

"One night. The thirteenth moon." Pell's face sobered. "Which Vess also knows is coming, and which her patron will not permit to arrive unattended. You will be walking into the one place in this valley where every hungry thing converges."

"Yes," Mara said.

"You're supposed to argue."

"I'm a taster, Pell. I've spent my life being handed terrible things and expected to keep my face pleasant." She picked up the recipe book. "Besides — Cook says the last course is the cook's decision. And my mother left instructions."

* * *

That night she read the recipe one final time, aloud, alone, the way her mother had taught her to test any instruction: *say it back before you commit it, so you know whose voice is really choosing.*

*Crown, rightly held. Mouth, nameless and royal. Name, freely given. Courage, unsubstituted.*

Four ingredients. She had three. The fourth belonged to an antlered prisoner at the edge of the world, who had spent four hundred years waiting for someone to walk to the border and ask.

*Serve warm,* her mother had written. *Nothing important was ever done cold.*

Mara blew out the candle. In the dark, the valley hummed its wrong sweet note through the stones, and somewhere above, the thirteenth moon — nearly full now, obscene, a coin someone had slipped into heaven's purse — poured its light down through the fog.

Two nights, Pell had said. Two nights until it filled.

She laid the tasting needle on the open page, across the pinned word *MINE*, and began packing for the last course.
