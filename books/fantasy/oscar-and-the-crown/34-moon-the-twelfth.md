## 34. Moon the Twelfth

The moon rose fat and almost finished, hanging over the Valley of the Unnamed like a coin someone had bitten and was about to spend.

In the root cellars beneath the palace, the fugitives had made a kind of home. It was not a comfortable one — the tunnels were older than the valley itself, carved by hands no archive could name, and they smelled of cold earth and older stone — but there was a fire in a brazier, a kettle that Pell refused to let anyone else touch on principle, and enough blankets to go around if nobody asked where the extra ones had come from.

Tomorrow night, the thirteenth moon would rise full. Tonight was for putting things in order.

Oscar went first.

* * *

He did it alone, because he thought it ought to be done alone, which was exactly the sort of decision Oscar had been making all his life and which everyone who loved him had learned to work around rather than argue with.

He climbed out through the cellar's side passage — the one Wren had found, the one that came up behind the goat shed, because of course it did; the ancestors had built their escape routes with an eye for livestock — and stood in the frost-bitten grass under the penultimate moon.

The luck sat in him like a swallowed coal. He had carried it since the border stone, since the antlered figure had leaned down out of the dark and whispered a name into his ear that only he could hear. It had paid for small miracles ever since: doors unlocked at a touch, arrows gone wide, a fall from the cliff path that should have broken his neck ending instead in a bramble bush soft as a bed. And it had taken its price too. His hands were old men's hands now, knotted and spotted, hands that had aged ten years in ten weeks.

"Right," Oscar said to the moon. "I'd like to give it back."

Nothing happened. The moon went on being enormous and nearly done.

"I know you're listening," he said. "You've been listening since I was nine years old and stupid over a dying goat. So listen now. I don't want it anymore."

The wind moved through the grass. Somewhere down the valley, a shrine bell rang once and stopped, as if embarrassed.

"I'm not ungrateful," Oscar said, and this was true, and saying it made his throat hurt. "You saved my life more times than I can count. But every time I use it, I feel like I'm spending something that isn't mine. And tomorrow—" He stopped. Started again. "Tomorrow Mara has to swallow a name whole, and I'll be damned — properly damned, the outside-world kind — if I'm standing next to her holding borrowed magic when she does it. She shouldn't have to wonder what I am while she's deciding what we all become."

The frost crackled. The grass bent.

And then, gently, the way a parent takes a heavy thing out of a sleeping child's arms, the luck left him.

It didn't hurt. That was the strange part. He had expected tearing, debt called due, some grand cosmic invoice. Instead it simply lifted out of him like a bird off a fence post, and the night felt suddenly, enormously ordinary — just cold air and wet grass and a moon doing what moons do.

His hands stayed old. He had known they would. Luck spent is luck spent; even a freed god does not run a refund counter.

But standing there in the dark with nothing in him but himself, Oscar found that his own self was heavier than he remembered, and warmer, and entirely sufficient.

"There," he said, to no one, to everyone. "That's mine. All of it's mine. Even the bad bits."

The grass rustled — approval or amusement, impossible to say — and Oscar went back down into the earth to tell the others, and to be told off for going alone, and to find that being told off felt wonderful.

* * *

Wren spent the evening doing the thing she was best at, which was talking to people until they stopped being afraid.

She did it in the great kitchen of the palace, which had become the holdouts' headquarters by a process nobody could quite reconstruct — something about the ovens, something about the fact that Lady Vess's people considered kitchens beneath their notice, and something, mostly, about the fact that Wren had declared it so and Wren had a limp she had earned walking away from a miracle, and nobody argued with that.

They came in twos and threes. Farmers with scythes they hadn't sharpened because sharpening them would mean admitting what they might be for. Shrine-keepers whose shrines had burned. A miller who had lost his name three days ago and answered now to "hey, you," and who wept quietly into his sleeve until Wren sat beside him and said, "I'll remember it for you. Tell me again. I've got room."

By midnight there were forty of them in the kitchen, sitting on flour sacks and chopping blocks, and Wren climbed up onto the big preparation table because she was five feet nothing and needed the height.

"Here's the thing," she said, in the voice she used for hard truths, which was the same voice she used for asking after people's mothers, warm and level and not to be argued with. "Tomorrow night, either Mara Kavanagh gives this valley its name back, or the thirteenth moon takes everything the slow way — our names first, then our faces from each other's memories, then, eventually, us. Those are the options. I've checked. I've checked very thoroughly, and I hate both of them, so I understand if you hate them too."

Someone laughed, shakily. Good. Laughter was load-bearing.

"But here's the other thing." She looked around at their faces, forty faces, and made herself see each one. "Whatever happens tomorrow — win, lose, or something nobody's thought of yet, which given our track record is honestly the most likely option — you are not invisible. You hear me? We have spent four hundred years being erased, and I know some of you got comfortable with it, and I did too, and that's not shameful. Comfort never is. But tomorrow the world looks in, ready or not. And when it does, I want it to see forty people who chose to stand in a kitchen and be counted. Not victims. Not miracles. People. Stubborn, flour-dusted, terrified people."

Silence. Then the miller — hey-you, nameless, weeping earlier — raised his cup of chicory coffee and said, "To stubborn, flour-dusted, terrified people," and forty cups came up, and Wren got something in her eye that she blamed on the oven smoke, and nobody believed her, and nobody said so.

Afterward, an old woman caught her elbow at the door. "Your leg," the woman said. "The gods fixed it once. If you fight them tomorrow—"

"They gave it back," Wren said. "Which means it was always theirs to take. My leg isn't a gift. It's a loan." She smiled, and it was mostly a real smile. "I've decided what I'm paying it toward."

* * *

Pell dictated his testament by candlelight, because he was a scholar to the marrow and a scholar's last act is always paperwork.

Mara held the candle. Wren had offered, but Pell had said, with the particular firmness of a man arranging his own affairs, "No. It should be the taster. You have the steadiest hands in the valley, and I intend to be difficult about details."

"You're not dying," Mara said, for perhaps the ninth time.

"I am seventy-one years old, I took a mace blow to the ribs shielding the archives, and I have a punctured lung that whistles when I laugh, which is why I have stopped laughing, which you will agree is the greater tragedy. I am dying slowly, which is the best kind, and I intend to use the time efficiently. Page twelve. Where was I?"

"The library catalogue."

"The library catalogue. Item: the complete works of Hessel the Younger, including the marginalia, which are worth more than the works. To be moved to high ground before tomorrow night, because whatever else happens, water finds books, and I will haunt whoever lets the founding treaty drown. Write that down. 'I will haunt.' Underlined."

Mara wrote it down. Underlined it.

Pell lay propped against his rolled cloak in the corner of the tunnel he had claimed weeks ago and decorated, in defiance of all sense, with three framed engravings of library shelving systems. His breathing was a thin whistle now, in and out, a kettle that would never quite boil.

"Item," he continued. "My notes on the Plague of Names. Four hundred and six names recorded, verified, and preserved. When the valley is named — if it is named — these are to be read aloud, one by one, in the market square, so that the people get them back in order. Alphabetical. Yes, I know it will take all day. Yes, I know that is sentimental. Sentiment is merely scholarship applied to persons, and anyone who disagrees may take the matter up with my ghost, who will be busy haunting a librarian."

He paused. The candle guttered. Outside, far above, the moonlight must have been coming through the cracks in the world, because the shadows in the tunnel shifted in a way that had nothing to do with flame.

"Mara," he said, and his voice changed, dropped its lecturing cadence, became simply an old man's voice. "There is one more item. Off the record."

"Everything's off the record. I'm the only one writing."

"Then it's doubly off the record." He looked at her, and his eyes were still sharp, still archivist-sharp, seeing the structure beneath the surface of things. "Forty years ago I burned pages of the founding treaty. You know this. What you don't know is why, and you deserve to know why before tomorrow, because tomorrow you walk up to that door carrying everything, and you should not carry a secret that isn't yours."

Mara set down the pen.

"The pages I burned listed the volunteers," Pell said. "The ancestors who gave up their names willingly. Your family among them. I burned them because your grandmother asked me to. She was alive then, sharp as a tack, ninety years old, and she came to me and said: 'If the young ones knew it was a choice, they would wonder why their family chose chains. Let them think it was a cage. Cages make people want out. Choices make them wonder if they were wrong to stay.'" He exhaled, long and whistling. "I have regretted it every day since, and obeyed her every day since, and I am telling you now because you are about to make the same choice she made, and I will not have you making it blind. It was a choice, Mara. It was always a choice. Which means tomorrow is one too."

The tunnel was quiet. Somewhere above, the palace settled and creaked like an old ship.

"Thank you," Mara said finally.

"Don't thank me. Haunt me. It's in the testament." He tapped the page. "Now. Item: my collection of border-stone rubbings, 181 specimens, to be stored flat—"

* * *

They gathered near dawn, all of them, in the main tunnel where the oldest carvings ran along the walls — the bargain, the names surrendered, the door drawn shut by patient ancestral hands. Nobody slept much. There was tea. There was bread Wren's people had baked, and cheese that Vess's abandoned stores had yielded, and Oscar cut it all with his old man's hands and refused help and was allowed, grudgingly, to refuse.

"So," Wren said, into the quiet. "Separate peaces. Who's got one? I'll start. Mine is: if this goes badly, somebody make sure the miller gets his name read first. He's suffered longest."

"Noted," Mara said. "Alphabetically he's somewhere in the M's, but I'll exercise royal privilege."

"You're not crowned yet."

"I'm crowned in the kitchen. Ask the cooks. They knelt and everything. There was a colander involved."

"It was a very dignified colander," Oscar said solemnly.

Pell wheezed — his version of laughter, rationed now like everything else. "Item," he managed. "My peace: I have read every book in the valley twice, and I regret nothing except the marginalia I skipped in my youth because I thought it frivolous. Frivolity, children, is where scholars hide their hearts. Check the margins. Always check the margins."

Oscar turned his cup in his ruined hands. "Mine's short," he said. "I gave the luck back tonight. Before anyone asks — yes, alone, yes, I know, yes, Wren, you can yell at me later. But I wanted to say it here, in front of everybody, so it counts. Whatever I am tomorrow, I'm it on purpose. No borrowings. Just me." He looked up, and grinned, and it was the same grin from the goat shed, from the cliff path, from every scrape and rescue. "Turns out that's plenty."

Wren reached over and squeezed one of those old hands, gently, like it was something precious, which it was.

They all looked at Mara then. She had been hoping they wouldn't. She had her mother's recipe book open on her knees, at the last page, at the recipe written in her mother's hand: *Unbinding. Serves one. Requires a crowned nameless taster willing to swallow the land's name like poison.*

"My peace," she said, "is that I've tasted everything this valley can throw at me since I was six years old. Nightshade. Hemlock. My mother's cooking, which was worse. And tomorrow I taste a god's own name, and I already know how it goes — you hold it on your tongue, you wait, you see what it does to you, and then you decide whether to swallow." She closed the book. "I've decided. I decided a long time ago, probably in a kitchen, probably holding a spoon. That's my peace. I'm not afraid of the flavor."

"That's the most horrifying romantic thing I've ever heard," Wren said.

"Thank you."

Outside, far above, the false dawn was coming gray and cold, and the thirteenth moon was setting, huge and nearly drained, sinking toward the mountains like something taking a last look before it came back to finish the job.

And it was then that the tunnel shook — once, softly, the way a house shakes when something very large sets its foot down very carefully nearby — and from the mouth of the passage behind them came a light that was not dawn, gold-green and moving, and a smell of summer meadows that had no business existing in the twelfth month.

Wren stood first. "What is that?"

From the darkness at the tunnel's end, deliberate and unhurried, came the sound of hooves on ancient stone.

Antlers appeared first, branching up into the shadowed roof, hung with a few late leaves that gleamed like struck coins. Then the rest of it: the guardian stag, the god grown gentle, its coat dappled with what looked like starlight, its breath steaming sweet as hay.

It looked at each of them in turn — the scholar, the girl with the loaned leg, the boy with empty hands — and last and longest at Mara, the crowned nameless taster with the book on her knees.

Then it lowered its great head, and set something down on the tunnel floor between them, and stepped back.

It was a key. Old iron, simple as a farm gate's, and warm.

"It's early," Mara breathed.

"No," said the stag, in a voice like wind through a whole forest at once. "*You* are. The moon is not the only clock, little taster. She is coming — the hungry one, wearing your Lady Corvane like a glove. She means to reach the door tonight, before the turn, while the lock is still half shut and easier to break." The antlered head swung toward the cliff-side of the valley, and its eyes were old grief and older patience. "So we go now. All of us. The storm has decided not to wait for morning — and neither, I think, have you."

Mara closed her mother's book, and stood, and found that her hands were steady.

"Well," she said. "We did want to skip the waiting part."

Nobody laughed. But everybody stood up.
