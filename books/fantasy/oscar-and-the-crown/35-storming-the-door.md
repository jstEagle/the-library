## 35. Storming the Door

The thirteenth moon rose like a coin someone had bitten and found wanting.

Mara felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her teeth, a taste in her mouth like copper and rain. She had spent her whole life tasting things for other people: poison for the king, salt for the cooks, fear for herself. This was different. The air itself had turned edible, and everything in it was going off.

"Left," Wren hissed beside her, gripping her crutch like a weapon, which tonight it was. "Vess's men are holding the orchard road. We go through the cider press."

"The cider press," Mara repeated.

"It's dark, it's narrow, and it smells so strongly of rotting apples that no one will want to follow us."

"That," said Mara, "is the most sensible military strategy I have ever heard."

They went over the wall behind the tannery—Oscar first, hauling himself up with hands that looked sixty years older than the rest of him, then Pell, who climbed the way an injured scholar climbs, which is to say badly, loudly, and with commentary. Wren went last, boosting Mara from below because her leg, healed by a god's gift and hardened by a valley's loyalty, was stronger than any of theirs.

From the top of the wall, Mara saw the whole valley laid out under the fat moon, and for one breath she almost wept. It was so small. That was the thing no map could have shown them, even if maps had been allowed to show it: the Valley of the Unnamed was barely eight miles end to end. A pocket country. A handkerchief of fog and barley. And it was hers—not by crown, not by blood exactly, but by the simple fact that she knew where every kitchen was.

Below, torches moved along the orchard road. Vess's mercenaries, thirty at least, marching toward the cliff door with the stolen map and the certainty of people who believe they are saving the world.

"They'll reach the door an hour ahead of us," Oscar said quietly.

"Then we'd better be faster than an hour."

"Mara." He caught her sleeve. His old-man hands were steady. "There are thirty of them and four of us, and one of us is a librarian."

"I'm aware of our numbers, Oscar."

"And you're wearing your apron."

Mara looked down. In the rush out of the root cellars she had, without thinking, buckled on her taster's apron—the long grey one with the deep pockets, the one she'd worn every morning for eleven years to stand between a king and his breakfast. She had half expected to feel foolish. Instead she felt armed.

"Yes," she said. "I am. Every tool of my trade is in these pockets. Come on."

* * *

The cider press smelled exactly as advertised.

They went through it bent double, single file, in a darkness so complete that Mara navigated by touch and memory—the press beam overhead, the sticky floor, the drain channel worn smooth by two hundred years of spilled autumn. Somewhere behind them, boots splashed along the orchard road, and a voice called orders in the clipped accent of the lowland sellswords Vess had bought with promises rather than coin.

Pell, wheezing: "I would like it noted that I have read about sieges extensively."

"Noted," said Wren.

"In none of them did anyone go through a cider press."

"Consider yourself a pioneer."

Mara smiled despite everything. That was the strange arithmetic of terror, she thought—it made small jokes enormous. She filed the feeling away the way she filed all flavors, and kept moving.

They came out through the press's far door into the goat pasture, and there the night changed.

The moon was full now, wholly full, and it was wrong. Not larger, exactly, but *closer*, hanging over the eastern ridge like a lamp brought down to table height. Under its light the fog that ringed the valley's borders had begun to glow, a pale gold, like broth. And everywhere across the pasture, the grass was moving against the wind—leaning toward the cliff, toward the Door Out, the way flowers lean toward sun.

"It knows we're coming," Oscar whispered.

"No," said Pell, and his voice had gone thin and scholarly and afraid. "It knows *itself* is coming. The land's name has been caged in that cliff since before any of us. Tonight the cage opens whether or not anyone turns the key. Everything alive can feel it. Look—" He pointed. The goats were standing in rows, silent, facing east. "Even the animals are witnesses."

"Then let's not keep the court waiting," Mara said.

* * *

They nearly made it.

The cliff path was a goat track, switchbacking up the eastern face, and they took it at a run—Wren astonishing them all, her healed leg eating the slope three steps at a time; Pell clutching his satchel of salvaged treaty pages to his chest like a child; Oscar ahead, reading the path by some borrowed instinct; and Mara in the middle, apron pockets bouncing, counting her tools as she ran. Knife. Vial of poppy syrup. Vial of her mother's final recipe, copied out in her own careful hand. A spoon. She had brought a spoon, because she was who she was, and if she was going to do the most important thing ever done in the Valley of the Unnamed, she was damned well going to do it with the instrument she trusted.

Halfway up, the torches reached the base of the cliff below them.

"There!" A shout, thin with distance. "On the path! The Kavanagh woman!"

And the arrows began.

Not many—the sellswords were shooting upward at night at running targets—but enough. One struck the rock beside Pell's head and burst into sparks that were not sparks, that hung in the air glowing gold like the fog, because the world's rules were already fraying. Another took Oscar in the shoulder and he went down without a sound, which frightened Mara more than screaming would have.

She was on her knees beside him before she remembered deciding to kneel. Taster's hands, quick and sure, finding the shaft, judging the angle. Shallow. Shoulder muscle. She'd dressed this wound a hundred times on hunters.

"Look at me. Bite this." She pressed a strip of leather between his teeth, then poured poppy syrup past his lips from a vial, thumb steadying his jaw. Her hands did not shake. Eleven years of standing between a king and death had taught them not to. "You'll live. You'll live and you'll complain about it, which is how I'll know."

"Mara," he slurred, eyes already glazing, "your apron has a spoon in it."

"I know what my apron has in it."

"Good. Because—" His gaze slid past her, up the path, and went wide. "Because they're here."

* * *

Six of them. Vess's vanguard, sent up the track ahead of the main force, and they came fast and professional, blades out, faces blank beneath the impossible golden moonlight. Wren stepped in front of Mara with her crutch raised like a quarterstaff, and Pell—Pell, seventy years old and built like a bundle of correspondence—planted himself on her other side and held up, of all things, a treaty page.

"By the terms of the Founding Accord," he announced, voice cracking but carrying, "violence within sight of the Door is sacrilege—"

A mercenary hit him with the flat of a sword and he sat down hard among the rocks, pages scattering.

What happened next, Mara would remember forever in pieces, the way she remembered tastes: sharp, separate, unmixable.

Wren's crutch whistling and connecting. The mercenary's surprise—he had expected a lame girl, and gods' gifts are heavier than they look. Another man grabbing for Mara's arm and finding instead that eleven years of lifting cauldrons builds a grip, and that a taster who has survived her mother's training does not freeze when grabbed; she turns *with* the grab and puts her shoulder into it, and eighty pounds of sellsword goes over the path's edge into the dark. The third man raising his blade toward her, and Mara reaching into her apron pocket with the calm of long habit—

—and throwing the contents at his face.

It was the powdered mustard-root she kept for masking bitterness in the king's medicine. It was not a weapon. It was, however, an extremely thorough education in what a face feels like when it meets powdered mustard-root, and while he was learning, Wren's crutch arrived.

Then it was quiet, except for breathing and the wind and the moonlight humming faintly, like a struck glass.

"Oh," said Pell from the ground, dazed, blood on his eyebrow, staring at the scattered treaty pages. "I appear to have been ineffective."

"You bought us four seconds," Mara said, hauling him up. "I've seen kings survive on less." She got Oscar's good arm over her shoulders. "Can you walk?"

"With assistance and complaints."

"Perfect. You're the most honest man in the valley."

They climbed.

* * *

The last stretch of path was the worst, because it was the easiest—wide enough for two abreast, smooth underfoot, the kind of path that invited pursuit—and the moon was directly overhead now, so full and so near that it cast shadows sharp enough to cut. Behind them, torchlight swarmed up the switchbacks. Ahead, around one final shoulder of rock, the Door.

Mara rounded that shoulder and stopped, and the breath went out of her.

It stood in no wall. It stood in the living cliff, a tall arch of grey stone that did not match the stone around it, carved—she could see now, even at distance—with figures: a procession of small kneeling people, hundreds of them, spiraling inward toward a central empty space shaped like nothing so much as a mouth. No hinges. No handle. Only a keyhole at its heart, and the keyhole was not metal-dark but flesh-warm, a soft red gleam, pulsing slowly.

Like a heartbeat. Like something waiting to be fed.

"The crown," Pell breathed beside her. "The keyhole wants the crown. The king told you—the crown is the key—"

"I have the crown."

She did. It was wrapped in a flour sack inside her apron, because she was the Queen of the Valley of the Unnamed, crowned in a kitchen among the pots, and queens apparently carried their regalia in baking supplies. She had thought, on the climb, that she would feel solemn taking it out. She mostly felt the weight of it, and the absurdity, and underneath both, a rising tide of something that was not quite fear and not quite grief.

Her mother's voice, suddenly, as clear as if the woman stood beside her on the path: *Taste it first, little mouse. Always taste it first.*

"Taste what?" she murmured aloud.

"The name," said the cliff.

Mara froze. The voice had come from everywhere—from the stone, from the fog, from the red pulse of the keyhole—and it was vast and slow and wounded, a voice like a whale might have, if whales had been imprisoned for centuries and had learned patience the hard way.

"You carry it," the land said. "In the sack. In the blood. In the girl who swallowed poisons until poison could not find her. Bring it closer, little taster. I have not tasted anything in nine hundred years."

* * *

The torches crested the rise behind them.

Mara turned, and there they were: twenty soldiers fanning out across the clifftop, and at their center, walking forward with her chin high and her eyes wet, Lady Vess Corvane.

She looked terrible. That was Mara's first thought, and her second, and her third. Vess had always been beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful, but whatever god she had prayed to after her defeat had answered too thoroughly—her skin had a faint translucence now, lamplight through porcelain, and when she opened her mouth to speak, the light inside her throat moved a half-second before her words did.

"Step away from the Door, Mara."

"You're glowing," Mara said. "Has anyone told you? You're glowing like a bad oyster."

"He promised me I would save them." Vess's voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "All of them. Every soul in this valley. Open the Door, lead them out into the wide world before the seal fails and the old god takes everything—names, memories, children. I am the only one willing to do what must be done. You hide in kitchens. I act."

"You sold us to something that eats names."

"I *saved* us from something that eats names! There is a difference, and history will draw it!" And there it was, the crack in her, the thing beneath the conviction—fear, enormous and unadmitted. "The moon is full, Mara. When it sets, the seal fails on its own, and everything my family worked for, everything *your* family traded away, burns. I won't let that happen. I can't."

Behind Vess, the soldiers spread into a line. Behind Mara, Oscar leaned on Wren, grey-faced, his ruined hand pressed flat against the cliff. Pell sat down deliberately on a rock, gathered his scattered treaty pages, and began, softly, to read the Founding Accord aloud—as if the words themselves were a wall.

Mara reached into her apron and drew out the spoon.

Vess stared at it. "Is that—"

"It's my spoon," said Mara. "I've tasted every danger this valley has served for eleven years. Poison, plague-rot, treason in a wine cup. I'm still here. So before you give me another speech, my lady, understand: I have already decided what I'm going to do, and it isn't either of the things you want. Not your escape. Not your rescue. Something my mother wrote down, and something the land just asked me for."

The keyhole pulsed. The fog on the borders flared gold. Overhead, the thirteenth moon stood at its zenith, so close now that its light had texture, weight, flavor—and Mara, taster to the last, could taste what was coming on the wind.

Bitterness. Enormous, ancient, patient bitterness. Nine hundred years of it, held in a caged mouth.

And beneath the bitterness, faint but unmistakable, sweetness.

"Open your mouth, little taster," said the land, gently. "I am about to serve you the oldest dish in the world."

Mara unwrapped the flour sack. The crown caught the moonlight. The soldiers raised their weapons. Vess screamed something. And Mara Kavanagh, royal food-taster, queen of a country that did not exist, lifted the crown-key toward the waiting door—

—and the keyhole opened like an eye, and looked at her, and *knew her name*.
