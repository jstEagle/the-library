## 36. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon came up over the Valley of the Unnamed like a coin dropped into a beggar's cup—too bright, too full, and thirteenth of its kind, which meant it had no business existing at all.

Mara Kavanagh stood before the Door Out of a country that did not exist, wearing a crown she had never wanted, in a dress Pell had dug out of an archive chest that smelled of cedar and centuries. The door stood in no wall. It stood in the cliff face itself, oak and iron in the middle of living stone, and it was locked with a name.

Her name. Or rather, the absence of one.

Behind her, Vess Corvane's mercenaries were coming up the goat path with torches, and behind *them* something worse was coming—a presence that made the torches gutter sideways, that made the goats on the far hillsides fall silent all at once, like a choir forgetting its song.

"Are you certain about this?" Oscar asked. His hands—aged ten years by a god's arithmetic, knuckles swollen, fingers spotted—hovered near her elbow as though he could catch her if she fell. He was nineteen. His hands looked fifty. "Because I've been thinking, and 'swallowing a name' sounds like the sort of thing where you only get to be wrong once."

"I've been wrong plenty of times," Mara said. "I'm due."

"That's not how being wrong works."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She turned back to the door. In its iron bands, letters moved like fish under ice—the land's true name, the stolen one, the one her ancestors had caged inside their own bloodlines for four hundred years so that magic would stay out and fog would stay in and nobody would ever have to be remembered by anything outside the valley.

Pell lay propped against a boulder twenty feet back, his ribs wrapped in Wren's spare sash, breathing in careful, expensive increments. He had insisted on coming. He had also insisted, twice, on reciting the relevant passages of the founding treaty, and both times Wren had put a hand over his mouth.

"Mara," Pell said now, weakly. "One last scholarly note. When you take the name in, it will not sit quietly. A name is not a word. It is a *weight*. You have spent your whole life learning to swallow what would kill anyone else. That is why it must be you. But even you—"

"Pell."

"—even you should know that the recipe your mother wrote calls it 'the final course,' and I have read enough of her marginalia to know she did not joke."

"She joked constantly," Mara said. "She just hid the jokes in the footnotes, where no one would look." Her mother had hidden everything there—in the margins of the recipe book, between the antidotes and the poisons, in the spaces where a careful reader learned that love could taste like harm and still be love. *Unbinding requires a crowned nameless taster willing to swallow the land's name like poison.* Mara had read the entry forty times. She had tasted forty kinds of death by now, professionally, in small doses. This was only the largest.

Wren stood at the cliff's edge, watching the torches climb. Her healed leg was braced and steady; whatever else the gods' gifts had done, they had given her a stride like a deer's. "They'll reach us in a quarter hour," she said. "Maybe less. Vess is driving them hard."

"Vess always drives hard," Mara said. "It's her best quality and her worst one."

"And the other one?" Oscar asked quietly. "The hungry one. The thing Vess let into herself."

Mara felt it even from here—a cold attention pressing up through the ground, patient and enormous, the way a trap feels before the trap springs. Vess's patron. The god of appetite, the one who answered prayers with interest. It was close now. It was very close.

"It won't matter," Mara said, and was surprised to find she believed it. "Once the seal comes down, there won't be room here for gods that don't belong. There will barely be room for us."

Oscar laughed—a small, cracked sound. "You say the most comforting things."

"I'm a taster. Comfort was never in my job description."

The moon cleared the ridge. Full. Thirteenth. The light came down the cliff face like water finding a channel, and the letters in the door's iron began to move faster, circling, gathering, a shoal of silver fish all turning toward her.

The door wanted to open. It had wanted to open for four hundred years.

Mara walked toward it.

---

Vess Corvane climbed the goat path with blood in her mouth and glory in her chest, and told herself—for perhaps the hundredth time since the palace—that this was rescue.

It *was* rescue. That was the thing none of them understood. Mara Kavanagh, kitchen-crowned and moon-struck, meant to swallow the valley's inheritance whole and unmake everything—Vess had seen the recipe book's final page, or enough of it, before the archives burned. And when the seal fell without order, without stewardship, without someone *holding the door*, every power outside these borders would pour in like winter through a broken window. The valley needed a regent. The valley needed *her*. Someone with a spine and a plan and, yes, a patron strong enough to hold the threshold while the rest of them scrambled through.

The hunger-god stirred beneath her breastbone, warm as swallowed coals. *Little regent,* it murmured, in a voice like a market bell rung underwater. *The crowned one stands at the door. Bring me the key.*

"You'll have it," Vess said aloud, and her mercenaries—good soldiers, frightened ones—pretended not to hear their commander talking to the dark. Let them pretend. Half of them prayed to her patron now anyway. She had given them that. She had given them *certainty*, which was rarer than gold and worth more.

The path bent around a shoulder of rock, and she saw them: the girl-king in her dead woman's dress, the kitchen boy with his old man's hands, the lame girl standing sentry, the archivist bleeding quietly against a stone. And beyond them, impossible, absurd, magnificent—the Door, standing in nothing, lit by a moon that should not exist.

Something in Vess's chest that was not entirely her own went tight with want.

*There,* said the god. *There it is.*

"Hold," Vess called to her men, and they halted in a ragged line, torches swaying. She stepped forward alone, because this part was hers, had always been hers, ever since she'd found the map in her grandmother's things and understood that the Corvanes had been waiting four generations for someone with the nerve to finish the family's real business.

"Mara," she said, and her voice carried beautifully in the thin air; it always had. "Step away from the door."

---

Mara turned. Of course she did. You didn't turn your back on Lady Vess Corvane; even the fog knew better.

Vess looked terrible and magnificent, the way she always did at the end of something—hair half-loose, cheeks hollowed, eyes burning with a light that Mara recognized now as borrowed. The hunger-god looked out through those eyes the way a tenant looks out through a window.

"Vess," Mara said. "You're standing in a place that doesn't exist, serving a thing that eats its believers, trying to steal a key from a cook. I want you to hear how that sounds."

"I hear how it sounds," Vess said. "I also hear what happens if we do nothing. I've done the arithmetic, Mara. I'm better at arithmetic than any of you." She spread her hands, and the torchlight caught the fine tremor in them—the god's grip, tightening. "Open the Door under my ward, and we walk out in order. Families first. Archives first. *People* first. That's all I've ever wanted. Order."

"You wanted to be the one holding the ledger," Mara said gently. "That's different."

"Everything is different when you're the one holding the ledger!" Vess's voice cracked upward, and beneath it, briefly, something else spoke—a second voice, vast and amused, and every torch on the path bent toward Vess like grass toward wind. "Step aside. Last kindness I have in me."

"The crown doesn't come off," Mara said. "And neither does the cook."

She turned back to the door.

Behind her she heard Vess draw breath—and then not speak but *pray*, aloud, in the old cadence, the way you'd call a dog home. The ground shuddered. The cold attention that had been pressing up through the mountain all night came up all at once, like a whale breaching, and Mara felt it arrive behind her: enormous, delighted, famished.

*Give me the key, little queen,* it said, in a voice that made the cliff grit shift and trickle. *And I will let your friends keep their names. Most of them. For a while.*

Mara looked at the moving letters in the iron. Silver fish, circling. Waiting for a mouth.

Her mother's voice, from the footnotes: *A taster's last duty is to go first.*

"All right," Mara said—to the god, to the door, to the moon, to her mother, to the ancestors whose names had been traded away so long ago that even their trade had been forgotten. "Dinner is served."

She pressed her lips to the iron band, and breathed in.

---

Vess saw the girl-king kiss the door, and thought for one mad instant that it was surrender—that Mara was giving herself to the metal, some kitchen superstition, some—

Then the letters *moved*.

They poured off the iron bands like water off a tipped jug, silver and endless, more letters than Vess had ever seen, more than existed in all of Pell's burned archives, and they flowed into Mara Kavanagh's mouth the way a river flows into a dry well—and kept flowing. The girl's throat worked. Her hands braced against the door. Her eyes went wide, then wider, then white.

*Stop her,* screamed the god, and for the first time since Vess had taken it in, the hunger in her chest sounded afraid. *STOP HER—*

Vess lunged. She was three steps away, two, knife already in her hand because somewhere in the arithmetic she had always known it might come to this, the regent's final duty, the ugly page at the end of the ledger—

The thirteenth moon reached its zenith.

And the name finished pouring.

What happened next, Vess would spend the rest of her life failing to describe. The world did not break. It *exhaled*. Four hundred years of held breath left the valley in a single rush, and the seal—not the door, the *seal*, the invisible skin drawn tight around the whole country—came apart like frost off a window in spring. Vess felt it go through her, felt the borders of everything dissolve, felt the outside world press close for the first time in centuries, vast and loud and *real*.

And the hunger-god, mid-strike, its weight already lifting out of her chest to seize the key it had been promised—

Met the name.

Not a weapon. Not a wall. Just the land's true self, spoken at last, filling every corner of the valley it had always been—the bedrock and the fog and the rivers and the buried shrines, all of it suddenly, unmistakably, *someone*. And there was no room in that someone for a foreign appetite. There was no room at all.

The god came apart the way a lie comes apart when the truth arrives: instantly, completely, almost politely. Vess felt it leave her—not ripped out, but *evicted*, its warmth and its coal-weight and its beautiful certainty gone between one heartbeat and the next, and what rushed in behind it was so ordinary it nearly broke her: her own heartbeat. Her own fear. Her own name, Vess Corvane, suddenly the only name in her mouth, suddenly *enough*, suddenly terribly small.

She collapsed to her knees on the goat path. The knife rang off stone somewhere below her. Around her, her mercenaries were on their knees too, weeping or laughing or praying to nothing at all, and above them the moon—the thirteenth moon—was quietly, sensibly, going out. Not eclipsed. *Correcting.* As if the sky had found an error in its own accounts and struck the line through.

The door stood open.

Through it, instead of the outside world, Vess saw fog—then the fog thinned, and through it, impossibly, dawn.

---

Mara came back to herself on her hands and knees on cold stone, tasting the world.

That was the only way to say it. Every breath was a flavor. The granite tasted of patience. The fog tasted of held-back rain and old grief. The dawn coming through the open door tasted of—she searched for it, astonished—of *bread*. Warm bread, somewhere far away, in a country she had never seen, baked by someone who had never heard of her, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever tasted.

The name sat in her chest. It did not burn, exactly. It *inhabited*, the way a great guest inhabits a small house—filling every room, rearranging the furniture, making the walls hum. She could feel the valley through it now, all of it, root and stone and stream, the way she had once felt poison in a spoon. The land was alive. The land had always been alive. It had been lying very still for four hundred years, the way you do when you're in a cage, hoping no one remembers you're there.

"I'm sorry," she whispered to it, in its own name, which she now knew and could never unknow. "We're sorry. All of us."

The land, in the manner of land everywhere, did not answer. But the fog lifted off the cliff face all at once, like a curtain drawn, and showed her the sunrise.

"Mara?" Oscar's voice, hoarse. His ancient hands helped her upright, gentle as a nursemaid's. "Say something normal. Please. Something boring. Tell me the soup needs salt."

"The soup," Mara croaked, "needs salt."

He laughed and cried at the same time, which was very Oscar. Behind him Wren had both arms around Pell, who was wheezing out what sounded like the beginning of a citation—"As per the treaty's fourth annex, dissolution of the seal constitutes—" and Wren saying, "Pell. *Pell.* We won. You can stop footnoting."

But Mara was looking past them all, down the goat path, to where Vess Corvane knelt alone among her kneeling men, empty-handed, empty-chested, staring at the sunrise like a woman reading a letter she'd waited her whole life for and found it addressed to someone else.

Their eyes met.

Vess did not look defeated. That was the terrible thing. She looked *freed*—and broken the way a bird is broken when the cage door opens and it realizes it has forgotten how the sky works. Four generations of Corvane ambition had just been unmade by a cook kissing a door, and Vess was kneeling in the ashes of it, and she was crying, and she did not seem to know why.

Mara started down the path toward her.

"Mara," Oscar said sharply. "Don't. She had a knife."

"She had a knife," Mara agreed. "She doesn't anymore." She kept walking, crown heavy on her head, name heavy in her chest, the whole remembered world opening around her like a hand. "And I make it a rule, these days, never to leave anyone unfed."

Below her, the fog rolled back from the valley floor, and for the first time in four hundred years, the Valley of the Unnamed lay open under the sky—visible, findable, *real*—and somewhere out beyond the border stones, in a country that had forgotten it, the maps were already beginning to redraw themselves.
