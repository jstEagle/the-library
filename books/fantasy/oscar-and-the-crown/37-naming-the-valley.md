## 37. Naming the Valley

Wren had spent her whole life learning the shape of the valley by its edges: the crooked fence behind the tannery, the place where the millrace went shallow, the border stones she was never, ever supposed to touch. She knew it the way you know a room you've been shut in—by bumping into things.

She did not know it from the top of Harrow Hill at dawn, with her healed leg braced against cold granite and the whole country spread out below her like something newly unwrapped.

"Is it always this big?" she asked.

"It's exactly as big as it's ever been," Pell said beside her. He was sitting down, because he was still bandaged from the shrine siege and because he was Pell, and sat on a flat stone with his testament papers weighted under a pebble in case the wind got ideas. "You simply never saw all of it at once. None of us did. That was rather the point of the fog."

The fog was going.

That was the thing Wren kept trying to say to herself, over and over, like a child practicing a word: *the fog is going.* It lifted off the valley floor in long slow ribbons, the way steam comes off porridge when your grandmother decides, finally, that it's ready. Underneath it the land lay green and brown and silver-rivered, and every field, every roof, every crooked fence looked startled—as if they'd been caught undressed after centuries of nobody looking.

And above it all, the thirteenth moon was setting, full and pale and done.

Wren thought about the night before. Everyone did; she could see it in the way Oscar kept flexing his scarred hands, and in the way Mara stood apart at the hill's crest, very straight, very quiet, with an empty look in her mouth like someone who has just swallowed something enormous and is being polite about it.

Because she had. Wren had watched her do it.

They'd all been there at the cliff door when the thirteenth moon rose—Wren leaning on her good leg, then remembering halfway up the path that she didn't need to anymore, which had made her cry for no reason she wanted to explain to anyone. Vess's mercenaries had come, and Vess herself, half-lit from inside by a god that wore her ambition like a coat two sizes too small. And then Mara Kavanagh, food-taster, nameless daughter of nameless mothers, crowned in a kitchen among the copper pots—Mara had tipped back her head and swallowed the land's own stolen name like a dose of bitter medicine.

Wren would remember the sound of it forever. Not a scream. A *ringing*, like a bell struck inside a mountain, like the whole valley clearing its throat after four hundred years of holding its breath.

Then Vess's hungry god had come apart mid-strike—not violently, oddly enough, but the way frost comes off a window in the sun, quietly surrendering—and Vess had folded up like a dropped puppet, and the antlered god at the border stone had bowed its great head to a kitchen girl, and the seal had gone out of the world like a candle pinched between wise fingers.

And now it was morning, and the fog was leaving, and Wren's leg worked, and she was standing on top of her own country seeing it whole for the first time.

"I feel like I should be doing something," Oscar said. He was twenty, or near enough, with the hands of a man of fifty and the face of a boy who hadn't slept. "Singing, maybe. Or weeping. Something ceremonial."

"You wept twice on the walk up," Pell said without looking up from his papers.

"That was grief. This would be joy-weeping. Completely different."

"You may joy-weep if you like," Pell said. "I'll note it in the record. *At dawn, the kitchen boy wept ceremonially.*"

Oscar considered this. "I'll wait until something better happens."

Something better happened.

It began, as far as Wren could tell, with the birds. There had always been birds in the valley—sparrows, crows, one heroic family of wrens in the woodpile behind her house whom she had named individually despite Pell's scholarly objections that names were precisely what the valley did not do. But now, as the last fog ribbons tore loose from the hills, birds came *in*. From beyond the borders, from the outside world that no map had ever admitted existed. Geese in ragged formation, a hawk riding air none of them could see, small brown flittings in the hedgerows that had never grown anything before because nothing outside had ever been allowed to visit.

"They're coming home," Wren said, and then corrected herself. "No—they're coming *first*. Like scouts."

"Like guests arriving early for a wedding," Oscar said.

"The valley does have a certain bridal quality this morning," Pell agreed gravely, and Wren laughed, and the laugh came out wet, and fine, so maybe she was joy-weeping too, and she decided not to tell him.

* * *

They came down off Harrow Hill as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and that was when Wren understood that the world outside had noticed them.

Not slowly. Not gently. All at once, everywhere, the way a sleeper notices a missing blanket.

Pell felt it first—he made a strangled noise and grabbed his papers. "The maps," he said. "Oh. Oh, *oh*." For a man who had spent decades cataloguing a country that officially did not exist, he looked like a father whose child had just taken first steps in front of strangers. "Every map beyond the border stones is wrong this morning. Every single one. Cartographers from here to the coast are going to wake up and find a blank space where their ink should be, and the blank space will be *shaped like us*."

"Will they redraw them?" Wren asked.

"They'll have to. The land won't be denied anymore." Pell's voice cracked on the last word, and he pressed his fist briefly to his mouth, composing himself with the desperate dignity of a man who had promised himself he wouldn't cry in front of the kitchen boy. "Four hundred years of erasure, undone overnight. Somewhere a royal surveyor is going to ride out to correct his charts and find a whole country sitting where the wilderness used to be, with roads in it, and chimneys smoking, and people waving at him."

"Do you think anyone will wave?" Oscar asked.

"Somebody will wave," Wren said. "Probably me. I'm very good at waving. Years of practice at festivals nobody outside ever heard of."

They took the river road toward the village, because that was where everyone would gather—everyone always gathered at the well square when anything happened, births and funerals and the one time a mule had gotten into the ale cellar, which remained the high-water mark of local excitement for a generation. People were already coming out of their houses, standing in doorways, pointing up at the naked sky.

Because that was the other thing. The stars were gone.

Not vanished—*replaced*. The valley's sky had always held its own private constellations, thirteen moons' worth of patterns that no astronomer beyond the stones had ever charted, because the sky itself was part of the seal. Now, in the pale aftermath of dawn, the old foreign stars were bleeding through, faint as watermarks, and the village astrologer—a retired goatherd named Doss who had been confidently wrong about the weather for forty years—was standing in the middle of the road with his mouth open, holding his star-charts upside down.

"Doss," Wren called, "you're holding those upside down."

"They're wrong either way!" he called back, with the joy of a man whose life's work had just been gloriously demolished. "Everything's wrong! Isn't it *wonderful*?"

The well square filled. Wren saw faces she'd known her whole life wearing expressions she'd never seen on them—the baker looking at his own hands as though they might belong to someone famous; old Marta from the dyehouse squinting east as if she could see the ocean from here, and maybe now she could, maybe now the horizon actually went somewhere. Children were running in circles for no reason at all, which Wren respected deeply, since she couldn't have explained her own urge to run in circles either.

And through it all, threading between the crowd like a needle through cloth, went Mara.

Wren watched her and felt the first cold thread of worry.

Because the crowd *parted* for her. Not out of deference—out of something stranger. Every eye found her. Every head turned as she passed, the way flowers turn, the way iron filings turn. A woman Wren didn't know—a holdout from the far farms, one of the ones who'd refused the gods' gifts—stepped directly into Mara's path and gripped her sleeve and said, "You. You're the one who spoke it. I dreamed your voice," and Mara said something gentle and moved on, and the woman stood there afterward with tears running down her face like she'd been touched by something holy.

"She can't hide anymore," Wren said quietly, to Pell.

"No," Pell said. His scholar's eyes were sharp with a grief he was trying to file under *observation*. "The unbinding required a vessel. She poured the name through herself, and some of it stayed. She gave away her invisibility—the one inheritance her bloodline ever valued—and bought us all visibility with it." He shook his head slowly. "Every prayer spoken in this valley will reach her now. Every eye will find her in a crowd. Forever. That was the price, and she paid it before anyone could stop her, which—" his voice did the cracking thing again, "—which was extremely like her mother, if the archives are any indication."

"Mara!" Oscar had climbed onto the well's rim, which was against about six local ordinances, none of which currently mattered. "Speech! We want a speech!"

"We don't want a speech," said half the crowd.

"We want a speech," said the other half, louder, because the other half included Doss the astrologer, who had committed fully to the new era of spectacle.

Mara looked at the crowd the way she must once have looked at a suspicious plate of the king's breakfast: carefully, professionally, checking for poison. Then she climbed up beside Oscar, and the whole square hushed so completely that Wren could hear the river a quarter mile off, running bright and loud over stones it had polished in secret for centuries.

"People of the valley," Mara said. Her voice wasn't big. It didn't need to be; the whole country leaned in to hear it. "I have tasted every meal our king ate for nine years, and I am pleased to report that this morning, for the first time, nothing here is poisoned."

A laugh rolled through the square, ragged and real, and Wren realized people had been holding fear in their bodies for days and were only now letting it out.

"The fog is gone. The seal is broken. Our name is spoken, and the world knows we exist." Mara paused, and Wren saw her hand drift to her collarbone, to the spot where the crown-key had hung, where something else hung now that no one could see. "I know some of you are afraid. I know some of you are grieving the gifts you refused, and some are grieving the gifts you accepted, and some of you are just grieving because change is heavy even when it's good. I'm not going to stand here and tell you everything will be easy. Outside that ridge are kingdoms with armies and merchants with ledgers and priests with opinions. They're going to come. But they're going to come to *us*—to a valley with a name, with a history, with people in it who survived four hundred years of being forgotten without forgetting each other." She looked around, and her eyes snagged on Wren's, and held for a moment, warm and tired and asking nothing. "We were never nothing. Now the maps agree."

The cheer that went up knocked a rook off the chapel roof. Wren cheered too, both arms, healed leg planted firm, and if she joy-wept, well, the record could show it, and welcome.

* * *

By afternoon, the first outsiders arrived.

Not armies—Wren had been privately rehearsing defiant speeches about armies all morning—but a cart. One cart, drawn by one exhausted horse, carrying a mapmaker: a round, sweating, ecstatic man in a city coat who pulled up at the edge of the well square, stared at the assembled population of the valley as though they were mirages, and then burst into tears.

"I've been riding since moonrise," he managed, producing a leather tube with shaking hands. "My instruments went mad at the border. My compass spins. My charts went *blank*—blank, do you understand, forty years of work, wiped clean as a whistle—and I followed the blankness, and here you all *are*." He unfurled a fresh sheet of paper, empty except for a penciled outline. "May I? May I draw you? I'll need names. Streets, rivers, hills—I'll need *everything*. I haven't stopped shaking since dawn. Is there tea?"

"There is tea," said the baker, already moving, because the baker responded to all crises and celebrations with baked goods and hot drinks, and honestly Wren thought the valley's foreign policy was in excellent hands.

Within the hour there were three more carts, and a bewildered pair of pilgrims, and a merchant whose ledger had developed a sudden passionate interest in a country he'd never heard of yesterday. The villagers clustered around them, trading names for names—*that's the millrace, that's Harrow Hill, that's my cousin Annet, she makes the best blackberry cordial in three counties, we never knew which counties, but we're confident*—and Wren stood in the middle of it with her heart so full it ached, watching her invisible home become legible, line by line, name by name.

Pell had set up a folding table and was conducting interviews like a general staff. "The eastern ridge is called the Sleeping Hound. No, we don't know why. Yes, it looks like a sleeping hound. Some things are simply true." He glanced up at Wren as she passed. "You should write yours down too, you know. Your account. Dawn on Harrow Hill, from the perspective of—" he hesitated delicately, "—of someone the miracle walked for."

Wren looked down at her leg. Two legs. Both hers. Both working.

"My account," she said, "is that I was lame for sixteen years, and a god fixed me for free, and I spent a season wondering what the bill would be, and then a girl who'd never done anything but keep other people safe swallowed a country's name so that all of us could be remembered." She shrugged. "I don't think I can write that down yet. Ask me in a year."

"Scholarship is patience," Pell said approvingly. "Also, ask me in a year whether I forgive myself for burning those treaty pages. We can form a club."

The light was going gold and long across the square when Wren finally spotted Mara again—at the edge of things, as always, except that now the edge of things wouldn't have her. A knot of children had found her and were demanding, with the ruthless democracy of children, that she tell them what the name sounded like in her mouth. And Mara was telling them—Wren saw her lips move, saw the children's eyes go wide, saw one little girl press her hands over her ears and then take them away immediately because whatever it was, she wanted more of it.

But as the shadows stretched, Wren saw something else. Saw Mara flinch, very slightly, at nothing. Saw her turn her head as if answering someone who wasn't there. Saw her close her eyes for a moment, jaw tight, a woman listening to a hundred whispered prayers arrive all at once in a voice meant for tasting one spoonful at a time.

Wren started toward her.

Across the square, Oscar had seen it too. He was already moving, bad old hands shoved in his pockets, weaving through the crowd with the practiced slip of a kitchen boy. Their eyes met over the heads of the well-wishers, and neither of them said anything, because nothing needed saying:

*She saved all of us. Now somebody has to save her.*

The mapmaker's pen scratched. The first star of the outside sky came out over the Sleeping Hound. And somewhere beyond the ridges, in capitals that had woken to find a new country on their maps, people were already asking questions about the girl who had spoken its name.

Questions, Wren suspected, that were going to arrive at Mara's door very, very soon.
