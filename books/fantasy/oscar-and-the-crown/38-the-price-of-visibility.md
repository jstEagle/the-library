## 38. The Price of Visibility

The first thing I learned about being visible was that it itched.

Not the skin—the skin was fine, if you didn't count the scar where Vess's knife had kissed my ribs and the newer one where the thirteenth moon's light had done its work. No, what itched was the attention itself, settling on me like wool in summer, like a cat that had decided I was furniture worth sitting on and would not be persuaded otherwise.

I had spent thirty years learning how to be overlooked. I could stand in a doorway and become part of the door. I could carry a tray through a crowded hall and leave no more impression than the smell of bread. It was a craft, invisibility, and I had been very good at it—good enough to survive a court that served no kingdom, good enough to taste poison for a king who ruled fog, good enough to keep my name folded up small inside me where nothing hungry could find it.

And then I had swallowed a god's name on a cliff under a full moon, and every scrap of that craft had burned out of me like fat off a hot pan.

Now I walked into the market square and forty heads turned at once, smooth as iron filings finding their lodestone. Now children pointed. Now dogs—dogs!—abandoned their bickering over a fish head to trot at my heels with expressions of profound devotion. Now, when an old woman in Tarn Row whispered a prayer over her rheumatic knees, the words arrived in my chest like a letter slipped under a door, warm and slightly damp, asking please, asking kindly, asking *Mara*.

Because they all knew my name now. That was the other half of the price. The valley had gone out into the world wearing the name I'd given it, spoken from my own mouth on the worst and best night of my life, and the world had looked back—and in looking back, it had found me too. Every map that redrew itself overnight drew a small star where I stood. Every prayer said within our new borders, whether to the antlered guardian in his winter forest or to the older quiet powers or just to the general mercy of things, found its way to me first, the way water finds the lowest ground.

I was, Pell informed me over breakfast on the fourth morning after the Naming, technically a minor deity.

"I want that noted," he said, "for the archives. Not worshipped—there's a distinction, and it matters legally—but *receptive*. You're a node, Mara. A well. People's hopes fall into you."

"I don't *want* to be a well."

"Neither did the valley want to be sealed for four hundred years." He adjusted his spectacles, which were new, made by the glassblowers who had come through the Door Out in the second wave of traders, and who still couldn't quite believe their luck. "But here we are, both of us carrying jobs we never applied for."

Pell had changed since the shrine siege. The burns along his arm had healed into pale rivers, and he wore them uncovered now, sleeves rolled, as if daring anyone to look away. He had also stopped burning things, which he considered his great moral achievement of the age. "I destroyed pages once to protect a secret," he told every new archivist personally, "and it nearly cost us everything. In this archive, we keep *everything*. We keep receipts. We keep apologies." There was a whole shelf now labeled APOLOGIES, and it was thicker than anyone expected.

I ate my breakfast while he talked. That was the joke nobody at court quite dared say aloud: the woman who had swallowed the name of the land itself still tasted every meal before the council ate it. Old habits. Old armor. And besides—I liked it. There was something holy about tasting, when you got down to it. Something about taking a thing into your body and saying: this is safe, or this is not, and meaning it with your whole life.

The porridge was fine. The honey had a note of smoke in it that I suspected came from Wren's holdout hives, which she swore were ordinary bees and everyone else swore had learned to sing.

* * *

The abdication took place in the throne room, which we had finally gotten around to renovating, mostly by removing the throne.

It had been Oscar's idea. "You can't hand power to a council and leave a chair shaped like a promise sitting there," he'd said, hands clasped behind his back—his healed hands, the ones the guardian stag had restored to him whole and unlined, though he still sometimes flexed them at night like a man checking a dream against the morning. "People will sit in it. Then someone else will sit in it. Then someone will start dusting it reverently, and that's how kings happen."

So we sold the throne to a sculptor from beyond the border, who carved it into eleven chairs—one for each member of the valley council—and a small stool for whoever was elected to make the tea, because Wren insisted that authority should begin with service or not at all.

Eleven chairs. And I stood before them in my plain grey dress, the crown of the Kavanaghs—a thin circlet of fog-iron, barely worth the name of crown—in my two hands.

"You don't have to do this," said Wren, from the third chair. Her leg was bare below the knee, the old miracle-healed leg, strong as an oak root. She'd chosen the valley over the miracle in the end, and the miracle, to everyone's surprise including its own, hadn't punished her for it. The gods of the valley, freed, turned out to be mostly patient. Mostly kind. Mostly *tired*, like old farmers at the end of a long season.

"Yes, I do," I said. "You've all seen what happens when I walk through the market. Forty people forget what they came to buy. The baker gave me a free loaf yesterday and then apologized to the loaf. Last week a man proposed marriage during a funeral."

"That was Bram," said Oscar. "He proposes at funerals. It's a grief thing."

"He proposed to *me*, Oscar."

"Well. You are very noticeable these days."

There was laughter in the room, the easy kind, and I let it wash over me and did not flinch, because flinching from warmth was another habit I was trying to break. That was the strange arithmetic of visibility: it cost me my peace and paid me back in moments like this, in being *known*, in jokes that landed because someone had bothered to learn exactly who I was.

"Here is the truth of it," I said, and the room went quiet, because when the woman who hears prayers says *here is the truth of it*, people listen; they can't help it. "A ruler should be able to be ignored. Should be able to fail quietly, change her mind privately, have a bad morning without three hundred people's hope curdling with hers. I can't ignore anything anymore. Every fear in this valley lands on me before breakfast. If I kept the crown, I wouldn't be ruling you—I'd be *leaking* on you. My bad days would become your weather."

"Weather's already weird here," muttered Tam the cooper, from the last chair. "Remember the singing bees."

"The point," I said, "is that the seal is broken, and the valley needs leaders who can be argued with. Who can be *wrong* in public and survive it. I will never again be wrong in private. So." I held out the circlet. "I set down the crown my ancestors used as a lock, and I give the council the valley my mother's recipe book taught me to feed: carefully, generously, and always tasting first."

Wren stood and took it from me. She weighed it in her palm, frowned at it like a cook frowning at a suspect egg, and then set it on the mantel above the council hearth, where it remains to this day, holding up a small clay pot of salt.

Nobody knelt. That was the rule, and Wren enforced it with the flat stare of a woman whose leg had been taken and returned and who therefore knew precisely which gifts were real.

* * *

Vess Corvane took her seat on the council nine days later, and the room did not catch fire, which several people had bet money on.

She came in dressed not in her old silks but in good dark wool, practical as a saddle, and she stood before us all with the half-consumed side of her face turned deliberately toward the light. The hunger-god's mark had faded after its unmaking—not vanished, but softened, a silvered patch across her cheekbone like a scar from an old burn, or frost on a window. She wore it uncovered. She had learned that trick from Pell, I think, or maybe we had all learned it from each other, in those strange tender weeks when the whole valley was limping and showing its wounds and discovering that wounds, displayed honestly, made better introductions than beauty ever had.

"I'm not going to apologize," she said.

"Good," said Wren. "We haven't time for performances."

"But I am going to account." Vess set a ledger on the table—of course she had a ledger; Vess had probably been born clutching a ledger—and opened it. "For every person my mercenaries detained. For the siege at the great shrine. For Pell." She looked at him directly, and Pell, to his credit, looked back. "For the pages I would have burned if you hadn't gotten there first. I believed I was saving this valley. I want that understood—not forgiven, *understood*. I looked at a country erased from every map and I thought: the crime isn't the door. The crime is that anyone ever thought we needed to hide."

"And now?" asked Tam.

"Now I've seen the maps redraw themselves." Her voice did something complicated on the word *maps*, and I realized, with the unwanted clarity that comes with hearing prayers, that Vess Corvane had spent her entire childhood invisible in a different way than mine—unloved rather than unnamed, unnoticed in a house that had plenty of eyes to spare. Some hungers you feed. Some you build a council around and give honest work.

The vote was close. It went seven to four, and the four who voted no shook her hand afterward anyway, because that was the new custom too: you could lose a vote and remain a person.

Her first act as councilor was to establish the Office of Borders—not to guard them, she was quick to say, but to *greet* them. Someone had to stand at the Door Out and welcome the cartographers and the merchants and the occasional pilgrim, and explain, patiently, for the ten-thousandth time, why the valley had been missing, and what it had cost, and what it was worth.

She was insufferably good at it. Of course she was.

* * *

The prayers were the hardest part.

I want to be honest about that, because the ballads—they write ballads now, actual ballads, one of them has me fighting Vess with a ladle, which never happened, though I admit the ladle was present—get this part wrong. They say I gave up my shadow-self and gained a thousand friends, as if the trade were clean. It wasn't clean. Nothing worth doing is.

Every night, prayers arrived. Not all of them—most hopes, thank goodness, still went about their business without me—but enough. The widow on Bell Lane asking her dead husband's pardon. The boy who'd failed his apprenticeship exam asking for courage to tell his father. Wren, some nights, asking nothing at all, just *thank you, thank you, thank you*, which arrived in my chest like sunlight through a curtain and was somehow harder to bear than any plea.

And the frightened ones. The ones who prayed at 3 a.m., the ones whose prayers tasted of copper and cold water. Those I felt in my teeth. I lay awake cataloguing sorrows like a taster cataloguing poisons, and I could do nothing for most of them except—what? Send Oscar around with bread? Leave a lamp lit? I began leaving my kitchen window open at night, and I told no one why, and slowly the valley figured it out anyway, because I was visible now, and visible things get understood whether they consent to it or not.

My mother would have called it a weakness, this permeability. She fed me poison from the age of six so that nothing could get into me. She built me like a locked room and called it love, and it *was* love, the only grammar she had, and I honor it even now. But a locked room, I've come to believe, is just a small version of the valley we lived in for four hundred years. Safe, and starving, and answering to no one, which is another way of saying belonging to no one.

I chose the open window. I choose it again every night, when the prayers come knocking like weather.

Some nights I answer them. Not with miracles—I have none, whatever Pell's archives claim. With soup, usually. With a visit. With sitting beside a bed and saying, "I heard you. I can't fix it. But you're not praying alone anymore." It turns out that's most of what prayer was ever for. The gods knew it. Even the hungry one, at the bottom of all its terrible appetite, had only been trying to stop being unheard.

* * *

On the evening before the council's first full season closed, Oscar found me in the kitchen, testing a recipe.

"What is it?" he asked, leaning over the bowl. His hands—those healed hands—were dusted with flour to the wrists. He'd taken to baking on his days off from ambassadoring, badly at first, then adequately, then, through the sheer Oscar-ness of him, wonderfully. The outside world had taken one look at our first ambassador—open-faced, unhurried, hands that remembered what they'd lost—and fallen in love, and he came home every month with treaties and trade terms and, inexplicably, seeds.

"Unbinding cake," I said. "Well—that's what I'm calling it. It was the last page of my mother's book. I finally worked out what she meant."

"Not the ritual?"

"No, that was the ritual. This is—" I tapped the page. Her handwriting, cramped and certain. "*One cup of the flour you were afraid of. Two eggs from the hen you couldn't catch. Sweeten past the point of sense.* She wrote recipes the way other people write letters. I think this one was for me. For after. For whenever I finally stopped being untouchable and needed to learn to eat like a person instead of a fortress."

Oscar watched me fold the batter—flour from the border fields, where the soil had woken up strange and generous; eggs from Wren's singing-bee-guarded hens; honey smoked over apple wood; and, because my mother's recipe demanded it, a spoonful of something sweet past the point of sense, which in practice meant the candied violets Oscar brought back from a town two weeks' ride east, where they apparently considered violets a staple food.

"It'll taste like nothing you've ever had," he predicted.

"It'll taste like being seen," I said. "Which is roughly the same thing."

We baked it together in the palace kitchen, among the pots and pans where I'd been crowned, and the cooks drifted in one by one pretending they'd smelled nothing and staying anyway, and by the time the cake came out the whole kitchen was full, and I cut it into pieces and handed them around, and I tasted the first slice myself, in front of everyone, the way I had tasted every dangerous thing for thirty years—

and it was safe. It was better than safe. It tasted like a valley with its name back, like fog lifting off a field at dawn, like a locked room with the window thrown wide and the wind coming through carrying somebody's laughter.

"To the taster," said Oscar, raising his slice.

"To the well," said Pell, raising his.

"To the queen who quit," said Wren, and the kitchen roared, and I raised my spoon—all I'd ever needed, all I'd ever been, all I'd ever be—and drank the toast down.

Outside, the moon rose: the first moon of the new count, young and silver and merely one of twelve, keeping honest time at last. And far off, at the edge of the orchard, something with antlers stood a while in the dark, watching the lit windows with the patience of old land, before turning, unhurried, toward the borders—to see who might be coming next.
