## 39. Oscar's Own Name

The morning the valley got its name back, Oscar woke to find a stag standing in his mother's garden, eating the cabbages.

Not eating them, exactly. *Blessing* them. Each leaf the great antlered head dipped toward came up afterward looking better than it had any right to—greener, firmer, dusted with frost-melt that glittered like sugar. The stag lifted its head when Oscar opened the door, and its eyes were the color of deep water under ice, and it regarded him with the patience of something that had once been worshipped by people who couldn't grow cabbages to save their lives.

"You," Oscar said.

The stag's ears tilted. Around them, the village was already stirring—the first morning in living memory that the Valley of the Unnamed had woken up *named*, and everyone in it had somewhere to be and someone to tell about it. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight, confident lines. Somewhere down the lane, Wren was arguing joyfully with three people at once about whether the council should meet in the shrine or the tavern. The fog that had hugged the borders for centuries had rolled back overnight like a blanket pulled off a sleeping child, and beyond the old border stones, Oscar could see hills he had never known existed, green and ordinary and *there*.

"I have questions," Oscar told the stag. "About the name you gave me. About what it cost. About whether my hands are going to keep doing—" He flexed his fingers experimentally. They still ached, those hands. Ten years of payment aged into them in a single night of fog and desperation, knuckles swollen, joints stiff as old hinges. He'd been twenty-two years old feeling like forty. "That."

The stag stepped closer. Its breath smelled of pine sap and rain on hot stone. And then, gently, it lowered its head and touched its nose to Oscar's ruined knuckles.

Warmth spread through his hands like sunlight through a window. Not magic, exactly—or not only magic. It felt more like an apology. Like someone setting down a debt they'd been carrying too long and realizing they'd overcharged.

The ache didn't vanish. But it loosened. The swelling sank. When Oscar flexed his fingers again, they moved the way young hands move, easy and unremarkable, and he made a sound that was embarrassingly close to a sob and decided the cabbage patch was private enough.

"More than it took," he said wonderingly. "You gave back more than it took."

The stag huffed—a sound that, from anything else, would have been a laugh—and Oscar had the sudden, unshakeable sense that the god was pleased with itself. Gods, apparently, could be smug. Who knew.

* * *

He went to find Mara before the village found him.

This was harder than it used to be. That was the strange arithmetic of the valley's new life: everything had changed for everyone, but no one's change was quite like anyone else's. Wren could walk, had always been able to walk now, in the way of miracles that rewrite history—but she'd chosen the holdouts' side in the end, and the miracle stayed anyway, because the land was free now and generosity was apparently contagious. Pell was alive, bandaged, and dictating his testament to anyone who stood still long enough, which these days included visiting merchants from *outside*, actual outsiders with mud on their boots from roads that appeared on maps. Vess Corvane was pardoned, hollowed, and terrifyingly sincere about her new role on the council, which everyone agreed was either redemption or the longest con in recorded history, and no one wanted to bet against her twice.

And Mara—

Mara was visible. That was the price she'd paid, swallowing the land's name like poison so it could be spoken aloud, and the valley had spoken it, and the maps had redrawn themselves overnight, and every eye in creation now found Mara Kavanagh wherever she stood. She couldn't slip into a kitchen unnoticed anymore. She couldn't taste a meal in the corner and vanish. She was the woman who named the country, and the country looked at her constantly, gratefully, exhaustingly.

Oscar found her in the palace kitchens, because of course she was in the kitchens. Some things survived even miracles.

She was standing at the big table with flour to her elbows, arguing with Cook about the correct ratio of butter to grief in a celebration loaf, and when Oscar came in she looked up—and here was the thing, here was the thing that broke his heart a little every time—she *saw* him see her. She registered being seen. She did it a hundred times a day now, he suspected, a small flinch of visibility each time, and she never let it show except in the half-second before she smiled.

"Oscar." She wiped her hands. "You look terrible."

"That's the old me. I'm the new me now. New hands." He held them out. "Look. No aches. Well—fewer aches. The stag came by this morning and ate your cabbages, by the way. Mother's cabbages. It says sorry. It doesn't say sorry, actually, it just looked smug."

Mara laughed, and the whole kitchen turned to look at her laughing, because the whole world turned to look at Mara now, and she bore it the way she'd borne poison all her childhood: steadily, with practice, one swallow at a time.

"The god came to you," she said, quieter.

"It fixed my hands. Or started to. It said—" Oscar paused, working at it. The words the stag had given him hadn't arrived as words exactly; they'd arrived as certainty, the way prayers used to arrive before the seal cracked. "It said the debt was mispriced. That luck bought with a borrowed name should have cost less, and it had charged a frightened boy a king's ransom because it didn't know its own worth yet. Now it does." He shrugged. "So it's refunding me. In installments. My hands are installment one."

"And installment two?"

Oscar took a breath. This was why he'd come. "It offered me a job."

* * *

They walked up to the border stone together, because Mara insisted on seeing it herself, and because Oscar wanted witnesses for what he was about to say out loud, in case saying it made it real and he needed someone to talk him out of it.

The border stones were cracked still—cracks from the moon-tenth night, when the fog had leaked and the outside world had glimpsed the valley for the first time in centuries—but the cracks were healing, filling with a pale new stone like bone knitting. Beyond them, the hills rolled away toward a road, an actual road, where yesterday a merchant cart had come rattling down with a driver who kept stopping to check his map and mutter, "But there's nothing *here*, there's been nothing here, my grandfather swore—" and then shrug and sell the villagers three barrels of salt at a fair price, because commerce, unlike geography, adapts quickly.

"They're coming," Oscar said. "Traders first. Then scholars, because of Pell's letters—he can't help himself, he's written to every archive from here to the coast. Then pilgrims, because of the shrines glowing. Then people who want things." He kicked at a pebble. "And the valley has never had to want anything back. We don't know how to bargain. We spent four hundred years not existing; we're going to be terrible at existing."

"Yes," Mara said.

"And someone has to stand at the door and explain us. Translate us. Tell the salt merchants which shrines they can picnic at and which ones will make them weep uncontrollably, that kind of thing." Oscar looked at his hands—young hands, refunded hands. "The stag asked if I'd do it. First ambassador of the named valley. It said I'd already spent a name learning what names cost, so I was qualified."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. On the other side of the border stone, wind moved through grass that had never been valley-grass, and it sounded different—looser, less reverent, like a language without manners.

"You'd be leaving," she said finally. "Going out. There'd be years when you'd be more out than in."

"I know."

"You spent your whole life inside a place that wasn't anywhere. You might want—" She stopped, and Oscar watched her wrestle with it, the taster's discipline versus the friend's honesty. "You might want to go *everywhere*, Oscar. You've earned everywhere. Nobody would blame you if the ambassador thing was just a polite way to leave."

And there it was: the trap of kindness, offering him the exit he'd have to refuse. Oscar grinned, because grinning was how he'd survived a childhood of feeding goats and dodging cooks, and some tools you kept sharp.

"Mara. I spent my whole life invisible in a place that wasn't anywhere, and the best day of it was the day a dying goat sneezed on me and I prayed, and something answered." He held up his healed hand. "I'm not leaving the place where my prayer got answered. I'm going to stand right here—at the exact spot where the answer came through—and I'm going to introduce it to everybody." He shrugged. "Besides. Somebody has to teach the outside world that our bread is better than theirs, and it should be someone who grew up carrying flour sacks."

Mara looked at him for a while. Then she did the thing she almost never did anymore, because eyes were always on her: she reached out and hugged him, quick and fierce, flour and all.

"The council votes tomorrow," she said into his shoulder. "Wren will cry. Don't tell her I said that."

"Wren won't cry. Wren will give a speech about the historical significance of goats."

"That too."

* * *

The vote was unanimous, which surprised no one except possibly Vess, who had prepared arguments and seemed genuinely disappointed not to deploy them. Wren did not cry. Wren gave a speech about the historical significance of goats, and then cried, and dared anyone to mention it.

Pell presented Oscar with credentials—an actual document, sealed with wax from the great shrine, written in Pell's smallest, most ceremonial handwriting—which began: *"Be it known to all persons, named or otherwise, that OSCAR, son of the village of [the village], keeper of goats, former borrower of names, is appointed First Ambassador of the Valley of ___..."*

The blank was deliberate. The valley's true name belonged to the land itself, and the land had not yet chosen to speak it to strangers. Mara had swallowed it so it could exist; speaking it was another matter, and the land, having waited four hundred years, was in no hurry. Pell had assured everyone this was normal. Pell assured everyone most unprecedented things were normal, in the tone of a man who had burned treaty pages and would spend the rest of his life footnoting his own guilt.

Oscar read the credential twice. "There's a blank where my home village should be too."

"Ah." Pell cleared his throat. "Yes. Well. Your village was never separately named—it shared the valley's erasure. When the land speaks, presumably it will speak all of you at once." He adjusted his spectacles, which were new, purchased from a merchant, and of which he was insufferably proud. "Until then, you are technically an ambassador from nowhere in particular. Which, given your upbringing, seems fitting."

"Pell, that's almost a joke."

"I contain multitudes. Now sign here, and here, and initial the margin note about goats."

* * *

His mother fed him for a week before he left, which was her way of signing documents.

On the last evening, the stag came to the garden again. Word had gotten around—word got around fast in a valley discovering gossip after four centuries of stasis—and half the village pretended not to watch from their windows as the god of the valley, guardian stag, eater of cabbages, stood in the Kavanaghs'-neighbors' plot while Oscar packed the last of his things.

It spoke to him the way it always had: not in words, but in weather. Tonight the weather meant *sit down a moment.*

Oscar sat on the garden wall. The stag lowered its great head, and between its antlers, in the space where a crown would sit if crowns grew on trees instead of heads, something shimmered.

"A gift," Oscar said. "Another one? I haven't finished installing the first one."

The shimmer resolved. It was small—a sound, really, more than an object. A name. His name. Not the borrowed whisper the god had lent him in the desperate days, the little luck-name that had aged his hands and broken them free of Vess's trap. Something older. Something that had been waiting.

Because that was the thing Oscar had understood only slowly, in the weeks since the naming, talking it through with Pell over endless tea: the god hadn't invented his borrowed name from nothing. Nothing came from nothing; Pell had a footnote on it. The name the god had whispered to a frightened kitchen boy over a dying goat had been drawn from the land itself—from the deep hoard of everything the valley had surrendered, everything sealed away behind the ancestors' bargain. Oscar's borrowed luck had been a splinter of the true name, loaned out like a tool from a locked shed.

And now the shed was open.

"This is mine," Oscar said slowly. "My actual name. The one my family would have had, if—" If the valley had ever been allowed one. If his ancestors hadn't traded names for safety. If the lock had never been locked.

The stag's ears tipped forward. *Yours,* said the weather. *Always was. Kept it safe. Kept everything safe.* And beneath that, so quiet Oscar nearly missed it, the apology underneath the apology: *Kept it so long I almost forgot whose it was.*

Oscar let the name settle into him. It didn't blaze or thunder. It fit the way his healed hands fit—easy, unremarkable, *his*. He tried it silently, the way you test a new boot, and found it had room to run in.

When he opened his eyes, the stag was watching him with those deep-water eyes, and Oscar laughed, wetly, helplessly.

"You know what's funny? I spent months terrified of you. Antlers and patience, lurking at the border stone, calling me 'little borrower.' I thought you were going to eat me like you eat cabbages." He slid off the wall. "And it turns out you were just holding my coat."

The stag huffed its laugh-huff. Then it turned its head toward the border, toward the road, toward the world, and the weather changed meaning: *Well? They're waiting. Go be somebody.*

* * *

He left at dawn, because ambassadors left at dawn; Pell had been very clear about the symbolism.

Half the village came to see him off, which was embarrassing, and Wren organized it, which explained the banner. Someone had painted FIRST AMBASSADOR across a bedsheet in letters that dripped, and someone else—almost certainly Cook—had pressed a basket of bread into his arms with instructions to "show those flatland people what rising dough looks like, they'll think it's sorcery, let them."

Mara walked with him the last stretch, to the border stone. She carried nothing, wore her plain gray clothes, and was nevertheless the most noticeable person in the valley, because that was her burden now, and she carried it the way she carried everything: like a tray of full cups across a moving floor.

"You'll write," she said.

"Every week. Whether there's news or not. Especially when there isn't—I'll invent some. 'Dear Mara, today a merchant's mule looked at me disrespectfully.'"

"The archives will treasure it."

They stopped at the stone. The crack through its middle had knit to a thin pale seam, like a scar that would always be there but would never hurt again. Beyond it, the road ran down through the green hills into the wide morning, and on the road, small in the distance, a cart was coming. Traders, probably. Or pilgrims. Or just somebody lost, which in the new valley was a temporary condition.

Oscar shifted his pack, checked his basket of bread, and found, annoyingly, that his eyes were doing the thing Wren's had done at the council vote.

"One more thing," Mara said. She reached into her pocket and pressed something into his palm: a spoon. Old, worn smooth, kitchen-plain. "From the palace set. The one I learned on." She closed his fingers around it. "So you can taste everything out there before you trust it. Old habit. Best habit."

Oscar looked at the spoon, and the bread, and the road, and the woman who had swallowed a country's name so it could say hello.

"Tell them we're coming," he said. "Tell them the valley's name is coming too, when it's ready. And tell them—" He stopped, and grinned, and meant it down to the ground. "Tell them breakfast is at dawn. There's always room."

He stepped over the seam in the stone, onto the road, into the world, and the world—vast, unmapped in places, badly mapped in others, full of people who had never heard of him and soon, he suspected, would—opened ahead of him like a page.

Behind him, faint on the wind, he heard the stag's laugh-huff, and the village cheering, and Wren starting a speech.

Ahead of him, the cart drew closer, and the driver leaned out, squinting, and called:

"Hallo there! Is this the valley? The one that wasn't here yesterday?"

Oscar smiled, set down his basket, and got to work.
