## 40. The Taster's Last Meal

The kitchen had never held so many people.

Mara stood at the great hearth at dawn, sleeves rolled to the elbow, and surveyed the chaos with the particular calm of a woman who had spent her whole life preparing for disasters and found that joy was simply another one of them. The long tables had been dragged out into the courtyard because no roof in the palace could hold three hundred souls, and the cooks—her cooks now, though she still thought of them as Pell's kitchen staff who had mutinied into her service—moved through the steam like a fleet of small determined ships.

"More butter," called Tamsin, who had once insisted a taster had no business touching a spoon and now wouldn't let anyone else near her stockpots.

"There is no more butter," said Aldous from the doorway, holding an empty churn like a man presenting evidence in a murder trial.

"There's always more butter."

"The cow disagrees."

"Then talk to the cow."

Mara laughed, and the laugh did what it always did now: it turned heads. Every head. A boy by the flour barrels looked up mid-sneeze; a scullery maid paused with a stack of bowls wobbling dangerously; even the cat—a fat orange tyrant who had appeared on the palace steps the week the valley got its name back and appointed itself Mara's personal supervisor—lifted its chin from the warm stones.

That was the price. That was the bargain she had swallowed along with the land's name, and some mornings it still caught in her throat like a fishbone. She could not be unseen. She could not be unheard. When she walked through the market, conversations hushed not out of fear but attention, every ear angled toward her the way sunflowers angle toward light. When she spoke, people listened as if the words might be instructions. Once, in the first terrible weeks, she had whispered *I'd like to be alone* in an empty corridor and heard, faintly, from three rooms away, someone say *the queen wants to be alone*—and then the whole palace had politely emptied itself around her like water finding its level.

She had learned, slowly, to aim her voice the way her mother had taught her to aim a spoon: precisely, sparingly, with full knowledge of consequences.

But this morning, being seen was the entire point.

* * *

The idea had come to her in the root cellars, of all places, three days after the coronation she'd refused and the abdication she hadn't.

She and Oscar had gone down to fetch winter stores—the ordinary kind of errand that still felt miraculous, errands, after a year of moons miscounting—and they had found Wren there, sitting cross-legged among the turnips, reading the carvings on the oldest wall by candlelight. The volunteers, the ancestors, the nameless ones who had chosen their erasure like choosing a coat against cold weather.

"They didn't do it to hide," Wren said, without looking up. She said things like that now, flat and certain, one leg healed and the other one hers again. "They did it to keep something safe. Us. They just forgot, over the generations, that they were the treasure too."

And Mara had thought: *we kept poison recipes for four hundred years. We kept antidotes. My mother fed me nightshade tinctures at seven years old so I would survive a world I couldn't see coming.* All that careful, ferocious love, expressed in the only language the sealed valley allowed—which was survival—and none of it ever served a meal. None of it ever fed anybody.

That night she wrote a menu. It took up eleven pages. It included, under protest from nobody because nobody dared protest, a dish her mother had made exactly once: barley stew with wild thyme and a scandalous amount of cream, cooked the evening before the poisons started, back when Mara was small enough to sit on the counter and steal thyme sprigs.

"You're going to feed the entire valley your childhood," Oscar said when he saw the list. He was peeling apples at the corner table, his hands moving with their old easy grace—healed, truly healed, since the stag had pressed its antlered brow to them in the courtyard and given back more than it had taken. He'd cried about that, privately, and pretended he hadn't, and everyone had politely pretended not to know.

"I'm going to feed them dinner," Mara said. "The childhood is free."

* * *

By noon the courtyard smelled like everything good the valley had ever produced.

There were complications, of course. There were always complications; Vess Corvane herself had taught them that a plan is just a disaster wearing nicer clothes. The complication today was named Hesper Dunn, aged nine, who had appointed herself Mara's assistant on the grounds that she wanted to learn poisoning.

"Not poisoning," Mara corrected, for perhaps the fortieth time, guiding the girl's hands as they stripped thyme from its stems. "Tasting. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"Poisoning is putting harm in food. Tasting is taking harm out—or finding it before it finds someone else." She held up a leaf between finger and thumb. "It's care, done with a very sharp eye."

Hesper considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My mum says you ate poison for the king for years and never died."

"My mother trained me for it from before I can remember. Fed me tiny amounts of everything dangerous until my body learned the difference between danger and dinner." She watched the girl absorb this. "It wasn't a kind thing, exactly. But it was love. Love comes out strange sometimes, in sealed valleys."

"Did it hurt?"

"Often."

"Do I have to?"

"No," Mara said, firmly enough that the word landed like a stone in a well. "Nobody has to do anything anymore. That's rather the whole point. If you want to learn tasting, I'll teach you properly—slowly, with honey first and hemlock theory last, and you may stop whenever you wish. That's the difference between my mother's school and mine."

Hesper beamed and chopped thyme with such violence that Mara quietly moved the good knives out of reach.

Across the courtyard, Pell sat wrapped in two blankets despite the mild autumn air, his leg still bound where the shrine siege had nearly taken it, presiding over the archive table like a benevolent ghost. He had insisted on attending, then insisted on working, then fallen asleep sitting up with a folio open on his knee, and Wren had covered him with a third blanket without breaking off her conversation about grain futures. His testament was finished now—all forty pages of it, dictated in his precise archivist's hand—and its final line, which he had made Mara read aloud back to him three times to check for errors, read: *"We were never victims. We were gardeners, and we mistook the wall for the garden. May the next generation plant better."*

He'd wept a little when she read it back. He claimed it was dust.

* * *

The king's chair sat empty at the high table, and it would sit empty forever, and that was right.

Mara had carried it out herself that morning, with Oscar's help, and set it at the head of the longest table facing the whole courtyard—not as a throne but as a memory. On its seat lay the crown, the old key-crown, retired at last from its career of locking things. Pell had suggested a museum. Vess—who walked with a cane now, half her hair gone white where her hungry god had burned through her, pardoned and prickly and doing the accounting work of the new council with terrifying competence—had suggested melting it down for plowshares. In the end they had compromised: the crown stayed on the chair, visible to everyone, belonging to no one, a reminder that power was a tool and tools should sleep when the work was done.

People touched it as they passed, sometimes. Just briefly. A fingertip to the metal, like touching a saint's foot in an old story. Mara understood. She'd spent her life invisible next to that thing, and now it was the object in the room that couldn't catch anyone's eye at all. There was a lesson in that which she suspected she'd be unpacking for decades.

"Mara." Vess appeared at her elbow with a ledger under one arm and a cup of mulled cider in her hand, looking, as always, like a woman who had audited the party and found it acceptable. "The council wanted me to ask whether you'd accept a seat. Again. Formally. They've drawn up the papers."

"Vess."

"I told them you'd refuse."

"You told them I'd refuse?"

"I told them you'd refuse dramatically, make a speech about the dignity of kitchens, and then do half the work anyway, unpaid, out of guilt." She sipped her cider. "Am I wrong?"

Mara opened her mouth. Closed it. "I hate how well you know me."

"It's the one skill the hunger god left me with," Vess said, and there it was—the flicker of grief under the dryness, quickly banked. She was better these days. She was worse some days. The council had pardoned her because Wren had argued loudest that Vess was the only person alive who fully understood what it cost to be eaten by ambition, and the valley needed someone who knew the price tags. "For what it's worth—I'm glad you're cooking instead. Ruling tastes worse than anything you ever tested for that sad old man. I would know. I tried it for six weeks."

"How is the council, honestly?"

"Bickering. Competently. Which is the most one can ask of any government anywhere." Vess glanced at the empty chair, and softened by one degree, which for Vess was practically an embrace. "He'd have liked this, I think. The fog-king. Lucid for one year at the end, and mostly confused, but he liked feeding people. Why do you think he kept a kitchen that size for a kingdom of nothing?"

Mara hadn't known that. She filed it away with all the other posthumous gifts the old man kept leaving her.

* * *

At sundown, she began the tasting.

Not for poison. For memory.

She stood at the head table with a clean spoon and a bowl of the barley stew, and the courtyard went quiet—not commanded quiet, not the hush of a court, but the collective leaning-in of people who had learned, over one strange and terrible year, that moments mattered and ought to be attended.

"This recipe belonged to my mother," she said, aiming her voice carefully, letting it carry because it must. "Sorcha Kavanagh. You'll find her in the new histories, in the chapter about the tasters—she fed her daughter poison for fifteen years so that her daughter would live. That was true, and it was love, and it hurt, and we are done pretending those three things cancel each other out." She lifted the spoon. "But before all that, she made this stew. Exactly once. And tonight I taste it for her—not to check it for danger, but to check it for her. To see if she's in it. That's what a taster does now. We taste for the people who are gone."

She ate.

Thyme. Cream. Barley cooked soft, the way her mother had cooked it, impatient with firmness, believing food should yield. And underneath—there, faint as a fingerprint on a window—something that was not an ingredient at all. Something that tasted like a kitchen with the shutters closed against a valley that didn't exist, like a woman humming off-key, like small fingers stealing herbs from the cutting board while a mother pretended not to notice.

"Oh," Mara said, and her eyes stung, and three hundred people watched her cry without looking away, and it was—she was surprised to find—not unbearable. Being witnessed. Even this. Especially this.

"She's in it," she reported, when she could speak. "Everyone eat."

The courtyard exhaled into motion and noise and the clatter of bowls.

* * *

She tasted seventeen dishes that evening.

The honey cakes, for Old Marta the beekeeper, dead eight years, whose hives had been the first thing to bloom when the seal cracked—she was in them, in the particular smokiness of the comb. The river trout, for the fishermen who had never fished the border waters for fear of what lived past the stones, and who had come back last spring with nets full and faces full of disbelief. The black bread for the nameless generations, risen with a starter older than the treaty, older than the erasure, a yeast culture passed hand to hand through four hundred years of forgetting—the one living thing in the valley that had never lost its name, because no one had thought to take it.

Wren brought her a plate of the lame girl's favorite childhood supper—eggs in butter, plain as rain—and demanded, "Taste for me. Tell me if my mum's in it. She died the winter my leg was bad, and I never—" Her jaw worked. "I never thanked her for carrying me."

Mara tasted. "She's in it," she said gently. "In the patience. Eggs in butter can't be rushed or they burn. Somebody cooked yours slow."

Wren nodded hard and went away blinking.

Oscar saved her a seat when her legs finally gave out, well past dark, and put a cup of cider in her hands, and did not say anything, which was his best quality among many.

"Seventeen dishes," he said eventually. "Any of them poisoned?"

"One. The venison pie. Tamsin's nephew got creative with the wild mushrooms."

"Tamsin's going to be insufferable about that forever."

"Tamsin is *already* insufferable about that forever. She caught it before I did, technically. First cook in valley history to out-taste the royal taster." Mara sipped her cider. "I've decided to consider it a legacy rather than an embarrassment."

Oscar laughed, and his hands around his own cup were steady and young, and somewhere beyond the border stones—no longer border stones now, just interesting rocks with moss on them—a great stag moved through the trees that everyone swore had antlers like winter branches, and everyone left a share of the harvest in the high field for it, and no one prayed to it anymore, because it had asked them, through whatever means a freed land asks, to please stop asking and start thanking. The valley was learning the difference. Slowly. Like a patient waking from a long sleep, remembering how its own limbs worked.

"Tomorrow," Oscar said, "you sail east. Ambassador stuff. My stuff, I mean—except you're coming to see me off, and everyone knows it, and half the valley is coming, and you're going to be *seen* seeing me off, and you're going to hate it."

"I'm going to love it," Mara said. "That's the horrible part. I'm going to stand there in front of four hundred people and be completely visible, and it's going to be fine, and I will never forgive any of you."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

Much later—when the fires had burned down to amber and the courtyard held only the stubborn, the sweethearts, and the cat—Mara stood alone at the head table with one last spoonful in a bowl that had gone cold.

It was nothing. A scrap of everything, really: a bit of stew, a crumb of cake, a shred of trout, a smear of honey. A composite. An ending.

She looked out over her valley—her *named*, mapped, remembered, impossible valley—and raised the spoon to the darkness, to the fires, to the sleeping houses and the watching stag and the ancestors carved in cellar walls and the reader, wherever you are, whoever you are, whoever you're about to become:

"To the nameless," she said softly, "who kept us. To the named, who came after. Eat well. Taste everything. And when love comes out strange—"

She smiled, and ate the last bite, and found that it was good.

"—it's still love."
