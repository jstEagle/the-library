## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The luck-knots sold best after dark, when the market lamps caught their copper thread and made it look like something more than braided horsehair and wishful thinking.

Silas had learned that in his first year at Vellamark. People didn't buy charms in daylight; daylight was for honest work and honest prices. But at night, under the paper lanterns strung along the Saltrow, with the harbor breathing its cold salt breath between the stalls, people wanted to believe a knot could hold their luck from unraveling. And Silas wanted—needed—to sell them exactly that much belief and not one drop more.

"Three coppers," he said to the woman now leaning over his table, turning the knot in her cracked fingers. She had a fishwife's forearms and a widow's eyes, and she'd been standing there long enough that he knew she wasn't shopping so much as hiding.

"Does it work?" she asked. They always asked.

"It works as well as you need it to," Silas said, which was his standard answer, and a true one in a way he could never explain. The knots were nothing. Horsehair, copper wire, a word spoken over them that was just a word, shaped like a blessing but empty as a bottle. He had spent seven years perfecting the art of selling nothing, because nothing was safe, and safety was the only luxury a swordspeaker had left.

But his hands—the hands doing the harmless braiding—were not harmless. Beneath the scars that crisscrossed his knuckles like old riverbeds, something still lived. Something warm. On cold nights it woke in his palms like a second pulse, and he would sit alone in his rented room above the chandler's shop and press his hands flat against the floorboards until the heat sank away into the wood, into the city, into nowhere, because letting it rise was how men ended up ash on the Warden pyres.

The fishwife paid her three coppers. She tied the knot around her wrist, said a small prayer to no particular saint, and moved off into the lantern light, and Silas watched her go with the peculiar ache he felt every time he sold a piece of his own silence.

The Saltrow night market ran the length of the harbor wall: forty stalls under forty lanterns, selling smoked eel and secondhand boots, tin whistles and tide charts, and—in one narrow corner stall with a striped awning gone gray from seven years of sea air—charms of absolutely no power whatsoever. Silas liked the corner. Corners were where you put things you didn't want looked at too closely.

He was reaching for another length of copper wire when the crowd parted the way crowds do when someone is coming through them without any intention of being polite about it.

She was small, sharp-elbowed, and moving fast—a woman in a scholar's coat that had once been good wool and was now mostly patches and ink stains, hair escaping its braid in gray-streaked wisps, clutching a book against her chest the way other people clutched children or knives. She came down the Saltrow like weather, shouldering past an eel-seller, ducking under a lantern string, and stopped dead in front of Silas's stall with the finality of a ship dropping anchor.

"You," she said.

Silas looked up at her with the flat, pleasant blankness he'd polished over seven years. "Charms are three coppers. Luck-knots, hearth-wisps, traveler's thumbs. No refunds."

"I don't want a charm." Her voice dropped, and her eyes—dark, quick, unblinking—did a slow circuit of the market before coming back to him. "I want the man whose name is written in this book."

Something cold slid down Silas's spine and settled in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Lots of names in books," he said. "None of them mine."

"Nakamura." She said it quietly, but she said it whole, and the sound of it went through Silas like a blade finding the gap in armor. Not Silas. Not the name he'd worn for seven years like a borrowed coat. *Nakamura*—the name of the order, the name spoken in oath-halls and burned into the Warden ledgers, the name of ninety-one dead men and women and one coward who'd run. "Silas Nakamura. Last oath-bound swordspeaker of the Ember Covenant. Born under the third moon of harvest, sworn at fourteen, fled the Night of Severance—" Her voice caught, not with pity but with fury, as if the words offended her personally. "—and listed as deceased by the Wardens' own rolls, which is a lie I can prove."

The market noise seemed to recede. Silas became very aware of the lantern above his stall, of the shadows it threw, of the three Warden patrols that walked the Saltrow on any given night, of the fact that his hands, resting on the table, had begun—traitorously, impossibly—to warm.

"Madam," he said softly, "you're going to get us both burned alive."

"Not if you listen." She set the book down on his table.

It was a heavy thing, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, its corners eaten to soft gray pulp. Ash had worked itself into the grain of the cover so deeply that no amount of cleaning would ever remove it; the book looked less like an object than like something that had survived a fire by becoming partly fire itself. Stamped into the leather, nearly worn away, was a sigil Silas hadn't seen in seven years and would know blindfolded, drunk, dying: the interlocked flame-and-moon of the Ember Covenant.

His mouth went dry. "Where did you get that?"

"The Undercroft archive. Third sublevel, sealed stacks, behind a false wall the archivists forgot about when they reorganized after the Purge." She said it the way other scholars recited their credentials. "My name is Wren Corvane. I was a reader there for eleven years, until I was dismissed six months ago for asking questions about exactly this book. I have spent those six months translating it." She leaned across the table, close enough that Silas could smell lamp oil and dust and something faintly scorched clinging to her. "And I am here tonight, at this hour, at this stall, because the book told me to be."

"Wren Corvane." He tested the name and found he disliked it already, which was reassuring. Names you liked got you killed. "You realize what you're describing is a death sentence. For both of us. Possession of covenant texts—"

"—is punishable by burning, yes, I've read the statutes, I helped catalog them." She flipped the book open with fingers that trembled only slightly, and Silas hated himself for noticing. "Look at it. Look at the date."

Against every instinct honed over seven careful years, Silas looked.

The page was vellum, browned with age, covered in a script he recognized with a sick lurch: high covenant hand, the flowing ember-script that only sworn speakers had been permitted to read aloud. But beneath it, in a different ink, a different hand had added a translation—and a date.

*On the turn of the moon when autumn first bites the harbor,* the translation read, *the Ward shall wake, and the last speaker shall be called home by his true name.*

Below it, in the same later hand, someone had written today's date.

Not approximately. Not the right season, the right month. The exact date. Tonight.

"This is a forgery," Silas said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "A cruel one. Or a trap. Warden informants plant books like this—"

"I forged nothing. I translated everything. There's a difference, and the difference is eleven years of my life and my entire reputation." Wren's jaw tightened. "Do you think I *wanted* this? Do you think I stood in front of the archive tribunal and let them strip my reader's chain and blacken my name because I enjoy ruin? I followed the text. That's all I've ever done. The text says the last speaker sells small charms in a harbor market, and I have spent four months walking the Saltrow every night looking at every face, and two nights ago I saw your hands move while you braided a knot, and I saw the copper wire bend toward your fingers like it *liked* you."

Silas's hands curled closed.

"That," he said, "was a trick of the light."

"Was it." It wasn't quite a question. She turned a page, and the vellum crackled like a small fire. "There's more. Listen." And before he could stop her, she began to read aloud—quietly, but aloud, in the open market, under the lanterns:

"*When the Ward ignites blue-white at moonrise, the door beneath the moon opens, and what was broken must be mended before the thirteenth turn, or both realms seal forever.*"

"Stop." Silas reached across the table and closed his hand over the page—not grabbing it, just pressing it shut, feeling the strange warmth of the vellum against his palm, warmth like the warmth in his own hands, and that frightened him more than the Wardens ever had. "Stop. Please."

For the first time since she'd arrived, Wren Corvane went still. She looked at his hand on the book, then up at his face, and whatever she saw there made her voice gentler, which somehow made it worse.

"You've known," she said. "All these years. You've known something was coming."

"I've known nothing." He withdrew his hand. "I've *hoped*. There's a difference, and the difference is staying alive."

"Is that what you call this?" She gestured—at the stall, the gray awning, the table of pretty useless knots. "Seven years selling horsehair to dockworkers. Hiding in a corner. You're not alive, Nakamura. You're shelved."

The words landed harder than they should have, precisely because they were true, and Silas felt something in his chest shift—a hairline crack in a wall he'd built stone by careful stone since the night he'd run and left ninety-one people to burn.

"You don't know anything about me," he said quietly.

"I know what the book says about you." She didn't flinch from his stare. "'*The last speaker will be afraid. Let him be afraid. Fear kept him living when courage burned the rest.*'" She paused. "Whoever wrote that knew you. Knew you well enough to forgive you in advance. And you're sitting here telling me it's a forgery."

Above the harbor, through the gap between two warehouse roofs, Silas could see the sky. Clear. Cold. The first bite of autumn in the wind off the water, exactly as the page had said. And low on the horizon, pale as a fingernail paring, the moon beginning its climb toward moonrise.

His hands were warm. Warmer than they should be. Warmer than they'd been in seven years.

"Take the book back," he said. "Go home. Lose yourself in some other archive. Forget my name—I'm begging you, forget it, because the moment you speak it again in public, we're both kindling."

"No," Wren said.

Silas stared at her. In seven years, no one had said *no* to him with such simple, infuriating calm. Customers haggled. Wardens threatened. Neighbors nodded and looked away. Nobody simply, flatly refused.

"Excuse me?"

"I said no. I'm not leaving, and I'm not taking it back, and you can't make me." She sat down on the stool across from his table—the customer's stool, the one no one ever sat in for long—and folded her ink-stained hands atop the blood-colored book like a scholar settling in for a long night in the stacks. "I have been dismissed, disgraced, disowned by my colleagues, and followed twice by men I'm fairly sure report to Magistrate Vey. My reputation is ash, my savings are nearly ash, and my future is, at best, ash. I have nothing left to lose except the truth, and I will sit in this chair until moonrise if that's what it takes for you to stop lying to yourself."

"You'll get us both arrested."

"Then we'll be arrested together. At least it will be interesting."

"It will be a *pyre*—"

"Then it will be a warm evening, and I'll finally be done being cold." Her eyes glittered, and Silas realized with dawning horror that she was not bluffing. This was not a woman who could be argued out of anything. This was a woman who had already lost everything except her purpose, and purpose, he remembered—the memory arriving like a bruise—was exactly what the old order used to say about people who couldn't be threatened, only killed.

He looked at the book. The sigil stamped in the leather. The date written in ash-brown ink. The moon climbing the sky like it had somewhere to be.

"Why tonight?" he asked, and hated how much it sounded like surrender. "If your book is real—if any of this is real—why does it matter *tonight*?"

Wren's expression shifted. The defiance stayed, but beneath it something else surfaced—something that looked, uncomfortably, like fear finally allowed to show its face.

"Because the prophecy doesn't end with the Ward waking," she said. "I translated the last page yesterday. I haven't told anyone. I came straight here." She drew a breath. "It says that when the door opens, it opens for *him*—for the last speaker—and that what waits on the other side remembers what his order did. All of it. And it has been waiting a hundred years to say so."

The lantern above the stall flickered, though there was no wind.

Silas looked down at his hands. Scarred. Careful. Seven years of harmlessness wrapped around a coal that had never gone out, no matter how hard he'd pressed it into the floorboards, no matter how far he'd run.

Somewhere out over the harbor, faint as a struck match, the first blue-white shimmer touched the horizon where the Ember Ward stood.

Wren saw it too. Her breath caught. "That's early," she whispered. "Moonrise isn't for another quarter hour. It shouldn't—"

The shimmer brightened.

Every lantern in the Saltrow guttered at once, and the market's noise dropped away as forty vendors and two hundred customers turned, as one body, to look at the impossible light blooming over the water.

Silas stood up so fast his stool went over.

"Take the book," he said, shoving it back into Wren's arms. "Walk away. Don't run—running draws eyes. Just walk, and don't look back, and lose that thing in the harbor before—"

The Ward flared.

Blue-white light poured over the rooftops like a tide, and Silas felt it hit him—not on his skin, but *under* it, in the scarred hollows of his hands, in the place where the old gift lived coiled and patient. It pulled. Not gently. Not like a summons.

Like a hook.

The world tilted. Lanterns stretched into ribbons of light. Wren was shouting his name—his *true* name, curse her, curse the book, curse the hundred-year-dead hand that had written it—and the ground beneath the Saltrow went soft as ash, and the last thing Silas saw before the street dissolved beneath him was Wren Corvane reaching across the falling market to grab his wrist with both ink-stained hands, refusing, even now, even at the end of the world, to let go.

Then the night opened like a book, and they fell between its pages.
