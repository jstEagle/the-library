## 4. The Orchard of Small Fires

The trees were burning, and none of them were consumed.

Silas stood at the edge of the ash orchard with soot settling on his shoulders like a blessing he hadn't asked for, and watched the flames move through the branches the way wind moves through wheat—gently, in waves, without hunger. The fire was white at its heart and gold at its edges, and where it touched the fruit—small round things like lanterns gone soft—it did not char them but lit them from within, until every tree seemed strung with a thousand patient candles.

He had seen burning before. He had made burning before. Seven years ago, on a night he still could not name aloud, he had stood in a circle of his brothers and sisters while the world's oldest promise came apart in their hands, and everything after that had smelled of smoke.

But this was not that smoke. This smoke smelled of bread baking. Of cedar chests opened after long winters. Of the inside of a warm coat.

"Silas." Wren's voice came from somewhere behind him, thin and strange, as if it had traveled a long way to reach him. "Tell me you're seeing this too."

"I'm seeing it."

"Good. Because if I'm mad, I'd rather we be mad together. It's less embarrassing at funerals."

He turned. She was on her knees in the ash, which was not gray here but silver, fine as flour, and she had her satchel open and was already reaching for her notebook with the blind instinct of a woman who had once believed the archive would save her life. Her hair had come loose from its knot. There was a smear of ash across one cheekbone like a war paint applied by a careless god.

Above them, where the sky should have been, hung the moon—but wrong, or rather *right* in a way no moon over Vellamark had ever been right. It filled half the heavens, close enough that Silas felt he could have read the small print of its craters, and it was not white but the color of an ember breathing: deep red at the rim, brightening to gold at its heart. Its light fell through the burning branches and made moving lace on the ground.

"The Undermoon," Silas said. The word came out of him unbidden, in the old cadence, the oath-cadence, and he clamped his jaw shut as if he could stuff the words back in. But Wren heard it. He saw her hear it.

"You know this place."

"I know *of* it." He looked away first. "Every swordspeaker knew of it. We were told stories when we took our vows. A realm beneath the moon, where the covenant was kept. Where the emberkin tended the fires that magic comes from." His scarred hands curled at his sides. "We were told we would never see it. That seeing it was for the end of things."

"And yet," Wren said, gesturing at the orchard, the impossible moon, the whole burning gentle world, "here we are. At the end of things, presumably."

"Presumably."

They walked into the trees.

The heat was nothing like fire's heat. It was the warmth of a hand held near yours without touching—the warmth of company—and it sank through Silas's travel-worn coat and into muscles he had not realized were knotted since the Ward ignited. The ash underfoot gave slightly, like fresh snow, and each footprint they left filled itself in behind them, so that the orchard bore no record of their passing. As if the ground itself were being polite. Or as if the ground did not want anything here to be followed.

The trees grew in long rows, ancient and deliberate, and between them hung what Silas at first took for cobwebs—threads of pale light strung branch to branch—and then understood were names. Not written. *Sung.* Each thread hummed faintly at a different pitch, and when he passed close beneath one, the sound entered his chest and became briefly a memory that wasn't his: a woman laughing in a kitchen; a child counting stars; two hands clasped over a table while something important was promised.

"Covenant-threads," he whispered. "They're covenant-threads. Each one is—" He stopped, because saying it plainly felt like trespassing.

"Each one is a promise someone kept," Wren finished softly. She had stopped walking. Her hand hovered beneath one of the threads, not quite touching, the way you hover your palm over a candle flame to prove to yourself it's real. "There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands." Her voice did something complicated. "And they're all going out."

She was right. Now that she said it, Silas saw it everywhere: threads burned dim, threads burned to nothing, and here and there a thread snapped with a sound like a distant door closing, and the little light of it drifted down through the ash and went dark before it landed. The orchard was dying the way a great choir dies—not all at once, but voice by voice by voice, until the silence where the music used to be becomes unbearable precisely because it is so quiet.

Somewhere ahead, something was crying.

It was not a dramatic crying. It was the crying of someone who has been crying for a very long time and has given up on doing it well—hiccupping, sniffling, occasionally stopping altogether as if embarrassed, then starting again. It echoed strangely between the burning rows, coming from everywhere and nowhere, and Wren looked at Silas and Silas looked at Wren, and neither of them said *should we*, because there was never really a question. Whatever else the Undermoon was, whoever wept in it was alone, and Silas Nakamura had spent seven years knowing exactly what alone meant.

They found her three rows deeper, sitting in the crook of a tree's roots as if the roots had grown around her like cupped hands.

She was small. That was the first thing—a child's size, though nothing else about her was a child's. Her skin was the gray-gold of banked coals, and cracks of light moved slowly beneath it, opening and closing like slow eyes. Her hair was actual flame, but flame at its most tired, the blue-hearted kind that burns low in a stove at the end of the night. She wore a dress that might have been woven from the same covenant-threads that hung in the branches, and it flickered at the hem, unraveling and reweaving itself in a rhythm like breathing. Around her feet, a ring of tiny fires burned in the ash—dozens of them, each no larger than a coin, each a different color—and as they approached, the small fires turned toward the strangers the way flowers turn toward sun.

The emberkin looked up. Her face was smudged with grief the way Wren's cheek was smudged with ash, and her eyes were enormous and ember-orange and utterly wrecked, and when she saw them she did not scream or flee or flare into some guardian shape. She simply stared, with the naked, hopeless hope of someone who has wished for company so long that company has become a wound.

"You're late," she said.

Silas blinked. "I—beg pardon?"

"You're *late*." She wiped her nose with the back of a hand that glowed faintly where her tears touched it, leaving little cooling tracks of light. "A hundred years late. A hundred years and thirteen days, if you count the way I count, which nobody does, which is why I do the counting." She sniffed again. "I've been waiting at this tree since the breaking. Mother said someone would come. Mother said the oath would call its keepers home. And I sat here, and I counted, and the trees went out one by one, and *nobody came*." Her voice cracked down the middle, and the ring of small fires around her flared in sympathy, then guttered. "Until now. When it hardly matters anymore. Which is very rude, if you want my opinion. It's very rude to arrive when the tea's gone cold and the house is falling down and everyone you loved is mostly smoke."

Wren crouched down slowly, the way you approach a frightened animal—or a frightened child, which amounts to the same care. "What's your name?" she asked gently.

The emberkin studied her for a long moment, light pulsing in her seams. "Bramblewick," she said at last, as if testing whether the name still fit. "I'm Bramblewick. I keep the small fires. The big ones are for the queen and the wardens-of-the-flame and all the important somebodies, but the small ones are mine. Every little fire a mortal ever lit on purpose—on a hearth, in a lantern, at a bedside for a scared child—that's mine to tend. That was mine to tend." She looked out at the dimming orchard, and her small fists closed in her lap. "Was."

"I'm Wren," Wren said. "Wren Corvane. And this is Silas."

At the name, Bramblewick went very still. The cracks of light beneath her skin slowed, then brightened, then brightened more, until she was nearly too radiant to look at, and the nearest covenant-thread above them began to hum a chord Silas felt in his back teeth.

"*Silas*," she repeated. "Silas who speaks to swords. Silas of the Order of the Ember Oath." She rose off the roots, drifting an inch above the ash, and the small fires rose with her like an honor guard. "You came. You actually—you *came*." And then her face crumpled, and the hope curdled into something older and sharper, and the temperature around them dropped even as the light rose. "You came a hundred years too late, swordspeaker. Do you know what happened while you were selling your little charms and keeping your little head down? Do you know what your order *did*?"

"Bramblewick—" Silas started.

"They broke it!" The word came out of her like a spark thrown from a forge, and every tree in earshot flared white. "The covenant! The oldest thing either world ever made! They took the oath-stone—the heart of it, the First Shard, the piece that remembers why any of this exists—and they *shattered* it, and the pieces fell out of the world like teeth, and the fire went out of the sharing, and my people started to fade, and the mortals forgot wonder, and a man in a fine coat laughed and laughed and *laughed*—" She was crying again now, hard, ugly, honest crying, and the small fires around her were dying one by one, winking out like eyes closing. "And I waited. I *waited*. Because mother said the oath would call its keepers home, and I believed her, and believing things is what I'm *for*."

The last of the small fires went dark.

In the sudden gloom, Bramblewick hung in the air, translucent at her edges, and Silas saw—with a lurch that had nothing to do with the fall through the Ward—that he could see the burning branches of the tree *through* her shoulder.

*Fading,* he thought. *She's fading.*

And seven years of careful, cowardly silence rose up in him all at once, and he found—to his genuine astonishment—that he could not swallow it back down.

"Yes," he said. His voice came out rough. "Yes, we broke it. My order broke it. I was there." Wren's head turned sharply toward him; he kept his eyes on the emberkin. "I was there on the Night of Severance, and when the stone shattered, I ran. I didn't fight. I didn't try to mend anything. I ran, and I hid, and I let them burn my brothers and sisters on the magistrate's pyres while I sold luck-knots to dockworkers and told myself staying small was the same as staying good." He knelt, slowly, in the silver ash, until his eyes were level with hers. "So you're right. You're completely right. We're late, and it's our fault, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise."

The orchard had gone very quiet. Even the covenant-threads seemed to lean in.

Bramblewick descended until her bare feet touched the ash. She looked at him for a long, long time—long enough that Wren shifted uneasily, long enough that somewhere overhead another thread snapped and its little light fell and died—and then she crossed the space between them and pressed one small coal-warm hand flat against Silas's chest, over his heart.

The warmth went through him like water through dry ground.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you're *still lit*. Under all that ash and hiding, you're still lit." She pulled her hand back and regarded it, as if it had told her something. "That's why the Ward dragged you here, silly. The covenant doesn't call the perfect. It calls the *lit*. It always has." She sniffed, scrubbed her face with both sleeves, and when she looked up again her enormous ember eyes had steadied into something that was not forgiveness, not yet, but was perhaps its front porch. "Well. Late is late, but late is better than never, and never is what I was expecting, so on balance I suppose I'm pleased to meet you."

"That's," Wren began, "remarkably gracious, considering—"

"It's not gracious, it's *arithmetic*." Bramblewick turned and led them deeper into the orchard, and the small fires relit themselves around her feet as she walked, one by one, like a congregation standing back up. "Come on. There's things you need to know, and we haven't got long, and when I say we haven't got long I mean it in the way that will make your mortal stomachs drop through your knees, so hold onto something."

* * *

She brought them to the heart of the orchard, where the trees grew in a ring around a clearing, and in the clearing stood a well.

Or what had been a well. It was built of black stone shot through with veins of gold, older than any masonry Silas had ever seen, and it rose from the ash to about waist height—and it was empty. Bone-dry. Cracked down the middle like a dropped bell. At its base, arranged in a careful circle, lay thirteen stones, and twelve of them were cold and dark.

The thirteenth glowed.

It was faint—a coal's worth of light, a heartbeat's worth—but it pulsed, slow and steady, and with each pulse Silas felt a tug behind his sternum, delicate and insistent, like a fisherman testing a line.

"This is the Moonwell," Bramblewick said. Her voice had changed—quieter now, older, the voice of a spirit remembering she was ancient. "Once, it was full. Once, it poured the covenant between the worlds the way a river pours between banks—magic flowing to mortals, memory flowing to us, back and forth, forever, because the oath bound it to flow and forbade it to pool. No one hoarded. No one starved. That was the whole of the law, and it fit in a song." She laid her small hand on the well's cracked rim. "Then your order broke the stone, and the river stopped, and all that's left is what pooled in the crack. One turn of the moon's worth. Look."

She pointed up, past the burning canopy, to the vast ember moon.

"See how it sits? It isn't moving. In the old days it crossed this sky in a single night and returned, thirteen turns, always thirteen, and each turn fed the well. Now it hangs where it stalled on the night of the breaking, and it moves only when a shard is restored to the stone. One shard, one turn. The shard you seek first will give it strength enough to begin again." She looked at them both, and the light in her seams dimmed to something almost shy. "But here is the arithmetic, and I'm sorry for it. If the moon completes its thirteenth turn—if all the shards are gathered and the oath reforged within thirteen turns—then the covenant wakes, and the realms stay open, and everything broken begins to mend."

"And if it doesn't?" Wren asked, though Silas could see she already knew. He could see it in the way she'd gone white.

"If the thirteenth turn ends and the stone is still broken," Bramblewick said simply, "the moon finishes crossing and does not return. The realms seal. Forever. No doors, no dreams, no small fires lit on purpose ever finding their way to me again." She smiled, and it was the worst smile Silas had ever seen, brave and tiny and cracking at the edges like her skin. "Your world goes on without wonder. Mine goes out without complaint. And I sit under this tree until there isn't enough of me left to sit."

The silence that followed was total. Even the covenant-threads held their hum.

Silas looked at the well—at the single living stone among its twelve dead siblings—and felt the tug behind his sternum strengthen, patient and certain, like something that had been waiting for him specifically and was not inclined to negotiate.

"Thirteen turns," he said. "How long is a turn?"

"A night and a day and a night, as the moon reckons it. Your scholars would call it roughly three of your weeks per turn, though time runs soft between the worlds." Bramblewick tilted her flaming head. "Why?"

Because, Silas thought, because that's the better part of a year, because Vellamark has Wardens on every bridge and a magistrate with a hundred years of stolen fire in his veins, because Wren Corvane is already writing in her notebook like a woman taking dictation from doom—

"Where," he heard himself say, in the old oath-cadence he had sworn off and could not seem to put down, "is the First Shard?"

Bramblewick's whole body brightened. The small fires leapt. Somewhere in the high branches, a covenant-thread that had been silent for a century gave a single clear note, like a struck glass.

"Drowned," she said, with terrible gentleness. "It fell into your world, into the harbor of your grey city, into a chapel your people built and then let the sea take. Saint Ovelun's, they called it, before the water came in and the prayers went out. It lies in the dark below the tide, still singing, still waiting." She reached out and touched the back of Silas's scarred hand, and her fingers were warm as a hearthstone. "The moon-door to reach it opens at low tide, three nights hence. So rest, swordspeaker. Eat, if you can eat ash-bread. Sleep under the burning trees, which are safe, I promise, safer than anywhere you've slept in seven years." Her ember eyes flicked to Wren, then back. "Because starting three nights from now, the two of you are going to need every hour of every turn—and the moon, at last, is beginning to move."

And far above them, with a groan like a hinge unused for a hundred years, the great ember moon shuddered—and took its first step across the sky.
