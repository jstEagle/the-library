## 6. Thirteen Turns, One Road

The ash orchard did not sleep, but it dreamed. Silas felt it as he sat with his back against a burning tree that gave no heat—felt the slow pulse beneath the ground like a heart remembering its rhythm. The flames in the branches moved the way candleflames move when someone is singing softly in another room: attentive, almost listening.

Bramblewick had been gone for what felt like an hour, though time here was a rumor. She had said she was going to *ask the roots*, and Wren had made a small strangled sound at that, and now they waited among the trees with nothing to do but be strangers to each other in a dying world.

Wren sat cross-legged on the ash-soft ground with her prophecy book open on her knees, transcribing by the light of the unconsuming fire. Her pen scratched. She had inked her fingers to the second knuckle and hadn't noticed. There was a smudge of it on her cheekbone too, high up, where she'd pushed her hair back—a dark comma against pale skin—and Silas found himself looking at it longer than was polite or wise.

"You're staring," she said, without lifting her head.

"I'm deciding whether you're mad," he said. "It's taking longer than I expected."

"Because the evidence keeps contradicting itself." She turned a page. "A sane man doesn't sell luck-knots for seven years while carrying swordspeech in his hands. A madman wouldn't have hidden it that well."

He looked down at his own hands. In this light they seemed ordinary—knuckles scarred from work, nails bitten, the calluses of a man who'd spent his youth gripping a hilt and his middle age gripping nothing. But he knew what lived under the skin. He could feel it tonight more than usual, the way you feel an old break before rain: the words coiled in him like a drawn bow, the syllables that could unmake locks and knit flesh and, once, long ago, had called fire out of stone.

"Bramblewick said thirteen turns," he said, because saying anything else felt dangerous.

"Thirteen turns of the moon until both realms seal forever." Wren's pen stopped. "Which means we have roughly one lunar month to find shards scattered who knows where, reforge a covenant your order broke, and avoid the Wardens who would burn us both if they knew what we are." She finally looked up. "I've read a great many doom-prophecies, Silas Nakamura. Yours is unusually efficient."

"It's not mine."

"No," she said quietly. "That's rather the point, isn't it? It names you anyway."

Before he could answer—the old flinch rising in him, the reflex of a man who had spent seven years being no one on purpose—the orchard shivered. Not wind. The trees leaned all together, like a crowd turning to look, and Bramblewick came pelting between their trunks with her feet leaving little scorch-blossoms on the ash, her hair streaming behind her like a banner of embers.

She skidded to a stop in front of them, breathless in a way that spirits perhaps shouldn't need to be, and her eyes were enormous and wet and shining.

"The roots remember," she said. "The roots always remember, even when nobody else does." She held up one small hand, and in her palm lay something that was not quite a leaf and not quite a coal: a sliver of grey stone veined through with faint gold, warm enough that the air above it shimmered. "This is a piece of the map. Or a piece of the key. Or a piece of the door. The roots aren't very precise, they're roots."

Silas went still. "What is it?"

"A shard-song," Bramblewick said. "When the covenant broke, it didn't die—it shattered. Thirteen pieces, because everything about the oath is thirteen. They fell into both worlds. Some are here, in the Undermoon, sleeping in places that used to be loved. Some fell through into yours." She closed her fingers around the sliver and it sank into her palm like a pebble into water. "The First Shard is in Vellamark. Your city. Under your harbor."

Wren was already writing. "Where under the harbor?"

"In the drowned chapel of Saint Ovelun." Bramblewick's voice went soft and strange, the way voices go when they're quoting something older than themselves. "Before the harbor was a harbor, there was a hill where people came to swear things. Then the sea took the hill, and the chapel stood anyway, because chapels are stubborn. The Wardens think it's a ruin. The fisherfolk think it's haunted. Both are wrong. It's *waiting*."

"Drowned chapel," Silas repeated flatly. "Under the harbor. Of the city where magic is outlawed and I've spent seven years pretending to sell buttons."

"Yes!" Bramblewick said brightly, and then her brightness guttered, and she looked suddenly very young and very tired. "There is a door. A moon-door. It opens when the tide is lowest—at the slack, when the sea forgets which way it was going. Only then. And only—" she hesitated, and her small hands twisted together, "—only for those the door remembers. The chapel was built on oaths. It will know an oath-keeper. Or..." her eyes flicked to Silas, gentle and terrible, "...it will know an oath-breaker. Either way, it will open. Doors like that can't help themselves."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere in the burning canopy, a flame shifted with a sound like a sigh.

"So we go back," Wren said at last. "Through whatever brought us here—which I assume is not a door we control—find our way to the mortal side, dive into a flooded ruin at slack tide, evade the Wardens patrolling a harbor we just vanished from in front of witnesses, retrieve a fragment of a broken god-contract, and do it again twelve more times?" She laughed, short and disbelieving. "Scholars have abandoned dissertations for less."

"We don't have twelve more times," Bramblewick said. "We have thirteen turns. One shard per turn, if the roots are right, and the roots are usually right even when they're rude about it." She reached out and touched Wren's knee, and where her finger rested, a tiny warmth bloomed through the fabric like sunlight through cloth. "You asked to come, scholar. Asking is easy. Coming is the hard part."

* * *

They walked after that, because Bramblewick insisted they needed to see something, and walking in the Undermoon was unlike anything Silas had known. The ground remembered footsteps a half-second after you made them—you could look back and see your own prints glowing faintly, fading like breath on glass. The sky overhead was not a sky but a vast slow turning, a wheel of darkness shot with silver, and Silas understood with a lurch that it was the moon seen from underneath, from inside, the far side of every night he had ever slept through.

Bramblewick led them along a ridge of black glass where rivers of emberlight ran in the crevices, and she talked the whole way, because talking seemed to keep the dimness off her.

"The covenant wasn't a law," she said. "People always make that mistake. Laws are cages. The covenant was a *promise with a body*. Human hands and ember-fire, sworn into each other, so that wonder flowed both ways across the veil. Your people gave their voices—we gave our warmth. Swordspeakers didn't command magic. They *carried* it, the way a bridge carries weight, and everyone crossed freely." She kicked a stone and it burst into harmless sparks. "Then your order broke it, and the flow stopped, and everything started pooling. Stagnant water goes bad. So does stagnant wonder."

"My order didn't break it," Silas said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "One man did. And the rest of us died believing we were saving it."

Bramblewick stopped walking. She looked up at him with those enormous eyes, and for a moment the childlike brightness fell away entirely and something ancient looked out of her face, something that had watched a world dim for a hundred years.

"I know," she said. "The roots told me that too. But knowing who broke it and getting over it are two different fires, swordspeaker." She resumed walking. "Come along. I want to show you the well."

* * *

The well stood alone on a plain of ash, a circle of old stones around a depth that gave back no echo. When Silas leaned over it, he saw not water but the mortal world—Vellamark at night, tiny and tilted, lanterns strung along the canal bridges like beads on a wire. He could see the market square where his stall stood empty. He could see the Ember Ward on its spire, dark now, dormant, and hated how much relief that sight gave him.

"It's a window," Wren breathed beside him. "Or a scrying pool. Do you know how much the archives would—"

"Don't finish that sentence," Silas said.

"Why not?"

"Because everything the archives wanted is why your kind burned mine."

Wren straightened slowly. For a moment the only sound was the faint crackle of the ridge behind them and the distant, tidal breathing of the turning sky.

"That's fair," she said. "That's actually completely fair, and I'm going to hate myself for saying this next part, but: I'm not *my kind*. My kind threw me out. I translated a forbidden text and published a footnote and they stripped my credentials and called me a fantasist in front of three hundred people." Her jaw was tight. "I have spent four years being told the covenant never existed by men who burn anyone who suggests otherwise. So yes, I want to write everything down. Because writing things down is the only weapon I've ever had, and I am *tired* of being unarmed."

Silas looked at her—at the ink-stained fingers and the defiant set of her shoulders and the smudge still on her cheekbone—and felt something in his chest shift like a plank settling in an old house.

"All right," he said. "Terms, then. If we're doing this, we do it properly. I've negotiated contracts before; I know how they rot when the terms are vague."

That surprised a laugh out of her. "Are we drawing up a contract? On what? Ash?"

"On trust. Which is worse than paper. Paper burns clean."

He held up one hand, folding fingers down as he spoke. "First term: no lies between us. Not comfortable truths—all truths. You tell me why a disgraced scholar crosses a realm to chase a prophecy, and I'll tell you why a swordspeaker sells charms instead of using them. Full price, paid upfront."

Wren's eyes narrowed. "Second term?"

"You don't touch the speech." He turned his palm up, showing her the scarred hand. "Whatever you see me do with it, however it tempts you—no transcriptions of the working verses, no experiments, no asking me to demonstrate. What happened to my order happened because clever people studied power they didn't understand. I won't help build another pyre."

"And third?"

He hesitated. This one cost him. "Third term. If it comes to choosing—if the Wardens corner us, if only one of us walks away—you let me choose. Don't argue. Don't sacrifice yourself nobly to prove a point. I've buried enough brave people. I haven't the room left for another grave."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. The moon-wheel turned overhead, and the well murmured with the lights of a city that wanted them dead.

"Counter-terms," she said finally. "One: you stop calling me 'scholar' like it's an insult. My name is Wren. Use it." She folded down her own fingers, mirroring him, and he noticed her hands were shaking slightly and that she was ignoring them with tremendous dignity. "Two: when we get back—and we will get back—you let me teach you what the prophecy actually says. All of it. Not the parts you can bear. You've been carrying guilt for seven years, Silas, and guilt makes terrible translators. Three—" and here her voice gentled into something that was almost, dangerously, kind, "—you eat when I tell you to eat and sleep when I tell you to sleep, because I've watched you for two days now and you treat your own body like a rented cart."

"That's not a term of trust, that's nagging."

"It's a term of *partnership*. Partners don't let each other fall apart quietly." She extended her hand, ink-stained, trembling, steady all at once. "Do we have an accord, Silas Nakamura, last of the swordspeakers?"

He took it. Her grip was stronger than he expected, and warmer, though that might have been the emberlight. Something passed between their hands—not magic, nothing so dramatic, just the plain animal fact of two people agreeing to stand in the same danger together—and Silas realized he had not shaken anyone's hand in earnest since the Night of Severance. Seven years of coins passed palm to palm, and none of them had meant anything. This meant something.

"Wren Corvane," he said, testing the name and finding it fit. "Accord."

"Accord," she repeated, and then, because she was apparently incapable of letting a solemn moment stay solemn: "Though I notice you didn't counter my eating term."

"I'm conserving strength for the drowning."

* * *

Bramblewick showed them the moon-door at dusk-that-wasn't-dusk, in a hollow beneath a hill made entirely of compacted ash, so old it had become stone. The door was simply a doorway standing free—no wall, no frame beyond its own edges—carved from grey stone shot with gold veins exactly like the shard-sliver she had shown them. Through it, Silas could see darkness and the smell of salt and, far below, the green-black gleam of seawater.

"It opens onto the drowned chapel," Bramblewick said. "But only at your slack tide, and only briefly—the door breathes with your moon. When the sea forgets, the door remembers. You'll have perhaps a hundred heartbeats to pass through before it closes." She looked up at them both, and her translucence was worse tonight; Silas could see the stone of the doorway faintly through her shoulder, and it frightened him more than any Warden could. "And Silas. When you stand in the chapel, listen first. The First Shard sings to oath-keepers. If you hear singing, follow it. If you hear *weeping*—" her small hand closed around his finger, hot as a struck match, "—run."

"Why would it weep?"

"Because pieces of a broken promise remember being whole," she said simply. "And some of them mind."

They made camp that night—or what served for night—in the lee of the ash-hill, and Bramblewick curled up between them like a banked fire, radiating a warmth that kept the Undermoon's chill at bay. Wren slept quickly and deeply, the sleep of the exhausted righteous, her book hugged to her chest like a child. Silas lay awake watching the turning sky and listening to the well-murmur of a city he would have to re-enter as a hunted man.

Seven years, he thought. Seven years of being small and safe and useless. And now the date had come, and the name had come, and a woman with ink on her face had shaken his hand and called it partnership, and somewhere under the cold harbor a piece of the promise his order died for was waiting to see if he was worth anything at all.

He should have felt terror. He did, a little. But underneath it, unwelcome and unmistakable, was something else—the feeling of a drawn bow finally being aimed at something true.

Beside him, Wren murmured in her sleep and turned toward his warmth without waking.

Silas closed his eyes.

Above them, the moon began its first turn.
