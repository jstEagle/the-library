## 7. The Drowned Chapel

*Silas*

The moon-door opened the way grief opens—slowly, then all at once.

It stood in the seawall below the old fish-gate, a seam of silver light no wider than a hand's breadth until the tide began to fall. Silas had watched it for three nights from the shadow of a salt-crusted archway, counting the water as it dropped, learning the door's moods the way he'd once learned the moods of his teachers: when it breathed wide, when it narrowed, when it seemed almost to listen. Tonight was slack tide, the hour the harbor held its breath between ebb and flood, and the moon-door stood open like a throat.

"You're staring at it like it might apologize," Wren said beside him.

"It might. Doors down here usually do."

She snorted, but her hands were shaking. He could see it even in the dark—the fine tremor in her fingers as she checked the oilcloth bundle strapped across her chest, the little knife she'd insisted on bringing though they both knew knives meant nothing against Wardens or water. She'd braided her hair back tight, scholar-fashion, and smeared her face with lampblack so that only her eyes showed, pale and furious and awake.

Silas knew those eyes now. Four days since the orchard, four days since Bramblewick had pressed the knowledge of the First Shard into his mind like a coal wrapped in cloth—*the drowned chapel of Saint Ovelun, where the first oath was sung over water*—and in those four days Wren Corvane had not slept more than two hours at a stretch. She read instead. She read everything: tide tables stolen from the harbormaster's office, hymnals, the waterlogged fragments of Ovelunite liturgy she'd copied out in her cramped archive hand. She read the way starving people eat.

"Patrol changes at the turn," she said now, low. "I watched them last night. Two lantern-boats in the outer harbor, one pair walking the mole. When the tide turns, the walkers go in to warm up. We have maybe forty minutes."

"Forty minutes." Silas rolled his shoulders. Beneath his coat, against his sternum, the scarred place where his order's sigil had been burned away ached the way it always ached before he used the speech. His body remembered what his mind kept trying to bury. "And if we're still inside when it closes?"

"Then we're chapel ornaments." She said it lightly. Her eyes said otherwise. "Bramblewick said the shard sings to its own kind. You're the closest thing to its own kind either realm has left."

"That's not comforting, Wren."

"I wasn't aiming for comfort. I was aiming for true." She pulled her hood up. For a moment she stood very still, looking out at the black water, and something in her face went young and unguarded. "My mother drowned off this harbor," she said, so quietly he almost lost it under the lap of the waves. "When I was nine. They never found her. I used to think—if I could just get under the water, just once, I'd find her waiting somewhere with an answer."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going underwater to steal a piece of God's broken promise out of a sunken church." A ghost of a smile. "Life has a sense of humor."

Silas looked at her—at this sharp, stubborn, half-drowned woman who had walked into his stall with a death sentence bound in calfskin and refused, absolutely refused, to leave—and felt the old fear rearrange itself around a new shape. It wasn't himself he was afraid for anymore. That was the trouble with people. That was why the order forbade attachments, and why every swordspeaker broke the rule, and why the burning hurt so much worse when there was someone watching.

"Stay behind me," he said. "In the water, you stay behind me. Whatever happens."

"Whatever happens," she echoed, and did not promise, because Wren Corvane had never promised anything she couldn't argue her way out of later.

They went down the moss-slick steps together, and the moon-door took them.

* * *

*Wren*

The Undermoon sea was not cold.

That was the first wrongness, and Wren catalogued it the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was how she kept terror at arm's length. The water should have been winter-black and biting; instead it was warm as bathwater and lit from within, a green-gold glow rising from far below, as if the whole ocean floor were a banked hearth. Fish drifted past with lanterns growing from their skulls. Kelp forests burned softly without ash, echoing the orchard above, and through the water came a sound she felt in her teeth before she understood it: singing. Distant, layered, many-voiced. The drowned chapel, calling its own kind home.

*Its own kind.* Not her. She kicked alongside Silas and watched him move through the strange bright water and thought about what Bramblewick had said, in that voice like a child reading a funeral rite: *He carries the last live coal of the old words. The shard will know him.*

Silas swam like a man who'd done this before—not gracefully, but efficiently, economically, a body that had learned long ago never to waste anything, including effort. Every few strokes he glanced back at her, and each time their eyes met through the luminous water she felt something she refused to name. Focus, she told herself. You are here for the shard. You are here because Vey has Tomas in a cell beneath the spire, and because a century-old book named a date and a name, and because the only way out of prophecy is through it.

The chapel rose out of the glowing deep like a memory surfacing.

Saint Ovelun's had been a real church once—Wren had found the records, the demolition writ signed ninety years ago, *structure condemned, foundations surrendered to the harbor expansion*. But the records hadn't mentioned this: that the chapel hadn't been torn down at all. It had been *sunk*. Lowered whole into the Undermoon sea, bells and buttresses and all, and settled on the seafloor with its spire pointing down toward the burning deep like a root. Its stone was furred with soft fire. Its windows, shattered in the mortal world, were whole here—great rose windows of colored flame, casting red and violet light across the seabed in slow turning wheels.

And the singing was coming from inside it.

They surfaced in the flooded nave. Or rather, they rose into air that existed anyway, a pocket of breathable dark beneath the vaulted ceiling, because the Undermoon made its own rules. Wren pulled herself onto a ledge of submerged pew and coughed out seawater that tasted of woodsmoke and honey.

"It's beautiful," she said, and hated how her voice shook.

"Don't look too long." Silas hauled himself up beside her, water streaming from his coat. In the firelight his scars stood out pale on his brown hands, the old burn-marks that ran from knuckle to wrist in patterns she'd once taken for accident and now recognized as script. Oath-script. Every swordspeaker had been written on, once. Every swordspeaker except the one who ran. "Places like this remember being loved. They hold on to visitors."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's a kindness. There aren't many left."

The water in the nave was waist-deep and still, a black mirror holding the burning windows upside down. At the far end stood the altar, and above the altar hung the bell—not in a tower but suspended from nothing, slowly revolving, a great cracked bronze shape whose fracture glowed like a fault line of dawn. Wren's breath caught. She knew bells. She knew what lived inside bells in the old stories.

But the bell was the Second Shard's house. That was twelve turns away, if they lived that long. Here, now, the First Shard lay closer—in the crypt, Bramblewick had said, *under the floor where the first feet sang*, sealed in the baptismal font that had never held water because it held something better.

"The font's beneath the altar," Wren whispered, unrolling the copy of her notes, ink smeared but legible. "Ovelunite chapels always kept the baptistery behind the sanctuary. There should be a stair—"

Light swept across the rose windows.

Not firelight. Lantern-light. Cold, white, mortal, wrong—a beam cutting through the underwater glass from outside, and with it, faintly distorted by water and stone, voices. Boots on stone. The scrape of a hull against the chapel's sunken roof.

Wren went cold in a way the warm sea couldn't touch. She looked at Silas. Silas was already sinking below the lip of the pew, one hand flat on the water's surface, and his face had gone very still—the particular stillness of a man listening with parts of himself that weren't ears.

"Wardens," he breathed. "Three boats. They're circling the spire."

"They can't know about the chapel. Nobody knows—"

"Vey knows." His jaw tightened. "He's known where the shards sleep longer than we have. He built the ban. Of course he keeps watch on the graves." He closed his eyes. When he opened them, the decision was already made, and Wren saw it land on him like a weight. "We go now. Down the crypt stair, take the shard, and out through the sacristy door on the far side before they finish their circle. Quietly. No speech unless—" He stopped.

"Unless what?" she asked, though she knew.

"Unless there's no other choice."

* * *

*Silas*

The crypt stair was narrow and drowned to the ceiling, and Silas went down it in a rush of held breath, Wren's grip on his belt like a hand on a rope thrown to a drowning man—which, he reflected grimly, was exactly what it was, just pointed the other direction.

The crypt opened into a round chamber where the fire in the stones burned blue. Bones lined the niches—not human bones, or not only. Some were long and curved like rib-struts of impossible birds, some small and jointed like the fingers of children made of charcoal. Emberkin dead. The covenant's first congregation, buried in the house of the god who'd promised to share Himself with mortals, and Silas swam through their resting place feeling every one of them watching.

The font stood at the chamber's center on a dais of fused glass—sand that had been turned to mirror by heat older than the city. It was granite, plain, the size of a washbasin, and it was empty, and it was singing.

That was the thing about the shards that no record captured. Wren's books described them as objects, relics, *fragments of the Covenant-Stone sundered in the Severance*. But standing before the font, chest aching, ears full, Silas understood they were not objects at all. The First Shard was a voice. A single note of the great chord that had once bound two realms together, locked in stone, singing to itself in the dark for a hundred years.

And it knew him.

The moment his scarred hand touched the font's rim, the singing changed. It found a pitch inside his ribs and matched it, and the ache beneath his sternum flared white-hot, and before he could stop it—before he could bite down on it, swallow it, do any of the hundred things seven years of practice had taught him—the swordspeech rose in his throat and answered.

He didn't mean to speak. The words came out anyway, in the old tongue, in the cadence of the oath-verse, and the crypt *lit up*. Blue-white fire poured from his mouth and hands, racing along the oath-script scars, leaping from niche to niche until every emberkin bone blazed like a struck match, and the font cracked open along seams no eye had seen, and there in the hollow heart of the granite lay a splinter of stone no bigger than a fingerbone, burning gold.

*No,* he thought, drowning in his own light. *No, no, be quiet, be quiet—*

Above them, through thirty feet of water and stone, a Warden's horn sounded.

* * *

*Wren*

Later—much later, in the safehouse, in the dark—Wren would try to describe what she saw in those three seconds, and fail, and keep trying anyway, because some things deserved the attempt.

She saw Silas Nakamura, charm-seller, tired man, stand upright in a flooded crypt with fire coming out of him like he was a lamp and the world had finally been allowed to see the wick. She saw the oath-script on his arms unwinding, letters of light lifting off his skin and hanging in the water around him, rearranging themselves into sentences in a language that predated language. She saw his face while it happened: not triumphant. Terrified. The look of a man watching his own grave open and finding it occupied by someone he used to be.

And she saw the shard rise from the broken font into his cupped hands, small and golden and impossibly heavy-looking, humming against his palms like a living thing recognizing its keeper.

"Take it," she gasped. "Silas—*take it and put it out!*"

He closed his fists around the shard and the light died so fast the darkness rang. For one heartbeat the crypt was black except for the blue bones. Then the lantern-beams swept through the high water of the nave above, and boots thundered on stone, and a voice—amplified by some siphoned trick of stolen magic, a voice like a nail driven through a board—called down into the water:

*"There! Below the altar—something burned—I saw it, I SAW IT—"*

"They saw the light," Wren whispered. Her heart was slamming against the oilcloth bundle. "Through the windows. Silas, they saw—"

"I know." His voice was flat and dead calm, which she had learned was worse than shouting. "Sacristy door. Now. Don't stop for anything."

They went up the far stair through a crawl of black water, the shard wrapped now in three layers of oilcloth against Silas's chest, and burst out onto a ledge of rock beneath the chapel's tilted western wall just as the first lantern-boat rounded the spire. Three figures in grey stood in it, crossbows leveled, white-lantern light carving the sea into cruel clean planes.

"There!" the youngest one screamed, and Wren would remember his face—young, pockmarked, frightened, a boy doing a job—because it was the last ordinary thing she saw for a long time.

A bolt hissed past her ear and exploded against the chapel wall in a bloom of freezing white.

"*Dive!*" Silas roared, and shoved her off the ledge into the warm dark, and followed, and the last thing Wren saw before the water closed over her head was the youngest Warden raising his hand—not a crossbow now, but open-palmed, fingers spread—and speak a word that bent the sea itself.

They had magic too. Stolen, siphoned, hoarded—but magic, real and aimed, and it was coming after them through the burning deep.

Silas's hand found hers in the dark and gripped hard, and around them both, reluctantly, furiously, the old words woke again like scars opening, lighting the way ahead in a trail any hunter in the Undermoon could follow home.

* * *

They broke the surface beyond the harbor mole as the moon-door slammed shut behind them with a sound like a book closing on a finger. Silas dragged Wren up the fish-gate stairs, both of them streaming phosphorescent water, lungs raw. Behind them, lantern-light bloomed across the Undermoon sea—three boats, then five, spreading outward from the chapel in a widening net.

"They'll search the whole realm," Wren panted, bent double, hair plastered to her blackened face. "They'll tear the orchard apart looking for us. Bramblewick—"

"Bramblewick can hide a mountain in a teacup. It's us I'm worried about." Silas straightened. In the lamplight of the mortal quay, his hands were still faintly glowing, the oath-script visible through his soaked sleeves, fading by degrees but not fast enough, nowhere near fast enough. He stared at them with an expression Wren had never seen on him before—not fear exactly. Resignation arriving late to meet the fear it had summoned.

"What is it?" she asked, though she already knew.

"A Warden saw me use it." He said it simply, the way men say diagnoses. "Saw the speech, heard the verse. By morning there'll be a warrant with my true name on it nailed to every gate in Vellamark. And yours beside it, Wren. Harbor traffic will be stopped. The gates watched. Every informant Vey pays—and he pays well—will be looking for two soaked strangers carrying something that sings."

Wren looked back at the black water of the harbor, at the distant glow of the world beneath it, and pressed her hand flat against the oilcloth bundle over her heart, where the First Shard hummed its single stubborn note against her palm.

"Then we'd better find somewhere to hide," she said, "before the city wakes up."

Somewhere across the rooftops, a bell began to ring the alarm—and did not stop.
