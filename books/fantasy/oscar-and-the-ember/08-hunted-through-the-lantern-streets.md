## 8. Hunted Through the Lantern Streets

The bolt came out of the dark like a sentence already written.

Wren heard it before she saw it—a wet, whistling hiss, the sound of a thing moving faster than regret—and then Silas shoved her sideways off the canal bridge and the world tilted into lantern-light and black water, and the bolt meant for his heart buried itself in her shoulder instead.

She didn't scream. She wanted to. She would remember, afterward, that she had wanted to and had not, and that this small stubbornness was the only dignity the night left her.

They had been running for eleven minutes. She knew because she counted things when she was afraid—steps, breaths, the gaps between the Wardens' whistles. Eleven minutes since the drowned chapel, since Silas surfaced from the slack tide with the First Shard clutched against his chest like a newborn, since the harbor patrol's lantern swung toward them and a voice like a slammed door shouted *there—the swordspeaker—the swordspeaker!*

Now they were on the rooftops above Lantern Street, where the city hung its paper lamps in strings of red and gold for the Festival of Small Mercies, and every light that was supposed to be beautiful had become a target painted on their backs.

"Down," Silas breathed, pulling her flat behind a chimney stack. His hands were shaking. She noticed that. Seven years of hiding, and his hands still shook.

Below, three figures in ash-grey cloaks moved along the canal path with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the night. Warden lanterns burned white at their belts—not flame but something colder, covenant-light stolen and caged, and it made Wren's teeth ache to look at it. The light swept across the water, across the bridge where she'd been standing two heartbeats ago, across the dark stain of her own blood dripping through the roof tiles.

*Blood,* she thought dully. *We're leaving a trail. Of course we're leaving a trail.*

The bolt's shaft snapped when she'd fallen; the head was still in her, seated just below the collarbone, and every heartbeat pushed a fresh wave of heat down her arm and into her fingers, which had gone strange and far away. She pressed her back against the chimney and breathed through her nose the way her brother Tam used to when the archive masters caned his knuckles: *in for four, hold for four.* Tam. Vey held Tam in some cell beneath the Spire, and here she was bleeding out on a rooftop over paper lanterns, and if she died tonight the prophecy would die with her, and Tam would rot, and—

"Wren." Silas's face was very close, grey in the lamplight, his scarred hands hovering over her shoulder without touching. "Stay awake. Look at me."

"I'm looking at you," she said, and her voice came out thin as onion skin. "You should know I'm cataloguing your failures even now. You let me walk point off a bridge while being hunted by crossbowmen."

"You walked point because you wouldn't do anything else." But there was no bite in it. There was something else in his face, something that frightened her more than the Wardens did—something raw and deciding.

Below them, a whistle. Then another, answering from the east. The net was drawing closed.

"They'll have the bridges," Silas said. "And the ferry stairs. And by morning they'll have every door in Vellamark with our faces nailed to it." He looked at her shoulder, at the blood soaking through her shirt in a bloom as red as the festival lanterns, and Wren watched him arrive at the edge of himself.

"I can walk," she lied.

"You can't."

"I *can*, Silas. We keep moving, we find Odile's—"

"If you climb down a ladder you'll tear it loose. If it's nicked the artery—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I've seen this wound before. On the Night of Severance. On a man named Corvin Hale, who took a quarrel meant for me, and who bled for six minutes while I stood there doing nothing, because doing something would have meant speaking, and speaking would have meant dying." His voice had gone somewhere flat and old. "I stood there, Wren. I counted his breaths. Forty-one."

The lanterns swayed overhead. Somewhere below, a Warden kicked open a door and a woman began shouting about her children.

"There are rules," Silas said, almost to himself. "I wrote them myself, carved them into my own bones so I couldn't forget. Don't speak. Don't shine. Don't be seen. A swordspeaker who uses the gift is a beacon; every ward-glass in the city will see it, and they'll come, and they won't stop coming." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "Seven years I kept those rules. Seven years selling luck-knots and lying with my whole life."

"Silas—"

"And you took a bolt for me." His eyes came up, and in the red lantern-light they were the color of banked coals. "So here is what I've decided about my rules."

He pressed both palms flat against her shoulder, one above the wound and one below, and Wren felt the heat of him first—ordinary human heat, trembling—and then something else underneath it, rising like water finding its level.

He spoke.

Not words, exactly. Not any language built by mouths. It was speech the way lightning is a kind of light—older, vertical, terrible. The syllables went through her chest and rearranged things. She felt the bolt-head turn, felt it draw backward out of her flesh with a sensation like a fishhook unmaking itself, felt her blood reverse its error and close behind it. The pain didn't vanish; it *changed*, became a bright pressure, a fullness, and then even that faded, leaving only the memory of hurt the way a bell leaves the memory of ringing.

Silas lifted his hands. In the hollows of his palms, faint as breath on glass, blue-white light was fading—the same light as the Ember Ward, the same light as the Undermoon.

"Oh," Wren said softly. "Oh, Silas."

Above the rooftops, across the whole sleeping curve of Vellamark, every ward-glass flared at once. She saw it happen—saw the distant towers answer him one after another, white fire climbing the Spire like ivy, like a shout passed from mouth to mouth: *here, HERE, HERE.*

"There he is!" The shout came up from the canal path, ragged with triumph. "The bridge! Cut off the bridge!"

Silas hauled her to her feet. His face was calm in the way of a man standing in a burning house who has finally stopped pretending it isn't burning.

"Run now?" she managed.

"Run now."

* * *

They ran.

Vellamark at festival-time was a maze designed by a drunk god: laundry lines strung between spires, rain barrels, pigeon coops, the sudden graves of courtyards four stories down. Wren followed Silas because he moved like water finding cracks—he'd spent seven years learning this city's body, every scar and joint of it, and now he spent that knowledge all at once, spending it the way he'd just spent everything else.

Behind them the whistles wove a closing pattern. Ahead, the rooftops ended at the Kettleman Canal, black and glossy, spanned by three bridges all blooming with Warden lanterns.

"The middle bridge has a toll-arch," Silas panted. "Under it. There's a maintenance ledge—smugglers' route, Odile showed me once. If we drop before the arch—"

"You want us to jump into a canal."

"I want us to jump into a canal *quickly*."

They went off the roof together. For one long moment Wren flew—which is to say she fell beautifully, arms wide, festival lanterns wheeling past her vision like embers in reverse—and then the cold took her whole, the canal closing over her head with a sound like a struck bell.

The water was a living darkness. Her healed shoulder screamed where the cold found the new-knit flesh. She clawed upward, broke surface under the stone lip of the toll-arch, and found Silas already there, hauling her into a ledge barely wider than a coffin, slick with weed, invisible from the bridge above if a person didn't know to look.

Boots crossed the bridge overhead. Lantern-light strobed through the arch's throat, turning the canal water to molten silver for three heartbeats at a time.

"—went in the water—"

"—nets at the weir, they'll surface or drown—"

Wren pressed herself against the stone, shivering so hard her teeth chattered in code. Silas's arm came around her, pulling her against his side for warmth, and she let him, because pride was a luxury for people who weren't hunted.

"That was swordspeech," she whispered. "What you did. Full covenant-work."

"Yes."

"You said using it was death."

"It was." He stared out at the silver-strobed dark. "I think I finished being that man somewhere around the moment you fell. Everything after is just... the rest of it."

Wren turned that over. Her scholar's mind wanted to take it apart, footnote it, file it—but her shoulder ached where the wound had been, a warm ghost-pain, and all her mind could say was: *he unmade a crossbow bolt with his voice, for me, knowing the price.*

"Forty-one breaths," she said quietly. "Corvin Hale. Is that why? All these years—is that the arithmetic you've been carrying?"

Silas was silent long enough that a barge went by, its wake slapping the stones.

"He had a daughter," Silas said at last. "I checked on her, the first year. Left coin. Never told her why." Another silence. "You remind me of him. That's not why you took the bolt. But it's why I couldn't watch it happen twice."

Above them, the boots receded toward the weir. The lantern-light thinned. And in the quiet, Wren became aware of a sound she hadn't noticed before—a faint, sweet chiming coming from inside Silas's soaked coat, from the oilcloth bundle pressed against his chest.

The First Shard, singing. Softly. Like something waking up.

"It knows," Silas murmured. He drew the bundle out an inch, and blue-white light leaked between the wrappings, painting his face with underwater colors. "It felt the speech. It's... answering."

"Answering what?"

"I don't know." His thumb traced the wrapping, careful as a man touching a sleeping child. "But Bramblewick said the shards remember the oath. And I just spoke a piece of it—broken, forbidden, mine." He looked at her, and there was wonder in him now, and terror, braided tight. "Wren, I think it's counting. I think it's telling me how many turns are left."

"How many?"

The shard pulsed. Once. Twice. Wren found herself holding her breath, the way she had before opening sealed archive cases, the way she always did before knowledge that could not be taken back.

Eleven pulses. Slow. Final. Like a clock in a tomb.

"Eleven turns," Silas whispered. "It cost us one, tonight. Running. Bleeding." He wrapped the shard away, and the light died, and the dark of the arch came back like a held breath released. "Odile's house. Now, while they're watching the water. Because in eleven turns, Vey binds the last shard—or we do. And either way, someone is going to stand at the covenant's heart and pay for it."

He offered her his hand. His scarred, ordinary-looking, impossible hand.

Wren took it.

Somewhere above the toll-arch, a Warden's whistle sounded—then stopped mid-note, cut short, as if the whistler had seen something in the dark that made him forget his own name.
