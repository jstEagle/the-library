## 10. Vey's Bargain

The spire of the Magistracy had no clocks, and this was deliberate.

Aldous Vey had ordered them removed in his first year—every bell, every hourglass in the public halls, every sundial scraped from the courtyard stone. A city that could not count its hours could not count its years, and a city that could not count its years would never notice that its magistrate did not age. It was a small vanity, perhaps. But small vanities, tended carefully over decades, became empires.

He stood now at the window of his private chamber on the forty-first floor, watching the lantern streets of Vellamark glitter below like embers raked across black iron. The moon was rising over the harbor, fat and pale, and somewhere beneath it—in the drowned chapel of Saint Ovelun, if his informants were worth the silver he paid them—a swordspeaker and a disgraced scholar were prying at the first shard of a covenant most of the city believed was a fable.

Vey smiled, and the smile cost him nothing, because no one was there to see it.

He turned from the window and crossed to the cabinet against the eastern wall. It was an unremarkable piece of furniture, dark walnut, brass fittings, the kind of thing that might hold ledgers or correspondence or a magistrate's collection of ceremonial wines. He unlocked it with a key he kept on a chain against his sternum, warm from his skin, and drew out a shallow dish of smoked glass.

Inside the dish lay what the ledgers called *residue*. What Vey called, in the privacy of his own skull, *the tithe*.

It looked like ash that had forgotten how to be ash—fine grey grains that moved when they should have been still, drifting against the draft, clustering toward the warmth of his hand the way moths cluster toward flame. Siphoned covenant magic, drawn up through the roots of the Ember Ward for seven years now, filtered through engines of brass and bone in the spire's cellar until it was thin enough, tame enough, to pour into a man without burning him hollow.

He tipped the dish to his lips.

The taste was always the same: cold iron and honey, the flavor of a promise made by someone else and broken in your favor. It went down like winter going down, and then the warmth came, spreading outward from his chest, and Vey closed his eyes and let himself feel—for the hundredthousandth time—the slow, luxurious unfurling of borrowed eternity.

His hands, when he looked at them, were still the hands of a man of fifty. They had been the hands of a man of fifty for nineteen years.

* * *

People imagined, when they imagined magistrates, that power was a matter of decrees and gallows. Vey had learned long ago that real power was quieter. Real power was knowing which bakeries sold bread with weevils and making sure the right wives found out. Real power was a ledger of debts so old and so intricate that half the merchant houses in Vellamark could not remember which of their grandfathers had signed them. Real power was the Ember Ward itself—that beautiful, terrible lattice of covenant-iron threaded through every street and sewer and foundation stone—which he had built not to suppress magic, as the proclamations claimed, but to *harvest* it.

The ban had been the cleverest thing he ever did, and he had done it with other men's hands.

He poured himself a measure of plum wine—he still drank, still ate, still kept the small animal habits that made him seem merely mortal—and settled into the chair by the fire, where the light was kind to a face that needed no kindness. On the table beside him lay a folder of grey leather, and inside the folder, reports.

He read them the way other men read love letters.

*Subject observed at night market, stall eleven, selling charms of negligible potency. Subject's left hand scarred in pattern consistent with oath-binding. Subject declined to register under amnesty of Year One. Subject believed to be Nakamura, Silas—last of the Order of the Severed Blade.*

Last of them. Vey turned the page with something almost like fondness. He remembered the Night of Severance with the clarity of a man remembering his wedding day. The great hall of the order, all candlelight and solemn faces, thirty swordspeakers gathered to renew the old pact—and Aldous Vey, then only a junior magistrate with a talent for administration and a hunger that frightened even himself, standing at the back with the amended oath-texts he had spent two years forging. A single altered clause. A single inverted word. *Surrender* made to read as *sever*, renewal made to read as rupture.

They had spoken the words themselves. That was the exquisite part. He had never needed to lift a hand against the order; they had shattered their own covenant with their own sacred voices, and the backlash had killed nine of them before they understood what they'd done. The rest he had handled more conventionally. Wardens, torches, the smell of burning wool and worse. All but one.

Nakamura. The young one with the quick feet and the quicker tongue. Vey had watched him run through the orchard behind the chapterhouse, and he had let him go—not out of mercy, though he was generous enough to enjoy the fiction of mercy, but because a surviving swordspeaker was a loose thread, and loose threads, given time, always led somewhere interesting.

Seven years later, the thread had led to a drowned chapel and a scholar named Corvane, and Vey set down his wine and permitted himself a long, slow breath of satisfaction.

Because here was the truth that no report had captured, the truth that lived in him like a second heartbeat: the shards were waking. He could feel it through the Ward, through the siphon-engines humming in his cellar—a new note in the old song, a resonance climbing through the lattice of the city like water climbing through a wick. The covenant was trying to reassemble itself. And every shard that woke made the residue richer, thicker, sweeter.

If the swordspeaker gathered them all and reforged the oath, the magic would flow free—to everyone, or no one, depending on which version of the prophecy one credited. Vey had read both versions. He had burned three of the four copies of the original.

But if the shards came to *him*—

He rose and went to the mirror above the hearth, and studied the face there. Strong jaw going soft. Grey threading the black at his temples, exactly as much grey as there had been nineteen years ago, frozen like a portrait. Eyes that his valet had once described, in a letter the valet did not know Vey had intercepted, as *the eyes of a man drinking from a well that belongs to someone else.*

"Patience," Vey told his reflection, and his reflection agreed.

* * *

The summons went out at midnight, carried by a Warden-captain whose loyalty was less a virtue than an arithmetic: Vey owned his brother's debts, his daughter's schooling, and the deed to a house in the Lantern Quarter that the captain did not know was mortgaged twice over.

"To the swordspeaker," Vey said, "not the woman. She's incidental—or she will be, once I know what she wants. Offer him terms. Speak them gently. Men who have spent seven years being hunted have forgotten what gentleness sounds like, and it undoes them faster than steel."

"And if he refuses, Magistrate?"

Vey looked out again at the sleeping city, at the Ward-iron glimmering faintly blue beneath the cobbles where the moonlight struck it just so. "Then refuse him loudly," he said. "Refuse him in front of witnesses. Let him see the door close. A man who believes he has chosen freely will fight you forever; a man who believes he has thrown away his one chance will spend the rest of his life wondering." He smiled at the captain, who shifted his weight like a horse feeling a whip it couldn't yet see. "We are not in the business of killing rebels, Captain. We are in the business of making obedience look like wisdom."

The captain bowed and withdrew, and Vey was alone with his fire and his folder and the faint, sweet hum of the siphons far below, singing their stolen song.

He took out paper—good paper, cream-colored, heavy in the hand, the kind of paper that made a man feel important before he read a single word—and began to write. Not a decree. A letter. He had learned that letters were more dangerous than decrees; a decree commanded, but a letter *conversed*, and conversation was where corruption did its finest work.

*To Silas Nakamura,* he wrote, and paused, savoring the name. Then:

*I write to you not as a magistrate but as a man who remembers the same night you remember, and grieves it differently than you might expect.*

That was good. That was very good. Every word of it would even be true, in the way that mattered: Vey did remember the Night of Severance, and he did grieve it—grieved it the way a miner grieves a seam of ore finally opened.

*You believe I broke the covenant. Consider instead that the covenant was already broken—rotted from within, hoarded by thirty families who called themselves an order and meant a dynasty. Magic pooled in their bloodlines while the city starved of wonder. I did not sever the pact. I severed the monopoly. What came after was regrettable. What came before was theft.*

He let the ink dry and read it back, and felt the familiar pleasure of it—the way the sentences arranged themselves like a hand extended in friendship, reasonable, weary, honest-seeming. This was the craft no one ever gave him credit for. Anyone could burn a heretic. It took artistry to make the heretic want to climb onto the pyre holding your hand.

*The shards are waking. You feel it—I know you do; your scars itch at moonrise, your dreams are full of burning trees. You cannot stop what is coming, Nakamura. But you can shape it. Bring me what you find, and I will give you what no swordspeaker has had in seven years: a name that is safe to speak. Amnesty under seal. A place in the new order—not a servant's place. An architect's.*

*Or keep running. Keep selling luck-knots to dockworkers and flinching at Warden boots. Keep the shards, and watch them wake the Undermoon into a war this city cannot survive, and die in it, and be remembered—if at all—as the last coward of a dead order.*

*You were the one who ran. I have never held it against you. Survival is honest. Come and survive with me, and make your survival mean something.*

*—A.V.*

He read it twice, adjusted a single phrase—*an architect's* rather than *a partner's*; partners expected equality, architects expected vision—and sealed it with wax the color of dried blood.

Then he sat back, and let himself imagine how it would land.

The swordspeaker would rage, first. Seven years of fear made men brittle, and brittle things cracked loudly. He would want to burn the letter, and perhaps he would. But words, once heard, could not be unheard, and Vey had built his letter to live in a man like a splinter—small, painless at first, working deeper every time he flexed. *I did not break the covenant. I broke the monopoly.* How many nights would that sentence keep him company? How many times, exhausted and hunted, would he turn it over and find, to his horror, that parts of it fit?

And underneath the argument, the offer itself, gleaming like a coin at the bottom of a well: safety. Legitimacy. Rest. Vey knew what seven years of running did to a man. It did not make him harder. It made him *tired*, and tired men did not weigh ideals; they weighed beds.

And if the swordspeaker refused—well. Refusal was data too. It would tell Vey where the man's heart sat, and hearts, once located, could be aimed at.

There was the woman, of course. Corvane. Vey tapped the folder thoughtfully. A scholar with a ruined reputation and a brother in his cells—yes, he had known about the brother for months; it was remarkable how many useful people had brothers, sisters, mothers, debts. If the gentle door failed, there were other doors, and some of them opened inward on the people a man loved. He did not relish it. He wanted that understood, at least by himself, in the privacy of his own skull: he did not *relish* it. Necessity wore whatever face it had to wear. The city needed order. The alternative to his stewardship was chaos, wonder running wild through the streets like a spilled fire, and Vey had seen enough of fire to know it always began as wonder and ended as ash.

He was the dam, he told himself. Dams were hated by rivers. It could not be helped.

* * *

Near dawn, the residue called to him again, and he answered.

This time, when he lifted the dish, the grains rose before he tilted it—rose streaming toward his open mouth like iron filings toward a lodestone, eager, and Vey laughed softly around them as they went down, because eagerness was new. The shards were waking, and the waking fed the Ward, and the Ward fed him, and for one vertiginous moment he felt what the emberkin must have felt at the height of the old covenant: the sense of being a lit window in a dark house, warmth pouring out in every direction.

Then it faded, as it always faded, and left him colder than before. That was the arithmetic no one warned you about. Borrowed fire cooled fast. Nineteen years of tithes, and the dose that had once lasted a month now barely carried him a week. The well was deepening. Or the well was emptying—he had stopped asking which, because either answer demanded the same response.

He needed the shards. Not wanted. *Needed.* The way a drowning man needs air, the way an addict needs the next dose and hates the word addict. If the covenant reforged itself without him, the flow would reverse, the siphons would choke, and everything he had frozen—his face, his hands, his careful, endless, golden noon of a life—would come due all at once, with interest. He would age nineteen years in nineteen minutes, and then he would simply stop, mid-stride, a magistrate-shaped heap of dust on the spire floor, and the histories would call it justice.

Vey did not intend to become a moral lesson.

He crossed to the window as the sky went grey, and below him Vellamark stirred—bakers, dockworkers, children, ten thousand lives moving through streets laced with his harvest-iron, none of them knowing that the flatness of their days, the grayness of their mornings, the way no one in this city dreamed anymore, was the taste of a man drinking their cup dry.

"The wonder comes back," he murmured to the glass, to the city, to no one. "When I am secure. When it is safe. Everything in its season."

It sounded, even to him, almost kind.

The Warden-captain returned an hour past sunrise, boots quiet on the carpet, and stood at attention with the particular stillness of a man carrying news he did not want to be holding.

"Well?" said Vey.

"He took the letter, Magistrate. Read it on the spot." A pause. "Burned it."

Vey nodded slowly, unsurprised, almost pleased. "And?"

"And then—" The captain's throat worked. "Then he asked me to tell you something. Said you'd want it exact."

"Say it."

"He said: *"Tell him the well is shallower than he thinks. Tell him I've seen what's at the bottom."*" The captain swallowed. "And then he walked into the moon-door at low tide, Magistrate. Him and the scholar both. With the First Shard."

For a long moment Vey did not move. The fire ticked. Far below, the siphon-engines hummed their patient hymn.

Then he laughed—one short, genuine bark of a laugh, the first in months—and turned back to the window, where the tide was sliding out of the harbor like a sheet being pulled off a bed.

"At the bottom of the well," he repeated softly, tasting it. "How poetic."

He rang the small silver bell on his desk, and when the secretary appeared he was already giving orders: double the patrols on the cathedral quarter, wake the archivists, bring him everything the Magistracy possessed on Saint Ovelun and the bells of Vellamark. Because if the swordspeaker had seen the bottom of the well, then the swordspeaker had been closer to the source than any living man—and that, Vey thought, straightening his cuffs, that was not a threat.

That was a map.

"Prepare my carriage," he said. "And send for the girl's brother. I think it's time he earned his keep."
