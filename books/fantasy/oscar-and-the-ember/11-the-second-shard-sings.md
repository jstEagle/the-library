## 11. The Second Shard Sings

The cathedral of Saint Vellamark the Tide-Blessed had not rung its bells in nine years.

Silas had heard them once, when he first came to the city—young, unscarred, still carrying the gift like a coal cupped in both palms. The bells had been bronze-voiced then, deep and certain, and the whole of Vellamark had paused beneath their sound as if the sky itself had spoken. Now they hung silent in their tower above the old quarter, black with pigeon filth and salt rot, and the faithful below crossed themselves against them the way one might cross oneself against a haunted house.

He and Wren climbed the tower stair at dusk, by the light of a single shuttered lantern, while the last of the day's heat rose up through the stone like breath from a sleeping animal. Two hundred and eleven steps. Wren counted them aloud under her breath, because counting was what she did instead of praying, and Silas let her count because it kept her voice low and her mind off the drop that opened beside the stair every fourth turn—a sheer fall past rusted iron bells to a floor of worn flagstones far below.

"You know," she said around step ninety, "most people who hide from the law choose hiding places without stairs."

"Most people who hide from the law haven't been told to come here."

"By a *mirror ghost*." Wren's laugh came out thin. Three nights ago Bramblewick's face had bloomed in the cracked looking glass at Odile's safehouse, translucent as breath on winter glass, and told them—her voice flickering like a guttering wick—that the Second Shard waited where the city kept its oldest song. Silas had known before she finished speaking. There was only one place Vellamark kept songs.

"Emberkin don't lie," Silas said. "It costs them too much. A lie is a broken oath in miniature. They can't afford small ones."

"And yet your entire order—" She stopped herself. He felt her stop herself, the way you feel someone step back from an edge. Since Odile's house, since he had finally said aloud the words *I fled* and *they died and I ran*, Wren had been handling him like something with a hairline fracture running through it. He understood why. He appreciated it less than he should have.

"My order broke the biggest oath ever sworn," he finished for her. "Yes. Say it. I'd rather hear it than feel you swallowing it."

Step one hundred and forty. The stair narrowed. Somewhere below them, out in the lantern streets, Wardens were still knocking on doors. Seven days since the drowned chapel. Seven days since the Warden had seen blue fire leap from Silas's fingers over black water and lived to report it. The warrants had gone up by morning: *SILEAS NAKAMURA, SWORDSPAKER, BURN ON SIGHT.* His name spelled wrong on the official placards, which would have been funny if it weren't also, in a way, true—he hadn't been that man in seven years, and the man he'd become was being burned in effigy in three districts.

And now they were climbing toward the loudest place in the city, because a dying spirit-child had sent them there.

"The bell," Wren said, at step two hundred, "is called the Ovelun Bell. Did you know that? Pre-ban casting, pre-covenant even, some say. It cracked during the Severance—not from any blow, from *grief*, according to the archive marginalia, though marginalia will say anything." Her scholar's voice had slipped into gear, the familiar rhythm that meant she was steadying herself. "They never recast it. They said no foundry could match the tone. I always thought that was sentiment. Standing here, I think maybe it was fear."

"Fear of what?"

"That if they poured new bronze, whatever lives in the old bronze would have nowhere to be."

* * *

The belfry opened around them like the ribcage of some enormous patient beast.

Moonlight fell through the arched lancets on four sides, laying pale lozenges across the floor, and the great bells hung above in ranks—the tenor, the trebles, the middling voices—all of them dark, all of them silent, all of them waiting with the terrible patience of things that had outlasted their purpose. Ropes dangled from the wheels overhead, frayed ends brushing stone. Pigeons muttered in the rafters and went still when the humans entered, the way animals go still around something they can't name.

And apart from the others, on its own beam, hung the cracked one.

It was larger than the rest, though it didn't look heavy so much as *tired*. A long seam ran down its flank like an old scar, dark as a healed wound, and where the moonlight touched its bronze surface the metal didn't shine—it *smoldered*, faintly, the way coals smolder under ash. Not firelight. Something older. Ember-colored, ember-deep.

Silas stopped three paces away and his hands began, very quietly, to burn.

Not pain. Never pain—that had been the first thing his teachers burned into him, that swordspeech was not suffering but *speech*, that the fire answered the truth of what was said. But his hands woke the way a sleeper wakes when someone speaks his name in another room. The scars along his knuckles, seven years white and silent, flushed a dull red-gold, and under his skin the old syllables stirred like fish under ice.

"It knows you," Wren whispered.

"It knows what I am." He flexed his fingers. Watched the glow pulse. "What I was."

He approached the bell the way you approach a grave you helped dig. Up close he could see that the crack wasn't empty—deep inside the seam, something glimmered, a sliver of light wedged in the wound of the metal like a splinter of dawn. The First Shard, pulled gasping from the drowned chapel, hung warm against his sternum where he wore it on a cord; now it warmed further, answering, and the two lights began to breathe in time with each other. In time with his heartbeat. That was the part he hated. That was the part that felt like being *recognized*.

"So we just take it?" Wren had her transcription kit open—she carried it everywhere now, ink and reed pen and the good rag paper, because a scholar who had lost one archive learned to carry archives on her person. "Pry it loose? Because I have a chisel—"

"No."

"No?"

"If we pry it, we get a dead splinter and a cracked bell and probably half the tower coming down on us." He turned his palm up. The glow in his hand steadied, brightened. "Shards aren't objects, Wren. They're *resonances*. Fragments of the covenant given weight. You don't dig a resonance out. You call it, and it comes—or it doesn't."

"Call it how?"

He looked at the bell. The bell, in some way he couldn't describe and wouldn't try to, looked back.

"It wants my voice," he said. And his stomach dropped through the two hundred and eleven steps, through the flagstones, through the bedrock of Vellamark itself, because he had known it since the stair, since the mirror, since the moment Bramblewick said *oldest song*. "It wants the oath-verse."

Wren went very still. "You swore never to speak it again."

"I know what I swore."

"After the Severance. You told me. You said you cut it out of yourself like—" She reached for his hands, stopped short of touching, her fingers hovering over the glowing scars. "Silas. You said the verse was the last thing your brothers and sisters heard before Vey's blades came down. You said you'd rather lose your hands than your voice say those words again."

"I remember." His voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "I was there."

Outside, far below, a Warden patrol passed along the close, boots ringing on cobbles, lantern-light swinging pale across the tower's lower windows. They both froze until it faded. When Silas spoke again it was barely above breath.

"Here's what happens instead. We leave. We take one shard to the Undermoon and tell Bramblewick we tried, and the Undermoon finishes dying, and the thirteenth turn comes, and everything Bramblewick is, everything the orchard was, seals behind a door that never opens—and Vey keeps his stolen years and his starving city, and the ban holds forever, and I keep my vow." He laughed, short and joyless. "There. That's the alternative. Shall I write it down for you? You're the archivist."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then she set her transcription kit down on the stone ledge, squared the paper, dipped the pen, and said, "Sing."

"Wren—"

"*Sing*, Silas. I'll take it down. Every word. If you won't keep it, then I'll keep it for you." Her eyes were fierce and wet in the moonlight. "That's what an archive *is*. It's what people can't bear to hold, held anyway. Now give it to me."

* * *

He stood before the cracked bell with the moon full on his face, and for a long time he could not begin.

The trouble was that the oath-verse was not a thing you remembered. It was a thing you *were*. Fourteen lines in the old tongue, the tongue that predated Vellamark, that predated the ban, that the emberkin had taught the first swordspeakers in the age when fire and flesh still spoke the same language. He had learned it at eight years old, kneeling on cold stone, his teacher's hand heavy between his shoulder blades: *Say it until it says you.* And it had. For twenty years the verse had said him—shaped his breath, his stride, his griefs. On the Night of Severance he had heard it in the mouths of the dying, sung broken and defiant as the blades came down, and he had sworn on the ash of his order that his voice would never carry it again.

Seven years of silence. Seven years of luck-knots and harmless charms and magic sanded down to nothing, and now—

Now the bell waited. And inside the bell, the shard waited. And inside himself, the verse rose like floodwater against a door he had nailed shut with his own two scarred hands.

"I need you to understand," he said quietly, not turning around, "that this is going to hurt more than the chapel. More than the wardens. This is going to hurt like the Severance."

"I know," Wren said.

"No. You know *about* it. It isn't the same." He closed his eyes. "When I speak swordspeech, I speak *truth made loud*. And the truth of me, Wren, the whole truth, the one under all the other ones—I am the last of them because I ran. The verse knows that. It has always known that. When I open my mouth tonight, it's going to say so."

"Then let it," she said softly. "Let it say so, and I'll write that down too. All of it. The running and the returning. That's the whole record or none of it is worth keeping."

Somewhere below, the city murmured. Somewhere beyond the city, the moon was already climbing toward its turn. Silas filled his lungs the way his teacher had taught him—*from the ground, boy, from the roots, fire doesn't live in the chest, it lives in the earth and borrows you*—and unnailed the door.

He sang.

The first line came out wrecked. Rust and rubble and seven years of disuse, a voice like a hinge long unswung, and he almost stopped—but the bell caught it, caught even that ruined sound, and the shard inside the seam flared, and the bronze began to *hum*. Low at first, below hearing, a vibration in the floor, in the bones of his feet, in the fillings of his teeth. The pigeons exploded upward into the night in a single clatter of wings and were gone.

*Ember to ember, breath to breath,* he sang, and the second line was stronger, and the third line broke him.

Because the third line was the line about faithfulness, *oath kept warm in the house of bone*, and his voice knew exactly what his voice was worth, and it said so. Swordspeech was truth made loud. The truth made itself loud. The sound that came out of him on the third line was the sound of the Night of Severance—of Magda Voss singing the verse while the Warden blades took her arms, of old Tomas Ibarra singing it with a spear through him and finishing the line anyway, of forty voices and then thirty and then twelve and then three and then *one*, one voice running barefoot through burning cloisters with the verse tearing itself out of his throat unbidden because the covenant does not care about your vows of silence, the covenant cares about the *oath*, and the oath was breaking, breaking, breaking—

"Keep going." Wren's voice, shaking, steady. Pen scratching. "Keep going, Silas, I have it, keep *going*—"

He kept going. It was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than fleeing, harder than seven years of small gray charm-selling, harder than the chapel and the wight-to-come and everything after. He sang the verse of the covenant into the cracked bell of a cathedral whose god had gone quiet, and each line cost him a piece of the armor he'd built, and the armor came away not in plates but in *names*: Magda. Tomas. Ilse, who could split falling rain with her blade-song. Ferro, who hummed when he sharpened. The children of the cloister, whose names he had refused to speak for seven years because names spoken aloud become real again, and he could not afford real.

The bell took all of it. The crack in the bronze drank the sound like a wound drinking wine, and glowed from within, ember-red, gold, white—and the shard rose.

It came up through the seam slowly, reluctantly, a splinter of solid dawn sliding free of the metal, trailing threads of light like a drowning man's hair letting go of the surface. It hung in the air before Silas's mouth, between him and the bell, spinning, and it sang back to him—his own verse, his own voice, played backward and forward and inside out, all fourteen lines at once, the whole oath folded into a space smaller than his thumb.

His voice failed on the fourteenth line. The last line, the sealing line: *and what I am I give to the keeping.* He got halfway through it and his throat closed, and the note died, and the silence rang worse than the sound.

The shard hovered. Waiting. The bell dimmed. Everything waited on the last line.

Silas stood in the ruin of his own singing, tears cutting tracks through the ash-dust on his face, and understood—with the clarity that only total exhaustion brings—that the shard would not come to him incomplete. That oaths are not oaths until they end. That somewhere in the Undermoon, at the covenant's heart, on the thirteenth turn, he was going to have to finish this line for true.

But that was thirteen turns away. Tonight, tonight, he did the only other honest thing left. He stepped forward, took the unfinished shard gently in both burning hands, and spoke the final line *unvoiced*—mouthed it, gave it shape without sound, a promise in pantomime, a debt acknowledged but unpaid—and the shard shivered, and dimmed to a soft ember-orange, and settled into his palm like a bird deciding to trust him.

*Not paid,* its warmth seemed to say. *But noted.*

Wren's pen had stopped. She sat on the stone ledge with fourteen lines of ink before her and her hands pressed over her mouth, and when Silas turned to her she lowered her hands slowly and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"I have it. All of it. Even the part where you stopped." She lifted the page. The ink glistened. "Even the part where you couldn't finish. It's in here. It counts. It's *real* now, Silas. You put it back into the world."

He looked at the page—at his whole self, the verse that had said him, lying flat and legible in another person's handwriting—and felt something he had no word for, something adjacent to grief and adjacent to gratitude, occupying the border country between them. She was holding the thing he could not bear to hold. She had made it bearable by making it *kept*.

"Burn it," he said hoarsely. "If we're taken—burn it first. Before they take you."

"I'll burn myself first," Wren said, and folded the page into the lining of her coat, over her heart, and he believed her, and that belief frightened him more than the Wardens ever had.

* * *

They came down the tower stair in silence, the two shards warm against Silas's chest, beating together like twin hearts learning a shared rhythm. At step one hundred he finally spoke.

"When we do this," he said. "At the heart. On the last turn. I'll have to finish the line. Voiced. All of it. And I think—I think finishing it means *giving*. The keeping goes both ways. That's what the verse has been trying to tell me since I was eight years old and too young to listen." His hand found the wall in the dark. "*What I am I give to the keeping.* I always thought it meant the order keeps the oath. I'm starting to think it means the oath keeps... everything else. And you pay with what you are."

Wren was quiet for four steps. Five. Six.

"Then we'd better make sure," she said at last, "that what you are turns out to be worth the trade." And before he could answer: "It is. For the record. As your archivist, I'm officially noting that it is."

Below them, through the tower's arrow-slit windows, the lights of Vellamark spread out to the harbor—and beyond the harbor, at the world's wet edge, the tide had begun to turn. Two shards of five. Ten turns of the moon remaining.

And on the wind off the water, faint but unmistakable, came a sound none of them had heard in the city for nine years:

A single bell, somewhere, tolling once.

Then twice.

Then, soft and patient, beginning again—as if every cracked and silent bell in Vellamark had heard the old verse sung at last, and was remembering, line by line, how to answer.
