## 12. Turn Seven: The Moon Turns Its Face

The rain came to Vellamark on the seventh turn of the moon, and it came like an apology—soft, unhurried, washing the soot and lantern-oil smell from the streets until the whole city smelled of wet stone and old iron. Odile declared it a holiday. She said it the way she said everything, flat and final, thumping a crate of dried figs down onto the safehouse table as though the weather had personally requested her permission.

"No one moves cargo in this," she said. "No one hunts anyone in this either. The Wardens hate getting their boots wet. So tonight, we are nobody, and we do nothing, and we are grateful for it."

Silas sat by the hearth with his hands wrapped around a clay mug of mulled wine that had gone lukewarm an hour ago because he kept forgetting to drink it. Across the room, Wren was at the long table under a lamp, surrounded by her transcriptions—the oath-verses she'd copied in her small, precise hand, the maps of tide-gates and moon-doors, the growing dossier of everything they knew about Vey's spire. Her hair had escaped its braid again. There was ink on her left cheekbone where she'd pushed a stray curl away without thinking.

He had been watching her for three days now, and he could not seem to stop.

It was a dangerous thing, noticing. For seven years he had made a discipline of not-noticing—noticing led to caring, caring led to names spoken aloud, and names spoken aloud were the first thing the Wardens asked for. But somewhere between the drowned chapel and the bell tower, between her blood soaking through his shirt and her voice steadying him through the second shard's song, the discipline had quietly failed, and now he noticed everything: how she read with her lips slightly parted; how she tapped her pen three times before committing a line; how, when she thought no one was looking, her eyes went distant and sad, and he wondered what—or whom—she was seeing.

*She is hiding something,* said the small cold voice that had kept him alive for seven years. *People who seek you out deliberately always want something.*

But the voice sounded thinner lately. Worn smooth, like a coin passed through too many hands.

"Silas." Odile dropped into the chair across from him, her bad knee cracking like green wood. "You're brooding."

"I'm resting."

"You're brooding with your eyes open. I've known swordsmen forty years, boy. Resting looks different." She nodded toward Wren without turning her head, a smuggler's trick of indirection. "The scholar hasn't slept since the bell tower. Make her. You've got the face for it—the one people listen to when they're too tired to argue."

"I don't have that face."

Odile snorted. "You have exactly that face. It's why I fed your order, back when feeding them would've gotten me hanged twice over." She rose, wincing, and paused at his shoulder. Softer, almost against the rain-noise: "Whatever you're not saying to her—say it or don't. But decide. Half-said things rot faster than unsaid ones. I've seen it sink ships."

Then she was gone, up the stairs to her ledger-room, leaving the rain and the fire and the two of them and all the words neither of them had touched.

Wren felt Silas's gaze the way she sometimes felt a page's worth before reading it—a pressure, a shape of meaning waiting to be unfolded. She kept her pen moving. If she stopped, she would look up. If she looked up, she would have to decide what her face did, and she had spent six turns of the moon carefully managing what her face did.

The transcription lay before her: the full oath-verse, restored from the cracked bell's resonance, written out in her hand while Silas had knelt beneath it shaking like a man standing in a river of ice. She had watched him sing words he'd sworn never to speak again, and she had understood, in that moment, something she still hadn't found the courage to say: that she had come to him not for the prophecy, not really, not anymore. She had come to him because he was the first true thing she'd found after years among falsified archives and polite lies—and because every step closer to saving Tam put her deeper into deception, and the weight of it was starting to bend her.

*Turn twelve,* she reminded herself. *Tell him at turn twelve. When we're close enough to the end that the truth can't undo us.*

That was what she told herself. But lies told to oneself were still lies, and she was, whatever else, an honest scholar.

"You should sleep," Silas said.

"I will."

"That's what you said last night."

"And yet here I am, having slept, and risen, and worked." She glanced up before she could stop herself, and there it was—that face Odile meant, the one that was mostly stillness but had, underneath, a warmth like banked coals. "Is this an intervention?"

"It's a request. From someone who needs you sharp tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," she echoed. The word landed strangely. Seven turns behind them, six ahead. The moon hung half-lit over the harbor, its face turned three-quarters away, like someone deciding whether to look at you or leave.

Silas crossed the room—not quickly, never quickly, he moved like a man conserving some inner fuel—and set his forgotten wine beside her lamp. Then, instead of retreating, he pulled out the opposite chair and sat, elbows on the table, and looked at her across the scattered pages as if they were two scholars arguing over a text instead of two fugitives counting down a moon.

"Read me something," he said.

"What?"

"Anything. Not the verses. Something old. Something beautiful. You have a scholar's voice—you read like the words matter." He hesitated, and she saw the admission cost him. "I haven't heard anyone read aloud since—" His jaw tightened. "In a long time."

So Wren turned through her papers until she found what she wanted: not covenant law, not prophecy, but a fragment of pre-ban poetry she'd copied as a girl, some anonymous Vellamark verse about the harbor before the Wardens, about lamps on black water. She read it low, pitched under the rain, and Silas closed his eyes, and for a little while the safehouse was not a hiding place but simply a warm room where a woman read poetry to a tired man.

When she finished, he didn't move for a moment. Then he said, quietly, "My mother used to read like that. Before." Before the order took him at nine years old, was what he didn't say; before swordspeech burned the ordinary life out of him like a brand through cloth.

"Silas." She turned her pen over in her fingers. "Can I ask you something and you won't deflect? Just for one question."

"One," he agreed, warily.

"After all this—after the thirteenth turn, whichever way it breaks—what do you want? Not what does the covenant need. What do *you* want?"

The rain filled the silence. She watched him reach for deflection and fail to find it, watched him sit with the question the way he sat with pain, straight-backed and silent, letting it cut.

"A garden," he said finally, surprising them both. "Somewhere outside the walls. My master in the order—he was the one kind thing in it—he had a garden behind the chapterhouse. Tomatoes. He used to say magic was just another way of gardening, coaxing things to grow that shouldn't." A breath. "I want tomatoes. I want a door I can leave unlocked. I want—" He stopped.

"Say it," she whispered. "Half-said things rot."

Something moved through his face then—fear, and under the fear, a stubborn tenderness that made her chest ache. He reached across the table, slowly, giving her every chance to pull away, and took her hand. His scarred palm was warm. The old power in him stirred faintly against her skin, like a cat waking, and she did not flinch from it, and something in him seemed to settle at that.

"Wren," he began—

The mirror screamed.

Not broke—screamed. Every pane of glass in Odile's front room went white at once, light pouring off them like frost given a voice, and Silas was on his feet with a knife drawn before the sound finished, body between Wren and the wall, seven years of instinct firing at once.

In the largest mirror—the tarnished oval above the dead fireplace—a face formed.

Bramblewick.

Except it wasn't, quite. The emberkin child appeared as she always did, round-eyed and ash-pale, crowned in small patient flames—but tonight her edges flickered like a candle in wind, and where her left hand should have been there was only a shimmering absence, a place where the fire had simply stopped being sure of itself.

"Bramblewick—" Wren was up, crossing the room. "What's happening to you?"

"Turn seven," the spirit said, and her voice, usually bright as struck flint, came thin and doubled, as though speaking through water. "The moon has turned its face, and I can see you only because the veil is tearing. Listen. Listen, oh, listen quickly, because I am spending myself to be here and I do not have much of myself left to spend."

"The Undermoon," Silas said. "It's worse than you told us."

"It is dying *fast*, swordspeaker." Bramblewick's flame-crown guttered. "The orchard burns smaller each night. The rivers of ember run shallow. Three of my sisters went dark between the fifth turn and the sixth—they did not die. Dying leaves something behind. They simply... stopped, like a word cut off mid-syllable." Her great dark eyes fixed on Wren, then Silas, and there was something in them Wren had never seen there before: fear. "The queen's memory told you the truth of the terms. But she did not tell you—could not know—how little time the terms leave us. We believed thirteen turns was generous. We were wrong. The seal does not wait for the moon to finish its course. The seal is coming early, and it is coming hungry."

"How early?" Silas's voice was flat, controlled, but Wren saw his knuckles whiten on the knife-hilt. "How many turns do we actually have?"

Bramblewick's image shuddered. Static crawled across her face like frost ferns.

"Two," she said. "Perhaps three, if the orchard fights. Turn nine, maybe turn ten, the door between realms begins closing on its own. After that, no shard can be carried through. No oath can be reforged. Everything ends—not loudly, not with burning. Quietly. Like a house going cold when no one tends the fire."

"Then we go to the Hollow Court now," Silas said. "Tonight. Rain or no rain."

"You cannot," Bramblewick said. "The moon-door answers to the tide and the turn both, and the seventh turn's door opens only at high water, hours from now, and only briefly. I am sorry. I am sorry—I know what waiting costs you. It costs me more." And then, softer, the child-voice fracturing: "Silas. Wren. I showed you the orchard because it was beautiful. I wanted your first sight of my home to be beautiful. I am glad, now, that you saw it that way. Whatever happens—remember it burning, and remember that it was not pain. It was only light, all the way down. Only light."

Her hand—the remaining one—rose, small fingers splayed against the inside of the glass, as if touching a window from the far side of winter.

And Wren, without deciding to, raised her own hand and pressed it to the mirror. Cool glass. Nothing else. But Bramblewick smiled, sudden and blazing, and for one heartbeat the flicker at her edges stilled.

"Found-family," the emberkin whispered, tasting the mortal words like sweets. "Odile taught me the word. I like it better than covenant. Covenants can break. Families—" The image stuttered. "Families choose. Hold fast till the ninth turn. Come through the door running. And Silas—"

"Yes?"

"When you find the Oath-Wight, it will wear faces you loved. Let Wren stand behind you. Not because you need guarding." A flicker, almost mischief, almost grief. "Because some truths are easier to tell when someone who chose you is watching."

The mirrors went dark all at once, every pane, and the room was only a room again—rain, lamplight, the smell of figs and woodsmoke—and the silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

Silas stood a long moment with his back to Wren, knife still bare in his fist, shoulders rising and falling. Then he sheathed it, turned, and looked at her, and whatever had been about to pass between them before the glass screamed was still there, suspended, unfinished—a held breath neither dared exhale.

"Two turns," he said hoarsely. "Maybe three."

"Then we spend tonight resting," Wren said, "and tomorrow preparing, and the ninth turn running." She picked up his abandoned wine and pressed it into his hands. "Drink. Sit. Finish your sentence someday."

A ghost of a smile. "Someday," he agreed, and it was a promise shaped like a deferral, and they both pretended not to notice, because some coals must be left banked to survive the night.

They worked until the lamp burned low—her transcribing, him honing blades and checking the map of the Hollow Court until he could draw it blind—and when she finally slept, curled in the chair with her cheek on her own folded arms, Silas covered her with Odile's good wool blanket and stood watch by the window, watching the half-turned moon drag its broken face across the rain.

Below, in the harbor, the tide was beginning to rise.

And far beneath the waterline, in a realm of ash and small fires, something vast and cold had begun, quietly, to count.
