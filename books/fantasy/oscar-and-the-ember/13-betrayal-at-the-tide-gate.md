## 13. Betrayal at the Tide Gate

The bell of Saint Aldric's had not yet finished its ninth toll when Odile came down the stairs with her hand pressed flat against the wall, the way she did now on bad days, as if the house might pitch beneath her like a ship.

"Someone's been in my ledger," she said.

Silas looked up from the table where he'd been rewrapping the Second Shard in oilcloth. It hummed faintly even through the cloth—a sound like a struck tuning fork remembered rather than heard—and he felt it answer something in his chest, the way it always did. Wren sat across from him with her transcription spread out before her, ink-stained fingers still moving, still copying the oath-verse in her careful archive hand. She didn't look up. She had developed the habit of not looking up when Odile spoke of danger, as though stillness were a kind of armor.

"What do you mean, someone's been in your ledger?"

Odile lowered herself into the chair by the cold hearth. She was sixty if she was a day, and her hands—hands that had once hauled contraband through three kingdoms' worth of customs houses—trembled now, and hated themselves for it. "I keep two ledgers. One for the wine trade, which is what the tax men see. And one for everything else. The safehouse routes. The names. The moon-door tides." She tapped her temple. "It's all up here too, but I write it down because memory is a liar that flatters you."

"And?"

"And the second ledger is four pages short." Her eyes, pale as canal water, found Silas's across the table. "Four pages. Torn, not cut. Someone in a hurry."

Silas went very still. Outside, Vellamark was settling into its evening noises—the groan of cart wheels on cobbles, a fishwife arguing with the tide, somewhere a fiddle badly played. Ordinary sounds. He had almost begun to trust them again.

"Perrin," Odile said quietly.

"You don't know that."

"I know that boy has been asking me questions for a month. Not the questions an apprentice asks—*how much*, *when do we sail*—but *who else knows this route*, *where does the door open*, *what does the magistrate pay for names these days*. I thought it was curiosity. Curiosity is cheap and common in the young." She rubbed her face with both hands. "I thought it was curiosity because I wanted it to be. That's the oldest mistake in smuggling, Nakamura. Wanting the cargo to be clean."

Wren set down her pen. "When did you last see him?"

"This morning. He said he was going to his sister's in Lowmarsh." Odile's mouth twisted. "He doesn't have a sister in Lowmarsh. He has a sister in the Spire district, and she married a clerk, and clerks talk to magistrates."

Silas stood. The Shard shifted under the oilcloth, and its hum deepened, as if it too understood what was coming. "We leave tonight. Before the tide turns. Bramblewick said the moon-door opens at slack water—we take the Shard back to the orchard, we don't wait for—"

"No," Odile said.

He stopped.

"You'll go at low tide, same as planned, because going early means going panicked, and panicked people drown in the Undermoon. But you won't go through the harbor gate." She was already rising, moving toward the back of the kitchen with the brisk economy of a woman who had packed a life into satchels more times than she could count. "There's another way down. The old smugglers' stair behind the salt warehouse—it comes out at the tide gate itself, below the Wardens' patrol line. Perrin knows about the harbor approach because it's in the ledger. He doesn't know about the stair because the stair isn't written anywhere." She glanced back at Silas, and there was something in her face he couldn't read then, though he would understand it later, turning it over in the long dark hours after. Something settled. Something already saying goodbye. "Get your things. Both of you. We've perhaps two hours before anyone acts on those pages, and I'd rather spend them walking than talking."

* * *

They went out through the coal cellar, down a passage that smelled of brine and old rope, and emerged into the blue hour of evening with the moon already climbing—fat and white and one turn past its halfway mark, so that it hung over Vellamark like a coin someone had flipped and not yet caught.

Wren walked close beside Silas, her satchel heavy with transcriptions. She hadn't spoken since the kitchen. He could feel her thinking, the way you feel weather coming—pressure behind the ears.

"She's staying behind," Wren said finally, low enough that only he could hear. Ahead of them, Odile moved through the alley shadows with surprising speed, her lantern shuttered to a knife-slit of light. "You saw it. In how she talked. She's planning to stay behind."

"I saw it."

"Then say something."

"To Odile?" He almost laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, bitter. "Wren, that woman ran guns past the Vellamark blockade during the Salt War. You don't talk her out of anything. You just hope she decides you're worth dying next to instead of for."

"That's not comforting."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

They crossed the empty fish market, its stalls folded and chained, gulls muttering on the pilings. The harbor opened before them, black water stitched with the reflections of moored lanterns, and beyond it the tide gate rose from the sea wall like the jaw of some drowned giant—the great arched sluice through which the harbor breathed twice a day, its iron teeth crusted with barnacle and weed. At its base, half-drowned at low tide, the moon-door waited: a seam of silver light in the stone that ordinary eyes never saw, but which Silas could feel pulling at him from thirty yards away, gentle and insistent, like a hand tugging his sleeve.

Bramblewick had told them: *the door opens when the sea forgets which way it's going. Slack water. Be quick, seedling. The Undermoon is thin as paper now.*

And then Odile stopped.

She stopped so suddenly that Silas nearly walked into her back, and he knew before she spoke, knew by the way her shoulders squared and her chin lifted, that the night had turned.

At the head of the quay, where the lamplight pooled yellow and thick, stood six figures in grey-and-crimson. Wardens. Their crossbows were not raised—that would have been crude—but they were held in that easy, ready way that meant the raising would take half a heartbeat. And in front of them, wringing his cap in his hands, stood a narrow-shouldered young man who could not make himself look at the house he had betrayed.

Perrin.

"Oh, child," Odile said softly. Not to Silas. To the boy across the water-dark quay. "Oh, you stupid, hungry child."

A voice carried across the stones, smooth as poured oil. "Mistress Odile Vasseur." Magistrate Aldous Vey stepped forward out of the lamplight, and Silas's whole body went cold, because Vey was not supposed to come himself. Vey sent men. Vey sent warrants and Warden captains and polite letters with wax seals. Vey did not stand on wet cobblestones at dusk with his grey coat immaculate and his hands folded behind his back like a man admiring the view.

But here he was. Older than his years and younger than his age, somehow both at once, his face unlined in a way that faces should not be, his eyes bright with borrowed fire.

"The tide is low," Vey called pleasantly. "I'm told that matters tonight. I'm told many things lately. My informant has been very thorough." His gaze slid, deliberate and cruel, to Perrin, who flinched as though struck. "Though I confess he undersold the company you keep. A Corvane. I thought your family had learned to stay buried in the archives where they belong."

"Vey." Silas stepped forward, putting himself between the magistrate and the others, and felt the old words rise in his throat, hot and eager, the swordspeech waking in his blood like a dog hearing its name. He strangled it down. Not here. Not with crossbows trained on Wren. "Seven years I stayed small. Seven years I sold luck-knots and kept my head down. Why can't you let the dead stay dead?"

"Because the dead are *useful*, Nakamura." Vey began to walk along the quay, unhurried, his boots ringing on the stones, and the Wardens moved with him in perfect formation. "That's the lesson your order never learned. Power hoarded is power protected. Power *shared*—" he smiled, and the smile was the worst thing Silas had seen in seven years, worse than the Night of Severance, because it was patient— "power shared is power wasted. Look at this city. Coughing on coal smoke, drowning its wonder in ledgers and law. I have given Vellamark order. All I ask is that no one dig up what I buried to get it."

"You mean the covenant."

"I mean the *mess*," Vey corrected. "Now. You're carrying something that sings. I can hear it from here—it's been singing to me for days, getting louder as you carry it about the city like a stolen loaf. Give it to me, and I give you the amnesty I offered before. Both of you. Full pardon, papers, passage anywhere in the nine provinces. The girl's brother walks out of my cells by morning." His eyes flicked to Wren, and softened with practiced warmth. "Tomas Corvane. He writes letters, you know. Very loyal letters. He asks about you in every one."

Silas heard Wren's breath catch beside him. Heard the small wounded sound she made and swallowed.

"Don't," he said quietly, without turning. "He's showing you the hook, Wren. Don't look at the bait. Look at the hook."

"He has my brother."

"He has a *story* about your brother. There's a difference, and you know it—you of all people know what a well-made document can lie about."

Across the quay, Vey sighed, and the warmth drained from his face like water from a pulled plug, leaving something ancient and starved underneath. "I had hoped to do this politely. I so rarely get to be polite." He raised one hand, and light gathered in his palm—not candlelight, not lamplight, but a deep ember-orange glow that made the shadows around him lean away. Stolen light. Covenant light, siphoned through seven years of slow theft. "Kill the scholar. Bring me the swordspeaker breathing. The old woman—" a pause, almost delicate— "the old woman can choose her side."

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do, each moment stepping on the heels of the one before.

Odile moved first.

Later, Silas would try to reconstruct it and fail, because grief makes a poor archivist. He would remember fragments: the flash of her shuttered lantern swung wide, sudden daylight-bright, blinding the nearest Warden; the roar of her voice—some old smuggler's cant, a signal cry, calling to a crew twenty years scattered or dead; the scrape of the boarding axe she had carried hidden under her coat the entire walk, an axe she must have taken from above the hearth while Silas wasn't looking, because she had known, she had *known* since the missing ledger pages, exactly what kind of night this would be.

"THE STAIR," she bellowed. "GO DOWN THE STAIR AND DON'T YOU DARE LOOK BACK."

"But—" Wren started.

"MOVE, girl!"

The first bolt took Odile high in the shoulder and spun her half around, and she did not fall, she *laughed*, a terrible joyous sound, and swung the axe into the jaw of the Warden closing on her. Silas drew steel and put himself into the gap, and the swordspeech tore out of him at last, no longer strangled: "*Stand!*"—and the word hit the three nearest Wardens like a wave, freezing their arms mid-raise, their muscles locked by the compulsion of commanded speech. Seven years of silence and the power came back to him whole, monstrous, glorious, and he hated every instant of how good it felt.

"Silas!" Wren had the Shard out of the oilcloth, holding it bare in both hands, and it blazed like a captive sunrise. "The door! It's opening!"

Below the quay, in the black gap beneath the tide gate, the moon-door had split the stone with silver. Slack water. The sea forgetting which way it was going.

"Odile!" Silas fought backward toward the edge, blade red, the frozen Wardens toppling like cut puppets as his word released them. "Come *on*!"

She was at the quay's edge already, leaning on the axe, blood sheeting down her arm, and she was smiling at him. That was the part he would carry forever. She was smiling as though he were the one who needed comfort.

"Boy," she said—she had never once called him *swordspeaker*, only *boy*, from the first night she'd fed him soup in a cellar full of echoes—"I'm seventy-one years old, my knees are gone, and I bleed like a stuck pig. I'd slow you down through that door and you know it." Behind her, Vey was walking closer through the chaos, unhurried still, drawing more light into his hands, and the remaining Wardens were regrouping, reloading, and there were more of them coming down the harbor road, lanterns bobbing like will-o'-wisps. "Somebody holds the line. That's the whole trade, in the end. Somebody holds the line so the cargo gets through. I've done it my whole life. Let me do it once for something that matters."

"We can hold it together—"

"*No.*" And she drove the axe head into the stone between them—not at him, never at him—splitting the quay's edge, opening a gap of dark water between the fight and the door. "Take the girl. Take the song. Finish it." Her voice cracked, just once, just barely. "Tell Bramblewick the trees should burn brighter for me. Tell them Odile Vasseur paid her debts."

Vey's voice cut across everything, no longer pleasant: "Enough. *Take them.*"

Crossbows sang. Silas grabbed Wren around the waist and jumped.

They fell together through the silver seam, through cold and light and the sensation of being turned inside out gently, and the last thing Silas saw of the mortal world was Odile Vasseur standing alone on the broken quay with her axe raised against the lamplight, laughing, laughing, refusing to fall—

—and then the Undermoon closed around them like a mouth, and the door sealed behind them with a sound like a struck bell rung underwater, and whatever happened on the other side, they would never see it, and that was its own kind of wound.

* * *

The ash orchard received them without ceremony. They landed hard among the black trees, and the small fires in the branches guttered at their arrival, dimming, and Silas understood dimly that the realm itself was weaker now, thinner, the way Bramblewick had warned—paper-thin, a lamp running out of oil.

Wren was sobbing. Not loudly. She knelt in the ash with the Shard clutched to her chest, making small, controlled sounds, the crying of someone who had taught herself to grieve quietly in libraries and archives where noise was punished. Silas crawled to her and held her, and let her, and did not tell her it would be all right, because he was done lying to people he loved.

"She knew," Wren whispered against his shoulder. "From the moment she found the ledger. She knew and she didn't send us away, she didn't turn us out to protect herself, she just—she just started planning how to die usefully."

"Yes."

"I never thanked her. I never—I ate her bread for twelve days and I never once—"

"She knew that too." Silas's voice came out rough, scraped raw. "That woman knew everything. It was her superpower. Knowing things and feeding people anyway."

The fires in the trees burned lower. Somewhere deeper in the orchard, a sound reached them—thin, wavering, unmistakably a child crying. Bramblewick. She had felt the door seal. She had felt the twelfth turn approaching and the Third Shard still unrecovered and the emberkin fading like breath on glass, and now she had felt something else arrive through the moon-door: the shape of a loss, carried in on the shoulders of the two mortals she had staked everything on.

Silas stood. His hands were shaking—with rage, he realized, not fear. Rage at Vey, cool and patient in his grey coat. Rage at Perrin, wringing his cap on the quay. Rage at himself, for walking into a trap he'd been warned of, for letting an old woman buy their escape with the only coin she had left.

Wren looked up at him, tear-tracked, ash-smeared, and he saw his own fury reflected in her face and watched it harden there into something with edges.

"They'll pay for it," she said. It was not a question. It was not even quite a wish. It was a sentence being drafted, precise as her transcription hand, and it frightened him a little, how much he loved it.

"Not them," Silas said. He looked east, where beyond the dimming orchard the Hollow Court waited with its Oath-Wight and its dead queen and its shard guarded by broken promises. Then he looked north, through the thinning veil, toward the mortal city and the spire where a thief in magistrate's robes was even now counting his stolen fire. "Not *they*. Him. Vey ends this. On the thirteenth turn, one way or another, Vey ends."

In the branches overhead, the small fires flickered—and then, all together, as if the orchard itself had heard him, they flared.

Deeper in the ash trees, the crying stopped. Footsteps came quick and light through the gloom, and Bramblewick appeared between the trunks, translucent at the edges, her ember-eyes enormous—and behind her, far off across the darkening plain, the Hollow Court's towers rose against a sky that had begun, ever so slowly, to lose its stars.

"The queen's court," Bramblewick whispered. "You have to go now, seedlings. Tonight. While the door between worlds is still bleeding light." Her small hand closed around Silas's finger, burning cold. "Before the moon finishes turning. Before *he* finds the last shard first."

Silas tightened his grip on the sword he no longer trusted, and the Shard sang against Wren's chest, and somewhere behind them a dead woman's laughter still echoed off the walls of a city that would never learn her name.

They walked toward the Court of Broken Oaths.
