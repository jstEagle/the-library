## 14. Undermoon, Dimming

The moon-door let them go the way a wound lets go of blood—reluctantly, and leaving something behind.

Silas came through first, boots striking ash that had been soft as snowfall when they'd left it. Now it crunched. That was his first warning. The second was the light.

The orchard had dimmed.

Seven turns ago—or however long ago it had been, time moving strangely between realms—the trees had burned with small, steady flames, each one a different color: rose-gold, deep amber, the blue-green of harbor ice. They had lit the ash-plain like lanterns hung in an endless garden, and their warmth had reached into him and found the cold place where his guilt lived and warmed it, briefly, against his will.

Now half those fires were out. The trees still stood in their patient rows, black-limbed and graceful, but whole avenues of them were dark, their branches bare of flame, and where fire should have pooled at the roots there was only grey powder that stirred without wind. The sky above—the Undermoon's sky, which was not a sky at all but the pale underside of the mortal moon seen from beneath—had thickened. Clouds of something like smoke moved across it, though nothing here truly burned.

Behind him, Wren came through the door with a stumble and a gasp, the Second Shard wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. She'd insisted on carrying it after the tide gate. After Odile. She carried it the way mourners carry ashes, close enough to feel the weight, too heavy to set down anywhere.

"Bramblewick?" she said, before she'd even finished finding her footing.

That was Wren now. Three words of greeting, and two of them were for the emberkin child. Silas said nothing. He was already walking.

They found Bramblewick where she always waited—at the orchard's heart, beneath the oldest tree, the First Mother whose trunk was wide as a chapel and whose crown held the largest fire, the one the emberkin called the Hearthlight. Except the Hearthlight was no longer large. It had shrunk to something the size of a cookfire, guttering, and beneath it sat Bramblewick on her heels, very still, in the way children are still only when they are trying very hard to be brave.

She didn't turn around when they approached. She said, "You're late."

"We came as fast as we could," Silas said. "The tide gate. We lost—"

"I know what you lost." Her voice was smaller than he remembered. "I felt it. When she died on your side, a hundred trees went out here at once. Like snuffed candles. Like someone walking down a row of them with wet fingers." She turned then, and Silas felt Wren's breath catch beside him.

Bramblewick was fading.

There was no other word for it. Her edges had gone translucent—the outer curve of her ears, the tips of her fingers, the hem of her little smock of woven embers—all of it thinning to glass, so that the Hearthlight behind her showed through her skin like a candle behind paper. Where she was most solid, at her center, her chest, she still glowed warm orange. But the glow was working harder now. He could see it pulsing. Striving. A heart pumping light instead of blood.

"Oh," Wren said softly. It wasn't a word. It was a wound making a sound.

"Don't do that," Bramblewick snapped, and some of her old fierceness flared, and for a moment her edges firmed. "Don't look at me like I'm already ash. I'm not. I'm *dimming*. There's a difference. Ash is done. Dim is just... waiting."

"Waiting for what?" Wren asked, kneeling to her level.

"For the thirteenth turn. For both doors to seal. For everything that's left of us to go out all together, polite and quiet, like good guests at the end of a party." She smiled, and it was the worst thing Silas had seen since the tide gate. "Or waiting for you two to stop being slow."

Silas crouched. Up close, he could see that even her eyes had changed—once they had been coals banked in white, but now the white had gone greyish, and the coals within flickered like the last stubborn embers of a fire nobody tended. "Tell us about the Third Shard," he said gently. "Everything. Leave nothing out because you think it will frighten us. Frightened is what we're for."

Bramblewick looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—a small, cracked sound, more cough than joy—and pressed her fading hands over her face.

"You sound like her," she said through her fingers. "The Queen. She used to say that. *Frightened is what we're for.* She said mortals made better covenant-bearers than emberkin ever did, because emberkin burn steady and mortals burn afraid, and fear is what makes you careful with holy things." She dropped her hands. "All right. Listen. And listen *all* the way through, because I've told this twice already to others who came for shards, and neither of them listened all the way through, and neither of them came back."

Wren had her journal out instantly—of course she did; grief had not slowed her ink one drop—and Bramblewick began.

"The Third Shard is not hidden. That's the first thing. The First hid itself in water because water forgets. The Second hid itself in song because song forgives. But the Third—" her translucent fingers curled, "—the Third is kept. It sits on the throne dais in the Hollow Court, in the Queen's own hall, under the light of the last true moonrise this realm ever saw, frozen there. And it is guarded by the Oath-Wight."

Silas went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the dimming orchard. He knew the word. Every swordspeaker had known the word, spoken late at night in barracks, the way soldiers speak of the things they hope never to meet. A wight made of oaths. Not broken by violence—but broken by *abandonment*. Every vow sworn to the emberkin and then walked away from, every promise the order made and unmade, every midnight assurance given to frightened emberkin children that *the swordspeakers will come, the swordspeakers will always come*—all of it, given weight and hunger and shape.

"It wears faces," Bramblewick said, watching him. "I can see you know that too. Good. Then I don't have to say whose."

"The order's," Silas said. His voice came out flat. "It wears the faces of the dead swordspeakers."

"It wears the faces of everyone who ever swore and failed." She tilted her head, and the gesture was heartbreakingly childlike even as her edges shimmered like heat-haze. "But mostly yours. Your order broke biggest. Biggest break, loudest voice."

Wren's pen had stopped. "How do we fight it?"

"That's the part nobody listens all the way through," Bramblewick said quietly. "You don't. Swords don't cut it—it's made of promises, and promises aren't cut, they're *kept or they aren't*. Fire doesn't burn it—it IS fire, the cold kind, the kind that lives in the gap between what was said and what was done." She looked up at Silas, and there was something ancient in her young face, something that made her suddenly seem far older than the child-shape she wore, older than the orchard, old as the first oath. "The only thing that has ever passed the Oath-Wight is truth. Plain, ugly, unarmored truth, spoken by the one it accuses. You cannot defeat it. You can only *answer* it. And most people can't. Most people would rather die than stand naked in front of what they've spent their whole lives hiding from."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the dark rows of trees, a single remaining flame guttered and went out, and none of the others seemed to notice.

"And if we fail to answer?" Wren asked. Her voice was steady. Her hand, Silas noticed, was not.

"Then it keeps you," Bramblewick said simply. "It adds you to itself. Another face. Another voice. There are voices in that thing that have been screaming for three hundred years." She stood, wobbling slightly, and Silas instinctively reached out to steady her—and his hand passed through her shoulder like smoke before finding purchase on something barely more solid than memory.

He pulled back as if scalded. Bramblewick looked at her own arm, at the place where his hand had sunk through, and said nothing at all, which was louder than any scream.

* * *

They made camp that night—or what passed for night beneath the underside-moon—in the lee of the First Mother, near enough that the dying Hearthlight threw their shadows long and trembling across the ash. Wren built a fire from fallen emberwood, which burned cold and blue and gave no heat, but gave light, and light was enough. Light was all anything had left to give.

Wren worked by that light. Of course she worked. Silas watched her transcribe everything Bramblewick had told them, cross-referencing fragments of the prophecy book, her pen scratching away like a small persistent insect against the vast quiet of the dimming realm. This was how she mourned, he was learning. Not with tears—though he'd seen those once, at the tide gate, quickly blinked away and denied ever after—but with work. With ink. With the furious belief that recording a thing was the same as honoring it.

"She'd have liked you," Wren said, not looking up. "Odile. She'd have liked Bramblewick too. Anyone who talked back."

"You talk back enough for all of us."

"Yes, well." A ghost of her old crooked smile. "Someone has to keep you humble, and the universe clearly isn't doing it."

He almost laughed. It caught somewhere in his chest and died there, next to everything else he'd buried lately. He fed another stick of emberwood to the cold fire and watched the blue flames climb.

"Wren." He hadn't meant to say her name. It came out anyway. "When we go to the Hollow Court. If the Oath-Wight takes my answer and finds it wanting—if it keeps me—you take the shard and you go. You don't negotiate. You don't wait. You go through the moon-door and you finish this."

Her pen stopped.

"I'm not going to argue with you about it," he continued, staring into the blue fire. "I know you'll want to. I know you'll have seventeen reasons ready before I finish the sentence. But I need you to hear me say it plainly, one time, so that later—whatever happens later—you'll know it wasn't bravado. Seven years ago I ran from my order while they burned. I told myself it was strategy. Survival. That living to fight was worth more than dying well. Maybe some of that was even true. But the truth underneath is that I was afraid, and I chose myself, and I have paid for that choice every day since in a currency I couldn't name until now." He finally looked at her. "So this time, I'm telling you ahead of time: I choose the shard. I choose the covenant. If the cost is me, pay it. Promise me."

Wren set down her pen with great deliberation, the way one sets down a blade.

"No," she said.

"Wren—"

"No. You don't get to spend yourself like coin and call it nobility, Silas Nakamura. I've read every account of the Severance that survived the burning. Do you know what I learned? That your order died because every single one of them decided alone what the sacrifice was, and none of them trusted anyone else with the deciding." She leaned forward, and the cold firelight carved her face into planes of light and shadow. "You want my promise? Here's mine: I will carry you out of that court on my back if I have to. I will drag you by the collar through the moon-door. And if the Oath-Wight keeps you, I will come back with every shard and every verse and I will make such a nuisance of myself in the halls of the dead that they'll beg Vey to take them back. You are not paying that price alone. That's the whole point, isn't it? That's what the covenant *was*. Shared burden. Shared fire." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I lost Odile four days ago. I am not doing this arithmetic again."

The ash plain was silent. Above them, clouds crossed the underside-moon, and the world dimmed and brightened and dimmed again, breathing.

"All right," Silas said at last, hoarsely. "Together, then."

"Together," she agreed. "Idiot."

From the darkness between the trees, a small translucent voice said, "I'm going to remember that word. *Together.* Nobody's used it around here in a hundred years." Bramblewick emerged from the shadow of the First Mother's roots, dragging something behind her—a bundle wrapped in woven fire, which she pushed toward Wren with both hands. "The Queen's map. Of the Hollow Court. I was saving it, in case... in case I had to go myself. But my hands pass through doors now. So it has to be you."

Wren unwrapped it carefully. Inside lay a sheet of what looked like beaten bark, and upon it, lines of silver fire traced the floorplan of a palace—hall upon hall, spiraling inward toward a throne room marked with a single burning sigil.

"There's one more thing," Bramblewick said, and now her voice had gone very quiet, very young. "In the throne room. Behind the throne. The Queen left a message. Her last word. It's part of why the Wight guards that place so fiercely—it knows what's written there, and it fears it, because the Queen's last word is the one truth it can't wear a face for." She looked up at Silas, and her coal-grey eyes reflected the dying Hearthlight, twin small fires in a face going to smoke. "Whatever it shows you in that court—every face, every failure, every voice calling you coward—I want you to hold onto one thing. The Queen trusted your order. Even at the end. Even knowing what you'd become. She left her last word for *you*, specifically, for whoever came. She believed someone would have the courage to hear it."

"Why?" Silas asked. "After everything. Why would she believe that?"

Bramblewick smiled, and for one moment—one flickering, translucent moment—she looked exactly like her queen must have looked, a thousand years ago, lighting the first tree.

"Because someone always comes," she said. "Eventually. Slowly. Afraid. But someone always comes."

She turned and walked back into the dark rows of trees, growing fainter with every step, until Silas could no longer tell where the emberkin ended and the dimming began.

* * *

They slept in shifts. Silas took first watch and sat with his scarred hands open on his knees, palms up, the way he hadn't allowed himself to sit in seven years—as a swordspeaker rests, awaiting the speech. The cold fire burned blue beside him. Down the dark avenue of trees, another flame went out, and then another, each extinction silent as a held breath released.

Wren woke near the end of his watch and came to sit beside him without a word, her shoulder warm against his arm, the only warmth in the entire realm. Together they watched the underside-moon crawl its slow arc toward its twelfth turning, and neither of them spoke of what was coming, because both of them were counting the same diminishing lights and arriving at the same unbearable sum.

At the edge of sight, far across the ash plain, the Hollow Court rose against the dimmed sky—a silhouette of towers and hollow spires, blacker than the dark around it, with a single window burning cold silver at its summit.

Waiting.

"They're dying faster than she said," Wren murmured. "Bramblewick said thirteen turns. Look at the orchard. At her. We don't have thirteen turns. We might not have two."

Silas stood, and brushed the ash from his knees, and looked at the distant court with its one cold burning eye.

"Then we walk tonight," he said.
