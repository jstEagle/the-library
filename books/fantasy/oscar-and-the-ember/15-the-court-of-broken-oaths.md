## 15. The Court of Broken Oaths

The Hollow Court had no roof, and yet it was not open to the sky.

Silas understood this the way one understands a dream: not with the eyes but with some older organ of knowing, buried beneath the ribs. Above the throne room's shattered colonnades there should have been stars—should have been the Undermoon itself, vast and bruise-colored, hanging low over the ash orchards they had crossed to get here. Instead there was only a darkness that pressed down like a held breath, and within it, faintly, the suggestion of a vaulted ceiling that had been torn away and remembered anyway.

The floor was black glass. Not smooth—cracked, crazed with a web of fissures that caught the light of Wren's lantern and threw it back in pieces. Every step rang faintly, a sound like a bell struck underwater. Somewhere in those cracks, Silas knew, lay the oaths themselves. Thousands of them. The promises mortals had made to emberkin across ten centuries, spilled here when the covenant broke and pooled in the lowest place of the realm like blood in the body's dependent parts.

Bramblewick would not come inside. She had stopped at the threshold—a broken archway hung with banners gone grey as cobwebs—and her small burning shape had guttered visibly, like a candle in wind.

"The Court remembers," she had said, and her voice, usually bright as struck flint, had been thin as paper. "It remembers everything anyone ever promised here. Be careful what you say, swordspeaker. In this place, words are not air."

Now she waited outside with the lantern-light of her own body dimming, and Silas walked the glass floor toward the throne, and Wren walked beside him, and their footsteps were the only sound until the dead began to speak.

* * *

It started with his name.

Not *Silas*. His other name—the true one, spoken over him at his swearing-in with salt and ember-smoke, the name he had not heard in seven years because hearing it meant being known, and being known meant being found.

"*Oath-bound.*" The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the cracked glass beneath his feet, from the dark above where a ceiling should be. It was an old woman's voice, dry as autumn leaves, and he knew it. He knew it the way you know a scar. "Sister Ashfall. Third Circle of the Swordspeakers of the Covenant. Sworn on the ember-stone at midwinter, year of the Long Frost."

"Master Nakamura." Wren's whisper was very close to his ear. "What is that?"

"You'll see." His mouth had gone dry. "Don't answer it. Whatever it says—whatever it sounds like—don't answer."

But the court was already filling.

They came up out of the glass the way reflections rise to the surface of still water: slowly at first, then all at once. Shapes of grey smoke and colder light, wearing robes he recognized, bearing weapons he had trained beside, carrying faces—

Faces he knew.

Sister Ashfall stood ten paces to his left, exactly where she had stood during drills, her hands folded, her silver braid coiled at her nape. She had taught him the first verse of the oath-verse when he was eleven and could not stop crying. Behind her came Brother Calloway, who had cheated at cards and laughed about it and once carried Silas four miles through a snowstorm with a broken ankle. Master Ilenne. The twins, Ferren and Odessa, inseparable in life and now inseparable in smoke. And at the center, tall and stern and kind, Master Edran Vael, First Sword of the Order, whose last living act had been to push Silas toward a window and shout *run*.

Thirty-one faces. He counted them without wanting to. Thirty-one, because two had been absent that night—one dead of fever the spring before, one lost to the order years earlier—and thirty-one was the number he woke counting some mornings, the arithmetic of his guilt performed so often the figures had worn smooth as river stones.

"They're not real," Wren said. Her hand found his sleeve. Through the fabric he felt her trembling, or felt his own tremor transmitted, impossible to say which. "Silas. Look at me. They're not real."

"No," he said. "They're worse than that."

Because the Oath-Wight was not a ghost. Ghosts were echoes, afterimages, things that lingered without will. This was a *made* thing—a guardian shaped by the queen's last magic out of the court's hoarded oaths, given form from the substance of broken promises. And since the greatest promise ever broken in this realm had been broken by swordspeakers, the guardian had taken its shape from swordspeakers.

From his dead. From his family.

The thing that wore Sister Ashfall's face turned its head. Its eyes were holes full of old light.

"You fled," it said, in her voice, with her exact cadence, the gentle scold she used for students who shirked their forms. "On the Night of Severance, when your brothers and sisters stood against the magistrate's blades, you fled."

"I know," Silas said. His hand had gone to his sword hilt without his permission. He made himself take it off. "I know what I did."

"You fled," said Brother Calloway's shape, stepping forward, and even in death the man managed to look wounded and wry at once. "We held the courtyard. We held it for you. Did you know that? Eleven minutes we held it. Eleven minutes, oath-brother, waiting for the signal that never came, because the one who was supposed to give it was already three streets away."

"I didn't know," Silas whispered. "I never knew how long—"

"Eleven minutes," the shape repeated. "You could count it in verse. You knew the verses better than any of us. You could have sung the binding and bought us twenty more. You ran instead."

The glass floor was cold through his boots. Colder than cold—cold the way the space between stars is cold, cold with intention. Around him the shapes of his order drifted closer, unhurried, terrible in their patience. Their feet did not quite touch the ground.

"Draw your sword," said Master Edran Vael, and his voice was the same voice that had said *run*, and hearing it now inverted into something else made something behind Silas's sternum crack like river ice. "Draw it, oath-breaker. Let us see if seven years of hiding has sharpened you."

"I won't fight you."

"You will fight us," said all thirty-one voices together, and the sound was a landslide, was a collapsing mine, was every bell in Vellamark rung at once by hands that hated him. "It is what you do. It is all you have ever done. When truth comes for you, you reach for steel. When grief comes, steel. When we come—"

The nearest shape lunged—not walking now but flowing, smoke given sudden velocity—and Silas's sword was out of its sheath before thought could intervene, muscle memory older than conscience, and the blade passed through the shape of Sister Ashfall like a knife through candleflame.

She reformed. They all kept coming.

* * *

He fought them for what felt like an hour and what his lungs told him was minutes. It was like fighting weather. Steel disrupted them for a heartbeat, scattered them into ribbons of grey, and then they knitted themselves whole again and kept speaking, kept accusing, and every accusation landed because every accusation was true.

"You let them believe the lie." That was Master Ilenne's voice, crisp as a page turning. "Vey told you the covenant was already broken, that the emberkin had betrayed the pact, and you believed him because believing him was easier than doubting a magistrate."

"He showed us the records—"

"You wanted them to be true," said Ferren's shape, and Odessa's beside it, speaking in unison the way twins speak. "You wanted permission to stop carrying the weight. Seven years you'd carried it. Along came a soft-voiced man with documents, and you set it down."

"I was *twenty-three*—" Silas's voice broke in the middle. He backed away across the glass, blade low, breathing hard, and the shapes parted around him like water around a stone and closed again behind. "I was young, I was tired, I—"

"We were young too," said Calloway. "We were tired too. We doubted too. And when the moment came, we chose each other, and you chose yourself."

The word went into him deeper than any blade he had ever taken. He stumbled. Went down on one knee on the cracked glass, and the cold came up through it into his bones, and the shapes of his dead gathered close, close enough that he could see that their faces were not quite faces—that up close the features were made of script, of written vows layered in thousands upon thousands of lines, and the eyes were gaps where the writing had failed.

Behind him he heard Wren moving, heard her voice pitched high and fierce: "Stop it! Stop—you're killing him, he's trying to tell you something—"

And Silas realized, kneeling in the court where every promise ever made had pooled and curdled, what Bramblewick had tried to warn him.

Words are not air here.

The Wight fed on evasion. Every deflection, every excuse, every half-truth he had swallowed for seven years—it drank them, and grew stronger, and wore his dead more convincingly. He had been feeding it since the moment he entered. Perhaps since the moment he fled.

There was only one weapon left in the armory.

* * *

"Stop," Silas said.

He did not shout it. He said it quietly, the way Master Edran had taught him to speak an oath: low, level, from the floor of the chest. And because words were not air in this place, the word *stop* became a wall. The shapes halted. The dark ceiling seemed to lean closer, listening.

"I'm going to tell you what happened," Silas said. "All of it. Not the version I've told myself. Not the version where I was tricked and overwhelmed and had no choice." He swallowed. His throat was sand. "The true version. And you can do whatever you want to me after."

The thing that wore Edran Vael's face tilted its head. Waiting. All thirty-one of them waiting, patient as winter.

"On the Night of Severance," Silas began, and his voice shook, and he let it shake, because he was done spending strength on steadiness, "Magistrate Aldous Vey summoned the order to the Hall of Accord under pretense of renewing the covenant. He presented documents showing the emberkin had violated the pact—that they had been siphoning mortal lifespans through the Ward. The documents were forged. I know that now. But here is the part I have spent seven years not saying out loud."

He looked up at the face of his teacher. At the script that made its cheeks, the hollows where its eyes should be.

"I didn't just believe the forgery. I *wanted* it. Three weeks before, I had quarreled with the council over the terms of my oath—I thought the order was too rigid, too secretive, too willing to spend its members like coin. I resented them. I resented all of you. So when a respectable man handed me a reason to think the whole structure rotten, I took it with both hands and I *thanked him*." His voice cracked again; he pushed through it. "When the severance rite began, I sang my verse louder than anyone. I was eager. Do you understand? I helped break it. Not passively. *Eagerly.*"

The glass floor hummed beneath him. The shapes had gone very still.

"And then the doors opened, and the Wardens came in with crossbows, and I understood in one white-hot instant that I had been the instrument of the murder of everyone I loved." He was crying now; he did not wipe his face. "And I ran. Not because I had a plan. Not because survival would let me fight another day—that's the story I tell myself, the story I've polished until it shines. I ran because I was afraid. Because dying is easy to imagine and hard to do, and because I am, at bottom, a man who wants to live. I chose myself. You're right. All of you are right. I chose myself, and I have dressed that choice in duty for seven years, and I am done."

The silence that followed was enormous.

It was not the silence of judgment. That was the thing that undid him—that as he knelt there, tears freezing on the black glass, the silence felt less like a verdict than like a *held breath*, the silence of a room full of people who have finally heard the thing they have been waiting years to hear.

"You fled," said Sister Ashfall's shape. But the accusation had gone out of the voice. What remained was only sadness, and underneath the sadness, impossibly, something gentler. "Yes. You fled."

"I fled."

"And you lived."

"I lived." A sob tore loose from him, ugly and involuntary. "God help me, I lived, and I hated myself every single day of it, and I sold luck-knots in a night market because it was the smallest, safest magic I could do, and every time a Warden looked at me I thought, good, let them find me, let it end—but it never ended, and I never stopped running, and I am so *sorry*. Not for surviving. For lying about why."

The shapes began to change.

It happened slowly, the way frost melts off a window. The script that made their faces loosened; the grey light inside them warmed from moon-color to ember-color; and the holes where their eyes had been filled—not with sight, but with something like recognition. One by one, they bowed their heads. Not in condemnation.

In acknowledgment.

Master Edran Vael's shape stepped forward—last, always last, the First Sword going down with his people—and knelt before Silas as Silas knelt, until they were eye to eye, teacher and student, both of them ruined and both of them honest at last.

"We were afraid too," the shape said, in the voice from the window, from the snowstorm of that night, from every lesson Silas had ever treasured. "Every one of us, on the last night, was afraid. Courage is not the absence of the running. It is what you carry back afterward." A hand of warm light settled on Silas's shoulder, weightless and heavy at once. "You carried it badly, little oath-brother. But you carried it."

"I'm sorry," Silas said again, because some words bear repeating, and this was one.

"We know," said thirty-one voices, no longer a landslide but a tide going out. "That was always the last term of the trial. Not the sword. Not the verse. Only this: that you come to the court of broken oaths and break the last one—the one you swore to yourself, that you were innocent."

The shapes rose upward then, all together, unwinding into threads of ember-glow that spiraled toward the torn place where a ceiling should have been, and the dark above accepted them the way soil accepts rain. Where they passed, the cracks in the glass floor sealed with seams of gold, slow as honey, mending.

And in the center of the court, on the dais before the empty throne, something remained.

It was small. That was the first absurdity of it—after the trial, after the dead, after the weight of seven years laid down at last, the Third Shard was barely larger than a robin's egg. It hovered a hand's width above the dais, turning slowly, and it burned the color of dawn seen through smoke: rose and gold and a green like new leaves at the rim. Light pulsed from it in rhythm, and it took Silas a long moment to recognize the rhythm, and when he did his breath left him entirely.

It beat like a heart.

"It's warm," Wren said. She had come up beside him without his noticing; her face was wet too, though she'd deny it later. She reached toward the shard and stopped short, fingers curling back. "Silas—it's warm. Like a coal. Like—"

"Like the orchard," he said. "Like Bramblewick. It's the same fire." He lifted it from the air, and it weighed nothing, and it weighed everything, and its pulse quickened against his palm as if it knew him, as if it had watched the whole trial and approved. "Three shards," he whispered. "Three of thirteen turns. Half the road behind us, half ahead."

Wren exhaled a laugh that was half sob. "Then we—"

The gold-seamed floor rang beneath their feet. Once, twice—a deep alarm-note rolling up through the glass, through the walls, through the bone of the world. Far away, beyond the archway where Bramblewick waited, the ash orchard lit suddenly red, and a sound reached them that Silas had hoped never to hear again in either realm: the horn-call of Vellamark's Wardens, blown from the mortal side of a moon-door.

Wren's face went white. "The chapel door. Someone's opened it from our side—and there's only one person alive who knows we've been coming and going through Saint Ovelun's."

Silas closed his fist around the shard, and its warmth bit into his palm like a vow.

"Odile," he said, and ran.
