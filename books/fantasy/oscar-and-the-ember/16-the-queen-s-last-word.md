## 16. The Queen's Last Word

The Hollow Court had no roof.

That was the first thing Silas understood as they crossed its threshold—where a ceiling should have been, there was only the Undermoon itself, vast and close and wrong, hanging so low it seemed he could have reached up and dragged his fingers through its pitted silver face. Its light fell straight down into the throne room like water poured into a cup, pooling on the floor in slow-moving tides that lapped at their boots without wetting them. The light was cold here. Not the warm ember-glow of the orchard, not the hearth-gold of Bramblewick's small fires. This was the light of something ending.

The court had been grand once. He could see the bones of its grandeur beneath the decay: pillars of black glass rising into the moonlight, each one carved with spiraling script that still faintly pulsed, like embers breathing under ash. Banners hung from hooks along the walls—or had hung, once; now they were ribbons of grey gossamer, dissolving even as he watched, their threads drifting upward instead of down, as if gravity itself had grown tired here and simply given up. The floor was a mosaic of interlocking flames rendered in mother-of-pearl and obsidian, and every tenth tile was dark, burned out, dead—a pattern of absence running through beauty like a vein of rot.

At the room's far end, upon a dais of seven shallow steps, stood the throne.

It was not what Silas expected. He had imagined gold, or fire, or some monstrous seat carved from a volcano's heart. Instead it was small. Almost humble. A chair of white stone shaped like two hands cupped together, palms up, as if the queen had ruled from a gesture of offering rather than dominion. And upon those cupped hands lay a single point of light—no larger than a candle flame, guttering, blue-white, the same color as the Ember Ward on the night it had torn him out of the world.

Wren stopped three steps behind him. He heard her breath catch, heard the soft click of her satchel as she gripped it against her chest like a shield.

"Silas." Her voice came out thin. "The walls."

He turned. The black glass pillars were not merely carved with script. They were *filled* with it—and with faces. Thousands of them, pressed just beneath the surface like flowers in pressed glass: emberkin, hundreds upon hundreds, their features serene or sorrowful or mid-scream, frozen in the moment of their fading. As the moonlight shifted, he could see that some of the faces were hollow now, translucent, worn away to suggestions. Others still held a dim flicker, eyes tracking him as he moved, mouths shaping words without sound.

"They didn't leave," Wren whispered. "They went *into* the walls. Into the court itself. When the covenant broke—" She swallowed. "When it broke, they had nowhere to go. So they became the architecture of their own mourning."

Silas looked back at the throne. At the small flame resting in the stone hands.

"And she stayed longest of all," he said.

* * *

Bramblewick had refused to come this far.

She had walked with them through the ash orchard's dying rows, past the boundary stones where the trees grew sparse and grey, her small form flickering like a lamp in wind—but at the court's threshold she had stopped as if striking glass. Her childlike face had gone old for a moment, ancient and terrible, and she had said only: *"Her last word is not for little spirits. It is for the one who holds the shards. Go. Listen. And whatever she shows you—"*

A pause. The flicker had steadied.

*"—believe it. Even when it costs. Especially then."*

Now, alone before the throne, Silas climbed the seven steps. Each one hummed under his boot, a low resonant note, and he realized with a chill that they were tuned—the dais was an instrument, and he was playing it by ascending. Behind him, Wren followed, and the notes changed to accommodate her weight, a second voice joining the first, dissonant and lovely.

At the top, he knelt.

"I don't know the forms," he said quietly, to the flame, to the faces in the walls, to whoever might still be listening. "My order taught me oaths, not courtesies. I am Silas Nakamura, last of the swordspeakers, and I have come to finish what we broke. If you will let me."

The flame did not answer. But the moonlight pooling on the floor began to move—swirling, gathering, drawing inward toward the dais in a slow spiral, and the temperature dropped until his breath fogged, until frost feathered across the obsidian tiles, until the whole vast room seemed to inhale.

Then the memory took him.

* * *

It was not like remembering. It was like being *unmade into* remembering.

One moment he knelt on cold stone; the next he stood in the same throne room, but alive, blazing, whole—pillars bright with living script, banners streaming crimson and gold, the mosaic floor unbroken and burning with reflected glory. The air itself tasted of cinnamon and struck flint. And everywhere, everywhere, there were voices: emberkin in their thousands, drifting through the hall like sparks on a warm wind, their laughter ringing off the black glass in bright cascades.

Before the throne stood a woman.

She was tall and spare, dressed in traveling leathers gone soft with age, her hair bound back with a cord of woven copper. No crown. No regalia. Only a sword at her hip—and Silas knew that sword, knew it the way you know your own reflection, because its twin had hung at his own side for twenty years before the Night of Severance. A swordspeaker's blade. His order's mark was etched in the fuller: the open hand, the rising flame.

But this woman was not of his order. She predated it. She *founded* it.

The First Oathkeeper.

Around her, the great lords and ladies of both realms had gathered—human and emberkin mingled without distinction, and Silas saw, with a lurch, how easy it had all been once. How natural. An emberkin spirit perched on a human lord's shoulder like a favorite falcon. A human child chasing a wisp of living fire across the mosaic, both of them shrieking with joy. No Wardens. No burnings. No fear.

The First Oathkeeper raised her hands, and the hall fell silent.

"You ask me why the covenant must be reforged anew each generation," she said, and her voice carried the particular weariness of someone who has answered this question many times. "You ask why one of us must stand at its center, holding the pact open, when surely the pact could simply... exist. Be written. Be done."

Murmurs of assent. Silas, standing unseen among the crowd—a ghost in his own vision—felt the question land in his own chest too. *Yes,* he thought. *Why? Why must it always be a person? Why must it always cost?*

"Because magic is not a river," the First Oathkeeper said. "Magic is a *fire*. And fire does not flow. Fire *consumes*, unless it is tended. Left alone, it gutters—or it spreads, and devours everything it touches. Look at Vey, if any of you doubt me. Look at what a man becomes when he hoards what was meant to be shared."

A ripple of unease passed through the assembly. Silas's skin prickled. *Vey.* The name was already known here, already feared, decades before the ban, before the Severance. The magistrate had been circling the covenant his entire life.

"So we place the fire in trusted hands," the First Oathkeeper continued. "We bind it to an order. We give it keepers." She paused, and something moved across her face—an expression Silas recognized with sickening familiarity, because he had seen it in his own mirror for seven years. Doubt. The doubt of someone about to make a mistake they cannot yet see the shape of.

"We give it *keepers*," she said again, softer. "And we pray that power, given to few, does not curdle in the keeping."

* * *

The memory shifted. Years slid past like cards being dealt.

Silas watched the order grow. Watched the swordspeakers take up their blades and their speech, watch them become the sole vessels of the covenant's power—honored, set apart, gradually *elevated*, until the word *keeper* came to mean *owner*. He watched the emberkin drift to the edges of human society, no longer companions but dependents, their magic flowing only through sanctioned channels, their freedom narrowing year by year. He watched the First Oathkeeper grow old watching this happen, powerless to stop the drift she had set in motion, because the covenant's structure—once built—could not be unbuilt by wishing.

And then he stood beside her deathbed.

She was very old, and very small, in a chamber above the Hollow Court where the windows faced the Undermoon. The emberkin queen sat beside her—Silas recognized her now from Bramblewick's descriptions, from the faded murals in Odile's safehouse: a figure of layered flame and patient eyes, beautiful the way a long-burning coal is beautiful, all her heat banked deep.

"The order will fail," the queen said. It was not an accusation. It was a diagnosis, delivered with the gentleness of a physician.

"I know," the First Oathkeeper whispered.

"You built a dam where I asked for a garden."

"I know." A rattling breath. "I thought—I believed—that if good people held the power, the holding would keep them good. But power doesn't work that way, does it. Power works on the holder."

"No," the queen agreed. "It works on the holder."

The old woman turned her head on the pillow. Her eyes found the place in the air where Silas stood—found it, *fixed on it*, and though he knew he was only a phantom in a memory, though he knew she could not possibly see him across a century of years, her gaze pinned him like a moth to a board.

"But there is another way," she said. "There was always another way. I chose the dam because the garden frightened me. Because a garden belongs to everyone, and I wanted—to be needed. To be *central*. Gods forgive me, I wanted to matter." Her hand crept across the blanket, and the queen took it. "Tell them. Tell whoever comes after. Tell them the true terms."

The queen bent close. And she spoke the covenant's heart aloud:

"It was never meant to be held. It was meant to be *given away*. One mortal, in every turning of the age, who loves the fire enough to release it—not to an order, not to a bloodline, not to a chosen few, but to *everyone*. To pour their gift out of themselves entirely, until nothing pools anywhere, until the magic runs free between the realms like rain, belonging to all or to none. That is the reforging. That is the price. Not a life laid down—a *self* laid down. The keeper must surrender the gift that makes them who they are, freely, knowing they will never hold it again, and love the world more than the having."

The old woman smiled. "Then let it be written," she murmured. "Let it wait for someone braver than me."

The memory folded closed around her last breath like a book shutting.

* * *

Silas came back to himself on his knees before the throne, and he was shaking.

The moonlight still pooled around the dais. The flame in the stone hands still guttered. But now, as he watched, it stretched—reaching toward him like a plant toward sun—and within it, a face formed: the emberkin queen, rendered in miniature, her patient eyes meeting his.

Her lips moved. No sound came. But Silas had spent his life reading the language of fire, and he understood.

*You see it now.*

"Yes," he said. His voice cracked on the single syllable.

Behind him, Wren made a small sound. He turned. She stood at the base of the dais with her journal open, her transcription stylus hovering, her face pale as paper. "The walls," she said. "Silas, the walls are *singing*. All of them. Every face—they're all saying the same thing—"

And he listened, and he heard it: a thousand voices beneath the glass, whispering in unison, the queen's last word repeated over and over through the fabric of the court itself, a truth that had waited a hundred years for ears that could bear it.

*A self laid down. The gift surrendered. Love the world more than the having.*

His hands. He looked down at his scarred hands, the hands that had hidden their magic for seven years, the hands that had sold harmless charms while the world starved of wonder. The hands that had healed Wren on the rooftops, breaking his own law, because he could not watch her bleed. The hands that had sung the oath-verse in the bell tower while she transcribed what he could not bear to keep.

Swordspeech. The gift that had defined him since childhood. The gift that had saved his life on the Night of Severance when every other gift failed. The gift that was, in the end, the only weapon he had ever possessed worth the name.

To surrender it would not kill him. That was the terrible mercy of it. He would wake tomorrow, and the day after, and walk through all the ordinary mornings of an ordinary life—with ordinary hands. Hands that could never again call fire, never again speak a blade awake, never again do the one thing that had made him *him*.

He thought of Vey, who had chosen the opposite path—who had taken the covenant's fire and hoarded it, fed on it, aged slowly while the city starved, and become a monster so gradually he likely couldn't name the moment of his own damnation. The dam. Always the dam.

He thought of the First Oathkeeper, dying with her doubt, begging the dark to remember her honestly.

He thought of Wren.

Of the way she looked at him—not at the swordspeaker, not at the last relic of a fallen order, but at Silas: stubborn, frightened, ridiculous Silas who argued about translation syntax and ate too fast and flinched at kindness. Of the almost-moment at Odile's table, turn seven, when the words had risen in both their throats and neither had spoken them, because the timing was wrong, because the world was ending, because some things deserve better than a stolen hour.

If he gave up the gift, he would still have that. Whatever *that* was, unnamed and unfinished—he would still have the chance to find out. The sacrifice did not demand his life. It demanded only the lie that he had been telling himself for seven years: that he was his power, that without it he was nothing, that the boy who fled the Severance had survived on magic alone rather than on luck and love and the simple animal refusal to die.

He had survived because Wren had refused to leave the stall. Because Odile had opened her door. Because Bramblewick had believed in him when he could not believe in himself.

The gift had never been the reason. The gift had only been the excuse.

"Silas?" Wren's voice, careful. "What did she show you?"

He rose. His knees ached. His hands had stopped shaking.

He looked at the flame in the stone hands—at the queen's face within it, waiting, patient as banked coals—and he bowed, deeply, the way he had never bowed to any magistrate or master.

"When the time comes," he said, "on the thirteenth turn, at the covenant's heart—I will speak the full verse. And I will give it all away."

Wren's stylus froze mid-stroke. "Give—what do you mean, *give*—"

"Not my life." He turned to face her, and he found, to his own surprise, that he was smiling. It felt strange on his face, like a coat borrowed from a younger man. "Something worse. Something better. Everything I am, in the only way that ever mattered."

Understanding dawned in her eyes, and with it, horror. "No. Silas, no—there has to be another—"

"There isn't. That's the whole point. There never was." He stepped down from the dais, past her, toward the doors and the dying orchard beyond, where Bramblewick waited and the moon counted down its final turns. "The order kept the fire because keeping made us important. Vey steals it because stealing makes him powerful. And the covenant breaks, every time, because no one—*no one*—has ever loved the world enough to simply let go."

He paused at the threshold. The blue-white light of the fading court washed over him, and for a moment the scars on his hands seemed to glow, as if the fire beneath his skin had heard him and was already beginning to grieve.

"Wren. Write it down. All of it. The terms, the verse, everything the queen said. Not for the archives." He glanced back at her, and his voice went quiet. "For after. For whoever comes next. So no one ever has to learn it the way I just did—alone, in a dead queen's house, with the moon running out."

She nodded, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand, and bent over her journal, and began.

Silas stepped out into the ash-fall, into the greying orchard, into the dwindling turns of the dying moon—and behind him, in the Hollow Court, a thousand faces in the black glass began, very softly, to sing.

And far above, beyond the thinning veil between worlds, the true moon wheeled toward its twelfth turn—and somewhere in Vellamark, in a spire of black iron and stolen fire, Magistrate Aldous Vey felt the covenant stir, and smiled, and reached for the last shard.
