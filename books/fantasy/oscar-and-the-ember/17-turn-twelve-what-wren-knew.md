## 17. Turn Twelve: What Wren Knew

The moon hung over Odile's safehouse like a coin held up to the light, and Wren Corvane could not stop looking at it.

Twelve turns. One remained.

She sat at the kitchen table with a cup of tea gone cold between her hands, watching the moon through the salt-fogged window, and the truth she had been carrying for months sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. It had grown heavier with every mile of the road, every shard recovered, every night Silas slept sitting upright against a wall with his sword across his knees like a man who did not believe he deserved rest. The stone had a name now. It had always had a name. She had simply refused to speak it.

*Corvane.*

Her family name. Her brother's name. The reason she had walked into the night market seven turns of the moon ago and put a century-old prophecy under the nose of the last swordspeaker in Vellamark.

"You're doing it again," Silas said from the doorway.

She startled badly enough that the cold tea sloshed against her fingers. She hadn't heard him come down the stairs—she never did; he moved like a man who had spent seven years practicing how not to be heard—and there he stood in the low light of the hearth, gray at the temples, his scarred hands wrapped around his own cup, watching her with those eyes that saw too much for a man who claimed to sell luck-knots.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at the moon like it owes you money."

A laugh escaped her before she could cage it—short, cracked, nothing like a real laugh. "Maybe it does."

He crossed the room and sat across from her without asking. That was another thing that had changed between them, turn by turn: he no longer asked permission to be near her. Neither of them did. Somewhere along the drowned chapel and the lantern streets and the bell tower where he had sung until his voice bled, they had stopped being two people thrown together by an ember-warded accident and become something else, something unnamed, something that sat across tables from each other in the small hours because sleep was impossible and silence was easier when shared.

The house creaked around them. Odile's safehouse had a way of doing that—the walls remembered things, Odile said, and sometimes at night you could almost hear the dead order's voices moving through the plaster like wind through reeds. Tonight the house sounded only like an old house settling, which was somehow worse. Empty houses made their own kind of accusation.

"Wren." He set his cup down. "You've been carrying something since the bell tower. Longer, maybe. Since before that. I'm a charm seller, not a mind reader, but I've spent seven years reading faces across a market stall, and yours has been saying *I have something to tell him* since we climbed out of the harbor with salt in our lungs."

"I don't know what you—"

"Don't." Gentle. He was always gentle when it mattered most, and she hated him a little for it. "Whatever it is, say it tonight. Tomorrow we go to the spire, and if you die tomorrow carrying this, I'll—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I'll be angry at a ghost. And I've had my fill of ghosts."

The stone turned over in her chest. Twelve turns of carrying it. Twelve turns of telling herself she would confess when the time was right, when the shards were found, when the covenant was reforged, when it wouldn't matter anymore. But it would always matter. That was the nature of stones like this one—they didn't dissolve. They only waited to be spat out or swallowed forever.

So she reached beneath her collar and drew out the cord she wore there, and on the cord, a small brass key on a rusted ring.

Silas looked at it. Looked at her. Said nothing.

"This is the key to the Corvane family archive box," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Number forty-one, sub-vault of the old chancery library. My father bought the lease before the ban, back when collecting covenant histories wasn't heresy. Everything I told you about stumbling across the prophecy—about pulling the wrong book off a shelf during an inventory—that was true, except for one word. I didn't stumble. I went looking. I'd been looking for three years."

She watched his face as she said it. Watched the stillness come over him, the particular stillness of a man bracing for a blow he has been expecting his whole life.

"The prophecy in Bramblewick's orchard—the one naming tonight's date, naming your name—I translated it in the spring. Months before I ever found your stall. I knew what the thirteenth turn meant. I knew about the shards. I knew the covenant realm was dying." She made herself keep going, made herself hold his eyes, because looking away would be the coward's version and she had promised herself, in the dark of many sleepless nights, that she would not be a coward about this one thing. "And I sought you out deliberately. I chose you, Silas. Not the other way around."

The hearth popped. Outside, far off, a patrol bell rang twice and went quiet.

"Why?" he asked quietly.

And here was the part she had rehearsed a hundred times and never once gotten through without breaking.

"My brother."

* * *

Tam Corvane had been arrested eleven days after the Night of Severance—or rather, eleven days after the anniversary of it, seven years ago, when the Wardens swept the archives and burned everything that mentioned the old covenant by name. Tam was not a scholar. Tam was a locksmith's apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a habit of asking questions, and one of his questions had been spoken too loudly in a tavern where a Warden informant drank: *if magic is so dangerous, why does the Magistrate's household never get sick? Why do his lamps burn blue?*

They took him that night. No trial. No sentence announced. Just gone, the way people went in Vey's city, into the administrative silence of the magistrate's justice.

"For six years I petitioned," Wren said. Her hands had begun to shake; she pressed them flat against the table. "For six years I wrote letters and filed appeals and groveled before clerks who wouldn't meet my eyes. And then I found the prophecy, and it named the date of the thirteenth turn, and it spoke of a last oath-bound swordspeaker, and I thought—"

"*You thought,*" Silas repeated.

"I thought: here is a man who can open doors I cannot. Here is a man whose gift can reach places petitions cannot. Here is the only person alive who might walk into Aldous Vey's spire and come out again, and all I had to do was point him at it." The words were ash in her mouth. She said them anyway. All of them. "I didn't know you, Silas. You were a means to an end. A weapon with a name I pulled from a dead book. And then—" Her voice failed, caught, failed again. "And then you healed me on the rooftops. You broke your own rule, the rule that kept you alive for seven years, to save a woman you'd known for eight days. And somewhere between the chapel and the bell tower I stopped being able to remember why I'd come, except that it stopped being about Tam and started being about—"

"About me."

"About us. About whatever this is. And every day I didn't tell you became its own lie, and the lies stacked up like wet pages, and now the whole book is ruined, and I'm sorry, I am so sorry, but I need you to understand that Tam is still in that spire. He is still alive—Vey keeps him alive, I've confirmed it twice through smugglers' channels—and tomorrow we storm that place, and I let you believe you were walking into it for the covenant alone, for the world alone, when really you were walking in for *my* world, my one person, my brother, and that makes everything I've done—"

"Wren."

"—a manipulation, a *use*, and I swore I would never be like Vey, using people as instruments, and instead I did exactly what he does, I just did it with better books—"

"*Wren.*"

His voice cracked across the kitchen like the First Shard singing in the drowned dark, and she stopped, chest heaving, tears finally spilling over and running hot down her face.

Silas rose. Came around the table. Took the cold cup of tea gently out of her hands and set it aside, and then knelt—knelt, the swordspeaker, the last of his kind, kneeling on Odile's worn floorboards so that his eyes were level with hers.

"When," he said, "did you decide you loved me?"

She blinked through the tears. "What?"

"It matters. Answer the question. When?"

"I—" She groped for it. "The rooftop. Or maybe before. Maybe the orchard, when you held Bramblewick like she was something precious instead of something strange."

"And when did you plan to tell me about Tam?"

"Tonight. Or tomorrow. Or—"

"Were you going to let me fight Vey tomorrow believing it was only about the covenant, and then slip away to find your brother yourself?"

"Yes," she whispered. "That was the plan. Until yesterday. Until I realized I couldn't carry it through the door with me."

Silas exhaled slowly. He stayed kneeling a moment longer, and she watched something move behind his eyes—not softness, not yet. Calculation first. Hurt first. She owed him the hurt; she would sit inside it as long as he needed her to.

Then he laughed. It was a terrible sound, ragged and disbelieving, half grief and half something else.

"Do you know," he said, "what I was doing, the night you found my stall?"

Wren shook her head.

"I was deciding whether to run. Seven years in Vellamark selling luck-knots, and that very night I had counted my coin and picked my road. South, past the marshes, some village where nobody had ever heard the word *swordspeaker*. I was leaving, Wren. If you had come one night later, I'd have been gone, and the prophecy would have found no one, and the Undermoon would have sealed with both realms inside it, dying." He looked up at her, and the lamplight moved in his eyes. "You think you used me. I think you arrived ninety minutes before I abandoned the world. Whatever your reasons were—you were the hand that pulled me back. I can't make myself regret that. I've tried. Believe me, these twelve turns, I've tried."

"But I lied."

"You did."

"And you forgive me?"

"I didn't say that." He stood, joints cracking, and paced once to the window, once back. "Here's what I know. You wanted a weapon and got a man. That's not a sin—it's a risk everyone takes when they love someone. And here's what else I know: you could have kept the secret through tomorrow. Through the spire. Forever. Nobody would ever have known. Instead you gave it to me the night before the worst fight of our lives, knowing it might cost you everything—including Tam, if I turned my back on him. That's not what Vey does. Vey spends people. You spent *yourself*." He stopped pacing. "It doesn't undo the lying. But it tells me who you are underneath the lying. And who you are underneath is someone worth choosing."

Wren's breath left her in a long, shuddering thread. "Even though I used you."

"Even though." He came back to her and took her hands—his scarred palms rough against her cold fingers, warm as banked coals even now, even with the gift dimming as the turns ran out. "But hear me, Wren Corvane, and hear me fully. Tomorrow we go to the spire for *both* of them. The covenant and your brother. Not one instead of the other. If you'd asked me to choose between saving the world and saving Tam, I'd have called you a fool and done both, because that's what the old oath actually meant—not sacrifice, not martyrdom, just *refusing to let anyone be spent*. Your brother included." His grip tightened. "But if you ever lie to me about something that size again—if you ever decide for me what I can bear to know—I will walk out of your life and not look back. Swear it."

"I swear it." The words came out broken but whole. "By every archive I ever desecrated and every one I ever loved. I swear it."

"Then it's done." He drew her up out of the chair and held her, and she felt the last of the stone leave her—not dissolved, never dissolved, but finally set down, shared, carried by four hands instead of two. Outside, the twelfth-turn moon slid free of a cloud and laid a road of white light across the harbor water, straight toward the black silhouette of Vey's spire.

They stood like that a long while, two liars holding each other in a house full of echoes, choosing each other anyway.

When they finally parted, it was because the mirror above the washbasin began to glow.

Bramblewick's face bloomed in the glass, small and flickering at the edges now, translucent as smoke, and her voice came thin as paper:

"Hurry, swordspeaker. Hurry, word-keeper. The thirteenth turn rises early—and Vey has begun already. The spire is *singing*."
