## 18. Storming the Spire

The twelfth turn rose swollen and wrong, a moon gone the color of an old bruise, and beneath it Magistrate Aldous Vey's spire stood like a needle driven through the heart of Vellamark.

Silas had seen it a thousand times from a thousand angles—had sold charms in its shadow, had flinched from its shadow, had learned to read its shadow like weather. But he had never seen it like this. The windows that had always been dark now bled light upward in slow pulses, amber and sickly gold, as though the building itself were breathing. As though it were *feeding*.

"It's drinking," Bramblewick said. She perched on the broken parapet of the tannery across the canal road, small feet swinging over the drop, her ember-light guttering with each pulse from the spire. "Can't you feel it? It's drinking the covenant. Every heartbeat of that light is a mouthful."

Wren stood at Silas's shoulder, her satchel heavy with everything they'd gathered—the transcribed oath-verse in three hands of writing, the maps of the spire's lower levels she'd memorized during her years in the archives before Vey had her expelled, and the two shards wrapped in wool and salt-cloth against her breast. Through the whole climb up the tannery stairs she hadn't spoken. Now she said, quietly: "The holding cells are below the waterline. Old flood galleries from when this was a customs house. If Tam is alive, that's where he is."

"And if he isn't?" Silas asked.

"Then I'm going to be very rude to a magistrate's corpse." Her voice didn't shake. That frightened Silas more than trembling would have.

* * *

They went over the wall at the change of the watch, when the Wardens' lanterns swung toward the harbor and left the spire's garden side dark. Bramblewick went first—not over the wall but *through* it, pouring herself between the stones like heat through a keyhole, and where she passed the mortar glowed cherry-red and softened until Wren could pull the bricks free with gloved hands. The hole they made was ugly and obvious, but ugliness was a luxury they'd surrendered twelve turns ago.

Inside, the gardens were wrong. Silas felt it before he understood it: the roses blooming out of season, the hedges grown into shapes no gardener would choose, all of it leaning toward the spire like plants toward sun. Everything within half a mile bent toward Vey's hunger. Even the ground seemed to slope uphill toward his door.

"The shards," Wren whispered. She pressed a hand to her chest, where the First Shard sat against her sternum like a second heart. "They can feel him pulling."

"Then we run faster than the pulling." Silas drew the knife Odile had given him—her last gift, taken from the tide gate where she'd died, still carrying a notch from a Warden's blade—and led them down through the hedge-maze toward the flooded undercrofts.

* * *

The cells were worse than Wren's descriptions, and Wren's descriptions had been grim.

Water to the ankle, black and cold. Cells of rusted iron bar-work set into the old customs-house stone, most of them empty, some of them not empty enough. Silas counted doors as they passed and made himself stop counting after nine. The air tasted of rust and something sweeter underneath, something rotten-gold, and he realized with a lurch that it was the same smell as the ash orchard—but *inverted*, the covenant's warmth turned to leavings, magic eaten and excreted into decay.

Bramblewick's light dimmed to a coal. "Don't look in the far cells," she said, very small. "Please don't look in the far cells."

Wren looked anyway. Silas heard her breath catch, heard her whisper a name that wasn't her brother's—an archive colleague, maybe, someone else Vey had swallowed—and then she squared her shoulders and kept moving, faster now, calling softly: "Tam! Tam Corvane!"

Silence. Then, from the deepest gallery, so faint it might have been water:

"...Wren?"

She was moving before the echo settled. Silas followed, knife out, and found them at the end of the row: a young man folded into a cell barely large enough for a coffin, wrists manacled above his head, feet just brushing the flooded floor. He was thinner than any living person should be, skin stretched over a scholar's knuckles, hair gone white at twenty-six. But his eyes found Wren's and lit, and for one moment the whole drowned gallery held nothing but two siblings' names spoken aloud.

"You came," Tam croaked. "You actually—I told you not to—"

"You told me a lot of things." Wren's voice cracked clean down the middle. She was already working the manacles, fingers shaking too badly for the lock, and Silas stepped in and cut them with Odile's knife, one stroke, two.

Tam sagged. Wren caught him. And that was when the lights came on.

Not lanterns. Not lamps. Every drop of water in the gallery ignited at once, hanging in the air as beads of golden fire, and by their light Silas saw the far end of the corridor fill with Wardens—a dozen, two dozen, crossbows leveled, and behind them, descending the stairs with the unhurried gait of a man entering his own dining room, came Magistrate Aldous Vey.

"There you are," Vey said warmly. "I was beginning to think you'd gotten lost."

* * *

He had changed. Seven years ago, on the Night of Severance, Vey had been a soft-bellied functionary with ink-stained fingers and a clerk's smile. The man who walked toward them now wore the same face stretched over different architecture—taller somehow, or standing taller, shoulders squared by stolen years, eyes lit from behind with that same rotten-gold fire as the water-beads. Magic pooled in him visibly now, Silas realized. All these turns of hoarding, all these years of siphoning, and the last shard's binding must have tipped him past what a human frame could politely contain. He glowed at the seams.

"Silas Nakamura." Vey pronounced the name like a vintage he'd been saving. "The last swordspeaker. Do you know, I've kept your file all this time? Thick as a psalter. Every charm you ever sold, every knot you ever tied, every child whose fever you broke with a whispered word when you thought no Warden was watching." He smiled. "I let you live, all these years. Did you wonder why?"

"I assumed you were lazy," Silas said.

Vey laughed—a real laugh, delighted, awful. "I was *farming*. A swordspeaker loose in my city draws the others out of hiding. Draws the curious. Draws scholars like the lovely Miss Corvane, digging where they shouldn't. You were bait, Nakamura. You've been bait since the night you ran." His gaze slid to Wren, to the shape of the shards beneath her coat. "And now you've collected everything for me. Two shards carried straight to my door. How thoughtful."

"He doesn't have the third," Wren said. Her arm was around Tam, but her voice was level, archival, the voice she used for citing sources. "It's in the Undermoon. In the Hollow Court. Your binding ritual needs all four—you know that better than anyone, it's why you've spent thirteen years crawling toward it instead of taking it."

Vey's smile thinned by a degree. "Thirteen years of patience," he agreed, "and tonight the patience ends. Give me the shards, and I'll let the boy walk out of here. Amnesty for all of you. I keep my bargains—it's the one virtue I have left."

"You don't have virtues," Silas said. "You have *interests*. And right now your interest is that we're standing between you and the moon-door, and you can't reach the Undermoon without us opening it, because the door knows its keeper's blood." He hadn't known that until Bramblewick told him an hour ago, crouched on the tannery roof; he watched the knowledge land in Vey's eyes and saw the magistrate's first flicker of genuine uncertainty. "So here's *my* bargain. You let us walk, and I'll consider whether to open anything at all."

For a moment, the gallery was perfectly still. Water dripped somewhere. A bead of golden fire popped overhead like a soap bubble.

Then Vey sighed, and the fire came down like rain.

* * *

Later, Silas would remember it in fragments: the water-beads detonating into a storm of scalding light; Wren shoving Tam behind her; Bramblewick rising off Silas's shoulder like a struck match and *blooming*, suddenly vast, suddenly the size of the orchard spirit she truly was, filling the corridor with white flame that ate Vey's gold fire and screamed—

"*RUN!*"

They ran. Silas dragged Tam; Wren laid covering sparks from the Second Shard, wild untrained bursts that made the Wardens scatter; and behind them Bramblewick burned a path through the flooded gallery, the black water flashing to steam, the iron bars of the empty cells glowing and slumping like candle wax. The far cells—she burned those doors open too, and whatever stumbled out into the steam, Silas did not look back to see, because there was no time and no mercy left in the world to spend.

They burst up into the spire's ground floor, into the grand entrance hall where Vey received petitioners, marble and gilt and a staircase wide enough for six abreast. Wardens poured in from every door. Crossbows racked. Somewhere above, the whole building pulsed with that feeding light, faster now, hungry, the last shard straining toward its master.

"Bramblewick—" Silas started.

"I know." She was small again, drained, flickering like a candle in wind. She looked up at him with eyes gone almost grey. "I can burn one more path, swordspeaker. One. Choose it well."

Silas looked at the stairs—at the pulse of gold bleeding down from the upper floors, at the thing wearing Vey's face that would follow them wherever they fled—and made the only choice there had ever been.

"Up," he said. "We go *up*."

* * *

The binding chamber occupied the spire's crown, a round room walled in glass on every side, the bruised moon huge beyond it. Vey had stripped it bare of everything except a dais of black iron, and on the dais, suspended in a cage of woven gold light, hung the Fourth Shard—turning slowly, singing a note that Silas felt in his teeth, in the scars of his palms, in the hollow where his oath used to live.

Vey was already there. Of course he was. The walls had been for mortals; the magistrate simply *rose*, borne up through the building on a column of his own hoarded fire, and landed on the dais with his hand outstretched toward the shard, and began to speak words in a language that predated the covenant itself.

"No!" Wren lunged forward—and stopped, because the glass walls filled with Wardens, dozens of them, crossbows trained inward, and Vey without turning around said pleasantly:

"Shoot the scholar if the swordspeaker moves. The boy is worthless; do as you like with him."

Silas stepped forward, alone, onto the open floor between the door and the dais. His hands were shaking. He let them shake. Seven years of hiding, seven years of selling luck-knots and lying low and telling himself survival was enough—and it had never been enough, had it, because Odile was dead and Bramblewick was fading and the woman he loved had traded her whole life for a prophecy and her brother's chains had been *his* fault, his running, his bait-shadow falling over everyone who came near him.

"Vey." His voice came out quiet, and the room's acoustics carried it into every corner, into every Warden's ear. "You want to know why the covenant chose oaths? Why it bound itself to speech and not to blood or gold or crowns?" He raised his scarred hands. "Because speech is the only power that costs the speaker. Every word I've ever spoken in the old tongue took something from me. That's the price. That's the *design*. Magic that costs nothing is just theft with better manners—and you, magistrate, are the most polite thief in Vellamark."

"Touching," Vey said. His hand closed around the Fourth Shard.

The light that erupted then was not gold. It was white, total, the color of the moment before lightning strikes, and when Silas could see again, the thing on the dais was no longer entirely a man. Vey had grown—or unfolded—his shadow thrown enormous against the glass, his body threaded with veins of burning script, the covenant's own language crawling over his flesh as the fourth shard tried to bond with a soul that had never once paid for anything it took.

He screamed. Briefly. Then he mastered it, and smiled with too many teeth, and looked at Silas with eyes like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Now," Vey said, "we finish this."

What followed, Silas's body remembered better than his mind did. Swordspeech was not fencing; it was argument made lethal, the old tongue turning motion into meaning, and he gave the room every verse he had sworn off for seven years—words that split the marble floor, words that turned the Wardens' crossbow bolts aside mid-flight, words that cost him blood from the nose and sight from his left eye and years he could feel leaving him like heat from an open door. Against him, Vey fought with everything he had stolen and paid for none of it, and the imbalance was the horror of it: Silas was *spending himself* to stand still.

But spending was the point. Spending had always been the point.

He drove Vey back along the dais verse by verse, and Wren was shouting something—she had the shards out, both of them, holding them high, and they were resonating, answering Silas's voice like bells answering a choir—and Bramblewick, tiny and nearly transparent, crawled up the dais iron and sank her small burning hands into the cage of gold light around the Fourth Shard—

The cage shattered. The shard fell. Vey caught it against his chest with both hands and *pulled*, hauling it into himself, and the white light swallowed the crown of the spire whole.

Through the blaze, Silas heard Vey laughing, and heard, beneath the laughter, the sound of the moon-door tearing open on its own at last—ripped wide by stolen power, a wound in the air, howling wind and the smell of the ash orchard pouring through.

"If I cannot bind the covenant," Vey roared over the roar, "I will *swallow its source*—"

He hurled himself backward through the tear, and was gone.

Silas stood at the edge of it, the Undermoon's cold breath on his face, and behind him Wren was screaming his name, and ahead of him everything he had ever been waited at the bottom of the light.

He jumped.
