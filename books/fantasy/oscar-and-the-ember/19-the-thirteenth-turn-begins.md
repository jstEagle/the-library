## 19. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The spire's crown chamber had no roof.

That was the first thing Silas understood as he climbed the last of Vey's stairs, Bramblewick's light guttering ahead of him like a candle in a wind that shouldn't exist indoors. Above them, the sky churned—clouds dragged into a slow spiral around the moon, which hung swollen and wrong over Vellamark, its face crossed by a hairline crack of shadow. The twelfth turn ending. Somewhere below, Wren was running with her brother's arm over her shoulders, and somewhere above, the thirteenth turn waited like a held breath.

The chamber itself was a wound. Vey had torn it open, floorboards ripped away to expose the spire's stone heart, and carved into that stone were channels—veins of silver that ran from the walls down into a central basin, and in the basin, floating an inch above the metal, the last shard.

It sang. Not with sound, exactly, but with the memory of sound—the way a bell keeps ringing after the ear stops hearing it. Silas felt it in his teeth, in the old scars on his hands, in the place behind his sternum where his swordspeech lived like a second heartbeat. The First Shard, pulled from drowned water. The Second, cracked free of its bell. And now this one, the Third, hovering in a cage of silver veins that pulsed with stolen light.

Vey stood at the basin's edge.

He did not look defeated. That was the horror of him. His coat was scorched, one sleeve hanging in blackened ribbons where Bramblewick's fire had caught him on the stair, and there was a bruise blooming along his jaw like a dark flower—but he stood straight, and he was smiling, and when he turned to face Silas, his eyes held the calm of a man who has already won and is merely waiting for everyone else to understand it.

"Magistrate Nakamura," Vey said. "Or should I say swordspeaker? You've spent seven years pretending to be neither."

"I'm neither," Silas said. He came up the last steps slowly, Bramblewick pressed close against his leg, her small form flickering between solid and translucent. "I'm just a man who sells charms."

"Then you've wasted a life." Vey gestured at the shard, and the silver veins brightened. "Do you know what I felt, the first time I siphoned covenant magic? Do you know what it's like to drink from a river while everyone else is told the river doesn't exist?" His voice dropped, went almost gentle, almost reasonable—the voice he must have used on the swordspeakers, seven years ago, the night he taught them to break their own oath. "They called me corrupt. I am not corrupt. I am *thirsty*. There is a difference, Silas. Corruption is a failure of character. Thirst is simply honesty about what you need."

"You fed on a dying realm," Silas said. "You let the emberkin fade so you could stay young."

"I let them fade because they were already fading." Vey spread his hands, and the light of the shard played across his face, smoothing it, making him look younger than his years, younger than any man had a right to look. "The covenant was broken before I ever touched it. Your order broke it. I only refused to starve alongside everyone else." He smiled again, wider. "But none of that matters now. Look at the moon, Silas. One turn left. When it completes, both realms seal forever—and everything inside each realm belongs to whoever holds the door."

Silas looked up. The spiral of clouds had tightened. Through the gap at its center, the Undermoon was visible—not as a reflection but as a presence, a vast dim country hanging inverted above the world, its ash orchards gray, its rivers running slow as cooling wax. Dying. He could see it dying from here.

"The door," Silas said quietly. "You mean the moon-door."

"I mean the source." Vey stepped back from the basin, and the silver veins rose from the stone like serpents scenting blood, winding up around his arms, sinking into his skin. He didn't flinch. He *inhaled*, the way a man inhales sea air. "The shards are keys, yes. But keys are useless without a door, and the door isn't in your drowned chapel or your cracked bell. The door is where the covenant was first spoken—at the meeting-place between realms. At the source." His eyes found Silas's, and they were bright, feverish, triumphant. "And I am going through it."

He seized the shard.

The sound that came out of the basin was not a sound—it was a *tearing*, a note so deep and so high at once that the windows of the crown chamber burst outward in a rain of glass, and the spiral of clouds overhead shuddered. Vey threw back his head and drank the shard's light into himself, and for one terrible moment Silas saw him as he truly was: not a magistrate, not a man, but a hollow thing wearing centuries of stolen wonder like a coat made of other people's warmth.

"No—" Bramblewick flared, hurling herself forward, a comet of small fire—but Vey backhanded the light aside without looking, and she struck the wall and dimmed, crumpling, her edges going thin as smoke.

Silas ran.

He didn't think. Seven years of hiding, seven years of small harmless charms and lowered eyes, and every one of those years burned away in three strides. His hands came up and the words came out of him—the old verse, the oath-verse, the speech of binding and unbinding—and the air between him and Vey turned to iron, to netting, to a cage of spoken syllables.

Vey walked through it.

The cage unraveled against him like cobwebs against a lantern. Stolen magic, drunk deep for decades, answered him the way Silas's own gift never had: instantly, totally, without cost. "Oh, Silas." Almost pitying. "You pay for every word. I get mine free. How do you imagine this ends any way but mine?"

But even as he said it, Silas saw it—the flicker. The shard's light moving through Vey like floodwater through a dam with a crack in it. The man was full past full, holding more than any single vessel could hold, and the seams were showing. A vein of silver light pulsed visibly at his throat, too fast, arrhythmic. His left hand trembled and he closed it into a fist to hide the trembling.

*He's overfull,* Silas thought. *He's a cup poured past the brim.*

And then Vey smiled, and stepped backward off the edge of the spire—

—and did not fall.

The air beneath him opened. It opened like an eye: a ring of pale silver light spreading in empty darkness, and beyond the ring, the Undermoon, close enough now to touch, its gray orchards turning slowly. The moon-door. Torn open from the mortal side by sheer stolen force, hanging in the night air above Vellamark like a wound in the world.

"The thirteenth turn begins tonight," Vey said, drifting backward into the light. "At its completion, the door closes forever. Whoever stands at the source when it seals owns everything—or nothing." He spread his arms, and the shard-light blazed through his skin until he was less a man than a silhouette filled with lightning. "I choose everything. Come stop me if you can, swordspeaker. Bring your expensive words."

He fell upward into the Undermoon, and the door began to close behind him, the silver ring shrinking, the edges crawling inward like ice forming over water.

Silas stood at the broken edge of the spire with the wind tearing at him and Bramblewick dimming at his feet and the door closing, closing, closing.

There was no decision. That was the strange mercy of it. He had spent seven years rehearsing cowardice, and in the end there was no room for it—the choice made itself, the way a river chooses the sea. He scooped Bramblewick against his chest; she weighed almost nothing now, a bird made of embers and grief.

"Bramblewick. Can you burn me a path?"

Her eyes opened. Faint. Stubborn. "For you," she whispered, "always," and her whole body flared—one last blaze of the old orchard-fire—and the shrinking ring of the moon-door froze, held open by a thread of flame no thicker than a hair.

It would not hold long. It would not hold her long either; he could feel her guttering against his ribs like a candle reaching its end.

Footsteps on the stairs. Fast ones. Silas turned, and Wren burst into the crown chamber with Tam half-collapsed against her shoulder, both of them streaked with soot, and her eyes went from the dying door to Silas's face and she understood everything in a single breath, because she was Wren, because she had read the prophecy months ago and translated every line of it and had always, always known how this chapter ended.

"No," she said.

"Wren—"

"*No.*" She shoved Tam toward the wall and crossed the chamber in six strides and hit him with the full force of her, fists against his chest, once, twice, and then her forehead against his collarbone, and her voice breaking apart. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to walk through a door into a dying world after everything—we haven't—I never told you—"

"I know," Silas said.

She pulled back and looked at him, and her eyes were furious and wet and more beautiful than anything the covenant had ever made. "Say it anyway. If you're going to die at the bottom of the moon, say it here, in the air, where I can keep it."

The door groaned behind him. Bramblewick's thread of fire thinned.

"I love you," Silas said. "Not the way the oath loved. Not the way the order loved—conditional, contractual, revocable. Just—plainly. Like bread. Like morning. I have loved you since the night you wouldn't leave my stall."

Wren made a sound that was half laugh and half sob. "That's the worst proposal I've ever heard."

"I'm a charm seller. My craft has limits."

She grabbed the front of his shirt and kissed him, hard, desperate, tasting of ash and ink and the archive dust of a hundred forbidden books, and then she pushed him away and wiped her face with her sleeve and became, instantly, entirely, the scholar again—spine straight, voice level, eyes blazing.

"The door needs an anchor on this side or it seals early," she said. "Prophecy, turn thirteen, third stanza: 'one hand mortal, one hand kindled, and the threshold held between.' I hold the mortal side. Bramblewick holds the kindled side. You—" her voice caught, and steadied, "—you go be the fool in the middle."

"It's what I'm best at."

"Don't die."

"I'll try very hard not to."

"Silas." She caught his wrist, and her grip was fierce, and under all the fury her hand was shaking. "Whatever Vey offered you down there—whatever the source shows you—come back. Not the oath. Not the power. *You.*"

He looked at her a long moment, memorizing her: the soot on her cheek, the ink stain on her knuckles, the way the moonlight turned her hair to hammered silver. Then he pressed his forehead to hers, one breath, two.

"Hold the door," he said.

And he stepped off the edge of the world.

Falling upward was like drowning in reverse. The silver ring swallowed him, and Vellamark vanished—its ten thousand lanterns, its canal-dark streets, its cathedral bell still cracked from their singing—and there was only the passage, a throat of light and cold, and Bramblewick's fire streaming from his shoulders like a banner, and far ahead, dwindling, a figure wrapped in lightning falling toward the gray heart of the Undermoon.

Behind him, fainter than a heartbeat, he heard Wren's voice, pitched to carry across the threshold, reciting the third stanza over and over like a woman holding a door against a storm with her own two hands.

Ahead, the Undermoon opened to receive them—all its ash orchards and dead rivers and the Hollow Court waiting at the center like a black crown—and Silas fell toward it with an emberkin dying in his arms and a thief of worlds falling beside him and one turn of the moon left to save everything.

The thirteenth turn had begun.
