## 21. Morning With Ordinary Hands

He woke to the smell of rain that had never fallen.

Silas lay on his back in the ash orchard, staring up at a canopy of burning trees, and for a long moment he could not remember why the sight of them did not frighten him. The flames moved through the branches like fish through water—slow, silver-edged, silent—and where they touched the leaves, the leaves did not blacken but brightened, as if each one were remembering what color it had been before the world learned to be afraid.

His hands were folded on his chest. He looked at them the way a man looks at a stranger's face in a crowd, half-hoping for recognition.

They were just hands. Scarred, knuckle-swollen, the right one still carrying the old burn from the Night of Severance where his own sword had seared him in farewell. But the low hum beneath the skin was gone. The words were gone. For seven years he had carried swordspeech like a coal sewn into his palm, and now when he reached inward for it—not testing, not yet, only reaching, the way a tongue finds the gap of a lost tooth—there was nothing there at all. No heat. No hunger. Just the quiet of an empty room after the fire has gone out and the family has gone home.

He should have wept. He thought distantly that he would weep later, probably at some absurd moment, over bread or boots or Wren's terrible handwriting. For now there was only this strange weightlessness, as if someone had finally set down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing.

"You're awake." Bramblewick's voice came from above him, and it was wrong—it was *right*—and Silas turned his head so fast his neck cracked.

She stood between two trees with her hands full of light. Not translucent anymore. Not fading at the edges like smoke deciding whether to stay. She was solid as a plum, round-cheeked and grinning, her ember-glow steady and warm as a hearth in winter, and she was *taller*, or seemed so, or perhaps she had simply stopped shrinking away from the world. When she laughed—and she was laughing, helplessly, the way children laugh when they have been given back something they were told was lost forever—the nearest tree flared gold from root to crown and rained soft sparks that turned to moths before they touched the ground.

"You're—" Silas sat up. His body ached everywhere, gloriously, the ache of a man who has survived himself. "You're solid."

"I'm *me*!" She spun once, arms out, and the orchard answered her—every tree leaning toward her like wheat toward wind. "The whole Undermoon is me again, do you understand? Do you? When you spoke the oath—" She stopped spinning, and her grin gentled into something older than her face. "When you gave it up, it didn't die, Silas Nakamura. It *spread*. Like seed. Like embers kicked loose from a bonfire. It went everywhere."

"Everywhere," he said. His voice sounded strange without the resonance beneath it. Plain. A man's voice, nothing more.

"Everywhere," Bramblewick confirmed solemnly, and then ruined her own gravity by giggling. "Even Vellamark. *Especially* Vellamark. You should see it. Well—you can't see it from here. Which is why you're still lying in the ash like a great sad log instead of going home."

*Home.* The word landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He listened for the splash and heard none.

Wren. Odile. The tide gate. He remembered all of it in order then, the way grief insists on being remembered in order: the ambush, the crossbow bolts, Odile's voice cracking out orders like a whip while she held the gate with nothing but a smuggler's cunning and a smuggler's stubbornness. He remembered leaving her behind because she told him to, because the moon-door was closing, because dead women cannot hold doors open but living ones can choose to stand in them.

"She made it," Bramblewick said quietly. She had crouched beside him without him noticing; her warmth reached him like sun through glass. "I checked. I've been checking all night. The mortal side of the door is... loud, this morning. Full of small voices. One of them swears like a dockworker and argues with everyone, and I'm fairly sure that's your scholar."

Relief hit him so hard it was almost pain. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until the light through his lids went red.

"And Odile?"

Bramblewick was quiet a moment. In the quiet, the orchard breathed—flames rising and settling, rising and settling, the slow respiration of a realm learning to live again.

"Some people," she said at last, "burn all the way through, and what's left isn't ash. It's light that got somewhere else. I think she's in the walls of that house of hers. I think if you put your ear against the kitchen beam, you'll hear her telling you that you're an idiot and to eat something." Her small warm hand closed over his scarred wrist. "Grief and gratitude braid together, swordspeaker. That's not a flaw in the weave. That's the whole pattern."

He let her pull him up. His legs held. His hands—his ordinary, emptied, unremarkable hands—hung at his sides and asked nothing of him, and for the first time since he was nine years old and the order first put the verse in his mouth, Silas Nakamura felt the particular freedom of a man whose body belongs entirely to himself.

* * *

The moon-door stood where they had left it, in the hollow beneath the oldest tree, but it had changed. Before, it had been a wound—a seam of blue-white light stitched shut around its edges, humming with the effort of holding two worlds apart. Now it was simply a doorway. Arched, unhurried, its surface moving like the skin of a pond in summer. Through it he could see the grey pre-dawn of Vellamark, gulls wheeling over the harbor, the first lamplighter making her rounds along the quay with her pole and her little brass flame.

"It'll stay," Bramblewick said, walking beside him. Her bare feet left prints of warm gold in the ash that faded slowly, like footprints in snow. "Doors don't need to be wounds anymore. It opens at low tide and moonrise both, and sometimes, if you're very polite, in between." She tugged his sleeve, and he knelt—she was still small enough that kneeling was required, and he found he wanted to—and she took his face in both hot little hands. "Listen to me. You gave up the thing that made you special. Do you know what that makes you?"

"Tired," Silas said.

"*Ordinary.*" Her eyes were ancient and delighted. "Which is the rarest thing in either realm. Guard it well."

She kissed his forehead, and it felt like standing too close to a bonfire in the best way, and then she stepped back and shooed him with both hands like a woman shooing a hen, laughing, solid, alive, and Silas walked through the door between worlds with wet eyes and no magic in him at all.

* * *

Vellamark smelled different.

That was the first thing. Seven years he had lived in this city, and he had never noticed how it smelled of held breath—of lamp-oil rationed and rainwater collected in secret and fear sweated into stone. This morning it smelled of bread. Of salt and tar and frying onions from the harbor stalls, ordinary smells, abundant, unguarded, and underneath them something else, faint as a memory of music: a sweetness like woodsmoke that doesn't sting.

The Ward was gone. He saw it in the way the lamplighter paused at her post, frowning at her flame, which had turned briefly blue-white and shaped itself, for half a heartbeat, into a tiny dancing figure before settling back to honest yellow. He saw it in the harbor, where a fisherman stood motionless in his boat, staring at the water, where—just below the surface, unmistakable in the early light—small lights were swimming. Not fish. Lights. Emberkin, tiny as minnows, darting between the hulls and rising to look at the faces above them with frank curiosity, and the fisherman was not screaming for the Wardens. There were no Wardens. Their watch-posts stood empty along the quay, doors hanging open, ledgers of names and crimes fluttering in the breeze like birds released.

A child ran past Silas on the steps down to the water, chasing a paper windmill that spun with no wind at all, faster and faster, glowing faintly at its edges, and the child was shrieking with joy and nobody was arresting anybody.

Silas stood on the quay with his empty hands and watched wonder return to his city the way tide returns to a drained harbor—slowly at first, then all at once, filling every crack.

There would be chaos. He knew that. There would be fear too, and abuse, and fools who mistook freedom for license, and hard years of learning what a covenant shared by everyone actually meant in practice. Wren would have opinions. Wren had already, he suspected, begun drafting them.

But this morning—this one grey, sweet-smelling, miraculous morning—a dockworker he half-recognized from his market days waved at him from a stack of crates and said, "Morning, charm-seller! Weather's strange, eh?" and nodded at the sky, where the clouds had arranged themselves, briefly and without any apparent purpose, into the shape of a laughing mouth.

"Strange," Silas agreed, and found he was smiling, and that the smile hurt, and that he did not want it to stop.

* * *

Odile's house stood where it had always stood, wedged between a chandler's and a derelict warehouse, its shutters the color of old wine. Someone had already hung black cloth from the upper window. Someone had already, judging by the smell, started cooking.

Wren opened the door before he knocked.

She looked as though she had been awake for three days, which she had. Ink on her fingers, a bruise going yellow-green along her jaw from the tide gate, her brother Tomas hovering behind her with a bowl of something steaming and the wary expression of a young man who has recently been rescued and does not yet know the etiquette. Her eyes went over Silas once, twice—the quick inventory of someone who has spent thirteen turns learning exactly how much of him can break.

"You're powerless," she said. Not a question. She had read the queen's memory with him; she had done the arithmetic of the covenant herself.

"I'm ordinary," he corrected, and her mouth trembled, and she hit him—once, flat-handed, on the shoulder, not hard—with all the tenderness she could not say aloud.

"You absolute *idiot*," she said, and then she hugged him, ink-stained fingers digging into his back, her forehead against his collarbone, and above her shoulder Tomas mouthed *thank you* with the solemnity of the very young, and Silas held on and let the well inside him fill at last with everything he had not wept out under the burning trees.

Inside, the house was warm in a way houses are not usually warm. The fire in the grate burned blue-white at its heart. The kettle, unattended, poured itself. On the kitchen beam—old oak, dark with decades of pipe smoke—someone had carved fresh letters, crude and deep:

**EAT SOMETHING, IDIOT.**

Silas put his fingers against the wood. It was warm. And beneath the grain, faint as a heartbeat, he heard—or imagined, or chose to believe, and decided the distinction no longer mattered—a dry chuckle, and the ghost of a voice like gravel and gin: *Took you long enough.*

He laughed. It came out broken and whole at once.

* * *

They buried Odile at evening, on the headland above the harbor, in ground that had never known a Warden's boot. Wren spoke the archive-blessing over her, the old formal words, and then abandoned them halfway through to say, roughly, "She fed runaways when feeding runaways could get you burned, and she died holding a door open for strangers, and if that isn't sainthood I don't know what the word is for," and nobody corrected her, and the sea agreed by turning briefly, impossibly, gold.

Bramblewick came to the funeral. She stepped out of a tide pool at the cliff's base, solid and solemn, carrying a single leaf from an ash-tree that burned without consuming, and she laid it on the grave, where it caught light and kept it, and does so still, they say, on foggy nights.

Afterward they walked back down into the city together—four of them now, a scholar, a freed captive, a spirit-child, and a man with ordinary hands—and the lamps of Vellamark lit themselves as they passed, politely, one by one, like a city holding its breath in hope.

Silas walked last. He flexed his empty fingers once, feeling only knuckles and old scars and the cool evening air, and found that the emptiness fit him like a coat cut to measure.

Behind them, far out in the harbor, something vast and gentle turned over in its sleep, and the water glowed.

And on the doorstep of Odile's house, waiting where no one had placed it, sat a small wooden box, sealed with wax the color of ember-light, bearing a mark neither Wren nor Silas had ever seen—

a spiral, coiled like a sleeping flame,

and beneath it, in handwriting that made Wren go white: *For the swordspeaker who is no longer one. We have questions. Come alone — or don't. The choice, at last, is yours.*
