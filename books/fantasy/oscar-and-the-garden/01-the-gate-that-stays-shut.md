## 1. The Gate That Stays Shut

The frost came late to the valley that year, and when it finally came it came politely, like a guest who knows the house is not his own.

Orsen Lockridge was in the garden before dawn, as he was every morning, kneeling in the dark with his bare hands in the cold earth because gloves made him clumsy at the fine work. The walled garden sat against the eastern slope of the valley, a rectangle of stone older than any house in Hollowmere, older than the chapel, older—though no one said so aloud—than the names of things. And set into its northern wall, flush with the stone as if it had grown there rather than been hung, stood the iron gate.

No one opened it. No one had opened it in Orsen's lifetime, or in Maren's mother's lifetime, or in the lifetime of old Tam Vetch, who claimed to remember everything and remembered most of it twice. The gate had no lock, no bar, no chain. It simply did not open. Hands pressed flat against its iron found it warm on the coldest days and cool on the hottest, and after a while they always took their hands away without quite knowing why. Children dared each other to touch it; children touched it; children grew up and told their own children the gate was nothing but iron and stubbornness, which was the valley's way of saying *holy* without frightening anyone.

Orsen touched it every morning. It was part of the work, though he could not have said who had taught him the work or why it needed doing. He laid his palm against the iron the way a physician lays two fingers to a wrist, and he waited for something—a pulse, a tremor, a word—and every morning of his forty-one years he felt nothing at all, and every morning he nodded anyway, as if satisfied, and went back to his roses.

This morning the gate was warm. Warmer than usual. He noticed it the way one notices a room has gone quiet: not by what was there, but by what was missing from the silence. He frowned at the iron, at the curling sigil worked into its center—a knot of lines like a root system seen through soil, or veins seen through skin—and then he let it go, because there were beans to think about.

That was the thing about Orsen Lockridge, the thing the valley knew him by: he let things go. Griefs, insults, questions. They slid off him like rain off oiled wool. Tam Vetch once said, over cider, that Orsen forgave so easily it was almost unnatural, and Maren had laughed and said that was just Orsen, and neither of them had thought about it again, because thinking about Orsen too hard was like pressing on a bruise you couldn't find.

He turned back to the garden, and the garden stopped him.

The beans were up. Not sprouted—*up*, waist-high, flowering white, heavy with pods ready for picking. In October. Under frost. Orsen stood very still among them, his breath making small ghosts in the air, and looked at the row of impossible beans the way a man looks at a door he is sure was locked.

They weren't the only ones. The roses along the south wall, which should have been bare and sleeping, were in full second bloom, deep red, absurdly lush. The lavender had gone purple again. The apple tree in the corner—the one that had never fruited well, that Orsen had half decided to cut down—was bent under apples green-gold and gleaming with morning wet, more fruit than branches, as if the tree had spent all summer hoarding and had lost its patience all at once.

"Huh," said Orsen.

It was not alarm. Alarm would come later, when alarm became possible. For now it was only the mild, tilted feeling of a man whose tools have rearranged themselves overnight: wrongness, noted, filed, deferred. He picked an apple. It came off the branch willingly, almost eagerly, warm in his palm despite the frost, and it smelled the way apples are supposed to smell in stories rather than in life. He ate it standing there. It was the best apple he had ever tasted, and this troubled him slightly more than the season did, though he couldn't have said why, and so—as ever—he let it go.

* * *

Maren came at midmorning, as she did three days out of seven, carrying bread wrapped in cloth and opinions carried loose.

"You've got frost-bloom on your cabbages," she announced from the garden door, before she'd even properly looked at anything else, because Maren catalogued other people's failings the way merchants catalogued stock. "I told you about the mulch. I *told* you."

"I mulched."

"Then why does it look like the cabbages have been out dancing all night?" She set the bread on the garden bench, a wide plank of silvered oak worn smooth by generations of elbows, and then she actually looked around, and her voice changed. "Orsen. Your roses."

"I know."

"It's nearly November."

"I know."

"Your *beans*." She crossed to the bean row and touched a pod with one finger, gently, the way she touched everything living, which was the softest thing about her and the thing she'd have denied fastest. "These can't be real. These are some kind of—" She stopped. Maren was a practical woman, and practical women in Hollowmere had a whole vocabulary of words they did not say: *magic*, *blessing*, *curse*, *omen*. The valley had no use for such words. Candles in Hollowmere burned straight and steady, always, in wind or draft or dying breath. Prayers said in the chapel rose exactly as far as the rafters and no farther, and everyone knew it, and no one minded much, because the valley was safe and quiet and full, and what more could anyone ask of a place? "Some kind of trick of the light," Maren finished firmly.

"Frost-bloom," Orsen agreed. "On the roses."

She gave him a look. It was a look with twenty years of friendship behind it, and beneath the friendship, if one dug—and nobody dug—a seam of something older and sadder, guilt perhaps, or grief, worn smooth as river stone from long disuse. "You're not worried."

"Should I be?"

"A normal man would be worried."

"I'm not a normal man, I'm a gardener. Things grow or they don't. These grew." He shrugged, and the shrug was genuine, and that was the strangest thing about him, though again, no one thought about it twice. "I'll can what we can't eat. Wren likes the pickled beans."

At the sound of her name, Wren appeared.

There was no other way to describe it. One moment the garden path was empty; the next, Wren was there, nine years old, gap-toothed, hair the color of wet acorns escaping its braid, standing at Orsen's elbow as if she had been poured into place. She had a talent for arriving that bordered on the uncanny. Maren swore the child slept in her own bed every night. Orsen had his doubts. There were mornings he woke to find small footprints in the dew along the garden wall, circling the whole perimeter, patient as a sentry's rounds.

"Wren," Maren said, in the tone mothers use when they already know the answer, "were you here before breakfast?"

"No," said Wren, with the transparent honesty of children everywhere.

"When did you leave the house?"

"Before breakfast."

Orsen hid a smile in the apple tree's shadow. Wren followed him everywhere—or followed the garden everywhere; the distinction had never mattered to her. She weeded beside him, badly and with tremendous conviction. She memorized the names of plants and invented better ones. She talked constantly, a bright running commentary on beetles and clouds and whether the moon got lonely, and Orsen listened to all of it with the unbroken attention of a man being paid by the hour, which was, Wren had once confided to her mother, her favorite thing about him. Everyone else listened the way people listen to children: waiting for the end. Orsen listened like the talking was the point.

Today she was quiet, though. Today she stood at the edge of the bean row and looked at it for a long time, and then looked at Orsen, and then looked at the iron gate, and her face did something complicated that no nine-year-old face should know how to do.

"The flowers are remembering," she said.

"What's that?" Maren asked.

Wren didn't answer her. She was looking at Orsen with those grave gray eyes, and Orsen felt, for one strange sliding moment, that she was going to say something enormous, something that would change the shape of the morning entirely—and then she blinked, and whatever it was sank away somewhere below her words, the way fish sink below ice.

"Nothing," she said. "Can I help pick?"

* * *

They picked until the baskets overflowed. Maren filled a second basket for the widow Hale and a third for the chapel, muttering about waste and gluttony and the shame of vegetables with no respect for the calendar. Orsen worked steadily, contentedly, his hands doing what hands do, and if the warmth of the day kept climbing past all reason—if the frost melted off the wall stones by noon and the bees came stumbling out of wherever bees go in winter, drunk and bewildered and grateful—well. Weather was weather. It happened to everyone.

Except that in Hollowmere, weather was supposed to happen *gently*. That was the bargain of the valley, unwritten and unspoken and understood by everyone down to the chickens: the world outside had storms and miracles and gods who answered or didn't, and the valley had none of it, only the long even breathing of ordinary days. Lightning never struck in Hollowmere. Fever never spread. The river never flooded. Old folk called it the hush, and meant it fondly, the way you'd mean a blanket.

By afternoon the hush felt, to Orsen, thin. He couldn't have explained it. It was like the moment in a familiar song when one instrument drops out—you don't miss it consciously, but your body leans toward the absence. He caught himself standing still in the middle of the path, head cocked, listening for nothing. Twice.

Wren watched him do it both times. She said nothing either time. But she moved a little closer to him afterward, and stayed closer, and when they sat on the bench at dusk splitting the last apple, she leaned against his arm with all her weight, the way she did when she wanted anchoring, and Orsen put his arm around her and held the anchor down.

"Maren says you found me," Wren said, apropos of clouds.

"Mm?"

"When you were young. At the gate. She says you were just *there*, one morning, like a mushroom."

"That's the story."

"Do you remember before?"

Orsen considered the question honestly, turning it over the way he turned over clods of earth, looking for what lived underneath. "I remember the garden," he said. "I remember learning the work. I remember Tam Vetch teaching me to graft, and Maren's mother bringing me soup, and the first winter, how cold the stone walls get. Before that—" He reached for it, the way he reached for it sometimes, usually at night, usually alone: the reach backward past the earliest memory, expecting to hit the soft blank wall where childhood goes fuzzy. His fingers, metaphorically speaking, found no wall. They found a door, and the door was shut, and beyond it was not darkness but *nothing*, a nothing so complete it wasn't even black. "Before that it's quiet," he finished.

"Quiet like the valley?"

He looked down at her. She was watching the gate, not him. The last light was coming off the hills, and the iron drank it, and the sigil at its center seemed—for just a heartbeat—to move, the way a knot of roots moves when the earth above shifts.

"Yes," Orsen said slowly. "Exactly like the valley."

Wren nodded, satisfied, as if a hypothesis had held. Then she yawned enormously, ruined the gravity of everything, and demanded to be walked home before her mother started *that* voice.

* * *

He walked her back through the blue hour, lantern swinging, moths already investigating. Hollowmere at dusk was a handful of lamplit windows scattered along the valley floor, smoke rising straight from chimneys because there was never wind enough to trouble it, the chapel bell tolling six with the punctual indifference it had shown for two hundred years. Neighbors called greetings. Someone's supper smelled of onions. Everything was exactly, perfectly, immovably fine.

At the Hale cottage door, Wren stopped, turned back, and looked up at him with sudden fierce seriousness.

"Don't touch the gate tomorrow," she said.

"Why not?"

"Because it's starting to hum," Wren said. "And I don't think it knows you yet. But it will."

She went inside. The door closed. Orsen stood in the lane for a long moment, lantern dripping light onto the frozen mud, telling himself that children said strange things, that children were weather vanes for adult anxieties, that there was nothing in the world to—

The bell tolled seven.

It had tolled six not four minutes ago.

Orsen counted, standing absolutely still in the empty lane, while the bell rang seven strokes, unhurried, correct, certain of itself. Then the valley settled back into its evening quiet, and somewhere a dog barked twice, and the smoke rose straight up, and everything was fine, everything was fine, everything was exactly as it had always been.

He walked home. He passed the garden wall. He did not touch the gate.

But as he climbed the stairs to his narrow bed, hand trailing along the cold plaster, he realized he could still feel the warmth of the iron in his palm—warmth that had transferred hours ago, warmth that should have faded, warmth that lay in his skin like a coal banked for morning.

Outside, out of season, out of all patience, the garden went on blooming in the dark.
