## 2. First Answered Prayer

The drought came in the third week of high summer, the way droughts always did in the valley—politely at first, then all at once.

Orsen noticed it first in the garden. The foxgloves along the east wall began to droop by midmorning instead of midafternoon, and the soil between the flagstones went pale and cracked like old porcelain. He had kept this garden for longer than he could precisely account for—he had a number of years he told people when they asked, and the number felt true enough in his mouth, though it sat strangely in his chest—and in all that time the valley had never been cruel to him. Dry spells came and went. The river dropped and rose. The garden sulked, then forgave him. That was the rhythm of things, and Orsen trusted rhythms the way other men trusted scripture.

But this year the dryness had a texture to it that troubled him. It wasn't merely the absence of rain. It was an absence with edges, as if something had drawn a careful line around the valley and said *no water past this point*. The clouds still gathered over the western ridgeline every evening, fat and silver-bellied, promising everything. And every evening they slid apart around the valley's borders like a stream around a stone, raining themselves out on the far hills while the garden below stood parched under a sky that seemed to be looking elsewhere.

He mentioned it to Maren when she brought bread on Thursday, because mentioning things to Maren was how he thought aloud without having to think alone.

"Clouds won't come in," he said, turning a loaf in his hands. The crust was dark and split along one side the way she always let it split, and the smell of it filled his kitchen the way bread smell should. "Three weeks now. I've watered from the cistern twice a day. The well's down a hand's width."

Maren wiped her hands on her apron and frowned toward the window, where the light lay flat and white on the garden wall. "Alderbrook's wells are down too," she said. "Farmer Iles lost half his barley. And yet—" She stopped, and Orsen watched her decide not to finish the sentence, which was a thing Maren did sometimes, folding a thought away like linen she didn't want him to see creased.

"And yet?" he prompted gently.

She looked at him a moment too long. "And yet nothing. Weather's weather." She kissed Wren's hair—the girl was sitting on Orsen's step, as she did most days, drawing in the dust with a stick—and left.

Orsen stood in his doorway after she'd gone, holding the warm loaf against his chest, and looked up at the sky. The clouds were massing over the western ridge again, right on schedule, great slow towers of them, and again they would slide apart, and again the valley would stay dry, and again he would tell himself it was only weather.

He almost believed it. But there was a word Maren hadn't said, and its shape stayed in the room after she left, the way the smell of bread did.

*And yet.*

* * *

Wren came to pray on Sunday.

Orsen didn't know that was what she was doing at first. She arrived after supper, as she often did, trailing through his gate without knocking because the gate had never once been latched against her, and she brought with her the particular solemnity of a nine-year-old who has decided something is important and intends to carry it like a full bucket of water—carefully, and without spilling, no matter how far she has to walk.

"Iles's dog died," she announced, sitting down on the kitchen floor beside the stove, where the flagstones held the day's last warmth. "Not the farmer. The dog. It just lay down in the heat and didn't get up."

Orsen set aside the hoe he'd been mending. "That's hard."

"Everything's dying a little," Wren said. "The beans in your garden have grey on their leaves. I saw it. And the river's so low you can walk across it without getting your knees wet, and the fish are all crowded in the deep pool like they're hiding from something." She turned her stick of charcoal in her fingers. She drew everywhere, on anything—slate, dust, the margins of Maren's account book—and what she drew was usually the same thing: the iron gate at the end of the garden, tall and narrow, rendered over and over with increasing detail, though she had never once seen it open. "Maren says we shouldn't complain about weather because complaining doesn't help. But I thought maybe asking might."

"Asking who?"

Wren looked at him as if he were being deliberately slow, which was a look she had perfected. "Whoever answers. That's what praying *is*, isn't it? You ask, and somebody hears, or nobody hears, but you've asked properly either way." She folded her hands in her lap, an oddly formal gesture for a child usually built entirely of elbows and motion. "Nobody prays here anymore. Have you noticed? In Alderbrook they used to have the little shrine by the crossroads, with the candles. They don't even keep candles in it now. Maren says prayers never worked here anyway, so why waste the wax."

"That's not—" Orsen began, and found he didn't know how to finish it. It was true. Everyone knew it was true, the way everyone knew the valley's name and the shape of the hills: prayers didn't take root here. Candles burned straight and steady and indifferent. Blessings spoken over crops were just words with good posture. The chapel in town still held services, but the priest delivered them with the mild embarrassment of a man reading letters addressed to someone who had moved away long ago. Magic, miracle, answered prayer—all of it slid off the valley like rain off oiled cloth. Children grew up knowing this the way they grew up knowing winter followed autumn. Nobody remembered deciding it was so.

"Anyway," Wren said, "I'm going to try. Right here, in your kitchen, because your house feels like it listens. Is that all right?"

Orsen should have said no. He would think about that afterward, in the long years to come, how easily he had said yes—how he had only smiled and said, "This house has never once refused to listen to you," and gone back to his mending, because the request sounded small. A child wanting to try a thing that never worked. What harm could live inside that?

"Close your eyes," Wren instructed, and he obeyed, mostly, keeping his lashes low enough to watch her through them, because watching Wren was a habit he had never examined and therefore never questioned.

She knelt on the warm stones. She pressed her palms together so hard her knuckles whitened. And she prayed—not the rote verses the priest recited, not the formal invocations printed in the chapel's worn books, but a child's prayer, plain and specific and utterly unguarded:

"Please. Please send rain. Not for me—I'm fine—but for the beans, and the fish, and Iles's other dog, and the barley, and everybody whose gardens are dying. Please. I'll give back whatever you want. I'll be good forever, or near enough. Please just make it rain."

Her voice was small in the kitchen. The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, the evening lay flat and dry and silent.

Nothing happened.

Wren opened one eye, saw Orsen watching, and closed it again with dignity. "It takes a moment," she said. "In the stories it takes a moment."

Orsen smiled and reached for his mending again—

—and the world stuttered.

There was no better word for it, though he would spend many nights searching for one. The kitchen did not change; the light did not dim; no thunder rolled. But something underneath everything *stuttered*, the way a candle flame stutters when a door opens in another room—a hitch in the fabric of the ordinary, a skipped heartbeat in the body of the world. Orsen's hand froze above the hoe. The hairs on his forearms lifted. And beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, beneath the bedrock itself, he felt—or heard, or *remembered hearing*—a single deep note, like a bell struck once in a drowned cathedral.

Then the rain came.

It did not begin. It *arrived*. One moment the window showed a dry violet dusk; the next the glass was sheeted white, and the sound hit the roof like gravel flung from the sky, and the first drops that struck the flagstones outside spat steam where they landed, so hot was the ground after three weeks of thirst. Orsen was at the window before he knew he'd moved. Rain fell in ropes. Rain fell as if the sky had been holding this one delivery for years and meant to be thorough. The gutters roared. The garden vanished into grey curtain.

And the smell—

Rain has a smell, and everyone knows it, the clean mineral breath of wet dust. This was that, and under it, rising through it like a note through a chord, was something else. Something older than weather. Orsen stood with his face close to the glass and breathed it in and could not name it and could not stop breathing it: a smell like cold stone deep underground, like the air in a room sealed for centuries, like incense burnt by no living hand. It smelled, absurdly, the way he imagined the inside of the earth must smell if the earth had been keeping a secret. It smelled like the space behind the iron gate.

"Wren." His voice came out strange to him. Flat. Careful. "Come away from the window."

Wren was already beside him, pressed to the glass, her eyes enormous, her charcoal forgotten. "It worked," she whispered. "Orsen, it *worked*. I asked and it—" She laughed, a bright startled sound, and spun to look at him, radiant, triumphant, nine years old and newly certain the universe took dictation. "Did you feel it? When it started? Like the whole house went *hmm*?"

Like the whole house went hmm. Yes. Exactly that. And Orsen, looking down at her flushed and joyful face, felt wonder rise in him like water in a well—real wonder, the kind he hadn't known since childhood, since whoever his childhood had belonged to—and felt dread rise alongside it, twin waters in the same shaft, rising together, indistinguishable.

Because the rain was wrong. He couldn't have said how, not yet, not in words he could trust. But every instinct he owned—which felt suddenly less like instinct and more like memory, like training, like *duty*—every thread of him pulled taut and pointed at the sky and said: this is not weather. Weather does not arrive on command. Weather does not smell of sealed rooms. Weather does not fall in answer to a prayer offered in a valley where prayers have never once been answered, in all the years anyone could remember, which was—

Which was how many years?

The question surfaced and sank before he could hold it. He let it go. There was a great deal, he was beginning to understand, that he let go.

"Orsen?" Wren tugged his sleeve. Her joy had cooled into something more careful; children read adult faces faster than adults read their own. "You look scared."

"I'm not scared," he lied, and was surprised at how easily that lie came, and more surprised at how familiar it felt in his mouth, like a tool returned to its proper hook. "I'm thinking. There's a difference." He crouched to her height. "Wren, what you did tonight—don't do it again. Not until we understand it. Praying is—"

"It's just asking," she said stubbornly.

"It's *knocking*," Orsen said, and did not know, until the words were out, why he had chosen that word, or why it rang in the room like the struck bell beneath the floor. "And we don't know what's on the other side of the door."

* * *

He walked the garden at midnight, because sleep was impossible and because the gardener in him needed to see what the rain had done.

What the rain had done was everything. The beds drank greedily; the foxgloves had straightened as if ashamed of their earlier drooping; water stood silver in every hollow of the flagstones. The garden had never looked so lush, so violently alive, so *satisfied*. Orsen moved down the paths in the thin moonlight and felt the garden's pleasure like warmth from a hearth, and that too was wrong—he had tended these plants for years and had never once felt them as anything but plants—and he filed the feeling away in the growing drawer of things he did not examine.

At the end of the garden stood the iron gate.

It always stood there. It was simply part of the garden, the way the wall was part of the garden: a tall narrow gate of black iron set into the far wall, older than any style of ironwork he could name, its bars twisted into patterns that resolved, when stared at, into nothing. No path led to it. No lock secured it, and no hinge allowed it—it had never opened in his memory, and he had long ago stopped wondering what lay beyond it, because beyond it was only the hillside, scrub grass and gorse, the same as anywhere. Everyone in the valley had such a gate somewhere, more or less; theirs happened to be in his garden wall, and he happened to be the keeper of the garden, and that was the whole of it. That was what he told visitors, when they remarked on it. That was what he told himself.

He put his palm against the iron.

He had done this ten thousand times—checking the bars, clearing ivy, steadying himself on the climb to the wall's top. The iron had always been exactly what iron is: cold, inert, honest.

Tonight it hummed.

Not vibrated—hummed. A note lived in the metal, deep and patient, the same note he had felt beneath the kitchen floor when Wren prayed, the drowned-cathedral bell, except now it was not fading. Now it sustained. Now, as he stood with his hand flat against the gate and the unnatural rain streaming down the back of his neck, the hum *rose to meet him*, climbing slowly through the iron and into his bones, and Orsen understood with a certainty that bypassed every reasonable objection he possessed:

It knows me. It has always known me. It has been waiting for me to touch it at the right moment, and tonight is the right moment, and I do not know how I know that, but I know it the way I know my own hands.

The rain intensified. Somewhere over the western ridge, thunder spoke—a long rolling syllable in no language he knew, and yet some shuttered room in him leaned toward it like a student toward a teacher's voice. The hush of the valley, the great soft silence that had lain over this place for as long as anyone could remember—the silence that made candles burn straight and prayers fall dead—that hush *stuttered* again, wider this time, longer, like a lamp guttering, like a held breath beginning to fail.

And in the gap of the stutter, Orsen heard—faintly, from very far away, or from very near, from directly behind his own heart—a voice. It did not use words. It used something older than words, something that arrived in him whole, the way smells arrive, the way grief arrives:

*At last. At last. At last.*

He snatched his hand from the gate.

The hum died. The rain went on falling, steady and heavy and smelling of sealed stone, and the valley lay silent around it, and Orsen stood alone in his garden with his heart slamming and his palm tingling, staring at a door that had never opened, that had no lock and no hinge, that was, as far as he had ever been permitted to know, nothing but iron and habit.

Behind him, small feet pattered on wet flagstones. He turned. Wren stood at the edge of the lamplight spilling from his kitchen window, wrapped in a blanket, soaked to the elbows, her eyes shining.

"I couldn't sleep," she said. "The rain's too loud. Isn't it wonderful?"

Orsen looked at her—at this child who had knocked on a door he hadn't known was a door, and woken something that knew his name without speaking it—and felt the wonder and the dread in his chest finally separate, the two waters splitting, and the dread ran deeper.

"Yes," he said. "Wonderful. Come inside, Wren. Out of the rain."

And behind them both, unheard, unfelt by anyone but the iron itself, the gate waited with its new note in its throat, humming softly to itself in the dark, like something remembering how to sing.
