## 3. What the Garden Remembers

The morning the garden began to remember, Orsen was on his knees in the bean rows with dirt to his elbows and a trowel between his teeth, and he almost didn't notice.

That was the thing about the garden: it had always done a little more than gardens should. It bloomed early and held late. Its roses opened in frost. The lettuces came up sweeter than any seed packet promised, and once—only once, years ago, when Wren was small enough to carry on one hip—a patch of chamomile had arranged itself, overnight, into a rough spiral that no hand had planted. He had told himself then what he told himself about everything in this valley: *the soil is good here. The water is clean. Things grow well where nothing else happens at all.*

So when he straightened, spat out the trowel, and saw that the nasturtiums along the south wall had climbed higher than they had any right to climb—had climbed, in fact, into the shape of something—he did what he always did. He looked away first. He finished the row. He gave the beans their water and the beans gave him their silence, and only after he'd wiped his hands on his apron and run out of ordinary tasks did he let himself turn around and look properly.

The nasturtiums had grown into a crown.

Not a wreath, not a trellis-loop, not any of the shapes a lazy vine might fall into by accident. A crown. Five points, each one a closed bud angled upward like a flame, the stems beneath them braided so tightly and so deliberately that Orsen felt his stomach drop as though he'd caught someone watching him through a window. It stood taller than his knee against the sun-warmed stone of the wall, gold-and-orange in the morning light, and it was unmistakably, insultingly, a crown.

He walked around it twice. He touched it—the stems were warm, warmer than the wall behind them, warm like something recently alive—and the whole structure shivered, not in wind, because there was no wind, but in something closer to recognition.

"All right," Orsen said aloud, because talking to plants was a habit he'd never bothered to be ashamed of. "All right. Very funny."

The crown did not laugh. The crown went on being a crown, patient as stone, and somewhere under his breastbone Orsen felt the faintest hum, like a finger run along the rim of a wet glass. The same hum he'd felt under his palm on the iron gate two nights ago, after the rain that smelled of old copper and older grief. The rain Wren had prayed for.

He had spent those two nights telling himself the storm had been weather. Strange weather, violent weather, weather with a taste to it—but weather. The valley had simply, for one night, forgotten how to keep things out. That was all. That could be all.

But crowns did not grow out of nasturtiums. Crowns did not grow anywhere.

He fetched the shears. He stood before the thing with the blades open, and found that he could not. His hands simply declined—not trembling, not weak, just *declining*, the way a man's hands decline to strike his own reflection. So instead he knelt again, awkwardly, in front of a wall of flowers shaped like a king's circlet, and said, "Whose are you?"

The garden, which had never answered him in thirty years of tending, answered.

It happened below hearing, more pressure than sound, a shift in the air like the moment before a summer storm. Every plant in the walled garden turned—not visibly, not all at once, but he would have sworn afterward that every leaf angled toward him by a degree, the way faces turn in a crowd when someone speaks your name. And at the far end of the bed, where the poppies grew wild and unsupervised, three new buds swelled and opened within the space of a single breath, red as coals, and arranged themselves.

Orsen knew cities. He had never seen one burn. But he knew, looking at the three poppies with their black centers like doorways and their petals flung outward like sparks off a roof, that this was a city burning. The shape of it was in the way the flowers leaned—one collapsing inward, one reaching, one already fallen flat. It was not a picture. It was a memory wearing flowers the way a ghost wears a sheet, and it was not *his* memory. He was sure of that the way you are sure of your own name. Whatever had burned, he had not seen it. And yet his eyes were hot, and his throat was tight, and he was grieving something he had no right to grieve, some loss that belonged to the poppies and not to him.

He sat down hard in the dirt.

* * *

Maren came at midday, as she always did, with bread wrapped in cloth and her sleeves rolled and the particular walk of a woman who has decided not to be worried and is losing the argument. Wren came behind her, as she always did, half a step back and one step sideways, orbiting him the way small moons orbit large quiet planets.

"You look terrible," Maren announced, setting the bread on the gatepost, which served them both as a table, an altar, and a border checkpoint. "You've got that face. The one where you're deciding whether to tell me something."

"I don't have a face like that."

"Orsen. I've known you twenty years. You have four faces: fine, tired, thinking-about-bees, and deciding-whether-to-tell-me-something." She crossed her arms. "This is the fourth one."

He meant to lie. He had a whole life's practice of lying gently, of smoothing things over, of keeping the valley's hush folded around its small strangenesses. But when he opened his mouth, what came out was: "Come and see."

They came. Maren first, wary, flour still dusting her forearms; Wren last, drifting, the way she drifted toward anything the adults were afraid of. And the garden, which had spent the morning holding its shapes like a held breath, let them breathe.

The crown was still there. The burning city too, though the poppies had begun to droop as cut flowers do, their memory fading even as it stayed legible. And along the herb border, where yesterday there had been nothing but sage and thyme gone slightly woody with age, a new growth had come up in the space of hours: pale, sweet-smelling, white as paper, and shaped—there was no other word for it—like a woman's face. Not a portrait. An impression. The lift of cheekbones in the curve of a stem, the suggestion of closed eyes in two tight buds, hair made of trailing vines that spilled over the bed's edge and kept growing, slowly, like it had all the time in the world.

Wren made a sound Orsen had never heard from her—not fear exactly, but the sound a child makes when the world confirms something she already suspected. "She's sad," Wren said.

"Wren—" Maren started.

"She's *sad*," Wren insisted, crouching at the border's edge with her chin nearly touching the white blooms. "Look at her mouth. She's sad and she's waiting and she thinks somebody's coming back." The child looked up at Orsen with her serious gray eyes, and what she said next went into him like a splinter and sat there for days: "Do you know her?"

"No," Orsen said. And then, because the garden was listening and lying near it had begun to feel like lying in church: "I don't think I know anyone from before this valley."

The words came out strange, heavier than he'd intended, and he watched them land on Maren like stones dropped in still water. She had gone very still. Stillness was not natural to Maren; Maren was a woman built of motion, kneading and hauling and scolding and laughing. But she stood now with the bread forgotten on the gatepost and her arms wrapped around herself, looking at the flower-woman, then at Orsen, then at the flower-woman again, and Orsen watched twenty years of a friendship rearrange itself around something she had been carrying alone.

"Maren," he said carefully. "What did you mean to tell me?"

"How do you know I meant to tell you anything?"

"Because you look like I feel."

That got a short, startled laugh out of her, the kind that breaks tension by admitting it exists. She sat down on the low wall beside the gate, patted the stone beside her until he sat too, and was quiet long enough that Wren, sensing the weight of the moment with a child's unerring instinct for important things, came and wedged herself between them and took Orsen's hand without asking, the way she'd done since she was three.

"You were young," Maren said at last. "Younger than Wren is now, no—that's not true, you were maybe nineteen. Twenty. It was the spring after Wren's grandmother died, the year the road washed out, and I'd walked up to bring the old keeper his bread because he'd stopped coming down." She paused. "Except there wasn't an old keeper anymore. There was just you. Sitting against the gate with your knees drawn up, in clothes that weren't valley clothes, with mud to your waist like you'd crawled here through a river, and you looked at me and asked—" Her voice caught, steadied. "You asked me where you were. And then you asked who you were. And then you asked if I knew, and I said no, and you believed me, and that was that."

"That's not *that*," Orsen said. His voice sounded far away to him. "People don't just appear at gates. People have people. Someone comes looking. There were searches, surely—"

"We waited for one." Maren's jaw set. "A season. Two. Nobody came, Orsen. Nobody ever came, and no village within a month's walk had lost a young man, and no traveler recognized your face, and eventually the valley did what the valley does, which is fold things in and stop asking. You took over the garden because the garden needed keeping and you were already here. And I—" She looked down at her hands, flour in the creases, twenty years of Tuesdays and bread and borrowed salt and a boy who became a man who became her closest friend. "I let it happen. I let you be the man who was simply *here*, because the alternative was telling you every day that you were a question nobody could answer, and I couldn't do that to you. I loved you too much. I loved you selfishly, maybe. I chose for you. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you why."

The garden rustled. There was no wind. The white flower-woman's vine-hair stirred against the stones, and the crown of nasturtiums caught the afternoon light, and Orsen understood—with the vertiginous clarity of a man stepping off a stair that isn't where his foot expects it to be—that the garden was not showing him these shapes at random. It was blooming memories. Someone's memories. Memories pressed into the soil the way seeds are pressed into furrows, waiting for warmth, waiting for water, waiting perhaps for a rain that smelled of copper and came because a child asked it to.

And none of them were his.

"They're not mine," he said quietly. "The flowers. What they're remembering. It's not my past, Maren. If it were—if I'd burned in that city or worn that crown or loved that woman—wouldn't they be *my* memories? Wouldn't they be coming up in me, not out of the ground?"

"I don't know," Maren said. "I don't know anything anymore. I know you appeared at a gate with no past, and I know that since the night of that rain, this garden has been saying things out loud, and I know that you're the only person in this valley who can touch the gate without flinching." She hesitated, and he saw her decide something, saw her shoulders square the way they did before hard work. "There's more. There's a letter. It came with you—or rather, it was tucked inside your coat, and the old keeper read it, and he gave it to me the winter before he died, and he made me swear—"

"Maren."

"He made me swear not to show you unless the garden ever spoke. Those were his words. *Unless the garden ever speaks.* I thought he was an old man rambling. I thought I'd never have to choose." She was already untying the inner pocket of her apron, the one Orsen had teased her about for two decades, the one she'd never once let him peek into. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry I waited. I'm sorry I chose."

From the pocket she drew a letter, folded twice, sealed with plain wax gone amber with age, soft at the corners from decades of being carried against a warm body. On its face, in a careful hand, one word:

*LOCK.*

Orsen did not reach for it. Wren's hand tightened in his. Behind them, softly, without any wind at all, the entire garden turned its thousand leaves toward the letter like a congregation turning toward a reading—and the humming under Orsen's breastbone rose, for the first time, into something almost like a voice.

Beyond the wall, out on the road, a figure had stopped. Ragged, dusty, travel-worn, standing perfectly still in the lane and staring at the gate with an expression Orsen could not yet read at this distance—except that it was not surprise, and it was not confusion.

It was recognition.
