## 4. The Pilgrim at the Wall

The stranger came up the valley road on the third morning after the rain, and Orsen saw her before she saw him, which was unusual. The road bent around the shoulder of the eastern hill like a question mark, and anyone arriving had to pass the garden wall before they reached the gate itself—this was by design, though Orsen had never known whose design. He was on his knees in the beet bed when he noticed her, a smudge of movement against the pale stone of the hillside, and he stayed kneeling longer than he needed to, watching.

She walked badly. That was his first impression—not injured, exactly, but worn, the way a boot sole wears through from years of honest roads. Her cloak had been gray once and was now the color of old dishwater, patched at both elbows with cloth that didn't match, and she leaned on a staff that looked less like a walking aid than like something she carried because she'd forgotten how to put it down. Her hair was dark going to iron at the temples, cut short and unevenly, as if she'd done it herself with a knife and no mirror.

Orsen stood, brushing soil from his knees, and felt the gate behind him do the thing it had been doing for three days: humming. Low, constant, below hearing and yet somehow in it, the way you can feel a distant bell through the soles of your feet. Since Wren's prayer and the rain that smelled of deep water and older things, the iron had not been quiet. He had taken to resting his palm against it each morning, the way Maren checked a fevered child's brow. Each morning it was warmer.

The pilgrim stopped at the wall. She did not come to the gate first, which interested him. Most travelers made straight for the gate—it was the only way in or out of the valley, an iron slab set into stone older than any masonry in the region, and it drew the eye the way a hearth draws a cat. But this woman stopped at the garden wall instead, laid one hand flat upon it, and closed her eyes as though listening to a heartbeat under the stone.

Then she opened them and looked directly at Orsen, and he understood that she had known he was there the whole time.

"You're awake," she said. Her voice was roughened, road-worn, but underneath it ran something else—a current of urgency held carefully still, the way a hand holds a bird. "Good. I've been afraid I'd find this place asleep."

"The valley doesn't sleep," Orsen said. It came out more curtly than he intended. "We're farmers here. We keep ordinary hours."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "Yes," she said softly. "You would say that."

She came along the wall to the gate, and Orsen found himself moving to stand between her and it without having decided to. It was instinct, the same instinct that made him check the latch twice each night, that had always made him uneasy when strangers lingered too long near the iron. The gate was his to watch. He could not have said where the certainty came from; it was simply in him, like knowing which plants wanted morning water and which would drown in it.

"I'm not here for your gate," the woman said, reading him again, damn her eyes. "I'm here because of what's behind it. And what's coming toward it." She planted her staff and studied him with the frank, unhurried attention of someone who had looked at many things and been lied to about most of them. "My name is Sable. I've walked eleven months to reach this valley, and I'll be honest with you, keeper—I'm too tired to be clever. May I have bread and a place to sit? I'll tell you everything I know, and it's worse than anything you've imagined, and then you can decide what to do with me."

*Keeper.* The word landed strangely, familiar and foreign at once, like a coat that fit perfectly and belonged to someone else. "There's bread," Orsen said finally. "Maren Ashdown brings it most mornings. There's a bench by the wall in the sun."

"Sun," Sable repeated, tasting the word. "Eleven months since I sat in sun that didn't cost me something." She followed him to the bench, and Orsen noted that she kept her distance from the gate as she passed it—not fearfully, but respectfully, the way one keeps distance from a sleeping animal. She knew what it was. The knowledge sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

He fetched water and the heel of yesterday's loaf, and while she ate—carefully, thoroughly, wasting nothing—he watched the road behind her, empty, and the hills beyond it, green and indifferent, and tried to feel the valley as it had always felt: sealed, safe, dull as a well-kept kitchen. He could not. The hum was in everything now. The beets had come up in three days instead of three weeks. The roses along the south wall were blooming their second flush of the year, out of season, out of reason, petals so red they seemed lit from within.

Sable followed his gaze to the roses. "They remember," she said, mouth full of bread. "That's what's happening, if you're wondering. Everything sealed away starts remembering when the seal thins."

"Nothing is sealed here," Orsen said. "This is just a valley."

"Yes," she said again, gently, and it was the gentleness that frightened him. "You keep saying that. Like a man saying *the floor is solid* while standing on ice."

* * *

Maren arrived mid-morning with Wren trailing her, and Orsen watched the meeting the way one watches weather turn. Maren took one look at the stranger on the bench and her arms went full of bread-basket and suspicion at once. Wren took one look and went radiant, because Wren loved strangeness the way other children loved sweets.

"Are you a pilgrim?" Wren demanded, planting herself in front of Sable with the fearless directness of the very young. "Pilgrims have staffs. Did you walk far? Are your feet rotten? Papa says pilgrims' feet go rotten."

"Wren," Maren said warningly.

"My feet are magnificent," Sable said gravely. "Rotten, but magnificent. Eleven months of walking."

"Where from?"

"Wren—" Maren again, sharper.

But Sable crouched down to the child's level, staff across her knees, and answered seriously, as one adult to another. "From the coast. From a city called Greyharbor, which used to have a temple to the Threshold Mother, and now has a hole where the temple was, and a lot of people who pray into the hole anyway." She held Wren's eyes. "Your friend here—the tall one who watches the gate—do you know him well?"

"He's Orsen," Wren said, as if reporting the phases of the moon. "He grows things. He lets me help even though I pull weeds wrong. He has a mark on his wrist shaped like a knot."

Orsen went cold all over.

It was such a small thing. A birthmark, low on the inside of his left wrist, a pale whorl like a fingerprint or a knot in wood. He had had it as long as he could remember—which, he realized with a lurch he kept off his face, was not actually very long. Twenty-odd years. Maren had told him he'd come to the valley as a young man. Before that, mist. He had never much minded the mist. Mist was restful, if you didn't look at it too hard.

Sable rose slowly. "May I see your wrist?"

"No," said Maren, stepping between them, basket clutched like a shield. "Whoever you are, whatever you're selling, you'll not—"

"Maren." Orsen heard his own voice, calm, almost detached. "It's all right."

He pushed back his sleeve. The mark sat on his skin, pale as ever, a whorled knot of lines that might have meant nothing at all.

Except that Sable, seeing it, made a sound like a man taking a blade between the ribs. She caught herself against her staff. For a long moment she didn't speak, and when she did, her voice had gone hoarse and reverent and terrified all at once.

"That's not a birthmark," she whispered. "That's the sigil of the Sealed Door. The old sign, from before the maps changed. I've seen it carved on ruins in four countries. I've seen it burned into the sky above Greyharbor the night the gods woke. It is the mark of the thing that holds the door shut." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet. "It's on *you*. Not inked. Not scarred. Grown. You were marked before you had skin, keeper. You are the mark."

The wind moved through the garden, and every rose on the south wall turned its face toward Orsen at once, all together, like a congregation.

No one spoke. Somewhere beyond the wall, a bird sang and thought better of it.

"That's nonsense," Maren said, but her voice shook, and Orsen saw her hands white-knuckled on the basket, and saw also—this was the part that hurt—that she wasn't surprised. Not entirely. Some corner of her had been waiting for this woman, or one like her, for twenty years.

"Sable," Orsen said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "You said you came because of what's behind the gate. Tell me what you think is behind my gate."

"A door," she said simply. "And behind the door, a god that ate prayers. Not answered them—*ate* them. Every hope ever lifted up in this world passed through the old doors, and one of them learned to swallow instead of carry. The others bound it. They walled it behind a seal woven from a whole country's worth of memory and sacrifice, and they hid the door in a hushed valley where magic couldn't reach it, and they set a keeper over it." She paused. "The stories disagree about what the keeper was. A priest. A king. A ghost. But they agree the gods slept as long as the door was held."

"And now?" Wren asked, small voice thin.

"Now the prayers are being answered again," Sable said. "All over the world. Rain where rain was prayed for. Cures. Curses. The old gods woke eleven months ago, and none of us knew why until we read the signs backward." She turned to Orsen, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "They woke, keeper, because someone *stopped holding the door.*"

* * *

They sat in the garden through the afternoon, and the light went long and gold, and Orsen listened to Sable talk because stopping would have been worse. She told them of Greyharbor's drowned temple and the shrines lighting themselves along the coast roads. She told of villages where the wells gave answers instead of water. She told of a storm two valleys east that had fallen in the shape of a kneeling figure, and of people who had knelt inside it and come out changed—or hadn't come out at all.

Maren sat with her arms folded and her jaw set, asking hard, practical questions—how many, how far, how fast—and Orsen loved her fiercely for it, for the ordinariness of her arithmetic against all this vastness. Wren sat cross-legged at Sable's feet, chin on her fists, drinking it in with the terrible appetite children have for the end of the world.

And through it all, Orsen's left wrist itched.

Not painfully. Just a warmth, a pulse, faint as a second heartbeat, and every time Sable said the word *door*, the pulse answered. He kept his sleeve down. He watched the gate across the garden, iron-dark and silent in the slanting light, and he thought: *I have pressed my hand against you ten thousand mornings. Have you been pressing back?*

When the shadows grew long, Sable stood, swaying with exhaustion, and asked only for a corner of the shed and a blanket. Maren, after a visible war with herself, offered her the spare room, because Maren was Maren, and hospitality was a load-bearing wall of her soul.

As Sable gathered her staff, she stopped beside Orsen at the gate. Up close she smelled of road dust and woodsmoke and something faintly resinous, like incense burned down to nothing.

"I'm sorry," she said quietly. "For the way of it. I've delivered bad news in nine countries and it never gets lighter."

"You said the gods woke because someone stopped holding the door." Orsen kept his eyes on the iron. "What does that mean? Practically. What does a person do, holding a door?"

"I don't know," Sable admitted. "The stories don't say. They only say the door must be *held*—and that whoever holds it forgets, because remembering is the one thing the seal can't survive." She hesitated, and then, gently, touched his left wrist, just above the hidden mark. The pulse beneath his skin leapt like a startled fish. "Whatever you were, keeper, before the mist—you agreed to this. That's the part the stories agree on loudest. Nobody gets marked like that unwilling."

Orsen looked down at her hand on his arm, at the ragged pilgrim who had walked eleven months to say one sentence to him, and heard himself ask the question he had been swallowing all day:

"If I'm the one who stopped holding it—then why hasn't the door opened?"

Sable's face did something complicated. Pity and dread braided together.

"Because it's not opening, keeper," she said. "It's *thawing*. Slow, from the edges. Which means you have time—but less than you'd think." She glanced past him, at the garden, at the roses still facing him like a silent choir, and her voice dropped to a whisper. "And it means the things the seal was made from are starting to come loose. Starting to *remember themselves*."

Behind her, at the far end of the garden, the flowers had begun, very quietly, to grow into shapes.
