## 5. Wren Sees the Door

Wren had known for a long time that the world was wearing a costume.

She was seven, which was old enough to know things and young enough that no one believed her when she said them out loud, so mostly she kept her knowing folded up small inside her chest, like the pressed flowers in her mother's Bible. She knew that the hens lied about how many eggs they'd laid. She knew that the river at the bottom of the valley sang to itself when it thought no one was listening, a low tuneless humming that made her teeth ache pleasantly. And she knew — had known since before she could say it in words — that Orsen Lockridge was not entirely a man.

He was mostly a man. That was the important part. Mostly was nearly all, and nearly all of anything was plenty.

But there was more of him than there should have been.

* * *

It started, as most of Wren's best and worst discoveries did, with bread.

Her mother sent her down the lane with a loaf wrapped in cloth still warm enough to fog the morning air, because Maren Aldous was a woman who believed grief and hunger were cousins and fed both the same way. The gate at the end of the valley stood where it always stood, iron and tall and shut, and beside it the walled garden where Orsen spent his days among flowers that bloomed when they shouldn't have.

Wren stopped at the top of the little rise above the garden wall, the way she always did, because from here she could see everything at once: the gate, the wall, the green roof of the tool shed, and Orsen moving between the beds with his sleeves rolled up, his hands dark to the wrist with good soil.

And behind him — the way it always was, the way it had been for as long as she could remember, though she'd only lately learned to look straight at it instead of around it — stood the other shape.

It was tall. Taller than Orsen by a head and then some, so tall its crown would have brushed the lintel of the gate if it had bothered to stand under it. It was thin in the way that shadows are thin, without any thickness at all, a darkness drawn in outline only. And it wore Orsen's silhouette the way a coat hangs on a hook: same slope of shoulders, same tilt of the head when he bent over the bean rows, same long-fingered hands — but stretched, elongated, as though someone had taken the shape of him and pulled it taffy-thin toward the sky.

It didn't move when he moved. That was the worst part. Orsen would reach to pinch back dead blossoms, and the shadow would simply be standing there, arms at its sides, while his arms went about their work. Sometimes, when the light came slantwise through the valley mist, the shadow's edges shivered, and Wren got the feeling — cold, certain, like a stone dropped into her stomach — that it was not standing behind him at all, but *inside* him, wearing him out from within, and that one day the coat might decide to stop being a coat.

She loved him anyway.

That was the thing nobody understood about knowing things. Knowing didn't give you a choice about what you knew, but it never gave you a choice about what you loved either. Those were two different doors, and only one of them opened from the outside.

* * *

"Orsen," she called, sliding down the rise on her heels, bread held high like an offering. "Mama says you're too skinny and I'm to fix it."

"Maren Aldous," Orsen said gravely, straightening and wiping his hands on his trousers, "has been saying that for twenty years and has yet to succeed."

"That's because you keep giving half of it away." Wren thrust the loaf at him. "To me. Which is counterproductive."

He laughed — his real laugh, low and surprised, like he hadn't expected to find it in his own mouth — and broke the bread, and gave her the bigger half, which proved her point exactly but she let it go, because warm bread was warm bread.

They ate sitting on the low wall by the gate, shoulder to shoulder, crumbs scattering for the sparrows. This was Wren's favorite arrangement in the whole world: herself and Orsen and the garden humming with bees, the iron gate cool at her back even in summer, as though it drank the heat out of the day and kept it somewhere no one could find.

Today, though, something was wrong with the gate.

Wren noticed it the way you notice a note sung flat in a song you've known all your life — not with your ears first, but with your skin. She turned around on the wall to look at it properly.

The gate was iron, black-green with age, worked into long vertical bars crowned with spearpoints, and set in its center a round medallion stamped with a sigil: a circle crossed by a single line, like an eye closed by a scar. Everyone in the valley knew the gate. Nobody opened it. Nobody tried. It wasn't forbidden, exactly; it was simply understood, the way it was understood that you didn't dig up graves or ask the river why it hummed.

But today, inside the iron —

Wren blinked. Rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, the way her mother did when the lamp smoke stung. Looked again.

There was a second door.

It hung in the middle of the iron one, shimmering, the way air shimmers over the bake-oven in August, except this shimmer was pale gold and faintly green, like sunlight through new leaves, like the color of the very first shoots in spring. It was door-shaped — tall and narrow, arched at the top — and it did not reflect the garden or the sky or anything at all. When Wren tilted her head, she thought she saw movement behind it, slow and vast, the way you can sense a mountain's shadow moving across a valley even if you never see it go.

"Orsen," she said quietly. "What's behind your gate?"

"The road," he said. "The road out of the valley. Same as it's always—"

"Not that one."

He stopped chewing. Wren watched him turn and look at the gate, and she watched him see nothing, absolutely nothing, just iron and rust and the closed ordinary world — and she felt something twist in her chest, because his face when he looked at it was so open, so trusting, so completely unaware that his own gate was pregnant with another door, and that his own shadow was standing ten feet away in the bean rows, watching them both.

Because it had moved. While they ate. It had come closer, silent as a thought, and now it stood at the edge of the garden where the light ended, and it was looking at the gate too.

No. Not at the gate.

At *her*.

Wren had never been looked at by a shadow before, and she hoped never to repeat the experience. There were no eyes in it, of course. But attention poured off it like heat off a stove, and all of that attention was aimed at her small self sitting on the wall with bread crumbs on her dress, and beneath the terror of it she felt — absurdly, unforgivably — *pity*. Because whatever the shadow was, it looked hungry the way the drought-stricken fields looked hungry, and hunger that old must hurt.

"Wren." Orsen's hand settled on her shoulder. His hand was warm and soil-smelling and real, and she gripped it hard enough to bruise. "You've gone white as milk. What do you see?"

She almost said *nothing*. She had said *nothing* her whole life, about the humming river and the lying hens and the shape behind Orsen, because grown-ups had a way of going strange and careful when children said true things, and she hated that strangeness worse than she hated being scared.

But the second door was shimmering brighter now, gold-green and breathing, and the shadow had drifted a step nearer, and something in Wren's chest decided that *nothing* had gotten too heavy to carry alone.

"There's a door inside the door," she said. "It's shining. Like a window with the sun behind it, except it doesn't hurt to look at. And—" Her voice wobbled. She steadied it the way Mama taught her, slow breath through the nose. "And there's someone standing in your garden, Orsen. He's been there my whole life. He looks like you. Only — stretched. Like your shadow got pulled too long and learned to stand up."

The silence that followed was the longest silence Wren had ever heard. Even the bees seemed to hold their breath.

Then Orsen did something she did not expect. He didn't laugh. He didn't pat her head and tell her she had an imagination like her mother's stew pot, always full. He turned slowly and looked at the empty place at the edge of the garden — the place where the shadow stood — and although Wren knew he couldn't see it, she saw him *try*. Saw him squint against the ordinary afternoon as though the ordinary were a language he'd forgotten he used to speak.

"And this person," he said at last, carefully, "looks like me."

"Like you, but wrong. Like a drawing of you done by somebody who only ever heard you described." Wren swallowed. "He doesn't move when you move. He just stands there. Except today he walked closer, and he's looking at me right now, and Orsen — I think he's sad. I think he's the saddest thing I ever saw."

Orsen's hand tightened on her shoulder. When he spoke again his voice had gone quiet in a new way, quiet the way deep water is quiet.

"Why sad?"

"I don't know." Wren looked at the shadow, really looked, past the terror, the way you look past a snarling dog to see the rope cutting into its neck. The shadow's stretched face had no features, and yet sorrow came off it like rain comes off a roof. "He's looking at the gate like — like it's his house and he's locked out. Or locked in. One of those. And sometimes when you're working, he puts his hands where your hands are. In the dirt. Like he's trying to remember how."

* * *

Orsen walked her home early that day, which he never did, and which told Wren more than anything he said. At the lane's bend she looked back once. He was standing at the gate with his palm flat against the iron, eyes shut, and the second door was glowing so brightly now that it lit the hollows of his face from the front, gold-green, leaf-colored, patient.

And the shadow stood directly behind him, so close that its outline touched his shoulders, and it had raised its long hands and laid them — gently, terribly, the way you'd lay hands on something you loved and could not keep — over the backs of Orsen's own.

Two pairs of hands on one gate. Two shapes wearing one man.

Wren ran the rest of the way home.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep. The moon came through her window in a long silver bar, and she lay in it thinking about doors inside doors and men who were mostly men, until the house creaked its night sounds and her mother's breathing went slow and even through the wall.

Then she heard the humming.

Not the river. The river's hum lived down in the valley bottom. This was closer, thinner, higher — a thread of sound coming from everywhere and nowhere, the sound a wet finger makes on a glass rim, if the glass were the size of the sky. Wren sat up. The silver bar of moonlight on her floor was trembling, rippling like water.

She crept to the window.

Down the lane, at the end of the valley, the gate was visible even from here — a small dark comb of iron against the paler dark of the hills. And rising from it, unwinding upward into the night like smoke from a chimney, was light. Gold-green. Leaf-colored. The second door had opened a crack, and whatever stood behind it was leaning close, drinking in the valley's silence the way a traveler drinks at a well.

And on the lane below her window, passing beneath her like a ship under a bridge, walked the shadow.

Up close it was worse and better than she'd thought. Worse because it was so tall its head passed the eaves of the houses, and better because now, in the moonlight, she could see that it wasn't empty after all. Its stretched silhouette was full of faint moving lights, tiny and numerous, like fireflies caught in cloth, like — like prayers, she thought, though she couldn't have said why, like a thousand small voices all saying *please* at once, worn smooth by carrying.

It stopped beneath her window. It raised its face — featureless, moonlit, endless — and Wren understood with absolute certainty that it could see her, that it had always been able to see her, that of everyone in the valley only she had ever looked back.

It did not speak. Shadows don't. But something pressed against her mind anyway, soft as a moth at glass, and left behind a feeling rather than a word, and the feeling was this:

*I am what he is. I am what he keeps. Do not be afraid of me. Be afraid for him.*

Then it moved on, down the lane, toward the gate, and folded itself into Orsen's sleeping house like a letter slid under a door, and the humming faded, and the moonlight lay still again on Wren's floor.

She stayed at the window until dawn grayed the hills. And when the sun finally rose she made herself a promise, pressing it flat inside her chest next to the pressed flowers, next to all her folded-up knowing:

Whatever was wearing Orsen's shape, whatever waited behind the shining door — it would have to get through her first. She was only seven. She was small as a seed. But seeds, she knew better than anyone in that valley, were the strongest things there were. Everything in the world began as one, and everything in the world, given time and stubbornness and love, could grow around.

Three days later, the village of Alderbrook stopped existing, and Wren was the only one who remembered it had ever been there at all.
