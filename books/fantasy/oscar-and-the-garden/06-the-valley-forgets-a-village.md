## 6. The Valley Forgets a Village

The bread did not come on Tuesday.

Orsen noticed it the way he noticed most things—slowly, and then all at once, like frost creeping across a windowpane. Maren had brought bread every Tuesday for as long as he could remember, which was itself a strange thing to think, because when he tried to pin down *how long*, the memory slid sideways like a fish in shallow water. Ten years? Fifteen? The garden had bloomed out of season for three springs running, or perhaps four, and he had stopped counting.

He stood at his gate—the iron one, the one no one opened—and looked down the road toward Alderbrook, where the smoke of a dozen chimneys should have been rising into the pale morning. There was smoke. That was the first wrongness. The chimneys were smoking, the fields beyond the hamlet were turned and dark with recent plowing, laundry hung on lines between two cottages, snapping gently in a wind that smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Everything about Alderbrook was exactly as it always was.

Except that Orsen could not remember the name of a single person who lived there.

He told himself it was nothing. He was not a man given to alarm; alarm required a certain confidence in one's own importance, and Orsen had never managed to cultivate it, in himself or anything else. He was the keeper of a gate. He kept it. He grew things in the walled garden beside it. People were kind to him, and he was kind back, and the sum of his ambitions had never exceeded that arithmetic.

But the namelessness itched at him all morning while he turned the compost and tied up the bean shoots that had come up in November, fat and green and utterly indifferent to the season. He tried to summon the face of Alderbrook's miller. He knew there was a miller—he could almost see the mill, its wheel turning slow in the brook—but the man's face was a smooth blank, like an egg. The smith. Blank. The woman who sold honey at the crossroads market, who had once given Wren a comb dripping gold and laughed at how the bees followed the child home for half a mile. Blank. Even her laugh was gone, and he had heard it a hundred times.

By afternoon he could not have said what the inside of any house in Alderbrook looked like, though he had sat in half their kitchens eating stew.

* * *

Wren came for him at dusk.

She came the way she always came, at a dead run, braids flying, taking the last ten feet in a leap that would have broken the legs of a less beloved child. But this time she didn't laugh when she landed. She grabbed his sleeve with both hands, and her knuckles were white, and her eyes were too wide, and she said, "Orsen. Orsen, Mama can't remember Tam."

"Tam?"

"The miller's boy." Her voice cracked on the name. "We used to catch frogs together. I know we used to catch frogs together, I can *feel* it, but I can't see his face anymore, and when I asked Mama she said—" Wren swallowed hard. "She said, *who's Tam?*"

The word *who's* went through Orsen like a nail through green wood.

They ran. He was not a fast man—he had the particular slowness of someone built for standing still—but fear lent him speed, and Wren towed him down the road by the hand like a small furious tugboat hauling a barge. The sun was going down behind the western ridge, and the light came long and amber across the plowed fields, and everything looked so ordinary that Orsen wanted to believe, wanted it desperately, that this was some childish misunderstanding, some game gone strange.

Then they reached the well at the center of Alderbrook, and he understood.

There were people there. A dozen of them, maybe more, gathered in the little cobbled square where the market stalls stood on Saturdays. They stood quietly, which was the first horror—not shouting, not weeping, just standing, in the way of people who have forgotten why they came outside. An old man held a bucket rope in both hands and stared down the well shaft without pulling. A young woman stood at her own front door, key in the lock, making no motion to turn it. Two children sat on the church steps, side by side, not playing, not speaking, watching the sky go dark with the patient attention of cats.

"They're still here," Wren whispered. "Why are they still here if nobody remembers them?"

"I don't know," Orsen said, and his voice sounded far away to himself.

Maren found them—or rather, Orsen saw her coming up the road from the valley with Sable beside her, the pilgrim's ragged gray cloak moving like smoke. Maren's face was wet. She had run too, though she was not built for running anymore, and she caught Wren and crushed her against her chest and held on as if the wind might take the girl.

"It's happening here too," Maren said over her daughter's head. "I went to fetch eggs from Hedda's—you know Hedda, the widow with the brown hen—and I got halfway there and realized I couldn't—I couldn't picture her door. I've been to her door a thousand times, Orsen. And then I couldn't remember whether Hedda was even her name. It felt right and wrong at the same time, like a word repeated until it goes strange." She was shaking. "And then I thought: what else? What else is going soft? I tried to count my birthdays and I got to nine and lost the thread. Nine years old. I'm forty-one, Orsen. I know I'm forty-one because I wrote it on the pantry wall when Wren was born, but I couldn't feel it. It was just a number somebody told me."

Sable stood apart from them, at the edge of the square, and the pilgrim's hooded head turned slowly, taking in the blank-faced villagers, the smoking chimneys, the laundry gone stiff and cold on its line. When she spoke, her voice had none of the wildness it carried when she spoke of gods. It was quiet and very tired, the voice of someone describing a thing they had seen before and hoped never to see again.

"This is how it feeds."

* * *

They gathered in the church, because it was the only building large enough and because Maren needed walls around her, and Sable stood in the pulpit like a woman giving a sermon she had preached in a hundred ruined towns. Candles burned on the altar—ordinary candles, burning ordinarily, because this was the valley and magic did not enter here. Orsen clung to that. The candles burned straight and calm. Whatever was happening, it was not *that*. Not yet.

"Alderbrook sits at the edge of the sealed land," Sable said. "You feel it, don't you? The valley is a bowl of quiet. Prayers fall flat here. Magic pools away from it like water off wax. You've lived your whole lives inside that hush and called it peace." She pulled back her hood. In the candlelight her face was gaunt, seamed, older than Orsen had guessed. "But the hush isn't empty. It's a held breath. And something on the other side of it has started breathing out."

"The god," Orsen said. His mouth was dry.

"The devourer. The eater of prayers." Sable nodded. "It woke hungry. And a thing that eats prayers, when prayers are thin, will eat whatever stands next to them in the soul. Memory. Memory is only a prayer you say to yourself. *Remember me. Remember this. Let it have mattered.*" She spread her hands. "It starts at the edges of sealed places because the seal is thinnest there. It licks along the boundary like fire along the hem of a coat. It takes the people furthest from the lock first."

"And the houses stay?" Orsen asked. "The fields, the smoke—everything stays, but the people go out of our heads?"

"Not out of your heads." Sable's voice dropped. "Out of the world. Look at them." She gestured through the window at the square, where the villagers still stood in the blue dusk, unmoving, untroubling each other. "They haven't vanished. They're being *unwritten*. Their bodies remain because bodies are cheap. But everything that made them persons—their names, their mothers' faces, the songs they knew, the debts they owed, the way Tam laughed when a frog jumped on him—all of that is being eaten. Carried off. Chewed into fuel." She paused. "When it finishes with Alderbrook, there will be a village full of walking shapes, and tomorrow no one will be able to say why the village is there, and the day after, the question won't even occur to anyone. The valley will simply have always had an empty hamlet in it. The forgetting will rewrite itself backward until it looks like it was always so."

Wren made a small sound against Maren's shoulder.

"But we remember them now," Maren said fiercely. "Right now, tonight, we remember. Tam. Hedda. The honey-seller at the crossroads—Orsen, tell me you remember her, tell me her name—"

And Orsen reached for it, reached with everything he had, and found—

Nothing. A shape where a person should be. A stall, a jar of honey, a child's sticky fingers, and no face above it, no name, only a hole wearing the outline of a woman.

"No," he whispered. "No, I had it this morning. I could almost—"

"That's how it works," Sable said. "It doesn't tear. It erodes. You'll spend days reaching for someone and finding them just past your fingertips, and then one morning you'll reach and there will be smooth stone where they were, and you won't grieve, because grief needs a shape to hold, and they'll have taken the shape with them." She looked at each of them in turn. "That's the cruelty of it. It doesn't steal your loved ones. It steals your ability to miss them."

The silence in the church was total. Somewhere outside, a villager began to walk—slowly, aimlessly, past the well and back again, past the well and back again, tracing a circuit with no origin.

"We fight it," Maren said at last, and her voice was the voice she used when a storm threatened the harvest, flat and stubborn and immovable. "How do we fight it?"

"You write," Sable said. "You speak names aloud. You carve them. Memory resists being eaten the way anything resists being eaten—by being tough, fibrous, full of gristle and argument. A name written on a doorframe is harder to swallow than a name floating loose in a head. Gather every soul in this valley tonight and make them say the people of Alderbrook out loud, over and over, until the names wear grooves in the air. It will slow it. Slowing is not stopping." She shook her head. "The only stopping is the door. The gate. The thing it's actually trying to reach."

All of them looked at Orsen. He felt the look land on him like a physical weight, and beneath it, faint as a pulse in the wrist, he felt the other thing—the hum. Three nights since the rain, and the iron gate still murmured under his palm when he touched it, a vibration like a struck bell too low to hear, felt only in the bones. He had taken to avoiding touching it. He took to avoiding thinking about why.

"It's eating its way toward me," he said. It was not a question.

"It's eating its way toward the seal," Sable corrected softly. "Whether you are what I think you are—that's a conversation for a darker night than this. But yes. The valley is a shell, and Alderbrook is the outermost layer of the shell, and it has begun to crack it open with its teeth." She stepped down from the pulpit. "Pray it stays hungry for villages a long time. Because when it reaches the middle of the quiet—when it reaches the gate and whatever holds it shut—it will not nibble."

* * *

They worked through the night.

Maren went door to door in the valley proper, hammering on shutters, dragging people out of bed, and by midnight the chapel in their own village was full—full of farmers and grandmothers and sullen teenagers and babies, full of candlelight and the smell of tallow, and Maren stood before them all with Wren's slate in her hands and made them do the thing no one wanted to do and everyone, in their bones, knew they must.

"Say them," she commanded. "Say the names of Alderbrook. Say them loud enough to be written somewhere the dark can't reach."

And they did. Haltingly at first, then with growing desperation, because every name spoken aloud seemed to summon another from the deep water of memory—a wife recalled a husband's fishing partner, a boy recalled the man who'd taught him to whistle through cupped hands, an old woman wept suddenly and said *Bettine, Bettine who made the funeral pies, oh, I'd forgotten Bettine and she fed me through my worst winter*—and the names piled up on the slate and in the air and in the grooves of throats, and Orsen stood at the back of the chapel and added his voice to theirs and felt each name go down like a stone into a well he could not see the bottom of.

But even as they spoke, he watched the holes widen.

The honey-seller never came back. He said *the honey-woman, the honey-woman of the crossroads* forty times, and the words stayed words, hollow, referring to nothing. By dawn, half the names on the slate belonged to faces no one could find anymore. By dawn, the list had become a memorial for people who were still, at that moment, standing alive in their village square, breathing, blinking, waiting for nothing.

Near sunrise, Wren fell asleep against his side in the chapel pew, her slate clutched to her chest, the chalk worn down to a nub. On the slate, in her careful child's letters, she had copied the names over and over, filling every inch, and then, when she'd run out of room, she had begun writing them smaller, and then smaller, cramming them into the margins, defending every scrap of space the way children defend things—with absolute, unreasonable ferocity.

Orsen looked at the sleeping child, and at Maren asleep sitting up across the aisle, chalk dust on her cheek, and he understood at last what Sable had meant about stakes. He had spent his life believing the gate protected an abstraction—the valley, the quiet, the way of things. But the god was not eating abstractions. It was eating Tam's laugh. It was eating Bettine's funeral pies. It was working its way inward, meal by meal, toward a walled garden and a woman who baked bread on Tuesdays and a girl who followed him everywhere like a shadow, and it would not stop, and it could not be reasoned with, and the only thing standing in its path was a gate that hummed under his hand like something waking.

He walked out into the gray dawn alone and went to the gate.

He put his palm flat against the iron, and the hum rose to meet him, stronger than yesterday, and this time—whether the night had stripped away his caution or the iron had grown bolder—he felt it answer him. Not like a bell. Like a throat clearing. Like something about to speak.

And beneath his feet, faint but unmistakable, the garden shifted in its sleep, and every flower in it turned, very slightly, toward Alderbrook.
