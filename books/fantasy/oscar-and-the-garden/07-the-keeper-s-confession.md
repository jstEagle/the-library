## 7. The Keeper's Confession

Maren had baked the same bread in the same oven for thirty-one years, and she knew—she *knew*—when something was wrong with a loaf before she ever cut it open. The crust would sit wrong on her tongue. The crumb would pull apart along a line she hadn't scored. So it was with the valley now. Something had gone wrong beneath the surface of things, and everyone could taste it, even if they couldn't name it.

She named it at four in the morning, standing barefoot in her kitchen with a letter in her hands.

The letter had lived inside the false bottom of her grandmother's flour chest for as long as Maren could remember keeping secrets, which was to say: since the night she'd carried Orsen Lockridge's predecessor down from the gate, weeping and raving about what he'd done, and he had pressed this folded paper into her hands with fingers like winter branches and said, *When he asks—and he will ask, they always ask—you give him this.*

He had never asked. Not once in thirty years. And so the letter had sat in the dark of the flour chest through Wren's birth, through her husband Tomas's long illness and quiet death, through every ordinary season of an extraordinary lie, and Maren had told herself each year that silence was a kind of mercy, that some doors were better left shut, that she was protecting him—

*You were protecting yourself,* said the small voice that lived behind her breastbone, the one that sounded unpleasantly like her mother.

The bread was ruined. She'd known it when she pulled it from the oven—the crust too dark, the smell of char where there should have been warmth. She'd thrown the whole loaf to the hens, who refused it too, which was its own small horror. Even the chickens knew.

Maren set the letter on the table and lit a candle, because some things deserved light, and because her hands needed something to do besides shake.

She had found him, all those years ago. That was the part no one knew—not even Sable with her sharp pilgrim's eyes, not even Orsen himself. The old keeper, Aldous, had come stumbling down from the gate one October night soaked through with rain that smelled of iron and old stone, and he had gripped Maren's arms hard enough to bruise and said words she had spent three decades trying to forget:

*I put him back together wrong. I was meant to let him go and I couldn't. He is not the keeper. He is the lock.*

And then he had died. Not gently, not in his bed—simply stopped, mid-sentence almost, between one breath and the next, as if whatever had been holding him upright had finally set down its burden. She had buried him herself behind the chapel, and told the valley he'd gone to live with cousins in the lowlands, and taken the letter home, and hidden it, and lied.

Because the alternative had been unthinkable. Because the letter, when she'd finally read it by candlelight with her heart hammering like a trapped bird, had not explained anything. It had only made demands.

*Do not tell him what he is.* That was the first line, in Aldous's cramped, failing hand. *A lock that knows it is a lock begins to wonder about doors. A lock that wonders begins to turn. Let him be a keeper. Keepers tend gardens. Locks get opened.*

She had been twenty-three years old, newly widowed in everything but fact, holding a letter that asked her to build a man out of kindness and concealment, and she had looked at the young stranger up at the gate—this gentle, lost soul who watered flowers that bloomed against every law of the season, who spoke to bees as if they might answer, who had appeared out of nowhere with no past and no name and no questions about either—and she had made her choice.

She chose him. She chose the family they would become. She chose thirty years of bread delivered over the wall, of Wren learning to walk in his garden, of festivals where he stood at the edge smiling his quiet smile, and all of it built on a foundation she alone knew was hollow.

*Family over duty,* Aldous had called it, in the last thing he ever wrote, and she had never been sure whether it was forgiveness or accusation.

Now the garden was growing memories. Now a hamlet full of people had been unwritten while their houses still stood with supper going cold on tables. Now a ragged pilgrim talked about gods waking and doors unheld, and Orsen walked around with a sigil on his wrist he thought was a birthmark, and the gate hummed under his palm like a struck bell, and Maren lay awake at night doing arithmetic she couldn't finish: how much time did a lie have left, when the truth started growing out of the ground?

Wren came down the stairs just past dawn, hair wild, dragging her blanket, and stopped dead when she saw her mother sitting in the dark with an unlit candle and a yellowed letter.

"You're crying," Wren said. Nine years old, and missed nothing.

"I'm not."

"You're doing the thing where you're not." Wren climbed onto the chair across from her, tucked her feet under her, and studied the letter with the ferocious attention she usually gave to beetles. "Is that why the bread was bad? I heard you throw it out. The hens wouldn't eat it either. That's an omen, probably. Orsen says chickens know things."

"Orsen says a lot of things." Maren wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Go wake him, would you? Tell him I'm bringing breakfast to the garden today. Tell him—" Her voice caught. She tried again. "Tell him I have something for him. Something I should have given him a long time ago."

Wren looked at her for a long moment, and Maren saw her own stubbornness looking back out of her daughter's face, and hated and loved it equally.

"You're going to tell him," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"Good." And Wren slid off the chair, wrapped her blanket around her shoulders like a cloak, and marched out into the grey morning toward the gate, a nine-year-old herald carrying news of a kingdom's ending.

* * *

The garden was worse.

Maren saw it from the lane and her steps slowed without her permission. She had watched that walled plot for thirty years—through droughts and floods, through the year Tomas died when Orsen had grown flowers the color of grief without knowing why—and she had learned its moods the way you learn a friend's face. This morning it was blooming shapes again, but faster now, greedier, as if the plants had discovered language and couldn't stop speaking.

There was a crown of white foxglove near the east wall, petals arranged in points no gardener's hand had trained. There was a spiral of black-eyed susans that hurt to look at directly, coiling inward like something falling. And near the gate itself, half-hidden under the rose arbor, a patch of pale blue flowers had opened into what was unmistakably a woman's face—eyes closed, mouth soft, as if sleeping, or as if singing something very far away.

Orsen stood among them with his back to her, and even from twenty paces Maren could see how carefully he was holding himself, the way a man holds himself when the ground has started arguing with his feet.

Wren was already there, perched on the low wall, narrating. "—and the blue ones are new since yesterday, and they smell like rain but not our rain, and Uncle Orsen won't touch them, look, he keeps his hands behind his back like they're—"

"Wren." Maren's voice came out steadier than she felt. "Inside. Please. There's honey cake in the crock."

Her daughter gave her a look that contained an entire argument, then slid off the wall and went, though Maren felt those bright eyes on her back the whole way across the yard, cataloguing, remembering. Wren forgot nothing. It was going to be both her gift and her burden, Maren suspected, and there was nothing to be done about either.

Then she was alone with Orsen, and the letter felt like a coal in her apron pocket.

"Maren." He turned, and smiled, and the smile broke her heart, because it was the same smile he'd worn for thirty years, the one that said *whatever you've brought me, whatever you know, I trust you*, and she was about to spend that trust like a fortune she'd stolen. "Your bread last week had caraway in it. I've been thinking about it all morning. Isn't that strange? I keep thinking about small things lately. Like my mind is trying to hold on to them."

"Orsen—"

"The garden's doing it again." He gestured at the foxglove crown, and his hand wasn't quite steady. "Sable thinks the flowers are memories. Not mine. Someone else's. She says—" He stopped. He looked at the blue face under the roses, and something moved behind his eyes, some grief arriving ahead of its own explanation. "She says I should ask myself whose they are. But I don't remember anyone. That's the trouble, Maren. I don't remember anyone at all, before the gate. You found me here. That's the whole story. You found me here."

It was the opening she'd waited thirty years for, and now that it stood before her she wanted to bolt the door against it.

"No," Maren said. "That isn't the whole story."

The words came out of her like teeth. She made herself keep talking.

"Aldous didn't go to the lowlands. There were no cousins. He came down from this gate one night in the rain, and he was dying, and I buried him behind the chapel, and I lied to everyone including you, and I have lied every day since, and I am so sorry, Orsen. I am sorry in my bones."

He had gone very still. The garden seemed to lean in around them; even the wind held off.

"He left a letter," Maren said. "For you. He said you'd ask, someday. You never asked. I told myself that meant you didn't want to know. I told myself a great many things." Her hand shook as she drew the letter from her pocket—yellowed, soft-cornered, sealed with wax that had cracked along its fold from decades of being taken out and put back. "I read it. I shouldn't have, but I was young and frightened and he was dead and you were standing up at that gate looking like the loneliest thing I'd ever seen, and I wanted—I wanted the family more than I wanted the truth. His truth. Anyone's."

She held it out.

Orsen didn't take it at first. He looked at her with an expression she had never seen on his face in thirty years of friendship, and she understood, with a lurch, that this was what betrayal looked like from the outside: not anger, not yet, just a man recalculating everything he knew.

"Thirty years," he said quietly.

"Thirty years."

"You knew. All this time. Whatever's happening to me—to the valley—you knew there was something to know."

"I knew what Aldous feared. I didn't know what was true." She pushed the letter toward him. "Read it. Then hate me, if you have to. But read it first."

He took it. His fingers, those careful gardener's fingers that had planted and pruned and steadied her through Tomas's dying, trembled as he broke the ancient seal.

Maren watched him read. She watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out. She watched him reach the line—she knew exactly which line, she had read it a thousand times in the dark—*He is not the keeper. He is the lock*—and she watched his knees soften and caught his arm and steered him onto the wall bench before he followed the flowers down into the soil.

"Lock," he said. His voice was strange, flattened, as if spoken from very far away. "He wrote—Maren, he wrote that I'm not a man. That I was *made*. That the prince—" He stopped. Started again. "There was a prince. Of somewhere. And they unmade him and made me out of what was left, and Aldous was supposed to—to let me go, to let the seal settle, and instead he taught me to garden."

"He loved you," Maren said. "However badly. However wrongly. He came down that mountain intending to end it, and he looked at you, and he couldn't. Same as me." Her laugh came out cracked. "Seems to be a sickness in this valley. Loving things we ought to let go."

Orsen stared at the letter. Beyond him, the blue flower-face had opened fully now, and in the flat morning light Maren could see it clearly for the first time: not sleeping. Singing. The petals around the mouth were arranged like the last note of a song, and the whole bloom trembled faintly, as if with a voice too distant to hear.

"What happens," Orsen said, "to a lock that knows it's a lock?"

Maren remembered Aldous's warning, written in a dying man's hand: *a lock that knows begins to wonder about doors. A lock that wonders begins to turn.*

"I don't know," she said honestly. "But I think we're about to find out."

And up the valley road, dust rising, came Sable at a run—shouting something Maren couldn't yet make out, something about the gate, something about the sigil on Orsen's wrist beginning to glow.
