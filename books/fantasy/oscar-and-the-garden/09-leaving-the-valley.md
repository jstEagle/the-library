## 9. Leaving the Valley

They waited for moonset the way mourners wait for a funeral to end—because there was nothing else to do, and because leaving was its own kind of grief.

Orsen spent his last hour in the garden. He had told himself he would not, that sentiment was a luxury they could not afford with Alderbrook's empty houses still standing in the next valley like a question no one dared answer aloud. But his feet carried him there anyway, through the dew-wet grass, past the wall where the flowers had grown into shapes he could no longer bear to look at directly—the crown of woven hawthorn, the woman's face turned up in a foxglove bloom as if waiting to be recognized.

He knelt among the beds and did not pray. Prayer was a door now, and doors were dangerous things. Instead he simply touched the soil, working it between his fingers the way he had every morning for twenty years, and let himself feel the enormity of what he was about to lose: not the garden itself, but the certainty of it. The simple, unexamined truth that this dirt was *his* dirt, these rows *his* rows, this life a thing that had simply always been.

By tomorrow, half of it might be ash.

Sable found him there. She moved quietly for a pilgrim; her ragged cloak made almost no sound against the bean rows, and when she crouched beside him at the edge of the lettuce bed she did it with the careful economy of someone who had learned long ago not to startle people who might be holding knives.

"You'll want to take cuttings," she said.

He looked at her. In the pre-dawn grey her face was all angles and shadow, the tattoo of the gate's sigil visible on his own wrist only because he'd stopped wearing his sleeve rolled down—he wanted to see it now, wanted to know his enemy by sight, even if the enemy was apparently his own skin.

"Cuttings," he repeated.

"From the garden." She nodded toward the gate, iron-black against the paling sky. "It's not just a lock, Orsen. It's a root system. Everything growing inside that wall grew from something on the other side. If you're going to walk into a country that eats memory, you'll want living things from home. Things that remember *for* you."

The idea settled into him strangely, half comfort and half horror. A garden that remembered so he didn't have to. He thought of Wren, who followed him everywhere, who had seen the second door shimmering inside the iron one and behind him a tall shadow wearing his silhouette—and who had hugged his leg anyway, pressing her face into his apron as if the shadow were just another part of him to be loved.

"Will it hurt?" he asked. It was the question he hadn't asked anyone yet, the one sitting under his ribs like a stone. "Crossing over. Sable—you've been out there. You know what the magic does."

She was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had lost its pilgrim's cadence, gone flat and honest.

"I've seen men cross thresholds and come out the other side weeping for mothers they never had. I've seen them forget their own names mid-stride and keep walking anyway, because the body remembers the road even when the soul doesn't." She met his eyes. "For you it will be worse. You were built in that valley, Orsen. Every memory you have was grown there like a graft. When the real world touches you—" She spread her hands. "Some of it will burn."

"And some won't?"

"Some won't." Her mouth twisted. "That's the cruel part. You don't get to choose which."

* * *

Maren came at the last light of the stars, carrying bread wrapped in cloth and a lantern she did not light. Wren trailed her, half-asleep, wrapped in a blanket too big for her, her small hand gripping her mother's skirt with the fierce unconscious grip of a child who has understood, in whatever way children understand such things, that tonight is not an ordinary night.

They ate standing up, in silence, at the edge of the garden. The bread was still warm. Orsen ate his portion slowly, tasting each bite as if it might be the last time the taste meant anything—as if, on the other side of the gate, the memory of Maren's brown loaf might dissolve along with everything else, and he would spend the rest of his life eating bread without knowing why it made him want to weep.

"I should have told you," Maren said suddenly. It was not the first time she had said it since the night of the letter. She said it the way she kneaded dough—over and over, working at something that wouldn't smooth. "Twenty years ago. I should have put the letter in your hand and said, 'This is who you are, decide for yourself.' I chose instead to give you a life. And now I'm giving you back to the world that made you, and I don't know which of those things was the greater cruelty."

Orsen swallowed. "You gave me twenty years," he said. "A table. A name. Someone to bring bread to." He looked down at his hands—keeper's hands, gardener's hands, calloused from work he could no longer be sure he'd ever truly done. "Whatever happens out there, Maren, no one gave me those years but you. Whatever I was before—I became someone in your kitchen. That's not a lie. That can't burn away, because it isn't false. It's just... mine."

Wren stirred against her mother's side. "I'm coming too," she said, in the small stubborn voice she used for declarations she intended to win.

"We've been through this," Maren began.

"She saw the door," Sable said quietly. "Before any of us. Children see true, out here where the seal is thinning. And if the devourer is rebuilding itself at the edges—if it's eating memory the way it ate Alderbrook—then I'd rather have the one person among us who can see what adults can't." She paused. "Also, she'll follow us anyway. I've watched her. She follows Orsen like a duckling. We'd spend half our strength looking over our shoulders for her."

Maren closed her eyes. Orsen watched her do the arithmetic of a mother's fear, the terrible sum where every answer costs more than she has. Then she opened them, and pulled Wren's blanket tighter, and said, "Then she walks between us. Always between us. And if anything comes out of the dark, she runs to me first, not to him." A pause. "No offense, Orsen."

"None taken," Orsen said, and found, to his surprise, that he almost smiled. "Given what's apparently wearing my silhouette, I think that's wise."

* * *

Moonset came the way Sable had promised it would—not gradually, but all at once, the moon dropping behind the western ridge as though yanked down by a hand, plunging the valley into a darkness so complete that the stars seemed to lean closer.

And the gate woke.

Orsen felt it before he heard it—a hum rising through the ground, through the soles of his boots, up through the bones of his legs, settling somewhere behind his sternum like a second heartbeat. The iron gate, which had not moved in living memory, which he had oiled and tended and tested every morning of his assumed life, shivered in its frame. Frost bloomed across its surface in patterns that were not frost at all but writing, characters forming and dissolving like breath on cold glass.

"It knows," Sable whispered. "It knows its hour. Walk, don't run. And whatever happens on the other side—keep moving. The threshold doesn't like hesitation."

Orsen took the cuttings from the satchel at his hip—one sprig of rosemary, one of the strange silver-flowered vine that had begun climbing the garden wall, a pressed blossom of the foxglove that wore a woman's face. Wren had insisted on the foxglove. He touched each one once, the way a man touches a talisman, and then put his hand flat against the iron.

The gate swung inward without a sound.

Beyond it was not the meadow that should have been there. Beyond it was dark—true dark, the dark of a place that had never known a candle—and a road, pale as bone, unspooling into distance. Cold air poured through the opening, smelling of rain and stone and something older than both, something that made the back of his teeth ache.

"Orsen." Maren's voice, steady as a hearthstone. "Between us. All of us together. Now."

He stepped through.

The crossing was like drowning in reverse. Cold flooded him first—cold like deep water, like winter wells, like the space between stars—and then everything else came with it, all at once, a flood with no order and no mercy. Magic. Real magic, unfiltered by the valley's hush, pouring into him through every pore, and his false memories caught fire.

He went to his knees on the pale road. He heard Maren shout his name, heard Wren cry out, felt hands grab his shoulders—but the burning was everywhere, and in the burning he *saw*.

The morning he'd planted the lettuce beds—that wasn't real. He watched it curl and blacken like paper held to flame, and beneath it was something else: a courtyard of white marble, a boy of perhaps ten memorizing the names of rivers. The day Maren first brought him bread—ash. Beneath it, a hall full of voices singing in a language that made the air shimmer. His twentieth birthday, the cake Maren had baked, the way Wren as an infant had grabbed his finger—burning, burning—but under the ash, a city on a hill, banners the color of dusk, a man kneeling before a crowd and saying *yes, yes, I will be your mortar.*

He gasped. He clawed at the road. Half of him was dying and half of him was being born and he could not tell which half screamed louder.

And then—stillness. Not peace. Just the exhausted quiet after a fire, smoke still rising from the ruins of twenty years.

Hands were on his face. Maren's hands, rough and warm. "Orsen. Look at me. What's left? Tell me what's left."

He opened his eyes. The world swam, then steadied. The pale road. Three faces bent over him, lit by starlight. Behind them, far away, the iron gate stood open, showing the valley beyond like a lit window—home, impossibly intact, impossibly far.

"Maren," he said, and his voice cracked on it, and he nearly wept with relief that the name still meant what it had always meant. "Bread. Your kitchen. Wren asleep on my bench with flour on her nose." He laughed, or sobbed, or both. "That stayed. That stayed."

"What burned?" Sable asked, and her voice was gentle in a way he hadn't heard from her before.

He searched himself the way a man searches a house after a storm, cataloguing what survived. "My father teaching me to graft apples." Gone—he could feel the shape of the hole where it had been, smooth-sided, clean. "The first winter here. The fever I had at thirty. My—" He stopped. Tried again. "There was a woman. I think there was a woman I almost—" But even as he reached for it, it slipped, and he understood with a lurch that it had already burned, that he was mourning a person whose name he had lost in the crossing, and that this was only the beginning of the accounting.

"The garden," he whispered. "Do I still remember the garden?"

"Every row," Wren said fiercely, gripping his hand with both of hers. "You know every row. You told me the beans sulk if you sing to them and the roses get vain. That's still there. I checked."

He looked at her—at this small, grave child who had inventoried his memories without being asked—and something in his chest that had been tearing all night finally, quietly, held.

He got up. His legs shook. The road stretched ahead of them, pale and patient, running toward a horizon where no mountains stood where mountains should be, where the stars overhead formed constellations he did not recognize and, he suspected, never would—constellations that had been unwritten from every map and memory, hanging above a country that had paid for his prison with its own existence.

Vessany. His country. His people, ground into the mortar of the seal. Somewhere ahead lay their drowned capital, and beneath it, the First Door.

Sable shouldered her pack. Maren took Wren's hand. The gate behind them, sensing their departure, began slowly—reluctantly—to swing shut, and Orsen did not watch it close. He found he couldn't. Some part of him was still standing in that garden, and he could not bear to see the window go dark.

They walked. The road accepted them the way water accepts a stone—with a soft closing sound, and then no trace at all.

They had been walking perhaps an hour when Wren stopped.

She was staring off the road, into the dark, at something none of the rest of them could see. Her face in the starlight was very pale, and very calm, the calm of a child deciding whether to tell the grown-ups something important.

"There are people in the fields," she said. "Lots of them. They're all facing us." A pause. "One of them is waving."
