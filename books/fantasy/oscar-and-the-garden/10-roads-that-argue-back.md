## 10. Roads That Argue Back

Sable had walked a hundred pilgrim roads in her life, and she had learned to read them the way sailors read the sea. A road that forked too often was lying. A road that ran straight through good grazing land was greedy. A road that hummed under the boots was either blessed or hungry, and the trick—the whole trick of her long, ragged, penitent trade—was knowing which before you committed your weight to it.

The road out of the valley's shadow was all three at once.

She walked at the front of their small procession because that was where she belonged, and because it kept her face turned away from the others. Behind her came Orsen Lockridge, moving like a man whose own body had been returned to him with some pieces missing. Half his false memories had burned away at the threshold—she had watched him stagger in the moonset light, clutching his skull as if something inside were being pried loose—and what remained was a leaner, stranger man. He kept touching things. Fence posts that weren't there. Air. His own wrist, where the sigil sat faint as watermarks on old paper.

Behind him, Maren carried Wren on her hip when the girl allowed it, which was less and less often now. The child walked with her chin up and her eyes everywhere, cataloguing a country that should not exist.

Because it did not exist. That was the first thing Sable needed them to understand, and she did not know how to say it without saying everything else.

Vessany stretched before them under a sky the color of pewter gone soft—hills folded in green and gold, stone walls dividing fields, an orchard heavy with fruit no one would ever pick. It looked inhabited. It looked *lived-in*, in the way a stage looks lived-in between performances: chairs set at angles suggesting recent departure, a well with its bucket still dripping, laundry strung between two cottages and dry as bone though no wind moved it. Everything here was a memory of use without the use itself. The country had been unwritten so thoroughly that even its absence had been tidied away, and what remained was this: a beautiful, patient emptiness wearing the shape of home.

"Stay on the stones," Sable said, not turning around. "The grass lies."

"The grass *lies*," Maren repeated flatly. "Sable, I have walked behind you for two days, and every hour you say something worse and then refuse to explain it."

"I explain what keeps you alive. The rest is confession, and I'm saving mine."

"You're rationing it, you mean."

That too. Sable adjusted the strap of her pack and said nothing, and felt Maren's stare settle between her shoulder blades like a hand.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a roadside shrine—one of dozens they'd passed, little stone alcoves built into the mile-markers, each one housing a carved figure worn faceless by weather or by something less patient than weather. Sable chose the shrine deliberately. Whatever else was true about her, and almost everything was, she knew how these places breathed.

Orsen sat apart, turning a sprig of rosemary over in his fingers. He'd pulled it from his coat three times since the gate; it was the only thing he'd carried across that he could not account for, and she suspected it was the only thing from the garden he'd let himself take. Wren was asleep against Maren's side, but her eyes were open a slit. The girl slept like a cat in a strange house. Sable respected that.

The whispering started at full dark.

It came from the shrine's carved figure—from all of them, probably, all along the road, a chorus of faceless mouths murmuring just below the threshold of words. To Orsen it would sound like wind. To Maren, like a stream. But Sable heard it plainly, because she had been listening to it for eleven years, and tonight it was saying her name.

*Sable. Sable, who walks for the Unchained. Sable, who has led the lock to the door.*

She got up and walked to the far side of the shrine and put her back to the stone and pressed her palms against her ears until the whisper dulled to a pulse.

When she came back, Orsen was watching her.

"You heard it," he said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing—*understanding*. "You flinched before I did."

"Roads talk in Vessany," she said. "Shrines talk louder. Best habit is to let them."

"What were they saying?"

"Nothing worth carrying." She banked the fire with her boot. "Sleep. We move at gray light. The roads rearrange after dark, and I'd rather not be standing on one when it does."

He didn't sleep. She could tell by his breathing, by the small sounds of him turning the rosemary sprig, by the way the firelight caught his eyes whenever she checked. And near midnight, when the whispering swelled into something almost like singing, he stood and walked to the edge of the shrine and looked down the road they'd come by—and found, as she'd known he would, that there was no road there anymore. Only meadow, unbroken, going on to the horizon as if no traveler had ever passed.

"It moved," he said quietly. "The road we walked today. It's just... gone."

"Vessany doesn't want us finding anything," Sable said. "Not the capital. Not the drowned palace. Certainly not the Door. The country was killed to build the prison, Orsen. Every hill and hedge here is mortar in the seal. You're walking through the inside of your own lock, and the lock knows what you've come to do."

He was silent a long moment. Then: "And you? What do you know about what I've come to do?"

The question hung there, gentle as a snare. Sable looked at the fire and thought about lying, and found—to her genuine surprise—that she was tired of it. Eleven years tired.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Tomorrow I'll tell you everything, and you'll want to leave me on the road, and I'll deserve it. But you should hear it rested, and fed, and with the child asleep. Some truths are kinder told in daylight."

"And some are kinder never told at all," Maren said from the shadows, "and you're not the one who gets to decide which kind this is."

"No," Sable agreed. "I'm not. Sleep, Maren."

* * *

Gray light found them on a road that had, overnight, decided to argue.

Sable saw it happen twice before noon. They would walk a stretch of lane between hedges, ordinary as any lane anywhere, and then the party would crest a rise and the road behind them would be a riverbed, or a plowed field, or simply a wall of hawthorn grown thick as brickwork. Ahead, the way branched constantly—not at honest crossroads but at odd, indecisive junctions, forks splitting into forks, paths peeling off toward horizons that shifted when you weren't looking directly at them. Once, Wren pointed back and said, very calmly, "There were three hills there yesterday," and there were four now, and one of them had a ruined tower on it that none of them remembered passing.

"The country isn't trying to kill us," Sable explained, when Maren demanded explanation for the fourth time. "Killing us would be simple, and nothing in Vessany is permitted to be simple. It's trying to keep us *lost*. If we wander long enough, we'll forget why we came. That's how it eats here—not flesh. Purpose. You'll wake up one morning remembering only that you're traveling somewhere nice, and you'll settle in some empty village, and tend some empty garden, and that will be the end of you. Happens to every pilgrim who comes unprepared. There are villages full of them. They wave at you as you pass. They look happy."

Wren, walking beside her, looked up with those grave dark eyes. "Do you look happy?"

Sable laughed despite herself—a short, cracked sound. "Ask me tomorrow, sprout."

But the shrines whispered all day, and by afternoon the whispers had stopped pretending to be weather. Orsen heard them too now; she could see it in the way his head turned at nothing, the way his hand went to his wrist. The country recognized what he was. The roads argued, the hedges leaned, and the faceless carvings murmured their offers—*rest here, stay here, plant here, forget*—and beneath it all, threaded through everything like wire through bread, the other voice. Her voice's owner. The one she served.

*Tell them,* it said, amused. *Or I will.*

At dusk they sheltered in the ruin of a tollhouse, and Sable decided she had run out of tomorrows.

* * *

She built the fire herself, fed it properly, waited until Wren was genuinely asleep this time—Maren had wrapped the girl in both their cloaks, and the child's breathing had gone deep and even—and then she sat with her back against the tollhouse wall, where they could all see her hands, and began.

"My name isn't Sable. Well—it is now. I gave it to myself on the road, the way pilgrims do. The name I was born with belongs to a girl from the coast of Callowmere who prayed to the wrong god at the right time, and I've spent eleven years making sure she stays dead." She turned her hands palm-up in the firelight. "You know the old gods are waking. You've seen it. Rain that smells older than weather. Gardens blooming memories. Villages eaten down to their houses. Everyone assumes the waking gods are the ones who were sealed away, sleeping, innocent, dreaming until someone opened the door."

Orsen's face had gone very still. "Someone stopped holding the door," he said. "You told us that at the wall."

"I lied about the shape of it. The truth is worse." She took a breath. "There is a god called the Unchained. Not one of the sealed ones—a rival. When the devourer was imprisoned, when Vessany was poured into the seal like molten lead into a mold, the other gods divided the spoils. Worship, territory, the prayers of the living. The Unchained got nothing, because the devourer had been its rival too, and the others thought locking it away was punishment enough. For a thousand years the Unchained has watched the seal hold and hated it. Not because the devourer suffers. Because a locked door is a debt unpaid."

Maren's arm had tightened around Wren. "And you serve it."

"I serve it." The words came out of her like splinters coming out of flesh, one at a time. "Eleven years ago my village was dying of blight. I prayed to every god whose name I knew and got silence—all the old names were thin by then, half-asleep, answering one prayer in ten thousand. And then something answered that wasn't thin at all. It offered me my village's life back. All it asked was that I walk, and watch, and find the lock when the door began to loosen. I said yes. My village died anyway. The god never intended to save it. The offer was just the hook, and I was just the fish, and I have been swimming on that line ever since." She met Orsen's eyes. "My task was to find you. To follow the sigil. To bring the lock to the First Door—because the Unchained believes that if the devourer is freed, the balance breaks, and in the breaking it can take everything the others hoard. It doesn't want you destroyed, Orsen. It wants you *opened*. You're not a man to it. You're a key, and keys exist to be turned."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a shrine whispered, satisfied.

Orsen looked at her for a long time. What frightened Sable was not anger in his face. It was the absence of surprise—the weary, terrible recognition of a man who had already begun to suspect that everyone in his life had been placed there.

"Wren," he said finally. "The drought. The rain. Was that you?"

"No." And here Sable laughed, a broken, astonished sound. "No, that was real. That was the one thing in this whole wretched pilgrimage that nobody arranged. The child prayed and something answered because the seal is failing, and I have been terrified of her ever since. Gods don't answer children by accident anymore, Orsen. Something is *choosing* to hear her."

Maren had not spoken. Now she rose, slow, with Wren still asleep against her, and stood over Sable the way a mountain stands over a valley. "You walked into our valley. You ate our bread. You let that child follow you around like a duckling. And the whole time you were measuring my boy for a keyhole."

"Yes."

"Why tell us now?"

"Because—" Sable stopped. Started again. The truth was a door too, and hers had been rusted shut for eleven years, and it hurt going through. "Because two days ago, on the road, the Unchained spoke to me and told me the trap it has waiting at the Door. And it wasn't a trap for the devourer. It was a trap for the child. The god has decided Wren is useful—heard prayers are a currency, and she's been minting them. I was told to deliver all three of you and consider the girl part of the payment." Her voice frayed. "I have done many ugly things on this road, Maren Winterbourne's ghost, but I find I have reached the end of what I'll carry. So. Here is everything. Here is my treason, laid out where you can see it. Do what you like with me. Leave me for the roads—they're fond of me, they'd keep me lost forever, and I'd deserve it. But don't turn back. The valley won't protect you anymore, and the only way through what's coming is forward, to the Door, ahead of the thing that hunts us."

Silence held the tollhouse. The fire burned down. Out in the dark, the whispering had stopped entirely, which was somehow worse—as if the whole country were leaning in to listen.

It was Wren's voice, small and sleep-roughened, that broke it. The girl had been awake the entire time; Sable realized it with a lurch as the child lifted her head from Maren's shoulder and regarded her with those ancient, unhurried eyes.

"You said the god wanted to open him like a key," Wren said. "Keys get worn down. Keys get *lost*." She yawned, devastatingly, a child's yawn in the middle of the end of the world. "We won't let you lose him. So you'd better walk in front where we can see you."

Orsen made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief. Maren sat back down slowly, arms still locked around her daughter, and stared into the fire, and did not forgive Sable, and did not send her away.

Sable lay down on the cold stone with her pack under her head and did not sleep, and somewhere past midnight she heard it: the whispering returning, but changed. No longer coaxing. No longer offering.

*Traitor,* said the voice of the Unchained, from every faceless mouth along every hidden road, soft as ash falling. *Traitor, traitor. I am already at the Door. I have always been at the Door. Bring me my key, little fish—or I will show you what I do with hooks that slip.*

Far off across the rearranging hills, in the direction none of them had yet traveled, something vast exhaled, and every candle in every empty window of Vessany guttered at once, and the road beneath Sable's cheek began, very gently, to move—carrying them, whether they willed it or not, toward the drowned capital and the Door beneath it, and toward whatever waited there with its hand already on the latch.
