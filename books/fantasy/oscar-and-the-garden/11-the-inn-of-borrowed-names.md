## 11. The Inn of Borrowed Names

Maren smelled the woodsmoke before she saw the building—a good smell, honest, the smell of bread and hearth and someone awake somewhere keeping watch. After three days in the ghost-country of Vessany, where the roads had argued with Sable and lost only grudgingly, and where the shrines at every crossroads had whispered offers Maren refused to let Wren hear, that smell undid her a little. Her eyes stung. She blamed the wind.

"There," Orsen said quietly, pointing.

The inn stood at the bend of a road that hadn't been there an hour ago—or perhaps it had been there all along and had simply decided, finally, to permit them. It was a low, wide building of grey stone, two stories, with a slate roof furred in moss and windows glowing amber against the dusk. A sign swung above the door on iron brackets: no words on it, only a carving of an open hand holding a small flame. The wood was old and silvered with weather, but the hinges were bright with oil, as though someone tended them daily. As though someone had been waiting a very long time for guests.

"It wasn't here yesterday," Sable said flatly. She stood with her wounded arm bound tight to her chest, her face grey with pain and suspicion in equal measure. "I've crossed this stretch twice. There's nothing on this stretch. There has *never* been anything on this stretch."

"Then it's come looking for us," Maren said. "Or we've come looking for it, and neither of us knows which." She adjusted the pack straps on her shoulders and touched Wren's hair. "Either way, my feet have opinions, and they all say yes."

"Mama," Wren said, in the low, careful voice she used when she'd noticed something the adults hadn't, "there are more lights inside than there are windows."

Maren looked. The girl was right. The glow behind the glass didn't sit right—too full, too many sources, like candlelight reflected off moving water. She felt the old familiar prickle between her shoulder blades, the one she'd carried since the night thirty years ago when she'd found a nameless young man standing barefoot before a shut iron gate, holding a letter he couldn't read because he couldn't remember letters.

But she'd learned something since leaving the valley, walking through a country erased from the world: fear was a poor traveling companion, and hospitality was rarer than gold out here. And whatever waited behind that door, Wren was shivering, and Sable's wound needed cleaning, and Orsen had gone quiet in the way that frightened her most—the way he went quiet when the country pressed against his half-burned memories, hunting for what remained.

They knocked.

* * *

The woman who opened the door wore an apron dusted with flour and a face like a river stone—worn smooth, patient, impossible to read. Her eyes were dark and very old and terribly sad, and when they fell on Orsen, something moved in them like fish under ice.

"Travelers," she said. Not a question. Her voice had the cadence of someone remembering how speech worked. "We have room. We always have room."

"You knew we were coming," Sable said from the back, her hand nowhere near her knife because her knife hand was the wounded one, and they both knew it.

"Everyone is coming, eventually." The woman stepped aside. "I am Hesta. I keep this house. Come in out of the dark, please. The dark here isn't empty, and it isn't kind."

Inside, the inn was warm in a way that reached into Maren's bones and loosened knots she'd carried since Alderbrook ceased to exist. A fire burned in a great stone hearth. Long tables gleamed with beeswax polish. Stairs climbed into shadow. And everywhere—this was what Wren had seen—everywhere there were people. A broad-shouldered man hauling a keg up from the cellar. Two women laughing over mending by the second window. An old fellow asleep in a chair by the fire, his boots off, his sock heels worn thin. A boy chasing nothing down the hallway above, his footsteps thumping overhead like a heartbeat.

The inn was full, Maren realized. Full of staff, full of life, full of noise—and entirely without guests.

Hesta followed her gaze. "You see them," she said. It wasn't quite a question either. "Good. Some travelers walk right through us. Those are the worst nights."

"What are you?" Sable asked, blunt as a hammer, because grief had sanded all her tact away years ago.

The flour-dusted hands folded together. "Dead," Hesta said simply. "Mostly. We were Vessany." She said the name the way you'd say the name of a child buried long ago—with love, and care not to crack. "When the seal was made, our country was poured into it like mortar into a wall. Our bodies went first. Then our names went out of the world's mouth. Then even our going was forgotten." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing Maren had seen since the empty houses of Alderbrook. "But a soul is stubborn. What was poured out left residue. We pool in places like this. We keep an inn, because keeping is what we remember how to do. And we trade."

"Trade," Maren repeated.

"Hospitality for remembrance." Hesta turned those old dark eyes on her. "A bed for a night costs one true memory of us, held and kept. A meal costs another. Nothing more. We don't take. We never take. Taking is what the hungry one does." Something flickered across her face at that—fear, old and worn smooth as everything else about her. "We ask. That's all we've ever been able to do. Ask, and hope someone passing remembers to answer."

* * *

They ate in the common room, and it was the finest meal Maren had eaten in her life, and she could barely taste it.

Barley soup thick with root vegetables. Dark bread still steaming. A stew of mushrooms and hare that tasted of thyme and something sweeter she couldn't name. The broad-shouldered man—Brammel, he said, offering his name like a gift he'd wrapped himself—kept her bowl full and watched her eat with an intensity that made her chest ache. The two mending-women brought honey cakes for Wren and sat down uninvited, which Wren, who had strong opinions about strangers, permitted with suspicious grace.

"That's Ysolde and that's Perrin," Wren announced, pointing at the mending-women, around a mouthful of cake. "You told me twice. I'm going to remember them forever."

Ysolde laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and pressed her hand to her mouth. "Oh," she said. "Oh, listen to her. Forever. Do you hear that, Perrin? We've got forever."

"We haven't," Perrin said gently, but she was smiling, and her eyes were wet. "But tonight's a good-sized piece of it."

Maren watched them and understood the shape of the trade completely, and it broke her heart along its oldest fault line. These people weren't ghosts haunting a place. They were people starving—not for food; the soup proved they had craft enough for that—but for the one thing the world had taken from them. To be remembered was to exist. Every name Maren learned was a plank laid back over a gap in the world. Every story she kept was a small resurrection.

So she did what she did best. She put her elbows on the table, refilled her own bowl, and asked questions.

"Brammel. Tell me about your cellar."

He blinked. "My—well. It's my father's cellar really, I just keep it now. He built the racks himself, oak, from trees off the north slope—" He stopped. His voice had gone strange. "No one's asked me that in a hundred years. Maybe five hundred. Time pools strangely here."

"The north slope of what?"

And then he was talking, really talking—about Vessany's northern hills where the barley grew blue-green in spring, about the harvest festival where they floated candles down the River Serle and the whole valley glowed like spilled stars, about his father who sang badly and his mother who pretended otherwise. Maren listened the way she listened to Wren's long rambling accounts of beetles and cloud-shapes: with her whole body, storing it, making it hers. When he finished, breathless, blinking, she said, "Blue-green barley and bad singing. I'll keep that. You have my word."

Brammel set down the ladle. He looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned away quickly, busying himself with the fire, and his shoulders shook once, twice, and she pretended not to see, because some kindnesses are kinder unseen.

Across the room, Orsen sat very still by the hearth, and the old man in the thin-heeled socks had woken and drawn his chair close, and the two of them were speaking in low voices. Maren caught fragments. *You were a gardener? No—a keeper. No—* And Orsen's face, lit sideways by the fire, wearing an expression she'd never seen on him: a man listening to a stranger describe a house he half-remembered living in.

* * *

Later, when Wren had been bundled upstairs into a bed that smelled of lavender and clean wool, Maren came back down expecting to sleep herself. Instead she found Hesta waiting with a candle and two chairs pulled up to the hearth, and a look on her face like a woman preparing for confession.

"You'll want to know what your man was asking me," Hesta said.

"He's not my man," Maren said automatically, and then, because lying to the dead seemed pointless, "but yes. He's mine to worry about. Family. Chosen."

"Chosen family holds tighter than born family, in my experience. Born family you're stuck with. Chosen family you fight for." Hesta settled into her chair. "Your Orsen asked me about Prince Aurel."

Maren's stomach dropped. Since the well—since the water had shown them all a young man's face wearing Orsen's grief like a crown—he had spoken of Aurel only once, briefly, in a voice stripped of everything. *He volunteered,* he'd said. *They didn't take him. He gave himself.* And then he'd walked all night and wouldn't say why that made it worse.

"I know the story as far as anyone does," Maren said carefully. "The prince who let himself be unmade so his country could be woven into the seal."

"That's the version carved in stone, and stones get things wrong." Hesta leaned forward. "I was a kitchen girl at the palace, before. I saw him the week before it happened. He wasn't solemn like a martyr, dear. He was terrified. He cried into his supper like a boy. And he kept saying—" her voice frayed— "he kept saying, *someone has to hold the door and everyone else has someone. I have no one. It should be the one who won't be missed.*"

Maren thought of a nameless young man at a gate, thirty years ago, holding a letter. Thought of a garden that bloomed out of season because its keeper had no seasons of his own. Thought of a child named Wren who had attached herself to that man like a shadow, and a baker widow who'd fed him bread until he learned to want things.

"He was wrong," Maren said. Her voice came out harder than she intended. "About being the one who wouldn't be missed."

"Yes," Hesta said softly. "It took him thirty years and four people to be wrong. That's rather the miracle, isn't it?"

The fire popped. Upstairs, faintly, Wren murmured in her sleep.

"Now," Hesta said, and her manner changed, became brisk, businesslike, almost embarrassed. "The trade. For the beds and the meal and what I've told you tonight, we ask remembrance. But there's a custom, if you'll indulge an old dead woman. We tell you our names properly—one by one, with a thing attached to each, a true thing, so the name has weight to carry. And you keep them. All night if you can. Sleep on them. Names kept through a night of sleep take deeper root than names merely heard." She smiled ruefully. "We've learned patience. We've had practice."

"How many of you are there?"

"In this house? Forty-one souls, give or take the ones who drift." Hesta rose and fetched a slate board and chalk, old habits asserting themselves. "Forty-one names. Most travelers manage ten or twelve before dawn. One merchant managed twenty-six, and we threw a feast over it." Her eyes glittered. "No one has ever managed all forty-one."

Maren rolled up her sleeves.

* * *

She started at midnight, and she did not stop.

Hesta gave them slowly, ceremonially, each name paired with its truth. *Brammel, who kept his father's cellar.* *Ysolde, whose laughter could startle cats off fences.* *Perrin, who mended sails before she mended clothes, and missed the sea every day of her life.* *Old Corvin, asleep in his chair, who had been the town's bell-ringer and could still name every hour by the weight of silence in a room.* *Tamsin, the flour on Hesta's apron was Tamsin's doing, the girl baked bread in her sleep, or had, once.* *Little Osric, who chased nothing down hallways because the game was the point, not the catching.*

Some were funny. Some were unbearable. *Maret, who proposed to his wife eleven times because she kept saying maybe.* *Liesse, who died at nine and had been waiting all this while for her mother to arrive, and Hesta's voice went gentle and careful on that one, and Maren wrote the name on her heart twice.*

She said them aloud, over and over, pacing the common room like a woman in labor, like a woman in prayer—which perhaps she was. Brammel brought her tea she didn't drink. Ysolde draped a blanket over her shoulders. Sometime past the deepest hour, Orsen came down and sat in the corner without a word, watching her with an expression she couldn't parse—until she realized he was mouthing the names along with her. Learning them too. Sharing the burden without being asked, the way he shared everything: quietly, completely, as if being useful was the only prayer he trusted.

By the time the windows went grey, she had forty-one names and their truths held in her mind like forty-one stones in a garden wall, and she was hoarse, and weeping without drama, the way you weep at weddings, and Hesta stood before her with both hands pressed to her mouth.

"All of them," Hesta whispered. "Forty-one. In one night."

"They're easy to remember," Maren rasped. "That's the trick of it. You made them worth remembering. Five hundred years of being worth it."

Hesta took her hands. The dead woman's grip was cool and firm and human. "When you reach the First Door," she said, "and whatever happens there happens—you will not be alone in it. Forty-one voices will walk beside you. Remembered things have weight, Maren of the valley. Weight enough to lean on. Weight enough, sometimes, to break things."

* * *

It was Wren's shriek that brought them all running up the stairs.

Maren took them two at a time, heart slamming, already composing horrors—but the girl wasn't hurt. She stood in the middle of the little bedroom, pale as milk, pointing at the window.

"There's someone outside," Wren whispered. "Standing in the garden under my window. He says—" she swallowed— "he says he's a god, a *small* one, he was very clear about that, he said it twice. And he says he knows a way past the roads that argue. And he wants to make a bargain." Her chin lifted, and Maren saw with a lurch that her daughter was frightened and fascinated in equal measure, which was always, always the dangerous combination. "And Mama—he's holding out his hand. And he says the price is *the person I'll miss most.*"

Behind Maren, in the doorway, Orsen had gone white as the dawn.
