## 12. Wren Bargains with a God

Wren had learned three things about the ghost-country in the four days since they'd crossed into it, and she kept them folded up inside her like the dried flowers she kept in the pocket of her coat.

The first thing was that the roads lied. Not the way people lied, with their faces going still and their eyes sliding sideways, but the way rivers lied—smoothly, patiently, as though lying were simply what they were for. She had watched the road ahead of them bend twice when nobody was looking. Once it bent to avoid a shrine; once it bent toward one. She hadn't decided yet which was worse.

The second thing was that her mother hummed when she was frightened. Maren thought nobody knew. But Wren had spent seven years learning her mother the way other children learned their letters, and she knew that the low, tuneless humming meant *I am afraid and I will not say so*, and that the bread Maren baked on the little iron plate over their fire those nights always came out burnt on the bottom because her hands shook.

The third thing—the most important thing—was that Orsen's shadow had stopped following him properly.

She'd noticed it two nights ago, by the light of a moon that came and went like a candle someone kept forgetting to tend. Everyone else's shadow lay behind them, obedient, stretched out by the moonlight the way shadows were supposed to be. But Orsen's shadow stood beside him. Upright. The height of a tall man. And when Orsen turned, the shadow did not turn with him—it waited a moment, then followed, the way a dog follows when it has remembered, reluctantly, whose dog it is.

Wren had not told anyone. Some things you told your mother. Some things you told Orsen. And some things you carried in your pocket next to the pressed flowers, because telling them would make them too heavy for anyone else to hold.

* * *

They found the crossroads at dusk on the fourth day, and Wren knew immediately that it was not an ordinary crossroads, because ordinary crossroads did not have doors standing in the middle of them.

There were no houses nearby. No walls for a door to belong to. Just the place where the ghost-road split into three pale branches, and there, planted in the bare earth as casually as a fence post, stood a door. It was small—smaller than the door of a cottage, smaller even than the door of a root cellar—and it was painted a blue so deep it looked less like paint than like a hole cut in the evening sky. It had no frame. It had no hinges that Wren could see. It simply stood there, shut, with a brass knob gone green with age, waiting the way a spider waits: patiently, and in the exact center of things.

"Nobody touch it," Sable said, in the voice she used when she wanted them to think she wasn't afraid. Her voice had been doing a lot of that lately. "Threshold gods are small, but they're *petty*. They collect debts the way magpies collect buttons."

"What kind of debts?" Maren asked. She had moved without seeming to move, and now she stood between the door and Wren, which was silly, because Wren was not the one who needed protecting from a door. Probably.

"Passage," Sable said. "That's what they trade in. A threshold god can open any way between any here and any there. And they'll do it gladly—for a price." She spat, delicately, to the side. "In the old days they took first steps. First words. The name you were almost given instead of the one you got. Small things, but yours."

Orsen said nothing. He had been saying very little since the well—that was another thing Wren carried in her pocket, the memory of his face in the water, the stranger's face wearing his frown—but he looked at the little blue door for a long time, and his hand drifted up to rub the mark on his wrist, the sigil that was not a birthmark, and Wren saw that he was afraid in the quiet way he was always afraid, which was worse than shouting.

"We don't need it," Maren said firmly. "We have legs. Roads go where roads go."

"The roads here go where the country wants them to go," Sable said. "And the country wants us lost. We've walked all day and passed the same crooked stone twice. You want to keep arguing with the ground, be my guest."

It was while the grown-ups argued—grown-ups argued the way weather happened, constantly and without anyone being able to stop it—that Wren stepped closer to the door.

Up close, she could hear it breathing.

Not loudly. Not the way a person breathes. More like the sound a house makes at night, settling, or the sound just before rain. In and out. In and out. And beneath the sound, faint as a fly trapped behind glass, something was humming—or singing, maybe, if singing could be done by something that had never had a throat.

*Hello,* Wren thought at it, because her mother had raised her to be polite even to furniture, and it seemed rude to leave a door un-greeted.

The knob turned.

* * *

"You will not," Maren said, "be speaking to it alone."

"I speak to the chickens alone," Wren said. "And the chickens are meaner."

"That is not reassuring, Wren Lockridge—" Maren caught herself, because Lockridge was not quite right anymore either, none of their names were quite right anymore, out here where names were currency and everyone was broke. She knelt down in the dead grass until her face was level with Wren's, and her hands were steady now, deliberately steady, the way they got when she was spending everything she had. "My girl. Whatever it offers you—and it will offer, they always offer—you come back and tell me before you agree to anything. Promise me."

"It might only talk to children," Wren said. "Thresholds like children. Children are always going through doors."

"That is exactly why I'm worried."

But in the end—because the road had indeed brought them past the crooked stone three times, because Sable's maps unwrote themselves faster than she could read them, because Orsen stood at the edge of the firelight with his shadow standing wrong beside him and said, quietly, "Let her try"—they let her try.

They let her walk out to the little blue door at dusk, alone except for the whole width of the valley of her heart, and sit down in front of it with her knees pulled up, the way she sat in front of the henhouse when a broody hen needed talking down.

"Hello," she said out loud this time. "I know you're in there."

The door breathed. The singing under the breathing got a little louder, the way a shy animal comes closer when you don't look straight at it.

"My name is Wren. I'm seven and three-quarters. I like plums, and stories where the monster turns out to be lonely instead of wicked, and I don't like turnips, which isn't relevant, but I thought you should know the whole truth." She smoothed her skirt over her knees. "We need to get through your crossroads. The road keeps un-remembering where it goes, and my mother's feet hurt, and Sable won't admit hers do too, and Orsen—" she paused, because Orsen was hard to explain, Orsen was the hardest thing in the world to explain and also the easiest, "—Orsen needs to get where he's going before the thing inside him gets there first."

The knob rattled. Softly. Almost hopefully.

"And I expect you want something for it," Wren said. "Sable says threshold gods take payments. So let's hear it, then. I haven't got all night, and neither have you, probably. Do doors sleep?"

The door opened.

Not wide—only a crack, a sliver of dark, and out of the dark came a smell like attics and rain-on-hot-stone and the space underneath stairs, and a voice. The voice was small and dry and old, like paper folding, like a key turning very slowly, and it spoke directly inside her ear although the door was three feet away.

**A bargain, then, small opener-of-doors. Safe passage for you and yours through the shifting ways—one day and one night, unlost, unmolested, the road holding still beneath your feet.**

"Yes," Wren said. "Go on."

**And the price:** the voice said, and it had the particular relish of something that had made this offer ten thousand times and been refused nine thousand and nine hundred of them, **is the person you will miss most.**

Wren went very still.

She was clever—everyone said so, usually in the exasperated tone people use for cleverness they've tripped over—but even if she had been dull as a post she would have understood what the door was asking, because the door said it slowly, savoring it, letting every word sink in like frost into a crack.

**The person you will miss most. When they are gone. Which they will be, sooner or later—everything goes, small opener-of-doors, everything goes through some door eventually. Give me that person. Not their body—I don't want their body, bodies are heavy and dull. Their going. Their absence. I will take the missing-out of you, and you will never ache for them again, never lie awake counting their footsteps, never stand in a doorway listening for their voice. Safe passage, and a heart with one hole fewer. It is a kindness. It is the kindest price I ask.**

The dark in the doorway leaned closer. Somewhere inside it, something like a hand, something like a hook, waited.

"And if I say yes," Wren said carefully, "I'd forget how much I love them?"

**No,** the door said, almost gently. **You would remember them perfectly. You simply would not mind losing them. That is all missing is, after all—love with nowhere to put itself. I am offering to tidy it away.**

Wren looked at the door for a long moment.

Then she reached into her coat pocket.

* * *

Here is what Wren had in her pocket, along with three smooth pebbles, half a plum stone she was saving to plant, and a length of blue thread:

Flowers. Pressed flowers, flat as secrets, from Orsen's garden in the valley. She had taken them the night before they left—not stolen, because she'd left a note, though the note mostly said *i bortowed these sorry* in handwriting that was more apology than alphabet. There was a sprig of rosemary that bloomed in the shape of a small crown, if you squinted. There was a white flower with a dark center that had grown, that terrible week, into the likeness of a woman's face, though the face had faded now to something gentler, something that might have been anyone, might have been everyone. And there was one more—a common thing, a wallflower, yellow-orange as butter, that had pushed itself up through a crack beside the gate. Orsen had almost weeded it. He hadn't. He'd said, "Leave it. Things that grow in cracks are trying harder than the rest."

Wren had pressed it in her mother's biggest book, under the heaviest pot, for exactly the right number of days, and carried it ever since, and touched it every night before sleep, the way other children touched charms against nightmares.

Because here was the fourth thing Wren had learned, the one she'd figured out all by herself, the one the grown-ups were too big to see anymore:

The garden remembered things. Real things. True things. And a flower that grew in a crack beside a magic gate, in a valley where nothing magical was supposed to grow, watered by a boy who was secretly a lock, tended by a man who was secretly a prince—that flower was not just a flower. It was a small true thing from a small true place, and Wren had a suspicion, a great big enormous suspicion, about what small true things did to hungry old powers.

She held it out flat on her palm, in front of the crack in the door.

"This," she said. "Instead."

A silence. The particular silence of an ancient being recalculating.

**Small opener-of-doors,** the voice said at last, dryly, **that is a weed.**

"It's a wallflower."

**It is a pressed weed from a valley of no consequence. I asked for the person you will miss most.**

"I know what you asked for." Wren kept her hand steady. Her knees wanted to shake and she told them, silently, *not now.* "But you made a mistake in your asking, and I'm going to tell you what it was, because my mother says you should always tell people when they're wrong, politely."

The door breathed. Waited.

"You asked for the person I'll miss most. But that's not really what you eat, is it? Sable says the old god eats prayers. And the shrines whisper offers. And the inn people—we met them, the ones who traded being remembered for soup—they said the country eats memories. Everything out here eats the same thing. It's not people. It's not absence." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped, the way it did when she told the chickens a secret. "It's *mattering*. You eat the proof that something mattered. That's why you want my missing—because missing is the loudest kind of mattering there is."

The dark in the doorway had gone very quiet. Even the breathing slowed.

"So here." She turned her palm, offering the pressed wallflower the way you offer a treat to something with teeth. "This flower grew in a crack beside a gate that holds a god in, in dirt that shouldn't grow anything, and a man who didn't know his own name chose not to pull it out. Every bit of it is mattering, pressed flat and kept safe. It's the most genuine thing I own. It's worth more than my missing, and I'll tell you why." She took a breath. "My missing would only feed you once. This flower—if you take it, you'll have to *keep* it. Keep it safe, keep it whole, carry it somewhere behind your hinges forever. And every time you feel it there, you'll remember that a seven-and-three-quarters-year-old girl out-bargained you with a weed. That's not a payment. That's a *gardener*. That's something growing in you whether you like it or not."

Silence.

Longer silence.

And then—Wren felt it rather than heard it—a laugh. A tiny, rusted, astonished laugh, like a gate that hadn't swung in a hundred years remembering how.

**You are a horror,** the door said, with unmistakable delight. **You are a small, sincere horror.**

"Thank you," Wren said. "Take it, then. Bargain's a bargain."

The dark reached out—she felt it, cool as the underside of a stair—and lifted the wallflower from her palm with a touch like a draft under a door. For one heartbeat the pressed flower hung in the crack of the doorway, glowing faintly gold against all that dark, a small stubborn piece of a valley that remembered, held in the mouth of a god.

Then it was gone. Taken in. Kept.

The door sighed—a long, settling, house-at-night sigh—and swung wide.

Beyond it, impossibly, was not darkness but road: their road, the ghost-country's pale way, running straight and steady and *remembering itself*, mile after mile unrolling under a rising moon, every stone in its proper place, every bend honest at last. And along the edges of the road, faint as breath on glass, small lights kindled—lantern-lights, shrine-lights, the lights of thresholds everywhere briefly acknowledging a debt paid in full.

**One day and one night,** the voice said, already sounding drowsy, already sounding, Wren thought, a little different—a little warmer, the way a house is warmer after someone has lived in it. **Unlost, unmolested. The road will hold still for you. Go, small opener-of-doors. And tell the tall one with the wrong shadow—**

Wren froze.

**—tell him the doors are watching him kindly. For now. And tell him a flower grows in me now. Tell him to come see it someday, when he is both of himself.**

The door closed, gently, like a hand tucking in a blanket. The little blue rectangle stood alone in the crossroads, breathing its slow breath, and if you listened very closely—Wren listened—you could hear, underneath, very faint, a new note in the old song. A green note. A growing note.

She ran back to the firelight and collided with her mother, who scooped her up and checked her over twice, front and back, like a hen with one chick.

"Well?" Maren demanded. "What did it take? What did you give it? Wren, *what did you promise?*"

"A flower," Wren said, breathless, glowing. "Mama, I paid a god with a weed and it *liked it*. And Mama—it knows about Orsen's shadow. It said doors are watching him. It said—" she caught her breath, and the last of it came out in a rush, small and cold in the warm firelight, "—it said to tell him to come back when he's *both of himself*, Mama. It said *someday*. What if someday means it thinks he's going to be somebody else first?"

Across the fire, Orsen looked up from the map that wouldn't stay written. His shadow stood beside him, upright and patient, and in the flickering light Wren could have sworn—for just a heartbeat—that it smiled.

Sable was already on her feet, rolling the useless maps tight, her face lit orange and grim.

"Then we'd better hurry," she said. "The drowned capital's three days south, and I don't think we're the only ones racing the tide."
