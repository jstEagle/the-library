## 13. The Prince in the Well

They found the well on the third day of walking through Vessany, at the bottom of a valley that had no name because names, here, were a kind of currency the country could no longer afford.

Orsen saw it first—or thought he did. A circle of stones, half-swallowed by grass gone silver with drought-dust, sitting alone in the fold of the land like an eye that had been crying for centuries and finally stopped. There was no village around it. No roofline, no fence post, not so much as a foundation stone to say that people had ever gathered here. And yet the well itself was too well-made to be alone. Its stones were fitted with a care that spoke of masons who expected their work to be admired, its rim worn smooth in one crescent where generations of hands must have leaned. Wells do not exist without villages. Villages do not leave wells behind when they go.

But this country had not *gone*. That was the thing Orsen was still learning, day by day, mile by mile, the way you learn the shape of a stone in your boot—slowly at first as an irritation, then all at once as a wound. Vessany had not fallen. It had been *unmade*, unwritten, subtracted from the ledger of the world with such thoroughness that even grief had been taken from it. There was no one left to mourn it. Mourning requires memory, and memory had been the mortar.

He said none of this aloud. He had learned on the roads that Sable watched him when he spoke of Vessany, and that Maren watched him harder, and that Wren—Wren simply took his hand, which was worse than any watching, and better than anything.

The party stopped at the edge of the grass. It was late afternoon, the light going long and honey-colored, and the country around them lay so still that Orsen could hear his own pulse. In the valley of his keeping, silence had always felt like safety—the hush of the sealed place, magic unable to enter, prayers going up like sparks that died before they caught. Here the silence was different. Here it was the silence of a room after everyone has left it, or been taken from it.

"The water will still be good," Sable said. She had said little since her confession on the arguing road—that she served a rival god, that she had been sent to open him like a lock—and she said little now, but her eyes went from the well to Orsen and back again with an expression he could not read. He no longer trusted himself to read anyone's expressions. He had spent forty years believing he knew his own face in the mirror, and he was no longer certain of even that.

Maren spread her cloak on the grass and sat Wren down on it, producing the last of the bread and the heel of hard cheese. "Eat first," she said, in the tone that ended arguments before they began. "Water after. You can be thirsty and dead of hunger both, but you'll be miserable about it."

"I'm not hungry," Wren said, already eating.

"You're always not hungry," Maren said, "and always eating."

It was such an ordinary exchange, so warm and worn and familiar, that Orsen had to turn away and look at the well. This was what the devourer ate, he understood now. Not souls in the way the old stories meant it. *This.* The small liturgy of mothers and daughters, the call and answer of ordinary love. When it had devoured prayers, all those centuries ago, it had not been hungering for worship—it had been hungering for the *sincerity* underneath, the raw human current that ran beneath words addressed to heaven. And when the seal had been made, when a whole country had been woven into a prison's walls, the god had gone starving ever since, feeding only on the edges of sealed places, on forgotten hamlets like Alderbrook, on whatever stray memories drifted close enough to catch.

Alderbrook. He still could not make himself think the word fully through. The houses standing empty with their shutters open, the tables set, the beds unmade, and not one living soul in the world able to say who had slept in them.

"Orsen." Wren's voice, at his elbow. She had followed him, of course. She followed him everywhere, had done since she was small enough to fit inside a bread basket, and there had been years when he'd wondered why, and years when he'd stopped wondering and simply been grateful. Now he wondered whether some part of her had always known what he was—whether children could see locks for what they were, the way they saw doors and keys.

She was looking at the well with her head tipped, the way she looked at things that interested her. "There's someone in it."

"There's water in it," Orsen said.

"There's someone *in the water*." She said it matter-of-factly, the way she reported the garden's out-of-season blooming, the second door inside the iron one, the tall shadow that wore his silhouette. Children see truly and then learn to stop; Wren had not yet learned. "He looks like you. But older. And sad in a way you aren't yet."

Orsen felt the words land somewhere beneath his ribs, cold as well-water themselves.

"Wren," Maren called gently, "come finish your cheese."

Wren went—but at the edge of the cloak she turned back and said, "Don't look too long. Mama says that about mirrors, and wells are just mirrors that fell down."

Then it was Orsen and the well, and the long light, and the silence of an entire erased nation pressing against his ears.

* * *

He knelt at the rim because travelers kneel at wells; it was reflex, habit, the muscle-memory of a life he now suspected had been written into him like a false inscription on a genuine coin. Forty years of being the keeper of a gate. Forty years of candles that wouldn't flicker, prayers that went unanswered, a garden that bloomed out of season because it remembered things he couldn't. Forty years of Maren finding him—young, empty-handed, empty-headed—at the gate with no past, and choosing, God forgive her, to give him one instead of asking where his had gone.

He leaned over the rim and looked down.

The water was far below, black at the center where the shaft deepened, ringed with green-gold where the afternoon light reached it. And in it—not a reflection. He understood that immediately, the way you understand a wrong note in a song you've known all your life. Reflections obey rules. They tilt when you tilt, blur when the surface stirs, wear your face and no other.

The face in the water wore his bones. But it was not his reflection.

It was a man perhaps ten years his senior, though age sat strangely on the features, as if time had touched them lightly out of respect rather than need. Dark hair bound back in the Vessany style—he didn't know how he knew it was the Vessany style, and knowing frightened him more than the face itself. A high collar worked with thread that caught the light even underwater. And the eyes—

The eyes were his. That was the unbearable part. Every other detail might have been a stranger's, but the eyes were the ones he shaved beneath every morning, the ones Wren had trusted since before she could walk, the ones Maren had looked into across twenty years of shared meals and unsaid truths. The same eyes, but carrying something his had never held: a weight of decision made and paid for. Sadness, yes, exactly as Wren said—sadness in a way he wasn't yet.

"Hello," Orsen said to the water, and his voice cracked on it, absurdly, like a boy's.

The man in the well moved his mouth. No sound came up the shaft, but the water carried the shape of the words, and somehow—through his palms on the stone, through the soles of his boots, through the marrow the way the gate used to hum under his hand—Orsen heard it anyway.

*You came.*

"I don't know who you are." His own voice sounded thin above the dark. "I don't know what I am anymore. So whoever you are, tell me plainly."

The man smiled, and it broke Orsen's heart, because it was the smile Maren had told him he made when he thought no one was watching—crooked on the left side, guarded, as though joy were something he'd been taught to ration.

*I am what you were,* the man said. *Before they needed a lock more than they needed a prince.*

The word went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor.

Prince.

The chapel archive rose in his mind—the maps that unwrote themselves as he read, the ink crawling off the parchment like insects fleeing fire, the single page that had resisted erasure longest: a coronation portrait, unfinished, the sitter's face scratched away but the crown remaining, hovering over nothing. *Vessany,* he had read. *Sacrificed to make the prison; its people became the seal's mortar.* He had grieved then, kneeling in the buried dark, for a homeland he never knew was his.

Now the grief changed hands. Because it was not only his country that had been poured into the walls of a prison.

It was *him*.

"I volunteered." The words came out of him flat and certain, and he hated how certain they were. "Didn't I. Nobody did this to me. I walked into it."

*There was no one else,* Aurel said—the name arrived in Orsen's mind complete, as names do, *Aurel of Vessany, last prince of a country already dying, offering itself up piece by piece to feed the making of the seal. The gods who remained were fading; the devourer was waking; and the working required a soul willing to be unmade. Not conquered. Not stolen. Given. That was the price of the lock: it had to be locked from inside.*

"So they took my memories. My name. My—"

*No.* Aurel's voice sharpened, the first hard edge in it. *You gave those. All of it. The working asked, and you said yes, and you said yes again when it asked for the remembering itself. You stood in the making of your own erasure and you blessed it.* He paused, and his image rippled, and when it stilled his eyes were wet. *I remember saying yes. I am the only one who remembers. That is what they left me—a face in the water, to keep the account.*

Orsen gripped the rim until the old stone bit his palms. The vertigo was total. He had spent weeks learning that his history was a lie, that he was not the gate's keeper but its lock, and he had thought that was the bottom of it. He had grieved the theft of a past. But there had been no theft. There had been a *surrender*, signed and sealed by his own hand, his own will, his own love for a people whose faces he could not summon and whose names had been scraped off the world.

He was complicit in his own vanishing. He had been the architect and the ruin both.

"Why show me?" he whispered. "Why now?"

*Because the seal is failing,* Aurel said. *And because someone must choose again. Once, you chose to disappear so others could remain. The choice is yours again, Orsen Lockridge—or whatever we decide to call ourselves. And this time—*

The water shivered.

Not from wind. Not from any movement of the man within it. The whole surface trembled like a struck drumhead, and the green-gold light curdled, and the temperature dropped so fast that frost feathered out across the stone beneath Orsen's fingers. Behind him he heard Maren rise, heard Sable's sharp intake of breath, heard Wren say, very quietly, "That's not him anymore."

The face in the well began to change.

Aurel's features slid like wax, stretched, thinned, and beneath them something else surfaced—something vast wearing the suggestion of a face the way the ocean wears the suggestion of a coastline. Its eyes were holes through which the water showed stars that were not stars. Its smile was patient in the manner of things that have starved for eons and know the meal is coming regardless.

And it spoke, and the well spoke with it, and the ground spoke, and Orsen's own bones rang like a struck bell:

***Come unlock me, little lock.***

Every word was a hand closing around him. Not cruelly—that was the horror of it. Gently. The way you speak to something you own.

***You have walked so far,*** it murmured, and the water lapped the words against the stone, tender as a tide. ***Through the country you were made of, toward the door you were made for. Do you know what I feel when you walk? Hope. I have eaten ten thousand years of prayers and never once tasted hope until you.***

"I'm not coming to unlock you," Orsen managed.

***No?*** The amusement in it was vast and soft. ***You crossed the threshold. You let the false memories burn. You carry the true face now, and the true name, and the key is nothing more than a willing soul at the willing door. You are doing everything I asked, little lock. You simply believe you are doing it for love.*** The stars in its eye-holes turned slowly. ***Perhaps you are. Love unlocks more doors than hate ever did. Ask your mother-friend. She kept you fed and lied to for twenty years, and look how faithfully you walked her road.***

"Don't—" Orsen's voice tore. "*Don't talk about her.*"

***Everything talks to me eventually. Everything sincere. That is all prayer is—sincerity with nowhere else to go. And you are the sincerest thing I have ever been shown. A man who gave himself away so completely that even his giving was erased. Come to the First Door, Orsen-Aurel-whoever-you-will-be. Stand before it whole, as they never let you be. And when you see what remains of you that is truly yours to give—***the water rose an inch, kissed the rim, subsided, gentle as a dog's tongue***—you will open me yourself. And I will be grateful. And I will eat so carefully.***

The presence withdrew like a tide going out. The water stilled. And for one heartbeat before it settled, Aurel's face returned—wrecked now, weeping openly, mouthing something over and over that Orsen could not hear and understood anyway, because it was the same sentence he had been saying to himself since the night he'd learned the letter's words, *he is not the keeper, he is the lock*:

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I chose, and I'm sorry.*

Then the well was only a well, full of dark water and evening sky, holding nothing but reflections that obeyed rules again.

* * *

He didn't know how long he stayed kneeling. When he became aware of the world again, Maren was beside him—she must have crossed the grass without him hearing, which frightened him, because Maren's footsteps were the first sounds he'd ever known and he had always heard her coming. Her hand was on his back, between the shoulder blades, steady as a wall beam.

"He talked to you," she said. Not a question. She had heard enough, then. They all had. The whole valley of the drowned country had probably heard; the dead of Vessany had probably heard it in whatever sleep they slept.

"He offered me mercy," Orsen said. His voice came out sanded smooth, hollow. "That's what frightens me. It wasn't a threat, Maren. It was kindness. It wants to eat us and it was *kind* about it."

"The worst ones usually are." She said it with the flat practicality of a woman who had raised a child alone and buried a husband and hidden a letter for two decades. Then her grip tightened. "Orsen. Look at me. Whatever it showed you down there—whatever face—that thing is very old and very clever and it lies the way rivers run downhill. Naturally. Constantly. Without effort."

"It didn't lie." He turned and met her eyes, and saw her flinch at whatever she found in his. "That's the problem, Maren. It didn't lie once. I *did* volunteer. I *did* stand there and bless my own erasure. I built my prison with my own hands and thanked the masons." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "All these weeks I've been grieving a stolen life. There was no theft. I'm the thief. I stole myself from myself and hid the evidence in a garden."

Wren appeared at his knee. She had been waiting, he realized, for exactly this moment—she had a genius for timing that would terrify him later, when he had leisure to be terrified. She climbed into his lap without asking, the way she hadn't done since she was six, and put both her small hands on either side of his face.

"You're still you," she said fiercely. "The water man is sad and the shadow man is hungry and you're still you. I checked."

"How could you possibly check?"

"I just looked at you really hard." As if it were the simplest magic in the world. Perhaps it was. "You make the same face when you're scared as when you're pruning the roses. Like you're angry at the branch but sorry for it. The bad men don't make that face."

He held her then, his face pressed into her hair, and felt the crisis move through him like weather—not passing, nothing that size passes, but *moving*, becoming something that could be survived rather than merely endured. Prince Aurel of Vessany. The lock. The willing victim, the willing jailer, the complicit ghost of his own life. None of it could be undone. All of it was his now, and the question was no longer *what was I* but the one Aurel had left hanging at the end, the question the devourer wanted answered one way and his family, perhaps, another:

Someone must choose again.

He lifted his head. Sable stood apart, arms wrapped around herself, watching him with an expression he finally, finally understood—because it was guilt, plain and naked, the guilt of a woman who had led him across a country toward exactly this well, exactly this conversation, exactly the opening her god required. She had confessed on the road and they had kept her anyway, and now the bill for that mercy stood waiting in her face.

"Sable," he said. She flinched. "How much farther to the First Door?"

Her throat worked. "Two days. Three, if the roads argue."

"Then we walk tomorrow." He set Wren on her feet and stood, and the evening wind came down the nameless valley and moved through his hair, and for the first time since crossing the threshold he felt the whole shape of himself, prince and keeper, lock and key, standing in one body under one sky. "Whatever I am, whatever I agreed to—I'm finished letting other hands write my ending. Even mine."

Behind them, unseen by any of them, the well water stirred one last time—and far below the reach of any light, something ancient and patient and terribly grateful began, softly, to count the hours.

In the dark of the grass, Sable drew her knife and looked east, toward the drowned capital, and whispered words in a language that had not been spoken aloud since the world forgot Vessany—and the words were a warning, and the warning was too late.
