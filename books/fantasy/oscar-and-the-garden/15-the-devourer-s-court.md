## 15. The Devourer's Court

The hunger-storm did not take them by force. That was the worst of it, Orsen thought as the world dissolved around him—no claws, no chains, no screaming wind. It simply opened, the way a mouth opens for bread.

One moment they were on the bone-white road through Vessany's ghost-country, Sable unconscious across Maren's back, Wren's hand locked in his. The next, the road was gone, and Orsen was falling—not down, but *inward*, as if the ground beneath Vessany had always been hollow and had only now remembered to swallow him.

He heard Wren scream his name. He felt her fingers torn from his. He tried to say something—he would never remember afterward what he meant to say, only that it mattered desperately—and then the dark closed over him like water over a stone, and he was alone.

*Alone,* he corrected himself, as he drifted in a darkness that had texture to it, a grain like velvet seen too closely. *I am never alone anymore. I have been carrying a god in my bones since before I could walk.*

"You came," said a voice that was not a voice. It arrived not through his ears but through his memories, wearing the timbre of every kind thing anyone had ever said to him. Maren's laugh. Wren's goodnight whisper. The chapel bell from a village he had never visited but somehow mourned. "You came all this way, little lock. Do you know how long I have waited for someone to come all this way?"

Orsen gathered himself. He had no body here—or he had one, but it was made of the same uncertain stuff as everything else, hands that were more suggestion than flesh. Still he straightened, because posture was a habit older than memory, and some habits survived even the burning away of false years.

"Where is she?" he demanded. "Where is Wren? Where are Maren and Sable?"

"They are where they need to be." The darkness warmed around him, gentle as a hearth-room. "You worry for them. Of course you do. You have worried for them since the day Maren Cole set a loaf of bread on your wall and decided, without asking anyone's permission, that you would be family. Shall we begin there?"

"Begin what?"

"Your life," said the devourer of prayers, with terrible tenderness. "All the ones I saved for you."

* * *

Light bloomed.

Not the thin, apologetic light of the valley, filtered through its permanent gray hush, but real sunlight—the kind that poured gold through a kitchen window and lay warm on worn floorboards. Orsen stood in a kitchen. His kitchen. Flour dust hung in the air like held breath. Bread rose under a cloth on the table, and outside the window, the garden bloomed out of season: marigolds in frost-month, roses in the dead of winter, exactly as they always had.

Maren stood at the hearth with her sleeves rolled, arguing cheerfully with no one about the proper way to knead dough. Wren sat on the floor, seven years old forever, braiding grass into crowns and naming each blade.

"This is—" Orsen's throat closed. "This is my house."

"It is," the devourer agreed. It had no shape here, or rather it wore the shape of the room itself, the warmth itself. "It is Tuesday. On Tuesdays, Wren follows you into the garden and asks you questions about the flowers until you run out of answers, and then you make up answers, and she knows you're making them up, and loves you for it anyway. On Tuesdays, Maren brings bread and complains that you let it go stale. On Tuesdays, nothing happens, because nothing has ever happened, because you are the keeper of a gate in a valley where nothing comes, and you will keep it until you die an old man in a chair by this window, and it will be enough. It will be *good.*"

"I know this," Orsen whispered. "This is true."

"Is it?" The warmth rippled. "You crossed a country that does not exist to reach a door beneath a drowned city. You saw your own face in a well and it belonged to a prince. Tell me, keeper—which of these lives smells more like yours? The flour, or the salt water?"

He reached for the table. The wood was solid under his palm. He could smell the yeast, could hear the particular creak of the third stair when Wren took them two at a time. Somewhere beyond the window, the gate stood shut in its wall, humming its low note, and he was the man who tended the garden beside it, and that was all, and that was *everything.*

"It's true," he said again, and heard the waver in his own voice.

"Then stay." The devourer drew closer without moving; the kitchen simply became *more*, the sunlight thickening to honey. "That is why I built this place. Not a trap—a gift. Every prayer I ever ate, I ate because mortals threw their longing at heaven and asked to be given things. I learned what longing tastes like. And you, little lock—you were made of longing. A prince who wanted so badly to save his people that he let them unmake him. So I kept the recipe. All these centuries, I kept it. Every life you might have lived inside that valley, if no one had ever stopped holding the door. Here is the first one. There are ten thousand more. I will show you all of them if you wish, and you may choose whichever fits best, and wear it, and no one will ever take it from you."

"The others," Orsen said. "Wren. Maren. Are they in this?"

"They are whatever you need them to be. They are here. Look how happy she is."

Wren looked up from her grass crowns and smiled at him with missing teeth, and Orsen felt something in his chest crack along a seam he hadn't known was there.

* * *

The kitchen became a wedding.

He had not expected that. He stood in a summer field behind the valley chapel—there was no chapel in the valley, there had never been a chapel, and yet here one stood, ivy-covered and sweet-smelling—and watched himself marry a woman whose face he almost recognized, a baker's daughter from Alderbrook, the hamlet that had ceased to exist. She laughed at something he'd said, and her laughter sounded like rain on a roof, safe and domestic and endless.

In the front row sat Maren, dabbing her eyes. Beside her, Wren—older now, twelve, coltish and solemn—held the rings on a cushion. In the garden afterward, the gate stood open for once, and people walked through it laughing, carrying pies and fiddles, and nobody feared anything, because the valley had never needed protecting, because there had never been anything to protect against.

"Do you see?" the devourer murmured, and now it had taken a shape at last: a tall figure standing at Orsen's shoulder, wearing his silhouette, exactly as Wren had once described—the shadow behind him that loved him. Its face was his face, aged kindly. "This is the life where you were only ever a man. No sigil. No seal. No god curled in your marrow like a stone in a fruit. You wanted that once. Don't pretend you didn't. I heard the wanting—I eat wanting. It was the first prayer you ever made and you didn't even know you'd made it: *let me be ordinary. Let me be just a gardener.* I have carried that prayer longer than you have carried your name."

Orsen watched the baker's daughter dance with Maren. He watched Wren chase fireflies. He felt the pull of it, deep and tidal—the ache to simply *stay*, to sink into this warm country where every grief had already been pre-solved, where the gate hummed harmlessly and love arrived on schedule like bread on a Tuesday.

"That isn't my life," he said quietly.

"No. But it could have been. Isn't that worse? Isn't it crueler, what I'm doing, than any torture? I could burn cities out of your mind, little lock. Instead I am showing you the man you might have been if the world had been kinder. And I am asking you: which of us hurt you more? Me, who devoured prayers—or them, who turned a living prince into a lock and called it mercy?"

* * *

They walked together through the lives.

Through a life where Orsen had died young and been buried under the garden's plum tree, and the whole valley had come to mourn, and the flowers on his grave bloomed out of season forever. Through a life where he had left the valley at twenty and become a mapmaker, drawing coastlines he'd never seen, and died old and famous in a city of towers. Through a life where Wren had grown up calling him father and he had never once wondered why the candles wouldn't flicker.

Each life was perfect. Each life was seamless. Each one ended the same way—with Orsen, in whatever form, looking up from whatever happiness he'd been granted, and finding the tall shadow beside him waiting patiently, wearing his face, saying: *this one can be yours. Say yes. You've suffered so much. You deserve rest. Let me give it to you.*

And that was the horror of it, Orsen realized, standing in a sunlit orchard in a life where he had never been anything but happy: the devourer was not lying. Every life was genuinely possible. Every joy was honestly rendered. This creature that ate prayers had spent eons learning the shape of human hope, and it built hope the way Maren built bread—with skill, with patience, with real care.

The cruelest offer in the world was not a threat. It was comfort, freely given, by something that meant it.

"Why?" Orsen asked. The orchard's apples gleamed red around him. "Why do this? Why build me heavens instead of hells? What do you get?"

The shadow was quiet for a long moment. When it spoke, its voice—for just a heartbeat—lost all its borrowed warmth, and underneath was something vast and hollow and unspeakably tired, like a cathedral after the congregation has gone home.

"Hunger," it said. "Do you know what I ate, little lock? Prayers. Ten thousand years of prayers. Every desperate plea, every gratitude, every whispered bargain at every shrine. And do you know what prayers are?" It turned his own kind-aged face toward him. "Prayers are *longing aimed at someone.* They are proof that somewhere, someone believes another being is listening. I ate ten thousand years of that belief, and it filled me, and none of it fed me, and I have been starving since before your country had a name. Then they sealed me away with a lock made of a soul, and the lock dreamed a small warm life, and I have lain in the dark beside that dream for three hundred years, listening to you love your neighbors, and I have never wanted anything the way I want what you have. So I built it. All of it. Every life. To understand it. To hold it. To ask you—" and here the great hollow voice faltered, and Orsen understood with a lurch that he was hearing something no god should ever feel— "*to ask you how it works.*"

Orsen stared at the devourer of prayers, the eater of nations, the storm that had swallowed his friends, and saw, beneath the vastness, a beggar holding out a bowl.

"You don't want to eat my life," he said slowly. "You want to be *given* one."

"I want to stop being hungry," said the god, and the orchard trembled around them like a reflection in troubled water. "Say yes to one of them. Stay in the kitchen. Stay at the wedding. Stay anywhere, and I will keep the door shut behind you myself, and I will finally, finally have made something instead of eaten it—"

But Orsen was no longer entirely listening. Because in the trembling of the orchard, in the seams showing between the borrowed worlds, he had glimpsed something: a thread of gray light running through the court like a root through soil, thin and stubborn and utterly familiar. It smelled of burnt bread. It smelled of scraped knees and arguments forgiven. It led away from the kitchens and weddings and orchards, deeper into the dark, toward a sound that made his heart seize—

A child's voice, small and furious and absolutely unafraid, shouting his name.

"WREN," Orsen roared, and the apple trees shattered like glass.

The court convulsed. The devourer reached for him—not with menace, but with panic, the way a drowning man grabs at flotsam—but Orsen had spent a lifetime tending things that grew toward light, and he knew a living root when he gripped one. He seized the gray thread with both hands, and it burned, and it held, and the false heavens tore away around him like fog off a morning field.

Behind him, in the collapsing dark, the devourer cried out—not in rage.

In loneliness.

And ahead of him, at the end of the gray-lit passage, Orsen saw a small figure standing in a circle of pressed-flower light, fists raised against the darkness, and beside her, kneeling, bleeding, refusing to leave—Maren.
