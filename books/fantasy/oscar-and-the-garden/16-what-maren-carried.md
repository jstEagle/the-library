## 16. What Maren Carried

The court of the devourer was made of everything that had ever been prayed for and never received.

Maren understood that before anyone told her. She felt it in her teeth, the way you feel a storm coming, or a lie. The walls around her were built of wishes stacked like stones—*let him love me, let the fever break, let my son come home from the war*—and each one had been swallowed whole, its ending eaten out of it, so that every prayer in those walls was still waiting, forever, for an answer that would never come. The light came from nowhere. The floor gave beneath her feet like bread dough, like something alive and patient.

Orsen stood at the center of it, and he was not standing at all, really. He was *held*, suspended in a warmth that looked gentle from the outside, wrapped in the devourer's idea of mercy.

She had seen what it showed him. They all had—the illusion had spilled past Orsen like water over a bowl's rim, and for a moment Maren had stood inside another woman's kitchen, watching a man who wore Orsen's face but not his carefulness laugh with children who had his eyes. A life where he'd never been a lock. A life where no one had ever had to lie to him by a gate at moonset to keep him safe.

It had been beautiful. That was the horror of it. It had been beautiful the way a painted window is beautiful when the sun is behind it—you can't see through it, only at it, and if you're tired enough, cold enough, grieving enough, you'll believe the colors are the world.

Maren was fifty-three years old. She had buried a husband, raised a daughter alone through two hard winters, and lied to a good man for thirty years because she loved him more than she feared the truth. She knew painted windows. She had lived behind one herself.

"Orsen." Her voice came out rough. "Orsen Lockridge. You look at me."

The court rippled. Somewhere far off—or very near; distance meant nothing here—a sound like a thousand mouths inhaling.

"He cannot hear you," said a voice that was not a voice. It arrived the way cold arrives, without direction, everywhere at once. "He is being given what he was denied. He is being given *enough*. Why would he turn from enough?"

Maren turned toward the sound, though there was nothing to face. Sable lay against a wall of woven prayers behind her, grey-faced, Wren pressed small against her side. The child's hand was fisted in Maren's apron and had been since they were taken, and Maren had not once tried to loosen it.

"Because I've seen 'enough,'" Maren said. "I've baked it. It comes out burnt on the bottom half the time."

* * *

Here is what Maren carried into the devourer's court, though she did not know yet that she carried anything at all:

She carried the smell of the first loaf she ever baked alone, at eleven years old, while her mother was sick abed—the loaf that came out black as a roof tile, and how her father ate a slice anyway, all of it, chewing steadily, and said, "Well. Now we know we don't have to worry about you getting proud."

She carried Wren at four years old, falling off the garden wall because she'd been trying to reach a bird's nest she had no business reaching, and the scrape down her shin that bled like a slaughtered pig, and how Wren hadn't cried until Maren cleaned it, and then cried with such fury and betrayal that Maren had to turn her face away so as not to laugh. And how Wren, sniffling, had said, "You hurt me worse than the wall," and Maren had said, "Yes. That's what love is, mostly," and had believed it more every year since.

She carried Tam. Not the Tam who died—though she carried that too, the wet morning of it, the way grief has a smell like pennies and rain—but Tam alive: Tam leaving his boots in the middle of the kitchen floor for the nine hundredth time, and her throwing the boot at his head and missing on purpose, and their argument about whether the south field should go to barley or beans lasting three days and ending not in victory but in soup, in sitting down across from each other too tired to be right anymore, in his hand finding hers on the table like it was the most obvious thing in the world.

She carried the argument with Orsen—the real one, the only true fight they'd ever had, six years ago, when he'd wanted to burn the east bed of the garden because the flowers there grew wrong, grew into shapes, and she'd stood in front of the flowerbed with a rake like a halberd and told him he'd dig up that bed over her body. He'd shouted. She'd shouted. Wren had watched from the window with enormous eyes. And the next morning Orsen had left a pot of honey on her doorstep without a note, and she'd left bread on his, also without a note, and neither of them had ever mentioned it again, and the flowers still grew wrong, and that was fine. That was *fine*. That was what forgiveness actually looked like—not a grand absolution, just honey and bread and going on.

She carried all of this the way a stone carries heat from a fire long after the fire's gone. Slowly. Stubbornly. Without knowing it mattered.

* * *

"You offer him comfort," Maren said to the everywhere-voice. "You call it mercy. But I know what you are. You eat prayers. You eat the *wanting* out of things and leave the shell. Look at your walls. Every stone here is somebody's hope with the heart chewed out."

"The hopes were not nourishing," the voice said, and there was something almost weary in it, vast and old and hungry in the way hunger is old. "They waited. Waiting is bitter. I took the bitterness and left them rest."

"You left them *nothing*." Maren's hands had closed into fists without her permission. "A prayer answered halfway is worse than unanswered. My girl prayed for rain last spring and got your rain, and it stank of you, and I watched wonder curdle in her face. Don't tell me about mercy. I've fed people for thirty years. Mercy isn't giving someone what they dreamed. It's sitting with them while they learn to want differently."

The court shuddered. Around her, the walls of prayers groaned like a ship in weather.

"You are small," the voice said. "Your memories are small. Burnt bread. Scraped knees. Do you truly believe these weigh against—" and here the court *showed* her, flooded her, generous and terrible: a hundred lives Orsen might have had, a thousand, each one warm-lit and complete, a husband in one, a father in another, a king in a third, each life polished smooth as river glass, each one *finished*, every prayer in them answered, every want met—

And Maren stood in the flood of it and found, to her own surprise, that she was not drowning.

Because the lives were perfect. That was the flaw. Every single one of them was *perfect*, and Maren had cooked for enough people, mourned enough people, forgiven enough people, to know that perfection was the smell of something dead. Real lives had burnt bottoms. Real forgiveness came with honey and no note. Real love threw a boot and missed on purpose.

"No," she said.

The flood stopped.

"I said *no*." Her voice cracked in the middle like green wood, and she let it. "You're showing him lives with nothing wrong in them. I've never had a day with nothing wrong in it. Not one. The day I married Tam it rained through the tent roof. The day Wren was born the midwife was drunk. The best meal I ever served had grit in it because Wren helped wash the greens and she was six and enthusiastic. And I wouldn't trade one of those days for your glass-smooth kingdoms, because none of yours ever *happened*."

"They could happen," the voice said, and now it was closer, or denser, the cold of it pressing at her skin. "That is what I offer. Not memory. Fulfillment. He was made to be a lock. I offer him to be a man."

"He *is* a man." Maren stepped forward—one step, then another, her boots sinking slightly into the dough-soft floor, toward the held shape of Orsen at the court's center. "A man who burns things. A man who sulks. A man who once didn't speak to me for a month over a flowerbed, and who leaves honey on doorsteps instead of saying sorry, and who taught my daughter the names of every plant in that garden including the ones that grow wrong. Especially those. You want to give him a life? He has one. It's *ours*. It's small and it scratches and we've hurt each other in it and stayed anyway. Staying is the whole of it. Staying is everything your pretty lives don't have, because nothing in them ever needed staying through."

* * *

Wren's grip tightened on her apron. Maren heard the child whisper, "Grandmam,"—not her grandmother, but the name Wren had invented for her at five and never surrendered—and felt the small fist like an anchor.

And something strange began to happen.

The nearest wall of prayers—the one Sable leaned against—flickered. One stone of it, one single wish, brightened instead of dimming. Maren could almost read it, could feel its shape: *let her forgive me by morning.* Small. Specific. Ugly and human, the kind of prayer you'd be ashamed to say aloud. And it was *warm*.

The voice went quiet in a way that was worse than speaking.

"What is that," it said.

"That?" Maren looked at the glowing stone, and understanding rose in her like bread rising—slow, inevitable, filling every hollow place. "That's mine. That's the night Tam and I fought about the field and I went to bed furious and prayed, ashamed of myself even while I prayed it, that I'd get over myself by morning. And I did. Barely. With soup." She laughed, and the laugh came out wet. "You couldn't digest it, could you? Because it's not a want. It's a *repentance*. There's no answer to steal. It already came true, ugly little thing, and it's still true, and it's *mine*, and you can't eat what's already been lived."

More stones were warming. All down the wall, all through the court, prayers were kindling like lamps at dusk—small ones, mean ones, glorious stupid human ones. *Let the fever break. Let me be brave tomorrow. Let him come home. Let me stop being angry. Let her scraped knee heal clean. Forgive me, forgive me, forgive me.* Not the great shining wants the devourer had hoarded. The small stubborn ones. The ones that had already been answered, badly, imperfectly, by other people's tired hands—by mothers cleaning wounds, by fathers eating burnt bread, by neighbors leaving honey on doorsteps.

Prayers that had been answered not by gods but by *people*.

"You see it," Maren said softly. "Don't you. You've been starving for ten thousand years at a table full of food, because you only eat the wanting, and wanting is the cheap part. The answer was never the miracle. The answer was always some fool like me, getting up before dawn, doing the small work with her hands. You can't eat that. It doesn't wait. It *acts*."

The court groaned. Cracks ran up through the dough-soft floor, and through them Maren saw—not the valley, not anywhere real, but simply *absence*, the illusion losing its footing.

At the center, Orsen stirred.

His eyes opened. They found her across the failing court—found her grey-haired and furious and crying without shame—and whatever spell of *enough* had held him, it broke the way a fever breaks: all at once, with sweating and shaking and a sound half sob.

"Maren," he said. His voice was wrecked. "It showed me—I *wanted*—"

"Of course you wanted," she said. "Wanting's the cheap part. Come here."

* * *

The court did not collapse all at once. It came apart the way a lie does—corner by corner, stone by warming stone, each little answered prayer burning through the wall that held it until the structure could not remember why it stood. The devourer's voice thinned to a wind, then to a whisper, then to something that sounded, astonishingly, like confusion—an ancient hunger meeting, for the first time, a thing it could neither swallow nor understand.

"You would choose the burnt bread," it whispered. "Over all of this."

"I'd choose the *baker*," Maren said. "Every time. That's the part you never tasted."

Then the floor gave way entirely, and the false lives poured off Orsen like water off oiled wool, and he stumbled toward them—toward Sable bleeding quietly against the wall, toward Wren with her fists full of apron, toward Maren—and the court of eaten prayers dissolved around them into dark.

But not empty dark. In the last of the light, Maren saw the small prayers still glowing, drifting free of the ruin like embers off a bonfire, hundreds of them, thousands—every one a small kindness someone had done for someone else, still burning, refusing to go out.

And in the darkness ahead, far below, something vast shifted. Something that had begun, in its eons-old hunger, to feel a new and terrible sensation.

Not satisfaction. Not yet. But curiosity.

Ahead of them, down through the broken dark, a faint green light was rising—the color, Maren realized with a jolt, of new shoots in early spring. The color of Orsen's garden.

The color of the First Door, waiting below.
