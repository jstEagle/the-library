## 17. The Original Door

The water remembered how to be a city.

Orsen felt it before he saw it—a pressure against his thoughts like a thumb pressed to the eye, the sensation of standing inside something that had once been full of people. They had descended for two days through Vessany's drowned capital, down drowned stairways and along avenues where silt lay over cobbles like grey snow, and everywhere the water was not merely water. It held shapes. It held echoes. When Sable's lantern swung across a plaza, Orsen saw faces in the ripples—brief, patient, watching—and when Maren took Wren's hand and pulled her close, the ripples rearranged themselves around them, making room, as if the city still kept its manners after all these centuries.

"It's not flooding," Sable said quietly. "It never flooded. The water came up to meet it when it went under. Like closing a hand."

They walked on the roofs. That was the worst part, at first—the realization, half a day in, that what Orsen had taken for a sunken street was a rooftop, and that every soot-dark rectangle beneath their boots had been a hearth. A country of kitchens. A country of suppers. He tried not to think about the mortar metaphor, and thought of nothing else.

Wren had stopped asking questions somewhere above the waterline. She walked between Maren and Orsen with Sable's bandaged arm slung over the mule they'd bought in a village that did not appear on any map because there were no maps left to appear on, and she looked at everything with her chin tucked, the way she did when she was filing something away for later. Children filed things away, Orsen had learned. Adults only thought they were keeping secrets.

* * *

The temple—if it was a temple; no one had ever told him what Vessany built at the bottom of itself—opened out of the last avenue like a throat.

It was not grand. That undid him more than grandeur would have. He had expected spires, gold leaf, the vanity of a people who knew they were building their own tomb. Instead: a low hall of dark stone, pillars worn smooth at hand-height where generations had rested their palms in passing, and a floor of pale river-stone laid in no pattern at all, just stones gathered one by one by someone with patience and love and no gift for geometry. Someone's grandmother had laid this floor. Someone's grandchildren had scuffed it.

At the far end, where an altar should have stood, stood the Door.

It was smaller than Orsen had imagined. Smaller than the iron gate in the valley, which had always seemed to him the largest object in the world, large enough to hold shut a whole sky. This was only door-shaped—waist-high, knee-high, he couldn't tell, because it would not settle on a size. It was made of a wood that might have been oak or might have been something older than trees, and it had no handle, no hinges, no lock. It simply stood there in the dry air at the heart of the drowned city, upright and plain and waiting, and the water stopped at the threshold of the hall as though it too knew better than to enter uninvited.

Around it grew a garden.

Orsen's breath left him in a sound he did not recognize as his own.

Flowers—white, small, five-petaled, growing straight out of cracks in the ancient stone without soil, without light, without permission. They ringed the Door in a loose, uneven circle, hundreds of them, some fresh and some gone to silver seed-heads, and the air in the hall smelled the way his own garden smelled at dawn: green and mineral and faintly, achingly sweet. His hands ached. He looked down and found them trembling with recognition, the way a man's hands tremble at the shoulder of a brother he buried young.

"They're the same," Wren whispered. She had slipped forward despite everything, drawn the way she was always drawn to living things. "Orsen. They're the same flowers. From your garden."

"Not from mine," Orsen said. His voice came out rough. "Mine are from here."

Sable lowered herself onto a fallen pillar with a grunt, cradling the wound Maren had re-bandaged that morning. Her face was grey with exhaustion, but her eyes were clear, and clear eyes on Sable had learned to frighten Orsen. "This is where it happened," she said. "Where they made you. Where your people—" She stopped, and for once in all their road together, chose kindness over precision. "Where you were planted."

"Planted." Orsen laughed, or something near it. "Yes. I suppose that's the word."

Maren put her hand on the back of his neck, the way she had when he was young and grieving things he couldn't name, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the flowers weren't already saying better.

* * *

They made camp in the corner of the hall, because there was nowhere else in the world to make it. Sable slept. Wren slept curled against Maren with her fist closed around a pressed flower she had carried since the valley, since the threshold-god, since the beginning of all this foolishness and grace. Maren sat awake beside her, sharpening a knife she did not need, and watched Orsen stand at the edge of the flower-circle for hours.

He could feel the Door. That was the truth he hadn't spoken aloud yet. From the moment they entered the hall he could feel it the way he had once felt the iron gate under his palm—that hum, deep and patient, except now it was not a hum but a voice too low to parse, a note held for centuries by throats that were all his. Every white flower was tuned to it. Every white flower was, he understood with a calm that frightened him more than terror would have, a name. A person of Vessany, folded small and blooming, holding one thread of the seal.

And the seal wanted finishing. That was the other truth. The Door did not speak, but it did not need to. It required what Sable had told them it would require, weeks ago, on the night she first confessed her god's name: a whole soul, willingly given. Not a piece. Not a memory, not a vow, not a prayer. All of him. The prince and the keeper both, poured back through the keyhole of himself, and then—

And then nothing. Then the flowers. Then a fifth-petaled whiteness among hundreds, holding a thread, humming a note, until the world ended or the devourer starved, whichever came first.

He found that he was not afraid. That was the strangest discovery of the night. He examined himself for fear the way he examined leaves for blight, turning each feeling over to see the underside of it, and found instead a terrible, settling rightness. Of course. Of course this was the ending. Everything else had been the long walk toward it—the garden that remembered, the gate that hummed, the village that forgot itself, the roads that argued, the inn full of hungry dead. He had been grown for this the way the flowers had been grown: patiently, lovingly, by hands that meant well.

"I can hear you thinking," Maren said. "You're loud as a cart on gravel."

"Maren." He didn't turn around. "If I do this, will you take Wren home? Through the inn. The dead will remember the way, now. They owe you that."

The knife stopped moving against the stone. "Orsen Lockridge—"

"Aurel," he corrected softly. "I should probably die under my real name. It seems rude not to, here, where they'd know it."

"Don't you dare." She crossed the hall and stood beside him, small and stubborn, smelling of woodsmoke and travel and burnt bread, and he loved her so much it was indistinguishable from pain. "Don't you *dare* dress it up as politeness. You don't get to make this tidy. You don't get to make this *honest*, either, whatever you've been telling yourself since the well."

"But it is honest." He finally turned to face her. In the dark, the white flowers gave off a light of their own, faint as frost. "That's what I keep coming back to. Every other path is a lie I'd be telling to buy time. Stay the keeper? There is no keeper. Never was—I'm the thing the keeper keeps. Break the seal and fight it whole? Maren, look at me. Look at what I am. I'm a hinge wearing a man's coat. The god ate prayers, and I *am* a prayer—one prayer, made flesh and given forty years of borrowed mornings. If I go through that Door whole, willingly, the seal is renewed for another age, and Wren grows up, and the valley stays quiet, and the world keeps its memories." He gestured at the circle of blooms. "They did it. My people did exactly this, on purpose, for strangers they'd never meet. The least I can do is finish what they started."

Maren was silent for a long moment. Water dripped somewhere in the drowned city above, patient as a clock.

"You were a boy," she said at last, "when I found you at that gate. Filthy, hollow-eyed, with no name you could say and dirt under your fingernails like you'd been gardening in a dream. And I lied to you. I let you believe you were the keeper, because a keeper has a life—a routine, bread on Tuesdays, a neighbor, a purpose—and a lock has only a purpose." Her jaw worked. "So maybe everything you are is my lie. But Orsen—Aurel—listen to me, because I'll only say it once. The bread was real. Tuesdays were real. The girl asleep over there loves you, and that is the most real thing in this drowned country or any other. Don't you tell me the Door gets to have all of that. Doors don't get to have anything. People do. And people give."

"That's what I'm doing," Orsen said gently. "Giving."

"No," said Maren. "What you're doing is *leaving*. I've buried a husband and a mother and most of my hopes, and I know giving when I see it, and this isn't it. This is you deciding you were never really alive, so it won't count as dying." She took his face in both hands, hard, the way she did when Wren was about to do something reckless. "But you were alive. You *are*. And if you walk through that Door tomorrow, I will grieve you until I die, and I will curse your stupid princely name every Tuesday over burnt bread, and I want you to sit with that tonight. All night. Because that's what you'd be spending. Not a soul in the abstract. *This.* Us."

She released him and went back to her knife, and Orsen stood alone among the flowers of his people, and did as she asked. He sat with it.

* * *

Dawn did not reach the hall, but something changed in the water-silence above, and Wren woke, and Sable woke, and the hour arrived the way hours do at the ends of stories: without ceremony.

Orsen washed his hands in the basin Maren filled from a canteen. He combed his hair with his fingers. These were gardener's habits and prince's habits both, and he no longer troubled to sort them. He knelt at the edge of the flower-circle and the flowers leaned toward him very slightly, all together, the way wheat leans after wind.

"Sable," he said. "When I go—your god. Will it honor the bargain? The seal renewed means the devourer sleeps again, means your patron waits another age?"

Sable looked at him for a long time. Something moved behind her eyes—regret, or arithmetic, or both. "My god honors bargains," she said. "In its own way. In its own time." Which was, Orsen reflected, the answer of someone who had spent her life learning exactly how much a god's word weighed, and had stopped hoping it weighed much.

He stood. He stepped over the first flowers. He crossed the circle, and the hum rose through the soles of his feet, through his shins, his knees, the old scar on his hip, the place behind his sternum where the false memories lived like swallows in an eave. The Door grew larger as he approached—or he grew smaller; the distinction had stopped mattering—and he saw now what he had refused to see before: that its grain was not woodgrain at all but writing, dense and flowing, the whole history of Vessany carved in letters too small for any eye but a lock's. He raised his hand. The surface was warm. Of course it was warm. It had been warm for a thousand years, holding the shape of the last hand that touched it.

His hand.

Behind him, Maren had risen. Sable had risen. The hall was very quiet, and the water above them hung motionless, and every white flower in the circle had turned—not drifted, *turned*—to face him, five-petaled faces lifted like a congregation at the moment of the blessing.

"One condition," Orsen said to the Door, conversationally, because he was a gardener and gardeners talk to what they tend. "Let me keep the mornings. Let them have the mornings. The bread, the rain, the child following me around like a shadow. Feed those to the seal first, before the rest. They're the strongest part."

The Door did not answer, but the hum deepened into something almost tender, and Orsen understood that it would take whatever he offered, gladly, the way the earth takes a body.

He thought of Wren asleep against Maren's side. He thought of rain that smelled older than weather. He thought, *this is the honest ending,* and reached out, and his fingertips touched the grain of names—

"—*No.*"

Small feet, running. Wren tore past Sable's grabbing hand, past Maren's cry, straight into the circle of flowers, and planted herself between Orsen and the Door with her fists balled and her chin up and tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her face.

"No," she said again. "You don't get to. This isn't—it's not fair, and it's not *right*, and doors aren't supposed to eat people, doors are supposed to *open*—" And before anyone could move, before Maren's shout or Sable's oath or Orsen's own strangled word could stop her, she dropped to her knees on the pale river-stone, wrenched open the little pouch at her belt, and began pressing seeds into the cracks of the ancient floor, one by one, fast and fierce, all around the First Door of the world.

"Seeds," she said, to the Door, to the god beyond it, to Orsen, to the whole listening dark. "Doors should be gardens. And gardens aren't graves."
