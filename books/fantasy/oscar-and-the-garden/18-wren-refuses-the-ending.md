## 18. Wren Refuses the Ending

Wren had learned a long time ago that grown-ups made their worst decisions when they got quiet.

Orsen had been quiet for three days. Not the good quiet, the kind he wore in the garden while he thinned the carrots and let her trail behind him asking questions he answered with half-smiles. This was the other quiet — the kind that came before someone left. She'd seen it once before, on the face of a tinker who stayed one winter in Alderbrook's edge and then walked out of the valley without saying goodbye to anyone, leaving his mended pots behind like shed skin.

So when Orsen knelt at the First Door and began unlacing his boots — *his boots*, as if he were preparing for something ordinary, something he might walk back from — Wren understood what was happening before any of the adults said it out loud.

The Door was beneath the drowned capital of Vessany, at the bottom of a flooded hall where the water stood perfectly still and perfectly clear, so that looking down was like looking into another sky. Pillars rose out of it, carved with names worn smooth by centuries of forgetting. And set into the far wall, half-sunk in stone and water and silence, was the Door itself: older than the iron gate in the valley, older than the garden, older than every map that had ever unmade itself. It did not look like a door. It looked like a wound that had learned to be patient.

"It requires a whole soul," Sable had said, propped against a pillar with Maren's cloak pressed over the wound in her side. "Willingly given. That's how it was sealed the first time. A prince who volunteered to be unmade."

"And that's what you're doing," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

Orsen didn't look up from his bootlaces. "Wren—"

"You're doing the thing." Her voice cracked in the middle and she hated it. "The thing where you decide everything by yourself and then tell us after, like we're not even *in* the story. Like we're just — just people who get told."

"Wren." Maren's voice, gentle and warning all at once.

But Wren wasn't finished. Something had been building in her chest since the inn, since the night she'd sat up listening to Mama say names over and over into the dark — *Halewick, Ostrelle, Bramma Vey, Tam the younger, Tam the elder* — pressing each one flat and careful between her ribs like flowers in a book. Since she'd understood that being remembered was the only weapon anyone in this whole drowned country had ever had.

"You said," she went on, stepping closer, her bare feet slapping wet stone, "you said the god eats memory. You said it ate a whole country because nobody remembered them hard enough. And now you're going to walk up to it and hand it *yourself*? Your whole soul? Everything?" She was crying now, furiously, the way she cried when she was angriest, which meant she barely felt it. "That's not sealing a door. That's *feeding* it. You're just — you're setting the table and lying down on the plate."

Orsen finally looked up. His eyes were wet too, and that was somehow worse, because Orsen never cried, Orsen was the steadiest thing in her whole life, steadier than the gate, steadier than the seasons.

"Wren," he said softly. "There's no other way. The seal is failing. I'm the lock. Locks don't get to choose."

"Then be something else."

"There's nothing else to be."

And that — *that* — was the moment Wren stopped arguing and started acting, because she knew, the way children know things, that words had already lost. He'd decided. Somewhere in those three quiet days he'd folded himself up small and made his peace, and peaceful people could not be argued with. They could only be interrupted.

She turned and dug into the satchel she'd carried across an entire erased country without telling anyone why it was so heavy.

* * *

The seeds had come from the valley garden. She'd taken them the night before they left, kneeling in the dark among the beds that bloomed out of season, filling a waxed linen pouch with everything she could find: marigold, memory-moss, the pale blue flowers that grew thickest along the gate's shadow and which Orsen had never named because, he'd admitted once, embarrassed, he didn't know their name — they'd simply always been there, growing toward the iron like they were waiting for something.

She'd told herself she took them because gardens needed replanting. She'd told herself lots of sensible things. But her hands had known, even then, what her heart was only now catching up to.

"What are you doing?" Sable asked hoarsely from the pillar.

Wren didn't answer. She waded to the threshold of the First Door — the water there was ankle-deep and cold as a held breath — and knelt down in front of it, close enough to touch the ancient wood, though she didn't. Instead she opened the pouch and began, with great deliberation, to plant.

There wasn't soil. There was stone, and still water, and the dust of a forgotten country. So she worked the seeds into the cracks between the flagstones, pressing each one down with her thumb the way Orsen had taught her — *not too deep, Wren-bird, seeds are shy, they want to know the sky is close* — and she talked while she did it, because Orsen also taught her that plants liked being spoken to, and if it worked for marigolds then it would work for gods.

"Doors should be gardens," she said, loud enough that her voice rang off the pillars and came back to her doubled. "Not graves. Not prisons. Gardens. Because a grave is somewhere you put things you've given up on, and a prison is somewhere you put things you're scared of, and both of those are just — just giving up with extra steps. But a garden—" Her voice wobbled. She planted another seed. "A garden is somewhere you keep coming back. Every day. Even when nothing's growing yet. *Especially* when nothing's growing yet. You don't give up on a garden. You can't. That's the whole point of it."

"Wren-bird." Orsen had risen. He was standing behind her, and she could hear the grief in his voice, all wrapped up in tenderness, and she refused to turn around because if she saw his face she would lose her nerve, and she was not going to lose her nerve. "Those won't grow here. There's no light. No soil. Nothing for them to hold onto."

"They held onto you, didn't they?" she shot back. "Back home? In a valley where magic can't even get in? They bloomed out of season for *years*. They grew a crown and a burning city and a lady's face, and nobody planted them, and nobody tended them right, and they did it anyway." She pressed the last seed into a crack directly before the Door's center, where the wood met the stone, where the seal ran thinnest. "They're the stubbornest things alive. Just like you. Just like us."

The water shivered.

It was a tiny movement, a ring spreading from where her finger touched the flood, but in that hall of perfect stillness it was thunderous. Above them, in the drowned dark, something shifted — the vast slow attention of the devourer-god turning its hunger toward this small kneeling child like an eye opening.

Maren moved first. Wren heard her mother's boots splash and then her mother was beside her, kneeling in the cold water, sleeves pushed up, taking seeds from the pouch without being asked. "Marigolds," Maren said firmly, to the Door, to the god, to the whole drowning dark. "My grandmother grew them. Orange as sunset. If you're going to haunt my daughter's garden, you'll do it properly."

"Mama—" Wren's breath caught.

"Hush, love. I'm planting."

Sable dragged herself off the pillar, gray-faced, one arm clamped to her side, and lowered herself down at the threshold's edge with a grunt of pain. "This is insane," she rasped. "You're planting flowers in front of a god that devoured a nation." She took a seed anyway. "I knew a shrine-keeper in the borderlands who grew thyme. Said it kept the bad dreams off. Can't hurt." She pressed it into the stone with a grimace that might have been a smile.

And then the dead arrived.

* * *

Wren heard them before she saw them: footsteps on stairs that had been underwater for centuries, unhurried, wet, *real*. She turned, and the innkeepers of the Inn of Borrowed Names were descending into the flooded hall — Halewick with his bar-towel over his shoulder, Ostrelle with her flour-white hands, old Tam leaning on young Tam, Bramma Vey carrying a lantern that burned underwater without a flicker. Behind them came others, dozens, scores — the erased of Vessany, the mortar of the seal, walking out of the dark because a girl's mother had spent a whole night learning their names and refusing to forget a single one.

They should not have been able to leave the inn. Wren understood that dimly, the way she understood most magic: it was about thresholds, and thresholds belonged to whoever loved them enough to tend them. The inn had been theirs. Now, maybe, so was this.

Halewick stopped at the edge of the water and looked at the three of them kneeling in the flood, seeds in their fingers, and his craggy face did something complicated. "Well," he said. "In two hundred years, no one's ever planted anything here."

"Someone has to," Wren said. "Everyone keeps trying to end things. Seal it, lock it, feed it, bury it. Nobody ever tries to *grow* anything."

Ostrelle laughed — a short, surprised sound, like bread crust cracking. Then she hitched up her skirts and waded in, and knelt, and began clearing debris from the cracks between the stones with her flour-white hands, making room for roots. One by one the dead followed. They came into the water and knelt at the threshold of the Door that had eaten their country, and they planted.

Bramma Vey sang as she worked — low, wordless, a tune that sounded like it had been waiting two hundred years for a reason. Old Tam told the stone about the orchards of Vessany, how the pears grew sweet as honey if you talked to them through the winter. Young Tam described the terraced vineyards above the capital, and the wind that came off the mountains smelling of rain and iron. Names poured out of them, and places, and small beloved details — the way the baker's wife hummed, the color of the schoolroom shutters, the dog who waited every day at the ferry landing — and with every detail the water warmed, just slightly, just noticeably, like a body remembering it had once been warm.

The devourer-god watched. Wren felt it watching, a pressure like deep water, like the moment before lightning. It was confused. She could feel its confusion the way you feel weather changing. It had spent eons eating prayers, eating memories, eating whole countries, and nothing in its long terrible diet had prepared it for people who knelt down in front of it and *gardened*.

The seeds began to sprout.

Not slowly. Not the way seeds were supposed to. Green split the stone cracks like the world cracking its knuckles, and pale shoots rose, and buds swelled, and the first flower opened — a marigold, orange as sunset, absurd and blazing in the drowned dark — and then another, and another, until the threshold of the First Door was fringed with bloom, a ragged defiant border of living color laid down like a challenge.

Orsen stood very still, and Wren finally turned to look at him.

He was staring at the flowers with an expression she'd never seen on him before. Not the keeper's calm. Not the lock's grim resolve. Something younger. Something that might have belonged to a prince who had once volunteered to be unmade because he couldn't imagine any other ending — staring now at an ending nobody had imagined at all.

"That's not possible," he whispered.

"You keep saying that," Wren said, standing, wiping her muddy hands on her dress. "You said it about the rain. You said it about the garden remembering. You said it about the inn. You're wrong a lot, for someone who's been alive for hundreds of years." She crossed the water to him and took his hand — the one with the sigil-mark, the lock's mark, the mark that had never once been a birthmark — and pressed a seed into his palm and closed his fingers over it. "So stop deciding how stories end. You're not the ending. You're the *keeper*. You were always the keeper. Mama said so, and Mama found you, and Mama doesn't lie about important things, only about burnt bread."

Behind the Door, in whatever place the devourer-god lived, something vast exhaled. The pressure eased — not gone, never gone, but changed, the way a starving animal changes when food is offered gently instead of seized. The flowers burned brighter. The dead of Vessany kept singing.

And beneath Wren's feet, in the crack where she'd planted the last seed, something answered back: a single new shoot, rising straight up toward a sun that hadn't existed five minutes ago, unfurling leaves that shimmered like the surface of still water.

The Door groaned. Not opening. *Listening.*

Then, from everywhere and nowhere, from the water and the stone and the space between the pillars, a voice — old beyond old, hungry beyond hunger, and yet, for the first time in eons, uncertain:

"*What is it you want, little gardener?*"

Wren looked up at the dark, at the god that had eaten a country, at the thing wearing her favorite person's silhouette, and she said the truest thing she knew:

"I want you to stay. Not locked. *Stay.* Like a garden stays. Where people keep coming back."

The silence that followed was the longest of her life.

Then the water began to rise — not in flood, but in light — and Orsen's hand tightened around the seed, and somewhere far above them, at the top of the drowned stair, dawn broke over a country that was beginning, name by name, to remember itself.
