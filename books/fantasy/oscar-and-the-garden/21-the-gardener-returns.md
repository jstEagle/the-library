## 21. The Gardener Returns

The valley announced itself before Orsen could see it.

It came as a smell first—loam and cut hay, woodsmoke from Maren's chimney, the particular mineral sweetness of the stream that ran behind the gate. Then a sound: the low hush of the pines on the western ridge, a sound he had heard every morning for thirty years without once thinking to call it beautiful. He stopped on the last bend of the road with the pack heavy on his shoulders and the cuttings wrapped in wet cloth against his chest, and he let the valley arrive in him all at once, like a letter long delayed.

He had walked out of this valley through an iron gate at moonset, carrying almost nothing, and half of himself had burned away at the threshold. He was walking back in through the same gate at moonset, months later, carrying more than he had ever owned. The symmetry was not lost on him. But then, so little was lost on him now. That was the strange inheritance of everything they had done beneath Vessany's drowned capital: nothing slipped past him anymore. Every sensation arrived whole, named, claimed. He knew now what it cost to be a man who noticed everything, and he paid it gladly, daily, like rent on a house he had finally been allowed to own.

Maren saw him first. She was standing in her dooryard with a lamp unlit in her hand—waiting, he realized, she had been waiting since dusk—and when their eyes met across the darkening field she did not run to him or shout. She simply set the lamp down on the fence post where its light could find him, and stood there, and let him come the rest of the way himself. It was the most Maren thing she could have done. Some loves announce themselves. Hers had always just quietly made room.

"You're thinner," she said when he reached the gate of her yard.

"I've been eating," he said. "Just not well."

"Then you'll eat well tonight." Her voice caught only slightly, on the word *well*, and he understood why. *Well* was a word that meant something different now. They had all come back changed in their bodies' small ledger—Wren taller by two fingers' width, Sable walking with a stick and a new gravity, Maren herself grayer at the temples than the seasons could account for. And Orsen had come back with a face that was and was not the face he'd left with. The false memories were gone, fed to the seal like seed-corn given back to the earth. What remained was thinner soil but honest: thirty years of bread shared over fences, a child's shadow following him between the bean rows, a woman who'd found a nameless young man at a gate and decided, without ceremony, that he would be family.

That was his now. All of it. Aurel of Vessany lived in him too—the prince who had volunteered to be unmade, who had watched his country poured like mortar into a prison-wall—but Aurel was no longer a weight he carried alone. He was a room in the house. Orsen visited him some evenings. He left flowers there.

"Wren's asleep," Maren said, "but she made me promise to wake her if you came tonight and not tomorrow."

"Then we should hurry," Orsen said, "before she wakes on her own and accuses us both."

* * *

She woke on her own, of course. Children always know. He had barely gotten his boots off in Maren's kitchen when small feet hit the floorboards above, and then Wren was down the stairs in her nightdress, hair wild, stopping three steps from the bottom as though she needed to confirm with her own eyes that the door in front of her was the real one and not one of the shimmering second doors she alone had always seen.

"It's you," she said, in the flat, certain voice she used for facts. "You're both of you. I can see it now. You look like Orsen but you stand like somebody older."

"That's the knees," Orsen said. "The road is hard on knees."

She crossed the kitchen and pressed her forehead against his chest, right over the place where the cuttings rode against his heart in their damp wrappings, and she stayed there a long moment. He put his hand on her hair. Above her head, Maren wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and pretended to be checking the stove.

"I kept your garden alive," Wren said into his shirt. "Mostly. The roses sulked but they didn't die. I talked to them every day like you said. I told them about Vessany even though they weren't there." She pulled back and looked up at him fiercely, daring him to laugh. "I think they liked hearing about it. Things like knowing they're thought of. Even plants. Especially plants."

"Especially plants," Orsen agreed gravely. "We'll go see them in the morning. Tonight—" he looked at Maren, who nodded, already reaching for bread and the good butter, the honey from the high comb— "tonight we eat."

And they did. It was nothing, that meal. Bread and butter and honey, tea that Maren brewed too strong because grief and joy both made her heavy-handed, an argument between Wren and Maren about whether honey counted as dessert. It was nothing, and it was everything, and somewhere in the middle of it Orsen understood with sudden, blinding clarity that this was the thing the devourer-god had never been able to digest—not because it was grand, but because it refused to be anything other than exactly what it was. Ordinary love does not perform itself. It cannot be eaten because it will not hold still long enough to be consumed; it keeps making more of itself, endlessly, out of nothing, out of burnt bread and scraped knees and arguments forgiven before the plates are cleared.

Sable would have called that counter-magic. Sable, resting at the shrine-house on the far side of the village with her leg propped and her rival god's service finally, fully renounced, would have used a fancier word. But Orsen was a gardener before he was anything else, and gardeners know that the fancy words are mostly mulch. Underneath, it was simple: you tend what you love, and what you tend, grows.

* * *

In the morning he went to the gate.

The cuttings had traveled wrapped in river-moss from the First Door's garden—that impossible terrace beneath the drowned capital where green things grew in the dark by the light of returned names—and he had carried them the whole way home pressed to his chest like a second heartbeat. Now he knelt in the walled garden beside the iron gate, in soil he had turned for thirty years, and began to plant.

The garden had not sulked in his absence so much as waited. Wren's care showed everywhere—a bean row staked with scrupulous fairness, weeds pulled but pulled hesitantly, as if uncertain of their guilt. But the flowers had grown strange in the months since the old gods resumed answering prayers. There was a bed near the wall that bloomed in the shape of a crown, still. Another that held the ghost-shape of burning towers. A patch of white flowers that arranged themselves, petal by petal, into a woman's face—Aurel's mother, Orsen knew now, though the knowledge sat gently in him, like a portrait hung in a room he rarely entered. These were the memories the garden had remembered for him when he could not remember himself. He did not pull them up. But he planted the new cuttings among them, deliberately, so that the old shapes would have company.

"What are they?" Wren asked, crouched beside him, dirt already on her nose. She had appointed herself assistant within moments of arriving and had accepted no objections, though none had been offered.

"They're from the First Door," Orsen said. "From the garden growing at the threshold of the oldest prison in the world—which isn't a prison anymore." He pressed a cutting into the soil and firmed the earth around it with his thumbs. "These flowers grow in places where something terrible was made into something tended. That's their whole nature. They don't forget what happened. They just insist on growing anyway."

"Like the inn," Wren said. The Inn of Borrowed Names, whose dead staff had traded hospitality for remembrance, and who now—remembered by name in a hundred mouths across two countries—had woken in sunlight, alive again, blinking, astonished, setting out bread in a dining room that existed on no map because maps had learned to find them again.

"Like the inn," Orsen said. "Like Alderbrook." The hamlet whose people had been eaten whole, and whose empty houses now had smoke rising from chimneys again, because memory returns when someone carries it home and refuses to set it down. "Like you and me."

Wren considered this with the seriousness she gave all horticultural matters. "The gate," she said. "Are you going to open it now? Since it's not a lock anymore. Since you're not a lock."

Orsen looked at the iron gate.

It had hummed under his palm once—the first tremor of the returning gods, the first crack in the valley's long silence. It did not hum now. It stood quiet and solid and shut, and it would stay shut, and this was the part he had thought about the whole long road home, turning it over like a stone until he understood what was written underneath.

"No," he said. "It stays shut."

Wren frowned. "But—"

"But not the way it used to." He laid his hand flat against the iron, and where his sigil-marked wrist touched the metal there was no binding now, no seal, no ache of enforced stillness. Only contact. Only a hand on a door. "Once this gate was shut because something inside was forbidden to leave and something outside was forbidden to enter. It was a wall wearing a door's shape. Now—" he pushed, gently, and felt the gate give a half-inch and stop, not locked but *choosing*, the way a sleeping cat chooses not to move— "now it's shut the way my workshop door is shut at night. The way a greenhouse is shut in winter. There's nothing in here being punished, Wren. There's a garden being kept. And gardens need doors, and doors need to close sometimes, and that's all a closed door has to mean."

Inside the gate, beyond the walled garden, the air shimmered very faintly—the threshold-place they had made together at the end, the space where a god starved on stolen memory had learned to take root instead, to be watered with true things, to grow slow and strange toward a shape none of them could predict. The devourer was still there. Would always be there, probably. But it was not caged. It was *cultivated*, and the difference was the entire story, and someday—Wren's someday, or her children's—it might bloom into something no prayer had ever imagined.

"The pilgrims will come," Wren said. "Sable says they're already coming. People who prayed and got answers, people who lost villages and got them back. They'll want to knock."

"Let them knock," Orsen said. "Knocking is allowed. Knocking is practically gardening—you make a noise, you wait, you see what grows." He smiled and ruffled her hair, and she ducked away from it and then, traitorously, leaned back into it. "And when they knock, you'll answer. Because you're going to learn this place, Wren. All of it. Which flowers remember, which ones only dream, which ones must never be asked direct questions before breakfast. I'm going to teach you everything, and then one day you'll keep the gate, and you'll shut it at night without locking it, and you'll understand that those are different things."

Wren was quiet for a moment, working at something. Then: "What should I call you? Maren asked me not to ask until you were ready. Are you ready?"

Ah. There it was. The question he had carried from Vessany like a stone in his boot, taking it out each night to examine by firelight. *Aurel*, the drowned capital had called him, the erased dead whispering their prince's name with such tenderness it had nearly broken him. *Aurel*, the reflection in the ruined well had shown, the true face beneath the keeper's. And *Orsen*—Orsen was a name invented by a predecessor's desperate kindness, a false label on a false life, the sort of name you give a stray.

But the false life had not been false. That was the discovery at the bottom of everything. The bread had been really shared. The child had been really loved. The garden had been really, stubbornly, season after season, kept alive by hands that did not know why they were keeping it. You could build a lie around a truth, and if you built it carefully enough and loved inside it long enough, the truth would grow up through the lie like a rose through a crack, until the crack itself became part of the flower.

"Maren found me at this gate," Orsen said slowly, "and gave me a name out of thin air, because everyone deserves a name and I didn't have one. Everyone I love has said that name ten thousand times. It's in the garden soil. It's in the way Wren says it when she's found something blooming out of season and comes running." He touched the sigil on his wrist—Aurel's mark, the prince's burden, honored now and at rest. "Aurel died at this gate, in a manner of speaking. He gave himself away so completely there wasn't enough left over to carry a name. What grew back in the empty place was someone new, and someone new was named by his family, and I choose what my family chose."

He looked at Wren, whose eyes had gone very bright and very wide.

"Orsen," he said. "My name is Orsen Lockridge. Keeper of the gate. Gardener. Yours."

Wren threw herself at him hard enough to rock him back on his heels, and somewhere behind them Maren, who had absolutely not been eavesdropping from the kitchen window, blew her nose with tremendous dignity and went to put the kettle on.

* * *

Evening came, and with it the ritual—or rather, the beginning of one, because rituals have to start sometime and this seemed as good an hour as any.

Orsen stood at the gate as the sun went down behind the western pines. On the other side of it, faint as breath on glass, the threshold-garden glimmered, and in its depths something vast and patient turned toward him the way all growing things turn toward light. Not hungry. Not yet gentle, either. Learning. He had promised it cultivation and he intended to keep the promise; you did not abandon a planting, whatever it had been before it went into the ground.

"Goodnight," he told it, through the iron. His voice was easy. Unafraid. "There's rain coming tomorrow—I can smell it, the good kind, the kind that's only weather. I'll leave the south bed to you. Mind the aphids."

The shimmer deepened, very slightly. If a god could be embarrassed at being reminded about aphids, Orsen thought, this one managed it.

He latched the gate—not locked, latched, the latch a courtesy rather than a command—and turned away, and stopped.

Because there, on the outside of the iron, low down where a kneeling person might reach it, a bud had formed overnight. It had not been there that morning. It was small and tightly furled and utterly unfamiliar, and it grew directly from the seam where the gate met the wall, as though the boundary itself had decided to flower.

Orsen crouched to look at it. He did not touch it. Gardeners know better than to rush a first bloom.

The bud was the color of dawn on the day he had walked out of the valley, and it was trembling—no, not trembling. *Opening.* Slowly, petal by patient petal, in the last of the light, while behind him Wren's voice called that supper was ready and Maren's lamp came alight in the window and the valley settled into its long, kind dark.

By the time the first star appeared, the flower was open. And inside it, written in gold so fine it might have been pollen, was a single line of script in a language no living tongue remembered—

a language Orsen found, to his astonishment, that he could read.

It said: *thank you.*

And beneath it, smaller, fresher, as if only just decided: *what shall we grow next?*

Orsen laughed aloud, alone in the blue evening with his hand over his heart where the cuttings slept, and turned toward the lamplit window, and the smell of bread, and the voices that meant *home*.
