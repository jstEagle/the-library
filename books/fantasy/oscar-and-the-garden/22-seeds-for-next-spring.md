## 22. Seeds for Next Spring

The letter began the way all of Wren's letters began, with ink on her fingers and the morning still cool enough to see her breath.

*Dear Sable,* she wrote, *the frost came early this year, and Orsen says that means we'll have a long spring, though I think he only says it because he wants it to be true. The garden is asleep under its first white blanket. Even the remembering-flowers have closed their eyes. I checked on the god this morning before breakfast. He's doing well.*

She paused, pen hovering, and smiled at her own sentence. Anyone else reading it would have thought her mad. But Sable would understand. Sable, three valleys east and half a country away now, tending a shrine of her own—the one she'd built from the wreckage of her old allegiance, all whitewash and honest stone. Sable, who wrote back every month in a cramped, blade-slanted hand, always beginning *To the Keeper-in-Training* as if it were a title Wren hadn't earned yet, and always ending *P.S. Tell Orsen he still owes me a proper apology for the thornbush incident*, which was her way of saying *I love you both* without having to say it.

Wren dipped her pen again.

*He's been resting deeper this season. Orsen says that's what cultivation looks like from the outside—not much happening, roots going down instead of up. I sat by his bed of soil for an hour yesterday and told him about the innkeepers' letters. He likes those best, I think. When I talk about names, the ground gets warm.*

Outside the little writing room's window, the valley lay quiet under snow. The gate stood where it had always stood, iron and patient at the end of the walled garden, and it was shut—but Wren knew now, the way you know your own heartbeat, that *shut* was not the same word it used to be. A prison shuts to keep something in. A door shuts because the people inside are busy, or resting, or not ready for visitors. The gate had learned the difference. So had everyone who passed through it.

Her mother's voice drifted up from the kitchen below, along with the smell of bread—slightly burnt, the way Maren's bread always was, the way Wren secretly hoped it always would be. Some imperfections were load-bearing. She'd learned that from watching her mother stand in a court built of eaten prayers and offer the devourer-god a memory of ruined loaves, and watching the great hunger flinch from it like a man from sunlight.

*My mother says hello,* Wren wrote. *She still burns the bread. She says it's tradition now.*

* * *

Orsen found her an hour later, as he always did, following the sound of her scratching pen the way he'd once been followed by a small determined shadow. He was older now—or looked it, which Wren had learned was not always the same thing. His hair had gone grey at the temples in the ordinary way, but his hands hadn't aged at all: strong, scarred, earth-dark at the knuckles, hands that had held a gate shut with nothing but their own stubbornness and then, later, held seeds over the threshold of a drowned capital while a child refused to let him die.

"You're writing to Sable," he said, reading it upside down with the ease of long practice.

"I'm writing to Sable."

"Tell her the thornbush was self-defense."

"It wasn't. You planted it across her shrine path on purpose."

"I planted it *near* her path," Orsen said with dignity, "and it grew with *enthusiasm*. That's not my fault. Everything in this garden grows with enthusiasm. It's in the soil." He crouched beside her chair, and for a moment Wren saw him as the pilgrims sometimes saw him when they came through the valley on the new road—the road that existed now, that appeared on maps, that led travelers past a walled garden where flowers bloomed out of season and no one minded anymore. They always asked if he was the famous gardener, the one from the stories, the lock that became a man that became a keeper after all. And he always said the same thing: *I'm just the one who waters it.*

But Wren had seen his face in the well-water too, once, when she was small. She knew Prince Aurel of Vessany lived in there somewhere, folded up inside Orsen Lockridge like a letter inside an envelope, and she knew which name Orsen chose every single morning, and she understood that this choosing was the bravest thing anyone in the valley did, braver than gates and gods and courts of eaten prayers. Choosing, daily, to be the person your family named you.

"What are you teaching me today?" she asked, capping her ink.

"Patience," said Orsen.

"We did patience last week."

"And how did it go?"

"I got bored and dug up the east bed to check on the bulbs."

"Then we're doing it again." But he was smiling. He took her out into the cold with the snow squeaking underfoot, to the row of bare roses along the south wall, and handed her the pruning shears the way another teacher might hand over a sword. "Show me."

Wren studied the canes. This part she knew—knew in her wrists and eyes, the way her mother knew bread and Sable knew the weight of a blade turned aside. "Dead wood first." She snipped a grey cane, showed him the brown center. "Then anything crossing or crowding. Then—" she hesitated at a green shoot, healthy-looking, reaching the wrong way, "—then the hard part."

"Go on."

"If I cut this, the rose will be angry all spring but grow better for it. If I don't, it'll tangle itself and bloom less and break in the wind." She looked up at him. "You're supposed to say something wise here."

"I'm supposed to let you figure out that wisdom is mostly just being willing to hurt something a little now so it doesn't break later." He shrugged. "Also: cut above the bud, angled away, clean stroke. Hesitation makes wounds."

She made the cut. Clean. The rose stood barer and truer against the snow.

"There are three kinds of flowers here," Orsen said, walking the row with his hands clasped behind his back, and Wren fell into step because she always did, shadow-turned-apprentice, apprentice-turning-something-new. "Ordinary ones. They grow, they bloom, they die, they come back. Then the remembering ones—you know them, the ones that shape themselves into faces and cities and crowns if you let them run wild. They hold what the valley lost and what Vessany got back. We prune them gently and never for shape, only for health. Their memories aren't ours to edit."

"And the third kind?"

Orsen stopped at the far corner of the garden, where the wall met the gate, where the soil was darkest and richest and warm even through the snow. Nothing was planted there. Nothing needed to be. But the ground had a settled quality, like a sleeping animal, like a house where someone is home.

"The third kind isn't a flower," Orsen said quietly. "It's whatever chooses to rest here. Gods, mostly. Small ones lately—a threshold god who naps under the honeysuckle and charges travelers one pleasant memory apiece for safe passage, which Wren, before you ask, is legal, we checked. And one large one." He laid his palm flat on the dark soil, the way he'd once laid it on the iron gate and felt it hum. Now the hum was different. Slower. Contented. "He was starving for eons, you know. Not for prayers—for real things. Burnt bread. Forgiven arguments. Somebody's actual unremarkable Tuesday. My mother—" he caught himself, and Wren's chest did the thing it always did when he said *my mother* about a woman dead a thousand years, "—my mother taught me that hunger like that doesn't need to be fought. It needs to be fed. Properly. Patiently. Like anything else you'd garden."

"So we feed him Tuesdays."

"We feed him Tuesdays." Orsen grinned, sudden and young. "And harvest festivals, and arguments about fence lines, and the sound of you practicing your letters badly, and Maren's bread. He's grown very fond of burnt bread. Says it tastes like honesty."

Wren laughed, and her breath made a small ghost in the cold air, and beneath their feet something vast and ancient and once-terrible shifted in its sleep, the way a dog shifts closer to the fire.

* * *

That afternoon the pilgrims came.

They came most weeks now, in ones and twos, up the new road with its waystones carved in two alphabets—the valley's and Vessany's, side by side, neither erased. Some came for miracles. Those Wren gently redirected, because the valley didn't really do miracles anymore; the old gods had receded to shrines and small favors, exactly as promised, and mostly what they granted these days was finding lost buttons and good parking for carts. But some pilgrims came for the other thing, the rarer thing, and those Wren brought through the garden gate herself.

They came to remember. Or to be remembered.

An old woman from Alderbrook—that was common, Alderbrook folk came often, still hungry for confirmation that they existed—stood at the remembering-beds and watched her grandmother's face bloom in a cluster of white flowers, petals arranged just so around a dark center, unmistakable. She wept. Wren stood beside her and said the names aloud with her, because that was the practice, that was the whole liturgy: someone says the name, and the flower holds it, and between the two of them the person stays real.

A thin man in traveling clothes asked, shyly, if the garden might hold a name he'd carried a long way—one of the innkeepers, he said, from the Inn of Borrowed Names, a woman called Ellery who had given him soup once in a country that wasn't on any map, and asked nothing but to be remembered, and he'd forgotten her for eleven years and only lately remembered, and couldn't forgive himself. Wren took his hands. "She's already here," she said. "Maren memorized them all, that night. Every name. They're in the beds, and in the chapel book, and in me—I've been learning them since I was six. Ellery sleeps under the blue ones. Come say hello."

By dusk the garden had collected four names spoken aloud, two offerings of bread (one burnt, deliberately, for the big sleeper), and a prayer for rain next spring that Wren passed along to the honeysuckle-shade god, who charged a button and delivered within the week, moderately, sensibly, smelling of nothing older than weather. That was the arrangement now. The gods answered, but they answered like neighbors. It turned out that was all anyone had ever wanted from them.

* * *

Winter deepened. Letters went out and came back.

Sable wrote that her shrine had its first permanent resident, a minor river goddess who had grown tired of wandering and liked the whitewashed walls, and that she'd taken on a student of her own, a sharp-eyed girl who reminded Sable uncomfortably of herself at that age, God help everyone. *Tell your Orsen,* she wrote, *that I've forgiven the thornbush. Tell him I haven't forgotten it. There's a difference, and he of all people should know.*

The innkeepers wrote—or rather, the living descendants of the innkeepers wrote, from the sunlit town where the drowned capital's survivors had resettled, where the First Door's garden had become a public park with a fountain and a plaque Wren found slightly too solemn. *The threshold blooms early this year,* they reported. *Your cuttings have taken. Aurel's roses are everywhere. People leave bread at the Door on Tuesdays. We don't remember why it must be Tuesdays, but we do it anyway, and the bread is always gone by morning.* Wren read that line aloud to Orsen and they looked at each other for a long moment, and neither of them said anything, and neither of them needed to.

Maren, for her part, wrote no letters, because Maren had never seen the point of writing to people you could shout at across a garden. But she taught Wren the bread, properly now, the whole inheritance of it—flour and water and salt and patience, and the secret final ingredient, which was letting it burn a little on purpose, because a household that could laugh at its own failures was a household no hunger could breach. "This is the recipe that killed a god's court," Maren said, sliding the dark loaf from the oven, and Wren said, "Mother, it didn't *kill* him, we *gardened* him," and Maren said, "Same thing in this family," and they laughed until the windows fogged.

On the longest night of winter, the whole family gathered in the kitchen—Orsen and Maren and Wren, and Sable arrived on the evening coach with snow in her hair and her student in tow, wide-eyed, clutching a gift for the garden—and they ate burnt bread and stewed apples and argued about whether the honeysuckle god was overcharging pilgrims, and afterward Wren stood at the window watching snow fall on the gate, and felt the whole valley breathing around her like a sleeping animal, like a house where everyone is home.

* * *

In spring, Orsen began teaching her the last lessons.

Not pruning. Not patience. The other things—the things that had no technique, only weight. How to sit with a pilgrim whose grief had no name. How to tell the difference between a prayer that wanted answering and a prayer that only wanted witnessing. How to feel, palm flat against the dark soil, whether the big sleeper was dreaming peacefully or turning toward some old hunger, and what to do in either case (in the first case: nothing, just keep him company; in the second case: burnt bread, spoken names, and if it was very bad, send for Sable, whose presence apparently reminded him of the moment a blade turned aside, which he found soothing).

"One day this will be yours," Orsen said, on a bright morning with the remembering-beds opening their first impossible blooms—a crown here, a woman's face there, a city burning gently and forever in a border of orange poppies, beautiful now, mourned properly, tended. "The gate, the garden, all of it. Not because you're a lock. You're not. Nobody is, anymore. Because you love it, and loving it is the whole job."

"What was the job before?"

"Fear," Orsen said simply. "Fear dressed up as duty. I did it for decades and I was good at it, and it nearly ate me. You'll do it better, because you'll do it for love, and love prunes instead of imprisoning." He pressed the pruning shears into her hands—her own pair now, bought with her own saved coins, handles worn already to the shape of her grip. "Cut above the bud. Angled away. Clean stroke."

"Yes, teacher."

"Don't call me teacher, it makes me feel ancient."

"You *are* ancient."

"I'm thirty-nine hundred years old and none of your business." But he was laughing, and the gate behind him stood open—not unlocked, not broken, just open, because it was a fine spring day and the garden had visitors coming, and doors that are places rather than prisons can afford to stand open when the weather's kind.

Wren walked the rows alone that evening, as she did every evening now, her evening round, her inheritance in motion. She spoke the names. She fed the sleeper his Tuesday bread, early, because he'd earned it. She checked the honeysuckle (napping), the threshold god's ledger (balanced), the remembering-beds (blooming out of season, gloriously, as always). At the far corner she paused, as she always paused, and laid her palm on the warm dark soil.

"Goodnight," she said. "Spring's coming. There'll be Tuesdays."

Beneath her hand, slow and vast and gentle as a tide, something that had once devoured prayers breathed out, and settled, and rested—in a garden, in a place instead of a prison, tended by a girl with ink on her fingers and a family that burned its bread on purpose, in a valley where the candles flickered however they pleased, and the prayers were answered like favors between neighbors, and the door stayed shut only because everyone inside was home.
