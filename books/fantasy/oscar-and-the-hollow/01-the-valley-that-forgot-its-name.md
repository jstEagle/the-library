## 1. The Valley That Forgot Its Name

The Hollow woke Zephyrine the way it always did: with the smell of wet stone and the sound of nothing.

No birds. The valley had never had birds, or if it had, no one remembered them well enough to miss them. Just wind moving through the pines in long, patient breaths, and beneath that the faint hum of the river, which ran cold and clear and utterly without fish. She lay a moment in her bedroll at the edge of the ferry town of Lowmark, watching frost feather across the canvas above her, and performed the morning ritual she no longer thought of as strange: she counted what she knew.

Her name was Zephyrine Voss. She was thirty-one years old, though she'd had to do the arithmetic twice last winter and once already this spring. She was sworn to the Warden, god of the valley, who had not spoken in ten years. She was good with a sword, better with a knife, and best of all at walking away from things.

She could not remember her mother's face.

That last one arrived every morning like a stone dropped into still water, and every morning she watched the ripples spread and waited for them to settle before she got up. It wasn't grief, exactly. Grief needed a shape to hold onto, and the memory had gone soft at its edges, then soft in its middle, until now there was only the fact of loss without the thing lost—a tooth socket where a tooth had been, the tongue returning again and again to the absence. She knew her mother had been tall. She knew she'd smelled of tallow and rosemary. She knew—because Sefa had told her, laughing, three winters ago—that she used to sing off-key while kneading bread. But the face itself, the particular architecture of brow and mouth that made one woman out of all the women in the world—

Gone. Eaten. The way the Hollow ate everything, slowly, politely, without malice.

Zephyrine rolled out of the bedroll, dressed in the dark, and went to find work.

* * *

Lowmark sat where the river narrowed enough for a ferry and the road from the southern pass widened enough for carts, which meant it lived on traffic the way a tick lives on a deer. By midmorning the market square smelled of tar, frying onions, and the wet wool of travelers who had crossed the pass overnight. Zephyrine moved through it with the ease of long habit, nodding to faces she knew by role rather than name—the ferryman, the smith, the woman who sold eel pies and whose name changed depending on who you asked, because nobody could hold onto it for long.

That was the first rule of the Hollow, taught to every child before they could walk: *the valley keeps you safe, and the price is your past.* No magic crossed the rim of the mountains. Whatever power the world beyond held—whatever gods, whatever sorceries, whatever hungry intelligences wore crowns or vestments—it stopped dead at the ridgeline like weather against glass. Inside, the Warden's silence filled the valley floor to rim, and in that silence nothing supernatural could live, could see, could reach. Merchants came over the pass fleeing debts and worse. Deserters came. Widows of men killed by things with names too terrible to say. They all came for the same reason Zephyrine's family had come two generations back: because in the Hollow, the monsters of the world were blind.

And because forgetting hurt less than being found.

She found the job at the signpost by the well, where contracts were nailed up like weather notices. A wool merchant named Doss Halloway wanted an escort to Grayfall, the mining town at the valley's far end—four days' travel through the deep Hollow, along the old pilgrim road. Pay was decent. Company was himself and his brother. Risk was wolves, weather, and rockslides, all of which Zephyrine considered honest dangers, the kind that announced themselves.

She took it, as she took most jobs, because routine was armor. Every day with a route and a purpose was a day the Hollow couldn't quietly take stock of her. She'd worked this out years ago, in the early days when she'd still been frightened enough to think about it: a mind in motion kept its possessions. Idle minds were looted. So she walked patrols, took escort contracts, drilled with the company on feast days, recited her inventory of self each dawn—and the erosion slowed. Didn't stop. Slowed.

"Voss." Doss Halloway was a round man with a merchant's smile and a farmer's hands, waiting by two laden mules with a younger version of himself fidgeting at the reins. "You'll start today? Weather's turning."

"Today," she said. "We make the tree line by dusk."

The brother—Pell, she thought, or maybe that was last year's brother; merchants hired new ones the way they bought mules—looked her up and down with the frank appraisal of someone who hadn't yet learned that mercenaries in the Hollow were mostly guards against boredom. "Just us three?" he said. "For four days?"

"Just me," Zephyrine said. "In the Hollow, I'm plenty."

It wasn't arrogance. It was geography. Swords worked fine here—iron didn't care about silence—but the things escorts usually guarded against out in the world didn't exist inside the valley. No hexed roads. No bandit-wizards. No summoned anything. Four days to Grayfall was four days of hard walking and bad cooking, and if you were lucky, nothing else at all.

They left within the hour.

* * *

The pilgrim road climbed out of Lowmark through terraced barley fields gone gold and stubbled, then into pine, then into the high grey country where the trees grew twisted and the stones wore beards of lichen. Shrines dotted the roadside every few miles—little cairns of stacked rock, some ancient and moss-swallowed, some fresh, all of them empty.

That was the thing visitors never understood about the shrines. In the world beyond, a shrine was a house with something living in it. Here, every shrine in the valley was a house with the door standing open and nobody home. Pilgrims still stacked their stones. Farmers still left offerings—bread, buttons, a child's carved horse, once, that Zephyrine had seen weathering on a cairn for six years. They gave to the Warden the way you'd speak at a grave: not expecting an answer, but unable to bear the silence unadorned.

Ten years ago, the Warden had spoken. Everyone old enough remembered it, though the memories had gone strange with age, worn smooth as river stones. He had been a god of thresholds and keeping, they said—keeper of the valley, warden of the door. His voice had been heard in the ringing of the ferry bell and the creak of hinges and the moment of stillness before a storm broke. And then, one autumn day ten years back, he had simply stopped. Not died—gods in the Hollow didn't die, the valley wouldn't permit it—but gone quiet, the way a man goes quiet in the next room. You could feel him there. You could feel him *listening*. He just never answered.

The priests had explained it a hundred ways: a great labor, a holy sleep, a test of faith. Zephyrine had taken her oath in the third year of the silence, kneeling at the great shrine above Lowmark with Sefa beside her, both of them young and certain that devotion was a kind of conversation and theirs would be the one to wake him.

Now she walked the pilgrim road past the little cairns and felt what she always felt passing them: the specific loneliness of speaking to someone who might be listening but would never reply.

"You're sworn to him, aren't you?" Pell said on the second day, jerking his chin at a cairn as they passed. "The silent god."

"I'm sworn," Zephyrine said.

"What's he like? When he—" Pell waved a hand vaguely. "Before. What was it like?"

Doss snorted from the lead mule. "Leave it, boy. Half the people who remember him don't remember him right anymore." He said it comfortably, the way you'd comment on the weather, and that comfort was its own kind of horror. The whole valley had learned to talk about its god in the tone of voice reserved for things that couldn't be helped.

"He was loud," Zephyrine said, after a while. "That's what I remember. Not volume. Presence. Like standing next to a waterfall. You felt him whether you wanted to or not." She paused, testing the memory the way you'd test ice. It held, barely. "I was nine when we came over the pass. He spoke to my mother at the gate shrine. One word. I've spent twenty years trying to keep that word, and I can't even remember what it was."

Pell looked at her with the wide eyes of someone raised on stories where gods answered prayers and heroes got rewards. Doss looked at the road.

"That's the Hollow," the merchant said. "It takes the top layer first. Faces, names, words. Leaves you the shape of a life with the paint peeled off." He clicked his tongue at the mule. "Cheaper than any sanctuary in the world, and the interest rate's murder."

* * *

On the third night, camped in the shadow of the Split Stone—a granite fist the size of a chapel that the old maps marked as the halfway point—Zephyrine woke to the feeling of being watched.

Not by anything. By the valley itself.

She'd felt it before, always in the deep Hollow, always at night: the sensation that the silence had texture, that it pressed against her skin like water against a diver, tasting what passed through it. Superstition, Sefa called it. The company called it Hollow-nerves and treated it with whiskey. But lying there under the indifferent stars, Zephyrine felt the pressure of it and did her counting again, quickly, like a woman checking her purse in a crowd.

Name: Zephyrine Voss. Age: thirty-one. Oath: the Warden's. Skills: blade, bow, wilderness, lies. People: Sefa. Brannoc. The company. Mother: tall, rosemary, off-key singing, one word at a gate shrine—

The face.

She reached for it and found the socket where it had been, smooth and empty, and understood with a clarity that arrived only in the small hours that it was not coming back. This wasn't the usual morning fog that burned off with movement and tea. Something had been taken in the night, quietly, while she slept, and the thief had left no tracks because the thief was the ground she slept on, the air she breathed, the whole patient valley that had sheltered her family for two generations and was eating them for payment, one memory at a time, so gently that most people thanked it for the service.

She sat up. The fire had burned to orange lace. Across the coals, Doss snored; Pell muttered through some dream. Above them the Split Stone stood against the stars, and beyond it the dark shoulders of the mountains closed the valley in, and somewhere up in that darkness, at the rim of the world, magic waited on the other side of the ridge like a wolf at a fence.

Safe, everyone said. Safest place in creation. Nothing from outside can touch you here.

Nothing outside, Zephyrine thought, staring into the coals. But the valley isn't outside. The valley is the wolf.

She didn't sleep again. At dawn she was up first, and she walked the day's march harder than she needed to, setting a pace that made the brothers complain, because motion was armor and she had just lost another piece of herself and intended to make the theft expensive.

* * *

They reached Grayfall on the fifth afternoon, a smoke-stained town stacked up the flank of the mountain with mine adits gaping in the slopes like missing teeth. Doss paid her at the assay office, counted coins twice, and offered her the return trip at the same rate. She declined. There was a faster way home along the eastern wall, alone, and she wanted—needed—the solitude, needed to walk and count and rebuild the walls of herself before she had to stand in front of other people and perform being a person.

The eastern path was narrow, a shepherd's track above the tree line, and she made good time with the evening light going long and copper across the valley floor below. From up here the Hollow looked almost gentle: the river a bright thread, Lowmark a scatter of roofs, the barley fields stitched gold and green. A country-sized cupped hand. A place that kept you.

Her boot dislodged a stone. It clattered down the slope, and the sound of it falling seemed very loud in all that silence, and Zephyrine stopped walking.

Because she had just realized something, and the realization landed in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She had been trying all day to reconstruct her mother's face—not the fact of it, the thing itself—and she had failed. Fine. That failure was five years old; she knew its weight. But somewhere between the Split Stone and here, between yesterday and this evening, she had also lost the sound of her mother's voice. Not the singing—she still had the singing, off-key, rosemary, bread. The *voice*. The ordinary speaking voice, saying ordinary things, calling her in from the rain.

And she could not remember ever having noticed it leave.

That was the dread, she thought, standing on the shepherd's track with the copper light dying and the vast gentle valley breathing below her. Not the losing. The losing you could survive; you survived it daily, the way a man survives bleeding if the wound is slow. The dread was that the Hollow was so *good* at it. So gentle, so thorough, so perfectly silent, that you never caught it in the act. You only ever found the empty sockets afterward, and by then you'd forgotten there had ever been teeth.

She stood there a long time. Then she did what she always did: she counted, she shouldered her pack, and she walked.

She came down into Lowmark after dark, two days later, stiff and hollow-eyed and telling herself the familiar lie—that the route and the pay and the drill would hold the walls another season, that the erosion was manageable, that everyone in the valley bled the same and she was no different.

The town was awake past its usual hour. Torches burned along the ferry landing, and a knot of people had gathered where the boats came in, and their voices carried up the street with an edge she'd never heard in Lowmark before—not festival noise, not argument. Fear, tightly held.

Zephyrine slowed, hand finding her knife hilt out of habit, and pushed toward the light.

At the center of the crowd, half in the water and half on the shingle, lay a man she didn't know, in clothes cut in a style she didn't recognize, bleeding from wounds that looked wrong somehow—too clean, too deliberate—and even from ten yards away she could see that he was dying, and that he was searching the faces above him with desperate, systematic urgency, as if looking for one face in particular.

His eyes found hers.

He stopped searching.
