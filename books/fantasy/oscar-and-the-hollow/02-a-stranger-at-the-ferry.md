## 2. A Stranger at the Ferry

The ferry town of Lowmark had exactly three rules, and Zephyrine Voss had broken none of them in the six years she'd been coming through: don't ask the ferryman where he goes when the fog takes the boat, don't pay with coin minted inside the Hollow, and never—under any circumstances—sing.

She sat on the dock's end with her boots off, letting the grey water slap at her heels, and tried to remember her mother's voice.

It was a thing she did now, a discipline like oiling a blade. Every morning she chose one memory and held it up to the light, turning it, checking for rot. This morning it was her mother singing at the washbasin—a tuneless, wandering thing, half-hummed, that had filled the kitchen of her childhood like smoke fills a room. She could remember the kitchen. She could remember the washbasin, blue enamel chipped at the rim, and the way steam beaded on the single window. But when she reached for the song itself, for the shape of the notes, she found only the space where it should have been, smooth and blank as river stone.

*You knew this yesterday,* she told herself. *Did you?*

That was the trouble with the Hollow. It didn't take memories all at once, the way a wound bleeds. It took them the way water takes salt—slowly, invisibly, until you tasted the cup and couldn't say what had changed. She'd been keeping a ledger for two years now, a small book of things she refused to lose, written out each week in her own hand: *Sefa laughs before the joke lands. Brannoc cheats at cards but badly. The Warden's shrine smells of cold iron.* And beneath those, the older entries, the ones she'd copied from an earlier version of herself without remembering writing them: *My mother's name is ———.*

The blank was still there. Six weeks running.

"Voss." The ferryman, Osk, stood behind her with his hands knotted in his apron, a man shaped like a mooring post and about as expressive. "Cargo came in early. Two crates, sealed, going up to the quarry road by dusk. Company rates."

"I'm not company anymore," she said. "I'm freelance."

"You're whatever pays," Osk said, which was true, and turned back toward his shed.

Zephyrine pulled her boots on. The water was cold even for late autumn, cold in the particular way Hollow water was cold—not biting, but *indifferent*, as if it had never once been warm anywhere. She'd stopped noticing that years ago. That was another thing the valley did. It didn't just eat your past; it ate your standards, your comparisons, your sense that anything had ever been otherwise. You woke up one day unable to remember that rivers were supposed to sing.

She was cinching her sword belt when the screaming started.

Not from the town—from the far bank, across the mile of grey water, where the ferry road came down through the birches to meet the landing. At first she thought it was an animal. Then she thought it was several animals. Then the sound resolved into something worse: a human voice stretched past what a human throat should hold, not screaming *at* anything but screaming *against*, the way a man screams against pain he has decided not to surrender to.

Osk came out of his shed. The two dockhands straightened up from their crates. Down the lane, doors opened along the ferry town's single street, and faces appeared in them, pale and attentive, the whole settlement turning toward the sound with the slow, collective motion of wheat bending under wind.

"He'll want the boat," Osk said quietly. "There's always one who wants the boat."

"The boat doesn't cross for strays," said one of the dockhands. "Fog's wrong today."

The fog *was* wrong. Zephyrine had noticed it an hour ago without naming why: it lay too low on the water, too evenly, like a sheet drawn over furniture in a house where no one lived anymore. In the Hollow, weather was mostly habit. This fog was doing something.

The screaming crossed the water.

She saw him then, or thought she did—a figure on the far shore, stumbling down through the birches, moving with the lurching urgency of a man whose body had been ordered to run long after running had stopped being possible. He fell twice getting to the landing. The second time he didn't get up so much as roll forward, and then he was at the ferry rope, hauling himself hand over hand along it, out over the water, and Zephyrine understood with a lurch that the rope ran slack between banks—that there was no barge attached, no raft, nothing but the rope itself sagging over a mile of indifferent grey—and he was crossing it anyway, on his hands, above open water, because whatever was behind him was worse than drowning.

"No magic in the Hollow," she murmured. "No magic on the water."

But something was following him. She couldn't see it. That was the point of it, she realized—whatever pursued him was made of not-seeing, a hole in the fog shaped like attention, and the birches on the far bank bent away from it in a wide circle, every tree leaning outward like spectators flinching from a blow.

"He can't make it," Osk said. "Rope's rotten on the middle third."

Zephyrine was already moving. She didn't decide to move; her body simply committed, the old mercenary arithmetic running faster than thought—*one man, one rope, one chance, and if the thing follows him across, we're all in its path anyway.* She hit the near landing just as the stranger reached the rotten stretch, felt the rope shudder under his weight, heard the fibers begin their dry, terrible unraveling.

"Let go!" she shouted. "Drop! I'll pull you out!"

He let go. Of course he didn't let go—the rope was everything, the rope was the only solid thing left in his world—but then the middle third parted with a sound like a snapped bowstring, and he dropped fifteen feet into water so cold it closed over him like a lid, and Zephyrine went in after him.

The cold was total. It wasn't temperature; it was *erasure*. The river didn't want her in it, and it communicated this not through pain but through a kind of subtraction—her hands went vague, her last thought went vague, the concept of *shore* went vague—and then her fingers closed on cloth, on an arm, on a jaw that was still moving, still trying to swim, and she hauled him toward the shallows through water that fought her with the patience of geology.

They came out on the shingle together, coughing, and she got an arm under him and dragged him up the bank while the fog behind them lay perfectly, perfectly still.

*It didn't follow,* she thought. *Why didn't it follow?*

Then she looked down at the man in her arms and stopped thinking about the fog at all.

He was maybe fifty, grey-bearded, dressed in traveling leathers gone soft with age, and he was bleeding from everywhere—or nearly everywhere. Not wounds, exactly. When she peeled back his collar she found lines cut into his skin, fine and precise, spiraling inward toward his throat like the grooves of a record, and from each line seeped blood in perfect, unhurried threads. He'd been *scored*. Someone or something had taken a blade to him the way a jeweler takes a loupe to a stone, marking him for removal.

His eyes found hers. They were clear. That was the worst part—clear and present and terribly calm, the eyes of a man who had already done all his panicking somewhere behind him on the road and had arrived, at last, at the far country beyond fear.

"You're of the Hollow," he rasped. His accent was nothing she knew—vowels rounded strangely, consonants placed like stones in a wall. "Good. Good. It can't see in here. It lost me at the water."

"It almost didn't," Zephyrine said. "What is it?"

"A question I don't have time to answer properly." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; blood came up with it, dark and thin. "So I'll answer it badly. It listens for names. Everything it hunts, it hunts by name. And I—" he pressed a hand to his chest, to the spiral grooves, "—I have been a very loud man for a very long time."

Behind them, Osk and the dockhands had come down to the shingle and stopped at a respectful distance, the way men stop at the edge of a grave being dug. The town had come out too. They stood along the lane in their doorways, watching, and none of them moved to help, and none of them looked away, and Zephyrine understood that they were memorizing this—adding it to the town's long inventory of things seen at the ferry and never spoken of again.

"Get him to the chapel," someone said. "Maren keeps bandages."

"There's no time for bandages." The stranger caught Zephyrine's wrist. His grip was astonishing—weak as he was, his fingers closed on her pulse-point with a precision that made her soldier's instincts sit up and bare their teeth. He knew exactly where to hold a person. "Listen to me. You carry a blade and you walk the valley roads and you pray to the quiet one—I saw the mark on your bracer, the Warden's circle, worn nearly smooth. You're oathbound."

"That's my business."

"It became my business the moment you pulled me from the river." He fumbled at his side, at a leather case strapped flat against his ribs under his coat, and his hands weren't working right—they kept missing the buckles, sliding off, and Zephyrine saw that the spiral cuts had reached his fingers, that his hands were failing him joint by joint. "Help me. Please. Take it off me. Take it *off*."

She hesitated one breath longer than she was proud of. Then she drew her knife and cut the straps herself, and the case came free into her hands—leather, scarred, heavier than it looked, warm from riding against a living body for what must have been years.

"What is it?"

"A map." He smiled, and there was blood in his teeth, and the smile was the most frightening thing about him because it was *relieved*. "A map of doors. Seven of them. Out of—" and here his voice did something she had never heard a voice do: it stumbled over a word that wasn't there. His mouth shaped a name, a country's name, and the air declined to carry it, and what came out instead was a pause, a silence shaped exactly like a word, and she watched him grieve that silence in real time. "Out of *there*," he finished. "Out of the place that isn't said. You've never heard of it. No one has. That was the first door, you understand—the erasure itself. They didn't burn it. They didn't conquer it. They *unsang* it."

"Who did?"

"The Chorus." He said the word carefully, laying it down like something explosive. "And they are coming for the map, and after the map, the doors, and after the doors—" His breath hitched. The spiral grooves at his throat had begun to darken. "After the doors, everything that ever escaped them. Which is why it cannot have this. Which is why *you* cannot give it to them."

"Give it to whom, then?"

"To whoever remembers enough to use it." His eyes searched her face, and whatever he found there made him go still. "Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you poor woman. You're nearly empty, aren't you? I can see it. The valley's been drinking you for years." He laughed again, weaker. "Of course. Of course it would be someone like you. Someone with nothing left to lose but the losing."

"I don't want your map," Zephyrine said. "I want you to drink this and tell Maren where it hurts, and in the morning you can find yourself a more literate courier."

"There is no morning." He said it without drama, the way a man reports the weather. "The scoring is a sentence, not a wound. When the spirals close, I close. I have perhaps an hour, and I intend to spend forty minutes of it making sure you understand what you're carrying, and the rest dying as loudly as I can manage, because loudness confuses it." His grip tightened on her wrist. "Swear you'll take it."

"I don't swear to dead men."

"Then swear to the Warden."

The words landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. Ten years of silence, ten years of prayers thrown into a well that never once echoed back, and this stranger—one hour from death, soaked in river water and his own blood—reached past all of it and put his hand on the oldest wound she had.

"You know the Warden," she said.

"I know what he paid." The stranger's voice had begun to slur, the syllables softening at the edges like wax. "I know what he gave up, and I know why the valley is silent, and I know—" a cough took him, long and wet, "—I know that the silence is not what you think it is. None of it is what you think. Read the margins. The map speaks in margins. And when you reach the seventh door—"

He stopped. His eyes had gone past her shoulder, up the bank, to the fog on the water.

It had changed. The stillness had changed—it was no longer the stillness of absence but of *attention*, the whole grey sheet of it drawn taut as skin over a drum, and Zephyrine felt the hair rise on her arms because she understood suddenly that it was listening. Listening for him. For his name, for the loudness of him, for the last bright ringing thread of a scored and dying man.

"The door," the stranger whispered. His hand found the front of her coat and pulled her close, close enough that she could smell the river and the blood and, beneath both, something like ink, like vellum, like a lifetime spent in archives. "The door still opens. Do you hear me? However long it's been shut, however deep they buried it—the door still opens *for the named*. For those who carry a name freely. That's the whole secret. That's all of it. The door still opens for—"

The fog exhaled.

There was no wind, but the fog moved—a single slow contraction, a mile-wide indrawn breath—and every person on the shingle flinched as one, dogs began to howl in the town behind them, and the stranger's eyes rolled back and his grip on her coat went slack.

"No," Zephyrine said. "No—stay with me—"

His lips moved. No sound came out. But she was close enough, close enough to feel the shape of the words against the air, and though the fog had taken his voice it had not, quite, taken the words themselves, and she read them off his mouth the way she'd once read banner-signals across battlefields:

*—the named. Remember me. Remember—*

And then the spirals closed, all at once, from the outside in, like a flower folding shut, and the man in her arms became a weight, and the weight became a body, and the body became, with terrible speed, a thing that had never had a name at all. She felt it happen. She felt the exact moment when looking at his face stopped meaning anything—when the features remained, grey-bearded, calm, ordinary, but the *person* in them drained away like water through sand, leaving behind a stranger so complete that she could not even grieve him properly, because grief requires a name and his was gone.

The fog settled. The dogs stopped. Somewhere in the town, a door closed.

* * *

They buried him at dusk, or rather Osk and the dockhands buried him and Zephyrine stood witness, because the ferry town had rules about strangers and one of them was that whoever pulled a body from the water owned its funeral. There was no marker. Markers required names. Osk suggested carving *Unknown Cartographer* on a plank, and Zephyrine heard herself say, "He wasn't unknown," and heard how strange her own voice sounded saying it, defending a name she hadn't caught, hadn't kept, hadn't thought to write down in the seconds she'd had.

That night she sat in her rented room above the ferry tavern with the leather case on the table in front of her and did not touch it for a long time.

The guilt was a strange architecture. She turned it over in her mind and found it had rooms she didn't expect. She felt guilty for taking the case—except she hadn't taken it; he had forced it on her. She felt guilty for not knowing his name—except knowing it might have killed her, might have painted her on the fog for that listening thing to find. She felt guilty for surviving, which was the oldest guilt she owned, the load-bearing wall of the whole structure, and around it the newer guiltes arranged themselves like fresh masonry: she had promised a dying man nothing, and in promising nothing had accepted everything.

*Read the margins,* he'd said. *The map speaks in margins.*

She unbuckled the case.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle with age, lay a map folded in eight, and the moment she unfolded it she forgot how to breathe.

It was beautiful. It was beautiful the way wounds are sometimes beautiful—precise, deliberate, impossible to look away from. Inked coastlines. Mountain ranges rendered in fine hatching. Roads stitched across the land like sutures. And in the upper corner, where a cartographer would write the country's name, there was instead a blank—a rectangle of untouched paper, edged in gold leaf, waiting for a word that the world had agreed to stop saying.

Seven marks crossed the map, each one a small inked doorway, each annotated in a cramped, slanting hand. Six of the doors lay scattered across countries she knew distantly, names she'd heard in taverns and tollhouses. The seventh was here. Inside the Hollow. Drawn in the valley's heart with a symbol beside it she recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water:

The Warden's circle. The same worn sigil on her own bracer. The god she'd served for ten years, marked on a stranger's map as a *door*.

And beneath the seventh door, in the margin, in handwriting she knew—knew the way you know your own reflection, knew the specific impatient slant of the letters, knew the little hook she always left on the terminal *e*—was written a single line:

*Zephyrine Voss remembers the way in.*

Her hand. Her handwriting. On a map she had never seen, in ink she had never touched, referring to a memory she did not have.

Outside, the fog pressed against the window, patient as debt. Zephyrine Voss sat alone in the lamplight with a dead man's inheritance and her own name staring back at her from a margin, and understood that the Hollow had not been eating her memories at random.

Something had been clearing the ground.
