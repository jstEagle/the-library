## 4. The Oath That Answers Nothing

The shrine to the Warden stood at the top of the ferry town's only hill, and it had been empty for ten years.

Zephyrine climbed it the way she'd climbed it a thousand times before—boots finding the worn hollows in the stone steps without her eyes needing to guide them. Dawn was still deciding whether to arrive. Mist pooled in the valley below like milk poured into a bowl, and somewhere beneath it the river ran on toward the fenlands, carrying its barges and its secrets. She carried the map case against her ribs, strapped flat beneath her coat where it lay against her skin like a second heartbeat she couldn't stop listening for.

She had not slept. That was becoming a habit she could no longer afford.

At the top of the steps, the shrine opened to the sky: four pillars of grey granite, a roof long since fallen or taken, and an altar of black stone that had never once, in all the years she'd prayed at it, shown so much as a scratch. No statue. The Warden had never permitted one—or so the old stories said. *You do not make an image of the one who watches you*, the ferry-town priests used to say, back when there were priests. *You simply leave the seat empty and trust him to fill it.*

Zephyrine knelt on the cold flagstones and did what she had done every morning since she was nine years old, when a one-eyed mercenary sergeant had taught her the words along with how to hold a knife.

"Warden," she said. Her breath fogged in the grey air. "I ask your leave."

It was the oldest form of the oath. Not *protect me*, not *give me strength*—the mercenaries' prayers were practical, transactional, almost rude in their simplicity. *I ask your leave.* Permission to act. And for ten years, the silence that answered had been the same silence it had always been: vast, patient, the silence of a room after someone has just left it. You learned to read your own conscience in it. You learned to treat the quiet as consent, because the alternative was to stop functioning entirely.

Half the company had stopped praying altogether. Brannoc said the god was dead or gone or had never been there at all, and he said it loudly, daring the sky to contradict him. Sefa still mouthed the words sometimes, but Zephyrine had caught her watching the altar with an expression she'd never seen Sefa wear toward anything else—embarrassment, as if they'd all been caught talking to a chair.

But this morning, kneeling with a dead man's map strapped to her body, Zephyrine noticed something she had spent ten years training herself not to notice.

The silence was different.

Not louder. Not softer. *Deliberate.* That was the only word for it, and the word frightened her more than any sound could have. It was the difference between an empty house and a house where someone is standing very still behind a door, listening to you walk past. For ten years the silence had felt like absence. This morning, with the map's leather warm against her ribs, it felt like *attention*.

She pressed her palm flat against the black altar. The stone was cold, colder than the air warranted, cold the way deep water is cold.

"Someone died two nights ago," she said quietly. "He gave me something. I don't know yet if I should keep it."

Nothing. Of course nothing. But the nothing had weight now, and texture, and she found herself thinking—unwillingly, the thought arriving like a splinter working its way up under a fingernail—that the silence had always had weight, and she had simply never been given a reason to feel it before.

"I ask your leave," she said again, and heard, in her own voice, how much the words had changed. They weren't a soldier's transaction anymore. They were a daughter's question, asked of a father who had stopped answering, and she hated herself a little for the tremor in them.

She stayed until her knees ached. Then she rose, brushed the frost from her coat, and went down the hill to lie to her captain about where she'd been.

* * *

"You were at the shrine again."

Brannoc didn't look up from the ledger. He sat in the company's rented room above the Salt Cellar tavern with the table buried under contracts, wage tallies, and a half-eaten breakfast he'd been ignoring since dawn. He was a big man going soft at the edges, grey-shot beard, hands like anchor chain, and he had a talent—rare among captains—for making a single sentence feel like a crossbow leveled at your chest.

"I go to the shrine most mornings," Zephyrine said. "You've never cared before."

"I've never cared because it never mattered." Now he looked up. His eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of river ice, and they held hers with the patience of a man who had watched her grow from a feral child into the best blade he owned. "Three days, Voss. Three days you've been somewhere else. Yesterday you gave Tallowin the wrong count at the ferry toll and argued with him about it. You, arguing about numbers. You checked our escort contract twice last night and then stood in the yard staring at the north road for an hour. I know what distraction looks like on my soldiers. On you it looks like a wound."

"It's nothing."

"Say that again without touching your left side."

Her hand froze halfway to the map case. Slowly, deliberately, she lowered it, and Brannoc's expression didn't change at all, which was worse than anger.

"The company has a rule," he said. "Older than me, older than the one-eyed bastard who raised you. Whatever a soldier carries into the Hollow, she carries openly or not at all. Because things that stay hidden in the Hollow—" he tapped the ledger, once, "—don't stay hidden. They get eaten, and what grows back in the hole isn't yours anymore."

"I know the rules, Brannoc. I've known them longer than some of your recruits have been alive."

"That's exactly what worries me." He closed the ledger. "You're the best I have. You know it, I know it, and I'd rather cut off my sword arm than lose you. So I'm asking once, as the man who taught you which end of a knife to hold: is whatever you're carrying going to get any of mine killed?"

And there it was—the question she'd been circling for three days, laid out plain between them. She thought of the stranger on the ferry dock, his blood spreading black in the lamplight, his fingers closing hers around the map case with the desperate strength of a drowning man. *The door still opens for the named.* She thought of the seven marks inked across a country that did not exist, and her own handwriting on a map she'd never written.

"No," she said. "Whatever it is, it's mine alone."

Brannoc studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded, slow and unhappy, the nod of a man filing something away for later.

"Then carry it alone," he said. "And Voss—if it starts costing my people, I'll take it from you myself. Sworn or not."

* * *

The flashback came the way it always came—not summoned but ambushed, rising out of nowhere the way a pike rises out of dark water.

She was mending a strap by the stable wall when it hit her: rain. Rain hammering on slate, ten years ago, and the whole company crowded into a shrine not unlike this one, and the moment the world went quiet.

They had been escorting a wool merchant through the western passes—the first contract she'd ever taken as a full sworn blade, seventeen years old and so proud of the oath-cord at her wrist she kept touching it like a bruise. There had been a storm. There had been a chapel with a leaking roof where they sheltered, and the company's old chaplain, Mother Ilse, dead now these six years, had led the evening prayer in her cracked voice while rain drummed like a thousand small fingers.

And mid-prayer, mid-sentence, mid-*word*—the silence had come down.

Not gradually. All at once, like a snuffed candle. Zephyrine remembered the exact sensation: that the prayer had been a rope everyone was pulling together, and someone had cut it. Mother Ilse's mouth kept moving. No sound came out. Not because the storm swallowed it—because the *sound itself* had been taken, plucked out of the air like a thread from a loom. Every head in that chapel had turned toward the east at the same instant, thirty soldiers moving as one body, all of them looking toward something none of them could name.

Later, the merchants' caravans brought the news eastward with them: the great shrines had gone dark. The high temples in the capital reported their oracles mute. In the borderlands, whole congregations had stood up mid-service and walked out, unable to say why, unable to say anything, because the words they needed had been part of what went missing.

Ten years. Ten years of the company adapting, the priests dwindling, the world learning to live with a hole in it shaped like a voice. Ten years of Zephyrine asking her leave every morning and reading consent in the quiet, because the alternative was unthinkable.

Unthinkable. That was the word she'd hidden behind. Because if the silence wasn't absence—if it was *choice*—

"—rine. Zephyrine."

Sefa's hand on her shoulder. The strap she'd been mending had a needle stuck in it, forgotten, her finger bleeding a thin line she hadn't felt.

"You went somewhere," Sefa said. She crouched beside her, brown-eyed, sharp-faced, the only person alive whose presence Zephyrine had never once had to perform for. "Same place you've been going for three days. Want to tell me, or want me to sit here and look at you until you crack?"

"Sefa—"

"I'm good at waiting. Ask anyone. Ask Brannoc, who once lost a week of wages betting I'd blink first."

Zephyrine almost laughed. Almost told her. The words were right there, stacked behind her teeth: *a dying man, a map, a country called Vael that nobody has ever heard of, my own handwriting in my own blood-warm hands.* But she saw, with terrible clarity, the shape of what would happen next. Sefa would insist on coming. Sefa would stand between her and whatever was coming down the north road, because that was who Sefa was, and Zephyrine would let her, because that was who *she* was, and one of them would not come home.

"I'm fine," she said. "Bad sleep. The shrine helps."

"Liar."

"Competent liar."

Sefa held her gaze a moment longer, then sighed and sat down fully on the straw, shoulder to shoulder. For a while neither spoke. The horses shifted and breathed. Somewhere across the yard, Tallowin was losing an argument with a farrier.

"When we were seventeen," Sefa said at last, "you told me why you still pray. Do you remember?"

"I remember everything," Zephyrine said, and heard the irony land on both of them.

"You said: *the day he stops answering is the day I'll need him most, so I'd better be practiced at talking to silence.*" Sefa turned her head. "That's today, isn't it. That's what today feels like."

Zephyrine looked at her oldest friend and could not answer, because the truth was worse than yes. The truth was that she had finally gotten what she'd asked for—ten years of practice at speaking into silence—and it turned out practice meant nothing. It turned out that when the silence started answering *back*, with attention instead of absence, with the pressure of something leaning close behind a door, all her practiced words fell out of her like coins from a torn purse.

"I have to go north," she heard herself say. "Soon. Alone."

Sefa went very still. "How soon?"

"Soon."

"And Brannoc knows?"

"He knows enough."

"He doesn't know anything, or you'd be confined to quarters right now." Sefa stood, brushing straw from her knees, and her face had closed into the expression she wore before hard rides and bad contracts—grief pre-emptively packed away for later. "Fine. Go north. Be alone. But Zeph—" she paused at the stable door, not turning around, "—whatever's eating you? Don't let it finish before you figure out whether you want to be eaten."

She left. Zephyrine sat in the straw with the needle still in her strap and her finger still bleeding, and understood, with the calm of a woman watching a fire reach the stairwell, that she had already chosen. She just hadn't admitted it yet.

* * *

She went back up the hill at dusk, when the shrine would be empty even of memory.

The mist had thickened. The valley below had become a white sea with island rooftops, and the black altar drank the last light like a mouth. Zephyrine unbuckled the map case and, for the first time since the ferry dock, opened it in the open air.

The leather unrolled across the altar stone. Seven doors marked in faded ink across a country whose borders were drawn in a confident, living hand—and in the margin, in cramped script she now knew was her own, the annotations she could not remember writing. *The third door lies. The fifth remembers. The seventh is not a door.*

And beneath the seventh mark, half worn away, a single word in the same handwriting:

*Warden.*

The wind moved through the empty pillars. The valley exhaled below her, vast and patient and full of things being forgotten.

"You knew," Zephyrine whispered to the silence. "You've known all along. Ten years of quiet, and it wasn't grief—it was *waiting*. Wasn't it."

The silence leaned closer. She felt it against her skin like the cold side of a pillow, like a hand hovering an inch above her shoulder, choosing not to touch.

Every instinct the company had ever trained into her screamed the same lesson: when the thing in the dark notices you, you move, you run, you put distance between yourself and its attention. Instead, Zephyrine Voss knelt at the altar of a god who had lied to her for a decade, laid her palm on the black stone, and made the oldest prayer she knew—one last time, meaning it differently than she ever had.

"I ask your leave," she said. "Answer me, or I go without it."

For three heartbeats, nothing.

Then, from the valley below—from the town, from the docks, from the direction of the north road—came the faint, impossible sound of many voices singing in perfect, gentle harmony.

The Chorus had arrived.
