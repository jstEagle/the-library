## 6. The Company Votes

The Grey Company kept its winter quarters in a defunct quarry at the Hollow's eastern lip, where the stone had been cut away in generations past and never filled again. Terraces of empty barracks clung to the rock like swallows' nests, and the wind came up the valley all night with nothing to stop it, moaning through the doorways of houses that had been abandoned before Zephyrine was born.

She had lived there eleven years. She knew which floorboards sang, which hearths drew properly, which of the old quarrymen's carvings on the stairwell walls were prayers and which were jokes. She knew the smell of the place—cold stone, tallow smoke, horse, iron—and she had thought, until very recently, that knowing a thing that thoroughly meant it could not be taken from her.

The map case sat against her ribs beneath her shirt, warm from her body, heavier than leather had any right to be.

Brannoc summoned her at dusk.

Not a shout across the yard, not a knock on her door—the way he would have done for any other soldier. Instead a boy appeared at her threshold with a lamp and said only, "Captain wants you," and did not meet her eyes on the way out. The whole camp had that quality tonight. She'd felt it since she rode back through the gates two days past: conversations folding shut like fans when she approached, Sefa's smile arriving a half-beat too late, the quartermaster suddenly unable to find her requisition chits. Eleven years of shared bread and shared blood, and the company smelled something on her the way dogs smell a storm.

Brannoc's quarters occupied the old quarry office, the one room with glass in its windows. He sat behind a desk he'd hauled up from some dead man's house, mending a bridle strap by lamplight, and he did not look up when she entered. That was Brannoc. He made you wait inside your own skin.

"Sit," he said finally.

She sat. The chair was a stump planed flat. She'd helped him plane it, years ago, on a night neither of them had been able to sleep after Vellin died in the pass. She remembered his hands guiding hers on the drawknife. She remembered—

She reached for the memory the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth, and found it still there. Whole. But she checked it now, always, the way a man checks a coin bitten between his teeth. Since the ferry town. Since the dying cartographer's blood had cooled under her hands and his map had gone into her keeping.

"Two days," Brannoc said, setting down the strap. "You've been back two days, and I've counted four lies."

"Captain—"

"The ferry run." He held up fingers, thick as tent stakes. "You told me the crossing was quiet. Renn says there was blood on the dock planks when you left, fresh enough to smear. You told me you traveled alone. The ferryman's boy says a man came off the water behind you, or you came off the water carrying a man—I've heard both versions. You told me you lost nothing on the road." His eyes came up, grey as the quarry stone, patient as erosion. "And you haven't prayed at the shrine since you got back. Eleven years, Zeph. You've knelt every morning of eleven years, sick or wounded or grieving. Four mornings now, nothing."

"I didn't think anyone was counting my prayers."

"No one counts them. That's what makes them worth noticing when they stop." He leaned back. The lamplight caught the scar that ran from his ear down into his collar, a relic of the border wars nobody outside the Hollow remembered fighting. "I don't care what you carry. I've carried things. Every person in this company is a bundle of secrets wrapped around a sword arm; that's why they end up here, in a valley where nothing can follow them in and nothing they were can reach them. But you've changed shape, Zephyrine. And when one of mine changes shape, the rest of them feel it, like a stone shifting in a wall. So. Tell me what happened at the ferry, or tell me it's none of my business—but say one true thing tonight, because I'm too old to build on lies."

Zephyrine looked at her hands. Callused, competent hands that had killed men and set bones and buried friends. They looked like a stranger's.

"There was a cartographer," she said. "He was dying when I found him. Something had been hunting him—something I didn't see, but he'd seen it, and whatever it was had already been *inside* him somehow, tearing him apart from the middle out. He gave me a map." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "A map of doors. Out of the Hollow. Out of—" she stopped, because the next word was a country's name she couldn't pronounce, a name that slid off the mind like water off oilcloth. "Out of somewhere that isn't supposed to exist anymore."

"And this map is against your ribs right now."

It wasn't a question. She nodded anyway.

Brannoc was silent for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the window glass, and somewhere down the terraces a horse complained about the cold.

"You know what I'm going to ask," he said.

"Yes."

"Then let me ask it anyway, so we both know where we stand." He folded his huge hands on the desk. "Give it to me. Not forever—give it to me to hold. We'll put it in the strongroom under the third terrace, under seal, and if it turns out to be what you think it is, we'll decide together what to do. That's how this company has survived everything else: nothing any one of us carries alone. Hand it over, Zeph, and tomorrow morning you kneel at the shrine like always, and this whole ugly week becomes a story we tell over bad wine in ten years."

"And if I don't?"

His face didn't move, but something behind it did—a flinch, quickly governed, the look of a man watching a bridge he loved begin to crack. "Then you're not one of mine anymore. Not because I'd cast you out. Because you'd already be gone, and I'd just be writing down what's true."

"That's not loyalty, Brannoc. That's ownership."

"It's *survival*." For the first time his voice roughened. "You think I don't know what you are? What half of us are? People who made bargains with powers too big to see the edges of. You swore yourself to a god who went silent ten years ago, and every day since, you've been a lit fuse looking for somewhere damp to lie down. This company is the damp. This company is the only reason you're alive instead of being a footnote in somebody's holy war. Don't you dare call it ownership." He exhaled slowly, mastering himself. "One night. Sleep on it. Give me your answer at dawn."

She stood. At the door she paused, and almost—almost—turned back. Almost took the map case from against her ribs and laid it on the desk between them, and felt the terrible lightness she imagined would follow.

Instead she said, "Goodnight, Captain," and walked out into the wind.

* * *

Sefa was waiting in the shadow of the stairwell, arms crossed against the cold, breath steaming.

Of course she was. Sefa Vane had been waiting in shadows for Zephyrine since the day they'd met, fifteen years ago, two recruits sparring badly in the rain while an instructor shouted that mercy was a habit you built before you needed it. Sefa was small and quick and laughed like a door falling off its hinges, and she had covered for Zephyrine through fevers, heartbreaks, one catastrophic gambling debt, and the entire first year after the Warden went silent, when Zephyrine had drunk herself toward the valley's edge more than once and Sefa had dragged her back each time by the collar, swearing at her the whole way.

Now Sefa just looked at her, and the look said everything the stairs couldn't: *you told him no.*

"He'll give you till dawn," Sefa said. "He always gives till dawn. It's not a threat, it's a ritual. Half the time people come to their senses by then and he lets them pretend they decided it themselves."

"And the other half?"

"The other half leave." Sefa pushed off the wall and fell into step beside her, toward the barracks, though they both knew Zephyrine wasn't going to the barracks. "Walk with me. The long way. There's something I want to show you before—" She stopped herself. Before what, she didn't say.

They walked the rim path above the quarry, where the wind came clean off the moor and the stars stood thick enough to swim in. Below them the company's lamps glowed in their terraces, small and stubborn, a constellation someone had nailed to the dark. Zephyrine had looked at that view a thousand nights. Tonight it looked like something she was already remembering rather than seeing.

"Sefa." She stopped. "Whatever happens at dawn—"

"Don't." Sefa's voice cracked like a whip, and then, immediately, gentled. "Don't do the speech. I've heard the speech. Everyone about to do something stupid gives the speech, and it's always 'whatever happens,' and it's always 'you've been my best friend,' and it always means they've already decided and they want absolution in advance." She turned, and in the starlight her face was fierce and wet-eyed at once. "So here's mine instead. Facts. Ready?"

Zephyrine almost smiled. "Ready."

"One: whatever's on that map, you showed me the corner of it three days ago when you thought I was asleep, and I saw the script, and I've seen that script before—in my grandmother's prayer book, the one she wasn't allowed to own. Two: my grandmother used to hum a song the Chorus banned, and last week I caught myself humming it, and I don't remember learning it, which means the Hollow ate the learning and left the tune, and that frightens me more than anything your map could show. Three:" she stepped closer, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, "the strangers who came asking about the dead cartographer—they were in the Ferrytown inn two days before you arrived. Asking. Singing, Renn said. In harmony. They're not done looking, Zeph. Whatever you're carrying, they will come into this quarry for it, and they will go through everyone I love to get it, and I am including myself in that list without hesitation, so don't you dare try to protect me by leaving quietly."

The wind filled the silence. Below, a lamp went out in the third terrace—one of the sentries changing shift.

"Sefa," Zephyrine said carefully, "if I give Brannoc the map, it goes in the strongroom, and the strongroom has a door, and doors can be opened by people who sing."

"And if you keep it, you're alone on the road with a target painted on your spine."

"Yes."

"So either way I lose you." Sefa laughed, short and bitter, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Fifteen years, and this is the choice. Fine. Then here's fact four." She pulled something from inside her coat and pressed it into Zephyrine's hand: a small canvas roll, tied with cord. Inside, when Zephyrine thumbed it open, lay travel rations, a phial of the good poppy tincture, a whetstone, and three gold pieces wrapped in waxed cloth. "I packed it yesterday. Before you even spoke to him. Because I know you, Zephyrine Voss, better than you know yourself these days—and I mean that literally, because half of you is missing, isn't it? I watch you reach for things that aren't there. Your mother's face. The name of the town you were born in. You think nobody notices." Her jaw tightened. "So take the pack. Take the map. Take the road. And when you find whatever door you're looking for—you *will* find it, you're the most stubborn creature God ever failed to answer—when you find it, you send word. Not a rescue summons. Just word. One line. So I know whether to grieve or wait."

Zephyrine's throat had closed entirely. She managed, "Sefa—"

"Fact five." Sefa grabbed her by the front of her coat, forehead nearly touching hers, and her whisper was ferocious. "If the Chorus comes here asking where you went, I'm going to lie. I'm going to lie so beautifully they'll write ballads about it. And lying to the Chorus gets people unsung, Zeph. I know what I'm buying with this. I'm buying it with my eyes open. So the only thing you owe me—the *only* thing—is to make it worth the price."

For a moment neither moved. Then Sefa released her, stepped back, and became briskly practical, the way she always did when feeling threatened her: "The south goat track leaves the quarry above the sluice gate. Avoid the main road until the second milestone; Tam's on watch tonight and he owes me, but he's honest, and honesty is a liability. And Zeph?" A ghost of the old grin. "Take the left fork at the standing stone, not the right. The right fork floods."

"How do you know about the standing stone?"

"I read your map while you slept. I told you—fact one." She shoved the canvas roll against Zephyrine's chest. "Go. Before dawn takes away your nerve. It takes everyone's."

* * *

Zephyrine did not go by the goat track.

She meant to. She packed in the dark of her cell—bedroll, the canvas roll, the map case against her ribs, her sword, and, after a long moment's war with herself, nothing else. Eleven years fit into a bedroll. That was the arithmetic of a mercenary's life, and she had always known it, and knowing it did not stop the grief from arriving on schedule.

But at the sluice gate she stopped. The goat track climbed away southward, pale in the starlight, free and anonymous. And below it, at the bottom of the quarry, the shrine stood in its circle of leaning stones, black against the frost-silvered ground.

Ten years of mornings. Ten years of kneeling on frozen earth with her palms open and her oath on her lips: *Warden, keep me, and I will keep what you cannot.* Ten years of silence that she had called faith, because calling it faith hurt less than calling it abandonment.

She climbed down.

The shrine was simple: a basin of quarry granite, worn smooth by centuries of other hands, holding nothing but rainwater and last night's ice. No idol. The Warden had never permitted one—or perhaps, she thought now with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, no idol had ever survived to be carved. She knelt. The frost bit through her trousers. She opened her hands the old way.

"I'm leaving," she said aloud, and her breath hung in the air like a small ghost. "You're not going to stop me. You haven't spoken in ten years; I doubt you'll start for this. But I want you to know it wasn't betrayal. I'm going to find out what you did. What you *are*. And if you sealed yourself away from me—if all those mornings, you were awake behind the silence, listening, choosing not to answer—then I hope the truth is heavy enough to break whatever's holding you shut. Because I have carried you for ten years, Warden. It's someone else's turn to carry something."

The silence answered, as it always answered: with nothing.

But as she rose, brushing the frost from her knees, she felt it—that same wrongness from the shrine visit weeks ago, the sense that the silence was not absence but *attention*, a held breath the size of a valley. And beneath her feet, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the stone seemed to hum. Once. Softly. Like something turning over in a long sleep, disturbed by a voice it almost remembered.

She stood very still for a long moment, heart hammering.

Then she shouldered her pack, took the left fork at the standing stone, and walked out of the only home she had left, down into the dark of the Hollow, toward the drowned chapel and the first of seven doors—never seeing, in the lamplit office above, Brannoc setting down his pen over a report he would never send, or Sefa climbing alone to the empty shrine to kneel where Zephyrine had knelt, and whisper to the silent god:

"Watch her, you bastard. It's the least you owe us both."
