## 7. First Door: Sealed

The map said the first door lay three days north of the ferry town, where the river Vell went underground and the land above it forgot what water was for.

Zephyrine walked it alone.

She had left before dawn on the fourth day after the company vote, taking only what a mercenary takes when she means to come back—rations for six days, rope, her short sword, the good knife at her hip—and one thing she did not mean to come back with: the map case, strapped flat against her spine beneath her coat like a second set of shoulder blades. Brannoc's face was still in her head, the way it had looked when she refused him. Not angry. Worse than angry. *Disappointed*, the way a father is disappointed, which is just anger that has given up trying to change you.

Sefa had covered for her. Sefa had told the captain she'd gone ahead to scout the north road for a contract that didn't exist, and Zephyrine had let her lie, and that was another weight to carry, and she carried all of them the way she carried everything: quietly, evenly, one foot and then the other.

The Hollow made it easy to walk and hard to think. That was its trick. The valley had no magic in it—that was why she'd stayed ten years, why half the mercenaries in the borderlands stayed—but it had something else instead, something that worked slower and cut deeper. The Hollow ate memory the way a slow river eats a bank. You didn't notice the loss. You noticed the *absence* where the loss should have been, a smooth blank place in your mind like a wall where a door used to be, and by then whatever it had taken was already downstream somewhere, dissolving.

She tested herself the way she always did now, every morning, a ritual as fixed as checking her blade's edge. Mother's face: she had lost that two years ago, or maybe she'd never had it and only believed she had; there was no way to tell anymore, and that was the cruelest part. The Warden's voice: still there, or the memory of it—the low warm hum he'd spoken with, back when he spoke at all, before the decade of silence. Her first kill: present, unfortunately. Sefa laughing so hard she snorted wine out her nose at the Ferryman's Rest: present. Brannoc teaching her to read terrain from bird flight: present.

The cartographer's face: fading. She could feel it going, day by day, the man who had bled out against her knee on the ferry dock pressing the leather case into her hands and whispering *the door still opens for the named*. She had tried to hold onto him deliberately, rehearsing his features each night like a prayer—a narrow face, gray stubble, eyes too large for it—but deliberate remembering in the Hollow was like carrying water in your hands. Every mile, a little less.

By noon of the second day she had stopped being able to remember whether he'd said anything else besides those last words. By dusk she suspected he had, and that it had mattered.

She walked faster after that.

* * *

The river Vell announced its disappearance long before you saw it. First the sound changed—the broad mutter of shallow water narrowing to a focused, urgent rush—and then the banks drew in, limestone rising on either side in gray shelves, and then the whole river simply poured itself into a crack in the world and was gone, swallowed whole, without ceremony. Downstream of the swallow-point the bed ran dry, a wide ribbon of pale stones and dead reeds, and the land on either side had the particular exhausted look of country that has been robbed of something essential and has stopped expecting it back.

The chapel stood in the dry bed itself.

Zephyrine saw it from the ridge above and stood a long time looking down, because the map had called it a *drowned* chapel, and this was clearly not drowned. It sat in the dry riverbed like a ship beached far from any sea: a small gray building, squat and round-apsed, its roof fallen in on one side, its bell tower truncated to a stump. Old flood-stains striped its walls in dark horizontal bands, high up near the eaves—higher than any flood she could imagine reaching here, higher than the banks themselves. Whatever water had once drowned this place had drowned it thoroughly and then, like everything else in the Hollow, forgotten it had ever been there.

She descended the ridge and crossed the stony bed with her hand on her knife, because open ground is open ground, and a lone figure walking toward an isolated building in empty country is a gift to anyone watching. Nothing moved except a hawk, very high, riding a thermal over the ridge. She watched it until her neck ached. Hawks were honest. If someone were waiting down there, the hawk would know, and the hawk would circle, and this one was drifting south with the unconcerned boredom of a bird that has found nothing worth diving for.

The chapel door faced upstream, toward the swallow-point, as if the building were watching the place where the river died.

It was a heavy door, iron-banded oak, and it was shut, and it was wrong.

Zephyrine had seen a thousand shut doors in her life and most of them were merely closed—wood meeting frame, latch meeting plate, the ordinary modesty of architecture. This door was not closed. It was *sealed*, and she knew it the way you know a corpse from a sleeping man: instantly, viscerally, with the skin before the mind. The door and its frame had grown together. Not rotted together, not warped shut by damp—*grown*, the oak flowing into the stone in smooth continuous grain, wood becoming mineral becoming wood again, as if the whole doorway had been dipped in some solvent that dissolved the boundary between things and then left them to heal. There was no seam. There was nowhere a blade could go. She put her palm flat against it and the surface was cool and faintly rough and utterly continuous, door and wall one object, the idea of *entrance* erased from it the way the Chorus erased countries.

"The door still opens for the named," she said aloud, to hear a voice in that silence. Her own voice sounded thin and borrowed.

The door did nothing, because doors don't, but the silence around her shifted—she felt it the way you feel weather change behind your back—and she stepped away from the chapel with her sword half-drawn before she understood that nothing had moved. Just the valley. Just the Hollow, listening. She was beginning to suspect the Hollow listened to everything said near its doors, and she resolved to stop talking to buildings.

She circled the chapel instead, and that was where she found the evidence.

Someone had been here recently. Recently enough that the Hollow hadn't finished eating it.

There were tool marks on the eastern wall, chisel scars around a section of stone beside the apse, and fresh ones—the pale raw brightness of newly quarried rock, unweathered, ungrayed. In the Hollow, she'd learned, even stone aged strangely fast, as if the valley were impatient with anything that insisted on having a history. These chips were white as teeth. Days old. Maybe less.

And there were footprints in the dried silt along the riverbed, boot prints, a single set, coming from the north and circling the chapel twice before stopping at the eastern wall. At the end of the second circuit, beside the chiseled stone, the silt held a small dark stain and a scatter of pale fragments.

Bone fragments. Small ones. Finger-sized.

Zephyrine crouched over them for a long time, not touching, reading the ground the way Brannoc had taught her to read it. One person. Alone. Working at the wall with a chisel, methodically, circling the building between attempts as if checking for another way in. And then—she traced the prints forward, past the stain—an abrupt end. The last prints were deeper, running strides, heading not away from the chapel but *toward* it, and then they simply stopped, mid-stride, in the open bed, as if the walker had been lifted off the earth or had stepped off the edge of something invisible.

No body. No blood beyond the small stain. No chisel—the tools were gone too.

She stood up slowly and looked at the eastern wall, at the chiseled section, and made herself see what she had been refusing to see. The chisel work wasn't random. It ringed a patch of stone perhaps the size of a shield, and within that patch, visible only now that she knew to look—visible only in the particular slant of late light—the stone showed a faint rectangular outline. A shape. A suggestion of a door that was not the sealed front door, hidden inside the wall itself, and someone had been trying to chisel their way to it, and the valley—or the door, or whatever kept these doors—had objected.

The bone fragments clicked under her boot heel as she stepped back.

"Named," she murmured. "The door opens for the named." She thought of the map case against her spine, of her own handwriting in margins she didn't remember writing, of her name written there in letters that were unmistakably hers. *Zephyrine Voss.* Written by her hand, in ink she didn't remember buying, on a map she didn't remember making.

Maybe the dead cartographer hadn't chosen her at all. Maybe the map had.

She made camp that night in the lee of the chapel's north wall, out of the wind that funneled down the dry riverbed, and she did not sleep well, and she dreamed of water rising in a gray room and a voice humming a tune she almost recognized, and woke before dawn with her knife already in her hand and no memory of drawing it.

* * *

They came on the third morning, and the hawk was right about nothing, because the hawk was gone.

She noticed the absence first—the sky over the ridge suddenly empty where it had held that bored circle for two days—and then she noticed the silence underneath the silence, the specific quality of quiet that means something living is holding its breath, and she was moving before she finished deciding to move, rolling left off her bedroll as the crossbow bolt took the space where her chest had been and buried itself in the chapel wall with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

She came up running, low, using the chapel's corner for cover, her mind doing what her mind did best—not fighting, *calculating*. One shooter, position high on the southern ridge, angle of fire says he expected me asleep facing the river. Bolt's already spent; a crossbow buys him twenty seconds, thirty if he's calm, and nobody who shoots at sleeping women is calm. Twenty seconds. Use them.

She sprinted the length of the chapel's blind north wall, boots sliding on silt, and came around the apse with her sword drawn and her body screaming at her to keep running north, run, run, the riverbed runs straight for a mile—

She didn't run. Running was what he'd expect, and running would put her in the open with a loaded crossbow behind her, and she had learned long ago—in a life whose edges were going soft but whose lessons stayed sharp as glass—that fear tells you to do exactly the thing that kills you. Fear says run. Fear says fight. Fear says anything loud.

So she did the quiet thing. She stopped.

She pressed herself into the deep shadow of the apse, sheathed her sword, and pulled the coil of rope from her pack, and waited, breathing through her mouth, counting.

He came down the ridge fast, careless with triumph, a lean figure in travel-gray with a crossbow in one hand and something glinting at his belt, and he followed her footprints north along the riverbed exactly as she'd known he would, because footprints are a story and people believe stories. He passed within eight feet of her. Close enough that she could see the stitching on his collar—fine, close, regular stitching, the kind of stitching that comes from uniform rather than habit—and see the thing at his belt properly for the first time.

A tuning fork. Steel, long-handled, wrapped in cord at the grip.

Her blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the morning air. She had heard of the Chorus the way you hear of a plague in a distant city—rumors, warnings, mercenary talk about agents who asked questions in harmony and left villages that remembered nothing of themselves. The dying cartographer had whispered a word against her ear as he bled: *Chorus*, and then coughed, and then said the thing about the door. She had hoped, these three days, that the word meant nothing here, that the Hollow's blindness hid her as it hid everyone.

The man following her footprints was Chorus. Which meant the Hollow did not hide her from them. Which meant the mark they'd left humming on her doorframe in the ferry town was not a warning.

It was a tether.

He stopped twenty paces out, in the middle of the open bed, and turned in a slow circle, frowning at the trail that ended at nothing, and Zephyrine watched him and felt her heart hammering and made herself think past the hammering. He'd reload soon. He'd double back. She had one chance and it was small and stupid and perfect.

She threw the rope.

Not at him—at the dry-stone scree slope at the base of the ridge behind him, the loose talus she'd marked on her first evening as the loudest thing in the valley. The coiled rope hit the stones with a clatter and a slide, a sudden racket of tumbling rock, and the scout spun toward it, crossbow up, back fully to her, and Zephyrine crossed the twenty paces like she'd crossed a thousand battlefields, silent, fast, and hit him at the knees.

They went down together in the silt. He was stronger—she registered that immediately, his arm like an iron bar across her throat, his free hand clawing for the fork at his belt—and she did not try to out-muscle him because she would lose. Instead she did the thing Sefa had taught her years ago, the ugly efficient thing, and drove her thumb into the hinge of his jaw while bringing her forehead down onto the bridge of his nose. Cartilage gave. His grip broke. She rolled clear, got her knees on his crossbow arm, and pinned it, and they froze there, both panting, his blood threading pink into the pale silt, and he looked up at her with a broken nose and absolutely no fear at all, and that was the worst thing she'd seen in ten years in the Hollow.

"You're the one from the ferry town," he said. His voice was steady. Mild. Almost pleasant. "Voss."

"Don't," she said, and put her knife at his throat, "say my name."

"It's a lovely name." He smiled through the blood. "Very singable."

"Who sent you?"

"The same one who sent the cartographer." His eyes flicked, briefly, toward the chapel. "You think he found that map. Nobody finds that map. It was *given*, the way a lure is given. You're not escaping anything, mercenary. You're delivering yourself."

"To what?"

"To the door." Something moved behind his mildness then, something hungry and patient, and for one moment his voice came out doubled, two pitches at once, a chord wearing a man's throat, and Zephyrine's skin crawled so hard she nearly cut him just to make it stop. "It's been waiting so long. It gets so hungry in the quiet places. Bring it the map. Bring it the name. It will open for you, and you will finally remember everything you've lost, and it will finally eat—"

She struck him. Not the knife—her fist, once, hard, and his head rolled and his eyes went vague, the chord collapsing back into one tired human voice, and he sagged into the silt breathing slowly. Unconscious, not dead. She knelt over him shaking, knife still drawn, and discovered she couldn't do the other thing. Ten years a mercenary. A hundred fights. She'd killed men who deserved it less. But lying unconscious in a riverbed, he was just a man with a broken nose, and killing him would tell her nothing, and she needed—badly, desperately—what he knew.

What he carried.

She unbuckled the tuning fork from his belt with hands that weren't quite steady. It was longer than she'd guessed, steel gone dark with handling, the cord grip worn smooth by many palms. When she lifted it, it hummed—faintly, barely, a vibration more felt than heard, a note sitting just below the floor of hearing, and the moment it touched her fingers the hair on her arms rose and the sealed chapel door, forty feet away, made a sound.

Not a groan. Not a shift of settling stone.

A *note*. One clean, low tone, brief as a struck bell, rising out of the fused oak and stone and dying away, and in the half-second of its sounding Zephyrine felt something answer in her own chest, some string in her that she hadn't known was strung, vibrating in sympathy, and for one impossible instant she *remembered*—not a person, not a place, but a word, a vast and shining word, a name the size of a country—

Then it was gone, and the silence closed over it, and the Hollow began eating the memory of it even as she grasped at it, the way it ate everything, smooth blankness spreading where the wonder had been.

She stood in the dry riverbed holding a stolen tuning fork, breathing hard, blood on her knuckles, and understood three things with total clarity.

The Chorus used these forks to sing to doors. The doors answered. And the note the first door had sung back—the note that had brushed against something buried in her chest—had not been a threat.

It had been a greeting.

Behind her, the scout stirred in the silt, moaning. Ahead of her, the chapel sat sealed and silent, keeping whatever it kept. And somewhere north, days away, the map promised a second door, and the map's marginalia—her own handwriting, her own forgotten hand—had written one line beside it that she had read a dozen times and never understood until now:

*Ask the Warden what he is standing on.*

Zephyrine looked north, toward the shrine of the god who had been silent for ten years, and for the first time since the silence began, she was afraid of the answer.
