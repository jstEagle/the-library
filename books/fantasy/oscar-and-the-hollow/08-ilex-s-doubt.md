## 8. Ilex's Doubt

The Chorus did not sing in the way Ilex had once imagined singing, before he was given to them. He had been a child then, and children imagine music as joy—something that rises out of you unbidden, like laughter or grief. What the Chorus practiced was nothing so generous. Their singing was a craft, a discipline, a blade honed flat. They sang in intervals that did not occur in nature: a third slightly too wide, a fifth that bent toward something unresolved and hungry. When twelve voices found those intervals together, the air itself forgot how to hold its shape.

That was the work. That was all the work.

Ilex stood now in the rehearsal vault beneath the citadel of Vharn, where the walls were lined with lead and ash-slate so that no song escaped before it was meant to, and waited for his cue. Around him, eleven others in grey robes arranged themselves in the crescent formation, each of them holding their tuning fork against the hollow of the throat, letting its faint vibration settle the instrument of their bodies into readiness.

At the center, on a plinth of black stone, lay a name.

It did not look like much. Names never did, once they were taken. This one was a curl of pale light no longer than a finger, coiled tight as a sleeping insect, dimming by the hour. It had been cut from its country three weeks ago, and it was nearly spent. A name could survive outside the mouth that spoke it for perhaps a month before it starved—and when a name starved, everything it named went with it. The roads first, unraveling into grass. Then the buildings, softening into hills that had always looked vaguely like ruins if you squinted. Then the people, who did not die but became *unaccounted for*, folded into other countries' ledgers as bastards, wanderers, coincidences. And finally the language, the songs, the recipes, the way mothers in that country had hushed their children—all of it thinning into the general weather of the world until no one remembered there had ever been anything to forget.

Ilex knew the sequence intimately. He had sung in four such workings. He could not have told you which countries. That was rather the point.

"Positions," said Handler Vess, from the gallery above.

She was a narrow woman with a voice like a struck coin, and she had trained Ilex personally for nine years. She had taught him that doubt was a dissonance, and dissonance was a flaw, and flaws in a chorus were not forgiven—they were *excised*. She was looking at him now the way she always looked at him before a working: measuring his pitch without touching him.

He took his place at the eastern point of the crescent. The name on the plinth shivered, sensing what was coming. They always sensed it. In his first year, Ilex had asked Vess whether names felt fear, and she had answered with terrible gentleness: *Everything that is spoken fears being unsaid.* He had thought it was meant as comfort. It had taken him six more years to understand it was a warning.

"Subject: remnant settlement, border province," Vess read from her folio, in the flat recitation they used so that the words would carry no melody of their own. "Population forty-one. Designation: remnants of the Vael erasure."

The word landed in Ilex's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*Vael.*

He did not know the name. Of course he did not know the name—that was the entire architecture of the thing. But he knew the *shape* of not-knowing, the way a tongue returns again and again to the socket of a missing tooth. There were gaps in him. Everyone in the Chorus had gaps; it was a professional hazard, they told the novices, smiling. You cannot unsing a country without breathing some of its silence. But lately Ilex had begun to suspect that his gaps were not incidental. Lately, when the older members sang certain intervals, something behind his sternum would answer—a resonance with no source, a harmony he seemed to already know.

And now: a village of *remnants*. People who had survived an erasure. Which meant people who had been *of* the erased country, carried inside it somehow, hidden in the folds when the name was pulled away. The Chorus called them remnants the way a butcher calls things trimmings.

"We are not burning a village," Vess had said, two days ago, when the order came down. "We are completing a working. An erasure left unfinished is worse than none at all. A half-unsung name festers. It leaks. It *wants*." She had paused, and added, with the mildness she used for the most important statements: "You of all choristers should understand why we do not leave Vael's business half-done, Ilex."

*Ilex.* His own name, spoken in connection with Vael, and he had felt that missing-tooth ache flare so sharply he'd had to look at the floor.

Now he raised his fork. Eleven forks rose around him. The name on the plinth curled tighter.

"Begin on my mark," Vess said. "Third inversion. We take the settlement's borrowed speech first—it has none of its own left, poor things, only scraps—then the memory of place. Standard dissolution. Forty-one voices' worth of forgetting to distribute among the archives below."

The archives below. Ilex had seen them once, early, before he understood what he was seeing: caverns beneath the citadel where stolen names hung in their thousands, dimming slowly, fed—not restored, *fed*—to something at the very bottom of the deeps that even Handlers did not speak of above a whisper. He had assumed, in his novice year, that the names were being kept safe. Preserved. That the Chorus was a kind of terrible ark.

He no longer assumed anything. He simply sang, because the alternative to singing was asking questions, and asking questions was how choristers were reassigned to the silent orders, from which no one came back chatty.

Vess lifted her hand.

The interval began.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it, the thing no one outside ever believed: it was *beautiful*. Twelve voices finding the wrongness together, the too-wide third opening like a door, and the name on the plinth beginning to come apart into light and less-than-light. Ilex held his line perfectly. He always held his line perfectly. His voice slid into the harmonic lattice and did its work, and far away—forty miles east, though distance meant little to a working—the forty-one borrowed voices of a village began to forget their fields, their well, their grandmother's way of saying *come inside, it's getting dark.*

And then the village's own residue rose up through the working.

This happened sometimes. When you dissolved a remnant settlement, whatever scraps of the original country still clung to the people would surface in the last moments, the way silt clouds water when you stir a riverbed. Fragments. A phrase of a language no one living spoke. A lullaby's cadence. A smell described in words that were dissolving even as they surfaced—bread, smoke, resin, rain on slate.

Through the lattice, Ilex heard one fragment clearly, because it snagged on him specifically, the way a burr catches one thread of a sleeve:

A woman's voice, singing a counting rhyme to a child. *One for the door, two for the door—*

His voice broke.

It was not dramatic. A quarter-beat of silence, a fraction of flattening, the kind of error a listener might forgive in a tavern singer. But the Chorus was not a tavern, and the lattice was precision itself, and a quarter-beat of silence in a dissolution working was a crack through which a name could leak. Vess's head turned. The eleven other voices compensated instantly, smoothing over his absence the way water closes over a dropped stone, and the working completed—somewhere east, forty-one people became forty-one unaccounted-for, and a scrap of country finished dying—but Vess had heard.

The name on the plinth guttered out. The vault fell silent. Lead and ash-slate drank the last of the resonance, and Ilex stood in the ringing quiet with his fork still raised, feeling his heartbeat in his throat.

"Again," Vess said, to the room. "From the top. We drill until the flaw is gone."

They drilled. Twice, three times, four times, the same dissolution performed against an empty plinth, the ghost of a working rehearsed for no audience but her. By the fourth repetition Ilex's line was flawless, and he hated how easily it came back, how his body obeyed while his chest still echoed with a dead woman counting doors.

"One for the door, two for the door—" Doors. Why doors? Why did the fragment feel less like a stranger's lullaby and more like—

Like something he had known.

* * *

Afterward, Vess dismissed the others and kept him.

The gallery above the vault smelled of tallow and cold iron. She poured two cups of the bitter bark-tea the Handlers drank and set one before him without asking, which was either courtesy or a trap; with Vess it was usually both.

"You broke on the seventh bar," she said.

"The interval was unstable. The fifth wanted resolving downward and the arrangement forced it up. Anyone would—"

"I didn't ask for an analysis of the arrangement." She sat across from him, hands folded, and regarded him with the patience of someone who had excised many flaws and expected to excise this one. "I asked why *you* broke. You have sung nineteen workings without a hairline error. Your pitch is the best in your crescent. So: why you, why that bar, why today?"

Ilex looked into the tea. Steam moved across it like something trying to remember how to rise.

"A fragment surfaced during the dissolution," he said. "A rhyme. It caught me off guard."

"Fragments always surface. You've been drilled for fragments since you were nine years old." She leaned forward slightly. "What was different about this one?"

And here was the moment—Ilex saw it clearly, the way one sees the edge of a roof in the dark—where he could lie. He was good at lying; the Chorus taught that too, taught you to sing falsehoods in perfect pitch. He could say *nothing*, and she might believe him, and he would go on singing, and the next village of remnants would dissolve as smoothly as the last.

But the rhyme was still in his ears. One for the door, two for the door. And underneath it, rising like water into a cellar, was a certainty he could not source: that he had heard it before. Not once. *Often.* In a kitchen, maybe. From a woman whose face lived in one of his gaps.

"I think I knew it," he said. "Before."

Vess was quiet for a long moment. Outside the vault, somewhere in the citadel's upper reaches, another crescent was drilling, and their harmonies came down through the stone thinned to almost nothing—a sound like wind deciding to be music.

"Every chorister carries residue," she said finally. "We've discussed this."

"This wasn't residue. Residue is noise. This was—" He stopped, because the next word was *memory*, and memory was the one thing a chorister was forbidden to claim beyond what the Chorus allotted him. They gave you a history: an orphanage, a recruitment, a vocation. They gave you exactly enough past to be loyal. Anything more was contraband.

Vess studied him, and Ilex watched her decide something. It happened in her eyes, a small closing, like a ledger being shut.

"When you were assigned to the Vael working—nine years ago, the primary dissolution—you were fourteen," she said. "Do you remember it?"

"No."

"No one does. We took those years from every chorister involved. Standard practice. A country that size requires... clean instruments." She sipped her tea. "Most of you came through without a mark. But you were young, and you had a particular quality even then—an unusual sensitivity to harmonic residue. It's why we trained you. It's also why—" She stopped herself, and smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near her voice. "It's nothing. Fatigue, most likely. Rest today. Report tomorrow at the sixth bell."

"Yes, Handler."

He rose. At the gallery door, her voice stopped him, mild as milk:

"Ilex. If you ever find yourself wondering what was in those years we took—don't wonder aloud. Wondering is a dissonance. And you know what we do with dissonances."

He knew. He walked back through the citadel's grey corridors with her tea still untouched behind him, and his hands, he noticed distantly, were shaking.

* * *

He did not rest.

He went instead to the Archive of Withheld Materials, which choristers were permitted to consult only under supervision—which was precisely why he went at the hour between shifts, when the supervisor's chair stood empty and the lamp-oil was low. The archive was a long room of lead-lined drawers, each drawer holding what the Chorus called *shards*: physical objects recovered from erasure sites, kept because matter sometimes resisted unsinging longer than meaning did. A key with no lock. A coin from a mint that had never existed. Letters in a script that unraveled if you read it too slowly.

He had come looking for one thing, and he told himself it was procedure, due diligence, a chorister's obligation to understand his subject material. He told himself this the whole time his hands searched the index for the designation *VAEL — REMNANT RECOVERY*, and found, beneath it, a single entry:

*Shard 4471. Recovered: musical notation, handwritten. Provenance: nursery, eastern province. Note: notation corresponds to no extant system. Cross-reference withheld.*

Withheld. Someone above him had looked at this shard and decided no one should see it.

Ilex drew the drawer. Inside, on a square of yellowed paper, someone had written out a simple tune in a notation that hurt to parse—each symbol almost familiar, almost legible, dissolving at the edge of understanding like a word on the tip of the tongue. But he did not need to read it. He hummed it, barely, under his breath, careful to keep it below any register the citadel's listening stones might catch.

One for the door, two for the door.

His knees went weak. He gripped the drawer's edge. Because now, attached to the tune, came the rest—not the words, those were gone, but the *context*, rising up through nine years of taken silence like bubbles through dark water: lamplight. A small hand in his. A garden somewhere warm. A girl's voice, laughing at how badly he counted.

A sister.

He had a sister. Or he had *had* a sister, in a country that no longer existed, in a life that had been cut out of him along with everyone else's memory of it—and the Chorus had handed him a tuning fork and taught him to do it to others.

Ilex stood in the archive for a long time, holding a dead nursery rhyme, and understood with total clarity that he was standing on the far side of a line. On this side of the line there was no unhearing the fragment, no unknowing the gap in himself shaped exactly like a garden. On this side of the line, the next order would arrive—he could feel it coming the way you feel weather in an old wound—and he would have to choose what he was.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, the sixth bell was still hours away, and already, in the handlers' wing, Vess was writing her report: *Chorister Ilex. Hairline fracture under working conditions. Recommend observation. Recommend, if recurrence, reassignment.*

Recommend, if recurrence, the silent orders.

He put the shard back exactly as he had found it, closed the drawer, and walked out into the corridor—past the listening stones, past the drilling crescents, past the whole vast machine of forgetting humming its perfect intervals—and made himself a promise so small it fit inside a breath:

*Find the door before they find the flaw.*

He did not yet know there was a map. He did not yet know there was a woman in the Hollow carrying it, with his sister's country written on it in her own forgotten hand. He only knew that somewhere east of the citadel, in a village that was now forty-one unaccounted-for, a door had been mentioned in a counting rhyme—one for the door, two for the door—and that doors, unlike names, were very hard to unsing.

The bell rang the sixth hour. Ilex straightened his robe, fixed his face into chorister calm, and went to report for duty—carrying, folded small behind his sternum, the first note of a song the Chorus had not taught him.
