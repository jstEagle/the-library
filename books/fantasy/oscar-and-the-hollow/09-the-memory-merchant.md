## 9. The Memory Merchant

The border slum was called Gullet's End, though no one living could say why, and that seemed fitting. It clung to the southern lip of the Hollow like something half-swallowed—a sprawl of leaning houses built from river-silt brick and salvaged ship-timber, where the fog off the valley met the smoke off a hundred cookfires and neither would yield. People came here to trade things they couldn't sell anywhere else. Teeth. Years. Names.

Zephyrine had been walking for six days since the drowned chapel, and the tuning fork rode against her hip in a wrapped cloth like a second knife. She'd tested it once, at dusk on the third day, striking it against her boot heel. The note it gave was thin and wrong, a sound with a hole in it, and for a moment she'd seen the air around her ripple as if something enormous had turned its attention toward the noise. She hadn't struck it again. But she'd kept it. In the Hollow you learned to keep anything sharp, even sounds.

The map case pressed against her ribs beneath her coat. Seven doors. One sealed shut beneath black water. Six more, scattered across a country she'd never heard of, drawn in a hand that might have been her own.

She needed someone who could read the script crawling through the map's margins—those looping characters that looked less like writing than like a language remembering itself. And there was exactly one person within a week's walk who dealt in what couldn't be bought with coin.

* * *

Gullet's End smelled of fish rot and tallow and, underneath it all, something sweeter and worse—the smell of memory going stale, like fruit left too long in a closed room. The slum's main street was a canal of mud between stalls selling charms that didn't work and cures that worked too well. Children with old eyes watched her pass. A woman sat in a doorway braiding rope and weeping without sound, and when Zephyrine caught her gaze the woman said, conversationally, "I've forgotten whether I buried him or married him," and went back to her braid.

Zephyrine kept walking. That was the first rule of the border towns: don't stop for anyone mid-forgetting. You couldn't catch it, but you could catch the weight of it, and weight was dangerous out here.

The merchant's shop stood at the street's end where the mud gave up and became marsh. It had been three different buildings once; now it was one, stitched together with beams at angles carpenters would weep over. A sign hung above the door bearing no words at all—just a spiral carved deep into grey wood, turning inward until it vanished into its own center.

Inside, the air was cool and thick as standing water. Shelves rose from floor to unseen ceiling, crowded not with books or bottles but with small objects of no obvious value: a child's wooden bird. A rusted key. A single glove, left-handed. Each one hummed faintly, if you stood close enough and were quiet enough, with the particular hum of something remembered.

"You smell of the valley."

The voice came from behind a curtain of beads made from knucklebones. The man who pushed through them was small and neat and ageless, with skin like tea left steeping too long and eyes the color of the fog outside. He wore a coat with forty pockets, each one bulging differently. His name, Zephyrine knew by reputation, was Corvess. No one knew his first name. Possibly he didn't either. Possibly he'd sold it.

"Corvess." She stayed near the door, where the light was. Old habit. New necessity. "I hear you translate."

"I hear many things." He settled onto a stool behind a counter scarred with knife-marks and burn-marks and one long groove that looked suspiciously like a claw had made it. "I also hear that a cartographer died at the ferry last month. Bled out. Whispered to a mercenary before he went." His fog-colored eyes did not blink often enough. "I hear she carries his map."

Word traveled fast in places with nothing else to do. Zephyrine let her hand rest easy near her belt. "I hear you deal in memories. So maybe we're both hearing stories."

"Maybe." Corvess smiled. It was a careful smile, the smile of a man who had practiced smiling until it stopped meaning anything. "Show me the script. I'll tell you what it costs before you show me the map. That way you can decide how much truth is worth to you while you still have the option of ignorance."

That was fair, in the way everything at the border was fair—technically, and cruelly. She drew out the map just far enough to expose one corner of marginalia, angling it so the looping characters caught the lamplight.

Corvess went very still.

Not shocked. Still. The stillness of a man listening to a sound he'd been told was extinct. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped its performance entirely, and that was somehow worse than the smile.

"That's Vaelic. High register. Pre-fall." He looked up at her. "Where did you get this?"

"A dying man gave it to me."

"Dying men give away what they can't carry." He drummed two fingers on the counter, once, twice. "I can read it. I can even teach you to read it, given time, and time is the one thing the Chorus will not give you—I know they've marked your door, girl, don't look surprised, marked things hum, and your map has been humming since it crossed my threshold. So. Translation, today, full and complete. My price is one year."

"One year of what?"

He gestured at the shelves. At the wooden bird, the rusted key, the glove. "Of you. One year of your remembered life, extracted clean. You won't miss it—you can't miss what you don't know is gone. But it will be gone. Truly gone. Not sleeping. Gone." He tilted his head. "You live in the Hollow, so you already know the arithmetic. The valley eats a little every day. I'm only offering to choose which meal it takes."

Zephyrine thought about her mother's face. About standing on the road home six weeks ago, trying to summon it—brown eyes? Her mother had brown eyes, she was almost sure—and finding nothing where the face should have been, just a warm shape and the smell of bread and a grief with no object attached. The Hollow had taken that. Had taken it gently, painlessly, while she slept.

A year, chosen. A year, controlled. It almost sounded like mercy.

"Which year?" she asked.

"Your choice. Any twelve months you have lived. But you must choose before I begin, and once the extraction starts, it cannot be stopped. Memory resists, at the end. It doesn't want to leave. If you flinch midway—" he shrugged "—I keep the shard and you keep the wound."

"And the translation?"

"Complete. Every line of marginalia. Every annotation. What the doors are, where they open, what they cost." He leaned forward. "And I'll tell you now, free of charge, because it changes the value of everything: whatever this map leads to, the price at the end of it is larger than a year. Larger than any year. Read that into your decision however you like."

Her hand had drifted to the map case again without her noticing. She made herself take it away.

"I need a moment," she said.

"Take several. I'm immortal-ish; time means little to me." He poured himself something dark from a clay jug and did not offer her any.

* * *

She walked the shelves while she thought.

It wasn't sentiment that slowed her—it was tactics. A mercenary's life was built on what she carried in her head: faces, routes, betrayals, the exact weight of a lie told well. She needed to cut away a year that wouldn't cripple her. Something soft. Something that hurt but didn't arm anyone against her.

There was a shelf near the back where the objects were different—not humming but *warm*, radiating a heat you felt in the teeth. She found herself standing in front of it longer than she meant to.

On that shelf lay a small tin soldier, paint flaked, sword chipped.

She knew that soldier.

She knew it the way you know a thing before you know you know it—her hand had reached for it before her mind caught up, and now she stood holding it, and the knowing came flooding sideways: a winter barracks, four years ago, the company snowed in at a waystation called Harrow Ford. Sefa carving toys from scrap wood because Brannoc's niece was due in spring and the captain was unbearable with anticipation. Sefa whittling a tin-looking soldier out of pine because there was no tin, painting it with crushed brick and lamp-black, presenting it to Zephyrine with a flourish and saying, *"Every army needs one soldier who never breaks. That's you. Now there's proof."*

Zephyrine had laughed. She could feel the shape of the laugh, the cold room, the taste of the bad wine, Sefa's grin, the whole of that winter—Harrow Ford, the whole year of it, blizzards and card games and the night she'd told Sefa about the Warden's silence for the first time, the only time, the night she'd said aloud *what if he's not silent, what if he's gone, what am I then*—and Sefa had said, *you're the one who stays anyway.*

That year. That was the year.

She set the soldier down with hands that weren't quite steady.

No. Not that one. That one was load-bearing. She didn't have many years like it—years that proved something—but she had that one, and giving it up would be like pulling a stone from an arch.

Better something older. Something before the company. Before the oath, even. There was a stretch of years in her late teens she barely touched anyway—apprenticeship to a caravan guard, hard miles, a teacher named Osk who'd died stupidly of a fever, a boy whose name she hadn't spoken in a decade. Take that. Take the whole gray span of it. It was dead weight. It was *safe* weight.

"I've chosen," she said.

Corvess looked up from his cup. "Quickly done."

"The year I was seventeen. Caravan work. Guard apprenticeship. All of it."

Something flickered across the merchant's face—there and gone too fast to name. Regret? Amusement? With men like Corvess every expression was a mask worn over another mask, and underneath there was probably just fog.

"Seventeen," he repeated. "You're certain."

"Certain."

"Hm." He set down the cup and produced from one of his forty pockets a shallow dish of black glass and a needle of white bone. "Sit. Hands flat on the counter. This part feels like rain starting."

She sat. She flattened her hands. The bone needle touched the inside of her wrist, light as a moth landing, and—

It did feel like rain starting. A pressure first, far away, then nearer, then everywhere, a fine cold ticking against the inside of her skull. Corvess murmured something in a language that wasn't Vaelic and wasn't anything else she knew, and the black glass dish began to fill—not with liquid but with light, a slow silver seep, like dawn coming up under a door.

And the year began to leave.

She felt it go the way you feel a word dissolve on your tongue when you repeat it too long. Seventeen. Caravans. Osk. The gray miles. The boy. She held on to none of it—that was the discipline, that was the whole trick, you had to open your hand—and the memories slid out of her like water through fingers, and it didn't hurt, that was the obscene thing, it didn't hurt at all, it felt like being *tidied*—

The dish filled. The rain stopped.

Corvess capped the dish with a disc of slate and set it aside, and it joined the great humming inventory of other people's yesterdays.

"Well?" Zephyrine said. Her voice sounded strange to her. Thinner. "Translate."

"First—" Corvess raised a finger. "Tell me what you remember of being seventeen."

"Nothing. That's the point."

"What do you remember of being sixteen?"

"Enough. Why does it matter?"

"It matters because seams show." He was already unrolling the map across the counter, weighting its corners with the tin soldier, the key, the glove—his hands moving with a strange tenderness, as if the paper were a body. "Memory isn't stored in years, girl. It's stored in webs. Pull one thread-year out and the neighboring threads pucker. Most people never notice. But you'll want to know where your puckers are, before the Chorus finds them for you."

He bent over the marginalia and began to read aloud.

* * *

His voice changed as he translated. It lost its market-stall smoothness and became something older, slower, the voice of a man reading scripture he didn't believe in a church he feared.

"'The seven doors are one door,'" he recited, tracing the loops with a bone-white fingernail. "'Seven mouths of one throat. They open together or not at all, and they open only from the inside.'" A pause. His nail moved down. "'Each door demands a toll proportionate to its distance from the center. The outer doors take blood. The middle doors take years. The inner doors take names.'"

Zephyrine's mouth had gone dry. "Names."

"Keep listening." He read on, faster now, the script seeming to pull him. "'The final door—the seventh, the center, the one that sleeps beneath the valley of silence—opens for no key, no blood, no bargain. Its lock has one mechanism alone. It requires a name freely given back.'"

"Given back to whom?"

Corvess didn't answer immediately. He'd gone pale, or paler, his tea-dark skin draining toward grey. He read the next line twice, silently, lips moving, before he trusted his voice with it.

"'For the door is not a place. The door is the one who gave his name away to hold it shut. And it opens only when another gives theirs to make the gift complete.'" He looked up. "Girl. Do you understand what this says? This map isn't directions. It's instructions for a sacrifice. Someone drew this as a *recipe*."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that hadn't existed a moment ago. On the shelf behind Corvess, a dozen small objects began, very softly, to hum out of tune with one another.

"The Warden," Zephyrine said quietly. "My god. The one who went silent ten years ago."

"If your silent god is who I think he is," Corvess said, "then he isn't silent. He's *spent*. A god's name is the loudest thing about him. Take it away and what's left is—" he spread his hands "—a shrine with nobody home. Or a shrine with somebody home who can't speak." He tapped the map. "And this says somebody's home. Somebody's been home for ten years, holding a door shut with his own emptied mouth, waiting for—" his fog-colored eyes fixed on her "—well. Waiting for whoever carries this map, apparently. Which raises the question I should have asked at the start."

He turned the map around so it faced her. Pointed one bone-white finger at the bottom corner, where in small, sure strokes—her strokes, she knew them, she'd written supply manifests and contracts in that same slanted hand for fifteen years—was written a single word:

*Zephyrine.*

"In your handwriting," Corvess said. "In ink that's older than the paper it sits on, which should be impossible. Signed to a country that was erased before you were born." He leaned back on his stool. "Who are you, mercenary?"

And here was the horror, the true one, the one she'd been circling for days: she opened her mouth to answer, and the seam showed.

Because the answer should have come easily—*Zephyrine Voss, born in—* and the *born in* was there, a town, a region, wet springs and a particular bell—and then the year after, and the year after that, and somewhere in the gray span where seventeen used to be there was now a smooth blank patch, a puckered seam, and pressing against the edge of that seam was something that didn't belong to the missing year at all. Something that had been tucked *inside* it, hidden, folded away like a letter slipped between the pages of a book.

A voice. Low, warm, tired. A voice like a shrine at dusk.

*"If they ever come for the map, little sworn-one—don't give them the year you think they'll want. Give them the year beside it. Hide what matters in what doesn't."*

The Warden. Ten years ago. The last thing he'd ever said to her, the night before the silence—and she had hidden it, without knowing she was hiding it, inside the most forgettable year of her life, the way you hide a key under a doormat.

And she had just sold the doormat.

"No," she whispered.

"Problem?" said Corvess.

She was already on her feet, already reaching for the slate-capped dish, and his hand snapped over it first with a speed that made the earlier slowness seem like theater. "The transaction is complete," he said, and all the gentleness was gone from his voice, leaving something ancient and immovable. "You knew the terms. Extraction cannot be stopped. Cannot be *reversed*. I told you—gone is gone."

"That wasn't just a year. There was something *in* it—"

"There is always something in it. That is the entire business model." But he was watching her with those too-still eyes, and slowly, slowly, he lifted his hand from the dish. "Although. Hm. Interesting. You're reaching for it like a woman reaching for a limb. Most sellers feel nothing. Ever." He nudged the dish an inch toward her. "Touch it. Just touch. Don't open it."

She touched it. The slate was cold, and beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something pulsed. Not her memories—all of them, the caravans, Osk, the gray years, they were in there, dim and distant, a stranger's life in a jar.

But threaded through them, tangled in the seventeen-year-old girl's ordinary days like a wire through wool, was the voice. The warning. Hidden in what didn't matter—which meant it was in the jar, which meant it wasn't destroyed, which meant—

"It's still legible," Corvess said softly. "Extracted memory keeps its shape. What's gone is your access to it. What remains is mine." He smiled the careful smile, and for the first time it looked like it was hiding something genuine, and she couldn't tell if that was better or infinitely worse. "So. We find ourselves renegotiating after all. I have a message you need, locked in a jar you can't open. You have something I have wanted for a very long time."

"Name it."

"Not coin. Not years—you're running low, and besides, yours taste of valley now." He drew from his coat a folded square of paper, travel-worn, sealed with a smudge of tallow instead of wax. "Payment deferred. A favor, to be collected later, unspecified, non-negotiable, owed to me by you, witnessed by—" he tapped the humming shelves "—everything in this room. That, and you take this letter. It arrived for you two days ago. Smuggled out of Chorus-held territory, which means someone paid in blood to get it here."

The paper trembled slightly in her hand. She knew the handwriting on the front before she broke the seal. Of course she did. She knew it the way you know a thing before you know you know it.

*Sefa.*

She broke the seal and read, and the shop, the shelves, the humming, the fog beyond the walls—all of it fell away, and the words stood alone in the dark like the last lit window in a burning street:

*They have me. Brannoc bargains, don't blame him, everyone breaks, I nearly did. They want the map and they want the one who carries it. Come alone or don't come, but know that I told them nothing, and know that I remember you, Zeph, I swear it, whatever they take I will remember you—*

The letter ended there, mid-line, as if the writer had been interrupted—or had run out of the one thing she was spending to write it.

Zephyrine looked up. Corvess was watching her with an expression she'd never seen on a merchant's face: something almost like pity.

"They took her memories first," he said quietly. "That's how the Chorus works. They take everything a hostage has, so that what's left begging at their cage is only love with nothing attached to it. Pure leverage." He nodded at the letter. "She wrote that from inside the taking. Whatever she remembers when you reach her—if you reach her—will be less than what she remembers now."

Outside, far off across the marsh, a sound rolled in from the direction of the valley. Not thunder. Not wind.

Three notes, sung in perfect harmony, hanging in the fog like marks on a doorframe.

The Chorus was singing. And they were getting closer.
