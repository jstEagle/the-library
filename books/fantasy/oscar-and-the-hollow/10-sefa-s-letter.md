## 10. Sefa's Letter

The letter came inside a wheel of cheese.

Zephyrine found it on the third morning in the border slum of Kettlemark, where the buildings leaned against each other like drunks holding one another upright and the smell of tanneries hung so thick she could taste it. She had taken a room above a dye-house because the fumes masked her scent from anything that tracked by nose, and because the landlady, a woman with a burn-scarred forearm and no curiosity whatsoever, asked no questions about guests who paid in advance and never slept.

She had been eating the cheese for two days.

It was good cheese—hard mountain cheese, waxed rind, the kind the company used to buy in bulk before long contracts—and that was what made her stop chewing. She hadn't bought it. It had appeared at her door wrapped in cloth, payment for nothing, and she had been too hungry and too tired to be suspicious. Now, halfway through the second day of it, some animal part of her brain finally caught up with the rest: *nobody gives you anything in Kettlemark for free.*

She set down the knife. Turned the wheel over. And there, cut into the rind where the wax had been scored and resealed, was a seam that wasn't a seam.

She pried it open with the point of her knife, working slowly, the way you'd disarm a trap, because it might well be one. Inside the hollowed center of the wheel, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a folded letter sealed with plain tallow—no crest, no mark. The paper was cheap. The handwriting was not.

She knew it instantly. You spend eleven years sleeping three feet from someone, you learn their hand the way you learn their breathing.

*Sefa.*

Her hands were steady as she broke the seal. That surprised her. Some part of her had expected them to shake.

---

*Zeph—*

*I don't know if this will reach you. I don't know how much time I have, or what they'll let me say, so I'm going to say all of it and trust you to sort the truth from the rope they've tied around my wrists.*

*They took me nine days ago. Not violently—that's the worst part. Two of them came to the ferry town singing something soft, and everyone on the street forgot I existed while it was happening. I watched a fishwife look straight through me like I was weather. Then they walked me out of town between them and nobody remembered I'd left.*

*Brannoc tried to fight. You know Brannoc. He got six of ours killed doing it, and then he stopped fighting, and I need you to understand why, because you're going to want to hate him and I need you not to.*

*They showed him the ledger.*

*I don't know what the ledger is. They opened it in front of him and he went grey, Zeph, grey like a dead man walking, and after that he did everything they asked. He gave them the company roster. He told them which roads you'd take out of the valley. He wrote your description in their book in his own hand. And every night since, he sits apart from us sharpening his axe and not talking, and I think—I think he's waiting for his moment, or I think he's already given up, and I honestly can't tell which, and that frightens me more than the singing ones do.*

*Here is what they want you to know, because they want you to come, and I am choosing to tell you anyway because you deserve to choose with open eyes:*

*They are keeping me alive as bait. Me specifically. They know what I am to you. They said it plainly, in front of me, the way you'd discuss freight. "The friend," one of them said. "The one who covers for her." They have been reading us for years, Zeph. Every favor, every lie we told for each other, every night we sat up drinking—they have it written down somewhere, catalogued, weighed. Our friendship is a lever and they intend to pull it.*

*So here is my advice, freely given, and I mean it: don't come.*

*Whatever is on that map—and I saw enough of their faces to know it matters more than anything they've ever hunted—whatever door you're looking for, finish it. Let me be the price. I have thought about this every night for nine days and I keep arriving at the same arithmetic: one of me, or a country none of us can even remember losing. I know which one I'd trade. I'd make the trade myself if they'd left me anything to make it with.*

*But I also know you. And I know you're already lacing your boots.*

*So if you come—and gods forgive me, some small cowardly piece of me hopes you do—then hear the rest:*

*They're holding me at a waystation called Grey Ford, half a day south of the shrine road, in a house with a red door. There are four of them plus handlers, and Brannoc and maybe eight of ours who surrendered after the fight. Brannoc is not broken. Watch him. When the moment comes—and there will be a moment; men like Brannoc always get one—he'll take it, and you should be ready to move when he does, not after.*

*The one called Ilex is different from the others. I can't explain how. He looks at me like a man reading a letter he wrote in a language he's forgotten. If any of them can be turned, it's him. Or I'm wrong, and he's the most dangerous kind of trap—the kind that wants you to believe it isn't one.*

*Last thing. They let me keep ink because they read everything I write and they think hope makes better bait than despair. Maybe they're right. But there's one thing I hid from them, small and stupid, and if you're reading this then it worked: check the seal. Not the wax—the impression under it. I pressed my thumb into the tallow before I sealed it, hard, the way I did when we were girls playing at spy-ring in the orchards. If the thumbprint is deep on the left side, it's truly me writing. If it's shallow or absent, burn this letter and run.*

*Look at the seal, Zeph.*

*Look now.*

---

Zephyrine looked.

She had read the whole letter first, hungry for every word, and only now turned the folded page over and held the tallow seal up to the grey light from the window. The wax was plain, unmarked. But underneath, pressed into it before the surface had set, visible only at an angle—

A thumbprint. Deep on the left side. The whorls sharp and clear.

Sefa's thumb. Sefa's hand. Sefa's stubborn, careful, infuriating mind, hiding a verification inside a trap she knew would be read.

Zephyrine sat very still for a long time.

The dye-house fumes seeped through the floorboards. Somewhere below, the landlady argued with a delivery boy about the price of lye. Kettlemark went on being Kettlemark, ugly and indifferent, and Zephyrine Voss sat on the edge of a rented bed holding a letter that was both a lifeline and a hook, and understood—with a clarity that felt almost like peace—that Sefa had done the bravest thing possible: she had told the truth through enemy eyes, knowing they'd allow it, knowing they'd *want* it allowed, and had buried one small true thing beneath it that only the two of them could find.

*Don't come,* the letter said.

*Some small cowardly piece of me hopes you do,* the same letter said.

And underneath both, in the pressure of a thumb in cooling wax: *it's really me. It's really me. It's really me.*

Zephyrine laughed once—a short, cracked sound with nothing in it—and put her face in her hands.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing the thing she was best at, the thing eleven years of mercenary work had ground into her: she made a plan, and then she made plans for the plan failing.

Grey Ford. Half a day south of the shrine road. A house with a red door. Four Chorus agents plus handlers, whatever handlers meant—she pictured clerks, wardens, people with ledgers and instruments. Eight of her own company, surrendered, demoralized, possibly complicit. Brannoc, unreadable, an axe-sharpening question mark. And Sefa, alive, functional, writing letters under supervision—which meant they needed her coherent, which meant they weren't breaking her, which meant either mercy or strategy, and Zephyrine had been a mercenary long enough to bet on strategy every time.

Bait stayed fresh. That was the whole point of bait.

Which meant she had time. Not much—but some. Long enough to think clearly about the question underneath the question, the one Sefa had answered with arithmetic and Zephyrine couldn't:

*One of me, or a country none of us can even remember losing.*

Because that was the real shape of it, wasn't it. The map rode against her ribs in its leather case, seven doors, one inside the Hollow, her own name written in her own forgotten hand. The final door required *a name freely given back.* She didn't yet fully understand what that meant—only that the memory merchant's translation had chilled her, and that the Warden's silence had started to feel less like abandonment and more like a held breath.

And the Chorus wanted the map. Wanted *her*. Had built a cage of love around her—Sefa's love, Brannoc's loyalty, eleven years of shared fires and shared watches—all of it catalogued and weaponized, all of it arranged into a corridor pointing exactly where they wanted her to walk.

If she went to Grey Ford, she gave them the corridor. She walked down it willingly, and everything she was—the map, the name, whatever she was becoming to the silent god she still prayed to out of habit—walked into their hands.

If she didn't go, Sefa died. Not quickly, probably. The Chorus struck Zephyrine as patient in the way glaciers are patient. They would keep her until she stopped being useful, and then unsing her, and the world would forget she had ever stood watch beside anyone, and Zephyrine would carry the mission home alone to a victory no one she loved would be alive to share.

*Love as leverage.* She could almost admire it. It was clean work. Cruelty usually was.

She laid out her gear on the bed and went through it piece by piece, the old ritual, hands moving while the mind worked. Rope. Rations. The tuning fork she'd taken off the scout at the drowned chapel—it hummed faintly when she held it near the map case, a thin silver thread of sound, and she still didn't know what it was for but she'd learned not to discard things that hummed. Her knives. The little tin of ash she kept for marking trails.

And the letter. She read it again. And again. By the fourth reading she had stopped seeing the words and started seeing the structure of them, the way you eventually see the scaffolding behind a wall, and something snagged.

*Watch him. When the moment comes—and there will be a moment; men like Brannoc always get one—he'll take it, and you should be ready to move when he does, not after.*

That wasn't a hostage's line. That was a soldier's line. That was Sefa thinking tactically, feeding her a coordination signal through a censored channel, trusting that Zephyrine would read past the plea and find the plan underneath.

*There is a plan underneath.*

Sefa wasn't just bait. Sefa was a locked door with a key hidden in the lettering. *Ready to move when he does, not after.* Which meant Sefa believed Brannoc would act—believed it strongly enough to stake Zephyrine's life on the timing. And Sefa didn't believe things lightly anymore; nine days in a cage burned optimism out of a person like fever.

So: Brannoc had a moment coming. Sefa had seen it forming. And the Chorus, confident in their ledger and their levers, had left a channel open wide enough for a thumbprint and a battle signal to slip through.

Zephyrine looked at the ceiling beam and let herself, for one full minute, feel it—the whole weight of it, the fear and the fury and the terrible tenderness of being known so completely by someone that your enemies could use that knowing against you. Eleven years of friendship, weighed and measured and priced. They had looked at the best thing in her life and seen a handle.

"Fine," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the Chorus wherever they were listening, to the silent god who still hadn't answered. "You want to pull the lever. Pull it."

Then she got to work.

* * *

She wrote two letters of her own that evening.

The first was short, and she burned it immediately after writing, because it was a test of her own honesty and she didn't need it surviving: *I choose her. I choose her and I don't care what it costs the mission, and if that makes me a bad instrument then the god can go on being silent about it.* She watched the paper curl and blacken in the dye-house brazier and felt no cleaner and no worse. At least she knew herself. That was worth something. That was maybe the only thing the Hollow hadn't eaten yet.

The second letter she wrote carefully, in her own hand, and sealed with her own thumb pressed deep into the tallow—left side, hard, the way Sefa had taught her without teaching her, twenty years ago in an orchard full of green apples.

*To whoever carries this back to Grey Ford:*

*You read her letter. You let it through because you thought it was a hook. Understand what you actually let through: a soldier's message. She told me where. She told me the strength. She told me to watch Brannoc. You censored a prisoner and missed the war in the margins.*

*Here is my answer, and I want it read aloud to your handlers, because I know they read everything too:*

*I'm coming. Not because you pulled the lever—because she asked me not to, and I have never once done what Sefa told me to do, and I refuse to start now that it matters. Your ledger has our friendship in it. It doesn't have this: that we are both stubborn past the reach of sense, and that you cannot build a corridor out of love when the people inside it know every stone.*

*Tell Ilex something, since he seems to be the one who listens. Tell him the drowned chapel door was fused shut from the other side, and that someone tried to open it recently with tools, and failed. Tell him I took the tuning fork off his scout and it hums near my map, and I'd like to know why. Tell him I think his masters are afraid of doors, and I want to know what a Chorus agent does with that thought on a long night watch.*

*And tell them all: I know Grey Ford is a trap. I'm coming anyway. Set the table.*

*—Z.V.*

She read it over twice. It was reckless. It was possibly suicidal. It revealed nothing of the map, the doors, the name, the Hollow's secret—everything that mattered stayed banked and dark—but it told the truth about everything else, and truth, she was learning, was a weapon the Chorus had no defense against, because they dealt entirely in erasure.

She sealed it. Then she considered how to send it, and smiled for the first time in days.

The landlady sold cheese.

* * *

The smuggler was a cheese merchant named Odo Pell who ran the Kettlemark–shrine-road circuit twice a month and had, according to the dye-house gossip, never once been searched, because his cart smelled so overwhelmingly of aged rind that dogs refused to go near it and inspectors waved him through with watering eyes. Zephyrine bought six wheels of his worst stock, overpaid obscenely, and asked him, in the flat tone of a woman purchasing a service rather than a favor, whether he delivered to Grey Ford.

Odo Pell looked at her for a long moment. He had the face of a man who had survived forty years of border commerce by knowing exactly which questions not to ask.

"Grey Ford's got soldiers in it these days," he said. "Quiet ones. Sing-songy."

"I know."

"And you want cheese delivered to quiet sing-songy soldiers."

"I want a wheel of cheese delivered to a woman with a red door over her head. The wheel goes in the kitchen. What happens after that isn't your concern."

He chewed on that, and on a strand of tobacco. "There's a fee for not-asking."

"There's always a fee for not-asking."

They settled it. He took the letter-wheel, marked it with a chalk cross only he would recognize, and loaded it into the cart's heart among its brothers, indistinguishable, invisible, smelling overwhelmingly of itself.

Zephyrine watched the cart roll out of Kettlemark at dawn, south toward the shrine road, toward the red door, toward Sefa, carrying her answer at the speed of a mule's walk.

Then she went back upstairs, packed, checked the map case against her ribs, and took out the tuning fork one more time. She held it near the leather. It sang its thin, silver note—the same note, she realized suddenly, the same exact note, as the sound the Chorus agents had made standing in her doorway, asking polite harmonized questions about a dead man.

They had left her a piece of their own voice. Or the scout had carried it, and she had robbed it.

Either way: she had their pitch now. And a woman who knows the enemy's pitch can learn the enemy's song.

She wrapped the fork away, slung her pack, and paused at the door of the rented room for one last look back—at the bed, the brazier, the ashes of the letter where she'd confessed to herself what she was about to do.

*Rescue serves the mission or dooms it.* That had been the question all day. She'd turned it over like a coin until the faces wore smooth.

Now, stepping out into the grey Kettlemark morning, she found she'd stopped caring which it was. Sefa had done the arithmetic and offered herself up as the smaller number, and it was the bravest, saddest gift anyone had ever given her, and Zephyrine Voss rejected it completely. Not out of nobility. Out of sheer, obstinate, eleven-years-deep refusal to let the world teach her that love was a weakness to be priced.

Let it be leverage. Leverage cuts both ways.

Half a day south, a red door waited. Behind it, a friend with a hidden thumbprint and a plan in the margins. Beside it, a captain sharpening an axe toward a moment only he could see. And somewhere in that house, a man named Ilex who looked at prisoners like forgotten letters, waiting—though he didn't know it yet—to be handed a truth he couldn't unsing.

She turned south and started walking.

Behind her, faint and far, so faint she might have imagined it, the Hollow's silence seemed to shift—like a held breath about to end.
