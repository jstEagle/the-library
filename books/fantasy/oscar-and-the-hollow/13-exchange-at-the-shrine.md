## 13. Exchange at the Shrine

The Chorus camp sat in the bowl of a dead field three miles north of the shrine, and they made no effort to hide it. That was the message. White tents in rows too neat for soldiers, banners of unbleached cloth that moved without wind, and at the center a pavilion hung with bells—small ones, copper-throated, that rang no sound Zephyrine could hear but that she felt in her teeth like a word held behind closed lips.

She watched them from the tree line for most of an afternoon, the map case heavy against her ribs, Sefa's letter folded soft as a wound inside her glove. *They have me. Brannoc is cooperating because they let him believe cooperation buys time. Come alone or I am unsung.* The handwriting had deteriorated toward the end, the letters losing their confidence, and Zephyrine had read it forty times looking for coercion in the spacing, a code in the slant. She found nothing but fear.

They knew she'd come. Of course they knew. You did not take a hostage from a mercenary company unless you wanted the mercenary, and everything the Chorus wanted lately wore her face and carried a dead cartographer's map.

So she would not sneak. Sneaking was what they expected, and sneaking was what their marks on doorframes were for—listening things, humming things, tuned to the footsteps of one particular woman. She had spent two days confirming it: every approach to the camp hummed when she came near, a faint resonant shiver in the air, like plucked strings waiting for their note.

Fine. She would give them the note.

* * *

She walked out of the trees at dusk with her hands empty and her sword left buried under a cairn she built herself, which felt like burying a limb. The sentries saw her at two hundred paces and did not shout. They simply turned, all of them along the whole picket line, in perfect simultaneity, the way a choir turns toward its conductor.

That was the thing about the Chorus that no report captured, no rumor conveyed rightly: they were never frightening individually. It was the together-ness. A dozen faces, calm and mild and attentive, all wearing the same expression the way a congregation wears vestments. They parted for her down the central lane of the camp without a word, and the silence between the tents was so complete she could hear her own blood.

Someone began to sing.

It was low, wordless, a single sustained tone from a woman near the cookfires—not a warning, not a challenge. A greeting, maybe. Or a test. Another voice joined it a fifth above, then a third below, until the harmony wrapped around the camp like weather, and Zephyrine understood what it was for. It was how they thought. It was how they spoke to each other beneath speech. And it was how they would know if she was afraid, because fear has a frequency, and bodies broadcast whether they mean to or not.

Her knees wanted to shake. She let them want it and refused them.

Instead she listened. Ten years in a valley where magic could not follow had stripped her of every gift except one: attention. The Hollow had taught her to hear the shape of silence, the grain of absence, and now, walking into the throat of the enemy, she finally understood why the Warden's silence had trained her so long. She heard where the harmony leaned. Where it wobbled. Where a voice held a note slightly sharp because the singer was tired, or doubtful, or watching her walk past with something other than duty in his eyes.

And on the fourth phrase, she opened her mouth and sang back.

Not their melody. Their harmony—the missing line, the space their chord left open, filled. She had heard it for half a mile and her ear had already done the work; ten years of listening to a god's silence will teach you to find the note that isn't there. Her voice was rough, untrained, a soldier's voice, but it landed in the gap precisely, and the whole camp stumbled.

Every singer faltered at once. The harmony cracked like ice underfoot. Heads turned—dozens of them, white-clad, mild-faced—and for one full breath nobody breathed.

Then the woman by the cookfire laughed. It was a warm sound, horribly out of place, and she clapped her hands twice. "Oh," she said, in a voice like honey poured over iron. "Oh, you're going to be *interesting*."

Zephyrine kept walking. "I've come for Sefa Anwar. You have her. I'm the price. Let's not waste the evening pretending otherwise."

"And the map?"

"Stays mine until I see her walk out of this camp alive and unmarked."

"That," said the woman, falling into step beside her—she was older than Zephyrine had guessed, silver-streaked, with the patient face of someone who had ended many countries and found none of it difficult—"is not how exchanges work."

"It's how this one works." Zephyrine stopped and faced her, and let herself be seen: unarmed, outnumbered, entirely certain. "You didn't take her friend to get the map. If you wanted the map you'd have taken it off the cartographer's body before he bled out. You took her to get *me*. Which means the map is worthless to you unless I open it—or unless whatever's written on me opens it. So here is my only offer. Her freedom, freely given, in exchange for me, freely given. Freely matters." She tapped her own sternum. "I've read your margins. Names given under duress don't sing."

Something flickered across the woman's composure—not anger. Appetite. "Where did you read our margins?"

"In a drowned chapel, on a door you people fused shut and failed to open. You're welcome for the reconnaissance." She resumed walking before her heartbeat could betray her. Courage was mostly momentum, she had learned; stand still too long and it curdled into honesty.

The woman accompanied her in silence to the central pavilion, where the bells hung motionless, and there she paused at the threshold and studied Zephyrine the way a jeweler studies a stone for the flaw that tells the whole story. "Sefa Anwar will be released at dawn," she said at last. "Unharmed, unmarked, with a horse and provisions. You will remain. And when we are finished learning what you carry, you will wish you had been merely killed." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, which was the worst part. "My name is Maureth. You may remember it. Few do, afterward."

"I'm bad at remembering things," Zephyrine said. "Ask anyone who still knows me."

Maureth's smile thinned by a degree. "Take her to the second tent," she called, and two white-cloaked figures materialized at Zephyrine's elbows—gentle grips, courteous, absolute—and led her away through the singing camp while the harmony rebuilt itself around her like water closing over a stone.

* * *

The tent they gave her was clean, warm, and locked from the outside, which told her everything about how the Chorus thought: even a cage should be comfortable enough that the prisoner stops noticing it. There was a cot, a basin, a lamp of some smokeless flame, and nothing sharp, nothing long, nothing with which a determined woman might improve her situation. On the small table, someone had left bread, cheese, and a clay cup of wine.

She ate the bread. She left the wine. In Vael—that nameless country whose shape lived now only in ink and in the ache behind her eyes—you did not drink what enemies poured. Some rules survived the erasure of everything that taught them.

She was still awake when the tent flap moved near midnight.

He came in without ceremony, a lean man with grey at his temples and the careful posture of someone carrying something fragile internally. He set a second lamp on the table, sat on the stool opposite her cot, and looked at her with an expression she couldn't immediately classify because she had never seen it on a captor before: reluctance.

"You're the one from the chapel," he said.

"You're the one whose scout lost a tuning fork."

His jaw tightened fractionally. "Ilex," he said, as if the name cost him. "I'm ordered to interrogate you."

"Tonight? I assumed dawn. Villains love dawn."

"The order says tonight." He unfolded a slip of paper, glanced at it, and she watched him decide not to read it aloud. He put it away. "Maureth believes you'll resist standard methods. She wants... a gentler instrument first. Rapport. Trust. The theory being that what you carry responds to names freely shared, so the interrogation should feel like friendship."

Zephyrine sat up slowly. "And do you believe that?"

"I believe orders," Ilex said, and it sounded like a man reciting a prayer he'd stopped hearing years ago. Then, quieter, almost against his will: "You sang our fourth harmony. Nobody does that. Nobody *hears* it."

"The valley taught me. Ten years of listening to a god say nothing—you learn to hear what's missing." She held his gaze. "What's your missing note, Ilex?"

For a moment—half a breath—he looked like a man standing at the edge of a memory he could not climb down into. His hand drifted to his collarbone, touched something beneath the cloth that might have been a pendant, and dropped. "That," he said, rising, "is exactly the kind of question Maureth pays me to ask other people."

He went to the flap, paused with his hand on the canvas.

"Dawn," he said, not turning around. "When they take you to her. Whatever you think you know about what's coming—it's worse than that. And better. I haven't decided which."

The flap fell shut. Outside, the camp sang on in its perfect, terrible accord, and Zephyrine lay back on the cot and pressed the map case flat against her heart like a second pulse, and waited for morning with courage braided tight around recklessness, wondering which strand would snap first.

Because she had lied to Maureth about one thing. The margins hadn't only told her how the doors opened.

They had told her what happened to those who opened them wrong.

And somewhere in this camp, tonight, someone had begun tuning the bells.
