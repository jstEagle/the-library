## 14. Two Prisoners

*Zephyrine*

They kept her in a tent that smelled of lamp oil and cold iron, and they did not tie her hands.

That was the first insult. The Chorus did not need rope; the mark on her wrist—pressed there when they took her at the shrine's edge, a small sigil like a tuning fork laid against the skin—hummed whenever she thought of running. It did not hurt. That was worse. It simply reminded her, with every pulse, that her body was no longer entirely hers to command.

She had been sitting in the dark of the tent for what felt like hours when the flap opened and Ilex came in carrying two cups of something steaming.

He set one down in front of her without ceremony and sat cross-legged on the opposite side of the low brazier, his coat folded neatly beneath him, his boots off. Off. As if this were a parlor and she a guest.

"You're not my interrogator," she said.

"I am, actually." He wrapped his hands around his cup. "I requested it."

"Why?"

"Because I'm the only one who'll ask you questions worth answering." He blew across the surface of his drink. "The others would ask where the map is. You'd lie beautifully, and we'd all waste the night. I'd rather ask you things you can't lie about."

Zephyrine lifted the cup. She sniffed it first—barley, salt, a thread of some bitter herb—and then drank, because refusing would look like fear, and fear was the last coin she could afford to spend tonight. "And what can't I lie about?"

"What Vael smelled like."

The tent went very still. Outside, wind moved through the Chorus camp, and somewhere a sentry shifted his weight, leather creaking.

"I don't know any country called Vael," Zephyrine said.

"No," Ilex agreed. "Nobody does. That's rather the point." He looked up at her, and in the brazier light his face was younger than it had seemed at the ferry town—no, that wasn't right either. Not younger. *Unfinished*, as though someone had begun carving him and stopped before adding the details that would make him particular. "I don't know it either. But my orders say it existed, and my orders are never wrong about anything except the things they've taken from me. So here is my question, mercenary: when I say *Vael*, does your chest hurt?"

She wanted to say no.

What came out instead, quietly, before she could stop it, was: "Like a bruise I can't find."

Ilex nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd suspected for years. "Mine too," he said. "Every time. And I've never known why."

* * *

*Ilex*

He should have been extracting information. That was the assignment Handler Verre had given him, delivered in that mild voice that made atrocity sound like bookkeeping: learn where the map is, learn who else has seen it, and if the mercenary proves resistant, begin the softening.

Instead he sat in a tent drinking barley tea with the enemy and telling her things he had told no living soul.

"The first country I unsang, I don't remember its name either. None of us do. That's the design." He turned his cup slowly on his knee. "We're given the name for the duration of the work—we have to hold it, speak it backward, syllable by syllable, while the choir sustains the counter-note. And when the work is finished, the name goes out of us like breath out of a room. We wake up tired. We wash our hands. We eat. There's always a meal after. Someone decided long ago that we should be fed after."

Zephyrine watched him across the coals. Her eyes were the color of river water over slate, and they did not flinch. "How many?"

"Countries?" He counted the meals instead, because the meals he remembered. "Eleven meals I can account for. Eleven mornings waking exhausted with my hands smelling of ash that wouldn't scrub off. So. Eleven."

"And you feel nothing."

"That's the thing." He set the cup down. His voice came out lower than he intended. "I don't know what I feel. Because whatever grief or shame belonged to those nights was stored inside the names themselves. They took the name and everything wrapped around it went with it. Do you understand? I might have loved someone in one of those countries. A whole country, eleven chances—there could be a person, a street, a garden, a debt I owed and never paid. And I can't even mourn them properly, because mourning requires a shape, and they cut the shape out of me along with everything else."

The fire ticked. Somewhere beyond the canvas, the camp's night-choir began its low maintenance drone, three voices holding the note that kept the mark on her wrist obedient. He hated that drone. He had never let himself hate it before.

"When did you start asking these questions?" Zephyrine asked.

"Three nights ago." He almost smiled. "You want to know what did it? A village order. Burn order, remnant survivors—the usual. Standard procedure, we've done it dozens of times, and I stood there with the torch and I thought: *what if I knew them?* What if one of these eleven countries I ate was mine? What if I was born somewhere I helped erase, and the only reason I can't grieve for it is because I'm the knife?" He shook his head. "Verre noticed the hesitation. Handlers always notice. I bought myself this interrogation because it was either sit across from you or stand in front of a village, and I no longer trust what I'd do with a torch in my hand."

"You think mercy makes you weak."

"I think doubt makes me *useful*," Ilex said, "and useful men get used until they're worn through. I've seen what happens to agents who hesitate twice."

Zephyrine leaned forward. The brazier lit half her face and left the other half in the kind of shadow that maps are drawn in. "Then stop hesitating," she said. "Decide."

"It isn't that simple."

"It never is. Decide anyway." She reached into her coat, slowly, letting him see every movement, and drew out the map case—not the map itself, but the case, scarred leather, the dying cartographer's blood still ghosted brown along the seam. She held it between them like a wager. "Your masters want this. Your masters' master wants what's *inside* this. And I am going to tell you what your own orders won't: what you people actually serve."

*Ilex*

He didn't touch the case.

His training said touch it, confirm it, signal the watchers outside the tent with the small gesture behind the ear, and this entire conversation becomes an asset acquisition. His training said the woman across from him was a remnant-sympathizer and a liar and a threat to the harmony of the world, and the word *harmony* snagged in him now like a burr, because he had begun to hear what the harmony cost.

"Tell me," he said instead.

"The thing beneath your citadel," Zephyrine said, "isn't a god. Gods have names. This thing eats them. Every country your choir unsings, every name you carry back singing backward—that doesn't vanish. It gets swallowed. Fed. You aren't erasers, Ilex. You're *kitchen hands*. Eleven courses and counting, and Vael was going to be the twelfth, and the Warden—" she caught herself, jaw tightening, "—the god I swore to gave up his own name to slam the door on it. Ten years of silence. I thought he'd abandoned me. He was choking himself to keep it fed on nothing."

The words landed in him with the specific weight of truth—he'd carried enough lies to know the difference. Lies slid off. This sank.

Because he had seen the vault. Once, early, before he'd risen far enough to understand what he was looking at: a door of black stone beneath the citadel, and a sound coming through it that was not a sound, a hunger shaped like listening. He had been told it was the First Silence, the holy origin, the note before notes. He had bowed toward it every morning of his adult life.

It had never once felt holy. He'd just never had another word for it.

"If that's true," he said slowly, "then the remnant burnings—"

"Are sweeping up crumbs," she said. "People who survived an unsinging because they were outside the border, or deaf, or stubborn. Leftover pieces the thing below can't reach on its own. Your choir burns the villages so the scraps don't linger." She tilted her head. "Did you really think a harmonious world required so much ash?"

He had asked himself exactly that, standing in front of the village with the torch. He had not dared answer.

Outside, the night-choir's drone wavered—one singer's breath failing, caught, recovered. In that half-second gap, the mark on Zephyrine's wrist flickered, and he saw her flex her fingers experimentally, testing the slack in her own body.

"Here's what I'm offering," she said. "Not the map. Not yet. The truth. Come with me to the second door—there's one under the shrine in the Hollow, under my god, and it's real, and it opens. When it opens, everything your masters swallowed comes back out. Including eleven names. Including whoever you loved in them, if anyone." Her voice dropped. "Including whether you loved anyone at all. You want to know if you have a sister, a garden, a debt? That door knows. It remembers what was eaten."

Ilex sat very still. Hope, he discovered, was a physical pain, located somewhere under the sternum, and he had apparently been carrying it untreated for years without naming it.

"They'll kill you for this," he said. "If they find out what you've told me."

"They're going to kill me regardless. The question is what I'm worth dead." She pushed the empty map case across the ground between them—an offering, empty deliberately, a show of trust that cost her something visible. "So. Agent of the Chorus. Kitchen hand to a thing that eats countries. Are you going to signal your watchers, or are you going to ask me how the door works?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, deliberately, keeping his eyes on hers, he reached up and touched the place behind his ear—and did nothing.

*Zephyrine*

Later—they would both remember later, in the way you remember the exact texture of a floor you nearly died on—Ilex pulled back the corner of the tent flap and showed her the camp.

It was worse than she'd imagined. Not the soldiers; soldiers were soldiers everywhere. It was the scaffolding. Rising at the camp's center, black timber lashed with cord made of something that was not hemp, climbing toward a cage of iron suspended against the stars. And in the cage—

"Sefa." The name tore out of her before she could soften it.

Her friend hung by the wrists, head bowed, hair matted, alive—alive, breathing, the shallow fast breathing of the exhausted but *breathing*—and around the cage stood six singers, holding a chord so low she felt it in her teeth. Not torture. Extraction. They were humming the memory out of Sefa one strand at a time, and even from here Zephyrine could see it working: the way Sefa's mouth moved sometimes, shaping a name that kept slipping away from her even as she tried to hold it.

"She's the bait," Ilex said quietly. "Verre's plan was always to trade her back to you—for the map. But now that you traded yourself instead, she's become a problem. Remnants in custody are processed within the week." He paused. "There will be a burning."

Zephyrine's hands closed into fists, and the mark on her wrist hummed warningly, and she forced her fingers open one at a time, because rage was a luxury and she had learned to budget. "When?"

"Four days. The full choir convenes for it—it's meant to be a lesson." He let the flap fall. In the dark again, his face was unreadable, but his voice wasn't; his voice had a crack running through it now, fresh and clean as ice before it gives. "I can get you out of this tent. I can probably get us both past the outer pickets—I know the watch rotations better than Verre thinks I do. But I can't open her cage alone, and I can't outrun the full choir, and once I do this there is no going back to what I was." He laughed, short and unsteady. "Which, granted, is a man who doesn't know whether he has a sister."

"Then we go for all of it," Zephyrine said. "Sefa, the Hollow, the shrine, the door underneath my god. Four days is enough—if we move tomorrow, before the choir convenes and while their best voices are scattered." She met his eyes across the brazier's dying coals. "But understand what you're choosing. On the other side of that door, everything comes back. Their names, your memories, my—" her voice caught, and she rode through it, "—everything the Hollow took from me. All of it returns when Vael is spoken aloud. And the Chorus ends. Every agent. Whatever you were before, whoever they cut out of you—you'll have to meet him."

Ilex was quiet for a long time. The coals settled. Beyond the canvas, the drone held its patient, hungry note.

"My handler suspects me already," he finally said. "By tomorrow night he'll have proof whether I act or not. So yes." He extended his hand across the ashes, palm open—a mercenary's gesture, she noted, not a chorister's. Somewhere under the training, under the washing of hands and the meals afterward, the man he'd been before was already reaching through. "All of it. The cage, the shrine, the door. And when it opens—"

"When it opens," Zephyrine said, taking his hand, "we find out who we were."

Neither of them saw the shadow detach itself from the tent's outer wall, nor heard it move away toward the command pavilion, moving with the silent certainty of someone who had listened to every word—and whose step, as it crossed the trampled snow, made no sound at all.
