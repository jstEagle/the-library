## 15. The Chorus's True Purpose

Ilex had been in the citadel eleven days before he understood what the singing was for.

He had assumed, as every initiate was taught to assume, that the Chorus's harmonies were instruments of erasure—that they sang a country's name apart from itself the way a surgeon cuts a tumor from living flesh. Unpleasant work. Necessary work. Work that required voices trained to hold perfect intervals while cities went quiet beneath them. He had done it twice before Vael, and both times he had told himself the same thing: *the world is cleaner without what we remove.*

The lie held until he descended.

It began with an order. His handler, Maestress Corvane, summoned him at the hour when the citadel's lamps burned lowest and gave him a task so mundane it felt like an insult: carry the sealed ledgers of the Vael operation down to the Archive Below and log them. Not copy them. Log them. She watched him with those pale eyes of hers while she said it, and Ilex understood that this was not errand but examination—the same test she'd been administering since the bridge, since the village of remnants, since the moment she'd caught hesitation on his face like a moth in her cupped hands.

"Take the east stair," she said. "Do not sing on the way down."

"Do singers ever?"

"No." A pause, thin as a blade drawn halfway from its sheath. "That is rather the point."

* * *

The east stair went down farther than any building should allow.

Ilex counted the turns out of professional habit—forty-one landings, each one colder than the last—and by the thirtieth the stone had changed character entirely. The upper citadel was built of quarried granite, dressed and mortared, proud. This was older rock, veined with something dark that might have been mineral or might have been vein, and the walls sweated a damp that smelled not of water but of absence. It was the smell of the unsung roads, of the places where Vael's erasure had left reality wounded: the scent of a room after a voice has stopped echoing.

His lantern guttered twice. Both times, the flame bent *downward*, toward whatever lay below, like iron filings finding their lodestone.

At the forty-first landing there was no door. There was only a threshold where the stone simply decided to be elsewhere, and Ilex stepped through it because stopping would have meant thinking, and thinking on that stair was a dangerous luxury.

The Archive Below was not an archive.

Oh, there were shelves—he saw them dimly in the lantern light, ranks upon ranks of them receding into dark, laden with objects that gleamed dull and patient. But they were not books. He came close to the nearest shelf because his body moved before his mind could forbid it, and he looked at what the Chorus had spent centuries gathering.

Names.

Not written names. Not carved ones. Each object on the shelf held a name the way amber holds an insect: a river-stone that hummed when he leaned near, humming the syllables of some drowned watercourse; a child's wooden bird that whispered a girl's name over and over in a voice gone thin with age; a bell with no clapper that nonetheless rang, faintly, endlessly, with the name of a town Ilex had never heard of and could not make himself hear clearly, because the name slid off his attention like rain off oiled cloth.

Erased things did not vanish. Erased things were *kept*.

He stood in the aisle with his breath fogging despite the warmth, and he understood, in the slow horrible way one understands a betrayal, that nothing the Chorus had ever destroyed had been destroyed. Every country unsung, every language unmade, every family dissolved into forgetting—all of it was here. Shelved. Preserved. *Stored.*

You do not store what you hate. You store what you intend to eat.

* * *

He found the ledgers' alcove by following the numbers Corvane had given him, and it was there, logging the Vael operation into a great ledger whose previous entries stretched back further than the Chorus officially existed, that he learned the shape of the truth.

The entries were catalogued like meals.

*Vael — received whole. Seven doors; six closed, one contested. Quality: exceptional. Note the Warden's sacrifice—self-sealing gods are rare, and the resonance of a god's surrendered name carries the vintage of an entire pantheon's age. Deliver to the Table upon resolution of the seventh door.*

*Prior entry: Sennivar — received whole. Quality: adequate. Somewhat thinned by remnant migration. Deliver to the Table.*

*Prior entry: [name illegible—Ilex's eyes refused to hold it] — received whole. Quality: the finest of my tenure. We shall not taste its like again for a century.*

*Ilex read the line three times. Then a fourth. His hand, holding the quill to log Vael's ledger among its fellows, had gone perfectly steady—the steadiness of a man who has stepped off a cliff and has not yet begun to fall.*

*A table. A tasting. A vintage. The words arranged themselves into a sentence he did not want to know and could not unknow: something beneath the citadel ate what the Chorus gathered, and the Chorus—his Chorus, his life, his purpose—was not a scalpel. It was a kitchen.*

*He thought of Maestress Corvane's pale eyes. Of the initiates singing in the practice halls, perfect and disciplined and proud of their precision. Of himself, on the night Vael was unmade, holding his note while a country screamed itself into silence—and he had felt, beneath the discipline, a fullness. A satisfaction. He had told himself it was the satisfaction of duty completed.*

*It had been appetite. Borrowed appetite. They fed him scraps of it to keep the singers willing.*

*He set down the quill very carefully, the way one sets down a weapon one no longer trusts oneself to hold.*

* * *

He should have logged the ledger and climbed back up. That was the mission. That was survival. Corvane was already watching him; a second hesitation would be his last.

But forty shelves away, in the lantern's reach, sat a bird of carved wood.

He told himself he only wanted to look. He told himself a great many things as he crossed the aisle, and none of them survived contact with the moment he picked the toy up. It was light. It was worn smooth by small hands. And when it touched his palm it stopped whispering the strange girl's name and began, instead, to whisper another—one that arrived in his mind not as sound but as recognition, the way a face arrives when you wake from a dream of someone you'd forgotten you knew.

*Ilex.*

*His own name. Spoken in a child's voice, from inside a memory he did not have.*

*He stood very still. The lantern shook in his other hand. And beneath the terror, rising through it like a spring through floodwater, came something else—not a memory, memories could be taken, he knew now how thoroughly they could be taken—but the *shape* of one. An outline left behind. The way a pressed flower keeps the flower's geometry long after the scent and color are gone.*

*A garden. Sunlight through leaves. Someone small running ahead of him on a path, laughing, calling back over her shoulder—calling a name that was not Ilex's and was, somehow, also entirely his to answer.*

*"Slow down," he heard himself say, in a voice ten years younger. "Slow down, you'll—"*

*Nothing. The shape ended. The bird whispered his name once more and went quiet, and Ilex stood in the dark beneath the world holding a dead child's toy and understanding that somewhere in these shelves was everything he had ever been made to forget—including, possibly, whether he had ever loved anyone enough to grieve her.*

*Corvane's voice, in memory, instructing the initiates: "We do not mourn what we remove. To mourn is to misfile."*

*Misfiled, then. He was misfiled. He had been all along—a man filed under loyalty who belonged under grief.*

*He put the bird back exactly where it had sat. He smoothed the dust. And he climbed the forty-one landings with the ledgers under his arm and his face composed into the blank competence that had kept him alive through two erasures, while behind his sternum something new and terrible took root and began, quietly, to grow.*

*Resolve does not feel like fire. He had always imagined it would. It felt like this instead: cold, precise, patient as the stone around him. A single note held against a chorus that believed he was still singing with them.*

* * *

Maestress Corvane was waiting at the top of the east stair.

She stood in the lamplight with her hands folded, and she smiled the way the Chorus smiled—warmly, precisely, with nothing behind it but training. "You were longer than the task requires."

"The archive is vast," Ilex said. "I got turned about."

"Did you." Her pale eyes moved over his face the way an appraiser's fingers move over porcelain, checking for cracks. "And did anything down there... turn about in you?"

*Careful. Careful.* He let confusion into his expression, measured, a spoonful and no more. "It's a grim place. Shelves of junk. Stones and toys and bells. I don't understand what half of it is."

Something eased in her shoulders—barely, barely—but Ilex had spent a decade learning to read the micro-movements of people deciding whether to kill him, and he marked it. "Junk," she agreed pleasantly. "Relics of resolved operations. You needn't think about them." She took the ledgers from his arms, and her gloved fingers brushed the binding of Vael's, and lingered. "Though I confess curiosity, Ilex. The prisoner we took at the shrine—Zephyrine Voss. You've been questioning her. Report."

"She's stubborn. Grief-hardened. She believes her silent god will save her." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him more than any performance of his life, because it was true and it was a lie and the difference between those things was the width of the knife at his own throat. "She'll break. They always break."

"They do," Corvane said softly. "Especially the named ones. Names are such fragile things to carry." She turned to go, then paused, her back to him. "One more thing. When the seventh door opens—and it will open—we begin delivery. The Table hungers, and Vael has ripened beautifully. You should be proud, Ilex. How many singers can say they helped serve a god?"

He bowed. "I'm proud," he said, and his voice did not shake, and later, alone in his narrow cell with the lamp out, lying on bare boards with his heart hammering like a fist on a door, he would take that sentence apart syllable by syllable and understand it for what it now was:

A vow, spoken in enemy harmony. A note held true.

They had made him a singer. They had never asked what song he would choose, given the choice—and the choice had just come down the forty-one steps to meet him, wearing a dead child's laughter and a mercenary woman's stubborn face.

Somewhere below him, Zephyrine Voss sat in a cell, still carrying the map, still refusing to break. Above him, Corvane walked the corridors humming a chord that made the walls forget themselves. And beneath them all, older than the Chorus, older than the shelves, something vast and patient waited at its Table for the next course.

Vael was not the last country to be eaten. But if Ilex had anything left to say about it—anything at all, even just one voice against a thousand—

it would be the first one ever taken back.
