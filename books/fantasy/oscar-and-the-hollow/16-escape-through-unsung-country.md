## 16. Escape Through Unsung Country

They ran out of Vael's absence at dawn, and the dawn was wrong.

Zephyrine had seen bad light before—the green-black bruise of storm skies over the Salt Marches, the amber haze that preceded wildfire—but this was different. The sun rose and its rays bent slightly, as if the horizon were a bowl and the world inside it had been scooped out and never quite refilled. The land ahead of them should have been farmland. She knew it should have been farmland because the cartographer's map said so, in her own handwriting: *three leagues north, grain fields, a well at the crossroads.* Instead there was a plain of pale grass that grew in perfect concentric rings, each ring a shade fainter than the last, as though someone had diluted the color of the earth with every pass.

"The unsinging," Ilex said behind her. He spoke softly, the way men do in cathedrals, though there was no cathedral here—only the rings, only the silence between them. "This is what's left when you pull a name out by the roots. The scar tissue."

"Then don't touch anything," Zephyrine said, and kept walking.

Sefa stumbled on the second ring. Not from exhaustion—they had all been exhausted for two days now, since the Chorus camp, since Ilex cut their bonds with his own ceremonial knife and walked out of the only life he'd ever known—but from something else. She caught herself on air that wasn't quite solid enough to hold her weight, and for a moment her foot sank an inch into ground that behaved more like water than earth, rippling outward from her boot.

"Sefa." Zephyrine turned back, took her arm. "Careful. The ground doesn't know what it is here."

"Neither does my head," Sefa muttered, but she gripped back, hard, and that grip was the realest thing in the whole pale morning.

They crossed the rings in single file. Where the grass grew faintest, sound died—not muffled, not dampened, simply *absent*. Zephyrine heard her own footsteps only when she remembered to listen for them, and twice she looked down to confirm her boots still struck the earth, because the evidence of her ears refused to cooperate. Ilex hummed under his breath, low and constant, some fragment of Chorus harmony, and when Zephyrine glared at him he shrugged. "It helps," he said. "A voice keeps the quiet from settling into you. Trust me. I know what the quiet does."

She did trust him, which was its own kind of wound. Three days ago she had been his prisoner. Two days ago she had watched him turn his knife against his handlers' orders and understood, in one vertiginous instant, that the Chorus could be betrayed from within—and that she would have to bet everything on the conscience of a man who had helped erase a country and couldn't remember if he'd loved anyone in it.

Now the three of them fled together across a scar where that country used to be, and the country itself was fighting them, or grieving them, or both.

* * *

The road ended mid-stride at noon.

Not faded. Not washed out. It simply stopped—a packed-earth lane running arrow-straight through the pale grass, wagon ruts crisp as yesterday, and then, at a line so clean it might have been ruled with a blade, nothing. Ahead of the line the grass grew in its strange concentric waves. The ruts didn't continue. The road didn't crumble or erode; it had been *unmade*, and the unmaking was still fresh enough to ache.

Sefa crouched at the edge and put her palm just above the line, not touching. "There's a village past here," she said. "I can almost—" Her brow furrowed. "I can almost see it. Chimneys. A bell tower. There's a bell tower, isn't there?"

"There was," Ilex said quietly. "Vaelmoor. Population four hundred and eleven." He said it like a man reciting penance. "I sang the order myself."

"You *what?*" Sefa's head came up.

"I sang the order. That's how it works. We don't burn countries down—we sing them out. Every name, every street, every child's nickname, every grave marker, folded into a chord and given to—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Given to something. I never asked what. You're not supposed to ask. The song takes your memory of the place as payment, and afterward you can't even remember that you've forgotten, except—" his hand rose unconsciously to his sternum, "—except there's a hollow where it was. Like a tooth pulled. You feel the gap with your tongue your whole life and you never learn why."

Zephyrine watched him say it and thought about the Warden in his shrine, sealed beneath ten years of silence, holding shut a door with his own surrendered name. A god who had pulled his own tooth. Who had let himself be forgotten rather than be used as a key. She had spent a decade praying to that silence as though it were abandonment. Now she knew it was sacrifice, and the knowledge sat in her chest like swallowed stone.

"We go around," she said. "The map shows a river crossing two leagues east."

"The rivers here don't run right," Sefa warned. "You'll see."

They saw. An hour later they crested a low rise and found the river—or the wound that had been a river—flowing uphill. Not dramatically, not in defiance of sense, but gently, patiently, water climbing the slope in a smooth silver ribbon toward a place where the valley narrowed into a whiteness that wasn't fog and wasn't light. Toward absence. The water went up and up and thinned as it climbed, and near the top it simply ceased, droplet by droplet, like a sentence trailing off.

"It flows toward where the river's name used to be," Ilex murmured. "Everything here leans toward its erasure. Water, roads, birdsong. The land remembers which way it was pulled."

"Can we cross it?" Zephyrine asked.

"At the bottom, where it's still ordinary. Before the climb starts." He was already scanning the banks. "But quickly. And whatever happens—don't drink it. Water flowing toward absence carries pieces of what was taken. Drink it and you'll remember things that never happened to you. Or worse, you'll start remembering *wrongly*, and the wrongness spreads."

Sefa laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "Listen to us. Rules for crossing cursed rivers. Six months ago I was arguing with Brannoc about bridge tolls."

Zephyrine didn't answer. She couldn't. Because six months ago she had argued with Brannoc too, and Brannoc was dead now—she was nearly certain, nearly, though no one had told them and no messenger had survived the pursuit long enough—and the grief for him kept arriving in installments, deferred by flight, paid in odd moments like this one, on hillsides, at wrong rivers.

They crossed at the knee-deep shallows, boots in current that pushed strangely upward, and none of them drank, and none of them spoke, and on the far bank Sefa paused and frowned at Zephyrine with a small, terrible politeness.

"Do I know you?" Sefa asked. "From before. From the company. I know I know you—I have your face in my head—but I can't find the rest of it. Your name is..." Her fingers pressed against her temple. "Your name is *there*. I can feel the shape of where it sits. Why can't I feel the name?"

Zephyrine's heart did something slow and catastrophic.

"It's the country," she said, keeping her voice level, because panic was a luxury and lies were a tool and she had been lying well since the Chorus stood at her door humming. "The erasure bleeds. It eats names first. Yours will come back once we're clear."

"When will we be clear?"

"Soon." She took Sefa's hand. "Come on. Keep talking to me. Tell me about bridge tolls."

And Sefa talked—about Brannoc's fury at the ferry guild, about the winter the company had eaten nothing but pickled eel, about small stupid beautiful things—and Zephyrine listened and fed her details whenever the thread frayed, and with every league Sefa needed feeding more often, and the gaps between the details widened like cracks in ice.

By dusk, Sefa had forgotten Zephyrine three times.

* * *

They made camp in the shell of a farmhouse that had half-finished the journey into nonexistence. Its walls stood; its interior was a suggestion. Rooms opened into rooms that shouldn't connect. A staircase ascended to a ceiling. In the kitchen, a table bore plates set for a meal no one had eaten, and the plates held shadows of food—not images, shadows, dark shapes on white ceramic where bread and stew had declined to keep existing.

Ilex built no fire. "Light draws the pursuit," he said. "And worse, light draws attention from the land itself. Places like this are still listening for the song that finished them. If they hear voices raised in harmony, they may try to... complete themselves. Around us."

"So we sit in the dark," Sefa said.

"We sit in the dark."

They sat in the dark. And in the dark, Zephyrine felt Sefa's hand find hers in the way old friends' hands find each other, blind and certain, and heard Sefa whisper:

"I'm losing you faster than I should be. Ilex says it's proximity to the wounds. He says everyone forgets eventually, that's how the erasure works, it propagates. But mine is going fast, Zeph. This morning I couldn't find your name. An hour ago I couldn't find your face. Right now I'm holding your hand and I'm telling myself it must be yours because whose else would it be, and I can't—I can't see—" Her breath hitched. "Talk. Please. Say things only you'd say. Give me something to anchor to before the tide comes in."

So Zephyrine talked. Low, in the blackness, she gave Sefa the inventory of a decade of friendship: the time they'd drunk the mercenary captain of Harrow's Ford under the table and stolen his boots; the night Sefa had stitched Zephyrine's shoulder by lantern-light and cried more than she had; the oath-day, when Zephyrine had sworn herself to a silent god and Sefa alone hadn't laughed. She poured it all out like a woman bailing a flooding boat with her cupped hands, knowing the sea was bigger, doing it anyway.

And somewhere past midnight, Sefa's hand went slack in hers, and Sefa said, politely, warmly, terribly:

"Thank you for staying with me. I know we just met, but you seem—you seem like someone I would have loved very much. Did I tell you that? I feel like I would have loved you."

Zephyrine lay in the dark and did not sleep, and grief accelerated through her like floodwater through a broken dam. Not the clean grief of death—death leaves a shape you can mourn. This was erosion. This was watching a friend become a stranger in real time, becoming a stranger *to the friendship itself*, until soon there would be nothing left to lose because there would be no one left who had ever lost it. The Hollow ate memories slowly, over years. Vael's wounds ate them in days. By the time they reached safety—if they reached safety—Sefa would look at her across a fire and see only a helpful stranger with a familiar voice.

*I will carry it for both of us,* Zephyrine promised the darkness. *You forgot. I won't. Someone has to remember us.*

Then she remembered what remembering cost, here, in these lands, and laughed once, silently, at the cruelty of the joke she'd made on herself.

* * *

Near dawn, Ilex woke them with a hand over Zephyrine's mouth and terror in his eyes—the first honest terror she had seen on him since he'd turned his knife on his handlers.

"Listen," he breathed.

She listened. At first: nothing, the dead-air hush of the wounded land. Then, beneath it, threading up through the floorboards of the half-real house, a sound she knew from her worst nights. Harmony. Many voices singing one voice. The Chorus, moving in column, perhaps a mile east, sweeping the scars in a search pattern she recognized because Ilex had taught it to her during interrogation, proudly, as doctrine.

"They've cut ahead of us," Ilex whispered. "They know where the map leads. They're not chasing anymore—they're *herding*. There's a corridor they want us to run down, and at the end of it—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. At the end of it was the citadel, and beneath the citadel was the hollow vastness that ate countries, and above it all sat a handler who had already marked Ilex's hesitation in the ledger of liabilities.

"How long before they close the corridor?" Zephyrine asked.

"A day. Less." He was pulling on his boots in the dark. "There's another way. South, through the worst of the scars—through the place where Vael's capital used to be. Nothing crosses it. Even the Chorus routes around it. But it's open, because nothing that lives wants to walk through the center of an unsung city."

"What's in the center?" Sefa asked.

Ilex was silent a moment. When he answered, his voice had gone thin and young.

"The palace. What's left of it. And the bells. They had ninety-nine bells in the great tower, and the tower fell, and sometimes—in wind, in storms—the bells still ring, though there are no ropes and no hands and half of them no longer exist." He met Zephyrine's eyes. "If we go through the capital, we will hear Vael ringing its own funeral. And the bells are the last remnant of the country's true name. Anyone who hears all ninety-nine at once remembers everything that was taken. Everything. Including the parts the Chorus paid in memory—including, for me—" his hand pressed his sternum again, that pulled-tooth hollow, "—including whether I loved anyone here."

Outside, faint and patient, the harmony swept closer along its chosen corridor, herding them south.

Zephyrine rose, shouldered the map case, and looked at Sefa—who smiled back at her with the courteous warmth of a stranger—and made her decision the way she made all of them now: quickly, wholly, with no permission asked.

"To the bells," she said. "Let's give this country its voice back."

They slipped out of the half-real house into the grey beginning of day and turned south, toward the fallen tower, while behind them the Chorus's song filled the corridor meant to catch them—and found it empty.
