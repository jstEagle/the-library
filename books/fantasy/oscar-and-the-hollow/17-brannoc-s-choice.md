## 17. Brannoc's Choice

The bridge at Cael's Ford had stood for four hundred years, and Brannoc Vey had crossed it perhaps two hundred times in his life, hauling salt and iron and once, memorably, a circus bear that no one in the company ever let him forget. It was a good bridge. Grey stone, five arches, wide enough for two carts to pass with an inch of grumbling between them. The river beneath ran fast and white with snowmelt, loud enough that you had to shout on the span.

He meant to die on it. He had known this since the night Zephyrine walked out of the camp, and he had told no one, because telling people you intended to die made them either sad or relieved, and he could not have borne either.

* * *

They had voted, three nights ago. That was the word Sefa used afterward—*voted*—as if the thing they'd done had been a civic matter, a tax dispute, something clean. Brannoc remembered it differently. He remembered the fire burning low because no one wanted to feed it, and fourteen faces turned toward him, waiting for the captain to make the hard thing simple.

"Hand over the map," he'd said, "or take your oath elsewhere."

He'd said it looking at the fire, not at her. That was the cowardice of it. Ten years he had led this company, and he had learned every trick of command, including the oldest one: that you can order a person to leave without ever once meeting their eyes.

Zephyrine hadn't argued. That was the worst part, the part that kept him awake through the watches after. She had simply looked at him—at last, at last he'd met her eyes—and there had been no anger in them at all. Only a kind of tired recognition, as if she'd known for years what he would do when the moment came, and had been quietly grieving it all along.

Then she'd gone into the dark, and Sefa had gone with her as far as the treeline, and come back alone with wet eyes and a set jaw, and no one had spoken for the rest of the night.

Brannoc sat by the dead fire until dawn doing arithmetic he couldn't stop doing. Fourteen soldiers. One map. One woman who had carried their pay, patched their wounds, pulled three of them out of a collapsing mine at Corren's Hollow without hesitation and without ever once mentioning it again. And what had she asked of them? To believe her. That was the whole price. To believe that the silence in the valley was a cage and not a sanctuary, that the polite strangers asking after a dead cartographer were wolves in choir robes, that the world was larger and crueler than the one they'd made their peace with.

And he had chosen the smaller world, because the smaller world fit inside a ledger, and Brannoc Vey trusted nothing he couldn't enter in a ledger.

* * *

Sefa found him at the bridge on the second morning of the pursuit.

She came down the eastern road at a dead run, horse lathered and blowing, and she didn't waste breath on greetings. "Chorus column. Forty riders, maybe more, moving up the valley road. They'll reach the ford by tomorrow midday."

"They're following her."

"They're following everyone." Sefa swung down, staggered, caught herself on the parapet. She looked terrible—hollow-eyed, road-burned, alive. "I got her and Ilex across the border country. They're making for the citadel from underneath, the back way, through the old quarry roads. But the Chorus split their force. A third of them are sweeping the valley, and the sweep will find her trail unless—" She stopped. Looked at the bridge. Looked at him.

Brannoc had been a soldier long enough to know what a question looked like when someone couldn't bring themselves to ask it.

"How many can you raise?" she said instead.

"Fourteen. Thirteen, if Tammo's fever doesn't break."

"And you'll hold a stone bridge against forty Chorus singers with thirteen tired spears."

"I've held worse positions with worse men," Brannoc said. "Though admittedly never against anything that sings."

Sefa laughed—a cracked, disbelieving sound—and then pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "She thinks you hate her."

"She thinks correctly."

"She thinks you chose the company over her, and she's made her peace with it, and she's carrying it like everything else she carries, which is to say badly, silently, and until it kills her. So if you're going to do whatever it is you're standing on this bridge deciding to do, Captain, you should know what it buys. Not absolution. There isn't any. It buys her hours, and hours are the only currency she has left."

Brannoc looked down the long grey length of the span, at the water shouting beneath it, at the far shore where the quarry roads climbed into hills that most maps had stopped bothering to draw.

"I don't need absolution," he said. "I need a position."

* * *

He spent the day preparing the way he'd spent thirty years preparing, because the work was the only prayer he'd ever trusted.

Tammo, fevered but upright, helped him pull the paving stones loose at the bridge's narrow throat—the third arch, where the parapet pinched to single file—and reset them loose over a hollow packed with dry brush and lamp oil. Dree and old Marta rigged the western approach with caltrops and a felled pine, half-cut so a rope would drop it across the road. The others sharpened, slept, ate, wrote letters they folded away without reading. No one asked why they were doing it. That was the strange mercy of mercenary companies: they did not require belief, only wages and a reason, and Brannoc gave them the truest reason he had.

"The Chorus took Sefa's sister's village off the map," he told them, gathered at the eastern end as the light went red. "Took it off the *map*. You understand me? Not burned. Not sacked. Unmade, so that no mother remembers birthing the children in it. These people came here asking polite questions in pretty harmony, and when we didn't sing back nicely, they took our friend hostage, and now they're hunting the only person in this valley who's ever fought for us instead of for coin." He let that sit. "I'm done being paid in coin."

Old Marta spat over the parapet, which among the company constituted a rousing endorsement.

That night he took out the letter he'd written and burned it unread in the cookfire. It had been addressed to Zephyrine, and it had begun *You were right*, and he knew that even if she read it, those three words would weigh less than nothing against the look he'd given—or hadn't given—across a dying fire. Some debts could not be paid forward. They could only be paid where they were owed, in the only coin the creditor could still use.

Hours. Sefa had said hours were the only currency she had left.

Very well. He would be rich.

* * *

The Chorus came at midday, and they came singing.

Brannoc heard them before he saw them—a thin, sweet sound threading up the valley road, many voices braided into one, and the horror of it was that it was beautiful. It sounded like the music played at harvest, like the songs mothers used to hum in the ferry town before the town forgot its own name. His men shifted on the barricade. Tammo's grip trembled on his spear. Even Marta, who feared nothing under the sky, went pale around the mouth.

"Don't listen to the tune," Brannoc said quietly. "Listen to the words. They're not singing *to* us. They're singing *about* us. Like we're already gone."

The column crested the rise at the eastern approach: forty riders in grey, unarmored, unhurried, their faces smooth and pleasant as travelers arriving at a festival. At their head rode a tall woman with silver-threaded braids, and when she saw the barricade, the fallen pine, the thirteen ragged spears, she did not order a charge. She simply raised one hand, and the singing stopped, and the silence that followed was worse than any war-cry Brannoc had ever faced, because it was *their* silence—it was ten years of empty shrines and forgotten faces compressed into a single held breath.

"Captain Vey," the woman called, pleasantly. "We know your name. Names are precious things. We should hate for yours to be misplaced."

"You know where it is," Brannoc called back. "It's on this bridge. Come collect it."

For a moment the pleasant faces flickered—something moving behind them like fish under ice—and then the woman gestured, almost sadly, and the column came on.

* * *

It went the way he had planned it, which was the only gift the day gave him.

The first rank hit the caltrops and the dropped pine and tangled screaming; the second rank pushed through onto the span and into the throat of the third arch, where the paving stones Brannoc and Tammo had loosened waited over oil and brush. He put a torch-arrow into it himself. The explosion wasn't large—that much oil never is—but on a stone bridge it didn't need to be. It needed only to be loud, and sudden, and to drop six riders and their horses through the broken span into water cold enough to stop a heart. The gap became a moat. The moat became a wall. And the Chorus, who erased countries with harmonies and unmade names with a gesture, discovered that they could not unsing four hundred years of granite, and that their beautiful voices carried no further than the edge of a hole.

So they climbed. And on the narrow span, in the smoke, with the river roaring below, it became the oldest kind of fighting there is—spear and shield and boot and elbow, shoulder to shoulder, no room for magic or music, only for weight and will.

Brannoc fought in the center, where the fighting was thickest, because that was where captains belonged and where he intended to be found. He killed the first singer with a spear-thrust through the pleasant face and felt nothing but the ordinary grim mechanics of it. He killed the fourth and the ninth. Marta fell beside him dragging two enemies off the broken edge with her, and did not scream, and he marked her passing with a grunt and a step forward into her place, because grief was a luxury the bridge could not afford.

They sang at him, in the end. That was how they broke the line—not with steel but with song, three of them locking their harmony on Tammo until the boy laid down his spear and smiled and walked off the western edge of the bridge into the white water, and Dree until she turned her blade on her own shield-arm, and then on Brannoc himself, a sweet triple chord that slid into his skull like a key into a lock and began, gently, lovingly, to turn.

*Forget*, the song said. *You are tired. You have been tired for so long. Set down the spear. Set down the name. Rest.*

And he *was* tired. Gods, he was tired—thirty years of road and ledger and loss, a decade of watching Zephyrine carry burdens he'd refused to help lift, a lifetime of choosing the smaller world because it fit inside the columns of a book. The song offered him the small world back. All he had to do was stop.

Brannoc Vey gripped the parapet with both hands and thought about a woman forgetting her mother's face on a road home, and how she had told no one, and carried it anyway.

"No," he said aloud, to the song, to the singers, to himself. "I'm keeping it. All of it. Every bad decision. Every debt. It's *mine*."

He tore free of the harmony the way you tear a wound open—completely, deliberately, knowing it will cost—and charged the singers with his sword held low, and the surprise on their lovely faces was the last new thing three of them ever saw.

* * *

Afterward—after the fires, after the survivors of the company dragged themselves to the western bank and counted themselves and found nine where thirteen had been—he was still standing. Barely. Leaning on the parapet at the bridge's center with a dozen wounds counting themselves across his body and the harmony still coiled somewhere behind his eyes, frayed but not broken, trying one last time to unpick him stitch by stitch.

The tall woman came across the rubble alone. Her column was broken, scattered, bleeding into the river, and still she walked toward him with the unhurried grace of someone to whom defeat was merely an unfamiliar word.

"You bought her hours," she said. "A handful. Was it worth it?"

Brannoc tried to answer and found the song had taken some of the words. He searched for others. He was a ledger-keeper to the last; he had always believed every account could be balanced, every column summed, every debt discharged in full. Standing there, leaking warmth into the grey stone, he understood at last that this one could only be spent, not repaid—and that spending it was enough.

"She'll say my name," he managed. "When this is done. Someone will. And it'll be *true*, when they say it. That's what you people never understood." He smiled, and tasted blood, and found it tasted like freedom. "You can't erase what's been given away."

He pushed off the parapet and met her on the span with the last of his strength, and the bridge at Cael's Ford held him up—one final crossing, one final toll—for exactly as long as he needed.

The river took him the way rivers take everything: without malice, without memory, onward.

* * *

Far to the south, past the quarry roads, past the wounds in the world where Vael's erasure still bled, Zephyrine Voss stopped mid-stride on a broken road and pressed a hand to her sternum, as though something heavy had been set down inside her—something she could not name, and would not need to.

"We have to keep moving," Ilex said.

"I know." She looked back the way they'd come, at the distant hills, at nothing she could see. "Someone just paid our toll," she said. "We shouldn't waste it."

And above them, at the citadel's black shoulder, the back way opened like a held breath finally released.
