## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with hiding in a guild of impossible things is that everyone assumes you're impossible too.

I'd spent ten years cultivating the opposite. I was Bram Nystrom, junior appraiser, third from the bottom of the Guild of Impossible Things, a woman whose most remarkable quality was her handwriting and whose second most remarkable quality was her ability to be forgotten by everyone above the rank of factor. I catalogued bottled thunderstorms and graded them for resale. I filed contracts for luck, leases on shadows, one deeply confusing deed to a Tuesday that had gone missing from a village in the Marchlands. I was meticulous, dull, and invisible, and I had worked very hard at all three.

So when the first ward shattered, I was the only person in the counting room who didn't look up.

That's not bravery. That's training. A swordspeaker learns to feel wards the way you feel a toothache—distantly, constantly, and with a low-grade dread of the day it stops being distant. The border wards had been humming along the edge of my awareness my whole life, a note so old I'd mistaken it for silence. When the first one broke, the silence changed key.

The second broke before anyone finished looking up.

"Earthquake," said Pellam from the next desk, not looking up from his inventory of bottled courage. Pellam could sleep through an apocalypse if it happened during business hours.

"It's not under us," I said. "It's *around* us."

He frowned at me. I went back to my ledger. Entry four hundred and eleven: one thunderstorm, bottled, temperamental, sold to the Widow Ashgrave of Corrow for the purposes of scaring off suitors. Condition: agitated. Notes: has begun responding to its own name when spoken aloud, which is against guild policy and also common sense.

My pen hovered over the notes column. My hand was steady. My hand is always steady; that's the curse of me. Inside, something was doing arithmetic it didn't want to finish. Two wards down. Then three. Each one that failed made the others lonelier, and lonely wards fail faster, like sentries who hear their fellows stop answering.

By the fourth, even Pellam looked up.

* * *

They came through the doors in a flood—the clients, I mean, though for one horrible moment I thought it might be something else. The Guild of Impossible Things keeps its public hall grand enough to make desperation feel dignified: vaulted ceiling, chandeliers that burned cold blue witchlight, a floor of black marble polished so thoroughly that people checked their reflections in it before asking for miracles. Tonight the reflections were all wrong. The witchlight guttered every time a ward fell, and each guttering made everyone's face flicker between themselves and someone older, or younger, or not quite themselves.

"Is it the Court?" someone shouted. A grain merchant, I think, the kind of man who bought weather futures and called himself a speculator.

"Is it war?"

"Is it *us?* Did we do something?"

Guild policy in a panic is simple: the factors circulate, the clerks hold the line, and nobody answers anything until the guildmaster says so. I held my station behind the appraisal counter with six other clerks, and we did what clerks do—we absorbed fear and issued receipts. A woman wanted to redeem a miracle she'd purchased years ago and never used: one true love, guaranteed, still sealed in wax. I told her redemptions required forty-eight hours' notice. She told me the sky was going out. I told her the sky was not guild property and therefore not our department.

I believed it right up until the moment I stepped into the transom light of the high window and saw it myself.

You have to understand what a starless sky means to someone like me. I was raised on oath-craft, and oath-craft teaches that everything holds because everything is witnessed. The stars were the oldest witnesses we had. Every covenant ever sworn under this sky was sworn *in front of them*, and they'd kept the minutes for longer than there had been language to read them by. They weren't lights. They were the audience.

And the audience was leaving, row by row, like patrons disgusted with the play.

It took an hour. I stood at the window and counted them out—not aloud, never aloud—and watched the dark spread like ink through water. Somewhere south, a ward fell with a sound I felt in my back teeth rather than heard. Somewhere north, another. The gaps between the surviving stars grew wider and stranger, and I caught myself thinking, with the detached professionalism that is my other curse, *that would chart beautifully.*

Then the last star in the western quarter went out, and the professional in me shut up, and what was left was just a woman at a window remembering that she had once promised, loudly and forever, to stand between two worlds—and had run away from the promise instead.

"Bram." Corvane's voice, at my shoulder. "Inside. Now."

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane is not a large man, but he has the gift of taking up more room than he occupies, and tonight he needed all of it. He stood on the central stair of the great hall while the crowd pressed and shouted below him, and he did something I had never seen him do.

He looked afraid.

Corvane afraid is worse than any omen. This is a man who once negotiated with a dragon over a disputed invoice and won concessions. This is a man who keeps a mirror that shows you your death and uses it to check his collar. If Corvane was afraid, then the world had presented him terms he hadn't drafted himself.

"Everyone inside," he said, and the hall quieted the way a struck bell quiets—not gradually, but in one clean decaying note. "The guildhouse stands. The guildhouse has always stood. Whatever is happening to the sky, it is happening *outside* these walls, and outside is where things go to become problems. Clerks: seal the ledgers. Factors: lock the vaults. Clients: you will be refunded your fears at standard rates come morning."

Someone laughed. It was the right thing to say and it almost worked.

"The wards—" began the grain merchant.

"The wards are being handled," Corvane said, and I watched him say it, watched the half-second delay before the words came out, and ten years of reading small print in impossible contracts finally paid off, because I recognized that delay. It's the pause of a man choosing which truth to bury.

He knows something, I thought. He's known since before tonight.

"Back rooms," he repeated. "Now."

The crowd moved. Crowds move when a man like Corvane insists; it's one of the impossible things he actually sells. And I turned to follow them, because following is what hidden women do—

"—Not you, Nystrom."

* * *

His study sits at the top of the guildhouse, behind a door of ironwood with no handle on the outside. I had been inside exactly twice in ten years, both times to deliver ledgers, both times acutely aware of the sword-shaped absence at my hip where a sword-shaped presence used to live. The oath-blade stayed where it always stayed, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of my trunk, sleeping. Sleeping, I told myself. Blades like mine don't sleep. They wait.

Corvane poured two glasses of plum brandy and gave one to me, which was the second alarming thing he'd done that night.

"You felt the wards go," he said. Not a question.

"I felt them go," I admitted. There was no point pretending. He'd known what I was since the day he hired me—he'd hired me *because* of what I was, though we'd never once spoken of it. That was our arrangement, written nowhere, honored absolutely: he gave me a hole to disappear into, and I gave him the courtesy of disappearing completely.

"And the stars?"

"The stars aren't mine to explain."

"No," he agreed. "They never were." He drank. Outside the window, the dark was total now, absolute, a sky like the inside of a closed eye. "Bram. I'm going to ask you to trust me one more time, and then I'm going to ask you to forget, and you're going to find the second request much harder than the first."

"That's usually how forgetting works."

He almost smiled. Then he crossed to his desk, unlocked the lowest drawer—the one I'd never once seen opened—and took out a single page of ledger paper.

I knew it instantly. You spend enough years around ledgers, you learn to know them the way shepherds know sheep. It was guild stock, guild watermark, guild ruling. But the entries on it were in Corvane's own hand, and the trade recorded there was dated nine years ago, and the item listed in the left-hand column made the room tilt sideways.

*One (1) living word, oath-bound, keystone grade.*

Keystone grade. There is only one keystone in the covenant between realms. There is only one living word holding it. I had spent a decade telling myself the covenant would hold without me, that my silence was cowardice and nothing more, that the worlds got along fine on their own momentum. And here was a page proving that my silence hadn't been cowardice at all. It had been load-bearing.

"What is this?" My voice came out flat. Flat is what happens when anything else would break.

"Housekeeping," Corvane said.

"Bram Nystrom doesn't do housekeeping. She does appraisals." I set down the untouched brandy. "Appraise it, then. That's what I'm for. What did you trade, and to whom, and what did you get?"

For a long moment he looked at me across the desk, and I saw him deciding—weighing the version of the truth he could give me against the version that might keep me safe, and I understood, with a dread that went through me like cold water through cloth, that whichever version he chose, he had already chosen it years ago. Before the wards fell. Possibly before he hired me. The guildhouse hadn't been shelter. It had been storage.

"Corvane," I said quietly. "What did you sell?"

And he answered me the only way he apparently intended to answer anyone tonight: he held the page over the candle flame and let it catch.

Paper burns fast when the words on it want to die. This one went up like it had been waiting ten years for permission, curling, blackening, the ink flaring green as the trade-name consumed itself—and I could have stopped it. Don't ask me how; some things I don't say even in my own head. But I stood there with my steady hands at my sides and let my guildmaster burn the only proof of what he'd done, because some animal part of me still trusted him, and a larger part wanted, desperately, to keep not knowing.

The ash drifted down onto the desk. One fleck landed on the back of my hand, still warm.

"Go to bed, Nystrom," Corvane said gently. "Tomorrow will be a difficult day for appraisals."

I went. Of course I went. Hiding is a discipline, and disciplines hold even when everything else fails.

But I lay in my narrow room in the dark of a starless night, listening to the guildhouse creak around its secrets, and I did the thing I had trained myself never to do: I reached, carefully, through ten years of silence, toward the wrapped blade at the bottom of my trunk.

It was awake. It had been awake for hours.

And far below, through three floors of stone and timber, I felt the guildhouse shudder as someone knocked at the front door—a knock with a wound in it, a knock that smelled of cold and chart-ink and a massacre I had spent a decade trying to forget.

Morning was coming. So was everything else.
