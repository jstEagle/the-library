## 3. A Cartographer of Nothing

I have been drawing a sky that no longer exists for eleven days, and I would like it noted that I have not once cried about it. Not properly. There was an incident on the fourth night involving a bottle of plum wine and the constellation of the Weeping Sisters, but I maintain that was allergies.

Here is my profession, for those who ask — and no one asks, which is a mercy: I am a cartographer of the heavens. Or I was. A star-chart is a promise, you understand. You draw the sky as it is, and the sky agrees to keep being that way, more or less, and sailors and pilgrims and one extremely lost duke pay you for the privilege of trusting it. It is the most honest trade in the world. The stars never lie to you. They simply burn, indifferent and punctual, until the night they don't.

Eleven days ago they didn't.

I had been asleep in my garret above the chandler's shop when it happened, and I woke not to sound but to its absence — the particular hush of a world holding its breath. I went to the window and looked up, because that is what I do, the way other people breathe, and the sky was bare. Not clouded. Bare. Clouds have texture, intention, a sort of gray gossip to them. This was nothing. A ceiling painted by someone who had run out of ideas halfway through the job.

I did what any professional does in a crisis. I sat down at my desk, lit three candles, uncorked my ink, and began to chart it.

That is how I found the wound.

You cannot draw nothing. Every apprentice learns this in their first year — you cannot render absence, only its edges. So when I set my pen to the place where the Pole Star should have been, my hand did something without consulting me. It drew a shape. An irregular ring, like a bruise, like the rim of a cup pressed too hard against skin, and inside the ring my pen refused to mark at all. The ink pooled and sat there, black and wet and patient, and I swear on whatever's left overhead that the paper felt cold under my palm.

I have drawn that wound forty times since. It is always the same shape. And every time I finish it, I get the distinct, unscientific impression that something on the other side has finished looking back.

So. That is why I am standing in the rain outside a building called the Guild of Impossible Things, wearing my second-best coat and rehearsing a speech to a door knocker shaped like a snail.

The guild occupies a narrow house wedged between a dye-works and a shop that sells, as far as I can tell, exclusively left-handed scissors. Its windows are fogged. Its sign is hand-painted and misspelled — *Imposible Things*, the S having clearly wandered off years ago — and I find this oddly reassuring. Anyone honest about their spelling failures might be honest about their inventory.

I knock. The snail rattles in its fitting like a bone in a bag.

A slot slides open at eye level, revealing one brown eye and a truly impressive quantity of eyebrow. "Closed," says the eyebrow.

"The sign says open."

"The sign is aspirational."

"I want to buy something impossible," I say, and then, because desperation makes poets of us all: "Specifically, a way through the dark."

The eyebrow performs a small, complicated dance. The slot snaps shut. I count to forty, getting steadily wetter, and then the door opens onto warmth and lamplight and the smell of — I tilt my head, cataloguing — old paper, tallow, dried lavender, and underneath it all, faint but unmistakable, blade-oil. Whetstone oil, the kind with a bitter almond note. My stomach drops through the floor before my brain can explain why.

"Come in, then," says a round little man with spectacles and ink to his elbows. "Mind the third stair, it bites."

* * *

The guildhouse interior is exactly what I expected and nothing like it: shelves to the ceiling, crammed with jars and boxes and things that shift when you look away from them. A bottled thunderstorm mutters on a high shelf like a sulking relative. A cat made, apparently, of smoke, watches me from atop a ledger with the smugness of a creature that knows it isn't real and has decided to be insufferable about it.

"A way through the dark," the round man repeats, steepling his fingers. He introduced himself as Quillmaster Fenwick, though he pronounced it in a way that suggested the title was self-awarded. "Might I ask what dark? Metaphorical or —"

"Actual. The sky went out eleven days ago. I'm a cartographer. I've been charting the absence." I unroll my latest sheet on his counter before I can lose my nerve. "There's a wound where the stars should be. It has edges. Edges mean structure, and structure means someone built it, and anything built can be entered, mapped, and — if one is very lucky — exited."

Fenwick puts on his spectacles, which he apparently keeps for moments requiring the appearance of competence, and studies the map. His face goes carefully blank, which in my experience means a man has seen something that frightens him and is deciding whether you're worth sharing the fear with.

"This is good work," he says finally. "Disturbingly good work. Where did you train?"

"Observatory at Callowmere. Before it burned."

"Mm. And you came to us because?"

"Because everyone official is saying the stars are merely obscured. Ash, they say. Weather. The Crown has issued a pamphlet." I tap the wound's cold center. "This is not weather. Weather doesn't have a *rim*. I need supplies, a route, and possibly a miracle. I'm told this establishment deals in all three."

"And your coin?"

"Enough. Plus this." I set down my father's astrolabe, brass gone green at the joints, older than both of us put together. Fenwick's eyes go soft and greedy in equal measure.

He's reaching for it when the curtain behind him moves, and she comes out.

Later, I will try to reconstruct that moment honestly, and honestly, it goes like this: a woman steps through a curtain carrying a ledger, and the lamplight catches her, and I think, *oh no*, in the specific tone of a person who has just recognized trouble and intends to walk directly into it. She's tall, dark-haired, with the kind of stillness that isn't calm — it's held, like a breath, like a blade in a scabbard. She moves like someone who learned to move around other people so as not to be noticed, and failed, because some people are simply doors that light insists on standing in.

She says, "Fenwick, the storm jar is trying to hail again—" and stops, because she's caught my expression, or her own reflection in it.

And then the smell reaches me properly. Blade-oil. Bitter almond. Whetstone and winter.

Ten years ago there was a valley called Harrowmere, and in it a garrison that had sworn itself to the border covenant, and one night in autumn everything in that valley died except me. I was fourteen. I survived because I hid under my dead mother's body in a root cellar and did not make a single sound for two days, and in all the years since, the one sense-memory I cannot drown with wine or work is that smell: blade-oil and blood and rain, the smell of the wardens who swept through killing everyone who'd ever spoken an oath. They were hunting oath-speakers that night. Everyone in Harrowmere had sworn something — harvest oaths, hearth oaths, small domestic promises to small domestic gods. It counted. It always counts.

I know that smell. I have spent ten years making sure I would know it anywhere.

"You," I say, and my voice comes out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "You were there."

She goes very still. Stillness like hers isn't innocence; innocence fidgets.

"Harrowmere," I say. "The massacre. You smell like their oil. You *move* like them."

"Fenwick," she says quietly, "our client seems confused."

"I'm not confused. I'm the girl from the root cellar." I laugh, and it comes out wrong, jagged. "Do you know how many nights I've tried to forget that night, and now it walks into a guildhouse carrying a *ledger*—"

"Vess." Fenwick's voice has changed; the merchant polish is gone. "Perhaps we might continue our discussion of rates another—"

"No." She sets down the ledger. Slowly. The way you set down a weapon you've decided not to use. "No, let her say it."

"Say what?" I demand. "Say that I watched the wardens empty a valley? Say that I've spent ten years drawing skies because the ground stopped being safe? What do you want me to say — that you're sorry? Were you even there? Do they let you *be* sorry?"

Something crosses her face then. Not guilt exactly — guilt is a performance, and this was private, involuntary, a hairline crack across something load-bearing. She looks at me the way I imagine I look at the wound in my charts: like something she drew without meaning to and now can't stop seeing.

"I haven't drawn my blade in ten years," she says.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Her eyes flick to my chart on the counter — to the wound — and I watch her go pale in a way that has nothing to do with me. Whatever she sees in that empty ring, it frightens her more than my accusations do. "Where did you draw this?"

"Every night since the stars went out. Why?"

"How long has it been growing?"

I stare at her. "It hasn't. It's the same size. That's rather the point of a wound, it—"

"It's the same size," she repeats softly, "because it's finished. Things that are still happening change. Things that are done just wait." She looks up at me, and here is the terrible part, the part I'll be turning over for days afterward: even now, even with ten years of dead between us, some traitorous part of me notices that her eyes are the exact gray of the hour before dawn, and thinks, *oh no* again, louder.

"The guild can't sell you a way through the dark," she says. "But I know where the dark starts. And I think—" she stops herself, jaw tightening, visibly at war with her own next sentence, "—I think you should come back tomorrow. Early. Before the others wake."

"Why?"

"Because the page Fenwick burned last night wasn't about the wards," she says, barely above a whisper, glancing at the round man, who has suddenly developed an intense fascination with his own spectacles. "It was about who sold them. And I think your wound and that sale are the same thing."

The smoke cat stands, stretches, and pours itself off the ledger like spilled ink.

"Tomorrow, then," I say, because I have apparently decided to trust the woman whose side killed my family, on the strength of one crack in her face and the fact that she looked at my map like it was a confession.

She gathers her ledger. At the curtain she pauses, back to me.

"For what it's worth," she says, not turning around, "I remember the root cellar. I was ordered to search it. I reported it empty."

Then she's gone, and I'm left standing in a guild of impossible things, holding a map of a wound, wondering which of us told the bigger lie — her, to the wardens, ten years ago; or me, right now, to myself, about how much I already want to see her again.
