## 4. What Corvane Sold

Ash, it turns out, remembers.

I learned this in my third year at the Guild, cataloguing a reliquary of a saint who had been burned twice—once by heretics and once, embarrassingly, by her own church—and whose ashes still whispered her last words if you held them to your ear on the anniversary of her death. The guildmaster had me write it up as *Echoing Remains, minor miracle, sentimental value only*. But I never forgot the lesson underneath the entry: fire destroys the thing, not the truth of it. Ash is just a memory with nowhere left to sit.

So when Corvane burned that page in front of us all—casually, almost bored, like a man disposing of a shopping list—I didn't panic. I watched.

He fed it to the hearth in the great room, the big one we use for thawing bottled blizzards and calming haunted furniture. The page caught, curled, and was gone in four seconds. Four seconds is nothing. But ash rises, and ash settles, and Corvane stood there for exactly as long as it took him to believe he'd gotten away with it before turning back to the frightened crowd of clients and telling them everything would be handled.

Everything would be handled. By whom? I wanted to ask. The stars were out—*out*, all of them, every light in the sky snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers—and the border wards had come down an hour before that, and our guildmaster's answer was to burn one piece of paper and promise us breakfast.

I waited until the third bell past midnight, when the clients had been filed into guest rooms or turned out into the dark, when the wardens' patrols were between rounds, when even the guildhouse itself seemed to hold its breath. Then I went back to the hearth.

The fire had been raked down to embers. Above it, on the mantel and the flue-stones and the soot-black lip of the chimney, the ash had settled in a fine grey bloom. To anyone else it would look like any night's residue. To anyone else, it would look like nothing.

To me it looked like handwriting.

Not words, exactly. Ash doesn't carry language; it carries *shape*. What I saw was the ghost of the page's structure—the faint darker smudge where a seal had pressed the paper, the clean oval where a signet ring had rested while the ink dried, and there, along what would have been the bottom margin, a scatter of ash that fell wrong. Fell too heavily. Ink-heavy. Something had been written along that edge in a hand that pressed hard enough to leave its weight behind even after burning.

I took a sweep of it with the little brass dust-vane we use for cleaning out bottled winds, and I carried the sample upstairs to my room, and I did something I hadn't done in ten years.

I spoke to it.

Not swordspeech—that I keep locked away where it can't hurt anyone, least of all me. Just the small domestic magic of my trade, the whisper-work every guild clerk learns: *tell me what you were.* The ash shivered in its dish. It couldn't speak, but it could arrange itself, and it did—grains sliding against grains until they formed the negative space of what had been written along the margin.

A name. An address. A place I knew only from maps of places that weren't supposed to exist anymore: **the Undercroft of the Old Exchange**, three levels below the guildhouse, sealed since before I came here, accessible through the wine cellar nobody used because Corvane kept buying wine nobody drank.

Of course. Of course he'd hidden it under the wine. Corvane had many talents, and one of them had always been hiding enormous things inside dull ones.

* * *

The cellar stairs creaked in the exact same places they'd creaked for a decade, which meant either the building was loyal to tradition or no one had come down here recently enough to wear the sound away. I counted the steps out of habit. Thirty-one. At the bottom, the wine racks stretched off into darkness, bottles sleeping in their dusty rows, each one labeled in Corvane's neat hand: *Vermillion '03. Vermillion '04. Vermillion, undrinkable, do not open.*

I found the loose stone behind the fourth rack because I'd always known it was there. Everyone had always known it was there. That was the point. A door everyone knows about stays shut; a secret door gets opened out of spite.

Beyond it, a passage sloped down into cold, and the cold had a texture. Not dampness—silence. The kind of silence the border wards used to hold back, the silence that eats names. My candle flame leaned toward it like a dog toward an open gate, and I felt the hair rise on my arms, and I thought, with the calm of a woman who has spent ten years practicing calm: *the Hollow Court has been under my feet this whole time.*

The Undercroft opened up around me like a held breath finally let out. It had been a trading floor once, back when the Exchange traded in things more interesting than grain futures—back when the Guild of Impossible Things was young and so was the covenant, and the two realms shook hands across tables like these. The tables were still here. So were the chairs. And on the far wall, where a mural of the twin realms should have been, there was a wound.

That's the only word for it. The plaster had rotted into a ragged hole maybe six feet across, and beyond the hole there was no cellar wall, no earth, no anything—just a darkness that my candlelight fell into and didn't come back from. It didn't reflect. It didn't echo. When I held the candle closer, the flame bent toward the hole like a supplicant, and I stepped back, because I am many things but I am not stupid.

And nailed to the frame of the wound—actually nailed, iron spikes driven into old wood—hung a contract.

It was written on vellum the color of old teeth, in ink that moved. Not shimmered. *Moved*, crawling slowly across the surface like insects too dignified to hurry. The Hollow Court's script. I'd seen it once before, ten years ago, on the battlefield at Cael Morath, written in the sky above the dead in letters made of absence. I had hoped never to see it again. Hope, as my mother used to say, is just disappointment with better posture.

I read it. I read it twice, because the first time I assumed I'd misunderstood, and the second time I understood perfectly and wished I hadn't.

**Terms of Sale.** The covenant between realms requires a keystone: one living word, freely bound, spoken by an oath-speaker of sufficient strength. Said word to be surrendered to the Hollow Court in perpetuity. In exchange: the continued existence, protection, and charter of the Guild of Impossible Things, in perpetuity, throughout the duration of the current age.

There was a signature line. There were two signature lines.

One bore Corvane's mark—the little fox-and-key sigil he stamped on every ledger I'd ever catalogued.

The other line was blank. Waiting. And beside it, in the moving ink, in letters that crawled like they already knew they'd won:

*A word may be given willingly or taken unwillingly. The Court is patient. The Court prefers willing.*

Below that, smaller, almost an afterthought, the way you might note a delivery time:

*The oath-speaker known as Bram Nystrom will be approached within the season.*

Me. My name, sitting in a contract older than my employment, in a cellar beneath my bed, in a deal struck—when? The vellum didn't say. But the wax seals along the bottom had aged unevenly, some fresh and some grey with years, and the oldest ones predated my arrival at the guild by months. Maybe by weeks.

Corvane had been selling me since before I walked through his door.

* * *

"You read faster than I expected."

I didn't jump. Ten years of practice again—it's remarkable what a person can train themselves not to do. I turned, and there he was in the passage behind me, holding a lamp, wearing his nightshirt under his coat like a man who'd dressed in a hurry and wanted you to think he hadn't. His face was carefully arranged into something gentle. Corvane's gentleness always looked arranged. That was the horror of it, I realized now: I'd mistaken craft for kindness for a decade.

"How long," I said.

"Does it matter?"

"Indulge me."

He sighed and set the lamp on one of the old trading tables, and for a moment he looked genuinely tired—an old man in a cellar at midnight with the sky falling in on his life's work. "The night you arrived. You came with a letter of introduction from a woman who died getting it to you. You came with a blade you never drew and a voice you never used, and I watched you catalogue a haunted harp without flinching, and I thought: there she is. The Court's price, delivered to my doorstep by the war she survived." He smiled thinly. "You were the easiest acquisition I ever made. You hid so well."

"I hid well," I said, "because you told me it was safe here."

"It was safe here. That was the entire arrangement." He gestured at the contract, at the wound in the wall, at the dark beyond both. "Ten years, Bram. Ten years the Court honored the terms, because the terms required your word intact. Unspent. Silent. Every day you didn't speak your swordspeech was a day the guild ate, and slept, and stayed off every list that matters. Your silence wasn't cowardice. It was the payment. I simply... neglected to tell you what it was buying."

"And now?"

"Now the wards are down and the stars are out and the Court has grown impatient. They want delivery. And I—" he spread his hands, "—am prepared to offer you a choice, which is more than they would give you."

"The choice being."

"Stay silent. Stay here. When they come to collect, I tell them the keystone is damaged, diminished, no longer worth taking—and they take the guild instead, but they'll take *you* too, eventually, as scrap. Or—" and here his voice softened into something that was almost tender, and was therefore unbearable, "—you go to them willingly. Freely given, the contract says. The Court prefers willing. You walk into that dark on your own feet, and the guild survives, and the age turns over quietly, and no one else has to burn."

"No one else," I repeated. "Except me."

"Someone always pays for a miracle, Bram. That's the first lesson of our entire profession. I'm merely asking you to pay the one you were always going to pay."

I looked at him for a long moment. At the fox-and-key on his ring. At the lamp throwing his shadow huge and wavering against the wound in the world. And I felt the thing I'd been choking down for ten years finally turn over and show its teeth—not grief, not even betrayal exactly, though God knows there was enough of that to fill a ledger. It was simpler than that. It was arithmetic.

He had taken my silence and sold it as if it were his to sell.

"Here's what I know about contracts," I said, and my voice came out level, which surprised me, and pleased me more than it should have. "I've catalogued four hundred and eleven of them. Do you want to know what they all have in common? Every single one assumes the other party can't read. Or won't. Or is too frightened to check the fine print." I stepped toward the contract, toward the crawling ink, and pointed at the clause he clearly hoped I'd skimmed. "*Freely given.* Those are their words, Corvane. Not yours. The Court wants willingness because willingness binds and coercion doesn't—which means everything you've done for ten years, every kindness, every safe harbor, every quiet corner you gave me to hide in, none of it counts. You can't deliver me. You can only introduce us."

His face did something complicated. "Bram—"

"And there's a second thing." I pulled my sleeve back, and there it was, the old scar circling my wrist where my oath-band used to sit before I buried it under leather and habit. "My word isn't mine to give anymore either. It was bound once, at Cael Morath, and binding broke something in it. Whatever the Court takes from me—if they take it—they'll get a cracked keystone. They'll get *me*, and I am far more trouble than I'm worth."

"You don't know that."

"I catalogue impossible things for a living. Doubt is my native language." I turned for the passage, then stopped, because there was one more thing and it deserved saying. "You burned that page tonight in front of forty people because you wanted us all to believe there was no record. But ash remembers, Corvane. Everything remembers. That's the whole problem with miracles—you can't destroy the evidence of them without leaving evidence of the destruction."

I walked past him. He didn't stop me. I think, in his way, he had already lost the argument and knew it, and was already composing the letter to the Court explaining why his merchandise had developed opinions.

At the top of the cellar stairs I paused, one hand on the rail, and said the thing I'd carried up thirty-one steps alongside my heartbeat:

"There's a cartographer downstairs who charted a wound in the sky. She thinks the guild can sell her a way through the dark." I smiled, and it felt like the first honest expression I'd worn in ten years. "She's right. She just doesn't know yet that the way through is me."

Behind me, faintly, from the Undercroft, I heard the ink begin to move faster.
