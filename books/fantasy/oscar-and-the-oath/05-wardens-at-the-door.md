## 5. Wardens at the Door

I have been lied to by professionals. Kings' surveyors, guild factors, a man in Saltmere who sold me a compass that pointed, with great conviction, toward nothing. I know the anatomy of a lie the way I know the anatomy of a coastline: where it bends, where it erodes, where someone has clearly built something on top of it and hoped no one would notice.

So when the woman with the ledger-stained fingers told me she had never held a sword, I did not believe her.

I also did not care.

That was the strange part. I sat across from her in the Guild of Impossible Things' back room—surrounded by bottled weather and a taxidermied griffin that had, according to its placard, died of embarrassment—and I listened to her deny the thing she so obviously was, and instead of calling the lie what it was, I found myself wanting to protect it. Wanting to protect *her*, which was worse, because I had known Bram Nystrom for less than two days and I already knew exactly one true fact about her: that ten years ago, on the night of the Vaelmarsh massacre, someone with her blade-oil scent had walked out of the burning alive while three hundred people did not.

The oil smelled of cedar and iron and something faintly sweet, like honey left too long near a forge. It is not a scent you forget. I had spent ten years trying to.

"You're staring," Bram said.

"I'm charting."

"Of me?"

"Everything gets charted eventually." I tapped my stylus against the table. "You have an interesting coastline."

"I don't have a coastline."

"Not literally. Obviously." I drew a small mark on my pad, mostly to give my hands something honest to do. "Metaphorically. Everyone does. Most people are very boring coastlines. Gentle slopes. A harbor or two. You're—" I stopped myself before I said something like *you're a cliff face over deep water with wreckage at the bottom*, because we had known each other forty-eight hours and even I have limits. "You're irregular."

Bram looked at me for a long moment. In the lamplight her eyes were the color of the sky used to be, back when the sky still bothered having a color. "You're a strange woman, Vess."

"I'm a cartographer of nothing. Strangeness comes with the trade."

And that was when the knock came.

Not a knock, really. A knock asks permission. This was more of an announcement—the kind of sound a fist makes when the fist belongs to someone who has never once in his life waited to be let in. Three impacts, evenly spaced, hard enough to rattle the bottled thunderstorm on the shelf behind me. Inside its glass, the little storm woke up and pressed itself anxiously against the walls like a moth.

Bram went still in a way I had learned to recognize. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The stillness of a deer that knows the shape of the hound's voice.

"That's not a client," she said.

"No," I agreed. "Clients knock like they're apologizing."

* * *

The guildhouse of the Guild of Impossible Things is built like a drunk man's idea of a fortress—crooked wings, doors that open onto each other, stairs that go up on both ends. Corvane designed it that way, or claims he did, though I suspect the architect simply took his money and his sanity in equal measure. The point is that there are many ways into the building and only one way into the back rooms, which meant that whoever was knocking had either been invited or had made arrangements.

Nobody makes arrangements with Corvane without paying. And nobody pays Corvane to knock politely.

We heard him greet them in the front hall, his voice doing the thing it does, all warmth and commerce, the voice of a man who could sell a funeral to a ghost. We couldn't make out the words. Then we heard the words stop mattering, because the front doors opened anyway, uninvited, and boots came in.

Six sets. Maybe seven. The tread of people who walk in step without meaning to, the way soldiers do, the way congregations do.

Bram stood up so fast her chair went over backward. She didn't catch it. She looked at the door, then at the window—it was bricked up, because of course it was, this guild and its relationship with natural light—and then at me, and her face did something I will be charting in my memory for years: it asked me a question without asking it.

Later, I would understand what the question was. Later, I would understand why answering it cost me something I hadn't known I owned. But in that moment all I knew was this: the woman who had lied to me about the sword was afraid, and I discovered—with genuine scientific interest—that I would apparently lie for her.

"Back room," I said. "Through the map archive. There's a door behind the charts of the Sunken Coast. Nobody goes in there. The charts are wrong and it depresses everyone."

"What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to do what cartographers do best." I picked up my stylus. "Improvise terrain."

She hesitated one heartbeat too long. I put my hand flat on the table between us—not touching her, just claiming the space—and said, "Go. Please."

She went. I heard the archive door whisper shut behind her, and then I turned to face whatever the world had decided to send through the front of the building, and I arranged my face into the expression I use for customs officials: pleasant, vacant, faintly inconvenienced.

* * *

There were six of them, and they were not what I expected, which annoyed me, because I pride myself on expectations.

I had pictured Oathwardens as the stories paint them: gilded, terrible, armored in scripture. What came through Corvane's front door were tired people in grey traveling cloaks, road-dusted, one of them favoring a knee. Their armor was plain steel under the cloaks, well-worn, mended in places. They looked less like holy terror and more like the world's most dangerous road crew.

Except their leader.

He was tall in the way that trees are tall—without effort, without apology—and he carried himself with the particular stillness of a man who has spent a decade being pointed at things. His cloak was the same grey as the others but cleaner, and at his hip hung a sword that I recognized on sight, which surprised me, because I had never seen it before.

I recognized it the way you recognize a description. Long grip. Guard shaped like a pair of outstretched wings. Blade-oil smell, cedar and iron and honey-at-the-forge, coming off it faintly even from across the room.

The same scent. The same school. The same everything that clung to Bram.

He crossed the hall to me—I was standing in the doorway of the back corridor, deliberately, a small woman in a large frame—and looked down at me with eyes that had the specific exhausted patience of someone who has asked a lot of questions in his life and received a lot of lies.

"You're the cartographer," he said.

"I'm a cartographer. Whether I'm *the* cartographer depends on your paperwork."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile's fossil. "Serrek," he said, and didn't offer a hand. Wardens, I'd later learn, don't offer hands to strangers; hands are for oaths, and oaths are not given lightly. "Oathwarden, third covenant. We're looking for someone."

"Everyone's looking for someone. It's the season. The stars went out; people get sentimental."

"The stars went out because someone broke the covenant." He said it quietly, without drama, which made it worse. "Ten days ago. And every road, every ledger, every lying little guildhouse between here and the First Hearth is going to be searched until we find out how."

Behind me, down the corridor, I heard the archive door settle. A sound like a breath being held.

"We're searching guildhouses now?" I said, keeping my voice light, keeping his eyes on mine. "What does the Guild of Impossible Things have to do with oaths? We deal in bottled weather and fraudulent relics. The most sacred thing in this building is the tax on miracle imports."

"Bram Nystrom," Serrek said.

And there it was. The name, spoken plainly, in a voice that held it the way you hold a wound—carefully, because it's yours, and angrily, because someone else made it.

I am not a brave woman. Let the record show. I chart dead skies and avoid live dangers, and my survival strategy since childhood has consisted almost entirely of being overlooked. Every instinct I owned told me to shrug, to spread my hands, to say *never heard of her* and let the tide carry this grey-cloaked brotherhood on past.

Instead I heard myself say: "Who?"

Serrek's eyes narrowed. "Bram Nystrom. Swordspeaker. Last of the oath-bound. She deserted the covenant the night it fell, and three hundred people at Vaelmarsh paid for her absence, and I have spent ten years—" He stopped. Recalibrated. The fossil-smile again, aimed at himself this time. "She was my shield-sister. I trained beside her. I ate beside her. And when the wards broke, she was gone, and the sky went dark, and I have chased her name across half the world ever since."

"That's very moving," I said. "But I chart stars, not sisters. There's no one here by that name."

"There's someone here by *some* name." He stepped closer. Up close he smelled of road dust and cold iron and, underneath it, something that might have been grief worn down to a nub. "A woman checked into this guild two nights ago. Paid in old coin. Signed the ledger as 'Wren.' Wrens don't pay in coin minted before the war."

"Guilds take old coin. It's called commerce."

"The ledger, then. Show me your ledger."

"Corvane handles the ledger."

"Then fetch Corvane."

"He's at dinner."

"It's midmorning."

"He eats when anxious. He's been anxious since the stars went out. It's a whole thing; there's a second lunch."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—I thought he was going to push past me. I thought about what was behind me: one crooked corridor, one badly hidden door, one woman holding her breath among charts of a sky that no longer existed. I thought about how easy it would be for six wardens to find her, and how I had known her for two days, and how none of the arithmetic of my life justified what I did next.

I stepped sideways. Into his path. A small motion, barely anything, the kind of thing you'd miss if you blinked—but deliberate, unmistakable, a body placed between a question and its answer.

Serrek looked down at me. At the space I had claimed. Something flickered across his face—not suspicion. Surprise. The surprise of a man watching a stranger do a thing that only family does.

"Why," he said slowly, "are you protecting her?"

"I'm not protecting anyone. I'm protecting the guild's privacy policy. It's very strict. There's a form."

"Vess." He read the name off my collar-tag, where cartographers stitch it, because we are a vain and legible profession. "Vess, the stars are dark. The border wards are ash. Whatever she told you, whatever story she's wearing—she is the reason the sky is dying. If you're hiding her, you're hiding the end of the world."

"I'm hiding," I said, "a woman who buys tea and flinches at thunderstorms. That's all I know about her. That's all she's let me know. And maybe that makes me a fool, but I've met enough of the world's certainties to last a lifetime, and I find I prefer her flinching to your certainty."

The hall was silent. One of the other wardens shifted. Somewhere above us, the bottled thunderstorm rattled in sympathy, or mockery, or both.

Serrek studied me for a long time. Then he did something I didn't expect: he stepped back.

"We're not leaving," he said. "We'll search the guildhouse. Every room. It may take a day. It may take three." His eyes went past me, down the corridor, to the unremarkable door at its end, and I watched him decide—not to look, but to *not* look, and I understood that he had noticed everything and was choosing, for reasons of his own, to spend that knowledge slowly. "Tell your friend with the old coin that running makes her look guilty. And tell her—" his voice caught, just once, a hairline crack in good masonry— "tell her Serrek kept the watch. All ten years. She should know that, before the end."

They withdrew to the front hall to wait. I walked back through the map archive on legs that felt borrowed, opened the door behind the Sunken Coast charts, and found Bram standing in the dark with her hand on the hilt of a sword I had never seen her draw—a sword whose guard was shaped like a pair of outstretched wings, whose oil smelled of cedar and iron and honey-at-the-forge.

She had heard everything. I could see it in her face.

"He wants you to know," I said, "that he kept the watch."

Bram closed her eyes. When she opened them again, they were wet, and furious, and afraid.

"Then it's worse than I thought," she said. "Because Serrek doesn't wait at doors, Vess. He waits at graves. And if he's giving us a day's grace—" she slid the sword back into its sheath with a click that sounded, in the dark, like a decision being made— "it's because he thinks we're already dead."
