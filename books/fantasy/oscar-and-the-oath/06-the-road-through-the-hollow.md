## 6. The Road Through the Hollow

I had spent ten years learning how to be quiet. It turned out I'd never once practiced being quiet with company.

Vess talked the way rivers talk—continuously, and as if the world would end if she stopped. She narrated our escape from the guildhouse in exhaustive detail while we climbed over the collapsed garden wall: which shutters Corvane's people had barred wrong, which alley stank of tallow versus which stank of fear, exactly how many wardens' boots she'd heard on cobbles (nine; I'd heard six, but arguing felt like a luxury). By the time we reached the orchard road east of the city, dawn was a bruise behind the hills and my ears were full of her.

"You breathe too loudly," she said.

"I'm sorry. Next time I'll hold my breath for the whole flight."

"See, that's the spirit."

She was walking with a rolled map under one arm like it was an infant, and every half-mile she stopped, unrolled a corner of it, frowned at the blankness where stars should have been inked, and rolled it up again. The map showed a sky that no longer existed, and she consulted it anyway, the way you might consult a dead parent about dinner.

"The First Hearth is nine days east," she said, "if the roads are honest."

"And if they're not?"

"Then we go through the Hollow." She said it lightly, but her thumb pressed white against the map's edge. "The wards fell everywhere at once, Bram. Not just your border. The Court didn't break in—it *poured*. Everything between here and the Hearth is either eaten or eating itself."

Nine days through country gone silent. I thought about the ledger drawer where my blade slept under invoices for bottled thunderstorms and secondhand luck, strapped now to my back beneath a fishmonger's cloak because a swordspeaker travels best disguised as a woman with a bad spine. I thought about Corvane's face when he told me what he'd sold. I thought about how he'd said *stay silent and be spared* like it was a kindness, and how the worst part was that some starving animal part of me had wanted to take it.

"Lead on," I said. "You're the one with the map of nothing."

"It's a very good nothing," Vess said. "I worked hard on it."

* * *

The first village was called—well, that became the problem.

It sat in a fold of green hills like any other village, smoke rising straight from its chimneys, washing hung out, a dog asleep in a doorway. We came down the hill expecting bread and directions. What we got instead was a woman at the well who looked at us with pleasant, empty courtesy, like we were weather.

"Can you tell me where we are?" Vess asked.

"Here," the woman said, smiling.

"The village. What's it called?"

A pause. Her smile stayed exactly where it was, fixed and bright as a painted one. "We live here," she said. "Everyone lives here."

Inside the tavern it was worse, because inside there were more of them. Farmers ate their stew and laughed at nothing. A man played a fiddle with three strings and seemed not to notice the missing fourth, playing the same eight bars over and over, contentedly, forever. When Vess asked the innkeeper his name, he looked down at his hands as though checking them for writing.

"I'm sure I had one," he said. "It'll come back to me. They usually do." He said it the way you say *the rain will stop*—not hope, just habit.

"They don't," I said quietly.

The Hollow Court didn't burn what it took. It subtracted. Names first—the smallest, most load-bearing thing a person owns—and then everything the name held up: whose daughter you were, whose grief you carried, why you planted barley instead of wheat, why you hummed that tune. What remained looked like life. It moved and ate and smiled. But it was a house with the furniture taken out, and everyone still sitting politely in the empty rooms.

We got out fast. On the road beyond the village Vess was silent for a full mile, which I later understood was her equivalent of screaming.

"My maps," she finally said. "Every star I charted—I named each one. That was the work. You can't navigate by something unnamed. A name is a handle. It's how you *hold* a thing." She kicked a stone off the road with unnecessary violence. "That's what they're doing. They're taking the handles off the world."

"That's very poetic for someone who nearly bit me this morning over ration portions."

"I did not bite you. I gestured aggressively near your bread."

I almost smiled. Then I remembered the innkeeper's hands, turning over and over looking for writing that wasn't there anymore, and the smile died somewhere on the road behind us.

* * *

By the fourth day the silence crept into the land itself. Birds still sang, technically—but the songs had fewer notes in them, like coins worn smooth. Rivers ran without sound. Our own voices came out slightly flattened, as if the air were charging a toll.

We stopped speaking except when necessary. Not because of the Hollow—because of each other, maybe. There's a particular intimacy to danger shared with someone whose sleeping face you've memorized against your will. Vess kept first watch, then I kept second, and on the third night I woke to find her watching me instead of the road, and neither of us pretended otherwise, and neither of us said anything, and the unsaid thing sat down between us like a third traveler and refused to pay its share.

On the sixth day we found the child.

* * *

She stood alone in the middle of a harvested field, maybe seven years old, hair full of chaff, staring at the gray smear where the sun should be. The villages around here hadn't even bothered to look alive—the houses stood open-mouthed, doors swinging—and yet this child was whole. Unhollowed. She saw us and did not run, which frightened me worse than running would have.

"Are you lost?" Vess called.

The girl shook her head. Then, in a voice scraped thin: "I'm waiting for my mother to come remember me."

Oh, I thought. Oh, no.

Because that's how it works, at the edges. The Court takes the adults first—they've got more name to lose, more weight of memory, richer pickings—and the children last, and a child left holding a name nobody else remembers starts to feel it slipping through her fingers like water. Somewhere out there was a woman who had loved this girl yesterday and today walked past her own front door without knowing why it made her sad.

"We can take you with us," Vess said gently. "There are people east who will help. People who keep records. Names. We'll write yours down and never lose it."

The girl considered this with the terrible gravity of children deciding whether to trust you. And then the light changed—not the light, the *absence* of light—and the field went cold, and I felt it before I saw it: that pressure on the eardrums, that sense of a vast mouth opening somewhere underneath the world.

They came out of the treeline without disturbing so much as a leaf. Three shapes, tall and wrong, faces like pages someone had erased and erased and erased until the paper wore through. Hollow wardens. The Court's fingers. Where they stepped, the grass forgot how to be grass.

And the girl screamed—a real scream, full of name and terror and everything the Court wanted—and started to run, and one of the shapes simply wasn't where it had been anymore, it was between her and us, arms unfolding like a book of bad news—

Everything in me rose at once.

I felt it come up my throat like fire finding a chimney: the swordspeech, the old word, the first word, the one that had been carved into me at nineteen with iron and incense and the weight of a covenant I hadn't chosen. Ten years I'd swallowed it. One word—one syllable of it, even a breath of it—and those shapes would come apart like smoke in wind. The oath-blade was already half-drawn, my hand moving without permission, the word gathering behind my teeth, huge and hot and *hungry*, after ten years of fasting—

*Bram.* My name, spoken aloud. The keystone. The living word Corvane had sold. If I spoke it here, in the Hollow's open throat, the Court wouldn't need to hunt me across two countries. Every erased thing for fifty miles would turn toward me at once like flowers toward a sun made of hunger. And Vess—Vess standing right beside me, Vess whose name I knew, whose sleeping face I'd memorized, who would be the first thing the dark reached for.

I let the word die in my throat. It went down like swallowing a coal.

Instead I did the ordinary thing. The small, human, insufficient thing. I sprinted.

Later, Vess would tell me I moved like something out of her old massacre-stories, and I'd tell her she exaggerated, and we'd both know I was lying. But all I knew in the moment was mud and breath and the girl's chaff-filled hair under my hand as I scooped her up mid-stride, and the hollow thing reaching, and Vess—clever, impossible Vess—flinging our entire store of lamp oil in an arc and setting it alight with the flint she always carried, because fire doesn't need a name to burn.

Fire doesn't need a name. Remember that. It may save your life.

The shapes recoiled from the flame the way a debt recoils from a paid ledger. We ran. All three of us, the girl on my hip growing heavier with every stride, past the open-mouthed houses, up the far slope, until the pressure lifted and birdsong returned—thin, worn, but *song*—and we collapsed in the ruins of a shrine where somebody long ago had carved names into the stone, dozens of them, generations of them, and I put the girl's hand flat against the carving.

"Hold on to these," I told her. "Names stick to other names. Yours won't slip if it's touching theirs."

She looked up at me with eyes too old for her face. "What's your name?" she asked.

"Bram," I said, and it cost me less than I expected. "Bram Nystrom. Write it somewhere, if you ever find chalk."

"Why do you look sad when you say it?"

"Because I'm carrying two," I said, before I could stop myself.

Vess glanced at me sharply. I busied myself with the girl's chaffy hair.

* * *

We reached the edge of the First Hearth's valley on the ninth day, at dusk, and stopped as if we'd hit glass.

Below us, the valley glowed. Not with starlight—there was no starlight anywhere anymore—but with hearthfire, real and orange and stubborn, a thousand fires burning in a ring around a single ancient flame at the valley's heart. The land around it was *named*. I could feel it like warmth on skin: the river knew it was a river, the stones knew they were stones, the air had texture and opinion again. An island of the world-as-it-was, holding out in a sea of forgetting.

The girl—whose name, recovered piece by piece over three days, was Tally—made a sound of wonder.

Vess unrolled her map across a boulder and stared at the blank sky portion, and I watched her expression change from exhaustion to something colder.

"Bram," she said. "Come look at this."

I looked. In the center of the wound where the stars should be, faint as breath on glass, new marks had appeared since morning. Marks she hadn't drawn. A slow spiral, tightening inward, its point aimed—with a cartographer's terrible precision—at the glowing valley below us.

Something was coming down out of the dark toward the First Hearth. Something patient. And it was descending along a path that, unless my eye had forgotten everything, traced the exact shape of a signature I'd spent ten years trying not to recognize:

My own.
