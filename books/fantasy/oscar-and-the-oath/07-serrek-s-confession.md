## 7. Serrek's Confession

He found us at the edge of the Weeping Fen, where the road gave up pretending to be a road and became a suggestion. I should have smelled him coming—oath-steel and ash, the scent of every morning of my first life—but the Hollow had been eating my senses for three days, and by then I was mostly nose-blind to anything that wasn't rot.

Vess felt him first. She went still over her maps, the way a deer goes still, and said, without looking up, "Someone's behind us."

"One someone or several?"

"The kind that walks like he owns the ground."

"That's Serrek," I said. "He never owned ground in his life. He just apologized to it so thoroughly it let him pass."

I turned around. There he was, exactly as my memory had filed him and nothing like it at all. Memory had kept him twenty-five forever: broad, bright-eyed, laughing with his helmet under his arm. The man walking toward us through the grey grass was forty-something and grey-eyed and moved like a man carrying something heavy in his chest that he'd stopped hoping anyone would offer to help with.

His sword was drawn. Of course it was. Protocol demanded it. Serrek had once spent an entire evening explaining protocol to me over bad wine, and I had once spent that entire evening planning his death in elaborate detail because the wine was *that* bad.

"Bram," he said.

"Serrek." I didn't reach for my blade. Ten years of not reaching for my blade; my hand had forgotten how. "You're a long way from the border you failed to hold."

Flinch. Good. I still had that much left in me—the cruelty, at least. The Guild of Impossible Things had never managed to trade that away.

"You deserted," he said. "The night the wards fell, everyone said it. The last swordspeaker ran before the sky went dark. That's why the covenant broke. That's what we were told."

"And you believed them."

"I *wanted* to believe them." His voice cracked on it, which was new. Old Serrek's voice had been a well-built wall; this one had a door hanging off its hinges somewhere. "It was easier than the other thing."

"Which other thing? There are so many lately."

Vess made a small sound beside me—not quite a laugh, not quite a warning—and I loved her a little for it, which was inconvenient timing, as usual.

Serrek stopped ten paces out. Close enough for a duel. Far enough for a conversation. He'd chosen the distance deliberately; I recognized the arithmetic. Wardens did their math in blood.

"Put up your sword, Bram," he said. "I'm not here to fight you."

"You're pointing a sword at me."

"I'm *holding* a sword near you. There's a difference. It's one of the last differences I have left."

* * *

We sat on a fallen boundary stone while the Hollow silence pressed in around us like deep water. Vess stayed standing, arms crossed, positioned between me and the treeline with the instinct of someone who'd mapped enough ambush sites to recognize one from the inside. Serrek drove his sword into the earth—a warden's formal gesture, meaning parley—and sat across from me like a man visiting his own grave.

"They sent me to kill you," he said. "Officially. Unofficially, they sent me because they think I'm the only one who can. And unofficially-unofficially—" he laughed, and it was a terrible sound, "—they sent me because they're hoping I die too. Fewer witnesses."

"Serrek. What happened?"

"What always happens. What was always happening." He looked at his hands. "You remember your oath-day?"

"I remember everything about my oath-day. That was rather the point of oaths."

"Do you remember being asked?" He looked up. "Actually asked. Given a choice. A moment where someone said, 'Bram Nystrom, do you consent?'"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Went back through the memory the way you go back through a house you've lived in all your life, checking rooms you've never entered because you assumed they were walls.

There hadn't been a question. There had been a circle of wardens, and my mother's hand on my shoulder pushing me forward, and the old speaker's voice rolling through the words like weather, and then the oath was *in* me, living in my throat, and everyone was smiling, and I was eleven years old, and I smiled too because they were smiling and because no one had told me there was any other available response.

"No," I said. "No, there wasn't a question."

"There was never a question." Serrek's voice went flat, the flatness of a man reciting something he'd repeated to himself ten thousand times trying to sand off its edges. "The covenant runs on coerced oaths. Children, mostly. The Order calls it 'the Calling.' Families are honored when their children are called. Refusal isn't refusal—it's 'hearing the call imperfectly,' and there are remedies for that. Remedies." He spat the word like a pit. "I administered remedies for nineteen years, Bram. I held down a boy in Vellin Marsh who cried for his mother while they bound the word into him. I told myself it was holy. I told myself the covenant needed it. The covenant needs it the way a leech needs a leg."

Vess had gone very pale. "The stars," she said quietly. "The maps I charted before they went dark—I could never make the light patterns resolve. They kept flickering, like—" she stopped. "Like something struggling."

"Like something struggling," Serrek agreed. "Every swordspeaker who ever lived, all of them at once, holding up the sky with words they never chose to speak. And when the Court offered Corvane a bargain, the whole rotten structure came down to one keystone. You." He looked at me. "They told us you deserted. But I read the dispatches. I've been reading everything since the wards fell, because when the sky goes out you start asking questions, and asking questions is how wardens end up sitting in fens talking treason." He leaned forward. "Corvane sold your voice. Your *living word*, freely given or not—it wasn't free, none of ours were free, but yours was the keystone, and he traded it to save his guildhouse and his ledgers and his own skin. And the Order knew. The High Warden signed the acquiescence. We've been guarding a prison and calling it a cathedral."

The fen was silent except for the silence itself, which had texture now, like wool pressed against the ears. I thought about the bottled thunderstorm that had known my name. I thought about ten years of ledgers, of small miracles catalogued and never spoken, of a safety that had fit me like a too-small coat. I had thought I was hiding from my brotherhood. It turned out I'd been hiding from their crime, wearing it as my own shame the whole time.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because I'm supposed to kill you, and I can't." He said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "Not won't. Can't. I drew steel on you and my hands wouldn't close. Nineteen years of discipline, and my hands finally learned what my mouth never could." He laughed again, that broken-hinge laugh. "Do you know what they do to wardens who fail? Nothing. That's the horror of it. Nothing. Because the Order doesn't need to punish doubt. It just waits. Doubt does its own work. I've watched good men hollow out over years, still wearing the white, still saying the words, until there's nothing left but the uniform walking around."

"So walk away," Vess said. Her voice was hard. "Take off the white. Come with us."

"To where?" Serrek spread his hands at the grey country. "There's nowhere the Order isn't, and soon there'll be nowhere the Court isn't either. No." He stood, and pulled his sword from the earth, and did the thing I will remember until I die, whatever dying turns out to be: he reversed it and held it out to me, hilt-first.

"Kill me," he said.

* * *

The rain started then, thin and cold, the first honest weather in days. It beaded on the flat of his offered blade.

"Don't be dramatic," I said.

"I'm not being dramatic. I'm being practical. If I return without your head, they'll know I've turned. If I return with it—" his jaw worked, "—I'd rather be dead than carry it. Either way I'm finished. The only question is whether I finish as a traitor or as a corpse, and I find I'd rather be a corpse. Corpses get mourned. Traitors get *explained*."

"Serrek—"

"You don't understand what I am now." For the first time his composure went entirely, and underneath it was the boy I'd trained beside, the one who'd shared his rations with me when I was new and terrified and pretending not to be. "Everything I built my life on was a lie with good posture. Every oath I ever swore—my own oath, the warden's vow, all of it—was taken the same way yours was. Pushed. Pressured. Dressed up as glory so we wouldn't notice the chains. I have spent my whole life enforcing a covenant that was forged in a nursery, Bram. Give me one clean thing. One true choice, even if it's the last one. Let me give you what nobody gave either of us."

And here is the truth I will defend to whoever asks, if anyone ever does: I understood him completely. I wanted, with a ferocity that frightened me, to take the sword. Not to kill him—to give him the gift he was begging for, the one uncoerced act in a life of conscription. It would have been mercy shaped exactly like violence, and the two of us had both been raised in an order that never taught the difference.

That was precisely why I couldn't do it.

Because if I killed him, even at his pleading, even as a kindness, I would be doing the Order's work one final time—deciding for another person what their life was worth, spending them for a principle. The whole architecture of my existence had been built out of people deciding things *for* each other. Mothers. Masters. High Wardens. Guildmasters named Corvane. And every single one of them had called it duty, or love, or necessity, right up until the sky went black.

"No," I said.

His face fell like a building.

"Listen to me, Serrek." I got up. My legs were shaking and I let them; he should see it. "You want one free choice? Then hear this: killing yourself by my hand is still the Order's script. Die-in-battle, fall-with-honor, the beautiful ending they painted for us when we were children playing with wooden swords. It's the same coercion, just pointed inward. You're asking me to be the last hand that binds you."

"Then what do I *do*?"

"Live." I pushed the sword back toward him, gently, the way you'd return something borrowed. "Unheroically. Messily. Without an ending anyone scripted for you. Go back, or don't. Tell the truth, or spend years working up to it. Be a coward tomorrow and brave next week. Nobody's watching, Serrek. That's the terrible gift. The sky already fell. There is no honor left to lose. All that's left is what you choose, one ordinary day at a time, starting with the extraordinarily ordinary choice to keep breathing."

He stared at me. Rain ran off his chin. Somewhere in that stare, something structural gave way—I heard it almost, a soundless collapse, a worldview settling into rubble. He sat back down on the boundary stone, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and put his face in his hands, and this time when he made a sound it wasn't a laugh.

Vess crouched beside him. She didn't touch him—she was a stranger, and strangers' comfort is a currency you have to earn—but she stayed, and staying is most of it.

After a long time he lifted his head. His eyes were red-rimmed and, for the first time since the fen, clear.

"The First Hearth," he said. "That's where you're going. To reforge it."

"How did you—"

"I told you. I've been reading everything." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, a boy's gesture in a worn man's body. "Then you should know two things. First: the Hearth hasn't accepted a forging in four hundred years, and the last three speakers who tried died in the attempt. The old rites require an oath, and the Hearth has grown—particular. Hungry, maybe. Second." He hesitated, and I watched him decide, consciously, deliberately, to commit treason. "The Court isn't following you. It's *herding* you. Everything between here and the Hearth has gone quiet too easily. The villages empty. The roads open. They're not trying to stop you, Bram. They're delivering you."

"Why?" Vess breathed.

"Because a covenant reforged at the Hearth belongs to whoever holds the Hearth when the fire catches." Serrek stood, and picked up his sword, and sheathed it—sheathed it, the parley formally ended, though which side of the parley he was on now, not even he could say. "And the Court bought the deed to that ground a hundred years ago. From the Order. Who sold it the same way Corvane sold you."

He turned to go, then stopped, half-turned, rain streaming off his shoulders.

"Bram. The boy in Vellin Marsh. After they bound him, he couldn't speak at all for a year, and when the word finally woke in him, the first thing it did—the oath did, through his mouth, without his leave—was say his mother's name. Over and over. Looking for her." His voice barely held. "If you reforge this thing, whatever you build it from—build it from something kinder. Promise me that. It's the only oath I'll ever ask of anyone again."

He walked north into the rain, toward the Order that would hollow him or the truth that might save him, and I watched until the grey took him.

Vess touched my elbow. "Can we trust him?"

"No," I said. "But we can believe him. Those aren't the same thing anymore, and I suspect they never were." I looked south, toward the mountains and the First Hearth and the trap waiting around its fire. "Come on. Apparently we're expected."

Vess tightened her grip on my sleeve. "By whom?"

"By everyone," I said, "which means, for once, we're right on schedule."
