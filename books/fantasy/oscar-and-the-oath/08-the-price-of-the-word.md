## 8. The Price of the Word

The First Hearth was not what the songs promised.

The songs—what few remained of them, since songs had a way of going silent in the Hollow's wake—described a forge of white fire on a mountain's crown, tended by giants, ringing with the hammer-blows of creation. What we found instead was a door. Just a door, set into the side of a hill that couldn't be bothered to be a mountain, with a lintel carved in letters so old they'd worn down to suggestions.

"Cozy," Vess said.

"Don't judge it yet."

"I'm a cartographer. Judging places is most of my personality."

I pushed the door open, and the hill swallowed us whole.

Inside, the Hearth was exactly as the songs described, which somehow made it worse. A forge of white fire burned at the center of a cavern vast enough to have its own weather, and the light it cast wasn't light so much as *attention*—it fell on you like a gaze, took your measure, and filed you away for later. The walls were hung with the remnants of every covenant ever struck: chains of iron and ivy, ribbons of starlight gone gray, a sword so old its edge had been prayed to more than sharpened.

And in the middle of it all, an anvil. Empty. Waiting.

"Well," I said, because someone had to. "It remembers us."

"It remembers *you*," Vess said quietly. The white fire had turned toward her too, but only briefly, the way you glance at a stranger before returning to the friend you came with. She didn't seem offended. She seemed relieved, which told me she understood something I was still refusing to.

I walked toward the anvil because my legs had decided this was happening whether I consented or not. As I drew close, the fire leaned in, and I felt it reading me—not my name, not my history, but the thing underneath both: the oath. Ten years of silence wrapped around a word I'd spoken at nineteen, kneeling in blood, promising myself to a covenant that had just gotten sold out from under me by a man whose ledgers I used to balance.

The Hearth knew. Of course it knew. It had been there when I swore.

"All right," I said to it. "Let's talk terms."

The fire flared, and knowledge arrived the way it always does at the Hearth—not gently, but all at once, like a ledger dropped on your desk from a great height. Reforging the covenant required an oath. Not any oath. An oath *freely spoken*, uncoerced, unforced, given by a living voice with no blade at its throat and no fear in its heart. That was why the old covenant had failed—or rather, that was why it had been *breakable*. Serrek's confession still sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: the wardens had known for generations that their oaths were taken under duress, sworn by children with swords at their backs, and they'd called it tradition because calling it coercion would have meant dismantling everything.

The Hollow Court hadn't broken the covenant. It had simply collected on a debt the covenant had been carrying since its founding.

But here was the fine print, and the fine print was written in fire: an oath freely spoken bound the speaker forever. No revocation. No renegotiation. No dying gracefully out from under the terms. You gave your voice to the covenant until the covenant ended—and covenants, as far as anyone knew, did not end. They were reforged, renewed, passed down like a debt no one wanted to inherit.

I had done ten years of silence by choice. This would be silence forever by contract.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Somewhere behind me, Vess went very still.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. It's just—" I gestured at the anvil, the fire, the entire absurd architecture of my life. "I spent a decade hiding from exactly this. Built a whole quiet little existence around never speaking again. And now the universe is offering me the same deal with worse benefits and calling it salvation."

"Bram."

"I know." I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "I know. Someone has to do it. There's no one else. That's the whole joke, isn't it? The last oath-speaker, standing at the last forge, holding the last bill."

The fire pulsed, patient. Fires that burn white are always patient. It's one of the things that makes them unbearable.

That's when Vess stepped past me.

I want to say I saw it coming. I want to claim some swordspeaker's instinct, some honed awareness of threat, that should have let me stop her. But the truth is uglier: I was so busy staring into the fire and feeling sorry for myself that I didn't notice her moving until she was already at the anvil, already laying her palms flat against the cold iron, already saying, in the clear, steady voice she used for naming stars:

"I'll swear it."

The fire swung toward her like a lighthouse.

"No," I said.

"You haven't even heard the terms."

"I've heard the terms. I've *lived* the terms. Vess, step back from the anvil."

She didn't. She looked at me over her shoulder, and her expression was the worst one—the calm one, the mapmaker's one, the face she wore when she'd already charted the route and was merely informing me of the terrain. "Think about it. Your voice is the weapon. Yours is the one the Hollow Court wants, the one Corvane sold, the one every warden between here and the border is hunting. If you swear, you're not saving the world—you're handing yourself back to everyone who wants to own it through you. But me?" She spread her hands. "I'm nobody. I chart dead skies. I smell of ink and bad decisions. If I bind myself to the covenant, the Court gets nothing it wanted, the wards get their keystone, and you walk out of here free."

"That's not how freedom works."

"It's exactly how freedom works. I read the fine print off the fire same as you did."

"The fine print says *forever*, Vess. Forever. You'd spend eternity chained to a covenant that just spent ten years being run by Corvane. You think he was the first corrupt keeper? You think the next hundred years will be better? You'd be bound to all of it. Every betrayal. Every compromise. Every time some guildmaster decides to sell the sky, you'd feel it happen inside your own mouth."

"And you wouldn't?"

"I wouldn't be doing it!" My voice cracked across the cavern, and the white fire shivered, drinking in the sound, cataloguing it. I lowered it. "Vess. Please."

For a moment neither of us moved. The forge hissed. Somewhere above us, through however many tons of hill, the starless night pressed down.

Then she said, softly, "You're asking me to stand here and watch you chain yourself up again. After everything. After the road, after the villages where people forgot their own names, after you nearly spoke for that child in Marrow's Ford—I felt it, Bram. The whole air changed when you almost opened your mouth. You think I can watch you walk back into that cage and just... make maps about it?"

"Yes," I said. "That's precisely what I'm asking. Because the alternative is watching *you* do it, and I have discovered, somewhat recently, that I am constitutionally incapable of that."

"Why?"

And there it was. The question I'd been walking around for six hundred miles, the way you walk around a hole in the floor. Why. Why had I lied to Serrek's wardens without hesitation. Why had I held her hand through the Hollow's silence-lands when touching anyone had been a decade-long habit I'd broken. Why did the thought of her voice bound forever hurt worse than the thought of my own.

Because I loved her. Obviously. Catastrophically. In the specific, inconvenient way that made every noble plan she proposed feel like a knife offered handle-first.

"You know why," I said.

"Say it anyway."

"This isn't the moment—"

"There's never going to be a better one! Look where we are, Bram. We're standing in the room where oaths are born. If you can't say it *here*, with the fire listening, then it doesn't count anywhere." Her voice shook, finally, the calm cracking down the middle. "Say it, or let me swear, because those are the only two things left on the table and I refuse to leave this hill having done neither."

The fire waited. It was very good at waiting. It had been waiting since before either of us was born, and it would go on waiting long after, and it did not care which of us broke first.

I crossed the space between us. Took her hands off the anvil—they were cold, ink-stained, trembling slightly despite everything—and held them against my chest, where my heart was doing something unsuitable for a legendary swordspeaker, namely breaking.

"I love you," I said. "Not as an oath. Not as a bargain. I'm not giving it to the fire and I'm not giving it to the covenant. I'm telling *you*, because you asked, and because if you swear yourself away to an eternity of servitude I will spend that entire eternity—your eternity, our eternity—being the woman who let it happen. And I would rather unmake the world than be her."

Vess stared at me. "That's the most romantic threat I've ever heard."

"I'm a novelist of impossible trades. It comes naturally."

"So what's the play? Because unless you've got a third option, one of us walks to that anvil, and I've got seniority—you started arguing first."

I looked at the fire. It looked back, inscrutable, ancient, and—for the first time since we'd entered—I thought I saw something in it that wasn't patience. Something almost like curiosity. Covenants were forged from freely spoken words, the Hearth had told me. Oaths freely spoken bound forever.

But the Hearth had also said *freely*. Emphasis on freely. Nothing coerced. Nothing forced. Nothing given under pressure, or fear, or the terrible arithmetic of one life weighed against two worlds.

Which meant the words I was about to try had never been tried before. Because everyone who stood at this anvil assumed the choice was between swearing and not swearing. Nobody had asked whether the fire would accept something smaller. Something human. Something that could be taken back.

"Vess," I said slowly, "when I give you my word—my actual word, a promise, not an oath—can you take it back?"

Her brow furrowed. "Of course. A promise isn't binding. That's what makes it a promise instead of an oath. Anyone can break a promise; that's why keeping them means something."

"Exactly."

Understanding dawned across her face, slow and bright, like a star remembering how to burn. "Bram. You're not thinking of—"

The door at the top of the cavern boomed once, deep as a funeral bell, and began to glow at its edges with a light that wasn't light at all but the absence of everything—a hunger shaped like a doorway.

The Hollow Court had found the Hearth.

And it had brought company. Through the burning seam of the door, I heard footsteps. Many of them. And beneath the footsteps, faint and horribly familiar, a voice I'd hoped never to hear again, dry as ledger ash:

"Bram Nystrom," Guildmaster Corvane called, "open the door. I've come to fix what I broke—or die trying. Preferably the second one happens to me slowly."
