## 9. Covenant of Ash and Starlight

I had spent ten years learning the weight of silence, and it turns out I'd been training for the wrong war.

The First Hearth did not look like a place where covenants were born. It looked like a kitchen that had given up on being a kitchen—a great circular room at the top of a tower that had no business standing, with a hearth in the center wide enough to roast an ox whole and cold enough to have forgotten fire entirely. No altar. No throne. Just stone worn smooth by centuries of feet, and above us, through a shattered roof, a sky with nothing in it.

Vess stood at my shoulder with her maps clutched to her chest like a shield made of paper. Which, to be fair, they were.

"It's colder than it should be," she said. "Even accounting for everything."

"Everything is quite an accounting," I said.

"I'm serious, Bram."

"So am I. That's the tragedy of me."

But she was right. The cold wasn't weather. It was attention. The Hollow Court had bought something with my name on it, and purchases of that size do not go uncollected. You could feel them coming the way you feel a debt coming—not a sound, not yet, but a flattening of the world, as if somewhere out in the dark a vast patience had stopped waiting and started walking.

Corvane met us at the hearth's edge. He looked older than he had three days ago, which was impressive work for a man who'd already looked like a dried riverbed. He had the burned page's ashes in a small iron box, and he held it like a man holds a thing that has bitten him.

"You came," he said.

"You sound surprised."

"I priced your cowardice into my plans a decade ago." He almost smiled. "You've become expensive, Bram."

"There's a ledger joke in there somewhere, but I'm too tired to find it."

His eyes went past me to Vess, then back. "The Court is coming for its keystone. When they arrive, there will be a moment—I've seen the terms; I wrote half of them—when the covenant can be reforged or surrendered. The Hearth will offer itself to whoever stands here. An oath freely spoken binds forever. That's the price. That's always been the price. The Court knows you'll pay it, because they know what kind of fool carries an oath-blade she never draws."

"And you're telling me this why? Last time we spoke you offered to let me live if I stayed quiet. It was very generous. There was even a chair."

"Because staying quiet only spares *you*." He set the iron box down on the hearthstone. "Two worlds end otherwise. I did the arithmetic wrong ten years ago. I'm correcting it now, at interest."

Before I could decide whether that was redemption or just better bookkeeping, the door at the bottom of the tower rang like a struck bell.

* * *

They didn't break it down. That was the horror of it. The Hollow Court doesn't break things; it *unmakes the wanting*, and the door simply forgot it was meant to be closed.

They came up the stairs without hurry—figures like gaps in the air, tall as doorways, wearing silence the way other things wear skin. Where they stepped, the stone went grey and featureless, not damaged but *edited*. Behind them the stairwell was smooth blankness, a corridor that had never happened.

And behind them, held by nothing I could see, floated Guildmaster Corvane's purchase contract, written in a script that moved like water running uphill.

"The covenant is voided," said the foremost figure. Its voice wasn't a voice so much as the memory of one. "The keystone is claimed. The realms are ours to fold."

"Funny thing about ledgers," Corvane said, stepping between them and the hearth. "Entries can be contested."

He turned to look at me, and for one moment—the first in all the years I'd known him—he looked like a man instead of a guildmaster.

"Bram Nystrom. I sold your word. I am sorry." Then, quieter: "Hold the door however you can. I'll hold it how I can."

"What does that—" I started.

He opened his mouth and spoke the first words of a guildmaster's binding—the old invocation every member of the Guild of Impossible Things swears and nobody ever means—and poured himself into it. Every miracle he'd ever brokered, every impossible trade, every bottled storm and caged dawn, came home to him at once, called in against the debt. He became briefly, terribly bright, a man-shaped furnace of everything the guild had ever done, and he threw himself across the doorway like a bolt dropped across a lock.

The Court pressed against him. The brightness dimmed. But the door held.

"Hearth!" Vess shouted at me over the roar of it. "Bram—the Hearth! Whatever it's waiting for—it's waiting!"

She was right. The cold hearth at the center of the room had begun, very faintly, to remember warmth. Not fire. Something older than fire. Awaiting input, like a clerk who has heard a thousand oaths and knows exactly which ones to distrust.

I walked toward it, and the Court's nearest figure turned its attention on me, and I felt my silence—the silence I had built like a house and lived in like a prisoner—reach out and try to hold me the way it always had. Stay small. Stay unsaid. Stay safe.

Ten years of safety. Ten years of suffocation with excellent bookkeeping.

"No," I told it. Out loud. My voice cracked in the middle like a girl's. I didn't care.

I reached the hearthstone and put my hand flat on the warm-not-warm surface, and the Hearth rose up through my palm like a question asked gently.

*What do you swear?*

And here is the thing nobody tells you about oaths: they're easy. That's their whole trick. *I swear forever. I swear unto death. I bind myself, take my sword-word, make me a weapon.* Easy words, because they cost nothing when you say them and everything after. The covenant had been built on easy words for a thousand years—coerced, conscripted, cornered into corners until swearing was just another word for surrender. Serrek knew it. The wardens had always known it. And the Court had purchased a keystone made of exactly that: a woman bound so long ago she couldn't remember being free.

The Hearth waited. The Court pressed. Corvane's light guttered.

I thought about Vess, who mapped a sky that no longer existed because someone ought to keep faith with lost things. I thought about a child in a hollow village whose name I nearly spent to save. I thought about a small shop of possible things I hadn't dared want yet.

So I didn't swear.

"I promise," I said.

The Hearth stilled. Even the Court paused, if a hole in the world can be said to pause.

"Not an oath. A promise. I promise to stand between these realms and whatever would fold them—for as long as I choose, and no longer. I give it freely. And I can take it back, any day, any hour, whenever I stop meaning it—which is precisely why it's worth having. Because it'll be true every single morning I wake up and mean it again."

My swordspeech rose in me, the old power, the blade-word that had made my legend, straining toward the shape of an oath it had spoken a thousand times—

*I refuse,* I told it. *You're not a chain anymore. You're a choice.*

And the swordspeech burned.

It didn't hurt, exactly. It hurt worse than hurting—it was *leaving*. Ten years of silence peeling away, and beneath the silence the oath, and beneath the oath the girl who'd sworn it, and the fire took all of it down to something small and ordinary and shaking. I felt my legend go out of me like heat from an open hand. I felt the exact moment the covenant caught what I offered—caught it, tested it, found it free—and accepted.

Light went up through the broken roof and out into the dead sky like a shout.

The stars came back.

Not all at once, and not the same—they returned changed, rearranged, a sky re-shelved by a librarian who'd been drinking—but they returned, burning steady, and the Hollow Court's figures thinned like smoke remembering it was only smoke. The contract floating above them unraveled mid-air, letters falling upward into the new starlight.

At the door, Corvane's brightness failed all at once. He folded down onto the stairs, an old man again, used up, spent to the last coin.

I reached him in three steps. His eyes found mine, and they were clear for the first time since I'd known him.

"Ledger's balanced," he whispered. Almost a smile. "Check my work."

Then he wasn't checking anything anymore.

* * *

Afterward, the tower was very quiet. The good kind of quiet—the kind with stars in it.

Vess found me sitting on the hearthstone with Corvane's hand still in mine, because I hadn't been able to decide when letting go became rude. She knelt beside me without a word and took my other hand, and we stayed like that while the sky outside did its slow, staggering recovery.

"Can you still speak it?" she asked finally. "The swordspeech."

I tried, privately, in the place where it had lived. Empty. Not gone like a wound—gone like a room after the furniture's been carried out. Swept clean. Ordinary.

"No," I said. And then, because it was true and I wanted to say true things now: "Good."

"You don't sound sure."

"I don't sound sure about anything. That's rather the point of a promise." I looked up at her, at the starlight catching the edges of her maps. "It comes back every day or not at all, Vess. Every morning I'll mean it again or I won't. There's no legend holding me up anymore. Just me, showing up."

"That sounds terrifying."

"It's the best trade I ever made. Don't tell the guild—they'll want it catalogued."

She laughed, wet and quiet, and leaned her forehead against mine, and above us the changed stars burned on, keeping no one's oath, needing no one's.

Somewhere below, boots on the stairs—steady ones, familiar ones, a whole company of them. Wardens. Keepers-now, perhaps, if the world was feeling merciful. Serrek leading them, maybe, carrying grief and questions and a brotherhood's ashes.

Vess squeezed my hand. "Company's coming."

"Let them knock," I said, and discovered I meant it, and discovered that meaning it was the whole covenant entire. "We've got a door, a hearth, and a sky. For once in my miserable life, I'm holding all the assets."

The footsteps climbed.
