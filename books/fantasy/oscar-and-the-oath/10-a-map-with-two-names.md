## 10. A Map With Two Names

*Bram*

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

I know this because the Guild of Impossible Things kept meticulous records even in its dying days, and I had spent ten years learning to think in ledger entries. *One sky, restored, condition: changed. Provenance: one promise, freely given. Cost: see attached.* There was no attachment. The cost was me, or the part of me that had been a legend, and honestly? The legend had terrible posture and worse conversation.

The new stars were not the old stars. Anyone who told you otherwise was selling something, and after Corvane's funeral—which was very well attended by people who had spent years avoiding him—I was in no mood to buy. The old sky had been a wall, beautiful and absolute, hung there by coerced oaths and maintained by wardens with swords. The new sky was more like a garden. It grew. Constellations drifted where they pleased, rearranged themselves over seasons, and if you watched long enough—and Vess always watched long enough—you could catch them mid-decision.

I liked that about it. A sky that could change its mind felt like a sky that understood me.

My voice, for the record, was completely ordinary now. That took some getting used to. For eleven years I had spoken in careful, flattened syllables, terrified that the wrong word pitched the right way would unmake a door, a man, a border. Now I could say "pass the bread" without checking whether the bread would obey. I could swear at a stubborn drawer. I once called a rainstorm a bastard, out loud, on my own doorstep, and the rainstorm did absolutely nothing about it. I nearly wept. Vess thought this was hilarious, which is one of approximately four hundred reasons I love her.

We had opened the shop in the spring.

* * *

*Vess*

The shop sat between Bram's old guildhouse and the harbor, in a building that had been three different failed businesses before us—a chandler, a fortune-teller, and briefly, according to a neighbor with strong opinions, an illegal oyster bar. We sold possible things.

Not impossible things. The Guild had cornered that market for centuries and look where it got them: dissolved, disgraced, and rebranded as keepers. Possible things were humbler. A compass that pointed at whatever you'd lost—not found it, just pointed, which was usually enough. Seeds from trees that hadn't grown yet. Maps, obviously. Vess's maps, drawn fresh each week against a sky that refused to hold still, and Bram's ledgers, because a woman who had catalogued miracles for a decade developed opinions about filing that you could not shake loose with a crowbar.

"Your handwriting is a war crime," she said, on the morning I named the constellation.

"It's legible."

"It's a ransom note written by a spider."

This was our love language. We were working on a second one, but the first was fluent.

Here is what I had not told her yet: I had finished the chart. The full survey of the new sky, every wandering constellation pinned down, every drift measured across two seasons of cold nights and colder tea. And in the northeast quadrant, low over the sea where the wound in the old heaven used to gape, there was a cluster of seven stars that had arrived together and never moved apart. They held formation like soldiers. They burned white-gold, the exact color of oath-steel before a speaker draws it.

I knew what they were. I had known for months. Naming them was the hard part, because naming a thing makes it yours, and these stars weren't mine.

They were hers. Or they had been, back when she still carried the blade they resembled—the sword she'd left standing in the First Hearth like a grave marker for her own legend, because you cannot sell an oath-blade and you cannot keep one either, not after what she'd done.

Bram came down the stairs with two cups of tea and her reading spectacles pushed up into her hair, which meant she'd forgotten they were there, which happened daily now, gloriously, mundanely, forever.

"You're staring at the ceiling," she said.

"I'm staring at the chart."

"The chart is on the ceiling. You nailed it up there while I slept, which raises several questions, chief among them *why*."

"So customers can see it," I said. "And so I could check my work." I turned the cup in my hands. "It's done. The whole sky."

She went quiet in the specific way she went quiet when something mattered—shoulders settling, breath going slow, the stillness of a woman who had once stood in front of armies. Then she crossed the room and stood under the chart with her tea going cold, and read it the way she read everything: thoroughly, silently, and slightly judgmentally.

"There," she said finally, tapping the glass with one finger. "That cluster. Seven stars, tight formation. You've left the name blank."

"I know."

"You never leave anything blank. You labeled the *empty patch* over the Hollow lands. 'Formerly a sky.' Very grim. Very you."

"Very me," I agreed. My heart was doing something undignified. "That one I couldn't name alone."

"Bram." She turned around. She had that expression, the one that meant she'd already guessed and was bracing against hoping. Ten years of hiding teaches a person to flinch from good news. I was teaching her, slowly, to stop.

"They came through the night your promise took," I said. "Seven stars, holding formation, the color of your blade. The whole coast has started calling them something already, actually. Sailors. They're calling it the Kept Word." I shrugged, aiming for casual and missing by a mile. "But sailors are sentimental and also wrong half the time. So. If you want to name it properly—"

"No," she said.

And then, before my face could do anything embarrassing:

"*You* name it. You drew it. You sat through two winters of frostbite for it. I just—" She gestured vaguely at herself, at all of it, the saved worlds and the ordinary voice and the tea. "I was the instrument. You were the one watching."

"That's not how I remember it."

"That's because you insist on being modest, which is insufferable in a living legend."

"I'm not a legend anymore. I'm a shopkeeper with a drawer problem."

"Then name it after the drawer," I said, "or let me do what I've wanted to do since the night the stars went out and I walked into your guildhouse smelling blade-oil and lies and the bravest thing I ever met."

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was, I suspected, the entire foundation of her character.

"Oathbreaker's Rest," I said. "That's what the wardens called you, for ten years. Serrek told me. They said it like a curse." I looked up at the seven white-gold stars holding their formation over the sea. "So I'm naming it Oathbreaker's Rest, and every navigator, every sailor, every child learning the sky will say it every clear night until the end of the world, and it will mean exactly what it should mean. Not a curse. A place where someone finally put the burden down."

Bram Yarrow, last oath-bound swordspeaker, first person in history to talk her way out of eternity, looked at the ceiling of our shop and cried into her tea.

I pretended not to notice. I'm kind like that.

* * *

*Bram*

Serrek visited on the first day of autumn, wearing keeper's grey instead of warden's black, and carrying a basket, which somehow unsettled me more than his sword ever had.

"We bring bread now," he said, when I stared at it. "It's part of the reforms. Keepers provide for the communities they watch over. Chapter four of the new covenant. Vess helped write it."

"Of course she did."

"She's very persuasive in committee."

"She once argued a lighthouse into moving."

He set the basket down and looked around the shop—at the compasses pointing at lost things, at the seed packets, at the chart on the ceiling with its seven named stars—and something in his face did the thing it had been doing for a year now, the slow unfurrowing of a man discovering that the world didn't end when he stopped believing in it.

"The northern villages remember their names again," he said. "All of them. Even the ones the Hollow ate decades ago. The records match. It's as if—" He stopped. He'd learned to stop himself around me, to phrase things carefully, though he needn't have. My most dangerous utterance these days was a complaint about oyster-related zoning disputes.

"As if the silence was never really gone," I finished for him. "Just held. Waiting for someone to hand it back something worth trading for." I shrugged. "A promise freely given doesn't just bind the giver, Serrek. It binds everything it touches. Even the dark. *Especially* the dark."

He nodded slowly. Then, because he was still Serrek, still my shield-brother under all the grief and the grey wool: "The order wants to give you back your rank. Formally rescind the desertion charge. There's a ceremony. There would be a *sash*, Bram."

"There is no force in this universe or the adjacent ones that gets me into a sash."

"I told them you'd say that."

"And you were right, and you may bill them for the prophecy."

He laughed. It still startled me a little, hearing him laugh—he'd gone so long without. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and said, quietly, the way men of our generation said the things that mattered: "The Hearth-keepers asked me to tell you the blade is still there. Standing where you left it. They've built a shrine around it, sort of. Pilgrims leave notes." A pause. "Mostly thank-yous. Some complaints. One marriage proposal, apparently."

"Forward it to Vess."

"I assumed it was for Vess."

"It was a joke, Serrek. I make those now. It's my whole personality."

He smiled, shook his head, and walked out into the ordinary afternoon, and I watched him go the way you watch a scar stop hurting—grateful, and a little amazed, and ready to get on with the day.

* * *

*Vess*

On the evening of the winter solstice—the longest night, the one we'd all learned to stop fearing—we closed the shop early and climbed the hill behind the house with a bottle of wine and a blanket and no agenda whatsoever, which was, I had come to understand, the entire point of everything.

Below us, the town lit its lamps one by one. The harbor moved gently. Somewhere in the shop we'd left unlocked, the compass pointed faithfully at nothing anyone needed finding anymore.

Bram lay with her head on my shoulder, tracing constellations with a finger, and I realized I was doing the thing I'd sworn off: memorizing. Charting her. Old habits. But I let myself do it anyway, because some maps you keep even when the territory agrees to stay.

"Penny for them," she said.

"I was thinking that I spent my whole life mapping a sky that was dying, and everyone thought I was mad, and maybe I was, a little. And now the sky's alive and changing and I'll never finish the atlas. Not truly. It'll be out of date the moment I ink it."

"Tragic," she said, dry as chalk. "Whatever will you do with your endless unfinished work and your successful shop and your—what was the phrase—'the bravest thing I ever met.'"

"Shut up."

"Make me. Oh wait." She grinned up at me, ordinary-voiced, ordinary-eyed, extraordinary anyway. "You can't. I'm just a woman saying words. They don't do anything at all."

"Everything you say does something," I said. "That's the whole problem with you."

She was quiet a moment. Out over the water, Oathbreaker's Rest rose above the horizon, seven stars steady in their keeping, and below it a new constellation I'd only just begun to chart—two bright points close together, closer than any others in the sky, drifting as one.

"What do you suppose those are?" Bram asked, nodding at them.

I looked for a long time. I thought about names, and burdens, and the difference between a map and the country it describes.

"I haven't decided yet," I said. "Ask me tomorrow. Ask me every tomorrow."

And beside me, in a voice that commanded nothing and promised everything, Bram laughed, and the sky went on making itself up as it went along, and neither of us was bound to anything at all—

except, freely, gladly, revocably never, to each other.
