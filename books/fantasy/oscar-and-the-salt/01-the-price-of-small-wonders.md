## 1. The Price of Small Wonders

The fog came off the salt flats at dawn the way it always did in Verrane — thick as wool, tasting faintly of old tears. Isandro Hartwell had lived eleven years inside that taste, and he had stopped noticing it the way a man stops noticing his own heartbeat: it was simply there, the city's pulse and his own.

He set out his stall before the market bells rang, arranging his wares with the practiced care of a man who had nothing left to hide but pretended otherwise. Charms against toothache. Charms for safe childbirth, though he sold those reluctantly; they never worked as well as mothers hoped. A row of luck-knots braided from grey cord, each one knotted nine times while whispering words that no longer whispered back.

That was the truth of it, the truth he carried under his tongue like a stone: the words didn't work anymore. Not really. They *moved* — a charm would warm in the palm, a knot would tighten on its own, small theater enough to convince a buyer — but the power behind them had gone out of the world ten years ago, the night his god stopped answering.

He still made the gestures. A priest who has lost his god is just a man in vestments; a charm-seller who has lost his god is a fraud with good posture. Isandro had chosen fraud. It paid better than honesty, and honesty, in Verrane, could get you hanged.

"Master Hartwell."

The widow Maren stood at the edge of his stall, hands folded so tightly her knuckles showed white through skin gone papery with age. She came every market-day now. She had been coming for three weeks, circling, touching things without buying, working up to whatever she was working up to.

"Mistress Maren." He smiled the smile he kept for customers — warm at the edges, empty at the center. "Fog's thick this morning. Careful on the cobbles."

"It's my son." She said it the way people said such things in Verrane, flatly, because grief here was a currency everyone was tired of counting. "Tobin. He went out on the flats last month. Harvest crew." She swallowed. "They found his coat."

Isandro's hands went still over the luck-knots. The salt harvest took men every season — the flats opened sinkholes without warning, or the fog came down wrong and a man walked in circles until his lungs filled. The Wardens called it an occupational hazard and taxed the funerals.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it more than he expected to.

"They never found him. Just the coat." Her eyes came up, wet and fierce. "I want a finding-charm. I know you sell them. I know they're not licensed, and I don't care. I want to know where my boy is. Even if he's—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "Even if he's somewhere I can put a stone."

*Mistress.* The refusal rose in him automatically, the way it had risen a hundred times. Finding-charms were the hardest kind, the kind that needed a listening god behind them, an ear turned toward the world. His charms warmed and tightened and did their little pantomimes, but when it came to actually *finding* something lost —

Except.

He looked at her face, at the three weeks of circling his stall, and something in his chest — some stubborn, stupid organ that should have scarred over years ago — turned over.

"Let me see your hand," he said.

She gave it. Her palm was rough with salt-burn, the mark of a woman who had worked the pans herself once. He closed her fingers around one of the grey luck-knots, then covered her hand with both of his, and did the thing he hadn't done in ten years: he reached.

Not with words. Words were for customers. He reached the way he had been taught at nineteen, kneeling in a chapel that smelled of tallow and cold iron, reaching for the presence that had filled the world back then — diffuse, patient, attentive, like sunlight through water. The Quiet One. The god who listened so the sworn could speak. Every charm Isandro had ever made had been a conversation: he asked, the god answered, the cord remembered.

He asked now. *Show me where the lost thing lies.*

Nothing answered.

Of course nothing answered. Ten years of nothing answering. But his hands knew the shape of the asking, and the knot in the widow's fist grew warm — genuinely warm, warmer than any trick of friction or oil — and pulled, gently, twice, toward the northeast. Toward the flats.

It wasn't the god. It couldn't be the god. It was probably residual — old prayers soaked into the braid like smoke into a wall, echoing without a speaker. It was probably nothing. It might not even be true.

But Maren's breath caught, and her eyes went wide, and she said, "Northeast. That's where the harvest crews camp," and Isandro felt the familiar sick lurch of being a fraud who had accidentally told the truth.

"That far, I can't promise," he said quietly. "Charms fade. Knots loosen. But if you hold it and walk the direction it pulls, and you keep saying his name — Tobin, just his name, plain, no fancy words — it'll do what it can."

"What it can," she repeated. She pressed coins into his palm — too many, he saw immediately, nearly double — and when he tried to give them back she stepped away from the stall with the dignity of a queen dismissing a court. "You've given me a direction, Master Hartwell. I haven't had a direction in three weeks."

She walked into the fog, northeast, holding the knot like a lantern.

Isandro watched her go and did not count the coins. He pushed them to the bottom of his till without looking, the way you'd bury something you were ashamed of.

*It'll do what it can.* What could it do? Nothing. The god was silent. Whatever had warmed that knot had come from somewhere else — some residue, some leftover — and leftovers ran out. He'd been selling the dregs of a dead faith for a decade, and someday soon even the dregs would be gone, and then he'd be just another man selling knotted string.

He was still staring at his empty hands when the bells changed.

* * *

Verrane had two sets of bells. The market bells rang the hours, bright and indifferent. The Warden bells rang only for raids — a low, grinding triple-strike that rolled through the fog like something heavy being dragged across stone.

Every vendor within earshot began, simultaneously, to pack.

Isandro moved faster than most. Ten years of raids taught a man economy: the false-bottomed crate first, charms swept in by the armful, the lid dropped and the latch thrown before the second bell finished tolling. The luck-knots went into a flour sack. The toothache charms could stay — those were technically legal, blessed by no one, mere folk-craft — but the finding-charms, the dream-quieting charms, the little braided protections against the Wardens themselves: all contraband, all hidden.

By the time the third bell faded, his stall looked like a poor man's notion of a poor man's stall. Cord. Beads. Dried herbs in paper twists. He sat down on his stool and arranged his face into the blank patience of an honest craftsman.

The Wardens came up Saltrow four abreast, grey cloaks beaded with fog, salt-crusted boots striking the cobbles in unison. They wore masks — white, smooth, featureless except for narrow eye-slits — and the masks were the worst part, worse than the bill-hooks they carried or the writs they nailed to doors. A masked man could be reasoned with. A mask could not.

At their head walked a lieutenant Isandro didn't recognize, young, his mask pushed up on his forehead to reveal a face barely older than a novitiate's. That was new policy, Isandro had heard: show faces in the morning markets, seem friendly, save the masks for the actual seizing. Kindness as tactics.

"Licensed goods only today," the lieutenant announced to the row, voice pitched to carry. "Inspection writ, Auditor's seal. Cooperate and be done by noon."

They worked down the row. Isandro watched them turn over a fruit-seller's baskets, run a gloved finger along a fishmonger's scales, crack open a potter's kiln and leave the shattered pieces on the ground when they found nothing. The potter, a big woman with forearms like hawsers, stood watching with her jaw tight and said nothing, because saying anything was how inspections became arrests.

Then they reached Isandro's stall.

"Name?" the lieutenant said.

"Hartwell. Isandro Hartwell."

"Charm-work?"

"Folk-craft. Knots and beads. No licensed invocation." He said it smoothly, the lie worn soft as river stone from handling. "No god behind it. You're welcome to look."

The lieutenant looked. That was the trouble with the young ones — they actually looked. He picked up a luck-knot, turned it over, held it near his lips the way trained Wardens did, testing for the faint warmth of active blessing. Isandro kept his breathing even and his hands loose and thought, with the discipline of long practice, about absolutely nothing.

The knot stayed cold. Of course it did. Everything was cold now.

"Where'd you train?" the lieutenant asked suddenly.

Isandro blinked. "Nowhere worth mentioning."

"The knotwork's order-taught. Nine-fold binding, tensioned left to right. That's liturgical pattern." The young man's eyes came up, sharp under the pushed-up mask. "Order of the Quiet Bell?"

For a moment the fog seemed very loud.

"I copied the patterns off a dead man's stall," Isandro said. "Dead men don't enforce licensing."

Something flickered in the lieutenant's face — doubt, maybe, or the particular hunger of a young officer who suspects he's found something worth reporting. He weighed the luck-knot in his palm one more time, and Isandro understood, with cold clarity, that the whole morning balanced on what the boy decided next.

A shout saved them both. Down the row, near the fishmongers, a scuffle — a runner, some kid, darting between stalls with a stolen writ-pole under his arm, half the market's children chasing him for the sport of it. The lieutenant swore, dropped the knot, and whistled two of his squad after the boy. The inspection dissolved into the general chaos of Verrane refusing, as it did daily and in a hundred small ways, to be governed.

Isandro exhaled slowly and began repacking his flour sack with steady hands.

Only when the Wardens had moved two rows on did he let himself feel the shake. It settled into his fingers, fine as salt dust. *Order of the Quiet Bell.* Ten years since anyone had said that name to his face. Ten years since he'd worn the grey-and-white, since he'd knelt in chapter-house silence waiting for a god who had stopped existing, since the night the whole order had gathered in the great chapel and prayed until dawn to a silence so total it had weight, and the abbot had finally stood and said, in a voice like a man reading his own death warrant: *We are released from our vows. Go home.*

Released. As if it were a kindness. As if the god had died honorably instead of simply — stopping. Isandro had spent ten years telling himself that story. Gods died. Faiths guttered. The world forgot, and forgetting was only weather.

So why did the lieutenant's question feel like a hook set behind his sternum?

* * *

He closed the stall early and took the long way home, through the lanes where the fog pooled deepest, past the tanneries and the shuttered prayer-houses that the city had converted to granaries and pawnshops once the gods went quiet. All of them quiet. Not just his. Every god in every chapel, everywhere, on the same night, ten years ago — the Night of Still Water, the almanacs called it, though nobody agreed on why. The world's ears had been stopped, all at once, and no prophet, priest, or scholar had ever produced a reason.

Isandro had his private theory, which was that there was no theory. Things ended. He'd built a life on that belief, and he intended to keep building on it, because the alternative was standing in a chapel at dawn praying to nothing, and he had done enough of that to last several lifetimes.

His shop sat at the end of Brine Court, a crooked two-story with a charm-shop below and rooms above, the windows fogged from within by the brazier he kept burning against the damp. He unlocked the door, stepped in, and knew before his foot found the first stair that someone was already upstairs.

There was a smell. Cold iron and incense — chapter-house incense, the blend the Quiet Bell used, frankincense cut with something mineral. Nobody in Verrane burned that anymore. Nobody in the world, probably.

He took the stairs quietly anyway, out of habit, and pushed open the door to find a woman sitting at his table with her boots up, eating his bread.

She was fifty now, maybe, grey shot through dark hair cropped short, a dueling scar bisecting one eyebrow. She wore no uniform, but she sat the way soldiers sit, and the sword leaning against his table bore the Quiet Bell's crest — a bell with no clapper — freshly polished.

"Serah," Isandro said.

"Isandro." Serah Voss chewed, swallowed, wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "Your bread's stale. Ten years, and you still can't bake."

"You broke into my house."

"Your lock's an insult. I could've picked it at twelve." She tipped her chair back onto two legs, studying him with the flat grey eyes that had unnerved novitiates and terrified enemies for as long as he'd known her. His commander. Once. The best of them, they'd said, and they'd been right, which was somehow worse. "Sit down, Hartwell. We need to talk about a debt."

"I don't have debts."

"You have one." She set the chair legs down. "Mine. You remember the winter the order collapsed — the pensions, the safe-passage papers, the way certain members of certain chapters got warning before the Wardens started sweeping ex-sworn? You remember who signed your exit writ?"

Isandro remembered. He had spent ten years trying not to. A forged writ in a neat hand, slipped under his door the week the purges began, with a single line appended: *This buys me a favor, owed indefinitely, redeemable whenever I choose. — S.V.*

He had known, all these years, that the favor would come. He had hoped, with the same dogged dishonesty he brought to everything, that it wouldn't.

"You're calling it in," he said.

"I'm calling it in." Serah leaned forward, and for just a moment the soldier's flatness cracked, and underneath it he saw something he had never seen on her face before, not even the night the god went silent. Fear. "Tomorrow, maybe the day after, you're going to have a visitor. A child. And when he comes, Isandro, whatever he says to you, whatever language he speaks — you send word to me before you do anything else. Before you run, before you sell him, before you burn your shop and disappear like you've been planning to for a decade. You send word to me."

"A child." Isandro heard his own voice go carefully neutral. "What child?"

Serah stood, took up her sword, and slung it across her back. At the window she paused, silhouetted against the fog, and said the rest without turning around.

"There's a boy walking toward Verrane out of the flats. Eight, nine years old. Alone. And Isandro — the Wardens have reports of him from six villages, and in every report, the same detail." Her hand tightened on the sill. "He's speaking Ilvaren. Fluently. Like it's his mother tongue."

The name landed in the room like a dropped bell.

Ilvaren. The country that had never existed. The country whose name wasn't written anywhere because there was nothing to write it on — no maps, no ruins, no graves, no songs, no war memorials, nothing. A hole shaped like a nation, which everyone agreed had always been a hole, and no one could say why the agreement felt so much like a wound.

"There is no Ilvaren," Isandro said.

"No," Serah agreed. "There isn't." She swung out the window onto the drainpipe with the ease of long practice, and paused there, half in fog. "Send word, Isandro. Because if the boy is real, then the Auditor knows he's real. And the Auditor does not permit things that are real."

She dropped into the fog and was gone.

Isandro stood a long time in his cold room, looking at the bitten bread, the polished sword-crest, the place where she'd been. Outside, the Warden bells rang again — distant now, another row, another search — and beneath them, almost too low to hear, the tide was coming in off the flats, carrying the smell of salt and something older underneath it, something that smelled, absurdly, impossibly, like white stone and singing.

He caught himself listening for it. Caught himself, for the first time in ten years, *praying* — not to anything, just the shape of a prayer, the empty gesture of a man reaching for a god who wasn't there.

The fog pressed against the glass. Somewhere out in it, a child was walking toward his door, speaking a language that had never existed.

And Isandro Hartwell, fraud, apostate, debtor, understood with terrible certainty that his ten quiet years had just ended.
