## 4. Oscar

The man's house smelled of salt and smoke and something older underneath, like a cellar that had never been opened. Oscar sat on a stool too tall for him, his feet swinging above the floorboards, and watched Isandro Hartwell move around the small room the way water moves around stones — carefully, always carefully, as if the world were full of things that might break if he touched them wrong.

Oscar knew things about this man he should not have known.

He knew that Isandro took his tea bitter, without honey, because honey reminded him of a morning he could not name. He knew that the charm-seller slept with his back to the wall and his boots within reach of his hand. He knew — and this was the strangest knowing, the one that sat in Oscar's chest like a swallowed stone — that Isandro Hartwell had once been called by another name in a language with no country left to hold it.

He did not know how he knew these things. That was the trouble with everything lately. The knowing came first and the understanding limped along behind, and no one — not the woman at the well, not the ferryman, not the gray-faced priest who had crossed himself three times and given him bread — believed a word he said.

* * *

He had been walking for nine days when the voice found him.

Oscar counted the days the way his mother — no, not his mother, the other mother, the real one, the one who was also somehow not real — had taught him to count: knots in a cord, one for each sunrise. Nine knots. Nine mornings of waking in ditches and hedgerows and once, memorably, in the dry bed of a canal that smelled of dead fish and old prayers.

Before that there had been the orphanage at Marrow's Ford, where the matrons wore gray and spoke in voices sanded smooth of anything sharp. Before that — and here his memory did a thing memory should not do, folding like paper, showing him two lives laid over each other like leaves in a book —

Before that there had been white stone.

He remembered it most clearly in the hour before sleep, when the matron's lamp went down the corridor and the dark came in close enough to touch. He would close his eyes and be somewhere else entirely: a palace on a hill above a green sea, its walls so pale they seemed cut from moonlight. Courtiers in robes the color of wet sand. A garden where the fountains ran not with water but with something thicker, brighter, that caught the sun and threw it back in pieces.

His mother had sung to him there.

Not the gray mother. Not the matron with her smooth voice and her bread-and-water kindness. His true mother, the queen — though she had never liked that word, he remembered, she had said *a queen is a job, my love, I am your mother first* — had sung to him in the tongue that made the Wardens' faces go white when they heard it now.

She would sit by the window where the sea-light pooled on the floor, and she would sing, and her voice was low and warm and it moved through the words the way a hand moves through water:

*Ael iruven, ael soranne,*
*the tide remembers what the stone forgets...*

He did not know what the words meant, exactly. And yet he knew them completely, the way you know your own heartbeat. When he sang them — and he sang them sometimes, quietly, into his pillow at the orphanage, because they were the only thing he had carried out of the white palace — the other children said he was making up gibberish. The matrons said he was making up gibberish. One of them had struck him across the mouth for it, gently, the way you strike a child you believe you are saving, and said, "We don't use sounds like that here, Oscar. There are no countries with those words."

But there *were*. There had been. He had stood on its balconies. He had been carried through its corridors on his father's shoulders while soldiers lined the walls — and the soldiers, oh, the soldiers were the part he could never make anyone understand, because the soldiers were made of brine.

They stood at their posts without moving for hours, days, and if you looked closely — and children look closely; it is the one advantage of being small — you saw that beneath their armor they were not flesh. They were seawater given shape and discipline, salt-crusted and gleaming, and when they saluted, droplets fell from their gauntlets and did not splash but hung in the air a moment, remembering, before they fell. His mother had told him once, laughing, that the brine-guard cost nothing to feed and never slept, and his father had said, not laughing, "That is because they are already dead, and the sea has only lent them back to us."

Lent. Everything in Ilvaren was lent. The land itself, his mother said, was borrowed from the ocean and would be returned someday, and the trick of a good life was to be worth the borrowing.

Then the borrowing had been called in all at once, and there was salt where the country used to be, and maps with a hole shaped like grief, and a boy of eight standing alone on a road with nine knots in his cord and two mothers in his head and no one, nowhere, who believed a single word he said.

Until the door with the crooked latch.

* * *

"You're staring," Isandro said, without turning from the stove.

"I'm thinking," Oscar said.

"Dangerous habit. Gets people hanged in this city." The man set a bowl of barley soup in front of him, and Oscar noticed — the way he noticed everything now, because noticing was the only weapon a child had — that Isandro gave him the bowl with the unchipped rim. He kept the chipped one for himself. Adults did not usually do that. Adults at the orphanage ate first and best and called it order.

"Eat," Isandro said. "Then sleep. In the morning we're going to talk about how you found this house, and why you knocked on mine specifically, out of every door in Verrane."

"I didn't find it," Oscar said. "It was given to me."

Isandro went very still. It was a small stillness, controlled, the stillness of a man who had learned that flinching invited questions. But Oscar saw it. He was beginning to learn to read this man the way he had once learned to read the faces of courtiers — which way the wind blew behind the eyes.

"Given," Isandro repeated.

"A voice." Oscar wrapped both hands around the bowl, letting the warmth climb into his fingers. "On the road. The third night, when the rain came sideways and I thought maybe I would just lie down in the ditch and let it have me. A voice, under the rain. Not in my ears. Under them, if that makes sense. Like hearing through the ground."

The fire popped. Isandro did not move.

"It said one thing," Oscar continued. "Over and over, until I had it by heart. It said: *the sworn one will know the door.*"

Something crossed the man's face then — quick as a fish under ice, gone before you could name it. But Oscar had seen it. Grief. Old grief, the kind that has stopped bleeding and started calcifying, like coral grown over a wreck.

"And you walked," Isandro said quietly, "for six more days. On the word of a voice in the rain."

"The voice knew your name," Oscar said. "I didn't. I only knew the door when I saw it. The crooked latch. The salt stains on the step in the shape of—" He stopped, because the shape was a word in the dead tongue, a warding-word, and saying it aloud in this city felt like striking a match indoors. "You know the shape," he finished instead. "I saw you look at it."

For a long moment Isandro said nothing at all. Then he sat down across from Oscar, slowly, the way men sit down when their legs have decided the matter without consulting them, and he looked at the boy for a long time with eyes the color of a winter harbor.

"What else does the voice say?" he asked.

"That's the thing." Oscar set down the spoon. This was the part he had rehearsed through nine days of walking, the speech he had prepared for whatever adult finally listened, and now that the moment had come the words came out smaller than they had sounded in his head. "That's the thing. It doesn't say anything else. It only says that. One sentence, over and over, like — like a bell that only knows one ring. *The sworn one will know the door.* I've been carrying it for nine days and I don't even know what door. Do you?"

Isandro looked into the fire. When he answered, his voice had a strange flatness, the flatness of a man reading from a document he hates.

"No," he said. "But I think I know who's asking."

* * *

Sleep did not come easily in a stranger's house, even a kind stranger's house, even a house that smelled faintly — underneath the salt and smoke — of the same incense that had burned in the white palace's chapels. Oscar lay on the pallet Isandro had made up by the hearth and stared at the ceiling beams and tried to sort his two lives into some kind of order, the way you'd sort shells on a beach: this one from the gray world, this one from the drowned one.

Except the shells wouldn't stay sorted.

He would reach for the orphanage — the dormitory smell of lye and damp wool, the matron's bell, the taste of porridge — and find the palace threaded through it, white colonnades growing out of the wash-house wall. He would reach for his mother's song and find the matron's hymn wearing his mother's melody like a stolen coat. Two countries occupying the same small skull, and neither one willing to grant the other asylum.

That was the loneliness of it. Not the walking, not the hunger, not even the fear. The loneliness was that he was the only border post between two worlds, and no traveler crossing him ever showed papers.

*Do you know what it is,* his mother had said once, in the memory that was also somehow a dream, *to be the last person who remembers a song? Every time you sing it, you are the whole country. And every time you forget a line, a little of the country dies with the line.* She had smiled at him then, sad and bright as sea-glass. *So remember well, little heir. Remember everything.*

*I'm trying,* Oscar told the dark. *I'm trying, Mama. But I'm eight.*

The fire had burned down to embers. Beyond the shuttered window, Verrane murmured its night-noises — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the far-off clang of the harbor bell counting the watch. An ordinary city. An ordinary night. Except that somewhere in it, a man who sold outlawed charms was sleeping with his boots within reach of his hand, and somewhere under the rain-soaked roads a voice was ringing its one bell, and somewhere — Oscar could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your teeth — something vast was stirring in all that salt, turning over in its long sleep, beginning to remember that it had a name.

He must have drifted, because the next thing he knew the room was darker and colder, and Isandro was crouched beside the pallet, shaking his shoulder, and the man's face in the ember-light was the face of someone reading bad news by lightning.

"Get up," Isandro whispered. "Quietly. There's someone asking after you at the harbormaster's. Asking about a child who speaks unlicensed sound."

Oscar's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the earth, all the way down to the salt.

"Unlicensed sound," he repeated. "My mother's song."

"They have a warrant for it now." Isandro was already moving, pulling a canvas bag from under the floor, his hands quick and sure. "So we're going to settle, tonight, whether that voice in the rain knew what it was talking about. Because either you lead me to a door, Oscar—"

He paused at the window, listening, and outside, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving with purpose.

"—or we're both about to find out what the Wardens do to the last of something."

Oscar stood, clutching his knotted cord, and for the first time since the white palace folded itself away inside him, he was not afraid of his own memories.

He was afraid for the man who was about to need them.
