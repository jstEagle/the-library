## 6. Salt on the Threshold

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a knock.

It was a boot heel against the doorframe — three strikes, measured, official, the rhythm of men who had never once been told *no* by a door in their lives. Isandro was already awake. He had been awake since the fourth bell, sitting cross-legged on the floor of his back room with Oscar's head heavy against his shoulder, listening to the fog press against the shutters like something trying to get in.

He'd known this morning would come. You always know. In Verrane, trouble arrives the way the tide does: you can mark its hour days in advance, and when it comes there is nothing to do but stand where you are and let it reach you.

"Stay behind me," he whispered to the boy.

Oscar blinked up at him, sleep still crusted in the corners of his eyes, and said nothing — which was itself strange, because the boy had not stopped talking since he arrived, a low river of Ilvaren spilling out of him day and night, into his soup, into his sleep, into the cracks of the floorboards where Isandro could almost hear it pooling. The silence now was worse than any word. It meant the boy understood too.

The second round of strikes came harder. "Hartwell." A voice through the wood, flat as slate. "Open in the name of the Wardens of Verrane."

Isandro rose without a sound. Ten years of selling outlawed charms to desperate people had taught him that the first rule of a raid is that the Warden who knocks politely is the one who has already decided to burn your stock. The polite ones want paperwork. The impolite ones want an excuse. This one wanted neither. He wanted a boy.

He crossed the shop floor past the shelves of his quiet inventory — luck-knots and fever-salves and the little brass bells he sold to sailors who didn't know they were buying hollow metal — and put his palm flat against the door.

Through the wood he felt them: four heartbeats, maybe five, arranged in the half-circle of men about to breach. Salt-armor creaked. A lantern hissed behind fogged glass.

"I'm licensed," Isandro called, and hated how thin his voice sounded. "Charmwright, third tier, ward-stamps current. Whatever you're—"

"We're not here for your stamps." The voice did not rise. That was the frightening part. "We're here for unlicensed sound. Open the door, Hartwell, or we open it for you, and the difference will matter to what's left of your shop."

*Unlicensed sound.* He turned the phrase over as he backed away from the door, and understood, with a cold settling in his stomach, that someone had heard Oscar. Someone in the market, or the widow with her luck-charm, or the neighbor who watched everything from behind her salt-fogged window — someone had reported a child speaking a language that made ink crawl and lamps gutter and old women weep into their washing. The Wardens had a word for it now. They'd had three days to make it bureaucratic.

He looked at the boy. Oscar stood in the doorway of the back room, small hands curled at his sides, mud-brown hair sticking up where he'd slept against the wall. His eyes were very calm. Children who have walked alone across a country that does not exist have eyes like that. Isandro had seen it before, in soldiers, in the dying. Calm as a held breath.

"There's a way out," Isandro said quietly. "I think. I'm not sure. I need you to do what you did last night."

Last night. He had not told anyone about last night, because last night made no sense. Oscar had woken screaming in a language Isandro should not have understood and did — words rising out of him like floodwater, and in the middle of the room, where the boy was pointing, the air had *opened*. Not a portal, not the theatrical tearing that stage-mages sold to drunk merchants. Just a road. A dirt road, hedged with white stone posts, running through his shop wall as if the wall had always been a lie, lit by a light like late afternoon in a country Isandro had helped burn. It had lasted four heartbeats. Then it closed, and the room smelled of cut grass and brine, and Oscar had looked at him with those calm eyes and said, in trade-tongue, his accent terrible:

"That's the road home. It's still there. It just needs saying."

Now the door shuddered under the first blow of a ram, and Isandro grabbed the boy's wrist and ran.

* * *

The back of the shop was a storeroom, and the storeroom was a dead end, and Isandro had always known that one day it would be the place they found his body. There was a window too narrow for a grown man, a drain that went nowhere useful, and a wall. Behind the wall, according to every map ever drawn of Verrane, lay the tannery yard and then the salt-flats and then nothing worth reaching.

Behind the wall, according to a boy who spoke the dead tongue, lay a road.

The ram hit the front door again. Wood cracked like a knuckle.

Isandro pressed his back to the storeroom wall and pulled Oscar in front of him, hands on the boy's shoulders, and felt how thin he was, all bone and stubbornness. "Listen to me. I don't know if this works twice. I don't know what happens if it opens halfway. But those men through that door will take you somewhere you don't come back from, so we're going to try, and you're going to keep talking no matter what you see. Can you do that?"

Oscar nodded. Then, with the gravity of a child correcting a fool: "It's not talking. Talking is for people. This is *calling*."

"Fine. Call."

The front door gave. He heard it — the splinter-crash, boots, the wet slap of salt-armor on flagstones — and Oscar opened his mouth, and the room changed.

The boy's voice came out in two registers at once, the piping of a child and underneath it something older, layered, choral, as though the words had been sung by many throats long dead and were only passing through this one on their way back up. Isandro knew the language. That was the wound of it, the thing he had never confessed to anyone: he knew it because he had burned it. Three years of campaign in a country whose name the maps refused, and the army's priests had taught the mercenaries just enough Ilvaren to read border-stones and shout orders over screaming. He had spent ten years pretending the knowing had washed out of him. It hadn't. It lived in him like shrapnel.

And now it answered.

The wall did not open. It *remembered otherwise*. One moment it was plaster and brick and damp; the next it was a hedge-row, white stone posts marching away into a golden afternoon, a dirt road worn soft by cart-wheels, and the smell — gods, the smell — cut grass and warm dust and the sea far off, a whole country's ordinary evening pouring through a hole in a tannery-side storeroom in Verrane. Through the opening Isandro could see, absurdly, a farmer's fence. A crow on the fence post, regarding them with one salt-white eye.

"Go," Isandro said.

Boots in the shop behind them. A Warden's voice, sharp: "Back room! They're in the back—"

Oscar went through the wall like water going downhill. Isandro followed, shoulder-first, and the world folded around him — one stride on storeroom flags, the next on warm dirt — and behind him he heard the crash of a Warden hitting empty air where a wall used to be, and the furious, baffled shouting of men standing in a room that now contained a hedge, a fence, and a crow, and no exit at all.

The road closed behind them like a mouth.

* * *

They ran anyway. You run even when the door is shut, because running is the body's way of believing you escaped.

The road took them through country that should not exist — and yet did, now, the way a bruise exists, blooming under the skin of the world wherever Oscar's words touched it. White stone posts every hundred paces. Fields gone to seed for ten years and then, impossibly, not: wheat standing in ranks, ripe, patient, waiting for harvesters who were ash. An orchard heavy with fruit no one had picked. A well with a rope still coiled on its lip.

And everywhere, faintly, the humming.

That was the part Isandro had noticed in the villages they'd passed since leaving the city proper, and it was happening again here, at the edges of the fields: people — real people, Verrani farmers, salt-gatherers, a carter with his mule — stopping what they were doing and humming songs they had never learned. He watched a woman set down her basket at the sight of them and stand with her lips moving around a melody, tears sliding down her face, her expression the purest confusion he had ever seen on a human being. She did not know why she was crying. She did not know the song. Her mouth knew both.

"They remember," Oscar said, trotting at Isandro's side, not even winded. "Not with their heads. With the rest of them." He said it the way other children report weather.

"How far does this go?" Isandro asked. "The road. Where does it lead?"

"Home." Oscar said it simply, and then, hearing the weight Isandro put on the question, added, slower: "I don't know where home is anymore. The palace is stones now. But the road knows. Roads remember longer than houses do. My mother said—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "My mother said roads are prayers that got tired of waiting in one place."

Isandro said nothing. He kept walking, one hand near the knife at his belt out of habit, scanning the hedgerows for pursuit, and let the boy's sentence settle into him like a stone into a pond. *Roads are prayers.* His god had been silent for ten years and he had told himself silence was death, absence, abandonment. It had not once occurred to him that silence might be imprisonment. That the prayer might be in there somewhere, tired, waiting.

They walked until the light went long and gold, the kind of gold that existed nowhere in Verrane, where the sun came down filtered through salt-fog like light through dirty wool. Here the air was clean enough to hurt. Isandro's legs ached. His lungs ached. Somewhere behind them, distantly, horns — the Wardens must have gotten word out, must be circling along the flats toward whatever gap their maps showed in the world, which was no gap at all, which was the joke of it: you cannot chase a man down a road that is not on your map.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen border-stone, one of the great carved pillars Isandro remembered from the war — remembered breaking. He ran his thumb along the fracture line and found, to his horror, that the break had begun to heal. Not mend — heal. New stone filling the crack like new flesh, soft, pale, veined with salt-crystal. The country was knitting itself back together around the boy's voice, stitch by stitch, and Isandro sat with his hand on the wound he had made and could not decide if he was witnessing a miracle or a hemorrhage.

He built a small fire, shielded, smokeless. Shared out bread and hard cheese and the last of the dried plums. Oscar ate like a soldier, fast and thorough, and then sat hugging his knees, watching the flames.

"You knew the words," the boy said suddenly. "In the storeroom. When I called the road. Your mouth moved."

Isandro's hand stilled on the fire-stick. "I don't speak Ilvaren."

"You do." No accusation in it. Just fact, the way children deliver facts, like small stones placed carefully on a table. "Badly. Like someone who learned it from soldiers." He looked up. "Were you a soldier?"

The fire popped. Far off, the horns again, or maybe wind. Isandro weighed the truth against the lie and found, to his surprise, that the lie wouldn't fit in his mouth — not tonight, not with the boy's calm eyes on him, not after the boy had torn a hole in reality to save them both.

"Yes," he said. "A long time ago. In a war I didn't understand, for pay I spent too fast, against a country I was told wasn't real." He fed a stick to the flames. "I'm not going to tell you the whole of it tonight. Some of it I may never tell you. But I'll tell you this much and mean it: I didn't know what we were burning. And I've paid for not knowing every day since, in a coin you can't see."

Oscar considered this with the terrifying seriousness of the young. Then he scooted closer, close enough that Isandro could feel the warmth of him, and said, "My mother said that too. About paying. She said everything true costs something, and the trick isn't finding what's free, it's choosing what's worth the price." He yawned hugely, a child's yawn, sudden and total. "Was I worth the price? Of hiding me?"

Isandro looked at him — mud-caked, exhausted, eight years old at most, carrying a nation in his mouth like a bird carries a song — and felt something shift in his chest that he had thought was scar tissue and was apparently not.

"Yes," he said. "Ask me again tomorrow and I'll say it faster."

The boy smiled, laid his head against Isandro's arm, and within minutes was asleep, breathing slow, one fist still curled around a handful of Isandro's sleeve.

Isandro sat awake a long while. The stars over this remembered country were wrong — brighter, denser, constellations he half-knew from a decade ago and had mourned as lost. The border-stone beside them hummed faintly, healing. And once, deep in the night, the boy talked in his sleep — a single sentence of Ilvaren, clear as a bell, and Isandro understood it without wanting to, the way you understand a blow:

*The sworn one will carry me to the door, and the door will ask what he loves most.*

Isandro stared into the fire until it burned down to salt-white ash, and did not sleep again.
