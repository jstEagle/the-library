## 8. Serah's Bargain

The Grand Auditor's office smelled of nothing at all.

That was the first thing Serah Voss had noticed, ten years ago, when she'd been brought before him as a newly sworn Warden-Captain — that a man who held the silence of a god in his keeping should have an office with no smell whatsoever. No ink, no lamp oil, no salt, though Verrane's air was never free of salt. No sweat, though the man worked eighteen hours a day behind a door of black ironwood. It was as if the room itself had been included in some vast act of forgetting, scrubbed down to bare geometry: desk, chair, ledgers, window, and the Auditor seated behind all of it like a signature at the bottom of a contract.

Now, standing in that same smell-less room, Serah watched him turn a page and understood, for the first time in her career, that she was afraid of him.

"The boy speaks Ilvaren," the Auditor said. He did not look up. His voice was dry paper, dry as the ledgers he kept, and it carried no accusation, which was somehow worse than if it had. "You were present at the Silencing, Captain. You know what one voice in that tongue can do."

"I know what the Wardens are for," Serah said. "I wrote half the protocols myself."

"You did." He turned another page. "Which is why it grieves me to tell you that your former commander has been selling unlicensed charms in the Salfmarket for the better part of a decade, and that three days ago a child arrived at his door speaking a language that does not exist."

The word *grieves* passed through the room without disturbing anything, the way a stone passes through water without leaving a ripple. Serah kept her face still. Ten years of command had taught her that much, at least: stillness first, questions later, and never let a superior see which question you wanted most to ask.

"Isandro Hartwell," she said. "He was released from the Order eleven years ago. His vows lapsed when—"

"When his god went quiet. Yes." The Auditor finally looked up, and his eyes were the color of old ledger paper, cream gone faintly brown at the edges. "The Order of the Quiet Bell traded its divinity for amnesty, Captain. You know this. You stood in the salt and you watched it happen. So you will understand that Hartwell's vows did not lapse. They were *collected*. And what is collected can be called due again — by us, or by whatever else remembers the debt."

Serah's hands were cold. She folded them behind her back, parade rest, the posture that meant *I am listening and I am not arguing yet.*

"What do you want from me?"

"Find him. Find the boy. Bring them both to me alive and unspoiled." The Auditor closed his ledger with a sound like a door shutting on a small room. "And Captain — do not speak to them. Do not listen to the boy. If he says a single word in your presence, hum until it stops. That is not a metaphor. Hum. The forgetting holds better against song."

She left the office with her spine straight and her thoughts in ruins.

* * *

Her quarters in the Wardenhouse had a view of the salt-flats, which everyone else considered a punishment posting and Serah had requested twice. At dusk the flats went the color of a dead man's lips, grey-pink, and the tide came in so slowly you could watch individual crystals catch the light and let it go. She stood at the window with her boots off and her collar open and tried to remember exactly what she had seen, ten years ago, on the night the gods went quiet.

That was the trouble with the Silencing. Everyone who'd been there remembered it differently, and everyone's version was true, because the erasure had gotten into memory itself the way damp gets into plaster. Her fellow officers recalled a pillar of white fire. A bell with no clapper, rung anyway. A long exhalation across the flats. Serah remembered salt.

They had been sent to the border-stones — the last of them, the ones Ilvaren's kings had raised when the country still had borders, back when maps admitted the country existed. The Order had marched out under banners they weren't permitted to look at directly, and the mercenaries had come with them, hired men with ash-grey cloaks and faces Serah had never been able to recall since, though she'd fed two of them from her own mess kit. Isandro Hartwell had been one of those faces. She was almost sure. There had been a young mercenary who'd argued theology with her over hardtack, quick and bitter and funny, who'd said *my god answers me, Captain, does yours?* — and she'd hated him for it and liked him for it in the same breath.

At the stones, the Archivist of the Order — a thin man whose name the records now gave as blank — had read from a book that made no sound even as its pages turned. And the salt had risen.

Not blown. Not thrown. *Risen*, the way a congregation rises, wave after wave of it, standing up off the flats in columns that caught the moonlight and held it wrong, and Serah had looked into those columns and seen —

She had never told anyone what she saw. Not the tribunal, not her confessors, not the Auditor himself when he'd asked, gently, whether she'd experienced any residual impressions. Because what she'd seen was people. Thousands of them, standing in the salt, mouths open, and they hadn't been screaming. They'd been singing. Hymns, work songs, a mother's nonsense tune for putting a child to bed — she could hear none of the words, only the shape of them, the rise and fall of a whole nation making music — and then the Archivist's silent book had turned its final page and every mouth in the salt had closed at once, and the silence that fell had been so total that Serah's own heartbeat sounded like blasphemy.

Afterward, the Order's god had stopped answering. All the gods had, everywhere, all at once, except the ones too small and mean to have ever mattered much. The theologians called it the Quietude and built doctrines around it. The Order called it sacrifice and took amnesty for services rendered. And Serah had taken her captaincy and told herself, for ten years, that whatever rose out of the salt that night had been a trick of exhaustion and moonlight, because the alternative was that she had helped bury a country alive while it sang.

She pressed her forehead against the cold glass.

*Complicity*, the seminarians called it, when they taught the doctrine of institutional sin. The sin you commit by standing in formation. By holding the line. By writing the protocols. She had spent a decade believing the Silencing had been mercy — that Ilvaren had been dying, plague-wracked, its people begging for oblivion, and the Order had granted a clean death instead of a long rot. That story had held her up like a keel. It had let her sleep.

Three days ago, a child had knocked on Isandro Hartwell's door speaking the language of the buried, and the keel had begun, very quietly, to crack.

* * *

She found him the way she'd always known she would: not through the Wardens' channels, which were thorough and slow, but through the old network of the Order's discharged — the sworn ones whose god had gone quiet and who drifted, afterward, into trades that skirted the law. A fence in the Tallow Quarter owed her. A disgraced liturgist owed her more. Between them she assembled Isandro Hartwell's decade in an afternoon: the charm-stall in the Salfmarket under a false license, the room above it where he slept alone, the widow he'd sold a luck-charm to at cost because grief made him stupid-generous, the debts he paid on time because he was, at bottom, an honest man practicing dishonesty badly.

He had not changed. That was the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone as she read the reports. Ten years, and the descriptions all agreed: tall, dark, quick-tongued, eyes that flinched from direct blessing. Still wearing the Order's old discipline like a coat that no longer fit. Still praying, the fence said — she'd laughed at that, then stopped laughing. Every morning, the fence swore, Hartwell stood facing east and moved his lips at nothing, and nothing answered, and he did it anyway.

*My god answers me, Captain, does yours?*

Hers had answered once, the night of the Silencing. In the moment before the last page turned, Serah had felt her god's attention swing toward her like a lamp — one instant of being utterly known, utterly held — and then the salt had closed its thousand mouths and the lamp had gone out mid-swing, and she had spent ten years telling herself the attention had been her imagination.

But she knew, now, watching the salt-flats go dark, why the Auditor had chosen her for this. Not because she was the best tracker in the Wardens, though she was. Because Isandro Hartwell would open his door to her. Because the boy's words might unmake a stranger, but an old comrade — a face from before the silence — might slip past whatever defenses a fugitive raises. She was not being sent as a hunter. She was being sent as a memory.

And the worst of it, the part that made her pour the second cup of wine she didn't want, was that it would work. She knew Isandro. He would see her at his door and feel relief before suspicion. He would let her in. He would make her tea with the good leaves he saved, because he'd always been generous stupid, and he'd ask about the Order, and somewhere in the answering she would have to decide whether she was there to save him or deliver him.

Unless she decided neither.

The thought arrived without permission, the way dangerous thoughts do. *Neither.* There was a third door, and it was the one the doctrine didn't draw: she could find him and warn him. Turn her back on the Auditor's commission and spend the rest of her life running from the institution that had made her, on behalf of a man she'd half-forgiven and a child she'd never met, for a crime she'd committed in formation with her hands at her sides.

Treason. Clean, simple, treason. She turned the word over the way she'd once turned over captured blades, checking the weight of it, the balance.

No. Not yet. She was loyal — that was the rotten heart of it, she was *still loyal*, loyal the way a dog is loyal to a master who beats it, loyal because loyalty was the only shape her life had and she couldn't imagine her hands doing anything but holding the line. But she would find Isandro herself rather than send a squad. She would see the boy with her own eyes before anyone laid hands on him. And if the Auditor's story held — if the child was a threat, a wound in the world, a thing that needed containing — then she would do her duty and hate herself cleanly, the way she'd hated herself for ten years, efficiently, in the hours before dawn.

And if the story didn't hold—

If the story didn't hold, then everything she'd stood in formation for was theft dressed as mercy, and the salt she'd watched rise had been a congregation, and the god who'd swung his attention toward her in that last lit second had been looking at her not with love but with *accusation*, asking her to remember, and she had spent a decade refusing.

She finished the wine. She buckled her sword-belt. Outside, the tide was coming in over the flats, patient as a debt, and far to the east — beyond the salt, beyond the places maps consented to describe — she imagined she could hear, very faintly, a child's voice saying words that had no right to exist.

*Hum*, the Auditor had said, when you hear it. *The forgetting holds better against song.*

Serah Voss stood in the dark of her quarters and, for the first time since the night the gods went quiet, deliberately did not hum.

Then she went down to the stables, saddled the grey mare, and rode out alone along the coast road toward Verrane — toward a charm-shop with a light burning in its upper window, where a man who prayed to nothing every morning was about to answer his door and find ten years of shared history standing on his step with a warrant folded in her pocket and, beneath the warrant, a question she was terrified to ask:

*What did we actually do, Isandro? What did I help bury?*

Behind her, in the black ironwood office she had just left, the Grand Auditor opened a fresh ledger, dipped his pen, and wrote a single line in a hand like frost forming: *Captain Voss dispatched. Contingency authorized.*
