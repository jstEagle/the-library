## 10. Where the God Went

The effigies did not walk so much as *arrive*, one stride at a time, as if the ground beneath them remembered being Ilvaren and consented to hold their weight.

Oscar kept pace behind the tallest of them — a woman, once, though salt had smoothed her face to the suggestion of one, the way waves smooth a bottle's message. She had been singing when they found him at dawn. Not with a mouth. With the whole of her, a low brine-hum that came up through the earth and into his teeth, and he had understood every word of it, the way you understand your own heartbeat.

*Follow, little heir. The chapel still keeps its hours.*

He glanced back. Isandro was three paces behind him, which Oscar had learned was as far as Isandro ever let himself be from a child in his charge, and his face wore the expression Oscar privately called *the ledger* — the one where all the numbers came out wrong and he refused to say so.

"They're leading us into the water," Isandro said. It wasn't quite a complaint. He'd stopped complaining somewhere past the third village of weeping strangers; now he just stated things, flat as slate, and watched.

"No," Oscar said. "They're leading us into what used to be."

The road had been climbing for two days, away from the coast and then curving back toward it, following a spine of black rock that no map had ever bothered to name because maps had never bothered to exist here. The land grew stranger as they went. Grass came up in rows, like it had been planted by someone patient. A stone wall ran alongside them for a mile and then simply stopped mid-field, its gate hanging open on nothing, and through the gate Oscar could see — not with his eyes, exactly — orchards heavy with white fruit, terraces cut into hillsides, a road going down to a harbor full of ships whose names were prayers.

Then he blinked, and it was only salt marsh and gulls and the gray sea eating the horizon.

"You see it too," Isandro said quietly. "Don't you."

"The country," Oscar said. "It's not coming back all at once. It's leaking." He thought about how to say the next part, the part that sat in his chest like swallowed ice. "And I think I'm the crack it's leaking through."

Isandro didn't answer that. He did something worse, which was put his hand briefly on Oscar's head as they walked, heavy and warm, and leave it there for three steps before taking it away. Oscar had decided a long time ago — back in the shop, back before the raid, back before everything — that he would not cry about kindness. It was a rule. He broke it sometimes anyway, but never where Isandro could see.

By late afternoon the black rock spine ended at a cliff, and below the cliff the sea had gotten inside the land, filling a valley like water poured into a cup, and standing up out of the middle of the drowned valley was a tower.

No. Not a tower. A bell tower, its topmost arches above the waterline, its bell long gone — Oscar knew the bell was long gone the way he knew his own hands — and beneath the surface, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, the pale suggestion of walls. A chapel. White stone. The same white stone as the palace in his other memories, the ones that weren't really his.

The effigies gathered at the cliff edge and went still, and their stillness was worse than anything they'd done yet, because it was the stillness of people waiting outside a door while something important happens within.

"It wants you alone," Isandro said.

"I know."

"I don't like it."

"I know," Oscar said again, and then, because Isandro had taught him that fear spoken aloud weighs less: "I'm scared. But it's been down there a long time, and nobody ever even knocked."

Isandro looked at him for a moment with an expression Oscar couldn't read — grief and pride fighting over the same territory — and then he began, without another word, to unlace his boots.

"What are you doing?"

"Coming with you," Isandro said. "Partway. To the threshold. Then I'll wait where the water ends and you go the last bit yourself, if it truly wants you alone." He flexed his fingers, and Oscar saw the old charm-light flicker there, weak as a dying candle, and saw Isandro pretend not to notice. "I've spent ten years thinking my god abandoned me. Turns out maybe it was locked up. If there's even a chance I get to hear its voice again—" His jaw tightened. "I need to stand at that door, Oscar. Even if it never opens for me."

* * *

The water was cold in the way of deep places, cold that didn't care about seasons or sunlight. They waded out along a causeway that revealed itself underfoot only when Oscar stepped — stone surfacing ahead of each stride like a thing being remembered into existence, sinking again behind them. Behind, on the cliff, the effigies had knelt. All of them. Facing the chapel. Their salt heads bowed, and the wind made a sound through them like a congregation holding its breath.

At the doorway — a great arch half-drowned, its lintel carved with words worn to shadows — the water reached Isandro's chest and Oscar's chin, and Isandro stopped.

"This is where my feet stop listening to me," he said, and there was something raw in his voice that frightened Oscar more than the dark water. "It won't let me closer. Ten years of silence and it still gets a vote." He laughed, and it wasn't a funny laugh. "Go on. I'll be right here. Right here, do you hear me? However long it takes."

Oscar nodded. His throat had closed up. He turned to the archway, filled his lungs with sea-smelling air, and ducked beneath the water.

* * *

Down was not what he expected. Down was air.

An air-pocket held itself inside the chapel like a secret cupped in two hands — a dome of breathing space beneath the flooded vaults, lit by light that fell through the water above in slow green ropes. The chapel floor was dry. The pews were dry. Dust lay on them undisturbed, and dust does not lie, and the dust said: *no one has entered here since the day the world forgot.*

Except that at the far end, where an altar should have stood, the floor was wet. One thin line of seawater crept across the stones, and it moved against every law Oscar had ever half-learned from Isandro's grumbled lessons — uphill, unhurried, patient as arithmetic — and where it touched the altar it stopped and pooled into a shape.

A shape like a kneeling person. Faceless. Made of a hand's-breadth of water and all the weight of the sea.

Oscar's legs nearly failed him. He gripped a pew. The wood was real under his palms — real oak, real grain, real and *old*, older than Verrane, older than anything — and that reality steadied him the way the charm-man's rough voice always did.

"H-hello?" His voice cracked in the middle. Eight-year-olds' voices did that. "I came because— the others said—"

The pool shivered.

"*Little heir.*"

The voice did not come through the ears. It arrived the way the memory-songs arrived, whole and everywhere at once, in the marrow, in the back of the teeth, in the space behind the eyes where his other life lived. It was vast — that was the first true thing he felt about it, vaster than the sea pressing overhead, vaster than the sky — and it was *wounded*, and the wound was the second true thing, and the two truths together made a sound in him like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

"You're the god," Oscar whispered. "You're the god Isandro used to serve."

"*I am what is left of the serving,*" the god said, and its vastness bent around something that might have been sorrow, the way light bends around glass. "*I am the bell with no bell-tower. I heard ten thousand prayers a day, and then the forgetting came, and my faithful were unwritten between one breath and the next, and I have been drowning in this pocket of quiet ever since, saying mass to the dust.*"

Oscar took one step, then another, down the aisle. The god did not feel dangerous. It felt *lonely*, and loneliness in something that large was the most terrifying loneliness imaginable, like a mountain grieving.

"Why me? Why did the voice send me to Isandro?"

"*Because the door out of a country exists only where someone remembers it whole, little heir. And the sworn one — the debtor — carried the last key anyone trusted.*" The water-shape rose, stretched, became taller than a man, a figure of thin bright water wearing the suggestion of robes and a suggestion of a crown and no face at all, and Oscar should have run and instead found himself walking closer, drawn like iron filings. "*You are the heir. The last vessel of the whole memory. Every hour you speak, my country bleeds home. And every hour it bleeds home, the debt comes due.*"

"What debt?"

"*His.*" The god's faceless head turned — toward the doorway, toward the man waiting chest-deep in the cold beyond the arch, who could not hear any of this. "*Isandro Hartwell burned my border-stones. He was paid in silver and sworn in my name to do it, and when the erasure came, my order bargained away my voice to purchase their amnesty. He has carried the guilt like a second spine for ten years. Guilt is a currency, little heir. It has always been a currency. And debts of that size can buy very large doors.*"

Oscar's stomach turned over. He thought of Isandro's hand on his head. Three steps. Only ever three steps of warmth, rationed out like the last of the bread.

"He didn't tell me."

"*Men rarely confess to children. They save it for firelight and cornered moments.*" Something moved through the god's voice that was almost gentle. "*Do not hate him for it. Hate is heavy, and you will need both hands free for what comes.*"

"Can I free you?" Oscar asked. "From the erasure. Can I let you out?"

The god went still, and its stillness filled the whole chapel the way the sea fills a hull.

"*Yes,*" it said at last. "*But hear the price before you ask twice. I am not dead, and I am not free, and the one who caged me — the Auditor, the thief in the archivist's coat — feeds on what leaks through you. Every wonder you restore, he swallows a portion. When the country returns whole, he will come to take it whole, and he is strong now on stolen divinity, stronger than your salt-dead faithful, stronger than the sworn one's failing charms. And when the final door opens, the magic will demand payment. Names. Memories. Selves. Everything must be paid for, little heir. That is the oldest law, older than me.*"

The green light through the water dimmed, or seemed to. Oscar hugged himself. Somewhere above, muffled by fathoms, he imagined he could hear Isandro pacing, refusing to sit, watching the doorway.

"And Isandro," Oscar said. "If I open the door — what happens to him?"

The god was silent for a long time. Water dripped somewhere in the dark vaults, counting seconds.

"*Ask him,*" it finally said. "*Or don't, and let him keep his secret until the firelight forces it out. Either way, look at him closely while he is still entirely himself. There may not be much longer to memorize.*" The water-figure sank, pooling again across the altar stones, and its voice thinned to a whisper fraying at the edges — a vast thing running out of strength, hoarding its syllables. "*One more thing, heir. Above you, on the road, the Wardens have found the kneeling faithful. The Auditor's hounds smell the leak in the world, and they are coming down the cliff path with irons sized for a child. Wake your debtor. Run for the high country. And when he tells you the truth of the border-stones — and he will, soon, because guilt is a currency and he is nearly bankrupt — remember that I told you first, so that when your trust breaks, you will know it can be mended. That is what heirs are for.*"

The pool spread thin, thinned further, became only water.

Oscar stood alone in the dry chapel under the drowned sky, nine years old and suddenly carrying four lifetimes, and the bell that had started ringing in his chest at the god's first word finally finished tolling — one long note that meant: *nothing will be simple again.*

He turned and ran for the doorway, for the cold, for the man waiting in the water with his boots off and his heart in his throat, and as Oscar surfaced gasping into the grey evening, Isandro seized his shoulders and searched his face and asked nothing, because Isandro always asked nothing, and that was the moment Oscar understood with terrible clarity that the asking was its own kind of debt.

Far above, on the cliff path, torchlight was already descending.
