## 11. Confessions at the Fireline

*Isandro*

They had been walking the remembered road for nine days when the world began to burn behind them.

Not truly burning — nothing in Ilvaren's half-remembered country burned the way Verrane things burned. What Isandro saw in the distance, past the last of the salt hills, was a line of white light crawling along the horizon like a fuse, and where it passed, the road beneath their feet went grey and forgot itself. He could feel it through his boots. A numbness. The particular deadness of ground that had never been walked on by anyone.

"They're unmaking it as they come," he said. "The Auditor's Wardens. They carry the erasure with them like a lantern."

Oscar stopped in the middle of the road and turned to look back. The boy had grown thinner these nine days, all elbows and hollow eyes under a hood too big for him, but he stood now the way he'd stood the first night on Isandro's doorstep — planted, immovable, a small king surveying a border.

"How far?" Oscar asked.

"Half a day. Less if they've horses." Isandro squinted. The light-line pulsed, steady as a heartbeat. "We need high ground before dark. There's an outcrop —" he searched his memory and found, absurdly, that he *had* a memory of it, a crag of white stone overlooking a valley that history insisted did not exist — "there. We can make a stand if we have to."

"A stand." Oscar looked up at him. "With what?"

Isandro patted the satchel at his hip. Charms rattled inside like teeth. Half of them were duds now; whatever current had once run from his silent god down into his hands had thinned to a trickle, and he'd been rationing it the way a dying man rations breath. But there were three fireline charms left, the old military kind, the kind that burned salt instead of wood and gave off a light no Warden wanted to look at.

"Enough," he said, which was a lie, and both of them knew it.

The outcrop rose out of the valley like a knuckle of bone. By the time they climbed it, the sky had gone the color of pewter and the first wind of evening was coming up out of the south, carrying the smell of the sea even here, a hundred miles inland, because Ilvaren's whole remembered body was made of brine. Below them the forgotten valley lay in the last light: terraced fields ghosting into existence as the boy's presence strengthened, stone walls rebuilding themselves out of mist, an orchard of trees that were more suggestion than wood. Somewhere down there, Isandro knew, there would be a village. There always was, along this road. Villages that woke for an hour and slept again, humming songs they'd never learned.

He built the fireline while Oscar watched. Three charms, set in a triangle around their camp, each one keyed to his blood. When he pricked his thumb on the charm's edge and pressed it to the salt-white wick, the flame that leapt up was colorless and cold-looking, and it threw shadows the wrong way.

"Wardens hate this light," he told the boy. "It shows what they actually are."

"What are they?"

Isandro hesitated. "Men," he said finally. "Just men. That's the worst part."

Oscar sat close to the fire, knees drawn up, and for a long time neither of them spoke. This was the rhythm they'd fallen into over nine days of flight — long comfortable silences broken by small lessons. Isandro teaching him which mushrooms lied about being safe. Oscar teaching him, in the dead tongue that came out of the boy's mouth like water finding its level, the evening prayer of a country that officially had never existed. Isandro had begun saying it without thinking. That frightened him more than the Wardens did. It felt like something growing back on a stump.

Tonight, though, Oscar didn't pray. Tonight the boy looked into the colorless flame and said, quietly, in Verrani: "I heard them talking. In the village yesterday. Before the effigies came."

"The villagers don't usually talk—"

"Not the villagers." Oscar's voice was flat in a way Isandro had never heard from him. "The Wardens' scouts. Two of them, ahead of the main line. They were looking for us, and I was hiding in the well-house, and they didn't know I could hear." He paused. "They said your name. Not Isandro Hartwell. Your other name. Your order-name."

The cold that went through Isandro then had nothing to do with the evening wind.

"Brother Cael of the Quiet Bell," Oscar recited, each word placed like a stone on a grave. "Sworn to the Silent God. Decorated for service at the Border-Stone Action, southern approach, year —" and here the boy faltered, because the date was in the dead tongue, and when he said it in Ilvaren's language the number meant something different than any Verrani calendar allowed. "Year the stones fell," he finished. "What does that mean, Isandro? What is the Border-Stone Action?"

Isandro looked at the fire. The fire looked back, or seemed to, its colorless light showing him his own reflection in the boy's wide dark eyes, and he understood with terrible clarity that there was no version of tonight where he got to keep lying. Nine days of tenderness. Nine days of teaching and being taught, of shared bread and shared watch, of the boy falling asleep against his shoulder on the third night when the fever from the marsh crossing broke. All of it standing on top of a thing Isandro had sworn never to say aloud, and the ground beneath it was burning now, coming for them at half a day's march, and the truth was going to reach Oscar one way or another — either from Isandro's mouth or from a Warden's.

Better mine, he thought. Whatever else I owe him. Better it comes from me.

"There were seven border-stones," he said.

Oscar went very still.

"White stone, taller than a man, set in a ring around the whole country. You've seen them in your memories — you told me about them. You said your mother touched one when she was a girl and it sang to her." His own voice sounded far away to himself, like someone else confessing. "They weren't just markers. They were anchors. Each one held the country's *name* in place — its weight, its reality. As long as the ring stood, Ilvaren couldn't be erased, because the stones kept insisting it existed. Every map, every ledger, every mind — the stones argued with all of it, and won."

"And the Action," Oscar said. His hands had closed into fists in his lap. "The Border-Stone Action."

"We were hired," Isandro said. "Not by Verrane. By a coalition — the neighbors, the trade leagues, the creditors. Ilvaren owed debts, after the plague years. Enormous ones. And a country that exists can be sued, embargoed, invaded. But—" He made himself go on, made himself not look away from the boy's face. "But a country that never existed owes nothing. Can't be pursued. Its treasury becomes salvage. Its archives become spoils. Someone very clever realized that the cheapest war is the one you win by deleting the enemy. So they hired soldiers who served a god whose whole doctrine was silence. Quieting things. Unmaking noise. And the Quiet Bell took the contract, because the alternative was—" he laughed, a short ugly sound— "because they offered us absolution. Our god said: do this, and we will forgive everything. Every mercenary sin. A clean slate, bought with seven stones."

"You burned them." Oscar's voice cracked down the middle. "You burned my mother's stones."

"I sanded them." The word came out of Isandro like a tooth. "That was the horror of it. No fire. Fire leaves ash, ash can be read. We sanded them down over three nights with salt-grit and silence-hymns, wearing the names off them grain by grain while the stones screamed — and they did scream, Oscar, I have heard them screaming for ten years every time I try to sleep — and when the last inscription was gone, the country just... thinned. Like ink in rain. People forgot mid-sentence. Ships lost harbors they'd sailed to for centuries. Mothers forgot children who'd been born on the wrong side of the ring." He swallowed. "I did the southern stone. Number four. I remember every grain."

The fire popped. Down in the valley, one of the ghost-orchard trees shivered in the wind, half-there.

Oscar hadn't moved. He sat with his fists in his lap and his face gone white as the stone they'd climbed, and when he finally spoke, his voice was the liturgical tongue, low and shaking — words Isandro understood and wished to God he didn't. A curse. An old one. The kind pronounced over traitors at the border, in the days when there had been borders to betray.

Then, in Verrani, barely audible: "You knew. On the first night. When I knocked and spoke and you understood me — you *knew* why you understood. You knew what you'd done. And you let me trust you anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?" The word tore out of him. "Why take me in? Why teach me, why feed me, why — why any of it? Was I a debt? Was I penance? Did the god send you a bill and I'm how you pay it?"

And there it was, the question Isandro had been dreading for nine days because he still didn't entirely know his own answer. He thought about the first night — the mud-caked child, the dead tongue falling out of his mouth like a knife out of a wound, and the voice in Isandro's skull that had spoken for the first time in ten years, faint as a dying coal, saying only: *the heir walks. the debtor will know the door.* He thought about whether he'd taken the boy in out of guilt or love or fear or some animal instinct older than all of those, and the honest answer was that he didn't know, that maybe no one ever knows which of their motives are which until it's too late to matter.

"All of it," he said. "Every reason, including the bad ones. I took you in because my god ordered it. I stayed because I couldn't make myself leave. I taught you to survive because I want you to survive. And yes — yes — because somewhere in me, some cowardly calculating piece of me believed that saving the last heir might balance the scales for the stone." He held the boy's gaze. "It doesn't. I know that now. Nothing balances. Some debts you just carry."

Oscar stood up. He was so small against the sky, eight years old and shaking with a rage that had ten years of a murdered nation behind it, and for one moment Isandro was certain the boy was going to walk straight off the outcrop and into the dark, away from him, toward anything.

Instead Oscar said: "The stones screamed. And you kept sanding."

"We were promised absolution—"

"*Did you keep sanding?*"

"...Yes."

The silence afterward lasted a long time. Long enough for the first stars to come out over the valley, over the ghost-fields, over the orchard that might have belonged, in another life, to the boy's family. When Oscar finally sat back down, he did not sit close. He put the whole width of the fire between them, and he pulled his knees up, and he stared at Isandro across the colorless flames with an expression Isandro had seen before — on the faces of widows in the market, the moment they learned the truth about the luck-charm they'd just bought.

"You'll still get me to the door," Oscar said at last. It wasn't forgiveness. It was arithmetic. "Because you're the only one who knows the way, and I'm the only one who remembers what's at the end of it. That hasn't changed." He lay down with his back to Isandro, facing the valley, facing the distant line of forgetting-light. "But don't talk to me tonight. Don't pray near me. I can hear your prayers now, Isandro. I hear everything you say to that silent god of yours." A pause, and then, quieter, the cruelest thing the boy could have said:

"And it never answers you, does it? Not once in ten years." Another pause. "Maybe it already took its payment. Maybe what's left in you is just the change."

Isandro sat his watch alone. He fed the fireline charms his blood twice more, and each time the flame guttered weaker, and each time he thought about the boy's words and could not argue with them. Sometime past midnight he began, without deciding to, the evening prayer of Ilvaren — the one Oscar had taught him, the traitor's prayer, the debtor's prayer — and got four lines in before his throat closed.

That was when he saw the lights. Not the Wardens' white line on the horizon.

Lights below. Down in the forgotten valley. Dozens of them, bobbing, converging on the outcrop from every direction — candle-flames carried by figures that moved wrong, that glimmered like wet quartz in the starlight, that had been walking toward this hill all night from villages up and down the remembered road.

The salt-effigies. The hungry congregation. Coming in their hundreds, and this time, Isandro realized with a lurch, they were not coming for the boy.

They were kneeling as they came.

* * *

*Oscar*

Oscar had not slept. He had lain with his back to the man who unmade his country and listened to the wind and rehearsed, over and over, all the things he would say if he were brave enough or cruel enough to say them. None of the rehearsals helped. Anger needed somewhere to go, and there was nowhere for it to go in the dark on a hilltop with the Wardens half a day behind and the door to his whole future locked inside the head of the one person he most wanted to strike.

So when the lights appeared in the valley, part of him was almost grateful. Something else to feel besides this.

"Isandro." He said the name deliberately, testing it, wanting the man to hear that he was still using it. For now. "Wake up. There's something coming."

"I see it." Isandro was already up, charm-satchel open, a blade of greenish light cupped in his palm. His face was wrecked in that light — grey, hollowed, ten years older than it had been at supper. "Stay behind the fireline. If they cross it, I'll—"

"They won't hurt me." Oscar stood. He didn't decide to; his legs decided, the way they'd decided at the drowned chapel, the way his mouth decided when the dead tongue poured out of it. "They never hurt me. They *serve* me. That's the problem — you've seen what their serving does to the land. But this time—" He frowned. The figures were close enough now to count, and the counting made no sense. Hundreds. More than hundreds. They filled the valley terraces like frost filling a window, and every one of them carried a light, and every one of them was sinking to its knees as it climbed, pressing its quartz-white brow to the burnt earth of the slope.

And they were not facing Oscar.

He walked to the edge of the outcrop, past the guttering fireline, and looked down, and understood.

Every effigy on the hillside faced Isandro. Kneeling to Isandro. Holding their little flames up toward him the way villagers hold candles toward a relic — or the way, said a cold small voice in Oscar's memory, his mother's stories said the penitents used to kneel outside the border-stones after doing a great wrong, holding their shame up to the stone and waiting for it either to forgive them or to fall on them.

"They know," Oscar whispered.

Isandro had gone rigid. "Know what?"

"They can taste it. What you did. It's *in* you — the hymns you used, the salt-grit, the screaming. You're marked, aren't you? All these years you thought your god abandoned you, and it wasn't abandonment." The understanding rose in Oscar like water rising in a flooded cellar, and with it, horribly, something that was almost pity. "You're not forsaken. You're *held*. You're being kept, the way a criminal is kept — saved up. Because the debt isn't paid yet, and the stones—" Oscar's voice caught, because the next part arrived in the dead tongue and he let it stay there, let it come out in his mother's language, the language of the things Isandro had helped erase:

*"The stones remember their sanders."*

Below them, the nearest effigy raised its head. Its face was smooth as sea-worn glass, but where a mouth would be, salt crystals had arranged themselves into letters. Into words. And all across the hillside, hundreds of glass faces turned upward, hundreds of mouths opened, and the whole forgotten valley exhaled a single sentence in a voice like a beach at low tide:

**"Debtor. The seventh stone stands. Come and answer."**

Oscar turned. Isandro Hartwell — Brother Cael of the Quiet Bell, sander of stones, keeper of a child he had helped orphan — stood at the edge of the fireline with the greenish light dying in his hand, and on his face was an expression Oscar had never seen on any adult: the naked terror of a man who has just learned that the punishment he chose for himself is not the punishment he's going to get.

"Where is the seventh stone?" Oscar asked.

Isandro's mouth worked twice before sound came out. "North," he said. "At the drowned chapel. Where your god is kept." He looked north, into the dark, and then — worse, much worse — he looked at Oscar, and asked, in a voice stripped of everything but honesty:

"Are you going to let them take me?"

Behind them, far to the south, the white line of the Auditor's forgetting crested the last ridge and began, patiently, to descend into the valley.

Between them and it, the congregation waited, kneeling, holding up their thousand small flames — and slowly, as one, they parted, opening a road northward through their own bodies, an invitation, or an escort, or a verdict.

Oscar looked at the road. He looked at the man. He thought about his mother singing, and stones that sang back, and the sound of ten years of sanding.

"Walk," he said, "in front of me. Where I can see you. And Isandro—" the dead tongue again, deliberate now, chosen, "—when we reach the stone, you'll kneel where I tell you, and you'll answer what it asks. Everything it asks. Swear it."

"I swear it," Isandro said, "on the only thing I have left."

"Which is?"

"My name," he said. "For now."

They went down into the kneeling dark together — the heir and the debtor, the boy and the man who had burned his world, walking north through a corridor of salt and candlelight while the erasure came on behind them like dawn coming on wrong. And above them, unseen by either, the sky over the drowned chapel flickered once, twice, like a lamp remembering how to burn — and something imprisoned there felt the seventh stone stirring, and began, for the first time in ten years, to hope.
