## 1. The Taster's Ledger

The ledger opened with a sound like a sigh held too long.

Hazel Voss sat at the narrow table in her rooms above the palace sculleries — rooms she had earned, not been given; there was a difference, and in Vell the difference was everything — and dipped her pen in ink the color of strong tea. Around her, the building settled into its evening bones: copper cooling on hooks, a mouse negotiating the wainscot, the distant percussion of the banquet kitchen two floors below, where forty cooks were building a feast for three hundred guests who would never know how many times death had been intercepted between the larder and their lips.

She wrote the date first. She always wrote the date first. A taster who did not date her entries was a taster who could not prove, later, what she had survived and when — and proof was the only currency that mattered in her trade. Then, beneath it, in her small, exact hand:

*Court Banquet — Feast of the Amber Tide. Attendance: His Majesty Aldric III; the Dowager Queen; nine ambassadors of the Salt Concord; assorted nobility, quantity uncounted, quality worse.*

She allowed herself the last joke. No one would ever read this page but her, and a woman who could not joke to herself about the quality of nobility was a woman already halfway poisoned by her work.

*First course: chilled melon soup with elderflower.* She had taken one spoonful from the serving basin, held it on her tongue for the count of ten, and waited for her body to file its report. Her body was a reliable clerk. It had been filing reports for eleven years, ever since Master Corvane had found her in the famine queues outside Tarnholt — a girl of fourteen with hollow cheeks and an unnervingly steady pulse — and asked her, in the flat voice he used for everything, whether she was afraid of dying.

"Constantly," she had said.

"Good," he had said. "The fearless ones die first."

He had been right, in his way. Fearlessness was carelessness wearing a crown, and crowns were the most poisonous things in any palace. Hazel had survived because she was afraid of everything, catalogued everything, respected everything. The arsenic in the wall plaster of the east wing. The mercury in the gilding of the mirrors. The nightshade that someone — she had her suspicions, never proven, filed under *S* for *someday* — had distilled into the honey served at the Dowager's birthday. Each entry in her ledger was a small treaty signed between her body and the world: *you may hurt me this much, and no further.*

*Taste report:* she wrote now, *melon clean. Elderflower clean. Note: cook Bastien has begun using river salt again instead of sea salt. Cheaper. Detectable. Speak to him gently; his wife is ill and pride is the only thing he has left to season with.*

That note went into a second column, the one no royal steward had ever seen, because Hazel kept two ledgers in one book. The left-hand pages belonged to the Crown. The right-hand pages belonged to her — or rather, they belonged to the arithmetic of being Hazel Voss, which was a kingdom smaller than Vell and harder to defend.

In the right-hand margin she recorded the other economy: the favors owed, the debts carried, the quiet transactions that kept a food-taster fed in more than coin. *Bastien: one kindness owed (salt). Cook-mother Ilsa: two kindnesses owed (looked away during the pigeon incident). Guild of Impossible Things: —*

Her pen paused above the dash.

The Guild of Impossible Things did not appear on any register of trades in the city of Corvale. It had no sign, no street address that stayed put, no membership rolls that a magistrate could seize. What it had was a door — an unremarkable door in an unremarkable alley behind the chandlers' row, which opened, for those who knew the knock, onto an archive that was larger inside than out, staffed by archivists who traded in things that could not properly exist. Yesterday's rain, sold by the bottle. The shadow of a hanged man, folded like linen. Letters delivered to the dead, postage due.

Hazel had found it the way everyone found it: by accident, at the worst moment of her life, when she had needed something impossible and had walked until her feet gave out and there had been a door where no door should be.

What she traded there was simple. The Guild dealt in memory as much as matter — memories were inventory, after all, the raw stock of every impossible thing — and a poison-taster's memory was a vault. Every toxin she had survived lived in her body *and* in her recollection with a precision that made archivists go quiet with want. In exchange, they gave her what she could not buy anywhere else: antidotes ahead of their invention. Warnings written before the events they warned against. And once — only once, and she had paid more than she liked to remember — a vial of her mother's voice, recovered from somewhere the Guild refused to name, singing half a lullaby before the recording frayed into silence like a thread pulled through cloth.

She still had the vial. She had never played it twice. Some inventory is too heavy to carry and too precious to spend, so you simply keep it, the way a country keeps its founding stone.

Hazel finished the banquet entries by candlelight. *Second course: pike in green sauce — sauce clean, pike carries a faint bitterness of bronze; the new pans, then. Flag for replacement. Third course: roast capon — clean. Wine, the amber vintage — clean, though His Majesty drank as though it were not, which is a different kind of poisoning and outside my jurisdiction.*

At the bottom of the page she drew the small symbol that closed every entry: a circle with a single line through it, like an eye seen edge-on. Master Corvane had taught her that mark. "It means *nothing got through*," he had said. "Say it to yourself every night, girl, or one night you'll forget to check." He had died four years ago of nothing poisonous at all — his heart, simply, tired — and Hazel had inherited his ledgers, his post, and his habit of saying *nothing got through* to the dark before sleep.

She said it now, quietly, to the candle. Nothing got through.

Then she blew out the flame, wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, tucked it into the false bottom of her bread box — the one place in the palace no thief would look, because no one steals bread from a taster — and went down the back stairs into the blue hour of the city.

* * *

Corvale at dusk smelled of woodsmoke and river and the particular sweetness of frying onions, which Hazel had long ago decided was the true national scent of Vell, whatever the heralds said about lilies and iron. She moved through it in her plain grey coat, hood up, unremarkable, a woman carrying bread home from the baker's — except that the bread was real and the destination was not, strictly speaking, anywhere.

The alley behind the chandlers' row was exactly as it always was: narrow, cobbled, smelling of tallow and wet stone, with a drain that gurgled like a contented old man. The door stood where it stood, which was nowhere in particular — set into a wall that, if you looked at it directly, seemed to belong to the chandler's shop, and if you looked at it sideways seemed to belong to no building at all. The wood was dark oak, older than the city around it. There was no handle. There was no keyhole.

There was a knocker shaped like a question mark, because the Guild had a sense of humor the way a knife had a sense of humor: technically present, mostly unwelcome.

Hazel knocked the pattern — two, pause, three, pause, one — and the door opened inward onto warmth and paper.

The archive breathed around her as she stepped through: the smell of ten thousand books, leather and dust and the faint ozone tang of things that were not entirely possible. Lamps burned without oil. Stairs climbed toward ceilings that politely declined to exist. Somewhere far off, a shelf rearranged itself with a sound like a deck of cards being shuffled by a patient giant.

"You're late," said Oscar Quill, without looking up.

"I'm punctual," said Hazel. "You're early. Those are different crimes."

Oscar Quill was the Guild's junior archivist, which meant he was responsible for the shelves nobody senior wanted: the misfiled miracles, the damaged impossibilities, the inventory that had gone slightly wrong in storage. He was twenty-six, tall in the way of things built to reach high places, with ink permanently staining the first joint of every finger and hair that looked as though it had been styled by a wind only he could feel. He looked up now, and his face did the thing it always did when he saw her — a quick brightening, immediately suppressed, like a lamp turned down before anyone notices it was up.

"The banquet went well?" he asked, in the tone of a man asking about weather.

"Nobody died. The king drank too much. The Salt Concord ambassador attempted to poison himself accidentally with his own ring, which was alchemical, not malicious, and I flagged it." She set the oilcloth bundle on his desk. "Banquet ledger, copied pages, as agreed. And you owe me for the ring incident. That's above my rate. Alchemical detection wasn't in our contract."

"It's in the *spirit* of the contract."

"The spirit of the contract doesn't eat."

Oscar smiled — the full version, brief and crooked, gone almost before it arrived — and turned to his desk to fetch her payment. Hazel let her eyes wander the aisle beside them while he did. This was habit, and habit was survival. Shelf 412: bottled weather, labeled in fading script (*Rain, Tuesday last, light regret*). Shelf 413: shadows, various owners, folded flat. Shelf 414 was empty except for a card reading *reserved — do not shelve — the space itself is the item*, which Hazel had stopped trying to understand years ago.

"Here." Oscar pressed a slim parcel into her hands, wrapped in brown paper and tied with plain string. Through the paper she could feel the shape of a small bottle and, beside it, a book. "Antidote compound, broad-spectrum, the new formula — better than what the palace physicians have, don't tell them, they'd cry. And the item you asked after."

Her breath went carefully still. "You found it?"

"A lead," he corrected. "Not the thing itself. There's a difference, and in the Guild the difference is everything." He echoed her own private catechism without knowing it, and she felt the strange small pleasure of being understood by accident. "A collector in the Marches claims to hold a recording of your mother's voice. The complete lullaby. Not half of it."

Hazel looked down at the parcel. Her hands, which had not trembled in front of hemlock, in front of arsenic, in front of a king choking on his own ambition, wanted very much to tremble now, and she did not permit it.

"What does he want for it?"

"That's the problem." Oscar's voice dropped, and he glanced down the aisle the way people glance when the shelves might be listening — which, here, they sometimes were. "He doesn't want money. He wants a trade. And the trade he's asking for is—" He stopped. Started again, differently. "Hazel, before we get into that. There's something else. I nearly didn't show you. I've been telling myself since noon that it's a forgery, a prank, one of the senior archivists hazing the junior man. But the ink—"

"Ink?"

He hesitated another moment, and Hazel watched him weigh it, watched the scales move behind his eyes — caution against curiosity, and curiosity winning, the way it always won with him, the way it was going to get him killed someday unless she was nearby to taste his dinner.

"Come see," he said finally. "But walk slowly. The shelf's been... rearranging itself around it all afternoon. Like the book is heavier than the shelf can hold."

He led her deeper into the archive, past the weather and the shadows and the reserved absence on Shelf 414, past shelves whose contents Hazel had learned not to ask about, until they reached a bay near the heart of the collection where the lamps burned a shade too steadily, as if holding their breath.

On a reading table lay a book.

It was unremarkable at first glance — bound in green leather gone soft with age, its spine cracked, its corners worn round. A century of wear, at least. Hazel had handled enough old volumes in this place to read age the way she read poison: in the texture, the smell, the surrender of the materials.

Then she saw the ink.

The book lay open to a page near the middle, and the writing on it was black and glossy and *wet*. It gleamed under the lamplight. As she watched, a letter finished drying — she actually saw it happen, the shine dulling to matte in the space of a blink — and she felt the floor of her certainty tilt, gently, like a ship adjusting to a swell.

"That's impossible," she said. "The binding is a hundred years old. The page is a hundred years old. The ink is—"

"Wet. Yes." Oscar stood beside her, close enough that she could feel the careful control in him too, two professionals pretending at calm. "I've tested it three ways. The parchment dates correctly. The binding dates correctly. The ink contains no preservative, no fixative, nothing. It is simply *still being written*. Look at the date."

Hazel looked at the date.

The numerals stood at the top of the page in a fine antique hand, and they named tonight. Not a night like tonight. Tonight. This evening, this hour nearly — the entry was timestamped to within the quarter-bell, and the quarter-bell had rung while she had been walking through the chandlers' alley.

And below the date, in the same patient, unhurried script, the text continued. Prophecy-form, the old recursive style the Guild used for foretellings, looping back on itself like a knot. Her eye caught fragments as it traveled down the page — phrases that snagged on her the way a fishhook snags on silk:

*...the taster whose tongue is a graveyard...*

*...the archivist who sells what cannot be owned...*

*...on the night the Amber Tide is feasted, the erasure begins at the founding stone...*

*...and the country of Vell shall be as if it had never—*

The sentence stopped. Not ended — *stopped*, mid-word, the final letters still glistening, waiting, as though the hand that wrote them had lifted from the page a century ago and would return at any moment to finish.

Hazel became aware that she had stopped breathing. She restarted it, deliberately, the way she had taught herself to do over hemlock.

"Whose handwriting?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, in a distant, cold way.

"That's the other thing." Oscar's finger hovered above the page without touching it. "I ran it against the Guild's specimen collection. Eleven thousand hands, five centuries. It matched one."

"Whose?"

He looked at her then, and for the first time since she had known him — through the pigeon incident, through the vial, through three years of careful trades across a desk — Oscar Quill looked frightened.

"Yours," he said. "Hazel. It's your handwriting. A hundred years old, and it's yours."

Somewhere deep in the archive, a shelf rearranged itself — softly, patiently, like a country clearing its throat.

Below the broken sentence, in fresher ink than all the rest, one more line had appeared while neither of them was looking:

*She will read this tonight.*
