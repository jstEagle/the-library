## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its archive the way other institutions kept their dead: below ground, in the dark, arranged by cause of departure.

Oscar Quill had worked the lower stacks for six years now, and in that time he had developed a professional relationship with the cold. It was not the honest cold of winter, which came and went and could be argued with by a fire. This was archival cold, the patient chill of stone that had forgotten the sun's name — and he was careful not to think too long about that phrasing, because in this building, forgetting was a trade good.

He carried the restocking basket on his hip like a midwife. Inside it: eleven items returned from circulation, each wrapped in oilcloth and labeled in his own cramped hand. A jar of yesterday's rain, still falling softly against the inside of the glass. The shadow of a man who had died owing money, folded flat as a bedsheet. A key to a door in a house that had burned down forty years before the key was made. Ordinary inventory, by the guild's standards. He shelved them without ceremony, whispering the catalog numbers under his breath because the shelves liked to hear their own names spoken — a superstition he'd learned from the archivist before him, who had learned it from the one before her, all the way back down the chain of hands to whoever had first noticed that the archive rearranged itself when left unattended for too long.

Not *haunted*, exactly. The guild's founders would have been insulted by the word. The archive was simply *particular*. It had opinions about adjacency. It resented being ignored the way an old dog resents being ignored — not with malice, but with a slow, deliberate redistribution of itself until you were forced to pay attention.

Tonight it was paying attention to him.

He felt it before he understood it: the sensation of walking through a room where someone has just stopped talking about you. The air behind his ears went taut. Somewhere three aisles over, paper whispered against paper, a sound like a moth turning over in its sleep.

"None of that," he said mildly, to no one, to everyone. "You'll get your attention when I've finished the returns."

The whispering stopped. Then started again. Closer.

* * *

Oscar had come to the guild at nineteen, hollowed out by a grief he did not discuss, and the guild had hired him precisely because of it. They preferred staff with holes in them. A whole person, the Mistress of Acquisitions once told him, tapping her temple, wants the world to stay put. A person with a missing piece is always half-looking for it. You'll find things the others walk past.

She had been right, though he resented the arithmetic of it. His missing piece had a name, and he kept the name in a locked drawer in his heart the way other men kept letters from the dead, and once or twice a year he took it out and read it and put it away again before it could stain anything.

Hazel Voss knew about the drawer. Not its contents — she had never asked, and he loved her a little for never asking — but she knew it was there. That was the currency between them, really. Not the favors they traded, though the favors were real enough: she tasted the guild's more volatile acquisitions before they went into the collection, since her body was a ledger of survived poisons and could report on a substance's intentions better than any assay; and he, in turn, kept her accounts balanced, tracked her debts and credits across the city's gray markets, found her buyers for the small illegal mercies she sold to feed herself. But beneath the commerce there was the simpler thing: two careful people who had recognized the care in each other and decided not to make a fuss about it.

He thought of her now, oddly, as he turned into the aisle where the whispering lived. Thought of how she would have said something dry and precise about a shelf that murmured, and how the saying of it would have made the fear smaller. That was her gift, though she'd deny having gifts at all. She made frightening things sit still long enough to be counted.

The book was on the third shelf, spine out, in a gap he could swear — *could* swear — had been empty that morning.

It was bound in ash-gray leather gone soft as moleskin at the corners, the kind of wear that takes a century of handling, and its title had been stamped in silver that had oxidized to the color of old spoons:

*A True and Particular Prophecy Concerning the Country of Vell.*

Oscar stood very still.

There were two problems, and his mind presented them one at a time, politely, like a clerk laying documents on a desk.

First: the country of Vell did not exist.

Oh, it existed as a word. Everyone used the word. You bought Vellish wool and drank Vellish wine and swore by the Nine Rivers of Vell, and every map in the kingdom showed it there, west of the Marrow Mountains, a country shaped like a cupped hand. But the guild's master ledger — which recorded not what things *were* but what things *had been paid for*, and was therefore the only honest book in the world — contained, somewhere in its thousand pages, an entry Oscar had never been permitted to read in full. He knew only its heading, glimpsed once as a senior archivist turned past it: *Vell — sold, terms undisclosed —* followed by a date one hundred years ago this season.

Sold. Not conquered, not dissolved, not renamed. Sold. And whatever that entry meant, everyone above his rank treated the word *Vell* thereafter the way you treat a door in your own house that you've agreed, without discussion, never to open. People spoke of the country constantly and never once asked themselves why no one they knew had ever come *from* there, or gone *to* there, or could say what language was spoken in it beyond the fact that the wine labels were written in something beautiful.

Second problem: the ink.

He took the book down and the weight of it surprised him — heavier than its size, as though the pages were packed with something denser than paper. He opened it to the title page, tilted it toward his lantern.

The ink shone.

A century-old book should have brown ink, faded iron-gall gone the color of dried blood or weak tea. This ink sat on the page wet and black and glossy, beading slightly where the pen had paused, and when he held the page close — closer than caution advised — he smelled it. Iron. Oak galls. A breath of vinegar.

Ink dries in minutes. This ink had not dried in a hundred years, which meant either that it had been applied recently, or —

Or that the book existed somewhere time had not gotten around to finishing.

"Don't," Oscar said aloud, to himself, in the voice he used on nervous apprentices. "Forgers exist. Wet-ink forgery is the oldest trick in the trade. Fresh ink, artificially aged binding, a provocative title. You've authenticated four forged prophecies this year alone."

This was true. Forged prophecy was a growth industry in the capital; despair, as the Mistress liked to say, always finds a printer. He had a system for them. He checked the binding thread — modern flax here, unmistakably; no, wait, the thread was hand-spun, uneven, pre-industrial. He checked the wormholes — genuine, ancient, and here was the strange thing: the wormholes stopped dead at the final quire of pages, as if the worms had eaten their way through a century and then found nothing worth eating. He checked the watermark. Held a page up to the lantern light and there it was, faint as breath on glass: the guild's own seal, the open hand holding the closed eye — a mark that had not been pressed into commercial paper in ninety years, because the papermill that made it had burned, and the fire, according to the guild's own records, had consumed the mold along with everything else.

So: paper a century dead, watermarked by a mill that no longer existed, bound in period-correct thread, wormed by honest worms — and filled with ink that gleamed like it had been set down while he climbed the stairs.

His hands wanted to shake. He gave them a job instead. He turned pages.

* * *

The early chapters were what you'd expect, if you'd read a hundred forged prophecies, and Oscar had. Plague years. A comet described in terms vague enough to fit any comet. Kings unnamed, wars unlocated, the usual prophetic fog that lets a fraud claim hits after the fact. The handwriting changed hands several times — different scribes, different centuries, the way a genuinely old book accumulates custodians — and each new hand began with a pious little note certifying that the previous copyist had died before completing the work, which was either tradition or an occupational hazard of the genre.

He was nearly relaxed. He was composing the incident report in his head already — *recovered item, probable forgery, recommend secure shelving pending authentication* — when the handwriting changed one last time.

This last hand was small and exact and utterly familiar, because he had spent six years reading it in the margins of returned items, on debt chits, on the little notes she tucked into payment envelopes instead of letters: *Your rates are robbery. Enclosed is robbery's opposite.* Hazel Voss wrote the way she did everything else — economically, precisely, with the flourish removed and only meaning left.

Except Hazel Voss did not write prophecy. Hazel Voss did not write in a hand that matched a scribe dead a hundred years. Hazel Voss had never, to his certain knowledge, touched this book.

The entry began without preamble, mid-page, as if continuing a conversation:

*In the hundredth year, the erasure completes its circuit, and the country that was sold shall be collected. Two shall stand outside the counting: the taster whose body keeps accounts, and the keeper of things that cannot be. Their names are written in the founding stone, and the stone does not forget, though everything else must.*

And then, beneath it, in the same small exact hand, as if the century between had been a single sitting:

*Hazel Voss. Oscar Quill. Tonight.*

* * *

The lantern guttered. Or the aisle breathed. In the Guild of Impossible Things these amounted to the same thing.

Oscar became aware that he had been standing motionless for some time — long enough that the cold had gotten into his knees and his shadow had gone stiff against the stacks. He also became aware, slowly, the way you become aware of water rising around your ankles, of the second strangeness.

The shelf was rearranging around the book.

Not dramatically. Nothing so vulgar as books flying. But the volumes adjacent to the prophecy — a ledger of confiscated lullabies, a box of unsent apologies, the memoirs of a woman who had lived her life backward — were shifting, minutely, by fractions of a finger's width, turning their spines *away* from the gray book the way sleepers turn from a draft. And the gaps between them were widening. The shelf was making room. It was doing the archival equivalent of stepping back.

"You don't do that," he whispered. "You've never done that."

The shelf did not answer, but the whispering he'd heard earlier had resumed, and now he could locate it, and it was coming from the book itself — no, not the book. From the *gap* the book occupied. A sound like paper remembering it was wood remembering it was tree, a slow recitation of everything it had been, and underneath it, barely audible, almost kind:

*— tonight, tonight, tonight —*

He shut the book.

The sound stopped. The cold stayed.

Oscar stood in the aisle of the impossible archive with a forbidden prophecy hugged to his chest like a child's doll, and forced himself through the checklist, item by item, because the checklist was the wall between him and the drop.

Forgery. Yes. Fine. Forgers could fake thread, fake wormholes, fake watermarks if they had the mold — and molds survive fires, don't they, someone could have — no. No one had that mold. He'd helped inventory the burn salvage himself. Next hypothesis: a hoax played by a colleague. Possible. The guild appreciated a joke the way a cathedral appreciates a whoopee cushion, but no colleague had access to this shelf, and none of them could write in Hazel's hand, and none of them would choose *tonight*, of all nights — the night of the royal banquet, the night Hazel would stand behind the king's chair with her silver spoon and her ledger heart, tasting for hemlock as she did every season. The date wasn't a guess. The banquet schedule was posted in the palace kitchens, yes, but the *specificity* — her name, his name, the hour — that wasn't hoax-work. Hoaxes want deniability. This thing wanted *witnesses*.

Third hypothesis, the one he had been circling like a bird over deep water: the book was genuine, and the future had simply gotten tired of waiting its turn.

He thought about the master ledger. About the entry headed *Vell — sold*. About how, in six years, he had never once met a citizen of Vell, though Vell appeared on every map, in every treaty, in every prayer for fair weather over the Nine Rivers. About how the wool was Vellish and the wine was Vellish and nobody could name a single town in Vell, and how he had noticed this exactly never, in thirty-one years of noticing things, because — and here the thought arrived with the soft terrible click of a lock turning — because he had been *arranged* not to notice, the way the eye is arranged not to see its own blind spot. Something had been sold out from under the world a hundred years ago, and the world had signed the receipt without reading it, and the receipt had a date on it, and dates come due.

*Tonight.*

"No," he said. Firmly. The way Hazel would say it. He imagined her standing beside him, arms crossed, looking at the gray book with the expression she wore for substances that lied about their nature. She'd say: *Books are objects. Objects obey inventory. Catalog it, wrap it, shelve it in the sealed case, and let the seniors lose sleep over it.* She would be right. She was almost always right, and her rightness had the specific gravity of fact.

But even in imagination she hesitated at the end of it, the way she hesitated whenever the guild's business brushed too near the question of what, exactly, had happened to countries that got sold. Even she kept a drawer.

The whispering started again. Softer now. Almost patient.

*— the taster whose body keeps accounts —*

He looked down at the book in his arms. Its cover had warmed against his chest, which leather should not do in archival cold, and through the oilskin wrapping of his memory he heard the Mistress of Acquisitions again: *a person with a missing piece is always half-looking for it.* And beneath that, older, in a voice he did not permit himself often: a boy's voice, laughing, calling him by a name that had gone into the drawer and stayed there.

The book named Hazel. The book named him. The book named *tonight*, and tonight was already moving toward them both whether he shelved it or not — she in her servant's black behind the king's chair, he here in the dark among things that could not be, and the distance between the palace and the guildhall was twenty minutes' walk on a night with wind in it.

He could carry it upstairs. Report it. Let the machinery of seniority grind it fine.

Or he could do the one thing the guild forbade and the archive demanded and his own hole-in-the-heart hungered for: he could read further. Because the prophecy had named the two of them and then stopped, mid-page, ink still wet — and ink stays wet, he understood suddenly, coldly, *only when the sentence is not finished being true.*

The book was waiting for tonight to happen so it could finish drying.

Oscar Quill, keeper of things that cannot be, wrapped the impossible object in his coat instead of oilcloth, blew out his lantern, and walked up out of the archive into the last ordinary evening the city of Vell would ever mistake for itself — while behind him, in the dark, the empty space on the third shelf quietly closed, as though nothing had ever rested there at all.
