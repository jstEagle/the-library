## 3. The Banquet That Wasn't

The king's cup arrived on a salver of tarnished silver, and Hazel Voss read it the way other people read letters.

She had been reading cups for eleven years — since she was fourteen and her mother pressed her palm flat against a table in their kitchen above the bakery and said, *Your tongue is a ledger, little bird. Keep good accounts.* Every cup since had been an entry: the arsenic in Lord Calloway's wine at the harvest feast (bitter almond, faintly sweet, like a lie told gently); the foxglove steeped into the duchess's tisane (a green taste, a slow green, the color of a garden gone wrong); the night three years ago when someone put distilled wolfsbane into the soup tureen and Hazel drank two spoonfuls before her tongue caught fire with recognition, and she spent four days in the palace infirmary learning exactly how much death her body could argue with.

Her body was a ledger of poisons survived. That was what made her valuable. Not immunity — there was no such thing, whatever the courtiers whispered — but tolerance, built dose by dose, scar by scar, until the common murders of the world slid off her like rain off oiled wool. She could take a sip that would fell a horse and live to describe the flavor in precise, clinical detail to the guards who came running.

Tonight's entry, then: hemlock.

She tasted it before the cup touched her lips, because a good taster reads the steam. The vapor off the dark red wine carried a ghost of parsley — that soft, carroty sweetness that hemlock wears like perfume over its true self, which is the smell of a meadow at dusk, green and innocent and utterly indifferent to what it does. Her mouth watered, which was the body's first mistake; her tongue catalogued, which was its second nature.

Hemlock. In the king's cup. At the banquet for the centenary of the Founding.

Hazel did not spit. Spitting was theater, and theater drew eyes. Instead she set the cup down with a small, deliberate click, turned to the captain of the guard standing by the dais — a broad, patient man named Ferren whose daughter she had once paid a guild favor to have schooled — and said, quietly, under the swell of the string quartet:

"Conium maculatum. Steeped, not powdered. Someone wanted it slow and certain. Check the cellar steward's hands for stains, and check whether the wine came up from the Vellmark stores or the new casks."

Ferren's face did the thing faces did around her: a flicker of respect, quickly folded away, because no one liked being reminded that the small gray woman beside the throne held more power in her tongue than they held in their swords. He moved off. Hazel remained standing, hands folded, the picture of professional calm, while inside her chest something ticked and counted, the way it always did after a finding.

*Entry forty-one,* she thought. *Hemlock, court vintage, centenary banquet. Attempted regicide number six this reign. Motive unknown. Outcome: prevented.*

That was the account balanced. That should have been the end of it.

But the hall was wrong.

She noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth — not all at once, but as a wrongness of contour, a hollowness where fullness should be. The Grand Hall of Vell's palace seated three hundred at the long tables, and tonight it should have been overflowing: ambassadors from Osterhale and the Salt Principalities, guildmasters in their chains of office, the whole glittering apparatus of a country congratulating itself on one hundred years of existing. The quartet had been playing. The candles had been lit — she remembered them lit, hundreds of them, beeswax and tallow, their light pooling gold on the silverware.

She looked up now, and the hall was empty.

Not quiet-empty. Not between-courses empty. Empty the way a room is empty when it has been empty for a long time. The long tables stood bare, their cloths gone gray with dust that had no business accumulating in the space of a heartbeat. The chairs were askew, some overturned, as if three hundred guests had risen all at once and simply ceased. The quartet's platform held four music stands and no musicians, and the sheet music on the stands was blank — not unplayed, but unprinted, white paper staring back at her like open eyes.

The candles were unlit. Not burned down. Unlit, their wicks pale and untouched, as though no flame had ever been asked of them.

And the cold — the cold was the worst part. It was the cold of a room that has been shut for years, the cold of held breath, of a house whose owners died in winter. It climbed through the soles of her shoes and settled in her knees.

Hazel's training said: assess. Her body said: run. She did neither. She stood very still beside the king's empty chair and let fear arrive, because fear was information, and she had learned never to refuse information, even when it came dressed as terror.

*Fear first,* her mother used to say. *Understanding second. Never confuse the order.*

"Hello?" she said, and her voice fell into the emptiness and did not echo. That was wrong too. The Grand Hall had acoustics designed to flatter orators; a whisper at the high table could be heard in the gallery. Her voice went out and stopped, like a bird hitting glass.

She turned toward the dais.

The prophecy book lay open on it.

She knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting — except it wasn't hers, and it wasn't anyone's she knew, and yet the sight of it landed in her with the certainty of recognition. A thick volume bound in green leather gone black at the corners, its spine cracked into segments like dried riverbed, lying open on the spot where the king's plate should have been. The pages were heavy stock, cream-colored, and the ink —

The ink was wet.

She saw the shine of it from ten feet away. Fresh ink, black as beetle shells, gleaming under the gray light that came from nowhere she could identify — because the windows, she realized, showed nothing. Not night. Not day. A white nothing, the color of a page before the word.

Hazel walked to the dais. Her heels sounded on the stone — or rather, they didn't; her footsteps made no sound at all, and she only knew she was moving because the floor changed beneath her. Silence had swallowed the room whole.

The book was titled, in faded gilt: *Concerning the Hundredth Year.* No author. No printer's mark. The date on the flyleaf was a century old.

But the ink was wet.

She read.

---

*In the hundredth year, when the stone remembers what was carved upon it, the erasure shall begin at the edges and move inward, street by street, name by name.*

*A taster shall stand in the hall of kings and find it empty, and her tongue shall know the truth before her eyes do: that the country is already dying, and has been dying for a hundred years, and none mourned because none remembered.*

*Two shall walk out through a door that no longer exists. One who has died many times and kept her name. One who will give his.*

*The taster's blood wrote this. The taster's blood will end it — or fail to, and Vell will finish forgetting, and even this book will forget it was ever written.*

---

Below that last line, still glistening, still drying even as she watched, was a single word in fresh ink:

*Hazel.*

Not "the taster." Not "she." Her name. Her exact name, spelled correctly, in a book dated one hundred years past, written in ink that had not yet dried.

The world stuttered.

There was no better word for it. The hall skipped — like a heartbeat catching, like a record's needle jumping a groove. For half a moment she was somewhere else entirely: standing in her mother's kitchen above the bakery, flour on her hands, the smell of cardamom bread in the oven, her mother saying something she couldn't quite hear — *little bird, listen, when the hundredth year comes* — and then the stutter passed and she was back in the dead hall, and the flour was gone from her memory's hands, and she could not remember what her mother had been about to say, only that it had mattered enormously, only that she had just lost something irreplaceable and would spend the rest of her life trying to remember what it was.

Hazel gripped the edge of the dais. The stone was cold enough to burn.

*Entry forty-two,* she thought, and the thought was almost a laugh, hysterical and thin. *Hall, royal, entire. Contents: absent. Cause: unknown. Prognosis:*

She couldn't finish the entry. Her mind refused the line.

She forced herself through the drill instead, the one Oscar Quill had taught her two years ago, back when their acquaintance was new and he'd noticed how she counted things when frightened — spoons, steps, breaths — and had said, in his dry archivist way, "Counting is just indexing. Indexing is just fear wearing spectacles. Here's a better system." He'd taught her to triage: *What do I know? What do I suspect? What do I need?*

What she knew: the hall was empty. The book was real. Her name was in it, in wet ink, in a century-old hand.

What she suspected: the hemlock was connected. Poison and prophecy, dinner and doom — her whole life had trained her to believe that nothing at a table was ever accidental. If someone had poisoned the king's cup tonight, on this specific night, then the poison was either part of this or a distraction from it, and both possibilities were terrible.

What she needed: Oscar. Because if anyone in Vell knew what a century-old book with wet ink meant, it was the guild archivist who traded in impossible things, the man who had once sold her yesterday's rain in a stoppered vial ("for grief," he'd said, "it works better than brandy"), the man whose ledger she secretly helped balance because the Guild of Impossible Things ran on favors the way the palace ran on coin, and favors needed keeping track of.

Oscar Quill, who restocked shelves that rearranged themselves. Who would know what to do. Who had better, for once, actually know what to do, because Hazel Voss was standing alone in a banquet hall where three hundred people had stopped existing mid-course, and her professional detachment was fraying like old rope.

She took one step toward the doors — and stopped.

Because the hall had changed again.

It was subtle. That was what made her stomach drop. The first wrongness had been total, unmistakable; this one was a matter of degrees, of drift. The dust on the tables was thicker. The overturned chairs were more fallen, their wood grayer, one of them now missing a leg entirely as though it had rotted through in the seconds since she'd looked. The white nothing beyond the windows had deepened by a shade, toward gray, toward evening-that-wasn't.

The room was aging. Or — no. She understood suddenly, with the clarity that fear sometimes granted, the clarity that arrived before understanding and dragged it along behind: the room was *being forgotten*. Layer by layer. Detail by detail. The candles had forgotten they were ever lit. The guests had forgotten they had ever come. And the room itself was beginning to forget it had ever hosted anything at all, reverting to some older, barer version of itself, shedding its present the way a snake sheds skin.

Street by street, the book had said. Name by name.

It had started here. Tonight. With her standing in it.

Hazel did the bravest and most ordinary thing available to her: she picked up the prophecy book, closed it carefully so the wet ink wouldn't smear — her name disappearing under the cover with a small final sound she felt in her teeth — tucked it under her arm, and walked out of the Grand Hall at a pace that was absolutely not running, no matter what her pulse said.

---

The corridor outside was worse.

The palace corridors at banquet hour should have thronged with servants: runners with messages, pages with wine, the whole circulatory system of a living court. Instead the halls stretched empty and dim, torch brackets holding unlit torches, and the portraits on the walls —

The portraits were fading.

She stopped before one she had passed a thousand times: old King Aldous the Third, the founder's grandson, famous for his beard and his bad temper. Except the beard was going. Not gray — *going*, dissolving into the canvas like sugar into tea, and the face beneath it was smoothing out, becoming generic, becoming no one. By the time she reached the end of the corridor and glanced back, the frame held a smear of flesh tones and a suggestion of a ruff, and nothing else.

Name by name.

Hazel walked faster. Under her arm, the book felt warm, as if freshly baked, as if alive. She tried not to think about that. She tried very hard to think about anything else: the inventory of the palace kitchens (she could recite it; she'd audited it for the guild), the list of poisons she hadn't yet survived (shorter every year), the exact weight of Oscar's ridiculous brass magnifying loupe that he wore on a ribbon like a fop's monocle and used to read marginalia no one else could see.

Think of Oscar. Think of the guildhall on Candlemaker's Row, with its door that was always slightly ajar and its sign that read, in letters that rearranged themselves when you weren't looking, *IMPOSSIBLE THINGS, FAIR RATES, NO REFUNDS ON MIRACLES.*

If anywhere in Vell had answers, it was there. If anywhere in Vell was safe —

The great doors at the end of the corridor stood open onto the entrance court, and Hazel stepped through them and stopped again, because the court was empty too, and the fountain in its center was dry, and the sky above was that same deepening nothing, neither night nor storm but absence, a ceiling of unmade weather.

And on the fountain's rim, in a city where she had worked for eleven years and known every face worth knowing, sat a man she had never seen before, dressed in traveling gray, with no face she could fix on. She looked at him and her eyes slid, the way they slid off a word repeated too often, and she could not have described him afterward to save her life. He was watching her. She was sure of that much. He raised one hand — greeting, or summons, or farewell — and mouthed something she could not hear across the distance.

Then he was simply not there, and the fountain rim was empty, and Hazel decided, with the cold practicality that had kept her alive through forty-one entries in her ledger, that she was leaving the palace immediately, that she was crossing the city to Candlemaker's Row before the streets finished forgetting their own names, and that she was bringing the book.

She hugged it tighter under her arm and set off into the whitening evening, her footsteps silent on stones that were, she noticed as she passed the gatehouse, beginning to lose their carved insignia — the crowned stag of Vell softening on the lintel, its antlers thinning, its crown flattening, its whole proud shape sinking back into raw rock.

Behind her, the Grand Hall forgot the banquet entirely.

Ahead of her, the city waited, patient as a held breath, and somewhere in it an archivist was shelving books in an archive that had just, she would learn, begun rearranging itself around a single impossible volume — the twin of the one she carried, still warm against her ribs, its ink finally dry.

*Entry forty-three,* she thought, stepping through the palace gates into streets that smelled, faintly and terribly, of nothing at all. *City, royal capital. Status: pending.*

She hoped Oscar was having a quieter evening.
