## 4. Streets With No Names

They ran.

Hazel had not run since she was nine years old and a cook had caught her stealing honey from the palace kitchens — and that had been a different kind of running, a child's running, with laughter somewhere behind it. This was the other kind. The kind where your lungs burn and your thoughts go thin as wire and you do not look back because looking back is how you die in stories.

The prophecy book was under her arm. She did not remember taking it from the dais. That frightened her more than the empty hall, more than the king's cup still sitting on its cloth with hemlock laced through wine that no one would ever drink. Her hands had decided things without her. Her hands had seen the open book and understood what her mind refused to: that it belonged to her now, or she to it, and either way they were leaving together.

"Left," Oscar said, breathless, at the bottom of the servants' stair. "Not left-left. Left toward the river. The guildhall's on Tallow Street — *was* on Tallow Street." He caught himself on the banister. His fingers, she noticed, were ink-stained to the second knuckle, and they were shaking.

"You don't know if it will be there."

"I know the guildhall has survived three fires, two floods, and an archbishop with opinions about our inventory." He pushed the door open onto the night. "It will be there. It has to be. It's the only building in Vell that knows what it is."

* * *

The city outside the palace gates was wrong in a way Hazel's body recognized before her mind consent to it.

She was, by trade, a connoisseur of wrongness. She could taste arsenic in a soup at forty paces, could feel aconite before it touched her lips, could read a kitchen's mood from the smell of its stockpots. Her whole life was the detection of small corruptions hidden inside ordinary things. And Vell City tonight smelled like a soup with something dead dissolved into it — familiar, nourishing, and underneath it all, faintly, sweetly rotten.

"Lampwick Row," Oscar said, and stopped.

The street sign hung above them on its iron bracket, swinging gently in a wind Hazel couldn't feel against her own face. The sign was blank. Not worn, not weathered, not scraped by some drunkard's knife. Blank the way a page is blank before anyone writes on it — smooth, pale, waiting. As though the street had never been named. As though the name had been poured out of it like water from a cup.

"That's new," Oscar said. His voice was very controlled. She was beginning to learn his registers of fear, and this — the flatness, the precision, the words chosen like tools — was the deepest one. "I walked this row yesterday. I bought chestnuts here yesterday. There was a sign. There was a man named Ferro who sold them and argued about salt and—" He pressed his palm flat against the sign, as if checking for wet paint. "Hazel. When did I buy chestnuts?"

"Yesterday. You said so."

"No. I mean—" He turned to her, and in the light of the corner lamp his face had gone the color of old paper. "What day is it?"

And Hazel opened her mouth to answer and found nothing there. Not fog, not confusion — absence. A clean little hole where the day should have been. She knew it was autumn because the air tasted of woodsmoke and rotting leaves. She knew she had worked the banquet tonight because her ledger was in her coat with tonight's date written at its head. But between those two facts lay a corridor with no doors.

"It's the twenty-third," she said slowly. "Of Sowmonth. I wrote it down." She took out her ledger, her poison-ledger, her double-life ledger, and showed him the line. *Sowmonth 23rd. King's banquet. Forty-one covers.* Her handwriting, steady as always. Proof, of a kind.

Oscar stared at it. "You write everything down."

"Everything."

"Keep doing that," he said, and something in his voice made her understand that he was not giving advice. He was making a request. Possibly a prayer.

* * *

They moved deeper into the city because standing still felt like drowning, and because Oscar kept saying *Tallow Street* under his breath like a man counting coins he wasn't sure he had.

The streets were emptier than they should have been. Not deserted — Vell City was never deserted; even at the dead hour past midnight there were bakers waking, watchmen walking, lovers arguing in doorways — but thinned, as though someone had lifted every third person out of the night with a pair of tongs. And the ones who remained were strange.

A woman stood at her window shaking out a tablecloth over and over, the same motion, the same snap of linen, with the fixed patience of a clock. Two men stood beneath a balcony mid-argument, and when Hazel passed close enough to hear, one of them was saying, "—and I told you, I *told* you, that we should have—" and then simply stopped. Not trailed off. Stopped, the way a candle stops, and stood there with his mouth open, looking at his friend with polite, bottomless puzzlement, as though the argument had been a room he'd stepped out of and could no longer find the door back into.

His friend patted his arm. Neither seemed distressed. That was the worst part. They looked like men who had forgotten something small — where they'd put their spectacles, whether they'd banked the fire — and had decided it wasn't worth the trouble of worrying over.

"Don't talk to them," Oscar murmured, pulling her along. "Don't ask them anything. Questions give the forgetting somewhere to land."

"How do you know that?"

"I don't. It just feels true, doesn't it? Like something you read once." He laughed, a short ugly sound. "That's what frightens me most, Hazel. Everything I know right now feels like something I read once. My own head feels borrowed."

Ahead of them, the Fountain of the Nine Daughters played its water into the square, and Hazel stopped, because the fountain had eight daughters. She knew it the way she knew her own teeth — the ninth daughter had been broken off in her grandmother's girlhood, some riot, some accident, and children still dared each other to sit in the empty plinth. But the stone in front of her held nine figures, all complete, all smooth and new, and the water came from nine jugs instead of eight, and when she tried to picture the gap where the missing daughter should have been, the picture wouldn't form. The gap had healed over in her memory like flesh over a wound.

"Oscar."

He followed her gaze. She watched him arrive at it — watched the moment the wrongness reached him, watched his lips move counting, *seven, eight,* and stop.

"The ninth," he said. "There was a ninth. She was broken off and the pigeons sat on her feet and—" He closed his eyes. "No. Gone. It's gone. It's not that I remember it wrong, it's that the *remembering* is being taken, the way you'd take a coin out of a purse, and the purse closes up after—"

"Stop," Hazel said. Not unkindly. But she said it firmly, because she had spent ten years watching courtiers talk themselves into terror and she knew the sound of a spiral beginning. "You're doing what the forgetting wants. Standing still and counting losses. We count later. In ink. On paper. We walk now."

Something shifted in his face — surprise, then gratitude, then a flicker of the wariness that was, she suspected, his native expression. "You're calmer than I am."

"Tasters are trained to swallow first and scream quietly." She tucked the ledger tighter under her arm. "Come on, archivist. Tell me about the guildhall. Out loud. All the way there. Names, doors, inventory. Give me something to hold."

"Why?"

"Because memory is heavier than fear," she said. "Carry enough of it and you can't float away."

* * *

So he talked, and she listened, and the talking was a rope between them across a dark drop.

He told her about the Guild of Impossible Things, which was officially the Guild of Stationers and Scriveners and unofficially the only honest business in Vell, since everyone else sold lies and called them futures. He told her about the inventory — carefully, vaguely, the way a man tells you about a locked room while walking you past it — and she let him keep his vagueness. She had secrets too. They were both people who understood that trust was not the absence of locks but the agreement to knock.

He told her about Tallow Street: the chandler's on the corner that had burned twice and smelled of smoke forever, the cobbler who sang hymns badly, the narrow door with the brass knocker shaped like a question mark, because the founder had believed that every visitor should arrive already asking. He described the reading room with its lamps trimmed to burn green so the ink wouldn't glare. He described the smell — dust, leather, tallow, and underneath it something older, like the inside of a violin.

"And the master ledger," he said, and hesitated, and she heard the hesitation and catalogued it. "The master ledger is in the vault. You'll see it. If we get there."

"*If.*"

"If," he agreed, and for the first time since the palace he smiled, thin and real. "You notice I'm not promising. Promising is for people whose world holds still long enough for promises to dry."

They crossed the Weavers' Bridge, and halfway across Hazel realized the river had no sound. The water moved — black, glossy, catching lamplight in broken ribbons — but the noise of it, that constant low conversation the river had muttered under every day of her life, was absent, and in its absence the silence was enormous, a silence with texture, a silence pressing on her eardrums like deep water.

She grabbed his sleeve. He was already looking down at the water, and his face told her he had noticed at the same moment.

"The river forgot how to make noise," she whispered.

"Or the noise was never part of the river," Oscar said. "Maybe it was part of us. Maybe the erasure isn't eating the city, maybe it's eating our *record* of the city, and the river sounds silent because the part of me that knew its voice went first." He swallowed. "My brother used to skip stones here."

"You've mentioned a brother before?" Hazel asked carefully.

"No," he said. "But I know I have one. I can feel the shape of him, the way your tongue finds the hole where a tooth was." His hand went to his breast pocket, where a slim book rode — his own ledger, she supposed, his own account-book of survival. Everyone in Vell, it seemed, kept books against forgetting. "There are things in my pockets I can't explain yet. I'm going to trust that future-me had reasons."

"Fools rush in where archivists fear to file," Hazel said.

That startled a laugh out of him — brief, cracked, genuine — and the laugh mattered. She felt it matter, the way a lamp matters in a large dark house. Two guarded people crossing a bridge over a silent river, laughing at a bad joke neither would remember tomorrow unless one of them wrote it down. She filed it away in herself, in the place where she kept things too important for paper.

* * *

Tallow Street, when they reached it, was holding.

Not intact — holding, the way a hand holds water. The chandler's shop was there, smelling of smoke forever. The cobbler's window was there. The brass question-mark knocker gleamed dully in the lamplight, and when Oscar laid his hand on it, the whole door seemed to exhale, a breath of warm dust and tallow and that violin-box smell he'd promised, and Hazel felt her knees nearly buckle with relief so acute it was indistinguishable from grief.

Inside, the guildhall glowed faintly green. Lamps trimmed low. Shelves rising into shadow, thousands of spines, thousands of titles, and every title — she checked, walking one aisle with her fingertips trailing the leather — every single one legible, named, *itself*. In a city dissolving into blanks, the guildhall was a page no one had erased yet.

"It's anchored," Oscar breathed. He moved through the aisles touching spines like a man greeting family after a long illness. "The whole building. Do you feel it? The ground here is *solid*." He stamped once, experimentally, and the floorboards answered with a clean knock. "Whatever the sigil is, whatever's happening out there — this place has weight. History has weight. Enough recorded history and reality can't get a grip on it to peel it off."

Hazel set the prophecy book down on the reading table, and the green light bent toward it slightly, the way candles lean toward a draft.

"Then we stay," she said. "We bar the door and we stay until whatever this is passes, or breaks, or finishes, and—"

"Hazel." Oscar had gone still by the vault door at the back of the room. His hand rested on the lock, but he wasn't looking at the lock. He was looking at the master ledger's shelf beyond it, visible through the little barred window — and his expression was one she had seen on faces across a banquet table, once or twice a year, when a taster's pause stretched a half-second too long and a host realized their soup was condemned.

"What's on that shelf?" she asked quietly.

"The master ledger records every sale the guild ever made." His voice had gone flat again, tool-precise. "Every impossible thing. Every buyer. Every price." He turned to her, and the green lamplight made his eyes look drowned. "And there's a page open, Hazel. The ledger opens itself when it wants to be read. It's open to a transaction dated one hundred years ago tonight."

He pulled the vault key from around his neck.

"The sale of a country," Oscar said. "Vell. Sold once before. And the entry says — it says — *repurchased, balance due, delivery tonight.*"

Outside, far away, something vast turned a page.
