## 5. What the Guild Sells

The guildhall smelled of dust and older decisions.

Oscar Quill had worked in the Undercroft for eleven years, and in that time he had learned that every archive has a heartbeat — a slow tidal rhythm of intake and withdrawal, of things arriving wrapped in oilcloth and things leaving in the pockets of people who had paid more than money. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its public face on the street level: a narrow shopfront with a brass bell and a clerk who sold love letters by the pound to sentimental undergraduates. But three floors down, past a door that only opened for those who already knew it existed, lay the true inventory.

He was restocking when the prophecy book found him.

That was how he would remember it afterward — not that he found it, but that it found him, the way water finds the low place in a floor. He had been shelving a crate of yesterday's rain, forty glass jars of it, each one labeled in his own careful hand with the date and the district where it had fallen. Yesterday's rain was a modest commodity as impossible things went; lovers bought it to anoint doorways, farmers bought it out of superstition, and once, memorably, a widow had bought six jars because her husband had proposed to her in a storm and she wanted the weather back. It kept poorly. It wanted shelving in the cool dark, and so he was crouched on the lowest level of Stack Nine, arms full of jars that chimed faintly against each other like distant bells, when he noticed the gap.

There was a gap in Stack Seven where there had never been a gap. He knew Stack Seven the way other men knew their own teeth. It held cartographies of places that refused surveying, hymnals in languages with no living speakers, a single boot that belonged to no pair and walked home by itself if left unattended. Every spine accounted for. And now, between the *Atlas of Unfinished Rivers* and the taxonomic folio of extinct hours, a book stood where nothing had stood that morning.

It was small. Bound in grey leather gone soft as skin at the corners. No title on the spine, which in the Undercroft was itself a kind of announcement; unlabeled books were either worthless or worse than worthless, and the guild's founding rule made no distinction between the two.

Oscar set down the rain and took it up instead.

The leather was warm. That was the first wrong thing. The Undercroft stayed at cellar temperature year-round; even in high summer the stone breathed cold up through the floors, and every object in the collection conformed to it within a day. This book sat in his hands like something recently alive. He turned it over. On the back cover, pressed into the hide in blind tooling too shallow to catch light unless you tilted it just so, was a sigil he did not know — a circle broken by a vertical line, the line descending through the circle's base into nothing, as though drawing itself downward forever.

He told himself it was a forgery.

This was, in fairness, his professional reflex. Half the items offered to the guild were forgeries, and half of *those* were interesting forgeries, and a tenth of *those* turned out to be genuine, which was why the guild employed him at all. A century-old prophetic text naming specific contemporary persons was among the oldest cons in the trade. You wrote your victim's name in fake-ancient ink, you let them find the book themselves — always themselves, never delivered — and you watched them walk willingly into whatever trap the margins contained. Oscar had catalogued eleven such forgeries. Two of their makers had been hanged. One had been promoted.

But the ink.

He opened the book carefully, by the spine, the way one handles anything that might be listening. The pages were foxed at the edges, browned the way paper browns across a hundred years of patient oxidation. The script inside was a fine secretary hand, faded to the color of weak tea.

And still wet.

Not glistening — he would have run then, or told himself he would have — but *wet*, the way ink is wet a breath after the pen lifts. When he tilted the first page toward the lamp, the letters threw shadows. Faded ink does not throw shadows. Ink a hundred years old does not throw shadows. Ink does not fade and remain wet, any more than a man can be old and unborn, and yet here it lay under his thumb, and here his thumb came away faintly grey.

He read the page.

> *In the hundredth year, when the ledger balances against itself,*
> *the taster shall taste her own name,*
> *and the archivist shall file the world under its true heading,*
> *and Vell shall be asked whether it ever was.*

Below this, in the same hand, smaller, as if the writer had leaned close to the paper:

> *Hazel Voss. Oscar Quill.*

His name. Her name. And beneath the names, a date rendered in the old court reckoning, the format that had fallen out of use before his grandfather was born, translated character by character into the plain modern tongue:

Tonight.

The shelf rearranged around the book while he watched. There was no other way to describe it. The volumes to either side shifted — not much, a finger's width each, a slow settling like books breathing — and the gap that had held the prophecy closed to precisely its thickness, snug as a tooth in a jaw, so that when he tried to return it to where it had stood, it fit as though it had always lived there. As though Stack Seven had been built around it a hundred years ago and had only that moment remembered to make room.

Oscar stood very still in the lamplight and performed the exercise he performed whenever the world misbehaved, which was to reduce it to inventory. One book. Wet ink, aged paper. Names: two. Date: one. Sigil: unknown, possibly decorative, probably not. Explanations available: forgery (insufficient — forgeries do not shelve themselves), enchantment (possible, but who enchanted it, and toward what?), error (his favorite explanation, and the one he clung to the way drowning men are said to cling to driftwood, except that driftwood floats and errors do not).

The lamp guttered. In the Undercroft there was no draft.

He took the book anyway. Rules said uncatalogued items went to the Registrar's cage, and rules were the architecture that kept the guild from eating its own members, but his hands had already decided, closing around the grey leather with a possessiveness that embarrassed him. Some part of him understood, even then, that he was not stealing the book. He was carrying out its instructions. The difference mattered. He wrote it down later so he would not forget that it mattered.

* * *

Hazel arrived an hour after midnight, which meant she arrived exactly when she always arrived, because Hazel Voss was the most punctual person Oscar had ever known, punctual about everything except her own safety. She came down the Undercroft stair with her ledger under her arm and the palace still on her — he could always tell; she carried the banquet hall in her shoulders, the way other people carried weather. Tonight the shoulders were higher than usual. Tonight she looked like a woman walking quickly away from something without running.

"You're here late," she said.

"I'm always here late."

"You're never here *this* late." She set her ledger on the reading table, and he saw that her hands were not entirely steady, and that she had seen something, and that she was deciding — right now, in front of him, with her whole disciplined heart — whether to say it. "Something happened at the banquet."

"I know."

Her eyes came up sharply. "You can't know. I haven't—"

"Show me your hand."

She frowned, but gave it to him, palm up, because they had traded favors long enough that trust between them had stopped being a decision and become a habit. The pad of her thumb was smudged grey.

They looked at each other across the reading table, two people holding the same evidence in different hands, and Oscar felt the strange relief that comes when dread finds company. Whatever was happening, it was not confined to his skull. That was comforting and catastrophic in equal measure.

"The hall emptied," Hazel said quietly. "Mid-toast. Not left — *emptied*. Like water through a sieve. And the candles went out all at once, and there was a book on the dais, open, and—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "I tasted hemlock in the king's cup. Then I tasted my own name. Don't ask me how. It tasted like — iron, and winter, and a word I've been saying since I was born without ever hearing it."

"Sit down," Oscar said. "There's something you need to see. But first I need to show you what the guild actually sells, because until tonight you've only seen the shopfront, and I don't think either of us can afford the shopfront anymore."

* * *

He took her down.

Past the Registrar's cage, where the registrar slept sitting up among her ledgers like a dragon grown old and bureaucratic. Past the Vault of Refused Sales, whose door was bricked over from the inside. Down stairs that were not on any plan of the building, because the guild had bought them from a house that no longer existed and installed them somewhere slightly adjacent to space. Hazel counted the steps aloud under her breath — he heard her lips moving — and he loved her a little for that, for meeting impossibility with arithmetic, and he said nothing, because some things must not be named while they are fragile.

At the bottom was the Master Gallery, and Oscar watched her face as the lamps woke for her, row by row, because everyone's face did the same thing eventually: the skepticism cracked, and underneath it was the expression children wear at the sea.

"Yesterday's rain," he said, gesturing as they walked. "You've seen the shop stock. Up there we sell it by the jar. Down here we keep the vintages — storms that ended before anyone could bottle them at the time, retrieved retroactively. A dead man's shadow, folded into a cedar chest; it walks the walls at night and we have learned not to watch. The last word of a drowned language, kept in a bell jar because it hums if you leave it near music. Doors out of closed countries." He paused at that one. "That's the category you need."

"Doors," Hazel repeated.

"A country closes itself — by war, by decree, by grief, it doesn't matter. Its borders seal, and after a while the sealing becomes total, and the country is no longer difficult to enter but impossible to leave. When that happens, someone always comes to us. And the guild sells them a door." He stopped before a tall case draped in canvas and did not uncover it. "We have sold four such doors in the guild's history. Three worked. The fourth opened onto a wall, and the family who bought it sued us, which tells you something about grief, that it will litigate."

"Vell," Hazel said.

The word dropped between them like a coin down a well.

"Not a border closure," Oscar said. "Worse. Come and see."

The Master Ledger lived on a lectern of black oak at the gallery's center, chained shut, its lock older than the lock's concept. Oscar had clearance — archivists of the third rank could consult the sales record, though almost none did, because the sales record was the guild's conscience, and consciences are heavy reading. He unlocked it. The chain slithered off the oak with a sound like a snake changing its mind.

The ledger opened itself to the relevant page.

That was the second wrong thing that night, and by then Oscar had stopped pretending the night contained right things. The page bore entries in many hands across many decades, the guild's whole commerce of the impossible laid out in columns of debit and credit. And there, under a date a hundred years past, written in a hand so fine it might have been engraved:

> *Item: One (1) country, entire. Designation: VELL.*
> *Condition: Used. Borders intact. Memory load: heavy.*
> *Sold to: [entry effaced].*
> *Price: One anchor, willing.*
> *Note: Buyer to collect upon the centenary. Delivery by sigil.*

"One anchor, willing," Hazel read aloud. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used for tasting, for naming a poison precisely so it could not frighten her. "Someone sold the entire country? Sold it *how*?"

"Not sold. Erased. There's a difference, and the difference is the price." Oscar touched the line of effacement where the buyer's identity had been scraped away — deliberately, recently, the vellum still rough there under his fingertip. "A country isn't land. Land stays. A country is a memory agreed upon by enough people. Carve the agreement out of the founding stone, and the memory unravels. Streets forget their names. People forget mid-sentence. The king forgets himself. It doesn't spread like fire, it spreads like forgetting spreads — everywhere at once and invisibly, until someone notices what's missing, and by then the noticing is going too."

"And the centenary." Hazel's hand had moved to her own ledger, clutching it the way soldiers clutch rifles. "Tonight. The delivery date."

"Tonight," Oscar agreed. "Someone has bought Vell back. Or rather — someone bought it a hundred years ago, with delivery scheduled for tonight, and the sigil on the founding stone is the invoice coming due." He turned the page, because archivists turn pages, because the next fact is sometimes mercy and sometimes the knife, and a man may as well know which. "And there's a clause here I need you to read yourself, because I've read it four times and I don't trust my own eyes anymore."

She read it. He watched her read it. The clause was brief:

> *Buyer's agent authorized to engage guild personnel. Standard rates apply. Loyalty of personnel negotiable per Schedule C.*

"Schedule C?" Hazel said.

Oscar didn't answer immediately, because answering required admitting what he had never said aloud, even to himself, in eleven years of faithful service: that the guild had always kept a schedule of prices for its own people. That every archivist, every registrar, every clerk had a figure attached somewhere in the deep files — not a salary, but a *price*, the sum or the thing for which they could be bought, calculated at hiring and revised annually. His own entry he had glimpsed once, years ago, by accident, and had spent the years since trying to forget.

Because it had not been gold.

His entry had been a name. A boy's name. His brother Tomas, erased from the registry rolls at age nine, struck from every census and christening list, forgotten by their mother mid-lullaby so that she sang the tune with no words in it, and Oscar alone remembering — which was the cruelest shape a memory can take, a song missing its lyrics, a chair missing its sitter. The guild's Schedule C offered, as his price, the restoration of one (1) brother, entire, condition used.

"They know what we want," he said hoarsely. "That's how the guild works, Hazel. We sell people the impossible thing they want most. And the ledger keeps accounts on both sides of the counter."

Hazel looked at him for a long moment. Lamps burned. Somewhere above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone and a layer of reality wearing thin, the city went on forgetting.

"You'd tell me," she said finally. "If they came to you with a price. You'd tell me."

"Of course," Oscar said.

It was not a lie, exactly. It was a door left unlatched, which is how betrayals prefer to travel — not through walls, but through doors someone forgot, or chose, to lock. He heard himself say *of course* and felt the word go into the air between them and stay there, small and round and loaded, like a coin placed on a table that neither of them had agreed to bet.

Above them, faintly, the guild bell rang once, though the shop had been closed for hours.

Neither of them moved to answer it.

Both of them knew, in the way one knows a knock at night, that whoever rang a bell past midnight in a city busy forgetting itself had not come to buy.
