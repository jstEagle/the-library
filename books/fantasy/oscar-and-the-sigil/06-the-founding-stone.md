## 6. The Founding Stone

The cathedral had been dying for longer than anyone in Vell could remember, which was precisely the problem now — no one could remember anything.

Hazel stood at its threshold with her collar turned up against the wind that funneled down the Avenue of Bells, and she counted what was left of the place the way she counted everything: like a ledger. Three walls standing, one open to the grey sky like a ribcage peeled back. A bell tower with no bell. Arches that held up nothing but weather. The stained glass had gone out of the windows a century ago or yesterday; it was impossible to say anymore, and every hour it grew harder to be sure there had ever been a difference between those two dates.

Behind her, Oscar Quill was breathing too fast. She could hear it over the wind — the quick, shallow rhythm of a man whose profession was knowing things and who had just watched most of them walk off the edge of the world.

"The guild records say the crypt is intact," he said. "The founding stone was moved below ground in — " He stopped. His mouth worked around the shape of a date and found nothing there to hold. "In some year," he finished quietly. "Some year at all."

"Say it anyway," Hazel told him. "Even if it's wrong."

"It's not wrong. It's *gone*. There's a difference." He came up beside her, and she saw that his hands were ink-stained to the second knuckle, as they always were, as if he'd been gripping his pens hard enough to break them on the walk across the city. "A forgery can be wrong. An erasure isn't even wrong. It just isn't."

She wanted to tell him not to talk like that. She wanted to tell him that words were load-bearing, that she'd spent her whole life believing a thing could be survived if you could name it — hemlock, nightshade, the slow sweet rot of oleander honey — and that the naming was half the antidote. But the street signs behind them had been blank all morning, white rectangles of wood where letters used to live, and a woman on the corner had forgotten her own child's face mid-sentence, standing there with her mouth open like a door into an empty room. Words were not load-bearing today. Today they were leaves.

So instead of comforting him, Hazel did what she always did. She took inventory.

"We have," she said, "one lantern, half a loaf, my ledger, your master book, the royal seal we haven't earned yet, and each other. That's more than the city has."

Oscar looked at her sideways. In the flat light his face was all angles and exhaustion, but something in it steadied. "You make survival sound like arithmetic."

"Survival *is* arithmetic. Everything else is garnish." She stepped through the cathedral's broken door, and her boots crunched on glass that might once have been saints.

* * *

Inside, the air changed. It was colder than the wind justified, and older, and it tasted faintly of stone dust and tallow and — this was the part that made the back of her neck prickle — of nothing at all. Hazel had spent fifteen years training her tongue to find meaning in the smallest traces: arsenic's ghost of almond, the metallic whisper of antimony, the way hemlock announced itself like a guest who believed himself welcome. Her palate could read a room the way Oscar read a shelf.

This room read as blank. Not empty — blank. Empty meant something had been here and left. Blank meant something had been here and been *taken*, root and record both.

"The crypt stairs are under the choir," Oscar said, holding the lantern high. Its light slid along walls where frescoes should have been, and were not, and where the plaster bore outlines — shadows of images burned into the surface like the memory of a memory. Here the suggestion of a crown. There, the negative space where a figure had knelt. The paintings hadn't faded. They'd been *excised*, cleanly, the way a careful surgeon takes a tumor, leaving the healthy tissue around it untouched so you understand exactly how much is missing.

Hazel touched one of the outlines. Under her fingertips the plaster was smooth and cold and utterly ordinary, and that ordinariness frightened her more than any poison ever had. Poison fought you. This didn't even notice you were there.

"Hazel." Oscar's voice had gone strange.

She turned. He was standing at the mouth of the choir, and the lantern light was falling down a stairwell that shouldn't have been lit — because far below, at the bottom of steps no one had climbed in a hundred years, something was glowing. Faintly. Patiently. The color of ink drying.

"That's not possible," Oscar said. "There's no light source down there. There's no *anything* down there."

"There's a date written in ash with our names in it," Hazel said. "We stopped trading in possible three streets ago." She took the lantern from him because his hands were shaking, and she went first, because someone had to, and because she had learned long ago that fear was just another flavor and she had trained herself to swallow all of them.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the building above could account for. Hazel counted them out of habit — forty-one, forty-two — and lost count somewhere past sixty when the walls began to change. The rough crypt masonry gave way to dressed stone, then to carved stone, then to stone so old its carvings had worn into suggestions: wheat sheaves, rivers, a procession of figures carrying something between them on poles, generation after generation of them descending through the rock like a single unbroken sentence.

"They carried it down," Oscar breathed behind her. "Look — the whole history of Vell, and every era, they're bringing the stone lower. Protecting it. Passing it hand to hand." His voice cracked on the last word. "Passing it hand to hand for a hundred years, and then everyone forgot what their hands were for."

At the bottom was a chamber the size of a modest chapel, and in its center, on a plinth of black basalt, sat the founding stone.

It was smaller than Hazel had imagined. She had pictured something monolithic, a boulder, a monument. Instead it was the size of a millstone, round-edged, worn smooth by centuries of touching hands, and it glowed with that drying-ink light from within, as though the dark inside it had been poured recently and hadn't set.

And across its face, cut deep and clean and only *half-finished*, ran the sigil.

She knew it instantly, the way you know your own name — and the knowing arrived with a horror attached, because she had never seen it before and yet every line of it was familiar as handwriting. Interlocking circles. A vertical stroke through them like a blade or a spine. Radiant marks at the cardinal points, each one shaped exactly like the tally-marks she made in her own ledger when a poison had been survived. Half the sigil was complete, its grooves still sharp. The other half waited in pale outline, sketched but uncut, unfinished, *waiting*, and the glow pooled in the completed portion like ink pooling in a pen.

Oscar made a sound low in his throat. "That's the Sigil of Erasure. From the prophecy book. It's — it's identical. Line for line." He circled the plinth without touching it, the way you circle a sleeping animal. "Half-carved. Why would anyone carve half?"

"Because the other half needs something," Hazel said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Nothing in this city gets left half-done by accident. Look at the tally marks, Oscar. Count them."

He counted. His lips moved. "Nine," he said. Then again, slower, as if hoping the number would behave differently. "Nine completed points. And nine spaces left empty."

Nine poisons she had survived. Nine entries in her ledger, nine scars, nine small deaths she had drunk her way back from. She had never told him the exact number. No one knew it but her and the dead apothecary who'd trained her.

"Don't," Oscar said, very quietly. He had seen her face. "Don't do the arithmetic. I know that look. You're doing the arithmetic."

"I'm a taster. Arithmetic is what keeps me alive." But her hand was rising toward the stone, and she watched it rise as if it belonged to someone else, drawn the way water is drawn downhill, the way the tongue is drawn to a flavor it cannot place.

"*Hazel.*"

"If it's going to take me either way," she said, "I'd rather taste it first."

She laid her palm flat against the founding stone.

* * *

The cold went through her skin and kept going, past muscle, past bone, into whatever it is that a person is made of underneath all the anatomy. For one endless instant she felt nothing — felt *blankness*, the same terrible blankness as the air above, as the scraped frescoes, as the signless streets — and she thought, with a strange professional calm, *so this is what erasure feels like from the inside; it feels like the moment before a flavor arrives.*

Then her mother's voice said, "On the centenary, the country will forget itself, and two shall stand outside the forgetting."

Hazel's knees nearly went.

Her mother had been dead eleven years. Her mother had died in a rented room above a dyer's shop with her daughter's hand in hers, and her last coherent words had been about the correct ratio of salt to ash in bread for mourning, and in eleven years Hazel had never once heard that voice again except in dreams, and the dreams had never sounded like *this* — this was not memory's soft approximation. This was the voice itself, warm and slightly roughened at the edges, the voice that had sung harvest songs off-key and recited recipes like scripture and taught her, cup by cup, diluted drop by drop, how to survive everything the world could pour into a body.

"On the centenary," the voice said again, coming from everywhere, from the stone, from her own blood, "the country will forget itself, and two shall stand outside the forgetting. One who has died nine times and carries the ledger of survivals. One who trades in what cannot exist. Between them they hold the door. Through the door, one. Beside the door, one. The stone remembers what the stone is given, and the stone is given—"

The voice stopped. Not faded. Stopped, mid-word, like a page torn out.

Hazel snatched her hand back. The sigil's glow pulsed once, twice, and settled into its patient simmer, and in the silence afterward she became aware that Oscar was saying her name, had probably been saying it for some time.

"*Hazel.* Your face. What did it show you?"

"Not show." Her own voice sounded foreign to her. "Told. It told me the prophecy. In my mother's voice." She wrapped her arms around herself, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not permit it. "Word for word. The phrasing, the pauses — Oscar, I've heard that recitation cadence my entire life. She used it for recipes. She used it for prayers. She used it for—" She stopped, because the next word was enormous and she could feel it leaning on the door of her mouth.

"For what?" Oscar asked gently.

"She used it for the family book." Hazel closed her eyes. "Every house in the Voss line kept one. A recipe book, we called it, but it wasn't only recipes. Remedies. Tolerances. Which child could stomach which bitter thing, and at what age, and how the dose was raised year over year. My mother called it our inheritance. I thought she meant the immunity. I thought the book was about *surviving*." She opened her eyes and looked at the half-cut sigil glowing in the dark like a wound that hadn't decided whether to bleed. "It was about preparing us. Generation after generation, dose after dose, a hundred years of women teaching their daughters' bodies to die small deaths and come back. We weren't keeping the recipes against misfortune. We *were* the recipe. The whole bloodline was one long preparation, and the dish it was making—"

"—is you," Oscar finished. He had gone very pale. In the sigil-light his face looked carved, another figure excised from a wall. "Nine survivors' marks. Nine points on the stone. Hazel, your family didn't write *about* the prophecy. Your family wrote the prophecy. They wrote it into themselves, one poisoned cup at a time, until the prophecy had a body to live in."

"And a date," Hazel said. "Tonight."

The word hung in the cold air between them, and neither of them reached for it.

She should have wept. Some distant part of her observed that a person ought to weep at a moment like this — learning that her mother's love had been also a weapon forged in her flesh, that every spoonful of bitter medicine administered with a kiss on the forehead had been a line of a spell being copied out in the only ink that lasted, which was bone. But the grief that rose in her was not the kind that spills. It was the kind that sets, like ink, like concrete, like the dried dark in the sigil's grooves. It filled her up evenly and made her heavier and clearer, and she understood, standing in the hundred-year dark beneath a country that was forgetting itself, that this was simply the newest entry in the ledger.

*Entry the tenth,* she thought. *Administered by inheritance. Antidote unknown.*

"Hazel," Oscar said. "Your hand."

She looked down. Where her palm had touched the stone, the skin was marked — faintly, in the exact color of drying ink, with a single radiant mark. The tenth point of the sigil, printed on her like a seal pressed into wax.

Above them, muffled by sixty feet of rock, the city made a sound she had never heard a city make: a long, sliding sigh, like a great book settling shut.

Oscar grabbed the lantern. "The guildhall," he said. "If anywhere in Vell still knows its own name, it's there — and if it doesn't, I need to see it go with my own eyes, because I am not—" His voice failed and reassembled. "I am not ready to find out from a distance."

Hazel took one last look at the founding stone, at the eight remaining empty spaces waiting in their pale outline like unspoken words, and she understood with total certainty that the stone was not waiting for a carver.

It was waiting for her to finish reading it.

She turned and climbed toward the dying light, her marked palm curled shut at her side like something precious, or poisonous, or both.
