## 7. Immunity Is a Kind of Wound

The guildhall cellar had a lamp that burned without oil. Oscar had never explained it, and Hazel had stopped asking, because some of the guild's inventory was best left unexamined — like the jar of yesterday's rain on the third shelf, or the dead man's shadow folded into a drawer like a black silk scarf. The lamp simply burned, and its light was the color of honey held up to a dying sun, and it was, she had come to feel, the only honest light left in Vell.

She sat at the long table with her ledger open before her and her sleeves rolled to the elbow, because tonight she was not reading it.

Tonight she was reading herself.

* * *

The ledger was a plain thing to look at — calfskin gone soft as an old glove at the corners, its spine re-sewn twice with waxed thread that didn't quite match. Inside, in her own hand, tight and neat as stitching: every poison she had ever taken, dated, dosed, described. It was the professional record of a royal food-taster, the document that proved her worth to a court that would otherwise have let her die nameless in a ditch for the crime of being expendable.

But it was also something else. It was a map of her body's history, written from the outside in.

She turned back to the first page, the one she almost never looked at, and read the earliest entry in a hand younger than her own, shakier, still learning how to hold a pen like a person who expected to live long enough to fill a book.

*Nightshade, tincture, four drops.* Age eleven. *Tongue numb by the count of forty. Vision doubled. Heart ran like a rabbit in a snare until dawn. Slept two days. Woke hungry.*

She touched her tongue to the roof of her mouth, an old habit, checking. Nothing there now but the ghost of a memory — the way nightshade made candlelight bloom into halos, the way her mother had sat beside her bed all night grinding more antidote than any child should need, muttering the words under her breath like a prayer or a recipe. Hazel had thought, for years afterward, that it was a prayer. She knew better now. Her mother hadn't prayed. Her mother had *measured.*

That was the inheritance no one spoke of in Vell: some families passed down land, some passed down names. The Voss women passed down tolerance. A body trained, generation by generation, to swallow ruin and keep walking.

Hazel turned the page.

*Arsenic, powdered, in almond milk.* Age thirteen. The court had called it an accident; the ledger recorded what the court preferred not to know, which was that accidents in the palace were rarely accidental, and that a taster who survived one became valuable precisely because someone, somewhere, wanted her dead and had failed. *Vomited blood at midnight. Skin peeled from palms like gloves. Nails grew back ridged. They never grew back smooth.*

She looked down at her hands in the lamplight. Even now, fifteen years later, the ridges showed — fine corrugations running root to tip, like the growth rings of a tree that had lived through fire. She had learned to keep them lacquered and ringed so the courtiers wouldn't stare. She had learned to call them *character*. What she meant was: *these are the places where I died a little and did not finish the job.*

That was the truth no one put in the official record. Immunity was not strength. Immunity was scar tissue laid over scar tissue, a wall built out of everything that had already gotten through. Every tolerance in her body had been purchased at full price. The hemlock she could sip now like tea had cost her three days of paralysis at nineteen, her lungs forgetting their work while a physician squeezed air into her by hand, hour after hour, until dawn came gray through the shutters and her diaphragm remembered itself out of sheer stubbornness. The aconite she could hold on her tongue had cost her a winter — her heart stuttering like a moth against glass, her hands too cold to hold a spoon, her mother feeding her broth drop by drop with a patience that looked like love and felt, even then, like training.

A small death for every entry. That was the arithmetic of her life. She had died perhaps forty times, in fractions — a tenth here, a quarter there — and each time the part of her that came back was subtly, permanently changed. Harder where the poison had been. Numb where feeling used to live. There were flavors she no longer tasted at all; whole registers of bitterness had gone silent in her mouth years ago, casualties of the early campaigns. She had told herself this was the price of the work. She had told herself it was noble.

Sitting alone in the lamplight with her own history spread open before her, she allowed, for the first time in a long time, that it might simply be sad.

* * *

The cellar was quiet in the particular way guild cellars were quiet — not empty-quiet but *attended*-quiet, as if the shelves themselves were listening. Somewhere above, the city of Vell went about its evening, and somewhere beyond that evening, if Oscar was right, something was eating the street signs.

Oscar. She turned her teacup — the guild kept decent tea even in an apocalypse, another item of impossible inventory — and thought about him the way she permitted herself to think about very few things: sideways, briefly, with her guard up even in her own head. He had gone to reread the prophecy. He had said he wanted to check the marginalia, which was such an archivist thing to say that she'd almost laughed, and then he'd looked at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere, and she'd understood that the marginalia might contain anything, including her.

So she worked. Work was what she had instead of prayer.

She took a fresh page and began the task she had set herself, the one that had seemed merely strange when she started it and now, entry by entry, felt like reading her own sentence: she was cataloguing not the poisons but the *wounds*. Not what she had swallowed, but what it had taken from her and what it had left behind.

*Entry the first: nightshade.* Left: a halo-vision that returned whenever she had fever, beautiful and useless. Took: nothing she could name, though her mother said she'd laughed less afterward, and Hazel suspected her mother was right.

*Entry the fourth: arsenic.* Left: the ridged nails, the skin of her palms grown thin as letter-paper. Took: her taste for almonds, forever. The court served almond milk at every feast, and she drank it flat and flavorless, tasting only cold.

*Entry the ninth: hemlock.* Left: a heart that beat slow and deliberate, a metronome that refused to be hurried by fear. Took: three days of her nineteenth year, and the illusion that her body belonged to her.

On and on, page after page, the lamplight pooling gold around her wrists. And somewhere past midnight, in the space between one entry and the next, the pattern surfaced — the way patterns do when you stop looking directly at them — and Hazel set down her pen because her hand had begun to shake.

Every wound had closed. Every scar had settled. But more than that: *nothing had taken.* Not once in twenty-six entries, across eleven poisons and fifteen years, had any toxin finished its work. Her body was a country that had repelled every invasion — but not by strength. By surrender and return. Each poison had killed a little of her, and each time what grew back had grown back *around* the poison, incorporating it, making room for it, until the venom was architecture and the architecture was her.

She was not immune despite the deaths. She was immune *because* of them.

And if Oscar was right — if the sigil erased, if it unwrote things from the world the way arsenic unwrote a heartbeat — then what did erasure do to a woman whose entire self was built out of things that had already been unwritten and had come back?

Her heart, that slow deliberate metronome, ticked through the thought.

It couldn't take her. That was the terrible, glittering conclusion assembling itself in the dark of the cellar. The sigil pulled at things, dissolved them, made the world forget them street by street and sign by sign. But Hazel Voss was a ledger of survived endings. Every part of her had already been lost once and had returned with the loss woven into it. You cannot erase what has already died and insisted on coming back. You cannot unwrite a word that has been written over so many times it has become the page.

She was safe. She was, possibly, the only person in Vell who was safe.

And the safety felt exactly like a trap, because she knew — with the same certainty with which she knew the weight of a poisoned cup by its rim — that safety like hers was never free. Someone had paid for it. Her mother, measuring doses into a child's milk with steady hands and wet eyes. Her grandmother, whose recipes she cooked from memory because the written ones had burned. Some ancestor at the beginning of the line, swallowing the first small death so that a girl a hundred years downstream could stand inside a prophecy and be the one thing in the country that the dark could not digest.

Her survival was not an accident. It was an *instrument*, tuned over generations, waiting for the night it would be needed.

She pressed her palms flat against the ledger, ridged nails against soft calfskin, and felt the bitter laugh rise in her chest and die there. All those years she had thought of her immunity as armor she'd forged herself, badge of her own competence, the one thing that made her more than expendable. And it wasn't armor at all. It was a key. Cut long ago, by hands she'd never known, for a lock she'd never seen — and keys don't get to choose which doors they open, or what waits on the other side.

*They will need you to survive,* her mother had said once, near the end, in the delirium of her last illness, and Hazel had assumed it was fever talking. *Remember that surviving is not the same as living, my darling. Remember they are different prices.*

She had remembered the words. She had not, until tonight, understood them.

* * *

She heard his footsteps on the cellar stairs — Oscar walked like a man trying not to disturb books — and she closed the ledger over her finger to mark the place, an old habit, though she knew every entry by heart.

He came down slowly, and in the honeyed light she saw at once that whatever he'd found in the marginalia had rearranged his face. Archivists wore their dread plainly; they weren't practiced at hiding anything except behind citations.

"You look," he said, "like someone who's been reading bad news."

"I've been reading me." She turned the ledger toward him, open to the column of wounds. "Eleven poisons. Twenty-six attempts on my life, counting the kitchen 'accidents.' I have died in fractions more times than I can count, and every fraction came back wrong in useful ways." She kept her voice level, taster's voice, the one that could announce hemlock to a king without spilling the cup. "Your sigil eats things by making the world forget them. But I am made entirely of things the world tried to forget and couldn't. I don't think it can get its teeth into me."

Something moved through his expression — relief, and beneath the relief a shadow she couldn't read, quick as a fish under ice. "That's good," he said. "That's — Hazel, that might be the first good news since the banquet."

"It isn't news." She looked down at her ridged nails, at the thin skin of her palms, at the map of small deaths she wore under her sleeves. "It's an invoice. Somebody paid for this, Oscar. Generations of somebodies. My mother measured poison into my milk when I was eleven years old and sat up all night watching me not die, and she did it on purpose, because she knew — somehow she knew — that someday the country would need a girl who couldn't be erased." Her voice stayed level. Her eyes did not. "I spent my whole life thinking I survived because I was careful. Careful people don't survive hemlock. They just die carefully."

Oscar sat down across from her. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the oil-less lamp burned its honest light between them, and upstairs the city went on forgetting.

"There's something else," he said finally, and the fish-under-ice shadow was back in his face, deeper now. "In the marginalia. About why the sigil was carved the first time. About who—" He stopped. Started again, differently, the way a man steps around a hole in the floor. "There's a buyer, Hazel. Whoever brought the sigil back didn't find it. They *purchased* it. Through the guild."

The cellar's attended silence sharpened.

"Through the guild," Hazel repeated.

"Vell was sold once before. A hundred years ago. It's in the master ledger — the real one, the one downstairs." His hands were flat on the table, steadying themselves. "Someone bought it back. And whoever brokers a sale like that doesn't stay anonymous for long. Not from me."

Hazel looked at him — at this man who traded in impossible things and hoarded guilt the way other men hoarded coin — and felt the trap close one notch tighter around her own heart. Because she had just spent the night learning that her survival was a key, and keys, cut and paid for, are always meant to be *used* by someone. The question that had been forming all night completed itself with a click she felt in her teeth:

If she was the only thing in Vell the sigil could not erase —

— then to someone, somewhere, she was not a person at all.

She was the one ingredient that couldn't spoil.

"Then we'd better find them," she said, "before they find me."

But even as she said it, even as Oscar nodded and rose and reached for the lamp, Hazel felt the slow deliberate metronome of her scarred heart stumble — just once, just slightly — the way it always did before the poison arrived.
