## 8. The Buyer

The guildhall had a smell for every hour, and Oscar Quill had learned them the way other men learned prayers. At dawn it was dust and old vellum, sweet as a church. At noon, ink and tallow. But at dusk — dusk was when the impossible inventory woke — the air turned strange: ozone and dried roses, candle smoke that remembered being a funeral, rain that fell somewhere else entirely. He stood in the Long Gallery at that hour now, cataloguing by feel because he did not trust his eyes anymore. His eyes had been lying to him for three days.

The shelf where the prophecy book lived had rearranged itself twice since Hazel left to feed the king. Once it had simply been *elsewhere* — third row, between a jar of yesterday's thunder and a box of unopened letters addressed to no one — and once it had been *everywhere*, its spine repeated down the whole case like a word whispered too many times until it stopped meaning anything. He had taken it down and locked it in the iron cabinet beneath the reading table, and still he could feel it through the floor, through his boots, through the soles of his feet, humming like a struck bell that would not finish dying away.

He told himself, again, the story he had constructed to survive the telling: *forgery*. A clever one, certainly. Wet ink a century after the fact was a trick any second-rate charlatan could manage with glycerin and patience. The names were coincidence — Hazel Voss was known in certain circles; *Oscar* was common enough; dates were dates. The shelf rearranging itself was draft and old wood settling. The city blanking its own street signs was civil unrest, or weather, or a mass hysteria of the kind that took towns sometimes, like dancing plagues, like laughing epidemics.

He was very good at telling himself stories. It was, in fact, his profession. The Guild of Impossible Things did not sell impossibilities; it sold *accounts* of them, plausible wrappers around things that should not fit inside any wrapper at all. And the first rule of the trade — the rule old Master Corvane had beaten into him with a ruler across the knuckles when he was nine years old and too clever for his own good — was: *never believe your own inventory.*

But tonight the inventory believed in him.

It started with the ledger. The master ledger, the great chained book on its lectern in the center of the Long Gallery, had begun turning its own pages. Not quickly. Not dramatically. Just once an hour, roughly, like a sleeper shifting under blankets, and each time it settled on the same entry, as if it were trying to tell him something in the only language it had.

Entry 4471. *Sold: one (1) country, entire. Vell. Condition: used, but sound. Buyer: anonymous. Price: [entry effaced]. Date: one hundred years ago next midwinter.*

Oscar had read that entry a hundred times in his apprenticeship. It was famous among archivists the way shipwrecks are famous among sailors — a cautionary tale, a horror story told to keep the young ones honest. A country sold out from under itself. The buyer never recorded. The price never recorded. The seller — this was the part that made apprentices lie awake — the seller's name had been scraped off the page with a knife, carefully, completely, leaving only a shallow scar in the vellum shaped like nothing at all.

Tonight, when the ledger turned to Entry 4471 for the fourth time, the scar had changed.

There was writing in it now. Fresh ink, wet at the edges, in a hand Oscar recognized because it was his own — not his current hand but his childhood hand, the round uncertain letters he'd written at eleven, practicing his signature over and over in the margin of an apprentice's practice book. The words in the scar read:

*Ask him what he'd take for it.*

He stood there a long time, listening to the gallery tick and settle around him, listening to the jars murmur their small impossible weathers to one another. Then he did what an archivist does. He got a lamp, and gloves, and tweezers, and he examined the entry the way you examine a wound — closely, without flinching, because flinching is how wounds win.

The ink was not glycerin. It was not any ink he knew. Under the lamp's magnifier it moved, faintly, the way oil moves on water — colors sliding through black, a sheen of something alive. And threaded through it, fine as hair, were fibers of paper that matched the ledger's own vellum exactly. The writing hadn't been added to the page. It had been *grown* into it, the way frost grows into a window. Whatever had written those words had done it from inside the book, from inside the hundred years, from inside the erasure itself.

*Ask him what he'd take for it.*

"Who is *him*?" Oscar said aloud, to the empty gallery, and the gallery answered him.

Not with words. With a knock. One knock, soft, from the far end of the Long Gallery, where the door stood — the guild's oldest door, the one they called the Exit, though no one living remembered exiting through it. It was a door out of closed countries, according to the catalogue. A door that opened onto elsewhere regardless of whether elsewhere consented to exist.

The knock came again. Polite. Patient. The knock of someone who has all night and knows you know it.

Oscar picked up the lamp. He considered, briefly and honestly, fetching Hazel. She was at the palace, feeding a king who might already have forgotten her name, in a city whose streets were forgetting themselves block by block. She was the most important person in the world right now, and also the most endangered, and the last thing either of them needed was for him to leave the guildhall unattended while some stranger knocked politely at the Exit.

Also — and he admitted this to himself with the clinical honesty he applied to damaged folios — he wanted to see who it was *alone*. Because if the broker had come for Hazel, then whatever happened next would happen to her. And if it came for him first, then it was a thing he could stand between. That was the shape his whole life had quietly taken, he realized, standing there in the smell of ozone and roses: he arranged himself between dangerous things and people who mattered. He'd done it with his brother, once, long ago. He had failed. He kept doing it anyway. Hope, Corvane used to say, is just grief that hasn't found its object yet.

He walked the length of the gallery. Two hundred paces. He counted them, because counting was a wall he could build between himself and fear, and the knocking continued at intervals, patient as tide.

When he reached the Exit, he did not open it. First rule of the trade: never open for a knock you didn't send. Instead he put his eye to the peephole — a brass fisheye installed by some cautious master two centuries back, wide enough to show the whole threshold beyond.

There was no one there.

There was a *shape* there. A figure in a grey traveling coat, cut in a style a century out of fashion, standing precisely where a visitor would stand, hands folded, head bowed as if in thought. But where the face should have been — where the lamplight from the gallery should have caught nose and brow and the wet gleam of eyes — there was smoothness. Not shadow. Smoothness. Skin grown over the place where a face goes, seamless as the scar in the ledger, and Oscar felt his stomach drop through the floorboards of himself, because he had seen that smoothness before. In the prophecy book's margins. In the blanked street signs. In the space where Vell's name used to be on maps.

The figure raised its head. Of course it knew he was looking. There were no eyes to meet, and yet he felt *seen*, thoroughly, the way a specimen feels seen under glass.

"Master Quill," it said. Its voice was pleasant. That was the worst part — it was a genuinely pleasant voice, warm, unhurried, the voice of a man who reads aloud to children. "Thank you for receiving me."

"I didn't receive you," Oscar said. "I haven't opened the door."

"No," the figure agreed, and there was something almost tender in its tone. "You never do, do you? Your guild. You build doors out of closed countries and then you stand inside them, cataloguing, and you never once walk through. A hundred years I've waited on thresholds like this one. You are the first to come look through the glass." A pause, courteous, giving him room to be ashamed. "That is why I came to you, Master Quill, and not to the taster. You are the one who understands transactions."

"What do you want?"

"To conclude a purchase." The figure unfolded its hands. From within the grey coat it produced a document — Oscar could see it through the fisheye, could see the wax seal glinting, and his breath caught, because he knew that seal. Every archivist of the guild knew that seal. The interlocked rings, the key with no teeth, the motto worn nearly illegible: *We Sell What Cannot Be Owned.* The guild's own mark, the old seal, the true seal, retired three generations ago when the guild reformed itself after the Vell sale and swore off trading in countries and souls. The figure wore it on a chain, and the seal was *warm* — he could see the heat-shimmer of it, faintly, in the cold corridor beyond the door. As if it had been held in a hand for a hundred years. As if it had never once been set down.

"You are not guild," Oscar said. "The guild retired that seal. Whoever wears it now—"

"—inherited it," the figure said mildly. "As one inherits anything: by outlasting everyone who might contest the claim. I was there when it was last used, Master Quill. I countersigned Entry 4471." The smooth face tilted, and though there was nothing to read in it, Oscar felt the tilt like a change in pressure. "You have questions about that entry. I can see it in how you hold your lamp. Everyone who reads that entry has the same question, and no one ever asks it aloud, because asking makes it real. So let me spare you. You want to know who sold Vell." The figure smiled — there was no mouth, and yet the smoothness shifted, and it was unmistakably a smile. "A member of your guild did. A senior archivist. Someone who stood exactly where you are standing, holding exactly such a lamp, and listened to exactly such a knock."

The floor seemed to tilt. Oscar gripped the doorframe. "That's not in the record."

"The record was amended," said the broker. "By me. I bought the amendment along with everything else. That is what ownership means, Master Quill. It means the past belongs to you too." It drew the document up between them, against the fisheye, so that the wax seal filled Oscar's entire curved view of the world. "But I have not come to discuss history. History concluded a century ago. I have come to discuss *you*."

"Me."

"You, and your heart's price. Every person has one. Most people never learn theirs, which is a mercy. Yours, I am given to understand, is a boy named Tam Quill, aged nine, who went out of the world before his time, and whose name — I will note this carefully, because I deal in names — whose name appears nowhere in any register, census, parish book, or grave roll in this country. Erased, Master Quill. Thoroughly erased. Not by sigil. By *grief*. By a family that could not bear the paperwork of mourning and paid a quiet clerk to unwrite him." The voice softened further, became almost gentle. "I can restore him. Fully. Flesh, memory, future. The boy at your mother's table, laughing, asking you to read to him. I can hand him back to you across this threshold tonight, and no one in the world but you will ever know he was gone, because no one in the world remembers he existed. Except me. And except you."

Oscar did not move. The lamp trembled in his hand, and he watched the light shiver against the doorframe, and he understood with terrible clarity that this was the moment his whole life had been aimed at, the way a river is aimed at the sea without ever deciding anything.

"And the price," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect.

"The taster," said the broker. "Hazel Voss. Delivered to this threshold, willing or otherwise, by midwinter's eve. In exchange: your brother, restored, and — because I admire thoroughness — your guild's debt to history discharged. Entry 4471 closed. The country stays sold, the sigil completes, and everyone in Vell forgets everything gently, painlessly, in their sleep, including you. You wake beside a boy who never died, in a world that never lost a country, and neither of you will carry the weight of knowing. That is not cruelty, Master Quill. That is *merchandise*. Forgetting, well-made, is the finest product there is. I should know. I make it."

There was a silence. In it, Oscar heard the gallery behind him: the jars murmuring, the ledger turning its pages toward Entry 4471 again, the prophecy book humming in its iron cabinet like a kettle near boiling. He heard, beneath all of it, the city — quieter tonight than it had been any night before. Quieter the way a room is quiet when someone has stopped breathing in it.

"My answer," he said slowly, "is no."

The smile on the smooth face deepened. "Of course it is. They all say no first. The archivist who sold Vell said no, the first night, standing right here. Said it beautifully. Said it with tears. And then he spent six nights telling himself stories — you are gifted at that, your guild breeds it into you — and on the seventh night he opened the door." The broker folded the document away, unhurried, and Oscar watched the seal vanish back into the grey coat like a moon going under cloud. "I do not require your yes tonight. I require only that you not say *no*. There is a difference, and you, of all people, know the difference. Refusal is a word. Consent is a direction. I will return. Thresholds are patient. So am I."

And then, before Oscar could speak again, the figure did something worse than any offer. It leaned close to the fisheye — close enough that the smoothness of its face filled the lens, featureless as an unwritten page — and it said:

"A gift, to help you decide. No charge. Consider it a sample of my product." And it breathed on the glass.

Where its breath touched, the brass fogged, and in the fog a picture formed, sharp as an etching: a kitchen. A low-ceilinged kitchen with a crooked chimney corner. A woman at the hearth — Oscar's mother, younger than he had ever been able to remember her, her sleeves rolled, flour on her forearms. And at the table, a boy of nine with a gap in his smile, hunched over a book, listening as an older brother — a boy of twelve with Oscar's own cowlick, his own ink-stained fingers — read aloud, badly, joyfully. Steam rose off the pot. The dog slept under the chair. Everything in the image was warm, and ordinary, and *impossible*, because Oscar had never once been able to picture his brother's face, and here it was, moving, alive, the boy turning to grin at the reader, mouth open around some joke Oscar could almost hear—

The fog cleared. The corridor beyond the door was empty. No figure. No coat. Only the cold, and the dark, and the faint smell of dried roses.

Oscar stood at the Exit for a long time afterward. He did not open it. He did not walk away. He stood with his lamp and his trembling hands and he told himself the story he always told himself — *it lies, it's a broker, brokers lie, the image was stolen from my own longing and painted onto glass, there is no boy, there is no restoration, there is only a trap wearing my grief as a lure* — and the story was true, and it did not help even slightly. Because the image had been accurate. The kitchen was exact. The cowlick was exact. The broker had not invented his brother; it had *found* him, in whatever archive holds the unwritten, and it had shown him a page he had spent twenty years pretending was blank.

*I refuse,* he told himself. *I said no.*

*You said no,* something in him answered, in his own childhood handwriting, *but you did not close the account.*

He climbed the stairs to the scriptorium and sat down at his desk and pulled a fresh sheet toward him, because when a man cannot trust his mind he must trust the record, and the record was his religion. He wrote, in his steadiest hand:

*On this night a broker wearing the guild's old seal offered me the restoration of my brother Tam in exchange for delivering Hazel Voss to the Exit by midwinter's eve. I refused. I did not accept. I did not decline to hear more. Let the record show all three.*

He read it back. It was honest. That was the problem. Every word was true, and together they described a man standing at the top of a stair he had not yet decided not to descend.

Below, in the gallery, the master ledger turned another page on its own, and settled, and waited.

And somewhere across the darkening city, in a palace where a king was learning to be hungry without remembering why, a woman who tasted poison for a living was lifting a cup to her lips — and Oscar, sitting alone above his own confession, felt the first cold edge of a thought he would spend the rest of this story trying not to think:

*If I do nothing, the sigil takes her anyway. If I do something, I take her myself. Either way she is handed over. The only question left is who gets to be the one who did it — and whether that person can live in the world that remains.*

He blew out the lamp. In the dark, the iron cabinet hummed, and hummed, and did not stop.

* * *

Down in the Long Gallery, past midnight, the ledger turned to Entry 4471 one final time before dawn. The scar in the vellum had grown new words again, in that same wet, living ink, in a child's careful hand:

*Seven nights, Master Quill. The previous archivist needed seven. I have taken the liberty of reserving yours.*

Oscar read it by the light of a single candle he did not remember lighting, and understood, finally, what kind of merchant he was dealing with.

One who counted.
