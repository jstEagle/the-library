## 9. Recipes for Remembering

The bakery on Larkspur Row had been dead for six years, but its ovens remembered how to breathe.

Hazel found it by smell before she found it by sight — a ghost of char and old bread rising through the cracks of a shuttered door, the way a body remembers warmth after the heart has stopped. She had passed this shop a thousand times in her life as a taster, cataloguing it without knowing: *Marrow & Rye, proprietress deceased, ovens cold, windows soaped against creditors.* Now the soap was flaking off the glass in pale curls, and through the gaps she could see the long wooden peel still hanging by the hearth like a sleeping bird.

"Here," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

Oscar stopped in the middle of the street, where the cobbles had begun to go soft at the edges — not melting, exactly, but *unsure*, the way a word goes unsure when you repeat it too many times. He was carrying the prophecy book wrapped in his coat against his chest, the way one carries an infant or a bomb.

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure it has an oven," Hazel said. "And I'm sure that right now, in all of Vell, an oven is the most dangerous thing we own."

She shouldered the door. It gave with a sigh of rot and flour dust, and the dark inside smelled of everything the city outside was forgetting: yeast, smoke, butter gone rancid and then gone holy with age, the mineral sweetness of stone that had been heated ten thousand mornings. Somewhere in the walls, mice had made their peace with history. Hazel lit the lamp she'd taken from the guildhall — one of Oscar's impossible things, a lamp that burned yesterday's light, soft and slightly out of season — and set it on the counter where the ledger of orders used to lie.

The order ledger was still there. She opened it. The last entry, in a hand she didn't know, read: *Twelve seed rolls for the widow Ansel's wake. Paid in kind.* Beneath it, nothing. Above it, a hundred years of small hungers, all of them answered.

"Well," Hazel said, to the mice, to Oscar, to the dead baker whose ghost she could feel standing just behind her left shoulder, disapproving of the dust. "We're open."

* * *

Her grandmother had taught her to cook the way other grandmothers taught prayers: by rote, by repetition, by refusing to explain until the child's hands knew what the mouth would never be able to say. *Feel the dough,* she'd say, pressing Hazel's palms into the wet warmth of it. *Don't think about the dough. Thinking is for people who've already forgotten.*

Hazel had been eight. She had thought it was only baking.

Now she stood in a dead bakery while a country dissolved around her, and she understood that her grandmother had been building an ark, plank by plank, recipe by recipe, and had never once said so.

The ovens took an hour to coax alive. There was coal in the cellar, black and patient, and kindling from a crate that had waited out its whole usefulness unburned, and Hazel knelt at the fire-mouth feeding it piece by piece, feeling the heat come up through the floor like a pulse returning. Behind her, Oscar moved through the pantry reading labels aloud in a low voice, an archivist even now, inventorying the end of the world: *flour, weeviled but sound; salt —* he paused — *salt, real salt, thank every god; honey crystallized to amber glass; dried plums; a crock of rendered fat sealed with wax, blessed be whoever did that.*

"And this," he said, and held up a bundle of cloth tied with string, gone grey with age.

Hazel looked. Her hands went still in the flour bin.

"That's hers," she said. "That's her spice bundle. Clove and black pepper and something she called crowleaf because she wouldn't tell anyone its real name." She wiped her hands on her apron without thinking — when had she put on an apron? — and crossed the room and took the bundle from him, and the string crumbled under her thumb, and the smell rose up out of the cloth like a voice rising out of sleep.

Clove. Pepper. Crowleaf. And underneath, faint as a watermark: cardamom, which her grandmother bought once a year from a trader who came over the mountains, and which she hid from her neighbors because a woman was allowed one extravagance and no witnesses.

Hazel stood in the ruined bakery holding a dead woman's spices and felt the erasure reach for her and *miss*, snagging on the scent the way a sleeve snags on a nail.

"Oscar," she said. "It can't take this."

He came to stand beside her. Through the soaped window, down the length of Larkspur Row, they watched a lamplighter forget the middle of his rounds — not fall, not cry out, simply stop, his pole half-raised, his face going smooth as an egg while the lamp above him guttered. Then the street itself seemed to lean toward the bakery, toward the smell beginning to gather in the warm air, and the lamplighter's face unsmoothed. He blinked. He finished lighting the lamp. He walked on, frowning, touching his own cheek as if checking that it belonged to him.

"The smells hold," Oscar said quietly. "Memory is something you feed people." He said it like a man testing whether a bridge will bear him. "Your grandmother's recipes. They're anchored somewhere the sigil doesn't reach."

"They're anchored in bodies," Hazel said. "In tongues. In the gut." She untied her apron and retied it, tighter. "You can't erase what someone has eaten. It's already become them."

She began to cook.

* * *

She started with the bread, because her grandmother had always started with the bread, and because bread was the longest argument a kitchen ever had with time.

Flour, water, salt, the wild yeast still living in the walls of the place itself — she scraped the bins and the crevices and coaxed a starter back from six years of silence, and while it woke she worked the dough with her whole forearms, the way she'd been taught, folding and turning, folding and turning, until the dough went from shaggy to smooth to *alive*, springing back against her knuckles with something like opinion.

Oscar sat on a flour sack and watched her, and after a while he began to talk, because people always talked in kitchens, because kitchens were where the truth kept its coat.

"My mother baked," he said. "Badly. Every week. Brick after brick. We ate them anyway." He turned the prophecy book over in his hands without opening it. "My brother used to trade our loaves to the neighbor for hers. Tam. He was good at trades. That was his whole genius — he could see what a thing wanted to be worth." His voice caught on the name and smoothed itself over, too practiced. "You know he's not in any record. Not one. I've looked for eleven years. It's as if Vell agreed with whoever erased him."

"Then Vell owes you," Hazel said. "Debts survive erasure. That's practically the guild's founding principle."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly folded away. But he laughed.

The first loaves went into the oven when the coals had gone the color of fox fur, and while they baked Hazel turned to the dishes that mattered more. She rendered the ancient fat slow and low, skimming it clean, and in it she fried onions until they went from white to gold to the deep brown-black that her grandmother had called *the color of patience*. Into that: the clove and pepper and crowleaf, crushed in her palm. Into that: the dried plums, softened in water that tasted of the crock's hundred-year patience. Over all of it, the last of the salt — real salt, thank every god — measured with a miser's care and a lover's hand.

The stew filled the bakery the way water fills a hull. Not the room — the *building*, wall to wall, up the stairwell, into the rafters where the mice fell silent and listened. It was a poor man's dish, a dish for the end of the month and the end of the year, a dish her grandmother had made on the night Hazel's mother left home and never came back, and it carried all of that in it, grief and sugar and smoke, and it went out through the soaped windows and down Larkspur Row like a rumor with legs.

The first knock came before the bread was done.

* * *

They came the way people come to a wake: slowly, apologetically, certain they have no right and unable to stay away.

The lamplighter first, still holding his pole, still touching his own face. Then the widow Ansel — impossibly, the widow Ansel, whose wake-bread was the last entry in the dead ledger, a small straight-backed woman of ninety-odd who looked at Hazel and said, "I know that smell. I buried my husband fifty years ago and I know that smell," and then stood in the doorway crying without any change in her expression, the way the very old cry, economically, having long since paid off their tears.

Then more. A cobbler with his awl still in his fist. Two sisters who had been forgetting each other's names an hour ago and now stood holding each other's sleeves. A boy of maybe ten who had wandered in from a street that no longer had a name, drawn down Larkspur Row "like there was a rope," he said, "a rope made of dinner."

Hazel fed them.

She fed them the way she had learned to feed kings — attentively, watching each face for the moment the flavor landed, the way a taster watches for poison. Only now she watched for the opposite: for the flicker of return, the widening of the eyes, the sudden grip on a companion's arm. *I know this. I know this. My mother made this. We had this at the harvest fair, there were paper lanterns, the river smelled like —*

"Like tar and sugar," the cobbler said suddenly, staring at nothing. "The river at festival. Tar and sugar and wet wool. I haven't thought of that in forty years and I could draw it for you now, every stall."

"Say it again," Hazel told him. "Say it to your neighbor. Make her say it back."

And he did, and the sisters did, and the widow Ansel began telling the story of her husband's wake in a voice like a bell with a crack in it, and Hazel understood what she was doing, what her grandmother had known: that memory was not a possession but a *dish*, that it spoiled if kept, that it multiplied only when served, that a country lived or died at tables and nowhere else.

The bread came out of the oven black-crusted and hollow-sounding, perfect. She broke the first loaf and gave the heel to the boy, because her grandmother had always given the heel to the youngest, and the boy ate it with his eyes closed, and when he opened them he said, "There's a king. I saw him once. He waved at me from a balcony and dropped his glove and everyone pretended not to see and he laughed—" and stopped, frightened by his own remembering, and Hazel put a second piece in his hands and said, "Keep going."

Outside, the dark pressed against the soaped windows. Down the row, another lamp went out — not blown, just *unremembered*, its flame losing its reason. But within the radius of the smell, within the warm border of clove and woodsmoke and browned onion, the street held. People held. They sat shoulder to shoulder on flour sacks and upturned crates, eating a dead woman's recipes in a dead woman's kitchen, and for a few hours the sigil's tide broke against them like weather against a lighthouse.

Oscar moved among them with the lamp, refilling bowls, and Hazel caught him once standing very still, watching the boy talk with his mouth full, and the look on his face was the look of a man doing arithmetic he hates. She knew that arithmetic. She was doing it herself.

*A few hours. This buys a few hours. What does it buy us past that?*

Duty said: run for the door, the guild's exit, the way out. Love said: ladle the stew, there are eleven bowls and eleven mouths and the eleventh belongs to a boy who remembers a king's laugh.

Love, Hazel decided, could win until dawn. Dawn was when duty got its turn.

* * *

It was near midnight, the neighbors dozing in a heap like cats, the ovens banked to a red murmur, when Hazel scrubbed the big table and spread out her ledger — her true ledger, the poison-book, the record of everything she had survived — and beside it laid her grandmother's spice bundle, and between them she placed a clean sheet of paper.

If memory was something you fed people, then it needed a cookbook. If Vell was going to live in her body, then her body needed instructions.

She wrote the way her grandmother had spoken: in imperatives, in measurements that trusted the reader's hands. *Onions until they give up. Salt like you mean it but not like you're angry. Fold until the dough stops being afraid of you.* The plum stew. The festival bread with the tar-and-sugar glaze. The wedding soup with the dumplings her mother had loved, which was why her grandmother had made it the night her mother left, feeding the leaving, feeding the grief, feeding the daughter who stayed.

Halfway down the page her pen slowed.

Because she had noticed, in the last hour, tasting the stew before serving it — a taster cannot help tasting — that something was missing. Not an ingredient. A *sense*. The stew had been right, the neighbors had wept, but Hazel herself had stood over the pot aware of a flatness, a missing note, the way a chord sounds when one string has gone dead.

She dipped her finger in the last of the broth and touched it to her tongue and waited.

Salt. There was salt in it — she had added it, she had measured it, the crystals were visible in the spoon. But on her tongue, where salt had lived all her life, loud and bright and essential, there was only a memory of salt. A description of salt. The word, not the taste.

The sigil was eating her senses. One by one. Starting, of course, with the one she loved most.

Hazel sat very still in the warm ruin of the bakery, listening to the neighbors breathe, feeling the oven's heat on her back like a hand between her shoulders. On the table, her grandmother's spice bundle sat open, and the smell of crowleaf rose up steady and stubborn, and Hazel thought: *then I will write it where it cannot be erased. Not on paper. Paper burns. Not in the ledger. Ledgers get lost.*

She looked at her own forearms, bare below the rolled sleeves, the skin mapped already with the faint scars of a hundred survived poisons — her ledger written in the oldest ink there was.

"Oscar," she said softly, across the sleeping room. "Do we still have that needle kit from the guild? And the ink?"

He looked up from the prophecy book, and whatever he saw in her face made him close it carefully, keeping his thumb in it, marking a page.

"Yes," he said. "Why? What are you going to do?"

Hazel picked up the needle and threaded it, and outside, far down the darkening row, something vast and patient turned its attention toward the smell of bread.

"I'm going to make sure," she said, "that Vell fits in a body."
