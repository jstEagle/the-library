## 10. The Door That No Longer Exists

The guildhall had grown quieter in the way a held breath is quiet.

Hazel felt it the moment they came up out of the bakery's cellar passage — not silence, exactly, but a listening quality to the air, as though the building were straining to remember what its own rooms were for. The corridor lamps still burned, but their light fell strangely now, pooling at her feet instead of reaching the walls. She counted doorways on the walk from the stair to the archive and got eleven, then thirteen, then nine, depending on which direction she walked.

Oscar walked beside her with his coat pulled close, and inside that coat, she knew, was a folded page he had been keeping his thumb in since the night of the bread. He thought she hadn't noticed. He had been keeping his thumb in things his whole life — ledgers, doors, secrets — as if pressure alone could hold them shut.

She said nothing. The needle-marks on her forearms ached under her sleeves, twenty-one small inscriptions in ink mixed with her own blood, each one a recipe, each one a promise: *I am the ledger now. I am the country's memory, carried in a body.* The sigil could eat salt, could eat sweetness, could eat the taste of rain on hot stone. It could not eat what had been written into flesh that had already died and returned.

"Master Quill," she said, when they reached the great archive doors. "Open your impossible inventory."

He looked at her for a long moment — that archivist's look, cataloguing damage — and then he set his shoulder to the doors.

* * *

The vault beneath the guildhall was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. It was the cold of a place where time moved slowly enough to be seen, like syrup poured over glass. Shelves rose into darkness on every side, and on those shelves sat the guild's true trade: jars of yesterday's rain, still falling softly against their glass; a dead man's shadow folded flat as linen; a bell that rang only for people who had forgotten something important; and, at the far end of the central aisle, alone on a plinth like an altar, the Door.

It was not large. That was the first thing Hazel noticed, and it undid something in her — all this terror, all this erasure, and the way out was a plain oak door with a brass handle worn dull by a century of hands that no longer existed. It stood free-standing, framed and hinged, opening onto nothing but the dark air of the vault.

"It doesn't lead anywhere," Oscar said quietly. "Not by itself. A door is just a decision made of wood. What it leads to depends on where you stand when you open it." He touched the frame the way another man might touch a relic, or a wound. "The guild bought this from a woman who walked out of a country that had already been erased. She sold it because it stopped working the moment her feet left home soil. A door out of a closed country — the rarest thing we ever stocked."

"And now?"

"Now Vell is being erased around us while we stand in it. So the door has nothing to open onto. Unless—" He stopped. His hand curled back from the wood.

"Unless," Hazel said. "Say the rest of it, Oscar. You're better at fine print than anyone I've ever known."

He turned to face her fully then, and in the lamplight she saw how hollowed he had become, how many nights of the prophecy book lived in the lines of his face. "An anchor," he said. "The door needs one end of itself fixed outside time. Someone standing beyond the erasure, holding the far side open, so there is somewhere *to* go. The broker—"

He stopped again, and this time the stop was different, heavier, and Hazel understood with a cold settling in her stomach that they had arrived at last at the thing he had been carrying since the market square, since the faceless man in the guild's old seal had spoken to him in the dark.

"Oscar," she said. "What did you trade?"

* * *

He told her standing up, because he could not have borne to tell it sitting down.

"The night the street signs went blank," he began, "the first night, after we ran from the palace — I went back for the master ledger. You were watching the door. And someone was waiting in the aisle between the poison indexes. He wore the guild's founder seal, the old one, the one we retired eighty years ago because—" his voice thinned— "because the men who used it stopped being men."

"He offered you something."

"He offered me my brother." The words came out of him like a tooth coming loose — relief and agony together. "Tobias. My brother died when we were children. Fever. He was seven and I was nine, and I have spent thirty years trading in impossible things because I wanted one so badly, and there he was, or the shape of him, standing behind the broker with color in his cheeks and our mother's eyes, and the broker said: *Give us the taster, and the remembered Vell will have room in it for one boy more.*"

The vault was very still. Somewhere above them, faintly, the guildhall creaked through its diminishing rooms.

"I refused," Oscar said. "Out loud, I refused. I want you to know that. But I didn't say no. There's a difference, and the difference is a whole language, and brokers speak it fluently. He heard the part of me that wanted it. He heard everything I didn't say, and he filed it away, and he has been patient ever since, because he knows — he *knows*, Hazel — that eventually I would come to you with this, or I wouldn't, and either way he wins. If I betray you, he wins. If I confess and you can't forgive me, the trust breaks and the door breaks with it, because the door needs two anchors and one of them has to be willing." His breath shuddered. "That's why I hid the page. That's why I've kept my thumb in this book for three days like a coward pressing on a wound. Because the prophecy says two may walk out, and I have been praying with everything in me that it means both of us, and I have been afraid to read closely enough to find out otherwise."

"Oscar." Her voice came out steadier than she expected, and she was grateful to whatever poison-toughened thing in her chest produced it. "Stop talking."

He stopped.

"You looked at your dead brother and wanted him alive. Anyone who wouldn't want that isn't someone I could trust; they'd be someone broken in a worse place than you are." She stepped closer, and took his hands — the archivist's hands, ink-stained, trembling. "You confessed before I asked. You stood here shaking rather than let me walk into that door trusting a lie. Do you understand what that costs a man who trades in secrets? You gave me the most expensive thing in this entire inventory."

His eyes were bright and wet. "And if I'd said yes? If I'd handed you over?"

"Then I would forgive that too," she said simply. "Because forgiveness isn't a payment for good behavior, Oscar. It's a recipe. You make it ahead of time, before you need it, or it's no good to anyone." She squeezed his hands once, hard, and let go. "Now stop crying and help me figure out how a door works."

* * *

It took them until the small hours.

Oscar fetched the master ledger — the real one, the century-old book whose margins recorded every sale the guild had ever made, including, a hundred years past, in faded brown ink: *One (1) country, Vell, complete. Sold to private buyer. Terms: remembrance withheld. Buyer to collect upon centenary.*

"Remembrance withheld," Hazel murmured. "They didn't just buy the land. They bought the forgetting."

"They bought the *right* to forget it," Oscar corrected grimly. "There's a clause-difference. Which means—" he turned pages with sudden urgency— "which means the forgetting itself is contractual. Contracts can be breached. Breach requires evidence. Evidence requires a record that exists *outside* the contract." He looked up slowly. "Hazel. What did you write on your arms?"

"Twenty-one recipes. Grandmother's dishes. In blood-ink." She pushed back her sleeve; the small dark letters caught the lamp-glow. "Why?"

"Because a contract cannot erase its own breach. And a body that has survived death twenty-one times is outside the contract — outside time's ordinary claim." He was almost laughing now, a wild, exhausted sound. "You're not just carrying the memories. You're carrying the *proof*. You are the discrepancy in the ledger. The door doesn't need to open onto Vell. It needs to open onto *you*."

They worked through the deep hours of the vault, Oscar reading the door's acquisition notes aloud while Hazel laid her palms against the oak. The wood was warm under her hands — warmer than it should have been, warmer than the cold vault justified, like something sleeping that had begun to dream of being used. The brass handle bore scratches, tiny ones, and Oscar found the note about those too: *Scratches on handle consistent with fingernails. Previous owner opened this door while her country was disappearing. Recommend: do not sell to anyone with family remaining.*

"We qualify on both counts," Hazel said dryly.

"Hazel." Oscar's voice changed, and she turned. He was holding the prophecy book, the page he'd guarded with his thumb finally open, finally surrendered. "Before we try this. You should read it. All of it. I won't hide anything from you anymore — not even the parts I'm afraid of."

She read.

*Two shall stand at the door that does not exist. Two shall carry the keys: the seal of the king who forgot, and the blood of the line that wrote. One shall cross. One shall remain, and become the stone, and hold the country's ghost together with his name, until remembrance returns to feed the sigil what it starved for.*

The words seemed to darken slightly as she finished them, though the lamp had not moved.

"One shall cross," Hazel repeated.

"Yes."

"One shall remain."

"Yes."

"And become the stone." She looked at him across the vault, across the little door with its scratched handle, and felt the full weight of what he was offering settle over them both like snow. "Oscar Quill. Were you going to tell me which one of us the prophecy meant?"

He met her eyes, and she saw that he had decided something in the last three days, in the dark of the archive, with his thumb pressed to the page like a man pressing on a wound — decided it alone, and meant to carry it alone, right up until the moment she pried it out of him.

"The prophecy doesn't say," he said. "But the door needs an anchor on the far side, and I know someone who fits."

* * *

Far above them, at the mouth of the alley behind the guildhall, the broker stood in the shapeless dark between two lamps, wearing the guild's retired seal, listening to nothing with great attention.

Around him, the city of Vell continued its slow subtraction. Another street had gone blank at midnight. A church bell had forgotten its note. In the fading bakery on Cooper's Row, the ovens were cold for the first time in ninety years, because no one could recall what they were for.

The broker smiled, or arranged the space below where a face should be into something adjacent to smiling.

The door was waking. Good. Doors were easiest to steal at the moment of opening.

He turned the old seal over in his fingers, once, twice, and waited, patient as ink drying, for the archivist to keep his appointment.

* * *

In the vault, Hazel rolled down her sleeve over the blood-ink recipes and put her hand flat against the door.

"Tomorrow," she said. "We try it tomorrow. Tonight, we sleep, we eat, and you—" she jabbed a finger at Oscar— "are going to tell me everything else you've hidden. Starting with why the acquisition notes say the previous owner's country was also called Vell."

Oscar went very still.

"...Because it was," he said finally. "A hundred years ago, Hazel. This has happened before. And last time, the person who crossed — the person who became the anchor—" He swallowed. "The notes list her name."

The lamp flickered.

"Her name was Voss."
