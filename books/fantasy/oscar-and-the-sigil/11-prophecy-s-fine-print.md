## 11. Prophecy's Fine Print

The candle had burned down to a coin of wax by the time Oscar Quill admitted to himself that he was not reading the prophecy anymore.

He was reading it the way a man reads a confession — slowly, in the guilty dark, with his thumb pressed flat over the sentence he could not bear to finish.

The guildhall's reading room had once been a place of comfort for him: the smell of vellum and lamp-oil, the hush of ten thousand books breathing out their dust, the particular silence of knowledge waiting patiently to be needed. Now the silence had teeth. Outside the tall windows, the city of Vell was going soft at its edges. Three streets east, the lamplighter's route ended at a corner that no longer had a name or, if he was honest with himself, a corner. The fog that came off the harbor had begun to look less like weather and more like an appetite.

And the book lay open under his hands, its ink still wet after a hundred years.

*The Book of the Founding Stone*, the spine said, though the spine was lying too; the book predated any founding stone Oscar knew of, or rather it had been bound the same year the stone was carved, which was the same year, according to every history the guild possessed, that Vell had been sold and struck from the world's ledger like a bad debt. The prophecy inside was written in a hand that changed as you read it — began formal and ecclesiastical, loosened into something more human, and toward the end became so cramped and urgent that the letters seemed to be trying to escape the page.

He had read it four times tonight. On the first pass he had been looking for himself, the way anyone does when a book names them. It named him twice. *The archivist who trades in impossible things.* Flattering, if you liked your flattery with a knife in it. On the second pass he had been looking for Hazel, because her name appeared on a page dated tonight, written down before either of their grandparents drew breath, and because thinking about Hazel was easier than thinking about the way the fog outside kept rearranging itself into streets that used to exist.

On the third pass, he had been looking for a way out.

On the fourth pass, he found the fine print.

It was tucked into the margin of the final leaf, in ink so pale it might have been water, might have been breath, might have been the author writing with the last strength she had. A clause. Prophecies, Oscar knew better than most men alive, were contracts, and contracts were written by people who expected to survive them. He had spent eleven years in the Guild of Impossible Things learning to read the asterisk. *This door opens both ways, terms apply. This rain fell yesterday and cannot fall again. This shadow belonged to a dead man and remembers being worn.* Every miracle in the catalog came with a condition small enough to hide in a margin.

He lifted his thumb from the sentence.

*Only one may cross. The other remains — as anchor. As keeper. As the sigil's hand made flesh, holding Vell together from within until remembrance returns or the last memory fails.*

He read it three times more, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something kinder. Words did that, sometimes, in this building. Books here were living inventory; a poem might sulk, an atlas might wander. But the clause did not move. Pale ink, patient ink. Ink that had waited a century for exactly one person to lean close enough to read it, and had perhaps been written for exactly one person to find.

Hazel would find it too. That was the thing. She was not a reader of margins the way he was, but she was a reader of everything else — of faces, of flavors, of the half-second tremor in a courtier's hand before a cup touched royal lips. She read poison in people the way he read provenance in paper. If he carried this page around with the weight of it showing in his shoulders, she would taste it within a day.

So he did the only thing an archivist could do with a truth too heavy to hold and too cruel to share.

He cut the page out.

His knife — the good one, bone-handled, bought years ago from the guild's own shelves, a blade that had once belonged to a censor and still remembered the work — went through the vellum with a sound like a held breath released. The margin-clause came free whole, a ragged rectangle of pale script no bigger than his palm. The book sighed when he took it. He was almost certain the book sighed.

He sat there a long moment with the scrap in his hand, feeling the quiet rot begin.

That was the word for it, he thought later. Rot. Not the loud kind, the kind that brought a building down, but the slow kind, the kind that got into timber and made it sound hollow long before anything fell. A secret told to oneself is still a secret told. Somewhere in the telling, the teller splits — the one who knows and the one who pretends not to know — and the seam between them lets in damp.

*I'll tell her,* he promised the candle. *When we're closer to the door. When there's a plan. When knowing won't just be cruelty.*

The candle guttered, and he chose, deliberately, to believe it had agreed.

* * *

Sleep did not come, so he went down into the stacks instead.

The guildhall at night was a country of its own, which was fitting, since it was currently the last fixed point in a dissolving one. Shelves rose past the reach of the lamps into darkness where older inventory slept. He walked the aisles without lighting anything, out of habit — some of the stock disliked flame — trailing his fingers along spines the way other men touch rosaries.

Here: a jar of yesterday's rain, condensation still on the glass, holding the smell of a storm that had already happened and could never happen again. Here: the shadow of a dead duelist, folded flat between sheets of black silk, twitching faintly when he passed, remembering hands. Here: a door, standing alone in its alcove, painted with a landscape that moved very slowly when you weren't watching, leading out of a country that no longer existed — the door they would need, the door that was currently the most useless object in Vell, because it opened onto nothing, because there was no longer a *nothing* properly registered to receive it.

An anchor outside time. That was what the door required. Two people anchored outside time, the prophecy said, and Oscar had clung to that plural all week the way a drowning man clings to driftwood, never examining whether the wood was sound.

*Two anchors,* the prophecy said elsewhere. *One to carry, one to keep.*

He stopped walking.

He stood in the aisle with the rain-jar glowing faintly beside him like a captured piece of sky, and he let himself do the arithmetic he had refused to do in the reading room. Only one may cross. One to carry Vell out through a door that opened onto nowhere. One to remain — as anchor, as keeper, as the sigil's hand made flesh.

Hazel's body was a ledger of poisons survived. The sigil could not erase what had already died and returned; that was the whole terrible elegance of her immunity, the key and the trap both. Which meant she could walk through the erasure untouched. Which meant she could carry the country out. Which meant —

Which meant the other chair at the table was already his.

He leaned his forehead against the shelf, against the cool leather of a dictionary of extinct languages, and breathed in the smell of old glue and older grief. There was a version of this where he told her tomorrow, plainly, over bread: *the prophecy says one of us stays, and we will decide together, and whatever we decide I will not lie to you again.* He rehearsed it. He was good at rehearsal; archivists rehearsed everything, because the archive taught you that the first telling of anything is always wrong.

But then he thought of her face when she'd fed the forgotten king, the fierce gentleness of it, the way she treated dignity as a dish that could be served to anyone. He thought of her ledger, every scar a small death survived, and how she had once told him, laughing over cheap wine, that she intended to die old and bored and full. He thought of her mother's voice reciting prophecy through her blood, an inheritance of endings she had never asked for.

If he told her now, she would refuse to leave. He knew it the way he knew watermark from woodgrain. She would refuse, and then neither of them would go, and Vell would finish forgetting itself with both of them standing politely at the exit, holding the door for each other into nothing.

So the secret stayed. And the rot spread one ring further, quiet as mold in a wall.

"I'm buying time," he said aloud to the shelf. "That's all. Time is stock. We trade in it."

The dictionary of extinct languages did not answer, but somewhere far off in the stacks, a book he could not see turned its own page, and he chose not to think about what it might have read.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and thin, like broth stretched too far.

Oscar had dozed at last in a reading chair, and woke with the page-fragment inside his coat, over his heart, where it lay like a coal that had cooled just enough to stop burning and start aching. He washed, dressed, and performed the morning rituals of a man with nothing to hide: he wound the hall clock that no longer mattered, he checked the seals on the rare-stock cabinets, he made tea strong enough to strip varnish.

Hazel came in with the dawn still wet on her, smelling of woodsmoke and the bakery two streets down, where she had cooked the night before and held the neighborhood together by sheer force of grandmother's recipes. Her sleeves were pushed up, flour to her elbows, and there was a smudge of ash on her jaw like a beauty mark drawn by someone careless.

"Salt," she said, without greeting. "I couldn't taste the salt in the bread last night."

His stomach dropped through the floorboards of the guildhall and kept falling. The sigil was eating her senses, starting with what she loved most. He knew this. He had known since the founding stone. And he heard himself say, easily, warmly, the lie sliding out polished: "Then we'll fix it. Salt is the easiest thing in the world to remember. It's in every sea and every tear and every grave. Whatever's taking it doesn't stand a chance against you."

She gave him a look — the taster's look, the one that weighed and measured — and for one horrible second he was certain she could taste the secret on him, bitter at the back of the throat, coating the tongue.

Then the light shifted through the high windows, and she looked away, and the moment passed, and the rot rang another year of growth.

"Oscar." She set down her satchel. Under the flour and the fatigue her eyes were steady as scales. "Last night I re-read my ledger. Every poison I've ever taken. And I've been thinking about what my grandmother used to say — that a recipe is just a memory you can feed people. And I've been thinking about the door, and the seal, and the stone, and—" She stopped. Laughed shortly. "I've been thinking about everything except the part I can't say out loud yet."

"And what part is that?" he asked, and hated how gentle his voice came out, how practiced, how much it sounded like a man hiding a page over his heart.

"That one of us isn't coming," she said. "Isn't that always the fine print? Doesn't every contract like this have it?"

The fragment against his chest felt suddenly hot as a brand. He reached for the teapot, for time, for any object in the room that would hold still and let him hide behind it.

"Hazel," he began — and meant to say *yes*, and meant to say *I know*, and meant, finally, after one long rotten night, to tell the truth.

What he actually said, as the fog pressed white against the windows and somewhere below them the guildhall's front door forgot how to lock: "Let me check the book first. Let me make sure."

She nodded, trusting him, and went to fetch her coat.

Oscar stood alone in the reading room with the prophecy lying open to a leaf that now ended mid-sentence, its missing margin warm inside his coat, and understood that he had just done the thing he could never undo. He had told her *let me make sure*, which was true, and had not told her *I already did*, which was truer, and the space between those two sentences was exactly wide enough for a man to disappear into.

Outside, the fog had reached the guildhall steps.

Inside, the book waited, patient as pale ink, for him to come back and read what remained.
