## 12. The King Who Forgot Himself

The palace had gone quiet in the way of things that are not sleeping but forgetting.

Hazel felt it before she saw it — a thinning at the edges of the air, the same wrongness she'd tasted in the banquet hall when the candles stood unlit and the hall stood empty and the prophecy lay open on the dais like a mouth mid-sentence. The corridors were still lit. The chandeliers still burned. But the light fell on nothing that seemed to hold it, and the marble under her boots gave back her footsteps a half-beat late, as though the floor had to be reminded that sound was something it did.

Oscar walked beside her, close enough that his shoulder brushed hers on the turns. He hadn't let more than an arm's length grow between them since the guildhall. She understood why without discussing it. In a city where street signs blanked themselves and citizens forgot their own sentences halfway through speaking, distance was a kind of erasure too.

"The throne room's this way," he said, and then stopped, frowning at the corridor ahead. "I think. I was here once, for a records petition. There was a fountain."

There was no fountain. There was no corridor, either — or there was, but it ended in a wall of pale stone where Hazel remembered doors: the ambassador's wing, the portrait gallery, the long windows looking south over the gardens. The wall was not built. It had simply been decided. The plaster didn't even try to look finished; it was smooth as the inside of a closed eye.

"It's eating inward," Oscar said quietly. "Streets first. Names. Now rooms." He touched the wall with two fingers, the way a physician touches a fevered brow, and pulled them back quickly. "It's warm. Did you feel that? Erasure shouldn't be warm."

"Everything about it is wrong," Hazel said. "The book was wet. The streets go quiet before they go. It doesn't unmake like fire or rot. It *revises*." She thought of her ledger, of corrections made in a careful hand — a figure struck through, replaced, the original left ghosting beneath the ink. Somewhere, something was striking Vell through and writing something else over it, and the ghost of the original was what they were all still walking around inside.

They found another way around, through a servants' passage Oscar swore he'd never known existed until he opened the door to it — which Hazel chose not to think about too closely, because the alternative was to wonder whether the passage had always been there or whether the palace, in its dissolution, was growing new veins the way a dying body grows new ones, desperate, misrouted, wrong.

The passage smelled of bread.

That stopped her. After days of air that tasted of nothing — after the blank signs and the silent bells and the baker's shop where the ovens were cold and the flour bins held only a fine grey dust that might once have been wheat — the smell of bread rising hit her like grief hits, suddenly and in the chest. She pressed her palm flat against the passage wall and breathed.

"What is it?" Oscar asked.

"Bread. Someone is baking." Her voice came out strange. "In a palace where no one remembers they're hungry."

*Someone remembers,* she thought. Or someone is hungering so hard that the memory has nowhere else to go but into the air.

The passage ended at a door she recognized — the small one behind the arras, the one the tasters used, because kings who feared poison also feared spectacle and preferred their food checked without an audience. She had come through that door four hundred times. She knew its hinges by their particular complaint, a dry note like a question being asked badly.

She pushed it open, and the hinges asked their question, and no one answered.

The throne room was empty.

Not empty of furniture — the throne sat where it had always sat, on its dais beneath the great window, the stained glass throwing its colored coins of light across the steps. Not empty of light. Empty of everything else. No guards. No courtiers. No petitions stacked on their stands. The banners of Vell hung from the rafters, but their colors had begun to drain, the deep blue of the royal house fading toward the blue of old dishwater, the gold thread going the color of straw left too long in rain. Erasure, working from the edges in.

And on the bottom step of the dais, sitting with his back against the cold stone like a child waiting out a punishment, was the king.

Hazel knew him instantly and did not recognize him at all. That was the horror of it — the face was the face she had seen at every state dinner for eleven years, the long tired jaw, the heavy lids, the mouth that smiled with only half its commitment. But the man wearing it had come loose from himself. He wore his robes without ceremony, half off one shoulder, and his crown sat beside him on the step like a hat set down by someone who intended to pick it up later and already knew he wouldn't. His hands rested in his lap, empty, and they moved sometimes, small motions, as if turning pages that weren't there.

He was humming. It took Hazel a moment to place the tune, and when she did her throat tightened: it was the grace-song, the little melody every Vellan child sang before meals, three notes up and two down, older than the dynasty, older than the founding stone, probably. *For what was grown and what was given, for the hands that made the making.* A song about gratitude for food. A song about remembering where things come from.

He couldn't remember his name. But he remembered how to thank.

"Oh," Oscar said softly, behind her. "Oh, no."

The king looked up. His eyes found Hazel with an effort, like a man focusing through deep water, and for a moment something in them sharpened — recognition of a shape, if not a person.

"You're the food one," he said.

"I'm the food one," Hazel agreed. Her voice was steady. Eleven years of tasting hemlock and nightshade and arsenic before breakfast had taught her how to keep a voice level while everything underneath it screamed.

"They took my name." He said it conversationally, the way a man reports weather. "I've been looking for it. I thought—" His hands turned their invisible pages. "I thought I'd left it in my coat. But I don't have a coat. Do I?"

"You have robes," Hazel said. "Good ones. You're the king."

Something flickered across his face — not fear, exactly. A kind of polite embarrassment, as if she'd reminded him of a debt.

"Am I?" he said. "That seems like a lot of responsibility for a man who can't find his coat."

* * *

She should have kept moving. Every hour the sigil ate deeper; the door out of Vell would not wait; somewhere in the city below, a broker wearing the guild's old seal was counting down to something. Oscar shifted behind her, and she heard the argument forming in his breath — *Hazel, we don't have time, Hazel, he's already half-gone* — and she raised one hand slightly, without turning, and the argument died.

Because she had seen what happened to the forgotten. In the streets, citizens went quiet mid-sentence and then went quiet altogether, their faces smoothing like water closing over a stone. The forgetting didn't leave people sitting in their own palaces, humming grace-songs. Something was holding this man together. Something small and stubborn and, she suspected, located somewhere below his ribs.

"Hunger," she said aloud.

Oscar blinked. "What?"

"He's forgotten his name, his crown, his kingdom. But he's hungry." She crouched in front of the king, careful, the way you approach a frightened animal or a lit fuse. "Your Majesty. When did you last eat?"

He considered this with the gravity of a man consulting ledgers that no longer existed. "I don't know. I keep almost-remembering. There was a cup." His gaze drifted toward the throne, toward some point just past it. "There was something wrong with the cup."

"There was hemlock in your cup," Hazel said. "I tasted it. I drank it instead. You're welcome, by the way."

He laughed then — a short, startled, genuine laugh, and for a second the whole man was there behind his eyes, the king who signed decrees and dozed through council meetings and once, memorably, sent back to the kitchens a sauce he called *aggressively beige*. Then the laugh faded and the water closed over him again.

"You're the food one," he repeated, but gentler now, certain. "You'll know what to do about the hunger."

Her satchel was nearly empty. They had fled the guildhall with documents and ink and very little else, and the city's markets had become places where vendors sold vegetables they could no longer name. But at the bottom, wrapped in cloth, she had protected three things: a heel of dark bread bought two nights ago from a baker whose name had already gone; a hard cheese rind saved from the guildhall's stores; and a small clay pot of honey, because Hazel Voss had survived poison for eleven years on the principle that you never go anywhere without something sweet to bring you back to yourself.

She laid it all out on the cold marble step like a treasury inventory. Bread. Cheese rind. Honey. The king watched her hands with an attention that was almost painful, the way starving men and dead men watch living hands.

"This isn't a feast," she said. "But it's honest."

"It smells like my grandmother's house," the king said, and then frowned, because he had no memory of any grandmother, and both of them knew it, and neither of them mentioned it.

She broke the bread first. Steam — faint, stubborn, the ghost of the oven it had left two days ago — rose between them. She tore a piece and dipped it in honey and held it out, and the king took it in both hands, carefully, the way you take something you've been told is breakable.

He chewed. He swallowed. And then he closed his eyes and sang.

Three notes up, two down. The grace-song, complete, unhurried, his cracked voice filling the empty throne room the way it must once have filled cathedral naves. *For what was grown and what was given.* The banners above them seemed to hold a shade darker in their blue. The stained glass threw its colors a little truer. Somewhere far below them, Hazel would have sworn she heard the city breathe.

"For the hands that made the making," he finished, and opened his eyes, and they were clearer. Not clear. But clearer. As if each bite were a lamp relit in a house going dark, illuminating one room at a time, briefly, while the rest stayed night.

"Eat," Hazel said, and fed him the way you feed anyone — steadily, without ceremony, cheese after bread after honey — and with each bite the man surfaced a little longer. He remembered the name of the river. He remembered his daughter, and said her name twice, saying it slowly like a man carrying water across ice — *Sera, Sera* — afraid to spill it. He remembered that he had hated his coronation because the crown was heavier than everyone promised and lighter than he deserved.

He did not remember his own name. That room stayed dark.

"I had it this morning," he said, apologetic. "Or I think it was morning. It was a good name. Short. Everyone used it." He looked at his empty hands. "Do you know what the strangest part is? I keep thinking it's in the bread. Like maybe if I eat enough—"

"Names aren't in bread," Hazel said gently.

"No." He smiled with half his commitment. "But hunger is. Hunger's in everything. That's how I know I'm still real. Things that aren't real don't get hungry." He looked up at her, and there was nothing confused in his eyes now, only tired and terribly present. "You understand that, food one. You taste things for a living. You know that flavor is proof."

Flavor is proof. She carried the words somewhere deep and did not examine them yet, because there was a coldness spreading through her tongue these past two days, a missing saltiness, a silence where salt should sing, and she was not ready to hear what that meant.

* * *

When the food was gone — every crumb, the cheese rind gnawed to nothing, the honey pot scraped clean with a fingertip — the king sat straighter on his step. Not kingly, exactly. But upright. A man reassembled around his hunger the way a city rebuilds around its well.

He looked at his crown for a long moment. Then he picked it up, weighed it in both hands, and set it deliberately aside.

"It doesn't fit anymore," he said. "Maybe it never did. Maybe that's what crowns are — weights you agree to carry until someone offers to carry them better." His eyes settled on Hazel, and they were the eyes of a man making a decision, not a man lost in one. "You fed me. When the whole country forgot me, you came through the little door with bread."

"You were hungry," Hazel said. "It wasn't strategy."

"Everything is strategy," the king said. "Except feeding someone. That's the one thing that never is." He reached beneath the collar of his robe — the good side, the side still holding its gold thread — and drew out a chain, and on the chain the royal seal of Vell: heavy bronze, worn smooth at the edges by generations of anxious thumbs, bearing the sigil of the founding house crossed with the mark of the crown. It was warm from his skin. Everything in this erased country was either cold as ash or warm as fever, and Hazel had learned to dread the warmth, but this warmth felt different. This warmth felt like a body.

"The archives say there's a door," the king said. "There's always a door. My grandfather sold one once — don't ask me how I know that, I don't, the knowing came back with the cheese." He held out the seal. "Two keys. That's how such doors work. One for blood. One for office." He paused. "You'll need the office one. I appear to have misplaced the blood one along with everything else."

"Your Majesty, I can't—"

"You can and you will, and you'll do it without arguing, because arguing takes time and we both know the walls are coming." He pressed it into her palm and folded her fingers over it, and his hands were shaking, but his voice was not. "I am dissolving, food one. Slowly, politely, from the edges in, like everything else. I cannot stop it. But I can decide what my last act of kingship is, and I find I want it to be useful." He almost smiled. "A coronation is just a feeding, when you think about it. You give a person their portion. You tell them what they carry. So consider yourself crowned — not with this"—he nodded at the crown on the step—"with that. The seal. The job. Whatever's left of Vell goes where you carry it."

Hazel's throat closed. Eleven years she had knelt before this man's table and swallowed his poisons and never once been seen, because a taster's whole art is being unseen, and here he was, nameless on a stair, seeing her more clearly than he ever had with a full memory.

"What was your name?" she asked him. "Your real one. Before the crown."

His face worked. He reached for it — she watched him reach, the invisible pages turning faster — and came back empty, and to her astonishment he laughed again, soft and rueful.

"Gone," he said. "Isn't that funny? I gave away my seal and I still can't give you my name." He patted her folded hand, once, twice, like a benediction. "Keep the seal. Keep the name of the country, if names are what it needs. Feed whoever you find in the dark. That's the whole of governance, as far as I can recall."

Behind her, Oscar made a sound — small, strangled — and when Hazel glanced back, he was staring at the king with his archivist's face, the one that meant he had just read a sentence in an old book that rearranged every other sentence around it.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," Oscar said. Too fast. "Nothing. We should go. While the light holds."

The king waved them toward the little door like a host dismissing beloved guests. "Go on, then. Mind the walls." Then, as Hazel reached the threshold, his voice followed her, quieter, almost cheerful:

"If you find my name out there — on a sign, in a book, anywhere — send it back, would you? I'll be here. I seem to live here." A pause. "And food one? Thank you for the meal. For what was grown, and what was given."

She looked back once, from the doorway. He had taken up his invisible pages again, turning them patiently in the lamplight, and the crown sat gleaming on the abandoned step like a coin dropped in a fountain — a wish someone else would have to spend.

Then the arras fell shut between them, and the hinges asked their dry question, and this time Hazel answered it, silently, with everything she had:

*Yes.*

* * *

The servants' passage was longer on the way back. Walls had moved. Doors had become suggestions. Oscar navigated by touch and by some private arithmetic, muttering shelf-numbers like prayers, and Hazel walked with the seal's weight swinging cold against her sternum and the king's words warming it from within.

At the first junction Oscar stopped, pressed his back to the wall, and looked at her — really looked, the way he did when a fact had gotten too large to carry alone.

"The page," he said. "From the prophecy. The one I showed you — the clause about anchors, about one leaving and one staying." His jaw worked. "There was a second page. Torn out. I found the stub."

"And?"

"And the stub has writing on the back of it. Just one line. Ink still wet." He swallowed. "It says: *the second key opens from the other side.*"

Hazel's hand closed over the seal through her shirt. From the other side. Which meant someone was meant to stand beyond the door — beyond Vell, outside time, in whatever waited where a country stops existing —

—and the door, wherever it was, had just begun, very faintly, very impossibly, to glow through the cracks of a world running out.
