## 14. What Hazel Cannot Taste

The bakery had been a bakery once, and then it was a memory of a bakery, and now it was a shell that remembered only its ovens. Hazel liked it anyway. The walls still held the ghost-shape of bread — a warmth in the plaster, a faint sweetness in the cracks — and ghosts, she was learning, were the last things the sigil bothered to eat. It went for the living first.

She woke before dawn on the flour-dusted floor behind the counter, her coat rolled under her head, Oscar's breathing slow and even on the other side of the dead hearth. Outside, the street made a sound she had never heard a city make: nothing. No cart wheels. No milk bells. Only the wind moving through doorways that no longer remembered they were doors.

She lay still a moment and took inventory, as she always did. It was how she began every day of her life: tongue against teeth, pulse at the throat, the ledger of herself balanced to the last line.

*Alive. Unpoisoned. Un-erased.*

Then she reached for the clay jug of water by the hearth — their one extravagance, carried up from a well two streets over because the well beside the bakery had gone blank, its water tasting of nothing at all — and drank, and broke her fast with a heel of dark bread and a pinch of salt from the paper twist in her pocket.

The bread tasted of ash and rye and the faint wild yeast of the air itself. Good bread. Honest bread.

The salt tasted of nothing.

Hazel sat very still, the way she did when a poison announced itself in her mouth — not with fire but with absence, a flavor lifting out of the world like a chair pulled from under a guest. She put another grain of salt on her tongue. Nothing. A third. Her heart did not speed, because fear was a taste too, and she wondered, with a coldness that had nothing to do with the morning, whether fear would be next.

Salt.

Of all the flavors in Vell, salt was the oldest one. Salt was the first thing a taster learned and the last thing she forgot: the mineral spine of every broth, the brine that held herring and grief and the sea itself, the crust on the festival loaves of Ashfall Week, the tears — she caught herself there, and stopped, and looked down at her hands.

Her hands were steady. Taster's hands. They had been steady through hemlock and nightshade and the king's slow arsenic and eleven years of court dinners where every smile was a loaded fork. They were steady now, and that steadiness told her everything: this was not poison. Poison would have been a mercy, a known country, a line item in her ledger. This was something else. Something reaching into her from the outside, patient as erosion, and starting — of course it would start here — with what she loved most.

The sigil was eating her senses one by one.

And it had begun with salt.

* * *

She did not wake Oscar. Some accounts you have to open alone.

Instead she built a small fire in the oven — the chimney still drew, stubbornly, as if the bakery's bones refused the erasure on principle — and she set an iron pan on the coals and cooked, because cooking was the only liturgy she knew.

Eggs from a farmer's abandoned coop, fried in butter until the edges crisped lace-brown. Onions sweated down to amber. A ribbon of salt pork, seared until the fat sang. She cooked the way her grandmother had taught her, back when Hazel was small enough to stand on a stool and her grandmother's hands moved around hers like two spoons in one drawer: *salt early, salt late, salt is the difference between food and fuel.*

She tasted each stage. Eggs: yes, rich and fat and alive. Onion: sweet, deep, almost weeping with sweetness. Salt pork: smoke, fat, pepper —

— and underneath it, where the salt should have been, a silence. A hole shaped exactly like the ocean.

By the time the light through the shutters turned from grey to gold, she understood the shape of the thing. It was not taking everything at once. It was taking in order. It was going down her list of loves like a creditor working through a debtor's estate, and it had started with the flavor she touched most often, trusted most completely, mourned most deeply. Her grandmother's last word to her had been about salt. *If you can still taste the salt, Hazel, you are still home.*

She could not taste the salt.

She was not home anymore, and neither was the home; both of them were being unmade at the same speed, and the sigil had simply gotten to her tongue first.

Hazel washed the pan. Dried it. Put it away in a cupboard whose hinges still worked, and stood for a moment with her hand flat on the cupboard door, feeling the wood remember being a tree.

Then she went looking for needle and ink.

* * *

Oscar found her an hour later, sitting cross-legged on the counter with her left forearm bare and her sleeve rolled to the elbow, a sewing needle heated black in the candle flame and a dish of ink — archive ink, his best iron-gall, the kind he rationed like blood — open beside her.

He stopped in the doorway with a bundle of scavenged candles in his arms and looked at her the way people look at a wound they cannot decide whether to touch.

"Hazel."

"It works," she said. "I tested it. The ink holds." She flexed her fingers to show him. "Better than paper. Paper burns. Paper gets erased." She nodded at the window, where the street beyond had gone thin again overnight, the cobbles pale as old bone. "Skin doesn't get erased. Skin just dies. And I've died more times than anyone in this country, so I'm reasonably confident mine will hold."

"What did you write?"

"Barley soup with smoked neck bone. Three onions, not two. My grandmother's rule about salt." She said it evenly, but her voice snagged on the word and she heard him hear it. "Oscar. I can't taste salt."

The candles slipped in his arms; he caught them against his chest. "Since when?"

"This morning. Maybe since last night. It came quietly. That's the worst part — poisons announce themselves. This one just... subtracts." She held up her arm to the light, and he came closer despite himself, despite everything, the archivist in him unable to refuse a document. There on the inside of her forearm, in letters still raised and angry-red, ran the first recipe: *BARLEY SOUP — SOAK OVERNIGHT — NECK BONE SMOKED NOT FRESH — THREE ONIONS — SALT WHEN IT FORGETS TO BE WATER.*

"The last line," Oscar said.

"My grandmother's. She said you salt the pot twice: once for the soup, once so the soup remembers being soup." Hazel looked at her own handwriting carved into her own body and felt something in her chest that was not quite grief and not quite triumph, some third thing with the weight of both. "The sigil eats what we love first. So fine. Then I'll love it hard enough to leave a scar. Every recipe I know goes on my skin before it can go out of the world. If Vell has to live somewhere, let it live somewhere the sigil can't reach without killing me. And it's tried that. Eleven years of trying. I keep a ledger."

Oscar set down the candles. He sat on the flour sack across from her, and for a long moment he didn't say anything, and Hazel recognized the silence because she used it herself — the silence of a person rearranging their face around bad news.

"There's something you're not telling me," she said. "You've been wearing that particular silence for two days."

"I'll tell you," he said. "Soon. When I understand it myself."

"You always say that."

"And I've always meant it." He met her eyes, and whatever lived behind his was heavy and folded shut like a letter he'd already sealed. "Let me help with the ink. You'll ruin your right hand writing with it cramped like that."

So he wrote on her while she dictated, which was somehow worse and better than doing it alone — his neat archivist's capitals marching up her arms, precise as catalog entries, each letter a small burning she refused to flinch from. *Nettle and pork rib stew.* *Ashfall honey-cakes, caraway in the crust.* *The king's coronation pheasant with sour cherries — I tasted it at fourteen and it is the reason I am a taster.* Down her left forearm. Up her right. Over the backs of her hands, where the scars already lay like a first draft: foxglove, 3 grains, survived. Hemlock, 2 drops, survived. Arsenic, slow, survived.

The recipes settled in among the poisons like doves landing on a battlefield, and Hazel thought: *there. Now my body is honest. Everything I've ever swallowed, everything I've ever saved, all in one ledger with a cover the sigil will have to bleed through.*

When he finished the last entry — *bread, dark rye, caraway, baked the morning the world ended, for Oscar, who ate it standing up* — he capped the ink and held her wrist gently, reading her skin like a page.

"This will scar badly," he said.

"Good," said Hazel. "Scars mean it happened. Scars are proof. That's all remembering is, Oscar — proof, kept somewhere safe." She smiled, and it cost her, and she paid anyway. "I am becoming the safest place in Vell."

* * *

That afternoon the sigil took the color blue.

She noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth — not all at once but in the gap, in the tongue's continual surprise. The sky over the rooftops went on shining, but when she tried to name its color, the name slid off. The crocuses pushing up through the cracked flagstones were simply *bright*, indescribably bright, bright the way a shout is loud. Blue was gone. Not the color — the knowing of it. Somewhere in her, a drawer had been emptied and the label left behind.

She sat on the doorstep and tested herself like a good accountant, sense by sense. Sight: diminished, one color short. Smell: intact — the bread-ghost in the walls still found her, thank every saint who'd ever starved. Hearing: intact. Taste: salt gone, blue gone from sight, and beneath her sternum a faint new numbness she chose not to name yet, because naming it might be inviting it.

It was eating toward her. Not randomly. Deliberately, lovingly, the way she herself ate a meal — best bites first? No. Worse than that. Like a taster working through a banquet course by course, saving the king's plate for last. It was savoring her. It had a hundred years of appetite and one century-old prophecy that said her name, and it intended to finish the meal.

"Then here is my counteroffer," Hazel said aloud, to the empty street, to the blank sign above the shuttered chandlery, to whatever patient thing was listening. She rolled up her sleeves. The recipes gleamed on her arms, scabbed and furious and legible. "You want me to forget? I have spent my whole life swallowing things meant to kill me and staying. I am the most poisoned woman in Vell. I am a ledger of survived deaths. Whatever you take from my tongue, I will carve into my skin. Whatever you take from my skin, I will teach to strangers. And whatever you take from them—" her voice cracked, and she let it, because defiance that never cracks isn't defiance, it's furniture "—whatever you take from them, I will cook again. From memory. From scar. From salt I can no longer taste but will measure blind, by heart, until the day you come for the heart too. And I will make you work for every single dish."

The street said nothing. But the crocuses stayed bright, and the chimney drew, and somewhere far off — or perhaps not far off at all, perhaps only one wall away, perhaps inside her own chest — Hazel heard a sound like a great stone lid grinding a fraction of an inch open.

Something had heard her. Something had begun to answer.

Behind her, inside the bakery, Oscar cried out — a short, sharp sound, the sound of a man finding a page torn out of a book he'd sworn was whole.

Hazel was on her feet and through the doorway before her mind caught up with her body, and she found him kneeling among the scattered candles with the guild's master ledger open in his hands, his face the color of the blank street outside.

"Oscar — what is it — what's missing?"

He didn't answer at first. He was staring at the place where a page had been, and his lips were moving, counting entries that were no longer there.

"Hazel," he said finally, and his voice was terrible and quiet, "the buyer's account. Someone bought Vell back — I showed you, weeks of research, the seal, the payment — it's all gone. Not erased. *Removed.* Cut out with a blade, recent, the vellum's still white at the edges—" He looked up at her, and she saw that he was holding the ledger the way she held her own forearm: like the only proof left in the world. "Someone doesn't want us to know who bought the country. And they got here ahead of the sigil."

Outside, the light thickened, going the color of old honey, of candle-fat, of nothing they had a name for anymore — and down the empty street, unhurried, came the click of a latch turning in a door that had forgotten how to be locked.
