## 15. The Broker's Bargain

The guildhall had gone quiet in the way a body goes quiet before it stops.

Oscar felt it in the shelves first — the way an archivist feels a fever in a book room, the faint wrongness of air that has stopped moving between spines. For eleven years he had known this building by its breathing: the settling of the west stacks at dusk, the tick of the catalog cabinet expanding in summer heat, the small sighs of paper remembering itself. Tonight the building held its breath, and every shelf stood too still, like soldiers awaiting an order no one wanted to give.

He was alone. That was the point of it. Hazel had gone to find the king — *the king who forgot himself*, she'd said, with that flat taster's pragmatism that was its own kind of tenderness — and Oscar had told her he would inventory what remained of the impossible things. It was even true. He had a ledger open on the reading table, and his pen in his hand, and columns of losses already entered in his neat guild hand:

*Yesterday's rain (three vials) — burned.*
*A dead man's shadow, folded — burned.*
*The last hour of a marriage nobody wanted — burned.*

He had wept over each one as it went into the brazier, and he was not ashamed of the weeping. An archivist who does not grieve his holdings is only a warehouseman. But grief is a slow thing and erasure was fast, and so he had burned them quickly, one after another, buying minutes the way a drowning man buys air, and now there was nothing left to burn but the catalog itself, and he could not yet make his hands do that.

That was when the lamps changed.

Not dimmed — changed. The light in the reading room turned the color of old varnish, amber going to umber, and the shadows of the shelves leaned inward as though listening. And in the doorway where the corridor met the reading room, where for eleven years there had been nothing but corridor, stood the broker.

Oscar had seen him twice before, always at a distance, always at the edge of some catastrophe: a tall figure in a coat cut from a fabric that refused to be described, wearing the guild's old seal — the door with no handle, the moon behind it — worked in tarnished silver at his throat. He had no face. Not masked; *unfaced*, the way a page is blank not because something covers the writing but because nothing was ever written there. Where features should have been there was a smooth suggestion, like a thumbprint pressed into warm wax and then smoothed away, and Oscar's eyes kept trying to assemble a face out of the absence and kept failing, and the failing hurt, somewhere behind the bridge of the nose.

"You're alone," the broker said. His voice was the worst part. It was a borrowed voice, or rather a voice made of borrowings — Oscar heard in it the cadence of Master Corvane, dead thirty years; the dry warmth of the woman who had sold him his first impossible thing; and underneath both, faint as a watermark, a boy's voice, young and frightened and very far away.

"I'm never alone," Oscar said. "I have eleven thousand books."

"Ten thousand, four hundred and six." The broker stepped into the room, and the floorboards did not creak, which was somehow more obscene than if they had screamed. "The rest were eaten Tuesday."

Oscar's pen had stopped moving. He noticed he was gripping it hard enough to blanch his knuckles, and made himself set it down, parallel to the ledger's edge, because a man who fidgets is a man negotiating from weakness, and whatever else happened in this room, he would not negotiate from weakness. "You've come about the offer."

"I've come because you've been carrying my offer around like a stone in your boot," the broker said, "and stones wear holes. Sit down, archivist."

"I'll stand."

"You'll want to sit for this part." And there was something in the borrowed voices — all of them, layered — that sounded almost kind, and that kindness was the most frightening thing in the building.

Oscar sat.

* * *

"The guild has always traded in impossible things," the broker began, pacing the length of the reading room with the unhurried gait of someone who owns the walking. "But do you know what our founders understood that you've forgotten? Nothing is truly impossible. Some things are merely *expensive*. Yesterday's rain costs a season of someone's memory. A dead man's shadow costs the shadow-caster's name. Every item in your inventory had a price paid by somebody, somewhere, and your ledgers record only the sale, never the cost. Sloppy bookkeeping."

"We record what we're given," Oscar said. "The costs are—" He stopped. He had been about to say *the costs are private*, and heard, even as he said it, how much that sounded like an excuse.

"The costs are hidden," the broker finished for him. "Yes. By design. By the design you're sitting inside." He stopped before the great master ledger, chained to its lectern since before Oscar's apprenticeship, and laid one unfaced profile against its cover. "Shall I show you the entry they never let you read?"

Oscar should have said no. He knew that the way you know a stair is missing in the dark — a whole-body knowledge, arriving a half-second too late to matter. Because the broker opened the master ledger without touching the lock, and turned past three hundred years of entries to a page near the beginning, and the page was not old.

It was new. The ink was wet. It gleamed in the varnish-colored lamplight like a wound.

**Item:** One country, Vell, entire.
**Seller:** The Founding Families, jointly and severally.
**Buyer:** [entry sealed]
**Price:** One hundred years of being remembered wrong.
**Terms:** At the century's end, the country may be bought back by any party holding two keys and one willing anchor.
**Anchor:** The first citizen who chooses to stay.

Oscar read it three times. The words rearranged themselves slightly on each pass, the way words did now, everywhere, as the sigil's rewriting spread through the world's grammar. But the last line stayed fixed. *The first citizen who chooses to stay.*

"You said the buyer's entry is sealed," he managed.

"It was sealed by me." The broker closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a coffin. "A hundred years ago, Vell was dying anyway. Plague in the low quarters, famine in the high ones, a king who answered the census honestly and found he ruled four hundred people and a rumor. The founding families came to the guild — to *me* — and asked for the impossible thing. Erasure without suffering. A country that could die without anyone having to bury it." He turned, and the lamplight slid across the smooth absence of his face. "I sold it to them. And then I learned what the fine print required. An anchor. Someone to stand inside the erased country and hold its ghost together from within, so it wouldn't scatter entirely — so it could, someday, be bought back. Someone who would stay while everyone else forgot."

The lamp flame bent toward the broker as if leaning in to listen.

"There were volunteers," the broker said. "There are always volunteers, at first. But volunteering is a decision made in company, and staying is done alone, forever, and every volunteer un-volunteered at the threshold. All but one." A pause, and in the pause all the borrowed voices fell away except the boy's, the young one, the far one, and for a moment the broker's shoulders were narrower, and his coat hung on a smaller frame. "Me. I was nineteen. My brother was dying of the plague, and the terms allowed one life to be preserved inside the remembered country — one person, kept safe in the ghost, alive as long as the anchor holds. I chose. I have been choosing it every day for a hundred years, which is what nobody tells you about a choice: it doesn't end when you make it. It just starts charging interest."

Oscar's mouth was dry. "You're the sigil's hand."

"I'm the sigil's *clerk*," the broker said, with something that might once have been humor. "The sigil is the contract. I'm what enforces it. A hundred years of standing in the dark holding a country's ghost together wears a person down to functions, archivist. I don't remember my mother's face. I remember that I had one. I remember the shape of the choice. Everything else has been spent keeping your city standing — yes, standing. Every street you walked today exists because I am holding it. Every name on every sign that hasn't blanked yet is a name I am carrying. Do you understand the weight of that? No. Of course not. You weigh books."

"And the buyer," Oscar said. "The entry says the country may be bought back. Someone bought it back. Who?"

The broker was quiet for a moment, and in that quiet Oscar heard the building settle — the real settling, the living one, the shelves breathing — and realized the guildhall was listening too.

"That," the broker said, "is the part I was not permitted to remember until tonight. Isn't symmetry elegant? You weren't permitted to read the sealed entry; I wasn't permitted to recall my own transaction. The contract protects itself." He drew something from inside his coat: a folded slip of paper, ordinary, soft at the creases, the kind of note a shopkeeper writes. He set it on the reading table in front of Oscar. "The buyer is the founding families' estate. Executed by proxy. The purchase order specifies restoration of Vell as it was in its final year — the plague year — with all persons preserved as of their last living moment within its borders."

Oscar looked at the slip. It was written in his own handwriting.

No — nearly his own. The letters were his, the slant was his, the little hook he made on descenders was his. But the ink was the sigil's gray, and the words were not anything he had ever written:

*Restore Vell. Preserve all souls as recorded. Anchor: O.Q., voluntary.*

"When did I write this?" he whispered.

"You haven't yet," the broker said. "That's the nature of a contract with the sigil. It is signed in advance by everyone it concerns. Free will, in Vell, has always been a matter of timing."

* * *

The lamp had burned down to a stub. Oscar did not remember time passing, which frightened him more than the broker did, because an archivist's mind is a clock, and his had skipped.

"Tell me the offer," he said. His voice came out level, and he was proud of that, and ashamed of the pride. "The one I've been carrying in my boot."

"Ah." The broker drew out a chair — the chair opposite Oscar's, the reading chair, Hazel's chair, though she would deny owning any chair — and sat, folding long hands over the table's edge. Up close, the unfaced quality was worse; Oscar could see the faintest texture, like skin seen through frosted glass, like a face behind a window that had fogged from the inside. "Your brother's name was Tam."

"Don't."

"He was seven when the fever took him. You were ten. You held his hand through the last night because your parents were dead and there was no one else, and he asked you whether the stars were holes in the sky or lights behind it, and you told him lights, because it was kinder, and he believed you, because you were his whole world and the world does not lie to a seven-year-old. He died at dawn. You have never told anyone about the stars. You have never told anyone that you checked, afterward — that you went out every clear night for a year and tried to decide, and couldn't, and finally decided it didn't matter because whichever it was, Tam was behind neither."

Every word landed like a needle finding a nerve Oscar had thought well-hidden. He had told no one. Not the guild's intake examiners, who asked about griefs because griefs were leverage; not the three lovers he'd had and lost to his own guardedness; not Hazel, who had shown him her ledger of scars and received in return only the curated version of his history. How did the broker— But he knew how. The sigil had eaten the city's records, and the guild's records, and somewhere in what it had swallowed was the intake file, the orphan registry, the death notice for Tam Quill, aged seven, cause: lungfever. The sigil didn't just erase. It digested. And now it was reading his own history back to him in a borrowed voice.

"The restored Vell," the broker said softly, "preserves all souls as of their last living moment. Tam is in there, archivist. Alive. Seven years old, mid-fever, waiting for dawn — but in the remembered country, dawn is a hundred years away and can be a hundred years more. He is warm. He is dreaming. He asked his question about the stars six hours ago and will ask it again tomorrow, and tomorrow, and if you are there, you can answer it again, and again, and this time you can take as long as you like deciding which answer is kinder." The broker leaned forward. "Stay. Be the anchor. Hold your country together with your name, the way I have held mine. And your brother lives. Not resurrected — *continued*. There is a difference, and the difference is everything, and you of all people, a man who trades in impossible things, knows that continuity is the rarest commodity in any market."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the door opens, Hazel Voss walks out carrying a country in her body, and the remembered Vell dissolves — including the wing of it where a seven-year-old boy is sleeping. She cannot carry him. Only the anchor holds the souls. No anchor, no souls. They don't suffer, if that helps. They simply finish." The broker spread his hands. "They finish what they were doing when the country ended. Tam finishes dying. Gently. As gently as a thing can end."

The silence after that had texture. It had grain, like the silence in a sickroom.

Oscar looked at his hands. They were an archivist's hands, ink-stained at the second knuckle of the right middle finger, a burn scar on the left thumb from the night he'd learned why you don't touch a grimoire's brass clasps. Hands that had held a seven-year-old's hand through a last night. Hands that had burned yesterday's rain tonight and called it strategy.

He thought about Hazel. That was the terrible discipline he set himself: think about Hazel first. Her ledger of survived poisons, each scar a small death she'd walked out of. The way she'd fed the bakery's neighbors her grandmother's recipes and the neighborhood had held together a few hours longer, because memory is something you feed people. The way she had forgiven him — *before he finished asking*, that night at the door that opened onto nothing — and how the forgiveness had felt less like absolution than like a debt he hadn't agreed to owe.

She would forgive him for this too. That was the unbearable part. He could see it already: her face going carefully neutral, the taster's face, the one she wore when the poison was in the cup and swallowing was the job. She would forgive him, and she would walk out alone into a foreign country, and she would cook, because cooking was how she breathed, and she would build Vell again dish by dish on foreign tables, and she would never once say his name with hatred in it, and that mercy would be the cruelest thing anyone had ever done to him.

And Tam would finish dying.

"No," Oscar said.

The broker waited. The lamp flickered.

"No," Oscar said again, and heard the crack in it this time, the hairline fault, and hated himself for it, because he had meant it to be load-bearing and it was already failing. "I won't trade her. I won't trade anyone. That's the whole lesson of this cursed guild — every item in the inventory was somebody's grief priced and sold, and I am not going to add Hazel Voss to the catalog. Find another anchor. Find the founding families' ghosts. Find—"

"She chose to come here," the broker said quietly. "Into the erasure. Knowing the prophecy named her. Knowing the fine print. She read further than you did, archivist — shall I tell you what page twelve says?" And when Oscar flinched: "Ah. You haven't read page twelve. Then you don't know what staying costs *her*, either. You're protecting a woman who has already decided what she'll pay, and calling it virtue." He rose. The chair made no sound. "I'll leave the contract with you. It signs itself at dawn, one way or the other — yours if you stay, hers if you go. The sigil is patient. It has a hundred years of practice."

The broker moved to the doorway, and paused, and for one strange moment the borrowed voices all receded and left only the boy's voice, very close now, very tired, nineteen years old forever:

"For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I hope you say no. I hoped it a hundred years ago too. Hoping is the last thing they leave you."

Then the doorway was a doorway again, and the corridor beyond it was only a corridor, and the broker was nowhere, the way a word is nowhere after it's spoken.

* * *

Oscar sat for a long time in the varnish-colored dark.

The contract lay on the table. He did not touch it. He had an archivist's discipline about documents: you do not touch what you are not prepared to alter, because alteration begins with contact. Ink wants skin. Paper wants hands. Every forgery in the guild's collection had started with someone picking up something they should have left flat.

Dawn was perhaps five hours off. Hazel would return from the palace with the royal seal — he had to believe that, the king who forgot himself remembering enough to hand over the second key — and they would go to the door, and she would drink the last poison in her ledger, the draught distilled from Vell itself, and she would die to the country so she could carry it, and he would hold her hand at the threshold because that was the one thing left he could give without stealing anything from anyone.

Except.

Except the contract specified *Anchor: O.Q., voluntary.* Except the broker had said it signs itself at dawn, one way or the other. Except page twelve, which he had hidden, which was burning a hole in his coat lining right now, which said what it said, and which Hazel must never—

Must never what?

He got up. His legs were stiff, an old man's legs at twenty-nine, the age the broker had been. He walked to the brazier where the ashes of yesterday's rain still glowed faintly, gray-gray-gray, the color of the sigil, the color of the contract's ink, the color of the sky over an erased country.

He thought of Tam asking about the stars. Holes or lights. He had said lights because it was kinder. He had spent a year checking and never decided.

He thought of Hazel at the threshold, drinking, letting go, her hand in his, trusting him with the one act a taster never performs lightly: swallowing.

He thought of a seven-year-old boy, warm, dreaming, six hours from a question he would get to ask forever.

The contract lay on the table, unsigned, signing itself.

Oscar Quill picked up the pen.

His hand did not shake. That was the worst of it — that was the detail he would carry across every border of whatever came next — his hand did not shake, and the nib touched the gray line beneath *Anchor: O.Q., voluntary*, and the ink rose to meet it like water finding its level, and somewhere in the walls of the guildhall, ten thousand four hundred and six books held their breath.

"Yes," he said, to the empty room, to the sigil, to the boy behind the frosted glass of his own heart. "Yes. Keep him alive. I'll stay."

The ink dried. Dawn was coming. And five streets away, in a throne room full of forgetting, Hazel Voss was kneeling beside a king, feeding him soup, telling him stories about a country that still believed it existed — and Oscar pressed his palms against his eyes and began, silently, in the professional cadence of a man cataloging his final acquisition, to memorize her face.
