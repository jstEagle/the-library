## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers the way other institutions kept their dead: carefully, privately, and with an eye toward what might be dug up later.

Paloma Underhill had spent nine years learning to read them. Not the grand volumes in the vault—those were for the Founder's eyes and the senior factors who'd sworn the long oaths—but the working books, the daily traffic of a trade that dealt in relics, rumors, and the occasional miracle that turned out to be neither. He sat at his desk on the third floor of the guildhouse with a cup of tea gone cold beside him and a consignment slip pinned under his thumb, and he did what he did every morning: he made the impossible small enough to file.

*One (1) reliquary locket, provenance disputed, seller claims descent from the Weeping Court. Contents: hair, human, silvered. Reserve: forty crowns.*

Ordinary. The Weeping Court had been extinct for two centuries; its relics surfaced every few years, usually forged, occasionally genuine, never dangerous. Paloma had appraised eleven of them in his time at the guild and had written the same careful sentence about each: *Devotional object, inert, no residual charge.*

He opened the velvet case.

The locket was small, tarnished brass worked into the shape of a closed eye. Inside, behind glass gone cloudy with age, a curl of white hair lay coiled like something sleeping. Paloma tilted it toward the window light, checked the hinge, ran his thumbnail along the seam looking for the telltale glue-line of a forgery.

And it hummed.

Not a sound, exactly. Not anything that reached his ears. It reached somewhere lower—the hollow beneath his sternum where, nine years ago, an oath had lived before it died with its god. A vibration like a struck bell heard through a wall, like a word spoken underwater by someone very far away and very much alive.

Paloma set the locket down on the felt with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a snake he is pretending is a rope.

He looked around the appraisal room. Empty, as it always was at this hour. Rain against the leaded windows. Somewhere below, the guild's morning machinery creaked along: carts in the yard, the factor's bell, Sable's voice rising in argument with someone about shipping tariffs. Normal. All of it normal. He picked the locket back up.

The hum came again, and this time it resolved—not into words, nothing so kind as words, but into *shape*. A prayer has a shape if you've been trained to feel it. Paloma had been trained by the best and worst teacher imaginable: a god who answered. He knew the architecture of petition the way a locksmith knows keys. This one was old, worn smooth by repetition. Someone had prayed this prayer ten thousand times into this little brass eye. *Let her sleep. Let her sleep. Let her sleep.*

And someone—or something—had said yes.

That was the impossible part. That was the part that made his hands go cold around the case. The gods had gone silent nine years ago. Every god, every altar, every covenant-mark in the world had gone dark in a single night, and the world had adjusted with the speed of things that have no choice. Priests became historians. Prophets became frauds or beggars, and sometimes both. Paloma had become a ledger-keeper, which was a way of being both without starving.

He had buried his own oath-mark under three layers of mundane work and one layer of deliberate forgetting. It sat over his left ribs, a scar in the shape of a spiral that no longer spiraled—a dead brand, he told himself, a scar like any other scar. He had not let himself think of it as a mark in nine years.

It ached now. For the first time since the Silence, it *ached*, low and deep, like a bruise on a bone.

"Damn," Paloma said quietly, to the empty room.

He did what a professional would do. He finished the appraisal. He wrote, in his neat ledger-hand: *Reliquary locket, Weeping Court style, genuine. Devotional object, inert, no residual charge.* And then, because he was honest in the small ways even when he lied in the large ones, he added a note in the margin, coded in the guild's second cipher, the one used only for items that unsettled the appraiser personally: *Hums. Check provenance chain. Do not store with other devotional lots.*

He closed the case. The hum faded but didn't quite die, settling into the background of the room like a fly in winter—too faint to find, too persistent to ignore.

* * *

Sable found him at lunch, as she always did, claiming the bench across from him in the guild refectory with the entitlement of twenty years' friendship. She was mid-rant before she'd finished sitting down.

"—and if Marlow thinks I'm paying harbor rates on a shipment he watched me personally escort through a checkpoint, he can take his tariff schedule and—" She stopped. Squinted at him. "You look terrible."

"I always look like this."

"You look like this," she agreed, stealing a roll off his plate, "but today you've achieved a new depth. Bad night?"

"Bad morning." He hesitated. This was the moment, he knew, where a smart man said nothing. Nine years of survival had been built on saying nothing. But Sable was watching him with that flat gray attention she gave to shipments she suspected of lying about their contents, and the ache under his ribs pulsed once, like a second heartbeat trying to introduce itself. "We got a lot in yesterday's consignment?"

"The usual. Vess's agents swept three markets." She tore the roll in half, absently. "Why?"

"A locket. Weeping Court."

"Sell it fast, then. Those move." She chewed, frowned. "What?"

"Nothing. Provenance question." He stood, gathering his plate. "I'm going back down to the intake room. There's paperwork."

"There's always paperwork," Sable said, and let him go, which was unlike her, and watched him go, which was more unlike her still.

* * *

The intake room was where the guild received the world's leftovers, and it smelled of dust, lamp oil, and old incense that no amount of scrubbing could evict. Paloma went past his own desk to the recent-accessions shelf and pulled the day's lots, all eleven of them, and laid them out in a row.

Then he did the thing he had trained himself never to do. He reached inward, past the ledger-keeper, past the mercenary, down to the place where the covenant had been, and he *listened*.

For nine years there had been nothing there. A silence so complete it had stopped feeling like silence and started feeling like anatomy—the absence of a limb so old you forget which side your balance leaned on. He braced for that nothing.

Instead he felt the locket. Faintly, from across the room, through the felt and the velvet and the brass: *let her sleep, let her sleep,* and beneath it, threaded through the answer like a filament through amber, something vast and patient and *wrong*, the way a portrait's eyes are wrong when they follow you.

His oath-mark flared. Not ached—flared, a hot wire drawn across his ribs, and Paloma gripped the edge of the shelf and breathed through it the way he'd once breathed through worse, in another life, kneeling on stones while a dying god pressed its last covenant into his skin.

The mark had gone silent with everything else. Dead brands don't wake. Dead gods don't answer prayers. Whatever was humming inside that locket had answered a prayer nine years too late, and the voice doing the answering belonged to no god Paloma had ever served—and he had served four, briefly, before the fifth had claimed him for good.

He thought of the shape of the answer again. *Vast. Patient. Hungry.* Prayers answered properly feel like being held. This felt like being *weighed*.

Paloma straightened, pulled the locket from the shelf, and carried it to the strongbox room, where he signed it into quarantine under a pretext so routine it wouldn't be questioned for days: suspected forgery pending metallurgical review. Then he climbed to the third floor, sat at his desk, took out a fresh sheet, and began—carefully, in cipher—to list every answered-prayer relic the guild had bought in the past month.

There were six. There should have been zero. There had been zero, every year, for nine years, and now there were six in a single season, and every one of them had come through channels Vess's agents controlled.

Outside the window, the rain thickened toward sleet. Down in the yard, a carter shouted at his mule. The guildhouse went on being the guildhouse, warm and ordinary and full of small debts.

Paloma wrote the sixth entry, blotted the page, and sat looking at the seven words he'd written at the bottom:

*All six hum. All six answer in one voice.*

Under his ribs, patient as a debt accruing interest, the mark began to ache again.
