## 2. Answers Nobody Asked For

The frost came to Marrowgate the way a debt collector comes: politely at first, then all at once.

Paloma Underhill heard about it in the guildhouse kitchens, where cook's boy Tam was showing off blisters on his fingers. "Wished for snow, didn't he," Tam said, cradling his hand. "Little Wren Halloway, down Cooper's Lane. First snowfall in nine years — everyone said so, everyone remembered it. And then it kept coming." He turned his palm toward the fire as if warming it might undo what cold had done. "Three days of it. The drifts took the door off his mother's house. Took old man Corver's greenhouse. Took Corver."

"Corver's dead?" Paloma asked, and hated how flat his own voice sounded.

"Frozen sitting up in his chair, they say. Smiling." Tam laughed, the way boys laugh when laughing is safer than thinking. "Got his wish, didn't he. Snow."

Paloma carried that word — *smiling* — through the morning like a stone in his boot. He had spent nine years teaching himself not to hear prayers. It had been the hardest discipline of his life, harder than blade-work or ledger-craft, because prayer is everywhere once you know its texture: the mutter of old women over market scales, the desperate arithmetic of gamblers, the silent screaming want of every soul who has ever stood at a deathbed. His god had gone quiet, and Paloma had learned to go quiet too, two silences folded into each other like hands.

But now someone was answering.

He saw the relic before noon. A fishwife brought it in wrapped in oilcloth — a child's wooden horse, cheaply carved, one ear chipped, worn smooth by small hands. "My girl prayed on it," the woman said, setting it on Paloma's appraisal table the way you'd set down something that might bite. "Prayed her brother home from the sea. And he came home." Her jaw worked. "All of him came home. Every drowned sailor from that wreck came walking out of the surf, dry as salt cod, smiling, and none of them would say a word. Just smiled. My boy hasn't spoken since. Doesn't eat unless I put food in front of him. Stands at the window all night, looking north."

North. Toward the Spire. Paloma wrote the thought down without knowing why.

The horse hummed when he touched it. Not sound exactly — pressure, a resonance behind his teeth, in the scarred oath-mark beneath his collarbone that had been a dead letter for nine years and now pulsed like a second pulse. He knew this feeling. This was what an answered prayer felt like from the outside: divinity caught in an object like light in amber. Except his god's answers had always tasted of rain and iron and patience. This tasted of *hunger*. Of something eating a meal it had waited a very long time for.

"Take it back," he heard himself say.

"It's worth something, surely—"

"Madam." He closed the oilcloth over the horse with exaggerated care, as if tucking in a corpse. "I'll pay you double appraisal for it, and you'll never touch it again. And if your daughter prays again — anything, a lost coin, clear weather — you come tell me first. Before anyone."

* * *

Within the week there were eleven relics in the guild's deep vaults, and within the fortnight, thirty-one.

The Guild of Impossible Things had always traded in miracles' leftovers — saints' fingerbones that were probably pig bone, love philtres that worked only on the gullible, prophecy dice that rolled true one time in six, which was better odds than most prophets managed. Appraising such things was Paloma's particular gift, or had been, back when his training meant something. Now the impossible was arriving daily at their door, and the guild did what the guild always did with the impossible.

It bought it. Quietly.

Master Quillon issued the directive in his dry, unremarkable voice, and that dryness itself was remarkable. Quillon was a man who got loud about miscounted change; now he was issuing sealed instructions — *acquire all answered-prayer artifacts, no questions recorded, sellers compensated generously and sworn* — in the tone of a man ordering ink.

"We're not cataloguing them," Sable said that night. She'd let herself into his rooms with the ease of twenty years' friendship, dropped onto his windowsill, and was cleaning her nails with a knife he'd have sworn she wasn't carrying when she walked in. "I checked. They're going into Vault Nine. The restricted one. The one even senior appraisers don't get into."

"The founder's vault," Paloma said.

"The founder's vault. Which is interesting, isn't it? Because Vess has been dust for sixty years, but her seal is still the only one opening it, and somehow Quillon's suddenly using it." Sable pointed the knife at him. "You know something, Underhill. You've known something since the Halloway boy's snow. You've got your appraiser face on right now, which means you've got your other face on underneath it."

"I know that a child wished for snow," Paloma said slowly, "and got enough snow to kill a man. I know that a widow asked for her husband back and got something wearing him. I know that every answered prayer in this city tastes the same, and it doesn't taste like any god I ever studied." He paused. "And I know they're all looking north."

Sable went still, knife forgotten. "The Spire."

"The Spire," Paloma agreed. Where the world wore thin. Where the gods had gone silent nine years ago, all at once, mid-breath, like a choir cut off by a slammed door. Everyone said *dead*. Paloma had wanted desperately to believe *dead*, because the alternative was that his covenant still bound him to something that simply refused to speak.

"There's a widow on Tanner's Row," Sable said after a moment, quieter. "Third one this week. Prayed her husband back from a fever. Got him back." She looked at the window, at the dark beyond it. "He sits at the table every evening and smiles at her. All evening. Doesn't blink near enough. Their daughter ran away to an aunt's house yesterday. Said the thing at the table wasn't watching her, but it was *listening* to her, and she could feel it."

"Someone should look at him," Paloma said. "Properly. With instruments."

"Quillon says the guild doesn't do fieldwork on active manifestations."

"And what do you say?"

Sable smiled the smile she used on people she was about to rob. "I say we never had this conversation, and tomorrow I happen to be walking past Tanner's Row."

* * *

What they found on Tanner's Row taught Paloma the shape of his dread.

The husband — Brannoc, formerly a cooper, formerly warm-blooded — sat exactly where his wife said he sat. He was a well-made imitation of a man, down to the calluses, down to the fever-scar on his temple that Paloma verified against the parish records was accurately reproduced. But his breathing was decorative. It moved nothing. When Sable passed her hand before his eyes, the eyes tracked it a half-second late, like a puppet remembering its cues.

And when Paloma leaned close — closer than sense allowed, closer than he'd leaned over any relic — and listened with the trained hollow place inside him where his god's voice used to live, he heard it.

Not words. A voice doesn't need words when it has appetite. It was vast and patient and *pleased*, the way a spider might be pleased, the way a larder-owner is pleased taking inventory. And threaded through that pleasure, faint as wire through wax, was something else — something that made Paloma's knees nearly buckle and his oath-mark blaze hot enough that he had to press his fist against his chest to keep it from showing through his shirt.

Underneath the hunger. Faint, frayed, half-strangled. A voice he knew.

Nine years silent, and here it was, buried inside the answering voice like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Like a man calling through a locked door, so muffled the caller might be dying, might be dreaming, might be shouting a warning or a name —

His god was not dead. His god was *inside* whatever was answering, and it was not answering. It was being *used*.

"Paloma." Sable's hand on his arm, sharp. "Your nose is bleeding."

He touched his lip, looked at the blood, and made himself step back from the thing at the table. It turned its head to follow him — smoothly, all at once, like a lighthouse beam — and it smiled, and in the smile he heard, clear as a bell struck underwater:

***witness***

Then nothing. The thing at the table went back to its smiling, and the widow thanked them shakily for visiting, and outside the snow was beginning to fall again, soft as ash, though no child had wished for it this time.

They walked six streets in silence before Sable spoke. "That word. Did it say—"

"Witness." Paloma wiped his nose. His hands were shaking and he let her see it, because she was Sable, and lying to her cost more than the truth ever had. "Sable, I need to tell you something, and I need you to let me tell it in order, because if you interrupt me I'm going to lose my nerve, and I don't think either of us can afford that anymore."

She looked at him across the falling snow — his oldest friend, his partner in twenty years of small dishonesties — and nodded once.

"My god isn't dead," Paloma said. "And I think our guild built the cage he's in."

Behind them, unnoticed, the snow on Tanner's Row had stopped falling upward of a certain height, gathering instead against the sky itself, in a slow white column, drifting north.
