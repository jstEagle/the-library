## 4. The Oath That Wouldn't Die

The mark woke on a Tuesday.

Paloma knew it was Tuesday because the guildhouse ran on Tuesdays—appraisal day, when the impossible things came down from the vaults and were weighed, catalogued, and priced according to how much trouble they'd cause. He was holding a brass astrolabe that had once belonged to a drowned astronomer, turning it under the lamplight, when the scar on his left forearm opened like an eye.

Not bleeding. Not tearing. Just—opening. The old covenant mark, a spiral of pale tissue he'd spent nine years telling himself was only a scar, flushed warm as bathwater and then hot as a forge, and through it Paloma heard a sound he had not heard since the gods went silent: his own name, spoken in a voice like pages turning.

*Paloma.*

He dropped the astrolabe. It rang against the appraisal table, rolled, and stopped at the edge without falling over—which it should have done, which any honest object would have done—and in the ringing silence of the guildhall he sat very still and listened to his blood move.

"Underhill." Magister Corvane's shadow fell across the table before the man did. "The drowned astronomer's piece. Is there a problem with it?"

"No problem." Paloma slid the astrolabe back into its velvet cradle with hands that almost didn't shake. "Static shock. Dry winter air."

Corvane looked at him a moment too long. The magister had a face like a closed ledger—all columns and no commentary—and eyes that had been appraising people longer than Paloma had been alive. "Dry winter air," he repeated. "In October."

"The season comes earlier every year."

It did, actually. That was the trouble. That was the whole terrible shape of things now: the frost on the windowsills that melted wrong, the snow that fell upward in the Tanners' Quarter, the widow on Candlemaker's Row whose husband had come home speaking in a voice that made dogs weep. The answers were getting bolder. And somewhere beneath Paloma's skin, something that should have been dead was stretching after nine years of stillness, flexing fingers it did not have.

He finished the appraisal. He logged the astrolabe. He walked home through streets where the frost grew in spirals—the same spiral as his mark, though he told himself that meant nothing—and he did not sleep so much as fall into it like a man falling into deep water.

That night, the ledger came for him.

* * *

He dreamed of the guildhall, but wrong. The shelves stretched up into darkness that had no ceiling, and the books breathed. When he pulled one down—a slim volume bound in grey leather with no title—he found it full of entries in handwriting he recognized because it was his own, except he had never written any of it:

*Day 1 of silence. Prayed at the shrine of the Ninefold Tide. No answer. Prayed again. The candle would not light.*

*Day 40. The faithful are leaving. I have counted them going like birds.*

*Day 200. I do not know if You are dead or merely quiet. There is a difference. I refuse to stop believing there is a difference.*

"I wrote those," Paloma said aloud, in the dream, in the dark. "I burned those."

*You burned what you could carry,* said the voice like turning pages. *I kept what you could not.*

The book turned its own pages. Entries he had never made unspooled past his thumb—prayers, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a small desperate ledger-line: *keep her safe, let the harvest hold, tell me why, tell me anything.* And threaded through them all, faint as watermark, a voice trying to answer and finding the answer crushed flat, pressed silent, sealed into stone at the edge of the world.

"You're alive." His throat worked. "You've been alive this whole time. In the Spire."

*Alive is a generous word. Present is more honest. I am present the way a man is present in a locked room.* A pause, long enough that Paloma felt it in his chest. *I did not call you to worship me, Paloma Underhill. I have had nine years to consider what I would say if the silence ever cracked, and I find I do not want your knees. I want your eyes.*

"My eyes."

*Witness. That was always the covenant's true shape—you knew that once. Not service. Not sacrifice. Someone who sees what the divine does and tells it true, even when the telling costs.* The pages rustled. *She has taken my voice and is wearing it. She answers prayers with my stolen mouth. Every prayer she answers falsely, the world believes it was me. I need someone to see the difference and write it down.*

"And if I do? What does it cost?"

The dream-ledger turned one final page, and the entry there was blank except for a single line, written in ink that moved like frost spreading across glass:

*Everything you cannot carry. Same terms as always.*

Paloma woke with the mark burning cold instead of hot, and the smell of ink in the room, and on his desk—his actual desk, in his actual rooms above the counting-house—a single sheet of paper he had not put there. On it, in his own handwriting, which he had absolutely not written:

*Fragment three of seven. The chapel under Fisherman's Stair. Go before they do.*

* * *

They were already watching him. He understood that by the third day.

It was small things at first. Corvane requesting his appraisal logs "for audit"—the first audit in eleven years of guild record-keeping. Two of the enforcers, Hesk and the twins, taking up posts near the archive stairs with the studied idleness of men pretending to guard nothing. The restricted stacks, which Paloma had been picking open for a month with increasing desperation, suddenly acquiring new locks, new wards, and a faint smell of burnt feathers that meant Vess herself had renewed the protections personally.

Vess. The founder. The woman whose memoirs he'd read by smuggled lamplight, page after sealed page confessing in beautiful, terrible prose exactly what she had done: how she had caught the gods like moths, how she had built the Spire around their screaming, how she had fed their voices into the thing she was growing in the dark. Paloma carried those pages in his chest now like swallowed coals. The guild had taken him in when he was nothing—a mercenary with a dead god's brand and no trade but violence—and the guild had built the cage that killed his god. Both things were true. Both things were true, and he did not know how to hold them in the same pair of hands.

On the fourth day, Sable found him in the map room, and he knew before she spoke that she'd been sent.

Sable Quill looked like what she was: the best acquisition agent the Guild of Impossible Things had ever fielded, a compact woman with grey-shot hair and a knife smile, who could talk a bishop out of his relics and make him thank her for the privilege. She had pulled him out of a burning safehouse in Kettering twelve years ago. She had taught him ledger-script. She was, in every way that mattered and several ways that defied accounting, his oldest friend.

"Audit," she said, dropping into the chair across from him. "Corvane's calling it an audit. We both know what it is."

"Do we?"

"Paloma." Just his false name, flat, no play in it. "Hesk saw you coming out of the restricted stacks at two in the morning with Vess's seal broken on the door. He reported it. I erased the report." She let that sit between them like a coin on a table. "Then I erased the log of the report existing. Then I stayed up until dawn wondering why I did that, and I've decided I'd like to hear it from you before I decide whether to do it again."

The map room was quiet. Somewhere below, the counting-floor murmured with the day's trades; somewhere above, frost crept down the windows in spirals. Paloma looked at the one person in the world he had never lied to about anything that mattered, and understood that whatever he said next would either save him or cost her everything.

"I was doing research," he said.

"On what?"

"Prayers."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "The answered ones."

"All of them. Answered and unanswered. Where they go. Who hears them. Who—" he chose the word carefully, watching her face, "—who isn't hearing them anymore."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Outside, snow began to fall—in slow, deliberate flakes that drifted sideways, then upward, then hung motionless in the air like a held breath. Neither of them looked at it. They had all stopped remarking on the weather; that was the city's new etiquette.

"You think the guild did something," she said finally. "To the gods."

"I think the guild's founder did something. Before either of us was born."

"And you've been breaking into her archive at night to prove it." Sable rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger, the gesture she made when the numbers wouldn't balance. "Paloma. If you're right—if Vess did *something* to the gods—then this guild isn't a sanctuary. It's a crime scene. And everyone we love works here."

"Now you understand why I didn't want to say it out loud."

"I understand why you should have said it to me months ago." Her voice didn't rise, which was worse. "You've been carrying this alone while the city freezes and our own people start asking questions, and the only reason you're not in a cell right now is that I'm better at erasing ledgers than Hesk is at reading them. That's not loyalty, that's arithmetic, and I resent being reduced to a sum."

"Sable—"

"Don't." She stood, straightening her coat with sharp little tugs. "I covered for you tonight. Tomorrow night I can't promise. Whatever you're doing—whatever you *believe*—you have maybe three days before Corvane stops trusting the paperwork and starts trusting his eyes. Use them."

She got to the door. Stopped. Didn't turn around.

"When we pulled you out of Kettering," she said, "you told me the oath-mark was just a scar. You swore it. You swore it with the face you use when you're telling the truth."

"It was a scar," Paloma said quietly. "It's waking up."

Sable's hand rested on the door handle a moment longer. Then she left, and the door clicked shut behind her with the soft finality of a ledger closing.

* * *

That night he went to the chapel under Fisherman's Stair.

It wasn't much—a squat stone cellar where the tide used to reach, salt-crusted, abandoned, its shrine-niche empty for nine years. But when Paloma pried up the loose flagstone the dream-ledger had named, his mark flared so bright it lit the dark like a lantern through skin, and there in the cavity lay a fragment of the covenant: a strip of vellum, ancient and silvered, humming with warmth.

Words moved across it as he touched it. Not commands. Not demands. An invitation, patient as tide:

*Seven pieces. Six remain. Bring them north, witness, and we will speak face to face—or leave them here, and live out your life in the warm dark, and I will not blame you. I have never blamed you. That is the first thing I need you to see.*

Paloma knelt in the cold with the fragment in his fist and his friend's warning in his ears—*three days*—and understood that the choice had already narrowed to a blade's width.

Behind him, at the top of the stairs, a lantern flared. Footsteps. More than one set.

Hesk's voice, bored and terrible: "Ledger says nobody's assigned to this district tonight. Care to explain what you're carrying, Underhill?"
