## 5. A Market of Broken Things

The relic was called a Widow's Tooth, and it had killed two men already.

Sable Vane knew this because the guild's ledger said so, in Paloma's own cramped hand: *Two prior owners deceased. Cause: grief, weaponized. Handle with gloves. Do not listen to it sing.*

She'd read the entry four times on the coach north to Grayhollow, and each time the last line snagged her like a fishhook. *Do not listen to it sing.* Paloma Underhill had spent nine years teaching the guild's junior appraisers that every relic sang something—a memory, a hunger, a prayer—and that the appraiser's whole art was learning which songs to hum along with and which to let die unheard. He did not write warnings lightly. He wrote them the way other men wrote wills.

So she went to Grayhollow with gloves, with guild scrip, and with a commission signed by Founder Vess herself: *Recover the Widow's Tooth. Full discretion. The guild does not negotiate with private collectors.*

The collector's house sat at the end of a street the winter had already begun to claim—gutters furred with ice, doorways breathing frost. Sable counted three locks on his door and picked them out of professional courtesy, because he was dead by then. She found him in his study chair, frozen mid-teacup, an expression of mild disappointment etched in rime.

The Widow's Tooth sat on his desk in a lead-lined box, humming.

She could hear it through the box. Through the walls. A thin, sweet note like a wineglass about to break, and underneath it—words. Not quite words. The shape of words, the way a footprint is the shape of a foot. Someone praying. Someone being answered.

*Careful,* she told herself, and did not take off her gloves, and did not listen, and put the box in her satchel without opening it, and only then noticed the second thing on the desk.

A ledger. Small, leather-bound, water-stained—the kind the guild issued to field agents twenty years ago, before they switched to the new folios. It lay open beside the teacup, and the handwriting filling its pages was not the dead collector's.

It was Paloma's.

Sable stood very still in the cold room while the frost crept up her boots, and read.

Not everything. Enough. Entries in Paloma's hand, dated across the past season: *Purchased from the Ferrier estate—one covenant fragment, provenance unknown, sings in a voice I know. Paid triple, burned the receipt.* And below it: *Vess built the cage. The gods are not silent. They are kept.*

Her first thought, absurdly, was that someone had forged his hand. Her second thought was that no forger alive could do it; she'd watched him write ledgers for nine years, had teased him about the way his letters leaned like tired men. Her third thought arrived slowly, wearing the face of every small strangeness of the past months—Paloma's absences, his locked drawer, the way he'd stopped joking about the answered-prayer relics the moment everyone else started laughing about them.

He'd been buying them. Quietly. For months. Every dangerous thing the guild sent agents to recover, he'd been there first, or after, paying out of his own purse, leaving no trail except this one—except the one trail that led here, to a dead man who had apparently been blackmailing him, or trading with him, or both.

The Widow's Tooth hummed in her satchel, patient as a debt.

She took the ledger too.

* * *

She didn't go back to the city. She went where the ledger's last entry told her to: a shrine outside Grayhollow, half-swallowed by a hillside, dedicated to no god currently on any registry. The stone above the door was worn smooth by centuries of touching hands, and carved into it was a spiral—not the guild's sigil, not any of the Nine's marks. Something older. Something that made the oath-scar on her own forearm, the guild-brand every agent carried, itch beneath her sleeve.

Paloma was there when she arrived. Of course he was. He knelt in the shrine's doorway with his coat off despite the cold, working at the base of the altar with a chisel, prying loose a stone that had no business coming loose. A lantern at his elbow lit dust motes and his breath and nothing else.

"You're supposed to be in Grayhollow," he said, without turning around. "Buying back a relic."

"I bought it." She held up the satchel. "I also bought this." She threw the ledger. It landed in the snow between them, pages fanning open to the entry she'd memorized: *Vess built the cage.*

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he set down the chisel, carefully, the way he did everything, and turned, and looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him before—not guilt exactly, and not fear. Readiness. Like a man who'd been waiting years for this exact knock on his door and had rehearsed what he'd say so many times the words had gone smooth.

"Sit down, Sable."

"Don't tell me to sit down."

"It's a long truth." He glanced at the sky, at the fat gray clouds pressing down like a lid. "And we have less of both than you think."

She stayed standing. He talked anyway.

He told her about the covenant mark under his collarbone, scarred and silent for nine years, waking three months ago like a struck bell. He told her about the answered prayers—the child's wish for snow that brought killing frost, the widow whose husband came home wrong, moving like a puppet with too many strings. He told her about the restricted archive, Vess's sealed memoirs, the passage he'd copied out and now recited from memory in a voice gone flat with horror:

*"The gods are not departed. They are sequestered. Divinity, unmanaged, is a public nuisance; managed, it is an asset. I have built the Spire a vault, and the vault a lock, and the lock a key that only I shall hold."*

"He grew up in a famine year," Paloma said. "Prayers went unanswered and her whole village died around her, and she decided the problem wasn't the gods' absence—it was their inconsistency. So she made them consistent. She made them absent." His laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "And the guild—the guild that took me in when my god died and my oath died with him, that fed me and taught me a trade and gave me a name worth having—that guild is the lock on the cage. Has been for two hundred years. We've been guards, Sable. All of us. Well-paid guards who never saw the prison."

The wind moved through the shrine behind him, and from somewhere inside it came a sound like a voice clearing its throat after a long illness.

"And your god," Sable said. "The dead one."

"Not dead." He touched his collarbone through his shirt. "Imprisoned. Collateral damage. He's been screaming into a pillow for nine years and I toasted his funeral every anniversary like a sentimental fool." He looked at her then, fully, and she saw how tired he was, how long he'd been carrying this alone. "I'm gathering the pieces of the covenant. There are seven. I have four. This shrine holds a fifth. When I have all seven, I go north, to the Spire, and I either free him or I die trying, and either way the winter Vess is building stops mattering to me personally."

"What winter?"

He told her that too. The false god grown from stolen prayers, fed on every answered plea in the city, needing one last fragment to finish its gestation—the fragment Paloma himself carried without knowing, sewn into the lining of the oath-medallion he'd worn since the day his god fell silent. Vess hadn't sent agents after him yet. She didn't need to. She only needed him to keep walking, carrying her final ingredient, until the ritual was ripe.

"She knows?" Sable asked. "That you know?"

"Not yet. But she will. She counts everything, Sable. That's the whole religion of her. Ledgers must balance." He picked up the loose stone from the altar's base and turned it over in his hands. Inside, embedded in the rock like a seed, glowed a sliver of something that wasn't quite light. "Which is why you have a choice to make, and I'm not going to make it easier by pretending otherwise. You can report everything. You should, probably. You're a good agent and I'm a traitor by every law the guild has. Or—" he held out the stone, the fragment, the piece of a god's broken word, "—you come north with me, and we find out what the world looks like when nobody owns the gods."

The snow fell between them, fine and dry as ash.

Sable thought about the guildhouse. About the apprentice she was training, who still salted her tea by mistake. About the reading room that smelled of tallow and old paper, the only place she had ever felt competent and safe at the same time. About Vess, whom she had met twice, whose handclasp was cool and brief and left you feeling audited. About nine years of partnership—of Paloma covering her mistakes and her covering his, of shared watches and shared whiskey and the particular intimacy of people who trust each other with ledgers, which is to say with the truth about money, which is to say with everything.

"You're asking me to burn down the only home I've ever had," she said.

"I'm asking you to notice it's a prison," he said. "You don't have to burn anything. You just have to stop guarding the walls."

She looked at the fragment glowing in his palm. She looked at his face, which she knew better than her own reflection, and which was now the face of a man asking her to commit treason beside him.

"No," she said.

His expression didn't change. That was the worst part. He'd expected it. He nodded once, tucked the stone away, and began packing his tools with those careful hands.

"I'll report the relic recovered," she said. "And I'll report that the collector's papers referenced irregular acquisitions by a guild employee. Unnamed. Under investigation." She pulled her coat tight. "That's what I'll say."

Paloma paused. "Sable—"

"That's what I'll say," she repeated, louder, and walked out into the snow before he could see what it cost her, though she suspected he already knew, because he always knew, damn him, that was the whole disease of him.

* * *

The ride back took two days. She spent them not sleeping, the Widow's Tooth humming in its lead box beside her like a conscience, and composing her report seventeen times in her head.

In the end she wrote it short. *Relic secured intact. Collector deceased, natural causes (exposure). Collector's records indicate unauthorized acquisitions of answered-prayer relics by a guild-affiliated party; identity unconfirmed; investigation recommended.* She sealed it, and she did not mention shrines, or fragments, or cages, or the Spire, or the specific gravity of Paloma Underhill's voice when he said *stop guarding the walls.*

Half the truth. She weighed it in her hands like an appraiser and told herself it balanced: enough to satisfy Vess's counting nature, enough to buy Paloma time, enough to keep her own hands technically clean. A good agent's report. A coward's report. Both things were true and she filed it anyway.

Vess received her in the founder's tower, among clocks that all showed different hours. She read the report twice, unhurried, her eyes pale and precise as scales.

"An unnamed party," Vess said. "How diligent of you to preserve the ambiguity. Names create obligations." She smiled, and it reached her eyes the way frost reaches a window. "Investigate further. Discreetly. If our anonymous acquirer surfaces, bring me the person, not the judgment. I'll do the accounting myself."

"Yes, Founder."

"Oh—and Agent Vane." Vess rose, crossed the room, and stood close enough that Sable could smell ink and cold iron. "The Underhill boy. You've partnered with him nine years. If he were ever to become... irregular... I would want to hear it from you first. Loyalty is a currency, and I pay better than anyone. Remember that."

Sable held the pale gaze and kept her face the smooth blank of a closed ledger. "Yes, Founder."

Walking out through the tower's winding stair, she passed a window and saw the city below, and the clouds above it, lower than they'd been that morning. Pressing down. Like a lid.

Somewhere north, a man was climbing toward a prison with pieces of a broken promise in his pockets, and she had just promised to hunt him, and the snow had begun again—fine, dry, falling like ash over everything she loved.

She realized, somewhere around the fourth landing, that she had lied to Vess after all. Not in the report. In the tower. Because loyalty was a currency, yes—but Sable had spent nine years in the ledger trade, and she knew a forged coin when she held one.

Vess had offered to pay her in loyalty.

Sable had never once sold hers.
