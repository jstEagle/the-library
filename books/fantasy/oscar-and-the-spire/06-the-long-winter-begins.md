## 6. The Long Winter Begins

The frost came in through the ledger room's window seam like a creditor who would not be refused.

Paloma noticed it first on the ink. He had left his pen uncapped overnight—a small sin, one of the small sins that made up his life—and by morning the ink had frozen solid in the well, black ice with a white bloom across it like the eye of a dead fish. The window was shut. The brazier in the corner had burned down to a red memory, but the room should not have been cold enough for this. It was autumn. It was supposed to be autumn.

He touched the glass and the cold went through him like a needle finding an old wound—the oath-mark under his collarbone, silent nine years, awake now for three weeks and aching the way a tooth aches before it breaks.

Outside, the city of Corvane lay under a skin of white that no almanac could explain. Not snow. Frost. It climbed the walls of the guildhouse in feathered patterns, and where it touched the prayer-lamps the poor kept burning in their niches, the flames burned blue and gave no heat. Paloma watched a lamplighter on the far street stand before one of those blue flames, rubbing his hands toward it, getting nothing. Then he watched the man stop rubbing, and tilt his head, as though listening to something inside the fire.

*That's how it starts,* Paloma thought. *First you warm your hands at the wrong light. Then you kneel to it.*

Three weeks since he'd found Vess's sealed memoirs in the restricted archive. Three weeks since he'd learned that the silence of the gods was not death but architecture—that Vess herself had built the cage, and that his own god, the quiet one whose covenant mark he carried like a scar from a war nobody remembered, had been collateral. Three weeks since Sable had covered for him with the enforcers and then asked her questions, gentle and terrible, that he still hadn't answered.

And three days since the frost began.

* * *

Vess summoned him at the ninth bell, which meant she already knew.

Her office sat at the top of the guildhouse, above the vaults, above the reliquary, above all the ledgers of impossible things—positioned, Paloma had long understood, so that everything in the building existed beneath its founder's consideration. She received him standing at her window, watching the frost climb the glass from the outside as if racing her to the view.

"Paloma Underhill," she said, without turning. "Appraiser second class. Keeper of small debts. Eleven years in my house."

"Ten years, eleven months," Paloma said. "You have the figure in your books."

"I have every figure in my books." Now she turned, and her face was the pleasant, patient mask she wore for donors and doubters alike. "Which is what makes the discrepancy interesting. Three weeks ago, someone accessed the restricted archive using credentials that belonged to a dead appraiser. Someone read pages of my memoirs that have been sealed since before this city had gaslight. And someone has been making inquiries—quiet ones, careful ones, *professional* ones—about shrines in the northern hills." She smiled. "You see my difficulty. I taught half these people their tradecraft myself."

"The archive has four hundred keys," Paloma said. "Half the senior staff could—"

"Don't insult us both. It's tiring, and we have so little time." She crossed to her desk and laid one hand flat upon it, and Paloma felt the temperature of the room drop—not gradually, but all at once, like stepping into deep water. "I'm going to tell you a story, and you're going to listen, because whether or not you are the leak, you are going to want to hear the ending."

He listened. There wasn't anything else to do.

"There was a god once," Vess said, "that no one worshipped loudly enough to matter. A god of debts paid and debts forgiven. Small prayers. Ledger-prayers. *Let me make rent. Let me bury my father. Let me get through winter.* Do you know why such gods die first, Paloma? Because their faithful only pray when they're desperate, and desperate people make poor congregations. They take and never tithe." Her fingers drummed the desk, once. "But a dying god is still a god, and a god—even a starving one—is a door. And doors can be *harvested*."

"And so you harvested," Paloma said quietly. "All of them. You didn't kill the old gods. You locked them in the Spire and grew yourself a new one out of what they dropped."

Something moved behind Vess's pleasant mask—not anger. Appetite. "You did read the memoirs."

"I read enough to know your false god needs feeding. And I know what's happening out there—" he gestured at the frosted window, at the city beyond it, at the lamplighter who had knelt to a blue flame— "isn't feeding. It's *weaning*. Those answered prayers aren't mercy. They're a thing teaching people to need it."

"They are a thing learning," Vess corrected. "There's a difference, and it matters. A newborn god must practice. Yes, the widow got back a husband who was wrong. Yes, the child's snow came down sharp enough to kill. Every birth is messy." She spread her hands. "But imagine it finished, Paloma. Imagine a god with no cage around it, no hunger it didn't choose, answering *every* prayer correctly, forever. No more silence. No more desperate people praying to things too starved to answer. That is not tyranny. That is *infrastructure*."

"That is a trap with better hinges," Paloma said.

For a moment neither of them moved. The frost on the window had reached the center of the pane, spreading in the shape of something almost like a hand.

"You have something of mine," Vess said at last, softly. "You've carried it for years without knowing. A fragment of a dead covenant, lodged in you like a splinter. My creation needs it to finish falling into winter—to finish *becoming*. I could take it from you. Don't mistake courtesy for weakness; taking it would be simple, and I have people downstairs for whom simple is a vocation. But a fragment torn free is diminished. Given freely, it sings." Her smile returned, warm as a hearth, false as painted fire. "So I am offering you partnership. Your name in the founding ledger. A share of a living god. All you have to do is stop believing in ghosts."

Paloma looked at her—at the founder of the only place that had ever taken him in, fed him, taught him to read the true weight of things—and felt the betrayal arrive a second time, colder than the first.

"You know what my god asked me?" he said. "Not worship. Never that. It asked me to *witness*. To remember what was owed and what was paid and to say so out loud, even if saying so cost me." He pulled his collar aside, and let her see the oath-mark burning on his collarbone like a coal. "Your thing out there doesn't ask. It answers questions nobody asked, and then it collects. We are not the same religion, Vess. And I am not for sale."

The pleasant mask finally fell away, and what was underneath was older and hungrier and very tired.

"Yes," she said. "Sable said you'd say something like that."

The name landed like a knife between his ribs. Paloma kept his face still—he'd had eleven years of practice keeping his face still—but inside, something large and load-bearing cracked.

"She reported half of what she learned," Vess continued, watching him with genuine interest now, the way one watches a specimen. "The inquiries about the shrines. Your research. She kept back the rest—your accusations against me, your talk of covenants. Loyalty, of a kind. Or arithmetic." She pressed a small brass bell on her desk. "Either way, Underhill, the arithmetic is done. You are a debt the guild cannot carry into winter."

The office door opened. Four enforcers in grey, and among them, not meeting his eyes, Sable—present, required, complicit by attendance. Paloma searched her face for the friend who had covered for him, who had carried his secrets like fragile cargo, and found a woman looking very hard at the floor.

"Take him to the cold rooms," Vess said. "We'll discuss terms when he's had time to think. The fragment keeps, but the season doesn't."

* * *

They marched him down through the guildhouse, past the appraisal floors where he'd spent a decade learning the weight of truth, past clerks who looked away, past the great ledger hall where the names of impossible things were recorded in ink that never froze.

Except today it froze.

He saw it happen. A scribe mid-stroke, her pen stopping, the black line blooming white as the ink crystallized on the page. Then the ink in the wells. Then the moisture in the air, hanging in glittering suspension. The frost was inside the guildhouse now, climbing the stairs ahead of them, moving with purpose, moving *toward* him—or rather toward the thing he carried, the splinter of covenant in his chest, calling it home like a bell calling a congregation.

One of the enforcers swore. Another raised a lantern, and the flame inside burned blue and gave no heat, and the enforcer holding it tilted his head, listening, just as the lamplighter had.

"No," Paloma said, and his voice cracked the trance. "Don't listen to it. Whatever it sounds like—it sounds kind. That's the trick. Kindness with a hook in it."

The frost reached them on the landing above the cold rooms, sheeting the stone in white feathers, and through the ice he saw it: shapes forming in the frost itself. Faces. Hundreds of faces, mouths open, mid-plea—the unanswered prayers of centuries, preserved like flies in amber, and every one of them turning, slowly, to look at him.

*Witness,* whispered something through the ice, in a voice that was almost his god's and was not, the way a portrait is almost a person. *You wanted to witness. Witness what we are becoming.*

"That's not witnessing," Paloma said to the ice. "That's an audience. And you don't get one. Not from me."

He drove his palm against the frosted wall, against the oath-mark, and the mark flared—not pain this time but *direction*, a compass-needle sensation, pointing north through the stone, through the city, through the coming dark, to the hills where his god's faithful had kept shrines, to the Spire beyond them at the edge of the world.

The last fragment. His fragment. The piece his god needed—and the piece Vess's thing needed—and there was suddenly, terribly, only one direction either of them could go.

The cell they put him in was iron and cold, and they took his knives and his lockpicks and his boots, which showed a certain amateurism, because they left him his belt. Nine years of guild training counted for something. So did desperation. It took him until the middle watch to work the hinge-pin loose, and when he dropped into the service corridor below, the whole guildhouse was screaming with frost and alarm-bells, and the city beyond the windows had gone fully, utterly white.

Snow was falling now—real snow, thick and silent, burying the streets in an hour. Killing frost in harvest fields. Blue flames in every shrine. And somewhere north, a false god finishing its fall into dominion, and Vess's hunters already spilling into the streets behind him with lanterns and chains.

He ran. Through the frozen garden, over the wall where the ivy had become a ladder of ice, into the white roaring streets of a city that had stopped being autumn. Behind him, the guildhouse bells rang the alarm he'd heard only twice in eleven years—the one that meant a keeper had stolen from the guild.

Ahead, north, through the snow: the road to the hills, to the shrines, to the fragments of a covenant that would cost him everything he remembered.

And leaning against the gatepost at the garden's edge, arms crossed, snow gathering on her shoulders, waiting for him with a packed bag at her feet—

Sable.

"I lied to her," she said, before he could speak. "About which shrine you'd go to first. We have maybe an hour. Can you walk fast and explain covenants at the same time?"

Paloma stared at her, at the bag, at the white road north where everything he was would be spent paying for everything the world had lost.

"Yes," he said. "Both of those. In fact, I've been practicing."

She threw him the bag. Together they ran into the long winter.
