## 7. Fragments and Forgiveness

The road north was white within a day of leaving the city, and after that it was only white, and the two of them moving through it like ink marks someone had forgotten to blot.

Paloma walked with his collar up and his hands in his sleeves and did not look back. He had looked back once, at the top of the ridge above Grayharbor, when the city was still visible as a smudge of gray against the frozen bay—had seen the frost climbing the harbor cranes like ivy, had seen ships locked fast in ice that had come in a single night—and he had made himself stop looking after that, because every look was a debt, and he could no longer afford debts.

"You're limping," Sable said.

"I'm walking."

"You're limping *and* walking. There's a difference. It's in my ledger."

He almost smiled. That was the cruelty of Sable: she could make you almost smile while your whole world came apart behind you. She had defected three days ago—not with speeches, not with tears, but by standing in the doorway of the safehouse with her travel pack already on her shoulder and saying, "I watched a woman freeze solid at a bread line while the Guild's couriers walked past carrying sealed writs. I'm done pretending the ledgers balance." And then she had fallen into step beside him as if they'd planned this for years, which in some way they had, though neither of them had ever said so.

"Left foot," she said now. "You've been favoring it since the river."

"The river tried to kill me."

"The river is a river. It does what rivers do." She dug in her pack without breaking stride and tossed him a twist of waxed cloth. "Willow bark. Chew it. If we lose you to a bad ankle before we even find a shrine, I'll be very annoyed, and I'll write it down."

He chewed. The bark was bitter and old, and somewhere beneath the bitterness there was another taste, faint as a coin held too long in the mouth—the taste of the first fragment, the one he'd pulled from the drowned chapel under Grayharbor three nights ago. A shard of slate no bigger than a thumbnail, carved with a word in a language that predated writing, and when he'd closed his hand around it, something had gone out of him like water through fingers.

He still didn't know what it was he'd lost. That was the obscenity of it. You couldn't grieve what you couldn't name. He knew only that there was a hole in him shaped like something warm, and Sable kept looking at him sideways with an expression he couldn't read, and once she had started to say—"Paloma, do you remember when—" and then stopped, and pressed her lips together, and hadn't finished.

She carried what he forgot. That had been the covenant's price, revealed in the dream-ledgers: *each fragment must be paid for in witness*. Not worship. Witness. His god had never wanted knees; it had wanted eyes, and memory, and someone to hold the account. But the fragments were broken, and broken things took payment clumsily. Each piece of the covenant he gathered tore a memory out of him and set it somewhere else—and Sable, walking beside him with her ledger-book and her terrible arithmetic, had volunteered to keep them.

"You don't have to," he had told her.

"No," she'd agreed. "I don't. Write that down too."

* * *

The second shrine stood in the ruins of a shepherd's village above the Vale of Thorns, and they reached it at dusk on the fifth day, when the cold had stopped being weather and become a presence, something following them with its own patient footsteps.

The village had died long before the winter—thirty years, forty. Roofs had fallen in; a stone well stood open-mouthed to the sky. But the shrine was intact, because shrines to Paloma's god had been built by people who expected to be forgotten and built accordingly: low, squat, half-buried in the hillside, roofed with slabs heavy enough to outlast empires.

Inside, the air was dry as paper. An old altar. A niche in the wall. And in the niche, exactly where the dream-ledger had shown him, a fragment: a strip of bronze, green with age, coiled like a shed snakeskin, inscribed along its length with tiny punched dots that resolved into meaning only when he wasn't looking directly at them.

Sable waited at the threshold. They had learned this much: she couldn't watch him take a piece. Or rather—he suspected, watching her jaw go tight as he knelt—that she could watch, but chose not to, because what happened next was not hers to see unless she asked.

"It's all right," he said. It had become their liturgy. "I'm ready."

"You're never ready," she said. "Nobody's ready. Say it anyway."

"I'm ready anyway."

He picked up the bronze.

The payment came the way it always did—not as pain, worse than pain, as *subtraction*. The world went briefly, perfectly silent, and in that silence a room assembled itself around him: lamplight, rain on glass, a desk in the guildhouse, and across the desk a young clerk with ink-stained fingers laughing at something he'd just said, and Paloma knew her name, knew the year, knew the joke—

—and then he knew none of it. The lamplight guttered. The rain stopped mid-fall. The laugh ended before it finished, and he was kneeling in a cold shrine holding a piece of bronze, and there was a woman in the doorway watching him with wet eyes and a ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, and he could not for his life remember why her face made his chest ache.

"Sable," he said. Her name was in the ledger too, written there in his own hand from a version of himself who had thought ahead. "Her name is Sable. She is my friend. She carries what I lose."

"That's right," she said, steady, steady. "That's right, Paloma."

"What did it take?"

She hesitated. He watched her decide—watched the whole argument happen behind her eyes in the space of a breath—and then she opened the ledger to a page near the front, where her handwriting sat dense and careful, and read aloud:

"*Fragment two. Cost: the first time Paloma understood what the Guild was for. Autumn, nine years ago. A clerk named Wren showed him how a debt could be forgiven without being paid, and he laughed, and it was the first time since his oath-mark went quiet that he felt like a person instead of a weapon.*"

The words went into him like water into cracked ground. He didn't recover the memory—he understood that was impossible, that it lived in her book now and nowhere else—but he recovered its *shape*, the way an amputee recovers an itch. He nodded slowly.

"Wren," he said. "I think I liked Wren."

"She still owes you four coppers," Sable said, and closed the book, and wiped her eyes quickly with the heel of her hand as if brushing away snow. "Come on. Cold's coming down off the ridge."

* * *

They slept that night in the shepherd's shrine because it was the only roof for miles, and because Paloma would not leave a fragment overnight where the false god's hunger could reach it. The pieces hummed faintly in his pack now—slate and bronze together, whispering to each other in a language of pressure and pulse, like two halves of a sentence trying to remember the middle.

He woke once, deep in the black hours, and Sable was awake too, sitting with her back against the altar, the ledger open on her knees, reading by the light of a stub of candle.

"Reading?" he asked.

"Keeping," she corrected. "There's a difference."

"Show me one."

She was quiet for a long moment. The candle fluttered. "No," she said finally. "Not tonight."

"Sable."

"You know what happens if I read them to you? Nothing. Nothing happens. That's the worst part. I can tell you every word of what you lost and you'll nod like a stranger hearing about someone else's mother." Her voice stayed flat and precise, which was how he knew it was costing her. "So I keep them. All of them. And someday—if we live, if the Spire gives you back anything—you'll get to meet yourself again, page by page, and I'll get to watch. That's the trade. That's the deal I made, and I don't renegotiate."

He lay in the dark and listened to the wind worrying the hillside, and thought about the shape of her kindness, which had always been built like her ledgers: double-entry, unsentimental, load-bearing.

"There were six shrines," he said. "In the dream-ledger. We've found two."

"I counted seven," Sable said.

He turned his head. "Six."

"Seven. Look—" She turned the ledger toward him, and there it was, in her cramped hand: a list of sites, coordinates, fragments of his god's scattered body, and the seventh entry circled twice, with a note beside it in fresh ink: *"This one isn't in your dreams. It's in Vess's memoirs. Page 214. She kept a fragment herself."*

The cold in the shrine deepened, or perhaps that was only him.

"The last piece," Paloma said slowly, "has been sitting in the Founder's private archive this whole time."

"Or her vault. Or her pocket. Somewhere we can't reach it." Sable blew out the candle. In the sudden dark her voice came steady and grim. "Which means the last piece of your god is wherever Vess is taking it. North, Paloma. Same direction we're going. Same direction everything's going."

Outside, far off across the frozen vale, something answered the wind—a sound like a voice trying on a thousand stolen prayers at once, deciding which fit best—and they both lay awake a long time, listening to it come closer.
