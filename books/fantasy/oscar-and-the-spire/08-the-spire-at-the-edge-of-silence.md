## 8. The Spire at the Edge of Silence

The Spire did not look climbable. That was the first honest thing it told us.

It rose out of the ice plain like a needle pulled from the world's spine, black stone veined with frozen light, so tall that its summit vanished into a sky the color of old bruises. No door. No stairs on the outside. Just wind that screamed in a voice I almost recognized, and snow falling upward, as if even winter here had forgotten which way was down.

Sable stood beside me at the base, her breath tearing away in ribbons. "Tell me there's another way."

"There isn't."

"Then tell me a lie. I'd settle for a well-crafted one right now."

I pressed my palm against the stone. The covenant fragments in my satchel—seven of them now, each paid for with a piece of myself I could no longer name—hummed against my ribs like caged birds. And the Spire answered. A seam of light split the rock before us, and stairs unfolded downward into the mountain's heart, then turned and climbed.

"Of course," Sable muttered. "Impossible things. It's your whole brand."

We went in.

* * *

The first trial waited on the third landing, and it was a woman made of dust.

She stood where the stair narrowed, assembled from ash and grave-soil and the grey residue of nine years' silence, and when she spoke, her voice was every voice I had ever failed to hear.

"You buried me in the spring," she said. "I prayed to you for rain for my crops. I prayed for forty days. My children ate boiled leather. I prayed until my knees broke open. You said nothing."

"I know," I said.

The words came out of me strangely—not chosen but surrendered, the way blood leaves a wound. Because this was not a demon wearing a grievance. This was the grievance itself. Every plea my god had ever ignored, unanswered, unacknowledged, had not simply died. They had fallen somewhere. And somewhere was here, stacked floor by floor through the Spire's throat, waiting for the one man who carried the covenant to walk past them and look them in the face.

"Say it plainly," the woman of dust demanded. "Why?"

"He could not answer." My throat worked. "He was already caged. The silence wasn't neglect. It was theft."

"That is an excuse."

"It is a fact." I made myself hold her gaze—dust does not have eyes, but hers burned anyway. "And it doesn't bring back your children or your harvest. I won't pretend it does. He failed you twice: once by being silent, once by being caught. I'm sorry. I can't unsorry it. But I will carry it up with me, and what I build at the top will be built knowing your name."

Her name surfaced in me like a splinter—Maren Ostwick, farmer, mother, dead eleven years—and she bowed her head, and came apart, and the dust settled over the stairs like a blessing or an accusation, and we climbed on.

There were hundreds more.

* * *

By the fortieth landing I had stopped counting hours. Time moved strangely inside the Spire; the windows showed night and noon and seasons that had never happened, all at once. By the sixtieth, my legs shook and my voice had gone raw from speaking truths I'd spent nine years burying under ledgers and appraisals and the comfortable fiction that a dead god asked nothing of anyone.

A soldier who had prayed for his brother's life and received a corpse. A sailor whose drowning prayer went unheard while his shipmates lived. A leper. A bride left at the altar who had begged not for love but only for the courage to walk out of the church alone, and been denied even that.

To each one I gave the same currency: honesty without flinching. Not *it wasn't his fault*. Not *the gods are cruel and we must accept it*. Just: *this happened, it was wrong, I remember you.*

Some of them raged. Some wept. One—an old man made entirely of frozen breath—simply nodded and stepped aside before I finished speaking, as if he'd only ever wanted to be counted.

Sable climbed beside me through all of it. She didn't speak during the trials. She kept her hand near her knife out of habit, though steel would have done nothing here, and she watched me with an expression I couldn't read in the stair-light. Pity, maybe. Or the careful face of someone memorizing a man she expected to lose.

On the ninetieth landing, she finally broke.

"This is a slaughterhouse," she said quietly. "Not of bodies. Of you. Each one takes something—I can see it. Your eyes are going strange, Paloma. You're forgetting things in real time. What color were my boots when we met?"

I reached for the memory and found a hole shaped exactly like it.

"I don't—" My stomach dropped. "Brown. They were brown."

"They were red," she said. "You complimented them for ten full minutes because you were too nervous to talk about anything else."

The hole gaped wider. Red. Yes. Red boots, the guildhall's bad wine, her laughing at me. Except now I was remembering her telling me, rather than remembering it myself. Secondhand. Already becoming history instead of life.

"How many do you have left?" she asked. "Memories. Warm ones."

I thought about it honestly, because the Spire punished anything less. "Maybe five. The trials take the ones I don't offer. The fragments took others. What's left is..." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "What's left is mostly you, Sable. Isn't that pathetic? Nine years of hiding, and the only things worth keeping all have you in them."

"Don't." Her voice splintered. "Don't spend those. Don't you dare spend mine."

"The last trial is ahead. I can feel it. It'll want the biggest thing I have."

"Then lie to it."

"You saw what happened to the man who lied on the fiftieth landing." We both looked at the scorch mark on the wall where a merchant's false contrition had burned him to a shadow that still, faintly, apologized. "It doesn't want worship. It wants witness. And a witness who keeps the best truth hidden isn't a witness at all."

She grabbed my collar. Frost crackled between her fingers. "Then give it to me first. Whatever it wants—give it to *me*. Let me carry it down. Let one person remember you whole."

I looked at her for a long moment. At the woman who had covered for me with Vess's enforcers, who had walked away from everything she'd built to follow a madman north, who had carried my discarded memories in a leather book for three hundred miles like they were relics—which, I suppose, they were.

"All right," I said. "But not yet. When it asks. Not before."

* * *

The last trial found us just below the summit, on a landing where the wind stopped entirely and the silence was so complete I could hear my own heart misfiring.

It was not a person. It was a child.

Small. Barefoot in the snow that fell upward. She looked at me with my god's eyes—the eyes I remembered from the first covenant, from the day I swore my oath at fourteen and felt something vast and gentle settle over my shoulders like a mantle.

"You prayed to me too," she said. "Did you know that? All these years. Not with words. With the way you flinched every time someone mentioned religion in the guildhall. With the way you kept my mark scarred over but never cut it out. You prayed constantly, Paloma Underhill. And I never answered. Not once."

"No," I said. "You didn't."

"I couldn't. Does that make it better or worse?"

"Worse," I said, and meant it. "If you'd been dead, the silence would have been grief. Instead it was a locked room. I spent nine years talking to a door."

The child tilted her head. "And now you've come to unlock it. What will it cost?"

"You know what it costs."

"Say it."

"My name. My memories. Everything that makes me *me*. The covenant reforges itself on mortal payment—that was always the term. I'll climb these last stairs as a man and reach the top as a blank page."

"And the warm things?" The child's voice softened into something ancient. "The red boots. The bad wine. The friend who followed you into the ice. The Spire requires the largest thing you carry. You knew this when you started climbing."

My hands were shaking. Behind me, Sable had gone very still.

"Yes," I said. "I know."

I turned to Sable. She was crying without any of the usual armor people wear while doing it—no anger to hide behind, no jokes, just tears freezing on her cheeks in the dead air.

"Come here," I said.

She crossed the landing like a woman walking to her own execution. I took her face in my hands. They were the last warm things either of us would feel for a while.

"When we met," I said, "you wore red boots, and I talked about them for ten minutes because I was terrified of you. When we found the Ledger of Small Debts misfiled, you laughed with your whole body, and I thought: whatever happens, whatever this oath does to me, I got to work beside her for nine years, and that was the good part of my life. Every good part. You're every good part."

"Paloma—"

"Carry it down," I whispered. "Don't read it until spring. Promise me."

"I promise," she said, and her voice broke on it, and I kissed her forehead, and I let it go.

I felt it leave me the way warmth leaves a dying fire—not pain, exactly, but a great dimming. The red boots went. The laughter went. Nine years of small mornings in the guild archive, tea going cold, her handwriting in the margins of my appraisals—all of it folded itself into a small bright coal and passed from my chest into hers, and she gasped and clutched her book of memories against it like a shield.

I stepped back. I still knew her name. I still knew she mattered. But the *why* had become a report instead of a wound, and I understood, with terrible clarity, that this was the price working exactly as designed.

The child-god watched me with something like sorrow.

"You could still turn back," she said. "The covenant would forgive you. It always forgives."

"That's the problem with it," I said. "It forgives too easily. Someone has to refuse to be let off the hook." I shouldered the satchel of fragments. "Let's finish this."

The final stairs rose toward a light that was not light—a vast pressure, a presence, a cage with something divine beating against its walls. And standing before the summit door, silhouetted against that imprisoned glow, was a figure in guild-grey who had no business being on this mountain.

Vess. Waiting. Smiling.

"Paloma," said the founder of the Guild of Impossible Things, "let's talk terms."
