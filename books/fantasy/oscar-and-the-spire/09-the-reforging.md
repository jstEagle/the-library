## 9. The Reforging

The summit of the Spire was a wound in the sky.

Paloma came up the last stair on his hands and knees, because standing had stopped being possible somewhere around the ten-thousandth plea. The trials were behind him now—every unanswered prayer his god had ever ignored, embodied and waiting, and he had faced each one with nothing but honesty, which was a currency he'd never carried much of. His voice was gone from answering them. His hands were black with frost that no longer felt like cold, only like weight.

Sable had turned back at the stair of the drowned child. He had given her his last warm memory to carry down—the smell of bread in the guildhouse kitchen, the morning he first felt safe—and watched her face change as she took it, watched her understand what she was holding. She had wept without sound. Then she had gone, because he asked her to, and because someone had to carry what he would lose.

Now there was only the summit: a plateau of black glass at the crown of the world, and above it, stars so close they seemed to lean in and listen.

And Vess.

She stood at the center of the plateau beside a thing that hurt to look at—a shape of gathered light, vast and fetal, all the stolen prayers of nine silent years knotted into something that wanted to be worshipped. The false god. Her god. It pulsed like a heart the size of a cathedral, and every pulse pushed winter outward across the world in rings.

"Paloma Underhill." Vess's voice was warm. That was the worst part. Nine years of ledgers and rumors and quiet kindnesses, and her voice was still the voice that had signed his guild papers when he had nothing but an oath-mark and a lie. "You climbed all this way to die. Let me offer you a better trade."

She turned, and in the starlight he saw how old she had let herself become—no glamour, no borrowed divinity on her face. Just a woman who had made one enormous decision decades ago and never once looked back.

"You think I'm a thief," she said. "I'm an *editor*. The gods were chaos, Paloma. A child prays for snow and gets frostbite. A widow prays for her husband and gets a thing wearing his face. You've seen it—the answers leaking through the cage. That is what worship buys. I built a better answer. Mine listens. Mine *understands*."

"It eats," Paloma rasped.

"It *consolidates*." She smiled, and it was almost gentle. "Here is my offer. Hand me the last fragment—it's in you; I can feel it humming behind your ribs like a second heart—and walk away rich. Keep your name. Keep your memories. Keep Sable. Keep everything the reforging would take from you. You'd be the only man alive who met a god's end and kept his own beginning. Partnership, Underhill. The Guild of Impossible Things has always been about choosing the impossible that pays."

The fragment stirred inside him. It had been stirring for weeks, since the dream-ledgers began, since his god's voice first reached him thin as thread: not asking for worship. Asking for *witness*.

Paloma got to his feet. It took longer than it should have.

"You know what's funny," he said. "Nine years ago I faked my own oath's death so I could hide in your ledgers. And the whole time, the real crime wasn't mine. It was yours. You didn't kill the gods, Vess. You just made the world deaf and called it peace."

"The world is *alive*," she said. "Look around you. Look at what I've grown."

He looked. And he made himself see it clearly, the way he'd made himself see every plea on the stairs—not excusing it, not despairing of it. The false god was beautiful the way an avalanche is beautiful. It was made of every prayer Vess had intercepted, every whispered hope she'd rerouted into herself for thirty years. It had no will of its own. It had only appetite, and her hand on its reins.

And reins could slip.

"That's the flaw in your ledger," Paloma said. "You think you own it. But prayer doesn't sit still. It compounds. One day that thing wakes up wanting more than you want to give, and there won't be a Spire high enough to cage *it*."

"I've calculated the risk."

"So did the gods' first priests." He pulled open his collar. The covenant mark blazed against his chest, silver-white, the fragments beneath his skin singing in a chord that shook frost off the glass. "I'm done calculating. I'm here to do arithmetic of a different kind."

Vess sighed—the sigh of a woman humoring a dying man. "Then you leave me no choice."

She raised her hand, and the false god opened its eye.

It had no face, but it had attention, and its attention fell on Paloma like a collapsing building. The winter in his bones roared. The fragment inside him screamed—not in pain, in *recognition*, the way a key screams when it finds its lock held by the wrong hand. Vess meant to take it out of him by force, to crack him open like a strongbox, and she had the power to do it. Nine years of stolen divinity said she did.

Paloma did the one thing she had never planned for.

He stopped holding on.

*Take it,* he thought—not to Vess, but downward, inward, along the thread that ran from his scarred mark through nine silent years to the imprisoned thing at the Spire's root. *Not her. Me. All of me. Witness.*

The fragment left him like breath after drowning.

It tore free of his ribs and hung in the air between him and Vess, blazing, whole—the last piece of a covenant broken three decades ago. Vess lunged for it, fingers wreathed in stolen light—

And Paloma caught it first.

Bare-handed. Bleeding. Mortal.

The moment his skin closed around it, he understood why Vess had never dared this. The fragment was a *term of contract*, and terms bind whoever holds them. In his palm, the final clause of the old covenant lay open like a page waiting for ink. Restore the god entire: pay with name and memory, pay with self, become nothing but servant forever. Or let it die.

Those were the old terms. Written by frightened people in a frightened age.

But Paloma Underhill had spent nine years among ledgers, and there was one thing every ledger-keeper knows that gods and founders forget:

*Contracts can be amended.*

"No," he said aloud—to Vess, to the false god, to the old dead terms burning in his hand. "I don't accept the payment. And I don't accept the silence either. There's a third line on this page. There always was. Nobody ever read far enough."

He drove the fragment into his own chest.

Not into the mark—*through* it. The covenant shattered and reformed around him in the same instant, and Paloma wrote as it reformed, not with words but with everything he was: every plea he'd answered on the stairs, every memory Sable carried down the mountain, every year he'd spent trading in small debts and impossible things. He wrote himself into the covenant—not as servant, not as sacrifice.

As *witness*.

A mortal bound within the divine contract, present at every hearing, guarantee of a single new term, absolute and eternal: **no prayer shall go unheard again.** Not answered blindly. Not ignored. *Heard.* Every god bound by the covenant would have a witness at their ear—a man with a ledger-keeper's patience and a mercenary's refusal to look away.

The false god shrieked.

Because that was the thing Vess had never understood: her creation was made entirely of prayers, and prayers are *voices*. Bind a witness into the covenant, make hearing itself holy, and every stolen voice inside her god suddenly had somewhere to go. Somewhere honest.

They went.

The false god came apart like a knot untying—thirty years of intercepted hopes streaming out of it, through the summit, down through the Spire's broken cage, home to the world. Snow that fell soft instead of killing. Widows' voices returning to widows' ears as grief instead of horror. The long winter cracked along its length like river ice in spring.

Vess screamed and reached for the unraveling light, trying to hold her god together with bare hands, and for one moment Paloma saw her truly: not a monster, just a woman who had heard one too many unanswered prayers and decided the problem was the gods rather than the silence.

"Paloma—" she said, and her voice broke on his name, and for a heartbeat she sounded like the founder of a guild for lost things, which was what she had been. "It could have been *order*—"

"It could have been a cage," he said. "Cages always get called order by the ones holding the key."

The last of the false god collapsed inward, and Vess fell with it—into the light, into the unraveling, into whatever waits at the bottom of a thing you built out of other people's voices. She did not cry out at the end. She only looked at him, and nodded once, almost approving, as if he'd finally balanced a ledger she'd left open for thirty years.

Then the summit was empty except for snow beginning to fall upward, and a god rising through the Spire's core like water finding its level.

It came up small.

That surprised him most. He had expected vastness, terror, the old overwhelming presence of his deity in its prime. Instead what rose out of the broken floor was barely taller than a man—diminished, worn, honest as a plain stone. It looked at Paloma with something that was not eyes, and it did not demand worship.

It offered him its hand.

"Witness," it said, and its voice was the thread-voice from the dream-ledgers, stronger now, warm now. "You paid more than the old terms asked. Name and memory, yes—but you paid it *forward*, not down. No god has ever done less and been given more."

"I didn't do it for you," Paloma said. His voice was already fraying at the edges—he could feel the memories going soft, years dissolving like sugar in rain. "I did it for everyone still praying."

"I know. That's why it worked."

The god touched his brow, gentle as snowfall, and the covenant sealed—not with thunder, but with the quiet click of a ledger closing true. Winter broke across the world in a single long exhale. Far below, at the foot of the Spire, Paloma knew without knowing how he knew, a woman was standing in sudden thaw, holding a warm memory that was about to become the only record of a man's life.

His knees gave. The god caught him—small, diminished, strong enough for that.

"What happens now?" Paloma whispered. The world was going soft and white at the edges. "What's left of me?"

"A man," the god said. "Missing some pages. Free of every debt but one—the one you wrote yourself. Go down the mountain, witness. Someone down there kept your place."

The last thing Paloma Underhill saw before the dark took him was snow falling gently on the summit of the Spire, melting where it landed, and stars leaning close overhead, listening—as if for the first time in thirty years, they could finally hear.

* * *

He woke to the smell of bread.

It was wrong, and he knew it was wrong, because he did not know why it mattered—but the woman sitting beside the inn fire looked up when he stirred, and her face was wet, and she was holding a loaf like it was something sacred.

"You're back," Sable said. "Mostly. Come inside. There's a lot you don't remember, and I wrote it all down."

She set a ledger in his lap. On the cover, in her handwriting: *Underhill — being an account of one life, kept faithfully.*

Paloma opened it. The first line read: *Your name is Paloma Underhill. You used to be a mercenary. Then you became something else. Start here.*

Outside the inn's window, spring was coming up over the edge of the world—and somewhere below the horizon, small and honest and listening, a god was learning to answer.
