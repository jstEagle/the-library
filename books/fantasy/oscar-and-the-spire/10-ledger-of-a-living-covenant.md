## 10. Ledger of a Living Covenant

Sable counted the days by the thaw.

It was a habit she'd learned from Paloma, back when he still had all his years to lose—mark the ledger not in coin but in change. So when the icicles on the guildhouse eaves began to weep, and the gutters sang their first thin music in four months, she took out the old book with its cracked leather spine and wrote, in her own careful hand: *Day one hundred and twelve. The water runs.*

She wrote it because he couldn't. That was the arrangement now, the one they'd made on the mountain without ever saying it aloud: she would keep what he lost.

The Spire had stood silent for nine days when the climbers came down. Not him—not yet. First it was the pilgrims, ragged and frostbitten, who'd followed the breaking winter north like birds. They came staggering into the lowland villages babbling about a light at the summit, about a voice that asked them questions instead of demanding things. *What did you pray for? Who did you tell? What did you do with your hands afterward?* A god that interviewed its faithful. Sable had laughed until she cried when she heard that, and then she had cried for a while longer, which was different.

On the tenth day, she saw him coming across the white plain.

He walked slowly. That was the first wrongness—Paloma Underhill had always moved like a man being chased by his own schedule, quick and lean and half-apologizing for taking up space. This man came on at the pace of someone who had decided the world could wait. His coat was ruined. His hair had gone grey at the temples where it hadn't been before, or maybe it had been and she'd never let herself notice. He carried nothing. The covenant fragments were gone from the world, spent, and whatever remained of them lived somewhere behind his eyes.

Sable went out to meet him. She got as far as twenty paces and stopped, because there was a question she suddenly needed answered more than she needed anything else, and she was afraid of it.

"Paloma," she said.

He looked at her with a long, level attention. Not blank. Not empty. But searching—the way a man searches a room he knows he's lived in but can't quite place.

"I know your face," he said at last. "I don't remember learning it."

"That's all right." Her voice held. She made it hold. "That's what I'm for."

* * *

They rebuilt the guildhouse through the spring.

Not the Guild of Impossible Things—that name died with Vess at the top of the Spire, along with Vess herself and the false god she'd grown from stolen prayer like a pearl around a wound. Sable had the old sign taken down and burned in the courtyard, and half the city turned out to watch it go. People had suffered under the impossible. They wanted possible things now. Bread that rose. Wounds that closed. Prayers that got an answer small enough to hold.

So the new sign read: **THE GUILD OF POSSIBLE THINGS**, and beneath it, smaller, painted by Sable's own hand because Paloma's letters came out crooked these days: *We trade in what can be done.*

He kept the ledgers. He was still brilliant at it—that hadn't gone anywhere; the covenant had taken his memories, not his mind—but the ledgers themselves had changed character. Where once the guild had recorded relics and rumors, prices and provenance, now every entry began with a question. *Who is asking? What do they actually need? What will it cost them to receive it?* He'd come down off the Spire with the theology of a witness burned into him: nothing gets given without being heard, and nothing gets heard without someone standing close enough to listen.

Sable stood close enough. That was her whole job description, and she performed it daily.

Some of what he'd forgotten was small. He no longer remembered the taste of the plum wine they'd drunk the night he joined the guild, and when she told him about it he listened like a man hearing a good story about a stranger. Some of it was enormous. He did not remember his mother's voice, or the name of the village that raised him, or the nine years of quiet service during which he'd pretended his oath was dead. When she gave those back to him, entry by entry, reading from her book in the evenings while he sat across the table with his hands folded, he received each memory the way you'd accept a returned coat—recognizing it, trying it on, finding it fit a body slightly changed.

"You were afraid," she said one night, reading from the entry marked *Year Three*. "You were afraid the silence meant you'd been abandoned. You used to stand at this window at midnight and press your hand flat against the glass."

"Did I?" He looked at his palm as if it might remember on its own. Then, quietly: "And now I know why it was silent. It wasn't abandonment. It was a cage. I was pressing my hand against the wall of it and calling it absence." He shook his head slowly. "I wish I remembered feeling that. It seems important to have felt it."

"You felt it. I'm telling you that you did." She closed the book gently. "That's how it works now. You felt things. I keep the proof."

He smiled at that—a real smile, unhurried—and Sable felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the day she'd reported only half of what she knew to the guild enforcers, choosing the institution over the man, and then chosen the man over everything, and watched a city freeze for it. She had done cowardly things and brave ones, sometimes in the same hour. He didn't remember any of them. She had decided that was mercy rather than injustice, though some mornings she wasn't sure, and on those mornings she wrote harder entries in her book, entries about herself, because someone ought to be keeping accounts of her too.

* * *

The god came to visit on a Tuesday.

Sable found it in the courtyard, and it took her a moment to understand what she was looking at, because she had expected—she didn't know what. Radiance. Terror. Something with too many wings. Instead there was a figure sitting on the mounting block by the stable door, small and unglamorous, wearing the shape of a tired traveler with dust on its boots. It looked like it had walked a very long way and intended to walk further, and liked walking.

"Is he in?" it asked.

"He's in the counting room."

"Good. I won't keep him long. I try not to keep anyone long anymore." The traveler-god rubbed its hands together like an old man warming them at a stove. "I've gotten smaller, you know. Deliberately. A god the size of a sky can't hear a single person in it. I wanted to be the size of a person, so I could hear one person properly. It turns out that's much harder work." It glanced up at her, and its eyes were ordinary brown, and behind the ordinariness was something vast looking out, politely, the way a whale looks out through a porthole. "You're the keeper."

"I keep his losses."

"That's a holy trade." It said it simply, without ceremony, and somehow that made it land heavier than any thunder would have. "Most gods demand worship. I've decided to demand accuracy instead. Someone has to remember what was paid."

Paloma came out into the courtyard then, a ledger under his arm, and stopped when he saw the figure on the mounting block. The two of them regarded each other across ten feet of spring mud, god and former servant, and Sable watched her friend's face go through recognition, grief, relief, and something new—something she suspected had no older name than *colleague*.

"You're really small," Paloma said.

"You're really missing thirty years." The god shrugged. "We match."

"We don't match. I'm going to get mine back. One entry at a time." He set the ledger down on the mounting block beside the god, deliberately, like a man laying down a card he'd been holding. "But I'm not going to get back the man who climbed the Spire. He finished. Whatever I am now is after him."

"And what are you now?"

Paloma considered it. Sable held her breath, because she'd wondered the same thing every day since the plain, and he'd never once said it aloud.

"Not a mercenary. Not a servant. Not a saint—I tried the saint posture for a week and it gave me a headache." He almost smiled. "A witness. Bound into the covenant as a mortal witness. Somebody stands between the prayers and the answer and makes sure both sides behave. That's the job. It doesn't pay well."

"It pays in being present," the god said. "Which is the only currency I honor anymore."

"Then we're solvent." Paloma picked his ledger back up. "Come inside. There's a widow on Elm Street who prayed last week for rain on her garden and got hail instead, and I want you to look at the entry and tell me whether that's your doing or just weather. We should establish the difference. Formally. In writing."

The god rose from the mounting block, dusted off its coat, and followed him toward the door like the most ordinary thing in the world—which, Sable supposed, was exactly what it had made itself into. At the threshold it paused and looked back at her.

"He'll ask you, eventually, what he was like before," it said. "When he does—tell him true. Even the parts that hurt him. Especially those. A witness who flatters isn't a witness."

Then it went inside, and the door swung shut, and Sable stood alone in the courtyard with the mud and the melting snow and the sound of two voices—one mortal, one nearly so—beginning to argue companionably about the theology of hail.

She opened her book to a fresh page. At the top she wrote: *Spring, first year of the new terms.* And below it, because the god was right and she had promised herself accuracy:

*He asks me who he was. I am learning to answer honestly. Today he asked whether he had been happy before the Spire, and I told him: sometimes, and never carelessly, and mostly in rooms where people needed him. He thought about that for a long time. Then he said, "That sounds like someone worth remembering," and went back to his ledgers.*

*He is not restored. He is rebuilt. I have decided these are different things, and that the second is better, and I am going to keep deciding that, every day, in ink.*

Below that, at the bottom of the page, she added one final line—the kind of line the old guild would have called impossible, and the new guild called merely pending:

*A petition came today, unsigned, written in a hand I almost recognize. It asks not for gold or healing or snow, but for a meeting: "Let the founder's daughter come to the guildhouse and ask what her mother built." Vess had a daughter. Nobody living knew that but me. I have not yet shown Paloma the page.*

*I will tomorrow. Witness means witness—even when the debt comes home.*
