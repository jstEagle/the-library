## 1. The Valley That Forgot

There are two kinds of people in the world: those who would find tending forty-one lamps along a valley border a punishment, and those who would find it a life. I have been both, depending on the weather.

My name is Emrys Winterbourne, and for nineteen years I have been the last apprentice of the Wardens' Guild of Hollowmere — which sounds grander than it is. The Guild once numbered three hundred. It had banners, and a bell tower, and a library so vast that the cataloguing of it was considered a career in itself. Now it has Guildmaster Corvane, me, a cook named Brannic who drinks more than he cooks, and a roof that leaks in exactly four places, all of them above my bed, as though the building itself had opinions about who should suffer.

The lamps are my work. Forty-one of them, set at intervals along the border stones that ring the valley like a necklace of grey teeth. Once — so the old records said, back when anyone bothered to read them — each lamp held a flame that never went out, fed on something finer than oil. Ward-fire, they called it. The light that kept Hollowmere whole.

Now they hold ordinary fire, when they hold anything at all. I fill them with lamp-oil rendered from fish fat because we can't afford whale or wax, and in winter the flame gutters and stinks, and if you stand downwind of one too long you come home smelling like a chandlery's misfortune. Sela says I've stopped noticing. Sela is wrong. I notice every time. I just pretend not to, because pretending is cheaper than grieving, and I've got a budget to keep.

* * *

That morning began the way most mornings begin, which is to say with Brannic banging a pot in the kitchen below and shouting something about porridge as though volume could improve it. I dressed by feel, found my boots where I'd left them — under the bed, in the one dry corner, a strategic decision — and went down into the guildhouse's great hall.

You should understand what the great hall looks like now. Imagine a cathedral built for giants, then imagine the giants left and took everything worth having with them. The long tables remain, scarred and pale. The hearth is big enough to roast an ox whole; we burn driftwood in it because it's free. Along the walls hang the shields of dead wardens, their paint gone the colour of old bruises. And above the far door, carved into stone so deep that even sixty years of dust couldn't soften it, runs the Guild motto:

*THE LIGHT REMEMBERS.*

I used to think that was beautiful. Lately I've started thinking it sounds like a threat.

Corvane was already at the high table, which meant he'd been there since before dawn, which meant either trouble or paperwork, and in my experience the two arrive together like a married couple. He's not what you picture when you picture a guildmaster. No white beard, no staff topped with a crystal the size of a fist. He's a lean, grey-templed man with ink-stained fingers and the particular stillness of someone who learned long ago that moving quickly attracts questions. His eyes are brown, unremarkable, and miss nothing, which I consider a design flaw in an otherwise serviceable face.

"You're up late," he said. It was half past six.

"The stars were interesting."

"They're always interesting. That's the problem with them." He pushed a plate toward me without looking at it. Porridge. Grey, like everything else in the hall. "Eat. Then I want the eastern run done before noon — lamps twenty-two through thirty-one. And Emrys—" here he did look at me, and something in the look made me stop chewing, "—after sundown, I want you on the border stones. Night watch."

I blinked. "Night watch? The full circuit?"

"Twenty-two through thirty-one again. After dark."

Nobody did night watch anymore. That wasn't tradition being broken; it was tradition being respected, in the way you respect a dead man by not asking him to dance. The night watch had been retired decades ago, along with the ward-bells and the oath ceremony and every other ritual that assumed the lamps held anything but fish fat and wishful thinking. There was nothing on the border at night. There was nothing on the border ever. The wards had been quiet for sixty years — sealed, sleeping, whatever word you preferred for the same silence.

"Is something wrong?" I asked.

Corvane returned to his papers. "Something is always wrong. That's what wards are for. You'll take the lantern horn and the storm-cloak. If anything seems amiss — anything at all — you'll wake me. Not Brannic. Me."

"Define amiss."

He almost smiled. Almost. With Corvane, the distance between almost and actually was a country you needed papers to cross. "You've tended these lamps your whole life, boy. You know what right looks like. Report what isn't."

It was the longest instruction he'd given me in a month, and the strangest. But strangeness, in Hollowmere, was a currency with no market. You pocketed it and moved on.

* * *

The eastern run took me past the mill, where Sela was arguing with her father's waterwheel, which had developed the habit of stopping whenever it felt insufficiently appreciated. She's the miller's daughter, my oldest friend, and she has the kind of face that makes her opinions known before her mouth catches up. When she saw me on the path she abandoned the wheel mid-negotiation and fell into step beside me, because Sela has never once let me walk anywhere alone if she could help it.

"Night watch," she said, before I'd told her anything. "Brannic talks in his sleep, and also while awake, constantly. So. Night watch. Why?"

"I don't know why. He didn't say why."

"He always says why. He once explained the entire taxonomy of moss to you because you asked what time it was."

"That was a different question."

"It was the same kind of question." She kicked a stone off the path with unnecessary violence. "Something's wrong. Something's been wrong for weeks, if you'd look up from your lamps. The birds left early this year. Did you notice? Whole flocks, gone over the pass in September, like they'd gotten a letter."

"Birds migrate, Sela."

"Not *early*. Not all of them. Old Tammera down at the spring says the water tastes different. Says it tastes like before, whatever that means — she's ninety, everything tastes like before to her." She glanced sideways at me, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for things she actually meant. "And the lamps. Don't think I haven't noticed you coming home smelling like a burned wharf twice as often as you should. You're filling them more. They're burning faster. Why would dying lamps suddenly get *hungrier*, Emrys?"

I didn't have an answer. That was the honest truth of it, and she knew it was the honest truth of it, and we walked in silence for a while, which between Sela and me was its own kind of conversation.

At the fork where the mill road met the border path, she stopped. "Come to the harvest dance," she said. "First frost festival. You always say you will and then you're 'on rounds.' Corvane can survive one evening without you."

"Maybe," I said, which is what I always say, and we both knew it meant no, and she both knew I knew, and that's friendship in Hollowmere for you: a long series of gentle mutual disappointments held together with bread and stubbornness.

She watched me go a moment longer than usual. "Emrys. If something's wrong — really wrong — you'd tell me?"

I thought about Corvane's face over the porridge. *Not Brannic. Me.* "If I knew what to tell," I said, "you'd be the first."

It wasn't quite a lie. It lived next door to a lie and borrowed its tools, but it wasn't quite one. I've had occasion since to think about how many sentences like that a person can say before the neighbourhood changes.

* * *

Lamp twenty-two sat at the base of the Watchstone, the tallest of the border markers, and I reached it as the light was going thin and gold. I trimmed the wick, scraped the soot, filled the reservoir, and did the small mechanical devotions that had replaced whatever grander rite the lamp was built for. My hands knew the work. That's the thing nobody tells you about a small life: it fits you eventually, the way a riverbed fits a river, and after enough years you can't tell whether you shaped it or it shaped you.

While my hands worked, my eyes wandered east, past the last lamp, past the last stone, to the pass.

The pass is the only place the valley opens — a grey notch in the mountains where the sky comes down low enough to touch. Nothing came through it. In sixty years, per the records, precisely nothing: no traders, no pilgrims, no letters. The world beyond existed for us mostly as rumour and as the subject of the few surviving maps, which disagreed with each other so thoroughly that I suspected the cartographers of working from imagination and spite. Beyond the pass were the old kingdoms, the ones that had fought the Thorn War until the war ended and the peace began, and the peace, as far as Hollowmere was concerned, consisted entirely of us not being part of it.

We weren't conquered. We weren't exiled. We were simply *kept* — a valley behind wards, with a guild to tend them, and a history that stopped at the border stones like a stream hitting ice.

When I was eight, I asked Corvane what was over the pass. He looked at me for a long time — long enough that I remember the exact quality of the lamplight on his face — and said, "More of the same, only worse." I believed him for years. I'm not sure when I stopped. Somewhere in the slow erosion between twelve and nineteen, *more of the same, only worse* had worn through, and underneath it I'd begun to suspect the truth, which was that he had never answered the question at all.

The first stars came out. I counted them without meaning to — an apprentice habit, the old tally, *seven and seven and seven, the sky keeps its books* — and they were all there, dim and correct, doing what stars do, which is nothing, beautifully.

I lit lamp twenty-three. The flame caught, steadied, burned the way flames burn: briefly, expensively, stupidly. A real ward-flame would have burned without me. Would have burned, the records said, *against* the dark, whatever that meant. I stood there a moment in the smell of fish-fat smoke and felt the restlessness move through me like weather — familiar, unwelcome, unfixable. The comfortable smallness of my life fit me perfectly, and some traitor part of me kept trying buttons that weren't there.

Then I heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. More the memory of a sound, arriving late — a hum, felt in the teeth rather than the ears, rising out of the stones themselves. The Watchstone hummed. I put my palm flat against it and the humming climbed, faint and wrong, like a bell struck somewhere far below hearing.

In sixty years, the border stones had been silent. Every record agreed. Every record, in fact, insisted upon it with the slightly excessive calm of a witness coached by a lawyer.

The hum faded. I stood in the dark with my hand on cold rock and my heart going like a millwheel, and I thought: *report what isn't.*

What wasn't, was that the stone had sung.

I looked up the line of lamps — twenty-four, twenty-five, twenty-six, little orange eyes receding into the dark — and beyond them the pass, black against blacker sky. And I noticed, though I couldn't have said why I noticed, that the stars above the pass looked thinner than the stars above the valley. As if something on the other side of the mountains had drawn a curtain halfway.

I finished the round. Of course I finished the round. I was raised by the Guild of Keeping Things Quiet, and muscle memory is a kind of loyalty.

But when I got back to the guildhouse, well past midnight, Corvane was not asleep. He was standing in the great hall with his coat already on, and the look on his face was one I had never seen on him in nineteen years, and it took me a moment to name it.

It was fear. Old fear. The kind that has been waiting a long time for company.

"Emrys," he said quietly. "Tell me exactly what happened on the stones."
