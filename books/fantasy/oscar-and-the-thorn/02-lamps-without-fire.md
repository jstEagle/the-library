## 2. Lamps Without Fire

Corvane listened to my report of the night watch with his arms crossed and his face arranged into something neutral, which for Corvane meant it was very much not neutral. He asked me three questions. Had I touched the stones? No. Had anything come over the border? Nothing but wind and one extremely ambitious hare. Had I told anyone? I said no, and that was even true, if you didn't count telling the hare, who had struck me as trustworthy.

"Good," he said, and then, as if he'd caught himself being generous: "Routine watch. That's all it was."

It hadn't felt routine to me. The stones had hummed under my palm like a hive deciding whether to swarm. But Corvane had a way of pronouncing things finished — cases closed, days done, questions retired — and I had nineteen years of practice at letting him.

So when he handed me the repair roster the next morning and told me to start with the eastern road lamps, I took it without argument. This is what my life was, and mostly I liked it: oil, glass, brass fittings, a ladder older than my grandfather's opinions, and forty-one ward-lamps strung along the valley roads like beads on a thread of dusk.

I should explain about the lamps, because everything else in this story hangs from them, the way a coat hangs from a nail you stop noticing until the day it's empty.

The ward-lamps weren't lamps, exactly. They were anchors — or they had been, once. Sixty years ago, at the end of the Thorn War, the founders had driven the wards into the earth along every pass and road, and the lamps marked where the ward-line surfaced, the way mushrooms mark where there's something vast and fungal underneath. In the old days, they said, each lamp burned with its own small piece of the ward's power, and the light never guttered, and travelers swore the roads were safe enough to sleep on.

That was before my time. By the time I was born, whatever fed the wards had gone thin. The lamps still stood, and the wards still held — nothing came in, nothing went out, and the valley stayed green while, according to our own maps, the world beyond had spent sixty years either burning or rebuilding, depending on whose rumors you preferred. But the lamps themselves had gone dark one by one, like guests leaving a party early. When I was nine, the last of them on the mill road went out, and Corvane had sat up all night with the Guild's remaining books open around him, and in the morning he'd said, "We'll maintain them," and that was that.

Maintain. Not restore. Not rekindle. Maintain. It was a word that did a lot of quiet work in Hollowmere.

* * *

By midmorning I was twenty feet up a ladder on the eastern road with a screwdriver in my teeth and a lamp housing open like a ribcage, and I want to be honest with you: I was happy. There is a particular contentment in fixing something that wants to be fixed. The lamp's brass collar had corroded; the glass pane was clouded with sixty years of valley dust, which is fine and mineral and gets into everything, including, I'm fairly sure, my character. I polished the glass with vinegar and newspaper until the morning showed through clean. I scraped the corrosion, dressed the fitting with tallow and ash, replaced the wick assembly even though no wick had drawn fuel since before I could walk, because a Warden maintains, and maintenance is mostly ritual with tools attached.

Below me, leaning against my ladder, was Sela.

Sela Vane ran her family's mill two fields from the guildhouse, which meant she had flour in her eyebrows by eight every morning and opinions by eight-oh-five. She had been my friend since we were both small enough to fit inside the same rain barrel, which we had tested. She watched me work the way she always watched me work: with her chin tipped back, chewing on a grass stem, radiating the specific patience of someone waiting to say something unkind out of love.

"You missed the harvest dance," she said.

"I was on watch."

"You're always on watch. You were on watch during the spring fair. You were on watch during my cousin's wedding, which, for the record, had dancing so bad it qualified as an emergency." She spat out the grass stem. "You know what's past the border stones, Emrys?"

"According to the maps? The Ashlands, the Thorn Marches, the sea, and certain death."

"According to the maps," she agreed. "And have you ever seen any of those things?"

"The sea," I said, "looks exactly like the diagrams."

She laughed, and the laugh echoed off the lamp housing, and I felt it in my chest the way you feel a bell through a floorboard. "One day," she said, "you're going to climb down this ladder, and you're going to walk east past the last stone, and you're going to keep walking, and the whole valley will talk about it for a week, and then you'll write us a letter describing the ocean, and it will take four months to arrive because the postal roads are apparently also diagrams."

"I can't leave," I said, and heard how flat my voice went. "The wards—"

"The wards held for sixty years without you personally polishing them." She said it gently, which was worse. Sela could be gentle the way other people could be cruel — precisely, and only when it would land. "Corvane won't let you go, is what you mean. And you won't make him."

I tightened a screw that didn't need tightening. "Somebody has to keep the lamps."

"Somebody," Sela said, "has to live in the town where the lamps are. Those are different jobs. You've just never noticed, because you've never left the space between them."

I wanted to argue. The trouble was that she was right, and we both knew she was right, and arguing would only delay the moment when I had to admit it. So instead I said, "Hand me the putty knife," and she did, and we let the silence be a truce rather than a defeat.

But I'll tell you what she planted, because she knew she was planting it. She had a way of saying things and then walking off before they sprouted, so you couldn't catch her looking smug among the seedlings. Longing, once named, doesn't stay quiet. It sits in you like water finding a crack.

* * *

There were eleven lamps on the eastern road. I finished the tenth by late afternoon, and it was nearly golden hour, that hour when the valley does its best impression of somewhere worth never leaving — long light across the barley, smoke standing straight up from chimneys, the mountains going purple and embarrassed about it — when I reached the eleventh.

The eleventh lamp was different, and I had known it was different since I was twelve.

It stood at the bend where the eastern road turned toward the pass, and it was older than the others — the ironwork was a style none of the Guild books matched, and the base bore a maker's mark worn almost smooth, though under the right light and with the right squinting you could make out something like a thorned branch wrapped around a star. Every year, when I made the rounds, this lamp gave me less trouble than the others. Its fittings never corroded. Its glass never clouded. Once, when I was fourteen, I'd dropped the entire housing down the hillside and retrieved it scratched but unbent, and I had decided then, privately, not to think too hard about why.

And once a year — usually near midwinter, usually when I was alone, usually when I was tired enough that I could pretend afterward I'd imagined it — it flickered.

Not lamplight. Lamplight flickers yellow and dies. This flickered white. Cold, steady, quick, like a heartbeat seen through skin.

I checked the road both ways. Empty — Sela had gone home, the harvest wagons were in, the whole valley had folded itself indoors for supper. Then I climbed the ladder, opened the housing, and looked down into the lamp's throat, where no flame had lived in living memory.

It was doing it now. A pale pulse in the dark, once every few seconds, patient as a thing that had been waiting sixty years and could wait sixty more. As I watched, it brightened — not much, not dramatically, nothing a passerby would notice from the ground, but unmistakably, deliberately brighter, the way a sleeper stirs when they hear your footsteps on the stairs.

Here is the thing about secrets. Nobody teaches you how to have them. The Guild taught me how to keep records, how to maintain, how to say nothing — but holding your first real secret is like holding your breath underwater; you discover immediately that your body has opinions. My hands started sweating on the ladder rungs. My heart performed a drum solo. Because I understood, in some animal part of myself, that this was not supposed to happen. The wards were failing. Corvane had said so for years, quietly, in the tone he used for weather. Failing meant fading, dimming, going out like all the others.

Failing did not mean waking up.

I did what any well-trained apprentice of the Guild of Keeping Things Quiet would do. I cleaned the glass. I dressed the fittings. I replaced the wick assembly, which was absurd, and I knew it was absurd, and I did it anyway because my hands needed a job while my mind ran in circles. And then I closed the housing, climbed down, packed my tools, and walked away without looking back, which took more discipline than the entire rest of the day combined.

At the corner of the lane I did look back. Of course I did. I'm honest with you about my failures or I'm nothing.

The lamp stood dark against the sunset, exactly as it should.

Except that as I watched, deep in the glass, low down where nobody ever looks — it pulsed. White. Once. Twice. Like something knocking politely from the inside.

Like something that knew I was watching.

* * *

Supper at the guildhouse was bread, hard cheese, and Corvane not looking at me, which was a full meal in itself.

He was at the head of the table with a ledger open, doing the accounts, except I'd seen him do the accounts for six years and he never turned pages that slowly unless the numbers were wrong or the thoughts were heavy, and the Guild's finances were boringly sound. He had the look of a man carrying something fragile across a crowded room and pretending it's a book. Twice he opened his mouth as if to ask me something. Twice he asked instead whether I'd oiled the ladder hinges. I had. He nodded as though this resolved a matter of state.

I thought about telling him. Let's be clear about that — let's be clear that I thought about it, sitting there with cheese in my hand, rehearsing it. *Master Corvane, the eleventh lamp on the eastern road is flickering. Not dying. Flickering. Like it used to. Like it's supposed to.* And I imagined what would follow: his face going careful, the ledgers closing, questions — had I told anyone, had I touched it, exactly what had happened on the stones last night, exactly, Emrys, leave nothing out — and beneath all of it, that look I'd seen at midnight, that old fear waiting for company.

I didn't tell him.

Understand, I told myself it was prudence. A good apprentice verifies before reporting; a good apprentice doesn't bring his master a ghost story about a lamp. I'd see it again tomorrow night, or I wouldn't, and either way there was nothing to say yet. That was the story I told myself, and it was even partly true.

But there was another part, smaller and less flattering, that I've had sixty miles and several disasters to examine since. Part of me didn't tell him because the flicker was *mine*. It was the first new thing that had ever happened to me in nineteen years of maintaining, and some greedy, starving corner of my heart wanted to keep it one more day before the Guild got hold of it and filed it under *routine*, and pronounced it finished, and sent me back up the ladder with a screwdriver.

Longing versus duty, Sela would have called it, if she'd known. She'd have said it kindly, and it would have landed like a hammer, and she'd have walked off before I could answer.

After supper I washed the dishes, banked the fire, and climbed to my room under the eaves where I kept the few things that were mine rather than the Guild's: three books, a whittling knife, a river stone shaped like a tooth, and a map of the world beyond the valley that I'd copied by hand from the Guild archive when I was fifteen, working by candlelight, tracing coastlines I would never see onto paper that cost me a month of saved allowance.

I unrolled the map on my bed. Somewhere past the eastern edge of it, past the mountains and the pass and the border stones that hummed like a held breath, the world was out there doing whatever worlds do. Wars ending. Roads opening. Oceans behaving exactly like diagrams.

And here in Hollowmere, one lamp on the eastern road had opened its eye after sixty years of sleep, and looked at me, and knocked.

I rolled the map away before I could trace anything. I blew out my candle. And I lay in the dark listening to the guildhouse settle — Corvane's boots on the stairs below, slow, pausing, starting again — and told myself that tomorrow I'd check the lamp first thing, and it would be dark, ordinary, dead as it had been all my life, and I'd laugh at myself and get on with the rounds.

The next night, I drew the midnight watch on the border stones again.

I remember thinking, as I pulled my coat on and stepped out into air colder than any midsummer night had a right to be, that the valley seemed quieter than usual. Not peaceful-quiet. Held-quiet. The kind of quiet in a room just before someone speaks.

Above the eastern pass, where the stars should have been thick as spilled salt —

the sky was already beginning to go out.
